Cellular and molecular mechanisms of asymmetric stem cell division in tissue homeostasis
Authors: Sema Bolkent
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe asymmetric cell division determines cell diversity and distinct sibling cell fates by mechanisms linked to mitosis. Many adult stem cells divide asymmetrically to balance self‐renewal and differentiation. The process of asymmetric cell division involves an axis of polarity and, second, the localization of cell fate determinants at the cell poles. Asymmetric division of stem cells is achieved by intrinsic and extrinsic fate determinants such as signaling molecules, epigenetics factors, molecules regulating gene expression, and polarized organelles. At least some stem cells perform asymmetric and symmetric cell divisions during development. Asymmetric division ensures that the number of stem cells remains constant throughout life. The asymmetric division of stem cells plays an important role in biological events such as embryogenesis, tissue regeneration and carcinogenesis. This review summarizes recent advances in the regulation of asymmetric stem cell division in model organisms.

INTRODUCTION

The stem cells in the tissues are both capable of replicating themselves and differentiating into a variety of cell types throughout the life span of the organism. They are also capable of differentiation into progenitor cells that will eventually give rise to more specialized cells. Stem cells do not normally directly give rise to differentiated cells. They produce transit‐amplifying progenitor cells committed to differentiate into a specific type of cell usually after a number of cell divisions. The most important feature that distinguishes stem cells from progenitor cells is that while producing the progenitor cell during division, it also creates its own backup. A multipotent hematopoietic stem cell normally divides to produce multipotent progenitor cells that will give rise to different blood cells. The sibling cells that can differentiate into all blood lineages of hematopoietic stem cells involve asymmetrically arranged lysosomes, autophagosomes, mitophagosomes, and the protein NUMB to keep stem cell properties (Loeffler et al., 2019). The endocytic protein AP2A2 has a role in asymmetric cell division of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (Ting et al., 2012). Strong stimulation of the T‐cell receptor has been shown to induce an increase in asymmetric stem cell division rates using live imaging. Generation of CD8 T memory cells is achieved by asymmetric stem cell division (Gräbnitz et al., 2023). In a self‐renewing adult tissue‐ the lining of the intestine and the outer layer of the skin‐ the stem cells are also constantly producing transit‐amplifying progenitor cells. The asymmetric cell division of stem cells is the division of a cell into a daughter stem cell and a differentiated cell through cell fate determinants (Bohl et al., 2022; Katajisto et al., 2015; Loeffler et al., 2019; Rujano et al., 2006).

Asymmetric cell division is a special type of mitosis. Asymmetric division of stem cells can occur mainly by two mechanisms: Cell polarization and Par complex proteins, which determine the fate of the cell, are effective in the separation of a stem cell and a differentiated cell. In 1983, Ken Kemphues and Jim Priess discovered the first Par mutant in Caenorhabditis elegans. Most Par genes are upstream regulators of cell polarization. The cortical polarity consisting of anterior and posterior Par proteins is crucial for the orientation and position of the spindle during the first zygotic division. Par proteins are asymmetrically localized anterior Par complex (Cdc42‐Par3‐Par6‐ atypical Protein Kinase C [aPKC]) and posterior determinants, for example, Par1, Par2 in the one‐cell worm embryo. The other set of proteins known as the Scribble polarity complex consists of Scribble, Discs large, and Lethal giant larvae (Lgl) in metazoa (Goldstein & Macara, 2007; Rodriguez et al., 2017). During asymmetric cell division, two distinct molecular localization are observed on the apical and basal poles. The Par3/Baz family of polarity determinants is namely as Bazooka (Baz) in Drosophila melanogaster. The aPKC/Par6/Par3 complex takes place at the apical pole for self‐renewal identity. This complex increases the concentration of Numb, an inhibitor of Notch signaling, at the basal pole. The asymmetric distribution of Numb is regulated by the Par3/Par6/aPKC complex. Wnt and Notch signals at the apical side cause stemness (Li et al., 2022). Notch signaling pathway is used to asymmetrically segregate regulators of self‐renewal in D. melanogaster neuroblasts. Notch inhibition signals induce differentiation in basal localization, whereas Notch activity remains high in the apical and promotes proliferation in neural stem cells called neuroblast division. Primary Notch ligand Delta is highly expressed early in neuroblasts and controlled by the intrinsic temporal program. It is suggested that early Notch signaling activity is required to terminate neurogenesis in D. melanogaster (Sood et al., 2023). The adapter protein Miranda binds with the transcription factor Prospero (Prox1 in vertebrates) and Brat (Trim32 in vertebrates) on the basal pole for differentiation (Lee, Wilkinson, et al., 2006; Mukherjee et al., 2015). Transforming growth factor‐β family signaling is a direct part of a cell‐fate commitment in embryonic and somatic stem cell renewal (Mullen & Wrana, 2017). In addition, Wnt‐driven asymmetric cell division is essential during development. The Wnt/β‐catenin asymmetric signaling pathway shows some differences compared to the canonical signaling pathway in nematodes (Lam & Phillips, 2017).

Most studied fruit fly Drosophila asymmetric cell division is regulated by a complex consisting of Pins (Leu‐Gly‐Asn repeat enriched protein [LGN] in vertebrates), Mud (nuclear mitotic apparatus [NuMA] in vertebrates), and Gαi polarity proteins that control the spindle orientation as well as aPKC/Par6/Par3 complex. Mushroom body defective (Mud), Partner of Inscuteable (Pins), and the heterotrimeric G protein alpha subunit (Gαi) form a complex to capture microtubules. Cell polarity determinants are only left in place if the mitotic spindle is properly oriented. Neuroblasts divide symmetrically and self‐renew when double‐mutated for Lgl, an inhibitor of aPKC, and Pins, a regulator of cortical polarity. Lgl, Pins, and aPKC regulate neuroblast self‐renewal versus differentiation (Lee, Robinson, & Doe, 2006).

Due to these mechanisms, stem cells are protected from mutations and can always remain intact as cells with the same genome (immortal DNA). The immortal DNA strand hypothesis is controversial and proposes that somatic stem cells segregate asymmetrically. According to this hypothesis, adult stem cells in each asymmetric division protect their template DNA strands from mutations accumulated during DNA replication (Cairns, 1975; Conboy et al., 2007; Elabd et al., 2013; Rocheteau et al., 2012). Chromosomes are not asymmetrically segregated in Drosophila male germline stem cells (GSCs) (Yadlapalli et al., 2011). Schepers et al. (2011) also reported that leucine‐rich repeat‐containing G protein‐coupled receptor 5 (Lgr5) intestinal stem cells randomly segregate newly synthesized DNA strands, in contrast to immortal strand hypothesis. The mitotic asymmetry from non‐random chromosome segregation may be due to replicative and chronological aging in stem cells (Charville & Rando, 2011). Template strand co‐segregation has been reported in asymmetric cell divisions based on template age. It has been reported that pre‐existing histones are selectively kept in the renewed stem cell and that the distribution of old versus new histones between sister chromatids is regulated by DNA replication components (Kahney et al., 2017). The daughter cell inheriting the younger templates has a more differentiated phenotype, but older templates are observed in the less differentiated daughter cell of muscle stem cells (Conboy et al., 2007). Hyperactive asymmetric stem cell division has been found in cultured psoriatic keratinocytes. Most of the non‐vertical divisions appeared to be much lower than the vertical ones. Vertical and parallel types of asymmetric cell division have also been reported in the inflamed mouse skin. An increased percentage of asymmetric segregation of BrdU was shown in the cell pairs of psoriatic epidermal cells. It has been reported that psoriatic stem cell division supports the immortal strand hypothesis (Jia et al., 2016).

Intracellular and extracellular factors play a role in asymmetric cell division (Figures 1 and 2). The extracellular asymmetry of stem cells is created by the microenvironment outside the cell, namely the niche. The stem cell niche is of special importance in mammals, for example, the niche for hematopoietic stem cells involves specific interactions with the bone marrow stroma, while the niche for intestinal stem cells involves interaction with the intestinal epithelium. The extracellular matrix, adjacent cells, and secretory proteins form the niche. These keep the number and condition of stem cells under control. As stem cells are formed with the stem cell niche, they show signs of aging over time according to their niche content (Behrens et al., 2014). The bidirectional interactions between stem cells and their niches are dynamic complex structures that include different cellular components, secreted factors, extracellular matrix, and immunological and metabolic controls. Membrane‐bound and secreted factors such as Wnt, SCF, Notch, and chemokines bind directly to the surface receptors of stem cells to regulate cell fate, self‐renewal, and polarity (Lane et al., 2014).

During asymmetric division, the stem cell divides vertically. Therefore, the stem cell contains immortal DNA strands with new synthesized DNA at its apical part, while the differentiated cell contains parental DNA with new synthesized DNA. Note the localization of apical and basal fate determinants in dividing a stem cell.

Asymmetric cell division involves the differential distribution of external and internal fate determinants, including signaling molecules, gene expression regulators, and polarized organelles. As a result, a single stem cell gives rise to two daughter cells, one of which retains its stem cell identity and the other of which loses its stem cell characteristics.

ASYMMETRIC STEM CELL DIVISIONS IN DIFFERENT SYSTEMS

Two different types of asymmetric cell division can occur asymmetric renewal and asymmetric differentiation in model systems such as C. elegans and D. melanogaster. The zygote of the nematode C. elegans divides asymmetrically for different cell lineages during embryonic development. The P1‐cell in the small posterior pole gives a germline cell instead of a larger anterior blastomere cell. Lack of the centrosomal kinase AIR‐1 reveals centrosome‐independent polarization as AIR‐1 controls polarity initiation in C. elegans (Klinkert et al., 2019). In Drosophila neuroblast division, the stem cell divides asymmetrically and contains the apical and basal “crescents” that are vertical fate‐determining factors (Yamashita et al., 2010). D. melanogaster neuroblasts divide asymmetrically to give rise to a self‐renewing neuroblast and a ganglion mother cell (Gallaud et al., 2017). The Baz‐Par6‐aPKC proteins are found in the apical cortex of mitotic neuroblasts. The basal part contains fate determinants such as Numb, Miranda, and Prospero for differentiation in Drosophila neuroblast (Bergstralh et al., 2013; Yamashita et al., 2010). Sensory‐organ precursor cells in the development of the peripheral nervous system of Drosophila divide asymmetrically into the pIIa and pIIb daughter cells with distinct potentials. The pIIa cell is activated by the Notch signaling pathway, giving rise to hair and socket cells, while the pIIb cell forms a glial cell. Numb, a target of aPKC, is localized at the anterior pole in dividing sensory organ precursors. Smad anchor for receptor activation (Sara) endosomes are inherited by pIIa after cell division. The sheath cell and neuron are formed by the subsequent asymmetric division of pIIb (Coumailleau et al., 2009; Gho et al., 1999). Male and female GSCs orient their mitotic spindle vertically toward the hub and cap cells, respectively. This results in fate asymmetry between two daughter cells. Drosophila female GSCs give a differentiating daughter cell called a cystoblast and another GSC. Drosophila male GSCs divide asymmetrically to self‐renew and produce a gonialblast. A pair of cyst stem cells encapsulates the GSCs and provides the necessary signals for GSC identity. Maternal centrosomes in GSCs create an active microtubule organizing center before daughter centrosomes have formed. Thus, specific recognition and binding of microtubules occurs to asymmetric sister kinetochores and sister centromeres. Centromeres are specialized chromosomal regions that are often defined by epigenetic characteristics. CENP‐A, referred to as CID in flies asymmetrically distributed among sister chromatids as an epigenetic determinant of centromere identity. The inner kinetochore protein CENP‐C is crucial for the assembly of CID in Drosophila female GSCs (Carty et al., 2021; Kochendoerfer et al., 2023). The kinetochore protein Ndc80 is asymmetrically localized and associated with centromere protein A (CENP‐A) enrichment (Ranjan et al., 2019), (Figure 3). Mammalian asymmetric cell division has similarities with many Drosophila‐specific proteins, as well as some characteristic differences. Many of the proteins that control asymmetric cell division in invertebrates are conserved in mammals: the G proteins, Pins, the Par proteins, Lgl, and Numb. However, mammalian Numb is apically localized in asymmetrically dividing progenitor cells, and Drosophila Numb is found in the basal daughter cell (Betschinger & Knoblich, 2004). Haspin‐mediated histone H3 phosphorylation at threonine 3 is essential for the asymmetric separation of old versus new histones enriched chromosomes in Drosophila male germline (Xie et al., 2017). Haspin‐catalyzed histone H3 phosphorylation at threonine 3 regulates asymmetric cell division ratio via Pax2, a transcriptional activator of Wnt5a in mouse embryonic stem cells (Gao et al., 2023). Whilst Drosophila has one Notch receptor, mammalian species have four types of Notch receptors (Notch1‐4) that are inhibited by the Numb protein. Notch ligands in Drosophila are Delta and Serrate whereas in mammals there are five Notch ligands (three from the Delta‐like family [Dll1, Dll3, and Dll4] and two Serrate orthologues known as the Jagged family [Jag1 and Jag2]) (Gozlan & Sprinzak, 2023).

WHAT ARE THE KEY MOLECULAR PLAYERS INVOLVED IN ASYMMETRIC CELL DIVISION PROCESS?

It is known that intracellular and extracellular mechanisms are essential for asymmetric stem cell division. Different distributions of cellular contents are crucial factors for the shape and size of the cell during asymmetric division. Proteins, DNA modifications and histone inheritance, prions, mRNA, non‐coding RNAs, and organelles determine the asymmetrically inherited during cell divisions (Shlyakhtina et al., 2019). Asymmetric cell division begins in the presence of the polarity axis. Cell fate determinants are located at the poles of the cell, allowing the two daughter cells to migrate along the polarity axis and eventually the two daughter cells separate by cytokinesis (Chen, Fingerhut, & Yamashita, 2016). Cytokinesis is the division of the cytoplasm that follows the equal division of the genetic material into the daughter cells. Two daughter cells at the end of cytokinesis remain connected for some time by an intracellular bridge known as the midbody. The inherited midbody after cytokinesis participates in asymmetric division via Sara endosomes, a subpopulation of Rab5‐positive early endosomes. Sara contributes to Notch‐dependent asymmetric cell fate cytokinesis (Loubéry et al., 2017). Segregation of mother and daughter centrosomes is biased in the asymmetric cell divisions. The proximal daughter cell expresses high levels of nuclear β‐catenin whereas the distal daughter cell contains molecules for differentiation in vitro study. The asymmetric distribution of Wnt/β‐catenin signaling components directs asymmetric inheritance of centrosomes and consequently cell fate (Habib et al., 2013). Wang et al. (2009) showed that the centriole‐specific protein Ninein is more concentrated in one of the two centrioles in interphase and that removal of Ninein disrupts the asymmetric segregation. The asymmetry of sister centrosomes is differentially enriched with proteins involved in centrosome specification. Klp10A of the kinesin‐13 family in Drosophila GSCs regulates asymmetric centrosomes (Chen, Inaba, et al., 2016). It has been suggested that the direct interaction of the cytokine receptor homolog Dome with the microtubule regulator Ebl in the Drosophila GSC may regulate centrosome/spindle orientation to allow asymmetric stem cell division (Chen et al., 2018). Megator, a nuclear matrix protein regulates Drosophila male GSC self‐renewal and asymmetric division through the mitotic checkpoint complex. Loss of Megator results in misorientation of centrosome and abnormal chromosome segregation (Liu et al., 2015). The centrosome‐associated primary ciliary membrane has been shown to play a critical role in the division of embryonic neocortical stem cells. The ciliary membrane remains at one spindle pole and is inherited asymmetrically by a daughter cell that maintains stem cell character (Paridaen et al., 2013). NuMA acts as a receptor for microtubule motor dynein at spindle poles and is associated with the abnormal presence of peroxisomes. NuMA is necessary to recruit dynactin to the cell cortex of keratinocytes. Peroxisome‐associated PEX11b is involved in the control of peroxisome partitioning into epidermal daughter cells. Appropriate peroxisomal localization may be required for NuMA as the spindle rotations and NuMA mislocalization have been reported in PEX11b‐ deficient keratinocytes (Asare et al., 2017).

Whether hematopoietic stem cells can switch between the symmetric and asymmetric division mechanisms is still under investigation. It is thought that hematopoietic stem cells in the native bone marrow niche undergo asymmetric cell division. Asymmetric stem cell division may be regulated by extrinsic fate determinants such as the stem cell niche. In asymmetric cell divisions, one daughter cell maintains stem cell identity, and the other commits to differentiating into blood cell types. Many soluble cytokines are molecular effectors of hematopoietic development (Takano et al., 2004). JAM3 and Tie2 membrane proteins as asymmetric markers in regulating hematopoietic stem cell fate have been reported (Girotra et al., 2020). Ligand‐receptor interactions are also likely to be a universal mechanism for asymmetrically dividing cells. However, many types of asymmetric division use different mechanisms. Lissencephaly‐1 as an intrinsic factor partially controls niche signal transduction and adhesion in Drosophila ovarian GSC self‐renewal (Chen et al., 2010). Rambhatla et al. (2005) showed that there is a down‐regulation of IMP dehydrogenase for guanine nucleotide biosynthesis and that immortal DNA strand cosegregation is regulated by this enzyme. However, the intrinsic and extrinsic stimulus factors of the stem cell and the bias of the asymmetric molecular segregation are still not fully understood.

MOLECULAR FATE DETERMINANTS

Molecular fate determinants such as Miranda, Prospero, Brat complex and Pins, Mud, and Gαi complex are preferentially segregated into one daughter cell to induce its specific fate during asymmetric cell division. It is known that organelles such as peroxisomes and endoplasmic reticulum also have asymmetric inheritance. Peroxisome organelles with various metabolic and non‐metabolic functions can be divided symmetrically or asymmetrically. Increased levels of inheritance of peroxisome protein 1, a peroxisomal membrane protein on older peroxisomes allow effective retention of these organelles in the mother cell by preventing their transfer to the daughter cell. Old and young peroxisomes populations segregate as asymmetrically during cell division in yeast. It has been suggested that the young organelles are preferably transported to the newly forming buds in wild‐type yeast (Kumar et al., 2018). The endoplasmic reticulum divides asymmetrically, and this may be responsible for correct spindle positioning before cell fate in proneuronal cells (Eritano et al., 2017). Additionally, stem cells inherit less old mitochondria during asymmetric division (Katajisto et al., 2015).

The double‐stranded RNA‐binding protein Staufen 2 is asymmetrically distributed during mammalian neural stem cell mitosis. This finding suggests that Staufen 2 plays a role in cell fate determination during active neurogenesis (Kusek et al., 2012). The Miranda scaffold controls the subcellular distribution of cell fate determinants, including Staufen, during asymmetric division of Drosophila neuroblasts. Staufen is required for binding to a coiled‐coil region of the Miranda cargo‐binding domain (Jia et al., 2015). Brat translational repressor binds directly to the Miranda central domain (Lee, Wilkinson, et al., 2006). Programmed histone inheritance is an epigenetic factor for cell fates. Asymmetric histone inheritance during asymmetric cell division is accepted as an intrinsic factor. Threonine 3 phosphorylation distinguishes pre‐existing from newly synthesized H3 during mitosis. Histone H4 also shows an asymmetric inheritance pattern in Drosophila. This suggests that the incorporation of new and old histones during DNA replication may play an important role in transmitting epigenetic information (Wooten et al., 2019; Xie et al., 2017). Metabolic reprogramming via nutrient‐sensitive PI3K‐AKT‐mTOR pathways, glutamine, and fatty acid metabolisms is important for the self‐renewal of stem cells (Ito & Suda, 2014).

Transcription factors such as Nanog, Sox2, and Oct4 cluster asymmetrically in stem cells and play a role in specifying cell fate (Kaneko et al., 2015; Kusek et al., 2012). The Smad1 transcription factor, which targets proteasomal degradation in human embryonic stem cells, is distributed asymmetrically during mitosis. This causes pericentrosomal asymmetries, leading to the unequal division of various cellular components (Fuentealba et al., 2008).

Recently, it has been suggested that the asymmetric distribution of microRNAs may also lead to asymmetric cell fate. Studies have shown that the microRNA‐34 represses Notch expression by binding directly to the 3′ untranslated region of Notch mRNA. It has been shown that miR‐34a directly targets Notch as a cell fate switch in colon cancer stem cells. Deletion of miR‐34a inhibits asymmetric division (Bu et al., 2016). Lnc34a, long non‐coding RNA, induces asymmetric division by targeting miR‐34a in colon cancer stem cells (Wang et al., 2016). miR‐146a stimulates the asymmetric division and increases Let‐7c level through degrading LIN28 in breast cancer stem‐like cells (CCSCs). miR‐146 inhibits Wnt activation in a Let‐7c‐dependent way (Liang et al., 2018). This suggests that miRNA molecules play a powerful role in controlling the biological information of asymmetric inheritance.

POLARITY‐DRIVEN ASYMMETRIC OR SYMMETRIC CELL DIVISION

Symmetric cell division is used by stem cells to expand their population, for example, during development or after injury. When a stem cell undergoes a symmetric division, this results in the development of two identical daughter cells. During symmetrical division, fate determinants segregate equally and horizontally between two stem cells. In the asymmetric division, spindle orientation and cell‐fate determinants lead to the formation of two distinct cells. As a result of this process, a stem cell and a differentiating cell with distinct cellular fates are produced following a single mitosis. Self‐renewal, which is one of the basic features of stem cells, is achieved through the process of asymmetric cell division. This can occur during development and in adults to maintain the homeostasis of the body. A balance between asymmetric and symmetric cellular division is necessary to maintain of normal development and homeostasis.

Cell polarity regulates the internal structure of the cell, causing a change in the shape of the cell. The control of stem cell niches prevents the specific differentiation of cells. Specialized subcellular structures, such as nanotubes, cytonemes, and ligand‐receptor interactions may control niche signals in stem cells (Chen, Fingerhut, & Yamashita, 2016). In addition, the polarity of individual cells is usually established by intracellular antagonistic interactions. Wnt signaling is crucial for the self‐renewal of the intestinal epithelium. However, the role of canonical Wnt signaling in hematopoietic stem cells has been controversial. A more than two‐fold increase in Wnt activity impairs the self‐renewal of hematopoietic stem cells (Bhavanasi & Klein, 2016). Integrin controls spindle orientation, leading to increased aPKC activity. This in turn controls Delta/Notch signaling in one of the two daughter cells, suggesting that adult intestinal stem cells divide asymmetrically (Goulas et al., 2012). Members of the MAPK family play diverse roles in the asymmetric division. Signaling by p38αβ MAPK is important in the asymmetric division of satellite cells. It has been shown that partial inhibition of p38αβ MAPK rescues the self‐renewal of aged satellite cells (Bernet et al., 2014). Dystrophin‐deficient satellite cells show a reduced number of asymmetric divisions and a loss of polarity (Dumont et al., 2015).

The asymmetric sorting of certain proteins into different cell regions is necessary for cell polarity. For example, ASH1 localization element and mRNA as a ribonucleoprotein complex localized to the tip of daughter cells promote asymmetric sorting in budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae. She2p, an RNA‐binding protein is required for the asymmetric sorting of the Myo4p/She3p complex sensitive to mRNA (Gonsalvez et al., 2003). In the Drosophila larval brain, type II neuroblasts undergo multiple asymmetric divisions, leading to the development of immature intermediate neural progenitors. The acetylation of multiple histone proteins activates earmuff expression in these immature progenitors. The study indicates that earmuff restricts their developmental potential and lead to asymmetric division of stem cells (Janssens et al., 2017). Loss of cell polarity in many organisms disrupts asymmetric divisions. When researchers investigated the effect of asymmetric or symmetric division on clonal dynamics by combining experimental approaches with mathematical modeling, they suggested that asymmetric cell division is an essential factor in preventing clone expansion (Bener et al., 2024).

ASYMMETRIC STEM CELL DIVISION IN SOME DISEASES

Several studies have reported that a decrease in the number of stem cells may be associated with the disease. It has been shown that the gradual loss of Drosophila GSC leads to tissue aging (Pan et al., 2007). The age‐related loss of stem cells occurs due to decreased interaction between stem cells and their niches. Inactivation of asymmetric segregation of cell‐fate determinants during stem cell division may contribute to cancer. Yan et al. (2023) suggest that the propagating speed of stem cells is promoted by asymmetric cell division. In this case, asymmetric cell divisions may preserve the cancer stem cell population. Cancer stem cells of various cancer types undergo both symmetric and asymmetric divisions. The transition of cancer stem cells from asymmetric to symmetric division plays an important role in cancer progression (Bergstralh et al., 2013; Yan et al., 2023). It is known that p53 gene mutations induce asymmetric self‐renewal control of adult stem cells at a genomic level.

Drosophila neuroblasts with mutations in Numb, Pins, Miranda, and Prospero have been reported to invade other tissues (Caussinus & Gonzalez, 2005). Large numbers of centrosomes in self‐renewing asymmetric stem cell division are associated with tumor formation (Castellanos et al., 2008). Cancer stem cells have stem cell‐like properties in many tumor types. It has been reported that cancer stem cells undergo asymmetric cell division versus symmetric cell division during early tumorigenesis (Cicalese et al., 2009). Asymmetric cell division is thought to contribute to intratumoral heterogeneity (Li et al., 2022). The balance between asymmetric and symmetric division is disturbed in late‐stage cancer. Increased symmetric division of cancer stem cells contributes to tumor progression. PKCı‐ELF3‐Notch3 signaling regulates asymmetric cell divisions in KRAS‐mediated lung adenocarcinoma (Ali et al., 2016). Cell‐cycle‐related proteins, including cyclin B1, cell division cycle 25 (CDC25) B, and CDC25C play an important role in the asymmetric division of polyploid giant cancer cells contributing to cancer heterogeneity. In both human ovarian and breast cancer, high levels of cytoplasmic cyclin B1 expression are associated with tumor grade and lymph node metastasis (Fei et al., 2019). It has been shown that p53 regulates polarity of cell division in mammary cancer stem cells. Nutlin3, a small molecule that causes p53 stabilization reduces self‐renewing divisions in mammary tumor stem cells by switching the division mode from symmetric to asymmetric (Cicalese et al., 2009). Chao et al. (2023) showed that there is asymmetric cell division in colorectal CCSCs, which is important for colorectal cancer. The early establishment of intratumoural heterogeneity is contributed to by the asymmetric division of CCSCs. It has been demonstrated that the number of asymmetric divisions is dramatically reduced in dystrophin‐deficient muscle stem cells. Thus, these cells directly contribute to the progression of Duchenne muscular dystrophy (Chang et al., 2016; Dumont et al., 2015). MicroRNAs associated with self‐renewal and differentiation play a role in cancer. A miR‐34a‐Numb‐Notch regulates asymmetric stem cell division in intestine and colon cancer (Bu et al., 2016). The proliferation of malignant cells in the bone marrow is a hallmark of acute myeloid leukemia, a stem cell‐driven cancer. The dynamics of leukemia have been studied using model analysis, with different feedback signal effects on symmetric and asymmetric division. The researchers concluded that small changes in the probability of cell fate play a role in the development of leukemia (Kumar et al., 2024). Several studies have shown that dysregulation of asymmetric division plays an important role in the development and progression of cancer.

PERSPECTIVES AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Questions about the identity of fate regulators such as niche signaling, spindle polarization, and inheritance of organelles need further investigation in healthy humans and disease states in light of Drosophila model organism studies. The elucidation of the molecular mechanisms of stem cell self‐renewal or differentiation may lead to the identification of novel targets for disease prevention and its therapies. Further studies are needed to understand how the choice between symmetric and asymmetric cell division is made under specific conditions in mammals, which are complex organisms. Understanding how and when asymmetric centromeres form is important for future research due to epigenetic differences in sister centromeres. Epigenetic dysregulation results in defects in cell fate determination, leading to diseases such as cancer and aging. Asymmetric cell division is closely related to stem cell biology and cancer. Maintaining the homeostasis of stem and non‐stem cells protects from pathologies such as tissue degeneration or tumor formation. The role of asymmetric stem cell division in cancer stem cells at different stages of human carcinogenesis remains poorly understood. It is important to investigate how the molecular mechanisms of asymmetric cell division respond to external and internal signals during tumourigenesis and drug response.

The fundamental question of how a stem cell decides whether to divide into symmetrical or asymmetrical cells is still not fully understood. Many mysteries that remain as a question mark in asymmetric and symmetric cell divisions need to be addressed using new experimental approaches and new hypotheses. In silico evaluation and computational systems biology can help by providing insights into the mechanisms underlying asymmetric stem cell division.
