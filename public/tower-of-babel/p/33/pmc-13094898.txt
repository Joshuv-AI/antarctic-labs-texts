The personal habits and sleep quality affect the health-related quality of patients with hypertension: The results of a cross-sectional study
Authors: Betcy George, Porkodi Arjunan, Abraham M. Ittyachen, Rajkumar Mani, Soney M. Varghese, Ramesh Chandrababu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Hypertension can negatively impact the quality of life and may influence sleep patterns. While traditional cardiovascular risk factors are well-established, the potential of irregular sleep patterns as a novel risk factor for heart disease is still not well understood. The objectives were to assess the prevalence of hypertension and its correlation with sleep habits and overall quality of life. We assessed the socio-clinical sleep quality index, health-related quality of life, and the factors linked to individuals with high blood pressure.MATERIALS AND METHODS:The outcome variables were evaluated using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) instrument and the MINICHAL scale. Settings and Design: This cross-sectional study involved 252 hypertensive patients in a tertiary care hospital in India selected using purposive sampling. Statistical analysis used: The relationship between sleep quality index (SQI) and health-related quality of life (HRQOL) was assessed using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. The relationship between socio-clinical variables, SQI, and HRQOL was analyzed using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.RESULTS:The systolic blood pressure averaged 146.92 ± 13.03, while diastolic blood pressure exhibited a median of 89 mmHg (80, 93). A substantial proportion (70.2%) of hypertensive patients reported poor sleep quality; the mean HRQOL score was 22 (7, 30). Alcohol consumption and occupational factors were significantly associated with SQI.CONCLUSION:Subjects who had a prolonged history of hypertension demonstrated a deterioration in their quality of life. A weak, yet statistically significant, positive correlation was identified between SQI and HRQOL, indicating a modest association.

Introduction

Hypertension is a major risk factor for overall morbidity and mortality. Affecting over one billion people worldwide, it is the primary risk factor for cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). India is currently experiencing rapid demographic and epidemiological shifts, with non-communicable diseases (NCDs) leading to significant disability, illness, and death. Emerging evidence suggests that severely irregular sleep patterns can substantially increase the risk of hypertension and CVDs. People with irregular sleep habits have higher odds of having hypertension compared to regular sleepers, highlighting sleep health as a separate factor. Sleep deprivation disrupts the autonomic nervous system, which affects blood pressure regulation and raises the risk of hypertension. Irregular sleep duration and timing may constitute new risk factors for CVDs beyond traditional ones.[1]

Quality of life (QOL) is a significant indicator of social well-being and evaluates the outcomes of hypertensive management. The disease’s chronicity makes people with hypertension agonize to have a poor health-related quality of life (HRQOL), representing more than 14 unhealthy days per month compared with normotensive individuals.[2] The HRQOL tends to be worse among those with associated comorbid conditions.[3]

Effective hypertension management is essential for the prevention of CVD and has a detrimental impact on both sleep patterns and overall well-being. Previous research studies have separately explored the link between sleep disorders and QOL. The current study provides a more targeted evaluation of the QOL domains most relevant to hypertensive individuals, revealing stronger and more clinically meaningful associations with poor sleep quality in Central Kerala. The current study examines the sleep quality index (SQI), HRQOL, and their relationship and associated factors in individuals with hypertension.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted at a tertiary care multi-specialty hospital. The study findings are presented in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [Supplementary Figure 1].

Study participants and sampling

The sample size was calculated with HRQOL as the primary outcome measure, the variable that corresponds to the highest sample size. Sample size estimation was done using the equation for estimating the mean:

where n = sample size, (Z”1-α/2”) 2 = confidence level of 95%, which is 1.96, and margin of error of 0.05. The anticipated mean (μ) was 42.93, d = relative precision at 6%, and the anticipated standard deviation (σ) from a previous study was 18.86.[4] Based on this formula, the initial estimated minimum sample size was 206. To ensure sufficient data, we enrolled 252 participants.

The study involved 252 participants admitted to the medical wards using purposive sampling. The participants were patients with hypertension, aged between 18 and 70, diagnosed with stage I or II hypertension as per WHO guidelines, and clinically diagnosed for 1 year or above. Exclusion criteria include patients with hypertension who are diagnosed with acute or chronic renal failure, with more than three BP-regulating agents, and with well-controlled hypertension.

Data collection tool and technique

The study was conducted from May 2023 to February 2024, and participant selection adhered to predefined inclusion and exclusion parameters. Following informed consent, sociodemographic and clinical data were collected. Participants maintained a seated posture for a 5-minute interval before the measurement of systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) using an automated non-invasive blood pressure measurement device (BPMD). Concurrently, data on Sleep quality (SQ) and overall QOL were collected. The entire data collection procedure for each participant approximated 20 minutes.

To ensure accurate BP identification, the researchers underwent a training program developed by the World Health Organization, measuring BP using an automated BP monitor.[5] The device accurately recorded both SBP and DBP values and presented the results on a digital screen.[6] We opted for a digital monitor due to its validated accuracy, ease of use, freedom from observer bias, and its environmental impact.

Sleep quality (SQ)

The primary outcome variables were SQI and HRQOL. SQI was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) tool, a widely accepted and validated instrument assessing the SQ and sleep disturbances over 1 month.[7] The PSQI is a 19-item, self-report questionnaire that evaluates seven key components: subjective SQ—the individual’s perceived sleep satisfaction; sleep disturbances—interruptions during sleep; daytime dysfunction—difficulties in maintaining alertness during the day; sleep onset latency—time required to transition into sleep; habitual sleep efficiency—the ratio of actual sleep time to the total time spent in bed; total sleep duration; and the use of sleep medications. The reliability value of PSQI is 0.84.[8]

Total SQ was calculated as the sum of scores across all subscales, ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating worse sleep quality. In line with previous research, a score of 6 or higher was classified as poor SQ.[9] Each component was rated as follows: SQ (0 = exceptional, 1 = good, 2 = fair, 3 = poor); sleep latency (0 = quick sleep onset, 1 = occasional difficulty, 2 = frequent difficulty, 3 = persistent difficulty); sleep duration (0 = >7 hours, 1 = 6–7 hours, 2 = 5–6 hours, 3 = <5 hours); sleep efficiency (0 = ≥85%, 1 = 75–84%, 2 = 65–74%, 3 = <65%); sleep disturbance (0 = infrequent and minimal, 1 = occasional and mild, 2 = frequent and moderate, 3 = persistent and severe); and sleep medication use (0 = none, 1= <1/week, 2 = 1–2/week, 3 = ≥3/week).[10]

Health-related QOL

The participants’ HRQOL was evaluated using the MINICHAL scale, a 17-item self-report instrument specifically developed to evaluate the impact of hypertension on QOL within the past week. Originating in Spain, the scale assesses two domains: mental (nine items, scored 0–27) and somatic (seven items, scored 0–21). An additional item evaluates the overall influence of hypertension on HRQOL. The translated version of the tool was used for the study after establishing language validity. Responses were anchored on a four-point Likert scale (0 = not at all, 1 = somewhat, 2 = a lot, 3 = very much), with a total score ranging from 0 (optimal health) to 51 (poorest health). The reliability value of the tool is reported as 0.87.[11]

Covariates

The study examined various socio-clinical factors, including age, gender, occupation, participation in social support groups, smoking, alcohol use, duration of hypertension, duration of treatment for hypertension, presence of comorbid diseases, and number of antihypertensive medications. These data were obtained through a self-reported questionnaire. Current alcohol use was categorized as consuming at least 8 ml of alcohol per week, based on the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism criteria.[12] The participants were classified as smokers if they reported consuming at least one cigarette daily for a consecutive year.

Ethical consideration

This investigation conformed to ethical standards and obtained approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (protocol number: MOSC/IEC/642/2022). Before the study, written informed consent was acquired, and data confidentiality was ensured.

Statistical analysis method

The data collected from 252 participants in the study were coded and transferred to SPSS version 26 software for analysis. Categorical data were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Data normality was assessed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For continuous variables, descriptive statistics such as mean and standard deviation were calculated for normally distributed data, while median and interquartile range (IQR) were used for non-normally distributed data. Spearman’s correlation and the Chi-square test were employed to examine relationships and associations between the study variables. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

In the study, 252 participants were identified and selected. The median age of the study participants was 61.5 years (55.75, 66), and 116 (46.03%) were aged 60 years or older. The majority of the participants were female, 130 (51.59%), and unskilled laborers, 138 (54.76%). Family was the most preferred source of social support among the participants, 237 (94.05%). Regarding substance use behaviors, the prevalence of smoking and alcohol consumption was relatively low, with only 50 (19.84%) and 52 (20.63%) reporting their use. A significant proportion, 186 (73.81%), had been diagnosed with hypertension within 1–10 years [Figure 2].

Clinical course of hypertension

The majority of them, 191 (75.79%), had been undergoing treatment for hypertension for an average of 10 years. Furthermore, 199 (78.97%) of the participants had other existing health conditions. The mean SBP was recorded at 146.92 ± 13.03, while the median DBP score was 89 (with an IQR of 80 and 93) [Table 1].

Discussion

Hypertension continues to be a major cause of CVDs, and its prevalence is rising over time. To comprehensively manage this chronic condition, assessing QOL and sleep patterns offers vital information regarding the physical and psychological burdens experienced by affected individuals. Additionally, it can be a crucial indicator of the effectiveness of different pharmacological or non-pharmacological treatments.

The present study evaluated the HRQOL and SQI in patients with hypertension. Additionally, we examined the relationship between these factors and socio-clinical characteristics. The median age was 61.5 years (IQR: 55.75–66), and 116 (46.03%) were aged 60 or older [Table 1].

While this number may appear counterintuitive for a hypertensive cohort recruited in a hospital setting, it likely reflects the inclusion criteria and the specific units from which patients were recruited. The age profile of the participants is consistent with previous research findings. For example, various studies revealed that the individuals were above 60 years old.[1314] This consistency enhances the applicability of our age-related results. With the aging population, it is becoming more important to fully understand the significance of hypertension in elderly individuals.

Our study found a relatively similar distribution of hypertension among both genders (130 females: 51.59%, 122 males: 48.41%). The previous literature had shown mixed results in this regard, indicating that hypertension prevalence varies between sexes. Contrary to the present findings, a study from India revealed men exhibiting a higher prevalence compared to women (16.32% vs 11.56%), signifying age as a significant risk factor for both genders.[15] Whereas literature from Pakistan reported a higher incidence among females.[16] A recent review stated that even though hypertension is more prevalent among males, females exhibit a pronounced upsurge in their thirties.[17] These findings suggest that gender-specific approaches may be necessary for hypertension management, despite current guidelines recommending the same treatment for both sexes.

In the study, unskilled laborers constituted the largest occupational group, comprising 138 (54.76%), compared to professional workers, 23 (9.12%). Previous studies showed significant differences across various occupations and regions. Research conducted in Brazil reported a high prevalence of hypertension, also highlighting a link between socioeconomic status and hypertension prevalence.[18] However, conflicting results from a study in Dubai indicated that skilled workers have a five times higher risk compared to professionals.[19] These findings suggest that, although there is a higher prevalence among unskilled laborers, the relationship between the two is complex and may be influenced by multiple factors.

In the study, 50 (19.84%) reported smoking and 52 (20.63%) reported alcoholism. A study carried out in Nepal revealed a higher prevalence of hypertension among current smokers.[20] In contrast, some other studies conducted in India and Spain both indicated that regular smoking and alcoholism reduced the attainment of target BP levels in treated hypertensive individuals.[2122] Collectively, the findings underscore smoking and alcohol consumption as significant risk factors for hypertension, impeding its management.

In the study, 199 (78.97%) had at least one comorbidity, with diabetes mellitus being the most prevalent, supporting a study from India, which also reported the same.[14] These findings emphasize the importance of considering comorbidities in hypertension management and research.

In this study, the majority of participants, 177 (70.2%), experienced poor SQ. Numerous studies consistently reported a high prevalence of poor SQ, ranging from 36.4% to 60.4% compared to. This finding suggests an important relationship between poor SQ and hypertension in normotensive individuals [Table 2].[2324]

In the study, the average HRQOL score in the mental domain was 11 (IQR: 4–15), and the somatic domain score was 8 (IQR: 3–14) with an overall score of 22 (IQR: 7–30) [Table 3]. Previous studies have reported varying MINICHAL scores across different populations. An Indian study reported worse HRQOL in both mental and somatic domains among patients with uncontrolled hypertension.[25]

The research revealed a weak but significant positive correlation between SQ and HRQOL (ρ = 0.169, P = 0.007). Similar modest associations have been noted in hypertensive populations from India and Saudi Arabia using the same MINICHAL instrument.[2627] However, this connection tends to weaken or even disappear after considering factors like depression/anxiety, suggesting that mood disorders might play a mediating role.[3]

Additionally, significant associations (P < 0.01) were found between SQI and alcoholism and occupation [Table 4]. A Chinese study reported that occupation type, particularly mental labor, is significantly related to SQ.[23] These findings underscore the complex relationship between hypertension and SQ.

Interestingly, the study assessed a longer duration of hypertension and its treatment to correlate with improved HRQOL [Table 5]. This supports the adaptation hypothesis that was previously noted using the MINICHAL instrument in India and Thailand.[2628] This stands in contrast to findings from several large cohorts in Asia, which indicate a steady decline in QOL as the disease progresses.[29]

The strength of the study involves its relatively larger sample size. Furthermore, this research enhances the current literature by offering empirical data regarding the correlation between SQ, HRQOL, and hypertension. Employing standardized instruments, such as the PSQI and MINICHAL scales, enhanced data reliability and comparability with previous research.

Limitations and recommendations

The main limitation is purposive sampling within a single hospital setting. This approach restricts the generalizability of the findings to the broader hypertensive population. Furthermore, recruiting patients from medical wards introduces a risk of selection bias. Hospitalized patients often constitute a more ill or complex segment of the overall hypertensive population compared to those managed in outpatient care. A key limitation is that medication adherence is assessed solely through verbal self-reports, which could be influenced by memory lapses and the tendency to respond in a socially acceptable manner. Additionally, excluding patients with well-controlled hypertension limits the generalizability of our findings to the wider hypertensive community, especially those who manage their blood pressure effectively. Due to the cross-sectional design of our study, we could not establish causality or determine the direction of the observed relationships.

Apart from that, as an observational study, we have to recognize unmeasured confounding. Although we have made adjustments for major socio-clinical variables, the relationship between the duration of hypertension and HRQOL could be modulated by other factors that were not directly measured or controlled in the present study, such as chronic levels of stress, certain eating habits, or physical activity that was not quantified.

These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive hypertension management, which includes routinely assessing HRQOL and SQ. This can help identify those at risk for poor outcomes and tailor treatment accordingly. In hypertensive patients with insomnia, especially where sleep specialists are unavailable, practical interventions like routine SQ screening and basic sleep hygiene education by general staff are vital. Referral to primary care or broader networks could improve overall well-being.

Further research could enhance our understanding of the association between these variables by exploring the long-term effects of sleep interventions and identifying specific sociodemographic factors that influence BP control. Additionally, a longitudinal or interventional study could be conducted to examine changes in hormones, neurotransmitters, and brain activity while investigating the underlying mechanisms that link SQ, HRQOL, and hypertension.

Conclusion

Hypertension is a major modifiable risk factor for mortality, and its management is a crucial strategy for preventing CVDs. This study examined the SQI and HRQOL among hypertensive patients, depicting a weak positive correlation between the two variables. This finding indicates only a modest association, signifying a holistic view of patient care, but represents only one among several factors influencing HRQOL in this population. Lower SQI and HRQOL were linked to alcohol use, occupation, and a chronic history of hypertension.

These results strongly support the need for comprehensive hypertension management that meets major national and global health policy standards. The study result is directly consistent with the WHO and the United Nations’ SDG 3 (Target 3.4), which aims to reduce NCDs. Nationally, the impact of the Indian Hypertension Control Initiative and the ambitious “75/25” program underscores the policy need to expand clinical focus beyond BP numbers. Strengthening the healthcare system’s approach to hypertension requires continuous monitoring and adaptation, prioritizing holistic care that addresses sleep and QOL as essential components of NCD management.
