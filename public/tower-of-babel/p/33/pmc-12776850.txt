Evaluation of Chitosan (Axiostat®) as a Pulpotomy Agent in Primary Molars: An In Vivo Study
Authors: Nidhish Krishna, Kavita Rai, R Manju, Nikita Lolayekar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn primary teeth, pulpotomy is a well-established intervention for managing cariously exposed coronal pulp, with formocresol long considered the gold-standard medicament. Recent research focuses on wound-healing phenomena and the use of regenerative materials like chitosan owing to its functional versatility and unique characteristics.AimTo assess the clinical and radiographic outcomes of using chitosan as a pulpotomy agent in primary molars.Materials and methodsChildren aged 4-9 years, free from systemic illnesses and in need of pulpotomy, were selected. About 50 teeth were assigned to two groups: group I (chitosan) and group II (formocresol), and all treated teeth received stainless-steel crowns. Clinical and radiographic parameters were assessed at 1, 3, and 6 months posttreatment. The Chi-squared test was applied to compare the parameters and analyze the differences between the groups at the various time intervals.ResultsGroup I (chitosan) demonstrated a clinical and radiographic success rate of 95.6%, while group II (formocresol) achieved 100%. The variation between the groups was not statistically significant.ConclusionThe study indicated that chitosan (Axiostat®) can be considered a reliable pulpotomy agent in primary dentition and an effective substitute for formocresol.How to cite this articleKrishna N, Rai K, Manju R, et al. Introduction

Maintaining the primary teeth until their physiological exfoliation is vital for a child's orofacial development, as these teeth are important for mastication and speech. Teeth treated with pulpotomy aid in preserving these functions by retaining them that might otherwise be lost.1 The pulp is a specialized mesenchymal connective tissue situated within a confined space, containing a complex network of blood vessels and abundant cellular and neural components essential for the tooth's immune response and sensory functions.2

During the initial phase of pulp inflammation, there remain untapped treatment options, with ongoing research concentrating on the modulation of vascular responses in damaged dental pulp tissue. For reversible pulpitis, vital pulp therapy is the treatment of choice in both primary and permanent teeth to maintain pulp vitality and function. The main objective is to maintain the vitality of the radicular pulp free from any pathological alterations till its exfoliation.2,3 The treating clinician should be aware of the sequelae of any treatment rendered to primary teeth. The ideal pulpotomy medicament ought to preserve the radicular pulp's vitality and health, confining it within a dentin chamber lined by odontoblasts, and be compatible with adjacent tissues while ensuring clinical and radiological success.4

Formocresol has been commonly utilized as a pulpotomy agent due to its straightforward application, low technique sensitivity, and high success rates.5 Nevertheless, the cytotoxic effects of formocresol on pulp tissue have been extensively studied, raising concerns about its systemic absorption, toxicity, and possible carcinogenic effects in humans.6 Primary molar pulpotomy has been examined using a variety of medications and methods, but a perfect pulpotomy agent is yet to exist. Research in recent years has highlighted the application of regenerative agents—including collagen, lyophilized freeze-dried platelets, and chitosan—as pulpotomy materials designed to enhance wound repair and reestablish the structure and function of affected radicular pulp tissue.7 Chitosan is a versatile polysaccharide biomaterial that is purified and deacetylated from chitin, which is sourced from the shells of crustaceans like crabs and shrimp. Due to its biocompatibility, biodegradability, ability to adhere to mucosal surfaces, and its antimicrobial and antifungal effects on living tissues, chitosan has been extensively researched for use in dental, biomedical, surgical, and tissue engineering fields.8

Chitosan has anti-inflammatory properties and supports tissue regeneration, though its role as a pulpotomy agent in primary teeth has been explored in only a few studies.9 Research on Axiostat® (Axio Biosolutions, India) highlights chitosan's hemostatic effect by serving as a scaffold for clot formation, which also ensures a secure seal at the wound site. This action stems from its positively charged amino groups, which interact with negatively charged blood cells to promote clotting and establish an antibacterial barrier. Chitosan also encourages reparative dentin and hard tissue formation, making it a suitable medicament for pulpotomy use.10

Therefore, this in vivo study was designed to clinically and radiographically evaluate the success of Axiostat® (chitosan) (Axio Biosolutions, India) as a pulpotomy medicament.

Materials and Methods

The present randomized controlled clinical trial received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (ABSM/EC/255/20212) and was registered with the Clinical Trials Registry—India (CTRI/2023/01/048898). This study was conducted in adherence with the ethical guidelines of the committee on human experimentation and the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as amended in 2000. Children of both genders between 4 and 9 years who reported to our institution with primary molars that required pulpotomy were included in this study. The procedure, along with potential discomfort, risks, and benefits, was clearly explained to the parents, and informed consent and assent were obtained before proceeding.

Teeth Selection

Among 46 children, 50 primary first and second molars (maxillary and mandibular) were included, and the sample was estimated based on the formula given, with 25 teeth in each group.

The criteria for inclusion were: (1) children of both genders aged 4-9 years, (2) deep carious lesions in vital primary molars, (3) absence of spontaneous pain, (4) bleeding from the amputated pulp site controlled within 5 minutes, (5) retention of more than two-thirds of the root length, and (6) no evidence of internal or external root resorption. Exclusion criteria included: (1) uncooperative patients, (2) inability to achieve hemostasis within 5 minutes of applying pressure with a damp cotton pellet (uncontrolled bleeding), (3) spontaneous, unprovoked pain, (4) presence of furcal radiolucency, (5) presence of sinus tract or fistula or tooth mobility, (6) history of allergies to seafood or cresol products, and (7) medically compromised children or those requiring special healthcare.

Pulpotomy Procedure

The pulpotomy technique was then performed by a single operator (NK). Required anesthetic procedures (inferior alveolar nerve block for mandibular teeth, buccal infiltration for maxillary teeth) were followed, and isolation was achieved using a rubber dam. After complete caries removal, coronal access was obtained with a high-speed bur with water spray to expose the pulp chamber, and coronal pulp was amputated with a sterile spoon excavator. The color of the bleeding was observed with the naked eye only, as magnification was not feasible during the study, and hemostasis was then obtained by placing a moistened cotton pellet for 5 minutes.9

Following the standard procedure, group I had chitosan sponge (Axiostat®, Axio Biosolutions, India) placed directly on the pulp stumps for 5 minutes before being removed. In group II, a cotton pellet soaked in a 1/5 dilution of Buckley's formocresol (Formocresol®, Pharmadent), then squeezed to remove the excess, was applied to the amputated pulp stumps for 5 minutes and subsequently removed. In both groups, the pulp chamber was filled with a thick layer of zinc oxide eugenol cement and then restored using glass ionomer cement. Finally, all pulpotomized teeth were restored with preformed stainless-steel crowns (3M ESPE) as the definitive restoration (Figs 1 and 2). Routine oral hygiene and postoperative instructions were given to the children and their parents. They were requested to report to the department if there was any pain, discomfort, swelling, or pus discharge, and all potential consequences were explained to them. The children were called back for clinical and radiographic evaluations at 1, 3, and 6 months following the procedure. During recall, clinical parameters evaluated included presence of pain, tenderness on palpation and percussion, swelling, sinus opening, mobility, and color of the tooth. Radiographic parameters evaluated included internal root resorption, external root resorption, periapical radiolucency, interradicular bone loss, and pulp canal obliteration. The treatment was considered clinically and radiographically successful if none of the abovementioned parameters were present.

Statistical Analysis

The data were organized using MS Excel (version 2019, Microsoft) and analyzed with the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS version 26.0, IBM). The mean age between the two groups was compared using a t -test. Clinical and radiographic parameters at various time intervals were compared between groups using the Chi-squared test. A p -value of <0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

The teeth selected for pulpotomy were distributed as follows: group I (chitosan) included four maxillary first molars, 11 mandibular first molars, five maxillary second molars, and five mandibular second molars. Group II (formocresol) consisted of five maxillary first molars, 10 mandibular first molars, four maxillary second molars, and six mandibular second molars. There were no statistically significant differences in age and gender distribution between the groups in relation to the incidence of pulpal inflammation, with an average age of 6.28 years (range 4-9 years).

Of the 50 primary teeth initially included in the study, 47 were monitored over a 6-month period, as three individuals did not return for their follow-up appointments. Throughout this duration, both clinical and radiographic findings were documented at three separate intervals.

At the 1-month follow-up, clinical assessment showed one participant in group I reported pain and tenderness on percussion, and so the tooth underwent pulpectomy and was rerestored. No other significant changes were observed in both groups. No statistically significant differences were observed between the groups for the evaluated parameters (p > 0.05).

At the 3-month follow-up, radiographic examination revealed internal resorption in one tooth from group I; however, the tooth remained asymptomatic and was monitored. Other than this case, no other significant changes were observed in both groups. At the 6-month follow-up, clinical and radiographic evaluations indicated no significant differences or findings between the groups, with the statistical analysis confirming nonsignificance for the parameters assessed (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Discussion

Pulpotomy is a commonly used method for managing cariously exposed coronal pulp in primary teeth. The success of vital pulp therapy relies on multiple factors, including a proper coronal seal, effective disinfection of the exposed site, the degree of inflammation, achieving hemostasis, the antibacterial properties and biocompatibility of the pulpotomy agents, and the isolation technique used throughout the procedure.11,12 The rationale behind pulpotomy is justified by the ability of radicular pulp tissue to heal following surgical amputation of damaged or infected coronal pulp. The pulpotomy procedure has been the topic of debate for several years, and it has evolved over time with new materials being introduced because of technological and scientific advancements.13

Formocresol had reported higher clinical success rates over a period of time, but on the contrary, histological evaluation shows varied results with necrosis and evident fixation of portions of the pulp tissue.6 The use of formocresol has sparked controversy over time because of its possible cytotoxic effects, leading to ongoing efforts to identify alternative agents and methods for pulpotomy treatment.2,9

Recently, the use of biomaterials has gained considerable attention, leading to notable progress in the development and application of safe and effective materials such as mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) and Biodentine. Given their well-documented effectiveness, these materials were not included in our study, and formocresol was chosen because of its extensive history and controversy regarding biocompatibility and safety, which has been a point of debate in pediatric dentistry. The study aimed to provide further insight into alternative materials to formocresol rather than comparing already established materials.14

Chitosan, a natural polysaccharide known for its biodegradability and biocompatibility, has diverse applications in dentistry because of its versatile functions, easy availability, antimicrobial qualities, and structural properties.8,15 Considering these benefits, chitosan was selected as the pulpotomy agent for primary molars in this study. The aim was to evaluate and compare the effectiveness of chitosan against formocresol as a pulpotomy medicament. The study evaluated, clinically and radiographically, 47 pulpotomized primary molars at 1-, 3-, and 6-month intervals. Commercially available Axiostat® (Axio Biosolutions, India) chitosan was used as the agent for pulpotomy, which was available in sponge form, less technique-sensitive, and easy to use.16 Following the procedure, a stainless-steel crown was placed to restore the tooth's structure and function while ensuring a biological seal to protect the remaining healthy pulp tissue from bacterial contamination.17

The primary objective was to clinically evaluate both the groups, and it was observed that at 1-month follow-up, clinical assessment of one tooth in group I (chitosan) reported pain and tenderness on percussion. It was then recommended for pulpectomy. At 3- and 6-month follow-ups, the clinical assessment of all the parameters showed no significant changes and findings in both the groups. The secondary objective of the study was to evaluate the groups radiographically. At the 3-month follow-up, radiographic analysis identified internal resorption in one tooth (distal root of tooth 74) from group I (chitosan); however, the patient was asymptomatic (Fig. 3). Radiographic evaluation at 1 and 6 months showed no significant alterations or findings in either group.

Failure—internal resorption

The final objective was to compare clinical and radiographic outcomes between the two groups at 1, 3, and 6 months. At the 6-month mark, the overall clinical and radiographic success rates were 95.6% for group I and 100% for group II. Although some clinical and radiographic failures occurred, no statistically significant differences were observed in overall success between the groups at any of the assessed intervals.

The findings of this study align with previous 6-month follow-up research, which reported success rates of 96.6 and 94.44% for the chitosan group, respectively.9,18 Markovic documented a clinical success rate of 90.9% and a radiographic success rate of 84.8% for formocresol pulpotomy.1 However, the current study observed a 100% success rate for formocresol pulpotomy, with no significant clinical or radiographic changes detected.

In the current study, the number of mandibular molars treated for pulpotomy with inflamed coronal pulp was significant; this may be attributed to transverse communications between the root canals. Hibbaed also noted that deciduous mandibular molars may contain lateral branches, connecting fibrils, and apical ramifications. These findings substantiate that primary mandibular first molars affected by dental caries are more vulnerable to pulpal therapy due to their extensive vascular anastomosis.19 In the present study, the placement and removal of Axiostat® was easy when compared to formocresol cotton pellet, as the cotton sherds can be cumbersome to use in the access chamber and interfere with hemostasis. The excellent clinical and radiographic outcomes of the pulpotomy procedure conducted using chitosan can be explained by its antibacterial characteristics, biocompatibility, regenerative, and osteogenic potential.8,15

The failures observed in this study may be attributed to several factors, including possible clinical errors in diagnosis and case selection—for instance, chronic inflammation of the radicular pulp might have been mistaken for a healthy pulp based on bleeding color. Additionally, the use of zinc oxide eugenol as a sub-base could irritate pulp cells.15 Internal resorption observed can be attributed to the reparative processes influenced by inflammation, acting here as a double-edged sword.20

The amalgamation of various properties makes chitosan a potential agent for pulpotomy and a breakthrough in the field of pediatric endodontics. Nevertheless, this study has some shortcomings. The main limitations of this study are the brief follow-up duration, absence of histological analysis, and limited sample size. Therefore, extended clinical trials with histological evaluations and larger participant groups are necessary to fully determine the effectiveness of chitosan as a pulpotomy agent in primary teeth.

Conclusion

Within the constraints of this study, it can be concluded that chitosan (Axiostat®) is a viable medicament option for pulpotomy in primary teeth and serves as an effective alternative to formocresol, which has known limitations and health concerns. No significant difference was found between the clinical and radiographic effectiveness of the two agents. Nevertheless, further randomized clinical trials with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up durations, and histological investigations are unquestionably required.
