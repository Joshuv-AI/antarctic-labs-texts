Dysregulation of Glial Gap Junction Protein Innexin2 Mediates Seizures and Disrupted Sleep in a Drosophila Model for North Sea Progressive Myoclonus Epilepsy
Authors: Nicola A. Grzeschik, Roald A. Lambrechts, Marianne van der Zwaag, Ellie Eggens‐Meijer, Ody C. M. Sibon, Jenke A. Gorter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNorth Sea Progressive Myoclonus Epilepsy (NS‐PME) is a rare genetic disease that presents at an early age with progressive ataxia, myoclonus, and epilepsy. The disease is caused by a mutation in the Golgi SNAP receptor 2 gene (GOSR2). However, the consequences of this genetic defect at the cellular level are still unknown. Using a NS‐PME Drosophila melanogaster model, it was previously found that knockdown of membrin, the fruit fly GOSR2 ortholog, in glial cells leads to progressive heat‐induced seizure‐like behavior in adult flies. Upon close inspection, we found NS‐PME flies not only show progressive heat‐induced seizures but also disrupted sleep architecture (higher number of shorter sleep bouts). Here we asked what glial cellular consequences could be underlying these phenotypes of membrin knockdown. First, we investigate whether these phenotypes are attributed to specific glial types; we found that these phenotypes were partly reproduced with specific knockdown of membrin in perineurial glia only. Next, we show that Innexin2, a gap junction protein specifically expressed in glia, is affected upon membrin knockdown, with an altered expression pattern and reduced protein levels. Finally, glial overexpression of Innexin2 in the background of glial membrin knockdown strongly ameliorates the heat‐induced seizure phenotype, disrupted sleep architecture, as well as Innexin2 expression pattern, corroborating the importance of Innexin2 in maintaining neuronal homeostasis in NS‐PME. These findings show that dysregulation of Innexin2 at least partially underlies the phenotypes of membrin knockdown in Drosophila melanogaster and could suggest a similar role of Innexin2‐like proteins in the human disease NS‐PME.

Introduction

North Sea Progressive Myoclonus Epilepsy (NS‐PME) is a rare childhood‐onset neurological disease featuring ataxia, myoclonus, and epilepsy. NS‐PME is caused by mutations in the GOSR2 gene, involved in ER‐Golgi vesicular transport (Boissé Lomax et al. 2013; Corbett et al. 2011; Linders et al. 2019). Mutations in GOSR2 associated with NS‐PME are shown to decrease the function both in silico (Völker et al. 2017) and in liposomes (Praschberger et al. 2017), without influencing its localization (Praschberger et al. 2017). However, what the downstream consequences of GOSR2 mutations in vivo are remains unknown.

An early Drosophila melanogaster model suggested that neuronal development might be affected by overexpression of disease‐associated mutant Membrin, the Drosophila GOSR2 ortholog, in a membrin null background in early developmental stages, resulting in phenotypes reminiscent of NS‐PME (Praschberger et al. 2017). A second Drosophila model, in line with the mutations causing a partial loss of Membrin function (Praschberger et al. 2017), showed that RNAi‐mediated knockdown of membrin exclusively in glia, but not exclusively in neurons, caused heat‐induced seizures in adult flies (Lambrechts et al. 2019). This latter model suggests a role for Membrin in glia in controlling neuronal excitability. However, the factors mediating this glial involvement have not yet been characterized. Identification of these factors in a Drosophila model could outline pathways relevant to human epilepsy, considering their connection to GOSR2/Membrin.

In addition to epilepsy, the relevance of glia–neuron interactions is well established for normal sleep architecture, especially in Drosophila (Bittern et al. 2021; Shafer and Keene 2021). Therefore, if Membrin exerts its effects on neurons indirectly via glia, sleep may be another complex neurological parameter to study in order to better understand the function of Membrin. Sleep phenotypes have been attributed to different glial subtypes and different glial factors have been reported to regulate sleep need, sleep signaling, and the timing of sleep and wakefulness (Artiushin et al. 2018; Blum et al. 2021; Cuddapah et al. 2019; Haynes et al. 2024; Li, Artiushin, and Sehgal 2023; Li, Aboudhiaf, et al. 2023; Li, Haynes, et al. 2023). Here, we aim to investigate whether glial membrin knockdown in the NS‐PME model also results in sleep disturbances associated with glia–neuron interactions.

Glial influence on neuronal functioning is often mediated by ions and neurotransmitters. These molecules are shuttled between cells via gap junctions: channels formed between two adjacent cells. Whereas in humans gap junctions are formed by connexins, in Drosophila these consist of the functionally and structurally analogous innexin proteins (Beyer and Berthoud 2018). Of all innexin proteins known in Drosophila, Innexin2 (Inx2) is specifically expressed in perineurial, subperineurial, and ensheathing glia within the nervous system (Ammer et al. 2022). Inx2 has been demonstrated to be of crucial importance for normal CNS development and blood–brain barrier function (Holcroft et al. 2013; Spéder and Brand 2014; Zhang et al. 2018). Additionally, Inx2 was shown to be crucial in perineurial glia to prevent heat‐induced seizures (Weiss et al. 2022), and glial Inx2 was implicated in total night sleep regulation (Farca Luna et al. 2017). We hypothesize that Inx2 could be involved in the glia–neuron interaction underlying the pathological effects of Membrin dysfunction in NS‐PME.

Here, we strongly connect impaired function of gap junction protein Inx2 to the detrimental downstream consequences of membrin knockdown, including heat‐induced seizures and disrupted sleep. The glial population involved in seizure susceptibility of membrin knockdown could be specified to the perineurial, subperineurial, and ensheathing glia, overlapping with the known expression pattern of Inx2. Additionally, Inx2 expression in the brain is affected upon membrin knockdown, and Inx2 overexpression partially rescues glial membrin knockdown phenotypes. This suggests an important role for glial gap junctions in the glial cellular pathways relevant to human epilepsy, particularly in NS‐PME.

Material and Methods

Drosophila Maintenance Crosses and Aging

Drosophila melanogaster stocks were maintained, and crosses were performed on the standard cornmeal food used at the Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center (BDSC; Nutri‐Fly Bloomington Formulation (66–113, Genesee Scientific) with propionic acid and nipagin) at 25°C with a 12–12 light–dark cycle. Most stocks used in this study were obtained from the BDSC and the Vienna Drosophila Resource Center (VDRC): Repo‐Gal4 (BDSC #7415, pan‐glial driver, here referred to as “Repo”), GMR85G01‐Gal4 (BDSC #40436, perineurial glia driver, here referred to as “PG‐Gal4”) (Jenett et al. 2012), GMR54C07‐Gal4 (BDSC #50472, subperineurial glia driver, here referred to as “SPG‐Gal4”) (Jenett et al. 2012), GMR56F03‐Gal4 (BDSC #39157, neuropile ensheathing glia driver, here referred to as “EGN‐Gal4”) (Jenett et al. 2012), GMR54H02‐Gal4 (BDSC #45784, cortex glia driver, here referred to as “CG‐Gal4”) (Jenett et al. 2012), Engrailed‐Gal4‐UAS‐GFP (based on BDSC #25752, but does no longer carry the UAS‐Dcr; wing disc driver, posterior compartment, here referred to as “En‐Gal4‐UAS‐GFP”), UAS‐membrin‐RNAi (VDCR #44534, here referred to as “membrin34”) (Dietzl et al. 2007), UAS‐membrin‐RNAi (VDCR #44535, here referred to as “membrin35,” no longer available from the VDRC) (Dietzl et al. 2007), UAS‐Innexin2‐RNAi (VDCR #102194, here referred to as “Inx2‐RNAi”) (Dietzl et al. 2007), and UAS‐Innexin2‐RR‐RFP (BDSC #90966, here referred to as “Inx2‐RR‐RFP”) (Miao et al. 2020). Alrm‐Gal4 (astrocyte‐like glia driver, here referred to as “ALG‐Gal4”) was a present from the Freeman Lab (Doherty et al. 2009), and UAS‐GOSR2‐WT (here referred to as “GOSR2‐WT”) was a present from the Jepson lab (Praschberger et al. 2017). As controls, two different GFP lines were used: A UAS‐eGFP located on chromosome II (Bason et al. 2019) was used in all instances where UAS‐eGFP was crossed to a driver line. UAS‐CD8‐GFP (BDSC #5130), located on chromosome III, was used to create recombinants between the third chromosomal drivers GMR85G01 (PNG‐Gal4) or Repo‐Gal4 and GFP. A complete list of commercially available fly lines, lines created in the context of this publication, and detailed genotypes can be found in the Supplementary File.

The UAS/Gal4 system (Brand and Perrimon 1993) was used to overexpress or downregulate genes of interest in all glia or glial subtypes using the drivers indicated in figures and legends. For the “original” North Sea Progressive Myoclonus Epilepsy model, Gal4 driver flies were crossed with flies carrying UAS‐membrin‐RNAi (line #44534 and/or #44535), as described previously (Lambrechts et al. 2019). For the seizure assay, each driver was tested with both UAS‐membrin‐RNAi lines. For subsequent analyses, for each driver the UAS‐membrin‐RNAi line with the most optimal combination of health and seizure susceptibility was selected (Repo‐Gal4 with UAS‐membrin‐RNAi #34 and PNG‐Gal4 with UAS‐membrin‐RNAi #35). For rescue experiments, a driver expresses both the UAS‐membrin‐RNAi as well as the UAS‐GOSR2‐WT previously reported by Praschberger et al. (2017), or the UAS‐Inx2‐RR‐RFP (Miao et al. 2020) rescue construct. To perform these experiments, recombinants were created between the Gal4 driver and UAS‐membrin‐RNAi, which were then crossed to the rescue construct. For these recombinants, UAS‐membrin‐RNAi line #35 was used because this fly line produced the strongest seizure incidence for all relevant drivers used. As controls, recombinants were created between the Gal4 driver and UAS‐GFP.

For all experiments (seizure assay, activity and sleep assay, sample preparation for Western blot, immunohistochemistry, or electron microscopy), F1 flies from the crosses were collected on CO2 on the day of eclosion (day 1). They were separated by sex and aged on Bloomington food at 25°C with a 12–12 light–dark cycle until day 8 for most crosses, or day 5 for crosses with recombinant strains Repo‐Gal4 and UAS‐membrin‐RNAi #35 due to their shorter lifespan (age is specified in the figure legends).

Heat‐Induced Seizure Assay

The heat‐induced seizure assay was performed as reported previously (Lambrechts et al. 2019; Sun et al. 2012). Except, on the day of the experiment, flies were not anesthetized with CO2. After aging, male and female flies were transferred to separate vials in groups of five to ten flies by use of a mouth‐pipet and left to acclimatize for at least 30 min. Genotypes and conditions were blinded at this stage. For the heat‐induced seizure assay, the vials were immersed in a water bath of 40°C for a total of either 120 s, or an extended 240 s to allow the investigation of more subtle changes. All experiments were conducted in the presence of age and genotype matched controls to control for confounding factors. Seizure incidence was defined as twitches of the legs, wing flapping, and eventual loss of standing position (scored as paralysis). Flies were only considered to have a seizure when leg twitching or wing flapping was observed, and full paralysis occurred. The start time of each paralysis was scored in 5 s intervals. Total seizure incidence per group was plotted as the cumulative percentage of total flies paralyzed at each 5 s interval for the duration of the heat‐induced seizure assay.

Activity and Sleep Analysis

Activity and sleep were determined by use of the Drosophila Activity Monitor (DAM) system (DAM2, Trikinetics, Waltham, United States of America). The DAM system is a validated tool in both activity and sleep research and was previously described (Dubowy and Sehgal 2017; Sitaraman et al. 2024; Vogt 2018). To test the activity and sleep, male and female flies were transferred on the day of eclosion to small glass tubes (5 mm x 65 mm), containing simple food medium (26.3 g/L yeast, 54.4 g/L sugar, 17 g/L agar, 1 mL/L of 10% nipagin). Experimental genotypes and control genotypes were tested in parallel to control for environmental factors. The small tubes containing flies were placed in the monitors of the DAM system and were kept there for the duration of 8 full days. Data collection of the DAM system was set to bins of 1 min. Activity and sleep were analyzed for the complete day 8 or day 5, depending on the genotype. Flies were presumed dead and excluded from activity and sleep analysis if the activity count was less than 100 counts per day (and visually checked at the end of the experiment). For the activity pattern, the activity data were binned per fly per hour and plotted as average for all flies of a genotype or condition. Sleep was analyzed using Shiny‐R DAM (Cichewicz and Hirsh 2018). Sleep was defined as a continuous period of inactivity for at least 5 min, an interval previously correlated to sleep as altered body position and increased arousal threshold (Shaw et al. 2000) as well as changed electrical activity patterns of the brain (Nitz et al. 2002). After analysis using Shiny‐R DAM, the individual sleep and activity bout data were downloaded and the total sleep time, number of sleep bouts, and bout length per fly were determined and plotted as average for all flies of a genotype or condition.

Western Blot

For Western blot, male and female flies were sedated and decapitated on ice using forceps and a surgical knife. For each experiment, the same number of heads was collected for each sample (between 12 and 15 heads). Heads were homogenized by sonication in RIPA buffer and centrifuged at 20,000 rcf for 5 min in a cold centrifuge. The homogenates were heated at 99°C for 5 min with 1× Laemmli sample buffer and 5% β‐mercaptoethanol (Sigma‐Aldrich). Equal amounts of sample were loaded on 10% mini protean TGX stain‐free gels (Bio‐Rad) and transferred onto PVDF membranes using the Trans Blot Turbo System (Bio‐Rad). The membranes were blocked with 5% fat‐free milk in PBS + 0.1% Tween for 1 h at room temperature, followed by primary antibody incubation at 4°C overnight. The antibodies used were rabbit anti‐Innexin2 (1:1000, gift from G. Ammer (Ammer et al. 2022)) and mouse anti‐Tubulin (1:5000, Sigma‐Aldrich, T5168). After a wash in PBS + 0.1% Tween, membranes were further incubated in 5% fat‐free milk in PBS + 0.1% Tween and appropriate horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies 1:5,000 (Cytiva), for 1½ hrs at RT. Finally, membranes were developed using Clarity Western ECL substrate (Bio‐Rad). The signal was visualized using the ChemiDoc imager (Bio‐Rad), images were exported, and densitometric analysis of the band was performed using Image Studio Lite (Li‐Cor).

Immunohistochemistry

For immunostainings of adult fly brains, female flies were sedated on ice and their brains were dissected in cold PBS. The brains were fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 20 min at RT, followed by three washes of 20 min each in PBT (1× PBS + 0.1% Triton‐X‐100) and incubation in primary antibody, Rabbit anti‐Inx2 (1:1000, gift from G. Ammer (Ammer et al. 2022)), for 48 h at 4 degrees. After a short wash (PBT), they were incubated with secondary antibody (Rb‐Alexa‐488) for 2 h, shortly washed again (PBT), and mounted in 80% glycerol.

For immunostainings of third instar larval wing discs, flies were mated and their offspring raised at 25°C on Bloomington Food. Wandering third instar larvae were collected after ~5 days, wing discs were dissected in ice‐cold PBS and fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 45 min at RT. The rest of the staining was performed as described above for the adult brains, with Rb‐Alexa‐633 being used as a secondary antibody. No antibody staining was performed for GFP or RFP, respectively, and “stainings” shown in Figures S4 and S5 are endogenous expression only. All samples were subsequently visualized/analyzed with a Zeiss‐LSM780 NLO confocal microscope using Zeiss Zen software. The images were processed using Photoshop (Adobe).

EM Processing of Adult Fly Brains

For electron microscopy, male flies of the annotated genotype were sedated and decapitated on ice, and the proboscis was removed. The remaining heads were fixed overnight on a rotator at 4°C in freshly prepared fixative containing 2% glutaraldehyde, 0.2% picric acid, and 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4). After three short washes with 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer, samples were post‐fixed using 1% osmium tetroxide and 1.5% potassium ferrocyanide in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer for 2 h at 4°C. Then, samples were washed with milliQ water at RT followed by dehydration in an ethanol series. Finally, the samples were embedded in epoxy‐resin (epon). Semi‐thin sections (500 nm) were stained with Toluidine Blue dye. Subsequent ultrathin sections (80 nm) were cut and placed on formvar‐coated 1 mm single‐slot copper grids. Grids were contrasted with 4% Neodymium (III) acetate for 40 min and subsequently washed on drops of milliQ water and dried, as described by (Kuipers and Giepmans 2020). Images were acquired on a Supra 55 scanning EM (SEM; Zeiss) using a scanning transmission EM (STEM). High‐resolution large‐scale STEM images were generated with the external scan generator ATLAS (Fibics). Individual tiles of the dorsal edge of the adult brain (including perineurial glia, subperineurial glia, and part of the cortex glia layers) were stitched together, and data were exported as an HTML file and converted to CZI files. Areas of interest were selected further and exported as BIG‐TIF images using Zeiss Zen software for further image analysis and visualization in Adobe Photoshop.

Statistical Analysis

Data were visualized and analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 8. Seizure assays were plotted in a cumulative manner as a survival function. Statistical significance was performed using the Mantel–Cox log‐rank test for all graphs comparing seizure incidence between different genotypes. To analyze the Western blot and sleep parameters, a two tailed unpaired Student's t‐test was used for analysis when data was normally distributed, determined by the Shapiro–Wilk test; otherwise, Mann–Whitney was used. Data are depicted as mean ± SEM. p‐values for all graphs: (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.0001, ****p < 0.0001) and statistical details are reported in the figure legends. Behavioral experiments were conducted at least twice with a sample size of at least 30 flies in total. Seizing lines were validated with an increased sample size. Behavioral data was pooled and plotted as the total number of flies tested. Western Blot was performed in triplicate. For Immunohistochemistry and Electron Microscopy, at least three samples for each condition were compared and representative pictures are presented. The number of samples included in each graph and the number of flies used for the seizure and sleep assays is indicated in the figure legends/behind the genotype in the graphs.

Results

Glial Membrin Knockdown Causes Heat‐Induced Seizures and Disrupted Sleep

Previously, it was shown in Drosophila that heat induces seizure sensitivity upon glial knockdown of membrin (Lambrechts et al. 2019). As in the previous model, here membrin was knocked down in all glial subtypes with a pan‐glial driver Repo‐Gal4 and UAS‐Membrin‐RNAi (#34) (see Figure 1A for an overview of all glial subtypes and Figure S1A for the expression pattern of Repo‐Gal4). Next, we expanded the model by using an additional RNAi line (#35), reported previously by (Praschberger et al. 2017), and investigated a rescue of this model with overexpression of wild‐type human GOSR2. We observed a cumulative percentage of flies seizing, plotted as paralysis in all graphs, between 81% and 98% within 2 min of exposure to heat for both RNAi lines in both males and females (Figure 1B,B′). Combining glial membrin knockdown with concomitant glial overexpression of wild‐type human GOSR2 fully rescued this seizure phenotype (Figure 1C,C′). This indicates that Membrin and human GOSR2 are structural and functional orthologs and underscores the suitability of the Drosophila model to investigate the function of GOSR2 in the human disease NS‐PME.

To identify possible changes in sleep architecture in the NS‐PME model, we characterized the activity and sleep patterns by use of the Drosophila Activity Monitor (DAM2) system (Figure 1D). Interestingly, upon glial knockdown of membrin, general activity is decreased (Figure 1E,E′ left panels). Upon closer inspection, we noticed abnormalities in activity patterning related to dark–light cycles, with decreased peak activity during dawn and dusk and persistent activity during night and midday siesta, especially in males (Figure 1E,E′ right panels). Next, we performed sleep analysis, determined as any bout of inactivity lasting 5 min or more. Sleep analysis demonstrated that upon glial knockdown of membrin, flies have more sleep bouts of shorter duration, with only a slight increase in total sleep for females (Figure 1F,F′). This suggests a dysregulation of normal sleep architecture upon glial loss of membrin, resulting in disrupted sleep.

To summarize, glial knockdown of membrin in Drosophila causes not only a sensitivity to heat‐induced seizures but also a dysregulation of normal sleep and activity patterns.

Perineurial Glial Membrin Knockdown Partially Recapitulates Behavioral Phenotypes

To determine whether a specific subset of glia with glial impairment is underlying the neurological dysfunction upon membrin knockdown, we proceeded to characterize the subpopulation of glia in which Membrin is required to prevent abnormalities. For this characterization, we examined sensitivity to heat‐induced seizures upon knockdown of membrin in specific glial subsets using different Gal4 drivers (previously characterized by (Kremer et al. 2017), see Figure S1A–F for schematics of single Gal4 expression patterns) during a 4 min heat‐shock. Interestingly, from all glial drivers, only selective knockdown of membrin in perineurial glia (PNG) led to marked seizure sensitivity at 4 min of exposure to heat, albeit to a lower extent than in pan‐glial membrin knockdown flies (Figure 2A,A′). Knockdown in all other glial subtypes alone was insufficient to produce seizure sensitivity (Figure 2A,A′, Figure S2A–D′), similar to the GFP controls. We observed a cumulative seizure incidence for membrin knockdown in PNG of 14% to 55% in females and 18% to 57% in males, dependent on the RNAi line (Figure 2B,B′). The seizure sensitivity upon membrin knockdown in perineurial glia was rescued by concomitant overexpression of wild‐type human GOSR2 (Figure 2C,C′).

Given the incomplete recapitulation of seizure sensitivity observed with pan‐glial membrin knockdown by perineurial glia alone, we examined whether other glial subtypes contributed to seizure sensitivity in the context of membrin knockdown in perineurial glia (see Figure S1G–J for schematics of double Gal4 expression patterns). We found a considerable additive effect on seizure sensitivity with membrin knockdown with both subperineurial (SPG) and ensheathing glia (EGN) (Figure 2D–F′), but no increase in seizure incidence compared to perineurial glia alone when either cortex (CG) or astrocyte‐like glia (ALG) was combined (Figure 2D,D′ and Figure S2E–F′). These findings suggest a synergistic relation between ensheathing/subperineurial and perineurial glia in the Membrin‐dependent prevention of seizures.

Upon finding the requirement of Membrin in perineurial glia to prevent heat‐induced seizures, we wondered whether this subset of glia also conferred the sleep phenotype observed upon pan‐glial membrin knockdown. Analogous to the seizure phenotype, we found that knockdown of membrin in perineurial glia was sufficient to induce disrupted sleep (more sleep bouts with a shorter duration), but no alterations in the activity levels or patterns were observed (Figure S3). Together, these findings underscore the importance of Membrin in perineurial glia for maintenance of neuronal excitability and normal sleep architecture.

Innexin2 (Inx2) Knockdown Mimics the Behavioral Phenotypes of Glial Membrin Knockdown

To test whether impaired function of Innexin2 (Inx2) could explain neurological dysfunction by glial membrin knockdown, we investigated whether RNAi knockdown of Inx2 could phenocopy the heat‐induced seizures detected upon membrin knockdown. First, we validated the functionality of the UAS‐Inx2‐RNAi construct with Inx2 antibody staining in third instar larval wings (Figure S4). After this validation of the construct, we found that knockdown of Inx2 with RNAi in perineurial glia alone causes heat‐induced seizures in females (Figure 3A). RNAi‐mediated Inx2 knockdown was larval lethal with the pan‐glial driver (Repo‐Gal4) and male pharate lethal with the perineurial driver (data not shown). We proceeded to ask whether, analogous to the findings of membrin knockdown, Inx2 knockdown in perineurial glia also interfered with the activity pattern and sleep architecture. Indeed, knockdown of Inx2 in PNG resulted in a dysregulation in sleep with increased total sleep time and a tendency toward disrupted sleep (an increased number of shorter sleep bouts) (Figure 3B,C). These findings reveal a previously unknown function of Inx2, consistent with the hypothesis that Membrin and Inx2 act in the same pathway.

We found that Inx2 protein levels were lowered upon pan‐glial membrin knockdown, but not significantly affected with perineurial‐specific membrin knockdown (Figure 4A,A′).

Given the known function of Membrin/GOSR2 in intracellular protein transport, we wondered whether the spatial distribution of endogenous Inx2 throughout the Drosophila brain was also affected upon membrin knockdown. Using immunofluorescence, we found a global endogenous expression of Inx2 in control brains in a fine, reticular, lattice‐like pattern and concentrated around compartmental boundaries (frontal view of the central brain) (Figure 4B,B′). Under conditions of glial membrin knockdown, Inx2 staining coarsely clustered in larger and sometimes almost globular structures (Figure 4C,C′). This observation was further illustrated in line scan histograms of the brain surface Inx2 staining (Figure 4B′′,C′′). In the line scan histograms, controls showed a basal intensity (arbitrarily defined as gray value intensity of ~50–75) with several peaks in intensity up to 250 gray value. Under conditions of glial membrin knockdown, Inx2 staining showed a reduction in this basal intensity (gray value intensity of ~50–75), reflecting the loss of fine‐grained patterning, and a persistence of peak intensity, reflecting the clustered patterning (Figure 4B′′,C′′). In addition, in cross sections of the brain, the cortical lining of the Drosophila brain, associated with the Drosophila blood–brain barrier and composed of perineurial, subperineurial glia and possible cortex glia (Kremer et al. 2017), showed an almost continuous tightly adhered double layer of Inx2 staining surrounding the brain in controls and a more scattered and disconnected pattern under conditions of membrin knockdown (Figure 4B′′,C′′). These findings, in combination with the lowered Inx2 protein levels upon membrin knockdown, suggest that Inx2 is affected in response to impaired function of membrin.

Glial Membrin Knockdown Results in Structural Alterations in the Brain

Upon observing the morphological alteration of the glial cells in the cortical lining with glial membrin knockdown, we proceeded to investigate the ultrastructural underpinnings of this alteration. Using electron microscopy, we saw in control brains that the cortical lining (scanned area illustrated in Figure 5A) can be subdivided into three surface layers: perineurial glia (Figure 5B′ green) with the neural lamella (Figure 5B′ yellow) as the outer layer, the subperineurial (Figure 5B′ turquoise) directly underneath with protrusion into the upper layer, and the cortex glia (Figure 5B′ dark blue) surrounding the neuronal cell bodies (Figure 5B′ light blue). Strikingly, in brains with glial membrin knockdown, the glial surface layers, including perineurial, subperineurial, and cortex glia, showed a disrupted pattern (Figure 5C,C′). The perineurial layer was wider than in control brains, the subperineurial and cortex glia were disconnected, and the protrusion of the subperineurial layer into the perineurial layer was less prominent (Figure 5B,C). All three types of glia contained structures of unknown origin in brains with membrin knockdown compared to control brains (Figure 5B,C). Together, these findings reveal distinct changes in the ultrastructural organization of the surface glial layer, both within individual cells and between neighboring cells.

Glial Overexpression of Inx2 Rescues Behavioral and Physiological Phenotypes of Glial Membrin Knockdown

Returning to the hypothesis that Inx2 functions downstream of Membrin in the maintenance of neurophysiology, we asked whether overexpression of Inx2 under conditions of membrin knockdown could rescue its neurological phenotypes. To overexpress Inx2, we used UAS‐Inx2‐RR‐RFP published by (Miao et al. 2020). We confirmed Inx2 expression of the UAS‐Inx2‐RR‐RFP construct through staining in third instar larval wing discs prior to use (Figure S5). Co‐expression of Inx2 in the background of glial membrin knockdown nearly completely rescued the seizure phenotype of glial membrin knockdown alone (Figure 6A,A′). Furthermore, the changes in activity and sleep architecture found in flies with glial membrin knockdown were attenuated by concomitant overexpression of Inx2 (Figure 6C,C′). Consistent with these findings, we observed that glial Inx2 overexpression in the background of a glial membrin knockdown also partially returned the Inx2 immunofluorescence pattern back to the wild‐type pattern, compared to the Inx2 staining pattern in glial membrin knockdown alone (Figure 6D–F′″).

Since the knockdown of membrin exclusively in perineurial glial cells is sufficient to sensitize flies to seizures, we wondered whether the overexpression of Inx2 in this same subset of cells would be sufficient to rescue this phenotype. Consistent with a cell‐autonomous rescue mechanism, we found this to be the case: heat‐induced seizure sensitivity upon the knockdown of membrin in perineurial glia is abolished by co‐expression of Inx2 in perineurial glia (Figure S6).

Altogether, this strongly supports a causal role for impaired function of Inx2 in the neurophysiological downstream consequences of glial membrin knockdown, culminating in sensitivity to heat‐induced seizures, as well as alterations in activity and sleep patterning.

Discussion

The identification of downstream cellular consequences of GOSR2/Membrin deficiency forms the first step towards a rational, targeted treatment for North Sea Progressive Myoclonus Epilepsy (NS‐PME). Here, we advance on the previously obtained insight that in a Drosophila melanogaster model of NS‐PME, knockdown of membrin in glial cells alone is sufficient to induce a progressive sensitivity to heat‐induced seizures (Lambrechts et al. 2019), by showing that its neurobiological effects extend to heat‐independent behavioral changes affecting activity and sleep architecture. We specify the glial effect of membrin knockdown to the glial subtypes: perineurial, subperineurial, and ensheathing glia. In addition, we identify the glial gap junction protein Innexin2 (Inx2) as a downstream factor affected by knockdown of glial membrin, which is causally connected to both sleep and the seizures. These insights provide the first step in connecting the genetic defect in GOSR2/Membrin in glia to a mechanism leading to eventual neuronal dysfunction observed in NS‐PME patients.

Model‐Specific Properties and Their Effects on Interpretation of Findings

In this study, we found a role for glial Inx2 in seizures and sleep architecture in a Drosophila model for NS‐PME. We support this finding with complementary biochemical and behavioral evidence. First, the behavioral phenotype of perineurial loss of Inx2 overlaps with perineurial loss of Membrin. Second, membrin knockdown affects endogenous Inx2 on Western blot and immunostaining. Finally, Inx2 overexpression results in the rescue of behavioral phenotypes caused by membrin knockdown as well as the Inx2 immunostaining pattern. Together, these data strongly support a role for Inx2 as part of the downstream consequences of membrin loss causing seizures and disrupted sleep architecture.

What exactly mediates the effect of membrin knockdown on Inx2 remains unknown. Inx2 may be affected by a reduced ER‐Golgi trafficking function of Membrin (Hay et al. 1998; Praschberger et al. 2017): gap junction proteins have been shown to be trafficked from ER to the Golgi apparatus (Guan et al. 2022), making this a plausible explanation. It was shown in M. musculus and C. elegans that with hampered trafficking, gap junction proteins accumulate in the ER and Golgi apparatus (Guan et al. 2022). In Drosophila epithelial tissue, it was found that Inx1 and Inx2 become mislocalized to the cytoplasm when the heteromeric partner is missing (Lehmann et al. 2006). Here, we observe a mislocalization of Inx2 in the outer layer of the brain, which may suggest its accumulation. However, in which cell types this apparent accumulation occurs and whether this mislocalization is confined to specific organelles or the cytoplasm could not be determined in this thin layer of cells.

Perhaps surprisingly, overexpression of Inx2 under circumstances of glial membrin knockdown results in the rescue of both behavioral phenotypes and the Inx2 immunostaining pattern, although partially. The model relies on RNAi‐mediated membrin knockdown, which only partially downregulates membrin expression (Lambrechts et al. 2019). We hypothesize that with partial membrin knockdown, the remaining functional Membrin traffics some of the available Inx2. Overexpression of Inx2 in this system therefore likely allows for more Inx2 to be trafficked by the remaining Membrin toward its proper location, illustrated by the finer grained structures reappearing. However, a large proportion of Inx2 will remain accumulated and mislocalized due to reduced trafficking, which explains why Inx2 immunofluorescence does not normalize upon its overexpression.

For the behavioral results, we observe differences in the magnitude of effects between experiments and sexes, in both seizure sensitivity and sleep architecture. Differences in magnitude between experiments can be explained by many factors, including environment, age, and genetic background. For example, the recombination of driver and UAS lines alters the genetic makeup, and the introduction of a second UAS may cause a dilution of the original effect (Li and Johnston 2001). Therefore, each experiment has carefully matched controls with the same type of recombination and number of transgenes. Regarding differences between sexes, it is a described phenomenon in Drosophila that the Gal4‐UAS system affects males more strongly than females (Brumby et al. 2011; Park et al. 2014). Indeed, we generally observe that males are more strongly affected than females, both for seizures, sleep, and on Western Blot (and even male lethality), but in some experiments, the effect is reversed. For example, when perineurial glia are combined with ensheathing glia, males seem to be less sensitive to seizures than females; this could suggest a different role of these glia in males and females. However, it is impossible to determine whether these differences are due to a differential role of the manipulated protein or cell type or a differential activity of the Gal4‐UAS system. Overall, both sexes show the same direction in effect, and therefore combined, they strengthen the findings discussed.

Gap Junction Proteins in Seizures and Sleep

In the context of intercellular communication, the importance of gap junctions appears self‐evident: junctions facilitate shuttling of bioactive molecules such as ions between neighboring cells. In Drosophila, junctions are formed by innexin hemi‐channels on either side of the junction, whereas in man, junctions are formed by connexins, which are both structurally and functionally similar (Bauer et al. 2005; Beyer and Berthoud 2018; Goodenough and Paul 2009). Alternatively, the relevance of Inx2 may not, or not only, stem from its known function as a gap junction protein. It was recently discovered that Inx2 also has a role in cell adhesion between glial cells of different types, controlling their spatial location during development (Das et al. 2023). This role, although only demonstrated in the Drosophila peripheral nervous system, is intriguing considering the disrupted glial pattern seen with electron microscopy in the outer layer of the Drosophila brain upon glial membrin knockdown. A related phenotype of abnormal wrapping by ensheathing glia was observed in a Drosophila model for progressive myoclonus epilepsy caused by mutations in PRICKLE1 (Chen et al. 2023). This may suggest a shared pathophysiological mechanism, which in the case of NS‐PME could be conferred by the downstream involvement of Inx2, affecting cell adhesion. A cell adhesion role, however, suggests a neurodevelopmental component, and this may not be amenable to treatment by the time the diagnosis is made. Therefore, the exact role of gap junctions in seizure susceptibility should be investigated further.

The importance of gap junction proteins in Drosophila epilepsy models was already illustrated previously: neuronal expression of a null allele of shakB2, a gap junction protein and member of the innexin family, was found to suppress seizures in seizure‐prone Drosophila mutants (Song and Tanouye 2006). However, ShakB2 is primarily expressed in neurons (Ammer et al. 2022). In relation to gap junctions in glia, it was shown more recently that disruption of intercellular calcium transport within the outer perineurial glial layer, facilitated by Inx2 gap junctions, causes seizure sensitivity (Weiss et al. 2022). In mammals, the main gap junction proteins in astrocytes, Connexin30 and Connexin43, have been studied in various epileptic contexts, but such observational results have been conflicting (Jin and Zhong 2011). However, deletion of both Connexin30 and Connexin43 in mice was shown to fuel neuronal excitability in a kainate model of epilepsy (Deshpande et al. 2020), which resembles our findings. Although these reports all highlight the importance of gap junction functioning in seizure susceptibility, a direct connection to the pathophysiology of human epilepsy has so far been lacking. Here, we show that such a pathway could directly underlie a human seizure disorder, NS‐PME. However, this must be expressed with caution, since Inx2 has no direct ortholog in humans. This has been proposed to result from convergent evolution, with connexins and innexins strongly sharing topology and function but not sequence (Welzel and Schuster 2022). Therefore, whether these results can be directly extrapolated to mammals cannot be readily assumed.

The role of Inx2 in sleep is perhaps a little more enigmatic than its role in seizure sensitivity. Although Inx2 was previously connected to Drosophila sleep architecture in cortex glia (Farca Luna et al. 2017), we found a more prominent role for glia of the surface layer, including perineurial and subperineurial glia. The latter have been implicated previously in sleep function (Artiushin et al. 2018; Li, Artiushin, and Sehgal 2023) and sleep architecture (Coll‐Tané et al. 2021). Perineurial glia have not previously been connected to sleep, but perineurial Inx2 has been found to control the physiological functioning of subperineurial cells in a circadian manner (Zhang et al. 2018). Based on the recapitulation of the sleep phenotype (without affecting the activity pattern) with perineurial manipulation, we hypothesize that perineurial cells play a more prominent role in maintaining sleep architecture, perhaps in conjunction with subperineurial glia via Inx2 expression. Interestingly, in mice, loss of astroglial Connexin43 has also been shown to disrupt normal sleep behavior by increasing sleepiness and fragmenting wakefulness (Clasadonte et al. 2017), illustrating that gap junction proteins can also affect sleep in mammalian systems.

Whether NS‐PME patients also demonstrate sleep abnormalities is currently unknown. In general, sleep disorders are known to be more prevalent in epilepsy patients, with their sleep macro‐ and microstructure known to be altered compared to controls (e.g., Calvello et al. 2023). NS‐PME patients often use multiple types of antiseizure medication known to influence sleep and suffer from comorbid movement disorders, complicating the isolated study of sleep in these patients. Therefore, these results are a first suggestion that sleep disruption could occur in NS‐PME as part of the disease rather than as a secondary phenomenon.

To conclude, we have identified that the gap junction protein Inx2 plays an important role in seizure sensitivity and sleep architecture in a Drosophila model for the human disease NS‐PME. Although the exact role remains to be elucidated, these findings provide new insight into the relation between the glial dysfunction of GOSR2/Membrin and the final neurological phenotypes by highlighting gap junction protein Inx2 as a possible intermediate in specific subsets of glial cells. Besides a role in seizure suppression, Inx2 in Drosophila surface glia has a crucial function in maintaining sleep architecture.
