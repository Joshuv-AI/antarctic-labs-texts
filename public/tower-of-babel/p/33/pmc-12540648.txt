Visuospatial performance and its neural substrates in Dementia with Lewy Bodies during a pointing task
Authors: Annalisa Bosco, Caterina Foglino, Lucia Guidi, Davide Braghittoni, Greta Venturi, Luisa Sambati, Luca Baldelli, Giovanna Calandra-Buonaura, Patrizia Fattori, Raffaele Lodi, Caterina Tonon, Micaela Mitolo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDementia with Lewy Bodies (DLB) is characterized by motor and cognitive deficits that often overlap with other neurodegenerative disorders, complicating its diagnosis. This study combined linear mixed-effects modeling and machine learning to investigate key parameters of pointing movements, saccadic behavior, and superior parietal lobule (SPL) volumetry in differentiating DLB patients from controls. DLB patients exhibited distinct motor impairments, including increased movement times, greater pointing errors, and spatially modulated deficits in pointing accuracy. Saccadic analysis revealed prolonged saccade latencies, larger amplitudes, and pervasive hypermetria, with notable spatial asymmetries in accuracy and amplitude. Specifically, reduced hypermetria for upward-directed saccades suggests direction-specific modulation in DLB, highlighting potential disruptions in visuomotor pathways. Brain volumetric analysis demonstrated significant volumetric loss of SPL, particularly in the left hemisphere, further implicating this region in the visuospatial and motor deficits observed in DLB. Interestingly, an inverse relationship between SPL volumetry and task performance was found, more evident for hand-related parameters. The integration of behavioral, saccadic, and volumetric data revealed that a combined approach highlights the complementary contributions of motor, oculomotor, and neural changes in distinguishing patients from controls. This study provides novel insights into the visuomotor and neural substrates underlying DLB and emphasizes the importance of adopting a multimodal approach to its diagnosis. The results go beyond traditional visuospatial assessments, offering a robust framework for the identification of DLB-specific biomarkers. Future research should explore the generalizability of this combined model across other neurodegenerative conditions to refine diagnostic tools and improve patient outcomes.Subject terms: Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Dementia with Lewy Bodies (DLB) is a neurodegenerative disorder belonging to the group of synucleinopathies, characterized by the abnormal aggregation of the protein alpha-synuclein in the brain. Synucleinopathies are among the most common causes of dementia after Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and include conditions as Parkinson’s disease (PD), Parkinson’s disease dementia (PDD), and DLB. In DLB, aggregated alpha-synuclein forms Lewy bodies (LBs) within neurons and Lewy neurities (LNs) in neuronal processes, leading to widespread neurodegeneration.

DLB is characterized by a progressive cognitive decline that is severe enough to interfere with daily activities, social interactions, or occupational functioning. Among the different deficits, the visuospatial ones are particularly significant, as they impact spatial awareness, coordination, and interaction with the environment and they are prominent markers of DLB1–4. Investigating these impairments is crucial in order to understand the cognitive profile of DLB and distinguish it from other dementias. These impairments are recognized as a key component of the cognitive syndrome associated with DLB5,6. Compared to patients with “pure” Alzheimer’s disease (AD), those with DLB consistently show more pronounced deficits in visuospatial, visuoperceptual, and constructional abilities1–3,7–10. These cognitive impairments, indicative of significant posterior cortical dysfunction, align with imaging studies that reveal greater hypometabolism in the parieto-occipital cortex in DLB patients compared to those with AD 11–13. This dysfunction may particularly involve the superior parietal lobule (SPL), a critical brain region for visuospatial processing. In monkeys, strong evidence suggests that the SPL represents a crucial node in the dorsal visual stream that is responsible for several pathways related to visuo-spatial processing and for the control of hand actions14–17. The dysfunction of such areas in DLB may contribute to the observed visuospatial impairments in patients with this condition.

Collectively, these findings suggest that severe visuospatial dysfunction may serve as a quantifiable marker of DLB, particularly in patients with a pronounced clinical phenotype, and could identify those at higher risk for rapid cognitive decline due to extensive Lewy-related pathology. Typically, visuospatial impairment in DLB often occur early and is seen in performance in copy tasks, shape detection, block design, and clock drawing and copying6. However, given the hypometabolism in the parieto-occipital cortex in DLB patients, a pointing task, which requires participants to visually identify and accurately touch specific targets, provides a practical and controlled method to investigate visuospatial deficits in DLB.

In this study, we aim to comprehensively explore visuospatial impairments in DLB by examining both motor and ocular parameters during a pointing task and correlating these with brain volumetry. Specifically, we analyze motor parameters such as pointing accuracy, movement time, task duration, and reaction time, alongside ocular parameters including saccade amplitude, accuracy, and latency. These metrics provide insight into the visuospatial and visuomotor deficits associated with DLB, given the central role of the occipito-parietal cortex, a region known to exhibit significant dysfunction in DLB, in spatial awareness and visually guided movement. Within the occipito-parietal cortex, in this study particular focus was given to the SPL which plays a crucial role in integrating visuospatial information and coordinating motor actions. In fact, the dysfunction in this region, more than in others, could underlie the spatial disorientation and impaired visuomotor coordination observed in the present study.

Additionally, we seek to define a combined model that is capable of distinguishing DLB patients from healthy controls based on motor performance and brain volumetric data, offering potential for improved diagnostic approaches and a deeper understanding of the neural underpinnings of DLB-related visuospatial deficits.

Materials and methods

Participants

Thirty-two participants were included in this study. Among them, sixteen were DLB patients (6 F; mean age = 75.41±6.38; mean level of education: 11.88±3.91 years; right-handed as assessed by the Edinburgh Handedness Questionnaire18 with score: 0.79±0.40) and sixteen were healthy controls (9 F; mean age = 67.87±9.63; mean level of education: 13.56±4.76 years; right-handed as assessed by the Edinburgh Handedness Questionnaire18 with score: 0.83±0.34) with no neurological or psychiatric disease reported.

For DLB the inclusion criteria were DLB diagnosis at the mild stage of disease (MMSE > 18) according to the most recent international criteria6, whereas for controls the absence of cognitive dysfunctions was required, as evaluated through neuropsychological tests (cognitive scores ≥ 2 SD). Diagnoses of DLB were performed by a neurologist (LS) who is experienced in neurodegenerative disorders. Exclusion criteria for patients and controls were as follows: (1) presence of posterior pole pathology, including age-related macular degeneration and known optic neuropathies including open-angle glaucoma, (2) ocular pressure of more than 20 mmHg, (3) vascular retinal pathology, including diabetic retinopathy, and (4) contraindications for MRI.

The local Ethical Committee approved the study (#CE20225) (Comitato Etico Area Vasta Emilia Centro).

Neuropsychological assessment

All participants were administered the following standardized neuropsychological battery: the Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE)19, a general screening test; verbal and visuo-spatial short-term memory were assessed with the Digit span and Corsi span forward tasks20, verbal long-term memory with the 15-word list recall test21, and visuo-spatial long-term memory with the Delayed recall of Rey-Osterrieth Complex figure 22 or the Delayed recall of Modified Taylor Complex figure 23; executive functions were tested using the Stroop Color and Word Test22 and Digit span backward tasks20 ; language abilities were assessed with the Verbal Associative Fluency Test24 and Category Words Fluency Test25; constructional apraxia was evaluated with the Copy of Rey-Osterrieth Complex figure 22 or Copy of Modified Taylor Complex figure 23, visuo-perceptive function with the Visual Object and Space Perception Battery26 and Judgment of Line Orientation27,28. The severity of depression and anxiety symptoms was evaluated using the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI)29 and the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI X1 and STAI X2)30,31.

All tests were standardized for age and years of education in the Italian population.

Methodology for brain volumetry extraction

A standardized brain MRI protocol was performed using a high-field Siemens MAGNETOM Skyra 3 T MRI scanner equipped with a head–neck high-density (64 channels) array coil. The MRI protocol included a T1-weighted 3D Magnetization-Prepared Rapid Gradient-Echo Imaging sequence [MPRAGE, 176 continuous sagittal slices, 1-mm isotropic voxel, no slice gap, echo time (TE) = 2.98 ms, repetition time (TR) = 2300 ms, Inversion Time (IT) = 900 ms, flip angle = 9°, acquisition matrix = 256 × 256, pixel bandwidth = 240 Hz, in-plane acceleration factor = 2, duration ∼5 min]. Voxel-wise differences in brain volumetry were evaluated on 3D T1 images using the freely available FreeSurfer v.6 software. The regions of interest (in Fig. 1) were extracted based on the parcellation of human cortical gyri and sulci according to Destrieux et al.32 and normalized to each participant’s total intracranial volume (TIV).

Experimental task

Figure 2 shows a schematic representation of the experimental setup (Fig. 2A), the target positions (Fig. 2B), and the trial sequence. Participants sat in a dimly lit room in front of a touchscreen (MEDDP-522, Monitor LCD 22”) with a 1920 × 1080 pixel resolution (Fig. 2A). A chin rest was utilized to keep the head stable during task execution. Partcipants’ eye positions were recorded using mobile eye-tracking glasses (Pupil Core, Pupil Labs GmbH, Germany). This device is composed of 3 cameras: a world camera which records the participant’s field of vision (resolution: 1920 × 1080 pixels; field of view: 100° fisheye; sampling rate 60 Hz) and two eye-cameras that record the observer’s eye movements (resolution: 1920 × 1080 pixels, sampling rate: 120 Hz). Before each experimental session, a monocular calibration process was carried out automatically using five pupil calibration markers (v0.4) located in the corners and center of the touchscreen. The psychophysical experiment was designed and generated using Matlab (Mathworks, USA) with the Psychophysics toolbox extension33.

Experimental setup. (A), Planar view of the experimental setup. (B), View of the target arrangements on the touchscreen. (C), Time sequence of the task. The fixation target (green dot) stayed on for 1.5 s and then the reaching target (red dot) was turned on in one of the four locations. As soon as the reaching target appeared on the screen, the participant had to reach the position of the target with his/her right hand. The fixation target disappeared as soon as the reaching target appeared. The trial was concluded when the participant touched the screen and then returned their hand to the mouse.

Each participant performed reach-to-pointing movements toward four different target positions displayed on the touchscreen, using their right hand. As depicted in Fig. 2C, the experimental procedure consisted in the presentation of a central fixation point that prompted the participant to press the left mouse button (Button press). The participant was then required to fixate on the central point for a variable duration, which varied randomly across trials between 1.5, 2, and 2.5 s. After this interval, one of the four reaching targets appeared (Go signal), indicating that the participant had to promptly reach that target position (Reach-to-point). Upon target appearance, participants were free to look at the target and then reach it with their hand, as in naturalistic visuomotor behavior. The end of the trial was triggered by a screen touch (End trial). The targets were presented at the distance of 5 deg in relation to the fixation point (Fig. 2B). This configuration ensured that both saccade and reach amplitudes were moderate, comfortable, and comparable across participants, while minimizing variability caused by differences in movement amplitude or direction. The 5 deg distance reliably elicits clear, single saccades and reaching movements without inducing fatigue or discomfort, particularly in older adults and clinical populations such as DLB patients.

After touching the target, it switched off and a new trial started. Participants were asked to look at the target when it was displayed and move the hand in a ballistic way (without pauses or interruptions), at a fast but comfortable speed, and as accurately as possible. During an experimental session, each participant completed 120 trials (30 trials for each target position).

Data analysis

The analyses were performed with customized scripts in MATLAB (The MathWorks; RRID: SCR_001622) and R (The R Project for Statistical Computing, version 4.2.1).

The accuracy of the reaching movements performed toward the four targets was computed using the pixel coordinates of the target positions on the monitor and the position of the index finger at the contact point with the touchscreen, i.e., the endpoint.

The temporal features of the reaching movements were analyzed by computing the following: movement time, defined as the difference between the time when the screen was touched and the time when the mouse button was released; task duration, calculated as the difference between the time when the fixation point appeared and the time when the screen was touched; and reaction time, defined as the difference between the time when the target was displayed and the time when the mouse button was released. All measures were calculated for each participant.

For gaze data processing, the Pupil Player tool was used to extract gaze position data in normalized coordinates, corresponding to the first saccade performed by the participant once the target position appeared (Pupil Labs, release v1.22, Berlin, Germany). We then converted the normalized values in degrees considering each participant’s viewing distance from the screen. For saccade detection, we calculated the velocity of gaze during each trial and applied a gaussian smooth function to reduce noise in the data. We identified the onset of each saccade as the first velocity value exceeding the threshold of 30 degrees/second (Sanz Diez et al., 2022). To refine our selection, we examined subsequent velocity values to ensure the presence of an initial acceleration followed by a deceleration. The offset of the saccade was then determined as the first velocity value, following the deceleration, that dropped below the threshold of 15 degrees/second. For each detected saccade window, we verified that the velocity values calculated, and confidence values of the eye tracker (relative to the quality of the eye image) were valid. We excluded saccade windows with infinite velocity values or those in which the number of data points with confidence scores above the recommended threshold of 0.6 (as specified in the Pupil Labs documentation) exceeded the number of unreliable values with confidence scores below this threshold. For each valid saccade window, we calculated the amplitude and latency relative to the target onset. Additionally, we assessed the accuracy of each saccade by determining whether it was hypermetric or hypometric, which was achieved by subtracting the distance of the target from the center of the screen from the calculated amplitude.

To investigate whether the pointing errors, movement time, task duration, reaction time, saccade duration, saccade amplitude, saccade latency and saccade accuracy were predictive parameters of the two sample groups, we used linear mixed-effects (LME) models for each parameter listed above. This model included both the main effects of group and target position and the interaction between these two (participants as a random factor), with a Matlab formula: parameter ∼group * target position + (group * target|participant)34,35. The LME model results are reported in terms of the fixed effects for the intercept, and the main effects of group (patients vs. controls) and target position, as well as the interaction between the two factors. For each effect, the beta coefficient (β), standard error (± SE), t-statistic (t), and p-value (p) are presented. The intercept represents the baseline performance (e.g., for controls at the first target position) and is interpreted as the reference level for comparison. A significant intercept indicates that this baseline performance differs significantly from zero. A significant main effect of group reflects a consistent difference in performance between patients and controls, regardless of target position, while a significant main effect of target position indicates that performance differs across target locations, independent of group. Interaction effects reveal whether the group differences depend on the specific target position. To further investigate the significant interaction between group x target position, we applied the Wilcoxon test, a non-parametric test, to compare group differences with the Benjamini-Hochberg correction for post hoc analysis for specific target position.

Next, we tested the statistical differences in the volumetry of the left and right superior parietal lobule (l-SPL and r-SPL, respectively) between the two sample groups, using the LME model with a Matlab formula: volume ∼group + (1|participant). However, to determine whether the observed significant volumetric loss in the superior parietal lobule (SPL) was a specific effect rather than a general reduction across the entire brain, we employed a random forest model. This machine learning approach allowed us to evaluate the accuracy of the model in classifying participants into the patient or control groups based on volumetric data. The random forest algorithm operates by constructing a multitude of decision trees and aggregating their outputs, providing both robust classification accuracy and insights into feature importance. By analyzing the contributions of 22 different brain regions to the model using the out-of-bag permuted predictor importance metric (left and right inferior occipital gyrus, left and right cuneus, left and right middle occipital gyrus, left and right superior occipital gyrus, left and right lingual gyrus, left and right angular gyrus, left and right superior parietal lobule, left and right precentral gyrus, left and right precuneus, left and right occipital pole, left and right temporal pole), we identified the region that was most influential in predicting group membership. This approach enabled us to verify whether the SPL played a uniquely prominent role in distinguishing DLB patients from controls or whether similar effects were evident across other brain areas. To validate this approach, we applied the Wilcoxon test to compare group differences with the Benjamini-Hochberg correction for post hoc analysis for each brain region and to obtain the distribution of all corrected p-values. The accuracy of the model and the ranking of region-specific influences highlighted the specificity of SPL involvement in the observed volumetric changes.

Results

In the present study, we investigated whether the pointing errors and temporal features of reaching movements, as well as the saccadic parameters and brain volumetry could serve as predictive parameters to distinguish between the patient and control groups. To this aim, we employed linear mixed-effects models (LMEs) to evaluate the main effects and interaction between group and reaching target position.

Analysis of temporal and spatial parameters of saccades to the targets

The analysis of temporal and spatial parameters of saccades to the targets revealed key differences between DLB patients and controls across the examined variables, including saccade latency, saccade amplitude, and saccade accuracy, that vary according to target positions. The mixed-effects model analysis revealed a significant interaction effect between group and target on saccade latency (Fig. 4A,B, β = 10.741±4.705, t = 2.283, p < 0.05). Brain volumetry analysis and machine learning classification for the differentiation of DLB patients from controls

Given its critical role in visuospatial processing, we conducted the volumetric analysis of the left and right superior parietal lobules (SPL) as shown in Fig. 7A. This analysis revealed significant differences between DLB patients and controls. The LME model revealed a significant group effect for the left SPL, with patients showing reduced volume (group effect: β = -797,81± 134,09, t = -5,9498, p < 0.001), whereas no significant group effect was observed for the right SPL. However, to ensure that the observed volume loss was specific to the left SPL and not indicative of a generalized reduction across the brain, we applied a random forest classification model to differentiate between DLB patients and controls based on volumetric data from multiple brain regions including the left inferior occipital gyrus, left cuneus, left middle occipital gyrus, left superior occipital gyrus, left lingual gyrus, left angular gyrus, left superior parietal lobule, left precentral gyrus, left precuneus, left occipital pole, left temporal pole, and their counterparts in the right hemisphere. The model achieved an overall classification accuracy of 0.81±0.06, indicating reasonable predictive performance. Notably, the feature importance analysis revealed that the left SPL provided the maximum contribution to the model’s classification performance (OOB-permuted predictor importance value for left SPL volume predictor: 0.3833), as illustrated in Fig. 7B. To validate the results of the Random Forest model, we performed statistical comparisons between patients and controls on the same set of brain regions used as predictors. Specifically, we calculated the distribution of p-values obtained from group comparisons of cortical volumetric data and applied the Benjamini-Hochberg correction to control for multiple comparisons (p < 0.05). This approach allowed us to assess whether the regions identified as important by the model also showed significant volumetric differences between groups. Notably, the smallest corrected p-value in this analysis corresponded to the comparison of the left SPL, as Fig. 7C shows, further supporting its prominent role in distinguishing DLB patients from controls.

Finally, we performed a correlation analysis between the volume of the left SPL and the temporal and spatial parameters of hand and eye movements that were significantly different between patients and controls in the LME model (Fig. 8). Figure 8A illustrates the differences in left-SPL volume and behavioral performance between the two groups. Left-SPL volume was markedly reduced in patients compared with controls. In terms of behavioral measures, patients exhibited greater hand pointing error, longer trial duration, and longer hand reaction time. A similar but less pronounced trend was observed for saccade latency, saccade amplitude, and saccade accuracy. Notably, the statistical differences between patients and controls for both SPL volume and behavioral parameters were thoroughly examined using the LME model in the previous paragraph. Figure 8A aims to highlight the opposite trends observed between the two groups: as left-SPL volume decreases in patients, behavioral measures tend to increase, suggesting an inverse relationship between brain structure and task performance. Figure 8B summarizes the correlation analysis across the whole sample, showing a negative association between left-SPL volume and the six behavioral parameters. This negative correlation was statistically significant for the first three variables (hand pointing error r = -0.3984, trial duration r = -0.4044, and hand reaction time r = -0.3682), whereas no significant correlation was found for eye movement parameters (saccade latency r = -0.0402, saccade amplitude r = -0.1320, and saccade accuracy r = -0.1545). These findings suggest that the relationship between left-SPL volume and performance is more evident in hand-related parameters than in oculomotor measures during visuomotor tasks.

Discussion

This study highlights the potential of integrating neuropsychological, behavioral, saccadic, and brain volumetric parameters to distinguish DLB patients from control participants. By combining behavioral measurements from a dynamic pointing and related eye movement task with cortical volumetric data, we identified distinct patterns of deficits in the execution of pointing movements and correlated saccades, offering novel insights into the motor and visuospatial impairments associated with DLB that go beyond the limitations of traditional visuospatial tests. Classical assessments, such as line bisection or figure copying tasks6, primarily evaluate static visuospatial abilities and may fail to capture the dynamic, task-specific deficits observed in real-world motor tasks. Our integrated approach highlights the relevance of functional performance and structural brain changes in understanding the complex visuomotor phenotype of DLB.

The analysis of spatial and temporal features of pointing movements revealed that DLB patients exhibited significantly greater errors compared to controls, indicating a pronounced deficit in movement accuracy. This pattern was evident across all target positions. This finding is in line with the clinical neuropsychological results of the Spatial perception tests performed by these sample of DLB patients shown in Table 1. The temporal parameters of reaching movements provided further insights into these impairments. DLB patients demonstrated prolonged reaction times and trial durations, reflecting delayed initiation and execution of movements, independent of target position. However, movement time did not differ significantly between the two groups, implying that while the execution speed of movements was comparable, the observed deficits were more related to preparation of movement which impacts movement accuracy. Using kinematic analysis, Fadda et al.36 compared the performance of DLB patients and a control group in the “hand-to-mouth” (HTM) task, a standardized functional task that simulates feeding37–39. They found that, similarly to the present study, individuals with DLB required a longer time to complete the task. Interestingly, segmentation of the movement’s cycle into the going phase, adjusting phase, and returning phase revealed that DLB patients spent a significantly longer time in the adjusting phase, but not in the going phase, in which they showed a similar duration to that of the control group (see Fadda et al.,36). This is in line with the results of the present study in which the movement time (that corresponds with the “going phase” of the study by Fadda et al.,36) is similar across DLB patients and the control group.

Saccadic analysis revealed notable differences between DLB patients and controls, with patients displaying longer saccade latencies, larger amplitudes, and consistent hypermetria that depend on target position. These alterations reflect impaired saccadic control and visuomotor coordination, which are hallmarks of DLB. The interaction effects observed for saccade amplitude and accuracy regarding the upper target position indicate that these deficits are not uniformly distributed across spatial locations. Specifically, the reduced hypermetria for the upper target suggests a potential direction-specific modulation of saccadic impairments in DLB, which may involve distinct visuomotor pathways or compensatory mechanisms that are not linked to a uniform spatial distribution of impairments. However, it is important to consider that performance across the visual field is not homogeneous. A well-established horizontal–vertical anisotropy (HVA) indicates better performance on the horizontal than on the vertical meridian40–42 (VM). Furthermore, visual performance is typically superior in the lower than in the upper visual field43–46. This asymmetry is especially pronounced along the VM, a phenomenon known as vertical meridian asymmetry (VMA). Specifically, contrast sensitivity and spatial resolution are enhanced in the lower half of the VM40. Therefore, the relatively preserved performance for upper targets in DLB patients, compared to controls, may reflect a disruption or flattening of the normal VMA. Such a pattern suggests that the disease may impair the normally superior processing of the lower visual field, potentially due to differential degradation of the underlying visual or visuomotor networks.

In 2005, Mosimann and coworkers47 investigated reflexive and complex saccades through electrooculography, finding significant impairments in both reflexive and complex saccade execution in DLB and PDD patients, suggesting a common disruption in saccadic control mechanisms within these disorders, perhaps reflecting disruptions in both the cortical and subcortical saccadic networks, including the parietal eye fields, frontal eye fields, superior colliculus, and basal ganglia. The increased saccade amplitudes and hypermetria we observed in DLB patients are consistent with findings of impaired volitional saccade control reported in the study of Mosimann and coworkers47. Hypermetria may reflect disrupted inhibitory control and coordination within the subcortical structures, such as the basal ganglia, that have a relevant role in the generation of saccades48, combined with cortical dysfunction in areas such as the posterior parietal cortex, which are critical for spatial encoding in both monkeys and humans14,49–54. However, our study uniquely highlighted interaction effects in saccade amplitude and accuracy, particularly for the upper target position, and focused on voluntary saccades linked to pointing tasks. This spatial specificity was not emphasized in the Mosimann et al.47 study, but it may provide additional evidence for differential engagement of visuomotor pathways depending on target direction. While Mosimann et al.47 discussed generalized disruptions across cortical and subcortical networks, our results suggest that these impairments may be modulated by direction-specific pathways or compensatory mechanisms. Interestingly, this spatial specificity of saccadic deficits contrasts with the pattern observed in pointing movements, in which errors did not show target position effects. This dissociation suggests that eye and hand movements are governed by partially independent neural mechanisms in DLB, leading to different spatial distributions of impairments. While pointing errors appear more generalized across target positions, saccadic alterations exhibit a more complex pattern, emphasizing the need to consider both modalities to fully capture the visuomotor deficits in DLB.

The volumetric analysis of the SPL provides a structural basis for these findings. SPL is a critical hub for visuospatial processing, integrating sensory inputs to guide spatial attention, movement accuracy, and saccadic control. In both humans and non-human primates, the SPL has been implicated in encoding spatial information necessary for the planning and execution of goal-directed actions, including pointing movements and eye movements55,56. Lesion studies in humans have demonstrated that damage to the SPL results in deficits such as optic ataxia, characterized by impaired reaching and saccadic accuracy, highlighting its role in coordinating spatial and motor information57. In humans, neuroimaging studies have further supported the SPL’s role in visuomotor integration. Functional MRI has shown activation of the SPL during tasks requiring spatial attention shifts and saccade planning, underscoring its role in linking sensory input with motor output58. Breveglieri and coworkers59 found that transcranial magnetic stimulation delivered on the left SPL during the planning phase of a reaching movement affected the encoding of 3-dimensional target positions by decreasing the accuracy of movements toward these targets. This causal evidence is in line with the present results, which show that DLB patients exhibit significantly longer reaction times and pronounced inaccuracies in pointing movements. These findings suggest that dysfunction in the left SPL may contribute to impaired spatial encoding and motor planning in DLB, leading to deficits in both the temporal and spatial domains of goal-directed actions. The convergence of neurostimulation and behavioral evidence underscores the critical role of the SPL in the integration of visuospatial information required for accurate and timely motor responses. In addition to group-level differences, the present study extends this understanding by directly examining the relationship between SPL volume and task performance across the entire sample. Our correlation analyses revealed a consistent negative association between left-SPL volume and key behavioral parameters, particularly hand pointing error, trial duration, and hand reaction time. These associations were significant across the combined cohort of patients and controls, indicating that reduced SPL volume is linked to poorer performance in visuomotor tasks. Although no significant associations emerged for eye movement parameters, the observed trends suggest that SPL structural integrity is more directly relevant to hand motor and visuospatial performance than to oculomotor control. Clinically, these results highlight the potential utility of SPL volumetric measures as biomarkers for identifying and monitoring visuospatial-motor impairments in neurodegenerative conditions such as DLB. From a scientific perspective, the integration of volumetric and behavioral metrics strengthens the evidence for a structure–function relationship in the SPL and underlines the translational value of combining neuroimaging with precise behavioral assessments in early disease characterization.

Moreover, several studies have observed dissociation in function between the left and right posterior parietal cortex, suggesting that the left is specialized in motor attention60 and temporal events61, whereas the right is specialized in spatial representation61,62. This, together with the evidence found by Breveglieri et al.59, is consistent with our findings of reduced left SPL volumetry in DLB patients, which correlated with impairments in movement accuracy and saccade execution. The SPL’s contributions extend beyond motor control to include aspects of spatial encoding that are critical in order to understand visuomotor impairments in DLB. The observed deficits in saccade accuracy and hypermetria in our study may reflect disruptions in the SPL’s ability to encode the precise spatial metrics required for movement scaling and direction. Furthermore, the reduced hypermetria observed for upward saccades could suggest compensatory mechanisms or differential engagement of other visuomotor pathways when the SPL’s function is compromised. All together this structural alteration aligns with the patients’ neuropsychological profile, which revealed widespread visuospatial and constructional impairments, as well as deficits in executive functioning and attention, all cognitive domains supported by parietal-frontal network integrity. Together, these converging findings suggest that SPL atrophy may represent a neural substrate linking the observed visuomotor deficits with the broader cognitive dysfunction characteristic of DLB patients.

The application of a protocol that combines behavioral and neuropsychological measures, saccadic parameters, and SPL volumetric data yielded a robust framework with which to differentiate DLB patients from controls. The integrative approach used in the present study leverages the multidimensional nature of DLB-related deficits highlighting the complementary nature of motor, cognitive, and structural changes in identifying the DLB disorder.
