Bilateral distal femoral trochlear block migration after medial patellar luxation surgery in a dog
Authors: Danielle M. McNabney, Soroush Irandoust, Kenneth Bruecker, Peter Muir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The objective of this study was to describe management of a 2-year-old neutered male mixed breed dog with bilateral trochlear block recession migration with unilateral revision surgery. This is a rarely reported complication of a common surgical procedure. A 3D model segmented from a computed tomography scan using Mimics was performed before surgery to help determine whether surgical reduction of the osteochondral trochlear block fragments was feasible and preoperative press fit was acceptable. Subsequently, the trochlear block fragment in the left stifle was surgically reduced. During surgery, the osteochondral fragment was maintained in reduction with a transcondylar Kirschner wire because of imperfect press fit. The right stifle with trochlear block migration was treated medically. Short-term follow-up examination at 5 and 8 weeks revealed improvement in lameness after surgery. 3D modeling was also used to investigate potential underlying causes for bilateral block recession migration which was determined to be multi-factorial with excessive sulcoplasty depth being an important reason promoting risk of migration. During planning for femoral trochlear block recession sulcoplasty, it is important for the cut to be deep enough to help minimize risk of reluxation of the patella. However, press-fit of the osteochondral fragment is impaired when the depth of the recession sulcoplasty is excessive.

Background

Medial patella luxation (MPL) is a common reason for pelvic limb lameness in dogs in the United States (Perry and Déjardin 2021). MPL is most commonly a developmental disease but can also occur because of trauma (Piermattei et al. 2006; Perry and Déjardin 2021). The patella is a sesamoid bone located within the tendon of the quadriceps muscle and normally resides in the trochlear groove of the distal femur (Roush 1993; Perry and Déjardin 2021). During range-of-motion, medial and lateral movement is limited by the medial and lateral trochlear ridges and the associated femoropatellar ligaments so that the quadriceps apparatus moves in a sagittal plane that includes the patella and the patella tendon insertion on the tibial crest (Evans 2013). Canine MPL is a multifaceted disease process involving several structural abnormalities causing abnormal alignment of the quadriceps apparatus with its proximal and distal attachments. These abnormalities can include a shallow femoral trochlea, coxa valga, diminished anteversion angle, femoral/tibial deformity, and patella alta (Piermattei et al. 2006; Clerfond et al. 2014; Perry and Déjardin 2021).

To reduce trauma to the articular cartilage and development of osteoarthritis, there are many surgical treatment options to maintain the patella within the trochlear groove and improve stifle biomechanics (Roush 1993; Brower et al. 2017). Common techniques include femoral trochleoplasty, tibial tuberosity transposition (TTT), medial retinacular release, and lateral retinacular imbrication. While these techniques will often successfully manage a patient’s luxating patella and associated clinical signs, some patients with significant limb deformities may require more advanced orthopaedic techniques, such as a distal femoral osteotomy (Remedios et al. 1992; Perry et al. 2017).

Several femoral trochleoplasty techniques can be used to achieve at a minimum trochlear groove depth of at least 50% of the craniocaudal patella width (Slocum and Slocum 1993; Roush 1993). Trochleoplasty involves removal of the cartilage and part of the subchondral bone to deepen the groove. However, fibrocartilaginous healing is considered inferior to other techniques with a slower return to function and patellar erosions within 4 weeks (Boone et al. 1983; Slocum and Devine 1985). Cartilage sparing techniques such as the wedge and block recession trochleoplasty are thought to limit the development of osteoarthritis (Boone et al. 1983; Piermattei and Flo 1997; Roush 1993). The recommended distal position is just proximal to the intercondylar notch and extending to the proximal joint margin (Roush 1993).

The complication rate after surgery has ranged from 13 to 48% (Gibbons et al. 2006; Arthurs and Langley-Hobbs 2006; Alam et al. 2007). Common complications include reluxation, implant migration or breakage, tibial tuberosity fracture, patellar tendon desmitis, femoral nonunion, and worsening osteoarthritis (Remedios et al. 1992; Gibbons et al. 2006). Although migration of the osteochondral fragment after recession trochleoplasty is a theoretical consideration, this complication has rarely been described clinically and has only been reported with uni-lateral wedge osteochondral grafts (Cashmore et al. 2014; Clarke 2013; Harari et al. 2000; Remedios et al. 1992). Given the limited reports of this complication, there is also a paucity of information regarding risk factors for migration of the osteochondral fragment. The aim of this case report was to investigate and present potential risk factors for bilateral trochlear block migration after bilateral trochlear recession resection and unilateral repair of the migrated osteochondral fragment in a dog.

Case presentation

A 2-year-old male castrated Golden Retriever Poodle mixed breed dog was presented to the University of Wisconsin-Madison UW Veterinary Care hospital in September 2024 with a history of bilateral pelvic limb lameness for several months. The patient had received bilateral surgical treatment for MPL in June 2024 after diagnosis of Grade 2 left MPL and Grade 3 right MPL. Radiographically, surgical treatment included bilateral trochlear block recession and a right TTT. After surgery, the owner described pelvic limb lameness worse on the left and a clicking noise associated with both stifles. The lameness did not respond to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medication and rest. Bilateral stifle radiographs performed ~ 4 weeks after surgery confirmed bilateral trochlear osteochondral fragment migration, so the dog was referred for specialty care.

On presentation, the dog was bright, alert and responsive. Orthopaedic examination revealed bilateral pelvic limb lameness with bilateral stifle effusion and crepitus. In both pelvic limbs, the patella was within the distal femoral trochlear groove. The right patella was located more medially and partially overlying the medial trochlear ridge. The dog was reactive to palpation of the distal femur, and a firm moveable fragment was palpated proximal to the patella in the right pelvic limb and on the lateral distal femur in the left pelvic limb.

The dog was sedated (4.8mcg/kg dexmedetomidine IV, butorphanol 0.3 mg/kg IV) and bilateral orthogonal stifle radiographs were made including skyline views of the patella. Radiographs revealed proximal displacement of the trochlear osteochondral fragments in both stifles, with the left also being displaced laterally (Fig. 1). Bilateral stifle osteoarthritis and effusion were present along with additional mineral opaque bodies adjacent to both patellae. The right TTT had healed. An Insall-Salvati (IS) index of 1.99 was calculated for this patient based on the lateral radiographs to evaluate for underlying patella alta as a risk factor for fragment migration. The IS index is used to determine patella proximodistal positioning and evaluate for patella alta or baja (Ocal et al. 2020). It is calculated by dividing the length of the patellar tendon from the distal patella to the tibial tuberosity by the length of the patella (Insall and Salvati 1971). Normal values vary by size of dog and based on the reference interval of 1.40 to 2.24, the dog of this report does not have evidence of patella alta (Ocal et al. 2020). The dog was discharged with continued nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medication and rest.

The dog was re-examined 4 weeks later. The owner reported improvement in lameness and activity level within the first two weeks. However, the dog developed diarrhea, and when the non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medication was discontinued, the lameness returned. On re-examination, the dog was bright, alert and responsive. The body condition score was 7 of 9. There was bilateral pelvic limb lameness with a stiff gait and external stifle rotation bilaterally with reduced stifle flexion. During trotting, the dog hopped on his right pelvic limb with a shortened stride. The dog was reactive during stifle range-of-motion bilaterally with marked stifle crepitus bilaterally. A computed tomography (CT) scan was performed under sedation with both stifles in extension (3.4mcg/kg dexmedetomidine, 0.05 mg/kg hydromorphone IV), which revealed findings consistent with the previous radiographs, displacement of the osteochondral fragment, and stifle osteoarthritis bilaterally. Remodeling of the osteochondral fragments and the trochleoplasty sites bilaterally and left long digital extensor tenosynovitis were also identified. The right distal femoral trochlea was 1.2 cm deep with the right trochlear osteochondral fragment measuring 1.9 cm long and 1.3 cm lateromedial width x 0.55 cm craniocaudal width. The left distal femoral trochlea was 1.3 cm deep with the left osteochondral fragment measuring 2.2 cm long x 1.1 cm lateromedial width and 0.56 cm craniocaudal width. Both trochlear recessions were noted to start proximal to the level of the intercondylar notch and end at the proximal level of the trochlear ridges.

To evaluate whether the shape of the osteochondral fragment would still fit the femoral trochleoplasty and guide presurgical planning, a 3D model of the femur was segmented from the CT image data using 3D modeling software (Mimics version 26, Materialise, Plymouth, MI, USA). In each stifle, the osteochondral fragment was segmented and then reduced into the trochleoplasty (Fig. 2). After virtual reduction of the fragments, the 3D models of each femur suggested the block recession fragments were appropriately sized in width and length for surgical reduction. However, it was evident that they did not have a good press fit into the depth of the groove (Fig. 3). Once reduced, the caudal margin of each fragment had evidence of gapping, particularly distally, and an exact fit could not be achieved for either, with the left being worse than the right. The CT scan also allowed for evaluation of estimated groove depth for the initial trochleoplasty as compared to the patellar depth along with location of the trochleoplasty. In the left stifle, the trochleoplasty was ~ 74% of the craniocaudal width of the patella and the right it was ~ 54%. The trochleoplasty site began distally, just proximal to the intercondylar notch, and extended proximally, at the level of the proximal extent of the trochlear ridges. The distal margin of the patella was at the level of the proximal margin of the trochleoplasty site with the limb in full extension. Staged surgical correction was recommended to the owner, starting with the left stifle, because the left pelvic limb was more severely affected at that time of presentation.

The patient was maintained on sedative medications and rest before returning to our hospital in February 2025 for surgical treatment of the left stifle. A blood sample was obtained, and a serum biochemistry, packed cell volume (PCV) and total protein (TP) were submitted and assessed as normal. For surgery, the dog was premedicated with dexmedetomidine (3mcg/kg IM) and hydromorphone (0.01 mg/kg IM). General anesthesia was induced with propofol (0.8 mg/kg IV) and ketamine (2.3 mg/kg IV). The patient was intubated and maintained on sevoflurane. An ultrasound guided femoral and sciatic nerve block were performed with ropivacaine (2 mg/kg peri-neural) after surgical preparation of the limb, and cefazolin (22 mg/kg IV) was given for prophylaxis. A lateral parapatellar approach to the left stifle was performed. The lateral joint capsule, tensor fascia lata, and femoropatellar ligament were markedly thickened. A lateral arthrotomy was performed, and the patella and patellar tendon were luxated medially to observe the femoral trochlea (Fig. 4). The free floating osteochondral trochleoplasty fragment was located 2 cm proximomedial to the proximal end of the trochlea with rounded edges. The trochleoplasty defect measured 1.5 cm mediolaterally x 2 cm proximodistally x 1 cm craniocaudally located in the distal femur starting at the margin of the intercondylar notch and extending 2 cm proximal to the level of the proximal extent of the trochlear ridges. The trochleoplasty was lined soft pink repair tissue. The trochleoplasty fragment was reduced after the caudal surface of the osteochondral fragment and the bed of the trochleoplasty were debrided to bleeding bone with a curette and a rasp (Fig. 4). Because manual pressure was needed to maintain reduction, a single 0.045” Kirschner wire was placed in a lateral to medial direction across the trochlear ridges and the fragment to maintain reduction, and the wire was cut flush with the bone. The articular surface of the patella had minimal wear except for ~ 0.5 cm of fibrous proliferative tissue noted on the medial and lateral margins. The joint was placed through range-of-motion, and the patella did not luxate. The surgery site was then lavaged and lateral imbrication of the joint capsule and tensor fascia was performed using a series of locking loop sutures. The remaining layers were closed routinely. Hyaluronic acid (22 mg) was injected into the stifle joint before closure. An adhesive bandage (Primapore, Smith & Nephew) was applied and acetaminophen (10 mg/kg IV) and meloxicam (0.1 mg/kg SQ) were given before recovery. Radiographs after surgery revealed appropriate implant fixation and joint effusion (Fig. 5).

The dog was given analgesic medication with hydromorphone (0.05 mg/kg IM q6h) and acetaminophen/codeine (12 mg/kg/2.4 mg/kg PO q8h) and gabapentin (11 mg/kg q8h) and trazodone (6 mg/kg q8h) for sedation before discharge 1 day after surgery. Oral gabapentin (11 mg/kg PO q8-12 h x 56d), trazodone (6 mg/kg PO q8-12 h x56d), acetaminophen/codeine (12 mg/kg/2.4 mg/kg PO q8h x 5d), meloxicam (0.01 mg/kg PO q24h x14d), and sucralfate (14 mg/kg PO q6-8 h x 7d) were prescribed. The owners were instructed to follow activity management guidelines for the first 8 weeks after surgery including cage restriction when resting and only controlled leash walks. It was recommended to have the incision evaluated in 2 weeks by a veterinarian for suture removal.

The dog was evaluated at UW Veterinary Care at 5 and 8 weeks after surgery. The owner reported improvement in lameness after surgery throughout the 8 weeks. By 4 weeks after surgery, the owner reported minimal lameness with leash walks. At the 8-week recheck, the owner reported good tolerance of 30-minute leash walks. Minimal lameness in the left pelvic limb was found on examination. On orthopaedic examination at 5 weeks, the right stifle was like earlier examinations. Moderate effusion and crepitus were found during range-of-motion in the left stifle with associated discomfort. The patella could not be luxated in either stifle on sedated exam. At the 8-week recheck, stifle effusion was considered mild bilaterally with continued intermittent crepitation on range-of-motion of the left stifle.

Left stifle radiographs were made under sedation (3.5mcg/kg dexmedetomidine IV, 0.2 mg/kg butorphanol IV; 3.7mcg/kg dexmedetomidine IV, 0.1 mg/kg butorphanol IV; respectively) at the 5- and 8-week recheck (Fig. 6). At 5 weeks, mild stifle effusion, remodeling of the trochleoplasty revision site, and a small bone fragment at the distolateral aspect of the intercondylar fossa were found. At 8 weeks, there was healing across the medial and distal aspects of the trochleoplasty site. Pin position was like that seen at 5 weeks. The fragment at the distolateral aspect of the trochleoplasty was unchanged. At 8 weeks, the owner was instructed to allow a gradual return to normal activity over an 8-week period including introduction of off-leash time within the house in the first 4 weeks followed by an additional 4 weeks of gradual introduction to unrestricted activity. Surgical correction of the right stifle was recommended to the owners.

During telephone follow-up with the owner 9 months after left stifle revision surgery, the dog was reported to clinically be doing well with continued mild bilateral pelvic limb lameness, which did not limit quality of life. The owner elected to manage the right stifle medically and not to perform revision surgery at the time of case report preparation.

Discussion and conclusions

Careful patient-specific preoperative planning for MPL surgical treatment is needed as this is a complex disease with multiple contributing factors which may need surgical correction. Surgical treatment commonly involves femoral trochleoplasty, lateral imbrication of the fascia lata, medial release, and TTT (Piermattei et al. 2006; Perry et al. 2017). In patients with surgical complications, preoperative planning is even more important. Interestingly, although correction of MPL is a common surgical treatment, migration of the trochleoplasty osteochondral fragment after surgery is rarely described. Development of this complication is likely associated with poor surgical planning and technical error.

To optimize presurgical planning for revision surgery in this case, a 3D computer model of the femur and the osteochondral fragment was made from the CT to help determine whether press-fit reduction seemed reasonable after the osteochondral fragment had been free floating in the joint for some time. If enlargement of the osteochondral fragments had occurred with healing, reshaping of the fragment would be needed at surgery. Also, substantial fragment resorption might preclude a reasonable press fit in the trochleoplasty site. The 3D model showed appropriate fragment sizing for reduction but revealed the caudal margin of the fragment did not have an exact press-fit with evidence of gapping caudally and inability to perfectly reduce, with the left being less conformed than the right. At surgery, we found digital compression was needed to maintain reduction in the left stifle suggesting the osteochondral fragment was smaller than ideal. This problem was addressed using Kirschner wire fixation. It is unclear whether the lack of press-fit is attributed to initial surgical correction or remodeling of the fragment after migration.

Staged unilateral versus concurrent bilateral correction in one surgery has long been debated regarding risk of complications after surgery. Performing a concurrent bilateral procedure did not increase risk on some studies (Cashmore et al. 2014; Clerfond et al. 2014; Gallegos et al. 2016). However, a more recent study showed that performing bilateral procedures increases the post-operative risk from 12% for unilateral to 23% for bilateral treatment (Sanders and Bevan 2019). Simultaneous bilateral trochleoplasty may have placed this dog at a higher risk for postoperative complications. Consequently, it was recommended to the owner that revision surgeries should be staged with the more severely affected limb treated first. Given the rarity of trochleoplasty fragment migration, other factors may also have contributed to this complication.

While MPL correction surgery generally has favorable outcomes for most patients, there are reports that patients with complications have poorer return to function, particularly for dogs with higher grade luxations (Dunlap et al. 2016). Grade IV MPL correction surgery has a reported complication rate of 24% (Arthurs and Langley-Hobbs 2006; Dunlap et al. 2016). The dog of this report reportedly had a Grade II and Grade III MPL on initial presentation to a veterinarian. The complicated outcome including the presence of a large intraarticular osteochondral fragment and the time delay between development of complications and revision surgery may help to explain the poorer return to function in the dog of this report, which increased development of degenerative joint disease as found on our pre-operative radiographs.

The overall goal of block recession trochleoplasty is to create a recession fragment which is maintained in the femoral trochlear groove through press fit. The pressure of the patella through range-of-motion during weight-bearing is intended to provide stabilization of the fragment through compression against the parent femur. If the patella re-luxates after surgery, there is higher risk of osteochondral fragment migration (Boone et al. 1983; Slocum and Devine 1985). Another factor which may increase the risk of fragment migration is patella alta. While this patient has an IS index of 1.99 and is, therefore, not classified as having patella alta (Ocal et al. 2020), the extended stifle radiographs and CT scan show evidence that the patella sits at the proximal region of the trochleoplasty site in extension. This may lead to greater compression across the proximal part of the osteochondral fragment, thereby creating a hinge effect, potentially enabling distal migration. When performing any femoral trochleoplasty technique, the goal is to achieve a depth of at least 50% the craniocaudal width of the patella to provide stable tracking of the patella within the groove with minimal risk of re-luxation (Slocum and Devine 1985; Slocum and Slocum 1993). Intraoperative measurement can help determine if the trochleoplasty needs to be deepened. In the dog of this report, we found the initial femoral trochleoplasty depth was ~ 74% and ~ 54% of patella depth for the left and right stifles, respectively. With excessive groove depth, contact pressure between the patella and the osteochondral fragment during weight-bearing is likely reduced, and could explain osteochondral fragment migration in this case.

In summary, bilateral trochlear recession migration has not previously been described in the literature, and descriptions of revision surgery are limited, despite MPL correction being a common surgical procedure in dogs. We report unilateral revision surgery of a bilateral trochlear block migration in a young Goldendoodle mixed breed dog. In this dog, excessive trochleoplasty depth, lack of a press-fit of the osteochondral fragment, proximal position of the patella in the femoral trochlea, and a bilateral procedure appeared to contribution to osteochondral fragment migration. In situations where there is poor press fit of the osteochondral fragment in the trochleoplasty site, augmentation via a transcondylar K-wire can enhance stabilization. Diagnostic investigation and presurgical planning were facilitated by building a 3D model of the distal femur and the osteochondral fragment to enhance diagnostic assessment of the anatomy of the distal femur. Ensuring correct depth of the trochleoplasty is an important part of preoperative planning.
