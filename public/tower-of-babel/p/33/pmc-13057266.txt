Integrated site selection framework for origin-based cold storage using GIS-MCDM and improved Harris Hawks optimization
Authors: Yujie Li, Fengyu Li, Xinting Yang, Xianyong Meng, Jiawei Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A well-planned layout for origin-based cold storage is crucial for minimizing post-harvest losses, reducing costs, improving logistics efficiency, and mitigating environmental impacts. To address gaps in existing research on multi-technology coordination and trade-offs among siting objectives, this paper proposes an integrated multi-method framework that combines Geographic Information Systems (GIS), multi-criteria decision making (MCDM), and an improved Harris Hawks Optimization (IHHO) algorithm for facility siting optimization. We develop an evaluation system that incorporates logistics infrastructure, natural conditions, and agricultural development and use a GIS-MCDM model with spatial constraints to delineate highly suitable areas. K-medoids spatial clustering and an economies-of-scale cost model are then used, with IHHO determining the optimal number, locations, and capacities of facilities. A case study in Helan County, China, indicated that highly suitable zones are concentrated in the south, accounting for approximately 1.25% of the study area; nine candidate regions were identified, and six optimal sites were selected. Scenario analysis revealed that higher fixed construction costs favor larger facilities, while growing demand supports centralized, high-capacity cold stores rather than dispersed, smaller ones. Overall, the proposed framework provides a systematic tool for scientific planning and suitability assessment of on-farm cold-chain infrastructure, with the potential to enhance logistics efficiency, reduce postharvest losses, and promote sustainable agricultural development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40766-2.

Introduction

The production of fresh agricultural products is essential for ensuring global food security and has continued to expand alongside agricultural modernization. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations1 (FAO, 2023), the global annual production of fresh agricultural products has exceeded 9.9 billion tons. Cold chain logistics (CCL) plays a central role in the global agricultural supply chain, and its strategic importance is growing under the combined pressures of accelerated climate change and rising demand for fresh foods. An efficient CCL system is widely recognized as essential for reducing post-harvest losses, improving food safety, and enhancing agricultural competitiveness. As the world’s largest producer and consumer of fresh agricultural products, China faces a particularly urgent need to accelerate the development of cold chain infrastructure in production areas. According to data released by China’s National Bureau of Statistics, the country produced 1.372 billion tons of fresh agricultural products in 2024, representing a 3.6% year-on-year increase2 (NBSC, 2024). To address the challenges associated with rising production volumes and the stringent temperature and humidity requirements during the distribution of fresh products, the Chinese government has prioritized the development of CCL infrastructure. As a part of this effort, it launched the “14th Five-Year Plan for CCL Development” as a systematic strategy. This plan establishes a crucial foundation for preserving the postharvest quality and safety of fresh agricultural products, minimizing distribution losses, and meeting consumers’ demands for high-quality and diverse products3. However, China’s CCL development began relatively late compared with that of developed Western countries, and the establishment of origin-based cold storage and preservation facilities has lagged significantly4,5. Consequently, agricultural products often cannot be transferred promptly to appropriate low-temperature environments postharvest, resulting in considerable postharvest losses and reduced farmer income6,7. Scientific planning and optimization of origin-based cold storage and preservation facilities are thus essential for safeguarding product quality and safety from the source, minimizing “first-kilometer” losses, and improving facility utilization and operational efficiency.

Site selection for logistics and warehousing facilities has been extensively studied, and these studies have provided a robust theoretical foundation and methodological guidance for the scientific planning and design of logistics networks8. Traditional site selection studies have primarily focused on quantitative analytical approaches and have commonly employed techniques such as mixed-integer programming (MIP) and fuzzy multi-objective optimization to construct mathematical models. These methods aim to achieve an optimal balance among objectives, including siting and operating costs9,10, service radius coverage11, and carbon emission control12, thereby addressing core optimization needs related to cost efficiency, service accessibility, and environmental sustainability. These models perform well when the number of objectives is limited, the data are precise, and the scales are manageable. However, traditional quantitative site selection methods have inherent limitations. They often struggle to incorporate multiple spatial dimensions associated with regional-scale facility location decisions, including natural environmental conditions, resource endowments, transportation infrastructure, and regional constraints. In addition, they are unable to adequately evaluate the potential impacts of qualitative criteria and fuzzy information that arise in real-world decision-making and that may influence subsequent construction and operation. In planning cold chain infrastructure for fresh agricultural products in production areas, these shortcomings can result in facility layouts that are misaligned with local development conditions, thereby reducing the feasibility and practical effectiveness of the resulting plans. To address these shortcomings, recent studies have increasingly employed multiple criteria decision-making (MCDM) methods for facility site selection and land suitability evaluation13,14. MCDM shows distinct advantages in addressing complex multi-criteria decision-making challenges by integrating quantitative and qualitative indicators15. Fuzzy set theory has been increasingly integrated with MCDM as decision-making environments have grown more uncertain16,17. In recent years, fuzzy set theory has continued to evolve to capture more complex forms of uncertainty18,19. For example, Sami et al.20 extended fuzzy set methodologies by introducing interval-valued T-spherical fuzzy sets into graph theory, enabling the simultaneous representation of membership, non-membership, and uncertainty measures. This allows more nuanced modeling of multi-source information and uncertainty.

MCDM methods integrate quantitative and qualitative criteria and therefore provide clear advantages in addressing multi-criteria decision-making problems under complex conditions. In recent years, they have been widely used for facility location and site suitability assessment21. Commonly used multi-criteria decision-making methods include the AHP (analytic hierarchy process), the EWM (entropy weighting method), and TOPSIS (Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution). The AHP has been particularly favored for its ability to decompose complex problems into a structured hierarchy. For example, García, J. L. et al.22 employed AHP to evaluate the optimal locations for agricultural product warehouses by considering corporate research objectives. In addition, Gao et al.23 proposed a grey relational analysis method based on a combined AHP–entropy weighting scheme and applied it for selecting CCL distribution centers. Traditional single-method MCDM decision frameworks, while relatively straightforward to implement, often struggle to simultaneously quantify spatial factors and optimize multiple objectives. To improve the interpretability, transparency, and credibility of facility location decisions under complex spatial constraints, researchers have increasingly integrated GIS with MCDM to develop site suitability assessment approaches24. For example, Wang et al25. developed a GIS-based evaluation system for siting CCL parks using entropy weighting and further incorporated Bayesian theory and K-means clustering to identify and refine optimal sites. Sun et al.26 combined GIS and MCDM methods to construct a multi-scale evaluation framework for siting aquatic-product logistics centers in the Nansi Lake region of China, thereby providing more effective guidance for the planning of CCL facilities in production areas. By leveraging GIS for spatial data processing and analysis, these approaches help overcome the limitations of traditional models in quantifying and visualizing spatial factors. However, GIS–MCDM suitability assessment methods typically lack the strengths of conventional quantitative location models in solving multi-objective and multi-constraint site selection problems. Moreover, in CCL planning for fresh agricultural products, existing studies have largely focused on the optimization of cold chain consolidation centers in consumption areas or urban distribution centers27–29. Research focused on optimizing cold storage site selection in production areas remains scarce, yet such research is critical for reducing losses in the “first kilometer” of the fresh agricultural supply chain.

Here, we focus on the “first kilometer” of fresh agricultural product logistics and aim to identify the optimal locations, number, and capacity levels of origin-based cold storage facilities. We propose an approach that evaluates the comprehensive suitability of potential sites within the study area under conditions that satisfy cold storage demand while minimizing total construction costs. Our approach employs a two-stage site selection optimization method that integrates GIS, MCDM, and optimization models. In the first stage, a spatial suitability evaluation model is developed based on GIS-MCDM; this model incorporates multidimensional factors, such as logistics infrastructure, agricultural development level, and natural geographic environment, to identify potential high-suitability sites. In the second stage, the K-medoids spatial clustering algorithm is combined with a cost optimization model that accounts for economies of scale, which is solved using the improved HHO algorithm to determine optimal site locations, as well as the optimal number and capacity levels of cold storage facilities in the high-suitability regions. An empirical study was carried out in Helan County, Yinchuan City, Ningxia, China, to validate the effectiveness and practical applicability of the proposed method. The proposed site selection decision-making framework can serve as a reference and provide technical support for the site selection and development planning of agricultural cold storage facilities in other similar major agricultural production regions. Materials and methods

Study area

Helan County, located in Ningxia, China, covers an area of 1,595 square kilometers (see Fig. 1). Situated on the eastern slope of the Helan Mountains and within the core Yellow River irrigation zone of the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region (105°45′–106°30′ E, 38°20′–38°50′ N), the county is strategically located and has abundant water resources. Helan has become a key national production base for cold-season vegetables, goji berries, and beef cattle, with an annual agricultural output of up to 800,000 tons. We investigated the site selection planning of origin-based cold storage facilities for fresh agricultural products in Helan County because of its significant production of agricultural products requiring cold-chain storage. Our objective was to provide a scientific foundation and decision-making support for optimizing the CCL system, safeguarding product quality, and promoting sustainable agricultural development.

The first stage

Figure 2 presents the overall framework for site selection. In the first stage, a GIS–MCDM-based evaluation model is developed to identify suitable areas for origin-based cold storage facilities. A spatial suitability index system is established using three dimensions: logistics infrastructure, agricultural development level, and natural geographic environment. Areas unsuitable for construction, including protected areas, scenic sites, public-sensitive zones, and incompatible land use types, are eliminated by applying exclusion criteria. Thematic layers for each indicator are subsequently generated using GIS, and indicator weights are determined through the AHP along with expert judgment. Sensitivity analysis is performed using the map removal technique across the 15 indicator layers. Finally, a GIS-based spatial weighted overlay analysis is conducted to produce spatial suitability results, which are classified into five levels: highly suitable (Level 1), suitable (Level 2), moderately suitable (Level 3), less suitable (Level 4), and unsuitable (Level 5).

Fig. 2Diagram of the site selection process framework.

Diagram of the site selection process framework.

The second stage

The second stage focuses on analyzing the highly suitable areas identified in the first stage to determine the optimal site locations. First, centroids of spatial grids within highly suitable areas are extracted, and the K-medoids clustering algorithm is applied to group them into multiple candidate regions. A site selection optimization model was then developed that integrates storage capacity levels with a total cost minimization objective. This model was solved using the improved Harris Hawks Optimization (IHHO) algorithm, yielding a cost-optimal layout for origin-based cold storage facilities and identifying the optimal site locations.

Exclusion indicators.

Natural and ecological protection areas hold significant ecological, environmental, and biodiversity value and are strictly protected under national legislation31. Accordingly, origin-based cold storage facilities should be located at least 500 m away from these protected areas to minimize potential impacts on local ecosystems and habitats. To reduce adverse effects such as traffic congestion and noise pollution from construction and logistics operations, a minimum buffer of 500 m from residential areas is also required. Areas located within 300 m of parks and within 800 m of hospitals are also designated as prohibited development zones to ensure the social acceptability and compatibility of cold storage sites with surrounding land uses.

The construction of origin-based cold storage facilities requires judicious land use, and site selection must consider local land use types30. Farmland is fundamental for food security, while forest areas provide crucial ecological and economic functions; both are strictly regulated under national laws and policies31. Land use conversion entails complex acquisition procedures32, substantially increasing compliance costs and project timelines33. Given the high land use value of farmland and forest areas, zones within 50 m of farmland and 300 m of forest are designated as prohibited development areas. Additionally, to minimize potential harm to aquatic ecosystems and mitigate flood-related risks to facility safety34, a minimum buffer of 300 m from rivers, lakes, and other water bodies is maintained.

Method for evaluating the site suitability

The AHP35 was combined with GIS to construct a weighted suitability evaluation model for site selection (Fig. 3). The AHP was originally proposed by Saaty in the 1970s. By assigning weights to different criteria and sub-criteria, the AHP translates subjective judgments into quantitative measures and provides a multi-criteria decision-making method that integrates qualitative analysis with quantitative computation36. This semi-qualitative, expert-based approach constructs mathematical models to quantify cognitive information that is difficult to measure directly, while ensuring consistency and supporting a systematic, transparent, and reproducible decision-making process37. In this study, the AHP method is employed to determine the relative importance of evaluation criteria for siting cold storage facilities in fresh agricultural production areas. Method for evaluating optimal site selection areas

Partitioning candidate areas based on K-Medoids

In ArcGIS 10.8, regions were transformed into vector polygons using the raster-to-polygon tool and subsequently segmented into uniform 200 m × 200 m grids. The centroid coordinates (latitude and longitude) of grids representing highly suitable areas were extracted using the Geometry Calculator. Subsequently, spatial clustering of these points was conducted in R 4.2.1 using the K-Medoids algorithm, resulting in the division of highly suitable areas into multiple candidate zones.

While K-means is the most commonly used clustering method, its sensitivity to outliers often compromises clustering accuracy41. As a robust alternative to K-means, the K-medoids algorithm provides faster computation and greater resilience to noise and outliers42. Compared with other algorithms (e.g., the k-means algorithm), K-Medoids can identify representative cluster centers that are actual points within each suitable-area cluster, rather than centroids that may fall outside suitable regions. Therefore, the K-Medoids algorithm is applied to spatially cluster the identified suitable areas and determine a medoid for each cluster. These medoids, which are contained within the set of suitable areas, can represent potential cold storage locations and provide a basis for subsequent siting optimization, consistent with the objectives of the first stage of this study. The main steps of the K-medoids algorithm are as follows:

Step 1

Randomly select k points from the spatial coordinates of highly suitable areas as initial medoids.

Step 2

Compute the Euclidean distances between all non-medoids and the k medoids, then assign each non-medoid to the cluster of its nearest medoid to form k initial clusters.

Step 3

For each cluster, attempt to replace the current medoid with a non-medoid; calculate the total distance from all cluster points to the candidate medoid; and select the point that minimizes this total distance as the new medoid.

Step 4

Repeat these steps until the cluster centroids remain unchanged or the maximum number of iterations is reached, at which point the algorithm terminates.

The clustering objective is to minimize the Euclidean distance between sample points and their respective cluster centroids. Clustering performance is assessed using silhouette coefficients43 to compare different numbers of clusters.

Site selection cost optimization model with capacity levels

(1) Problem description.

Our focus is on the site selection and construction of cold storage facilities for the “first kilometer” segment of farmers. Building upon the prior clustering of candidate areas, the primary objective of this phase is to identify the optimal facility locations, determine the optimal number of facilities, and assign their capacity levels, all while minimizing total investment costs and meeting the cold storage requirements of local agricultural products. Furthermore, the effects of economies of scale on site selection decisions are considered. Economies of scale describe the reduction in unit production costs as operational scale increases and reflect the relationship between input factor concentration and economic efficiency44,45. (1) Harris Hawks optimization algorithm.

(1) Harris Hawks optimization algorithm.

Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO) is a recently developed population-based metaheuristic algorithm43 that simulates the cooperative hunting behavior of Harris hawks. It is known for its simple structure, ease of implementation, and strong convergence performance, and has been widely applied in CCL network optimization44,45. The HHO algorithm typically comprises three phases: global exploration, a transition from global exploration to local exploitation, and local exploitation. The prey’s escape energy E determines the hawks’ hunting strategy. As E varies, the algorithm adaptively shifts from exploration to exploitation.16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E=2{E_0}(1 - \frac{t}{T})$$\end{document}

When |E| ≥ 1, the HHO algorithm remains in the exploration phase to search for prey. When |E| < 1, it enters the exploitation phase to pursue the prey. In this expression, E denotes the prey’s escape energy, which decreases gradually over iterations; t is the current iteration; T is the maximum number of iterations; and E₀ is the initial state of the escape energy, where E₀ ∈ [0, 1].

Exploration Phase

When the escape energy satisfies |E| ≥ 1, the algorithm operates in the global exploration phase, during which the Harris hawks search for prey using the following two strategies.

17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X(t+1)=\left\{ \begin{gathered} {X_{rand}}(t) - {r_1}|{X_{rand}}(t) - 2{r_2}X(t)|,q \geqslant 0.5 \hfill \\ ({X_{rabbit}}(t) - {X_m}(t)) - {r_3}({B_L}+{r_4}({B_U} - {B_L})),q<0.5 \hfill \\ \end{gathered} \right.$$\end{document} 18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X_m}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{{i=1}}^{N} {{X_i}(t)}$$\end{document}

In this equation, X(t + 1) denotes the position of an individual at iteration t + 1; Xrabbit(t) represents the position of the best individual; X(t) is the current position of the individual; r1, r2, r3, r4, and q are random numbers in the interval (0, 1); BU and BL denote the upper and lower bounds of the search space, respectively; Xrand(t) is the position of a randomly selected individual in the population; Xm(t) is the mean position of the population; and N is the population size.

Exploitation phase

When |E| < 1, the HHO algorithm enters the exploitation phase. Let λ denote the prey’s escape probability. In this phase, the algorithm selects one of four search strategies based on the values of λ and E, as described below.

Soft encirclement: When |E| ≥ 0.5 and λ ≥ 0.5, the algorithm adopts the soft encirclement strategy, with the position update expression given as:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X(t+1)=\Delta X(t) - E|J{X_{rabbit}}(t) - X(t){\mathrm{|}}$$\end{document}20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {\mathrm{X(t)=}}{{\mathrm{X}}_{rabbit}}(t) - X(t)$$\end{document}

In this expression, ΔX(t) denotes the difference between the current position vector of a Harris hawk and the prey’s position vector, and J is a random number in [0, 2] that simulates the prey’s movement behavior and is resampled at each iteration.

Hard encirclement: When |E| < 0.5 and λ ≥ 0.5, the algorithm adopts the hard encirclement strategy, and the position update expression is given as:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y={X_{rabbit}}(t) - E|J{X_{rabbit}}(t) - X(t){\mathrm{|}}$$\end{document}

Progressive rapid dive with soft encirclement: When |E| < 0.5 and λ < 0.5, the model at this stage is expressed as:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{X(t+1)=}}\left\{ \begin{gathered} Y,F(Y)<F(X(t)) \hfill \\ Z,F(Z)<F(X(t)) \hfill \\ \end{gathered} \right.$$\end{document}

Here, F denotes the fitness value of the minimization problem; Y and Z are defined as follows:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y={X_{rabbit}}(t) - E|J{X_{rabbit}}(t) - X(t){\mathrm{|}}$$\end{document}24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{Z=Y+S}} \times LF(D)$$\end{document}

In this expression, D denotes the dimensionality; S is a random vector of size 1 × D; and LF(D) represents the Lévy flight function, which is calculated using Eq. (25).25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$LF(x)=0.01 \times \frac{{\mu \times \sigma }}{{{{\left| v \right|}^{\frac{1}{\beta }}}}},\sigma ={\left( {\frac{{\Gamma (1+\beta ) \times \sin (\frac{{\pi \beta }}{2})}}{{\Gamma \left( {\frac{{1+\beta }}{2}} \right) \times \beta \times {2^{\left( {\frac{{\beta - 1}}{2}} \right)}}}}} \right)^{\frac{1}{\beta }}}$$\end{document}

In this expression, µ and v are random numbers in the interval [0, 1], and β is a default constant set to 1.5.

Piecewise chaotic mapping

In the original HHO, the population is initialized by randomly generating individual positions, which may not provide sufficient diversity when solving complex problems. To enhance the global search capability of the HHO algorithm, this study employs a piecewise chaotic map46 to initialize the population.26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x(t+1)=\left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} {\frac{{x(t)}}{p},0 \leqslant x(t)<p} \\ {\frac{{x(t) - p}}{{0.5 - p}},p \leqslant x(t)<0.5} \\ {\frac{{1 - p - x(t)}}{{0.5 - p}},0.5 \leqslant x(t)<1 - p} \\ {\frac{{1 - x(t)}}{p},1 - p \leqslant x(t)<1} \end{array}} \right.$$\end{document}

The piecewise control factor P takes values in the interval [0, 0.5].

Lens imaging-based opposition learning

Lens imaging-based opposition learning is inspired by the light refraction phenomenon of convex lenses. Based on the convex-lens imaging principle, an opposite position is generated for the current target point, thereby enhancing the ability of populations to escape local optima47.27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{t}^{{new}}=\frac{{{B_U}+{B_L}}}{2}+\frac{{{B_U}+{B_L}}}{{2k}} - \frac{{{x_t}}}{k}$$\end{document}

As the number of iterations increases, k increases, and the corresponding search range of the opposite solution gradually narrows. The magnitude of k is inversely related to the range of the generated opposite solution. By adjusting k, lens imaging-based opposition learning achieves a dynamic acceptance capability. The expression for parameter k is given as follows:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k={\left( {1+\sqrt {\frac{t}{T}} } \right)^{10}}$$\end{document}

Comparison of standard benchmark functions

To evaluate the performance of the IHHO algorithm, six benchmark functions from CEC200548 were selected (Table 6). The unimodal functions F3, F4, and F5 were used to assess convergence speed and search accuracy, whereas the multimodal functions F12 and F13 evaluated the global search capability. The fixed-dimension multimodal function F15 was used to examine robustness and performance on complex landscapes. The IHHO algorithm was compared with the original HHO, GWO49, WOA50, ALO51, and EO52 algorithms. Each algorithm was independently run 30 times on each benchmark function to ensure statistical reliability. Optimization accuracy and stability were assessed using the mean and standard deviation of the results, with smaller values indicating better performance. In all experiments, the population size was set to 100, and the maximum number of iterations was set to 100. All algorithms were implemented in MATLAB and executed on a computer with an Intel® Core™ i7-7700 CPU (3.60 GHz), 8 GB RAM, and a 64-bit operating system.

Scenario analysis

First, the impact of variation in the fixed construction cost Cmk of cold storage facilities with different capacity levels on the number and capacity levels of cold storage facilities constructed in candidate areas was analyzed. Six variation ratios of fixed construction costs Cmk were set for cold storage facilities across three capacity levels: no change, ± 10%, ± 30%, and ± 50%. In the experimental design, each of the three capacity levels was simulated separately under the six cost variation scenarios. Individual variation in the fixed construction cost Cm1 for small cold storage facilities corresponds to Case 1, while the fixed construction costs Cm2 and Cm3 for medium and large cold storage facilities correspond to Case 2 and Case 3, respectively, resulting in a total of 18 experiments.

Second, how variation in farmers’ cold storage demand influences both the number of cold storage facilities constructed and their capacity configurations within candidate regions was examined. In real-world production and operations, farmers’ demand for low-temperature storage services is dynamic, influenced by seasonal fluctuations, changes in cropping patterns, market price variations, climatic variation, and policy incentives. Accordingly, four demand scenarios were established to simulate their impact on site selection: a + 40% increase (Case 4), + 20% increase (Case 5), −20% decrease (Case 6), and − 40% decrease (Case 7). The proposed optimization model for fresh agricultural product cold storage site selection was applied to assess the outcomes under these scenarios.

Figure 6 shows the spatial evaluation results of site suitability for origin-based cold storage in Helan County, revealing clear spatial differentiation. Within the study area, highly suitable zones are primarily concentrated in the southern region, with scattered distributions in other areas, covering approximately 15.04 km² (1.25% of the total area). Additionally, weak agricultural industry clusters and insufficient cold-chain logistics infrastructure further reduce its suitability for siting cold storage facilities. Suitable land is also limited in the northern and eastern regions, largely due to extensive high-quality farmland within the core Yellow River irrigation zone, which is unsuitable for non-agricultural development.

Identifying the optimal alternative area

The centers of 374 grid cells within the highly suitable zones were first extracted as spatial sample points, followed by clustering analysis using the K-medoids algorithm. To determine the optimal number of clusters, silhouette coefficients were systematically calculated for values of K ranging from 2 to 20 (see a in Fig. 7). The highest silhouette coefficient (0.7141) was observed at K = 9, indicating that this configuration achieved the optimal balance between intra-cluster cohesion and inter-cluster separation. Based on this result, nine spatial cluster centers were identified (see b in Fig. 7), each representing a candidate area and serving as the spatial foundation for subsequent site selection optimization. The clustering results reveal a heterogeneous spatial distribution of highly suitable zones, with cluster centers densely concentrated in the southern core agricultural region of Helan County; eastern and western areas displayed a more scattered pattern that closely mirrored the spatial pattern identified in the GIS-based suitability assessment.

A total of 34 village-level agricultural production bases in Helan County were selected using the spatial coordinates of the nine candidate cold storage sites identified through the K-medoids clustering algorithm. Parameters related to cold storage capacity levels are shown in Table 10. The IHHO algorithm was implemented in MATLAB 2021b with a population size of 100 and a maximum of 200 iterations. Using these parameters, the model was solved to generate a cost-efficient layout for origin-based cold storage facilities.

Scenario analysis

Figure 10 illustrates how changes in fixed construction costs for cold storage facilities of different capacity levels affect the number and capacity levels of facilities in the final site selection plan, based on baseline costs defined in Table 12. In Case 1, where fixed construction costs for medium- and large-sized facilities remain unchanged and only those for small facilities vary from − 50% to + 50%, the narrowing cost gap between small and medium-sized facilities reduces the former’s cost advantage. As small facility costs rise, their economies of scale diminish; although medium-sized facilities still have higher fixed costs than small facilities, their lower unit operating costs make them more advantageous under a total cost minimization objective. Consequently, the construction of medium-sized facilities is favored over that of small facilities. In Case 2, where only medium-sized facility costs vary, a reduction in the fixed costs of medium-sized facilities narrows the cost gap with small facilities. This favors the construction of medium-sized facilities due to their greater cost efficiency (enhanced economies of scale). An increase in the fixed construction costs of medium-sized facilities ultimately narrows the cost gap with large facilities, which favors the construction of the latter due to their enhanced economies of scale. In Case 3, where only large facility costs vary, a 10% cost reduction in the fixed costs of large facilities narrows the cost gap with medium-sized facilities; despite the higher absolute costs of large facilities, their superior operational efficiency (enhanced economies of scale) results in lower total costs, which favors increases in the number of large facilities. Moreover, an increase in the fixed construction costs of large facilities widens the cost gap with medium-sized facilities. This reduces the economies of scale of large facilities, which favors the construction of medium-sized facilities for minimizing system-wide total costs while satisfying cold storage demand.

In summary, economies of scale are the primary determinant of cold storage site selection. Relative changes in fixed construction costs (i.e., variation in the cost gap between facilities of different levels) significantly influence site selection preferences. Specifically, when the fixed cost gap between a given facility level and its higher-level counterpart narrows, higher-level facilities with lower unit operating costs become more favorable under a total cost minimization objective (enhanced economies of scale) framework. Conversely, wider cost gaps reduce their cost advantage. Therefore, effective planning of origin-based cold storage requires a holistic evaluation of fixed costs, operating costs, and relative differences across facility levels, rather than solely considering absolute costs, to achieve total cost minimization and optimize resource allocation within the cold chain system.

Four demand variation scenarios for origin-based farmers’ cold storage needs (gn) were examined (Table 12). Table 13 summarizes the comprehensive effects of demand fluctuations on the optimal cold storage site selection plan, including facility location, number, capacity level, utilization rate, and cost structure. Under a 20% demand increase (Case 5), the total number of cold storage facilities remains at six, but the number of large (level-3) facilities increases from one (baseline) to three. Average utilization reaches 96.3%, with total costs rising to 35.74 million yuan. With a 40% demand increase (Case 4), five of the six facilities are large-scale, utilization increases to 97.3%, and total costs increase to 41.39 million yuan. In both cases, economic costs (including fixed construction and operating costs) account for 86.2% and 85.9% of total costs in Case 5 and Case 4, respectively, while transportation costs (Case 5: 4.2%; Case 4: 4.3%) and loss costs (Case 5: 9.6%; Case 4: 9.8%) remain comparatively low. These results indicate that large facilities, due to their enhanced economies of scale, achieve lower unit operating costs and effectively amortize higher fixed construction costs. Their centralized deployment also allows broader service coverage, avoids inefficiencies from dispersed small facilities, and mitigates transport-related losses. Therefore, prioritizing high-capacity facilities is essential for optimizing system-wide costs under increasing demand.

Under a 20% demand reduction scenario (Case 6), the total number of cold storage facilities is reduced to four, and the number of large facilities increases to two. This configuration maintains a high average utilization rate (96.3%) while reducing total costs to 24.11 million yuan. Economic costs account for 85.2% of total costs, whereas transportation and loss costs slightly increase to 4.5% and 10.3%, respectively. These findings suggest that even when demand decreases, maintaining some large facilities helps preserve cost-efficiency due to their lower marginal operating costs for addressing unserved demand compared with small, dispersed facilities. However, under a 40% demand reduction (Case 7), a configuration with four medium-sized (level-2) facilities is optimal, with total costs further reduced to 18.4901 million yuan and utilization to 93.9%. As the fixed costs of large-scale facilities can no longer be adequately amortized under lower demand (reduced economies of scale), a configuration with medium-sized facilities is optimal for matching current demand levels.

Across all scenarios, cold storage utilization remains above 93%, demonstrating the model’s robustness and efficiency under demand fluctuations. Regions W1, W4, and W9 are consistently selected in all scenarios, highlighting their strategic value as stable hubs within the origin-based cold chain network. These regions exhibit strong adaptability in terms of their location, infrastructure coverage, and service coverage and should be prioritized as core nodes in the planning of a resilient and scalable cold storage network.

Discussion

The results indicate that the integrated GIS–MCDM–IHHO location decision-making framework can identify candidate areas with well-developed production infrastructure, high levels of agricultural development, and favorable natural conditions, and can further provide an optimal location plan under a cost-minimization objective. For cold-chain systems in agricultural production areas, a rational facility layout, shorter transport distances from fields to cold storage, and improved logistics conditions can maximize precooling efficiency and preservation effectiveness, thereby reducing spoilage rates and improving product quality and shelf life53. This not only directly reduces post-harvest losses but also strengthens the market competitiveness of products supplied from production areas, benefiting farmers, distributors, and consumers alike54. Scientifically planned and well-distributed cold chain facilities in production areas can also aid coordination among production, precooling, processing, consolidation, and transportation nodes, effectively reducing overall transportation costs and cold chain resource consumption55. For large-scale and intensive agricultural production and logistics clusters, these efficiency gains are particularly critical because they support regional agricultural sustainability and food safety assurance56. This study proposes a GIS–MCDM–IHHO-based site selection framework that provides decision-makers with a practical tool for siting production-area CCL facilities. The evaluation system integrates multiple criteria, and spatial quantification enables experts and policymakers to rapidly identify spatial development patterns in local agricultural production and logistics systems and to propose appropriate solutions, thereby supporting informed siting decisions.

Traditional site selection approaches often rely on a single MCDM model and place strong emphasis on expert judgment and static weighting schemes. As a result, they frequently struggle to jointly account for spatial constraints and economic optimization requirements. To address this deficiency, some studies have integrated GIS techniques with MCDM to evaluate site suitability. While such integrated approaches can effectively capture spatial patterns and improve decision-making efficiency, they generally lack the ability to optimize multiple site selection objectives. In addition, although multi-level facility layout planning, site selection objective optimization, and intelligent algorithms have been applied to cold chain networks, most previous studies focus on distribution terminals or urban cold chain nodes; however, the specific planning needs of CCL infrastructure in production areas have received limited attention. To overcome these limitations, this study integrates GIS–MCDM with intelligent optimization algorithms to support the siting of “first - kilometer” CCL infrastructure for fresh agricultural products. We propose a two-stage decision-making framework, ranging from land suitability assessment to final site optimization, as illustrated in Fig. 2. By identifying suitable areas across the study region and coupling them with siting optimization models that jointly consider key constraints (e.g., the spatial distribution of candidate sites, facility capacity requirements, and service accessibility), the proposed framework optimizes facility layout and enhances the operational efficiency of production-area cold chain systems.

It should be noted that suitability assessment for cold storage siting across different production areas should be tailored to local conditions; siting criteria should be adapted to regional contexts, and the evaluation framework should be adjusted accordingly to reflect local requirements. Nevertheless, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, how seasonal fluctuations in agricultural output affect cold storage utilization was not assessed. Second, transportation route optimization between production sites and storage facilities was not addressed. Finally, the analysis is also constrained by the spatial resolution of the available data. Future work should explore machine learning and deep learning approaches57 to support complex decision-making in production-area CCL systems. For example, dynamic receiving-cycle models could be developed to estimate cold-chain demand in production areas over time. In addition, machine learning should be integrated with optimization algorithms to predict and optimize vehicle routing, delivery time windows, dispatch frequency, and loading rates; vehicle routing optimization should also be incorporated into the site selection decision framework. Finally, the long-term effects of climate change on cold-chain demand should be assessed. Using higher-resolution spatial datasets would further improve the accuracy of the results. Collectively, these research directions could strengthen the resilience and sustainability of production-area cold-chain infrastructure networks.

Conclusion

We investigated the scientific layout of origin-based cold storage for the “first-kilometer” cold chain of fresh agricultural products. A two-stage site selection method that integrates GIS, MCDM, and the HHO algorithm is proposed, and a representative case study was performed using data from Helan County, China. In the first stage, a framework of 15 indicators relating to the natural geographic environment, agricultural development level, and logistics infrastructure was constructed, and spatial constraints were defined. A GIS-MCDM model was employed to assess land suitability, and high-suitability areas covered 1.25% (15.04 km2) of the county. These areas were clustered in the southern part of the study area and dispersed at the edges of the study area. Furthermore, we integrated K-medoids spatial clustering with a cost optimization model that considers economies of scale, and the HHO algorithm was used to optimize the locations, number, and capacity levels of cold storage facilities. The results of the case study indicated that Helan County requires six cold storage facilities (total capacity 33,000 tons) to efficiently meet the demand of 34 production bases, and all selected sites were located in the high-suitability area (e.g., Haijixing Logistics Park, Ligang Town), with facility utilization exceeding 90% and total costs at 29.8409 million yuan. The large cold storage facility (W4) emerges as a key hub due to its significant economies of scale, serving five bases with a utilization rate of 98.2%.

Further analysis indicates that economies of scale is the key factor affecting site selection decisions. When the fixed costs of high-level cold storage facilities are reduced by 10%, their construction frequency increases by 37.5%. When cold storage demand increases by 40%, large-capacity facilities should be prioritized, and operating costs are minimized when large facilities comprise 83.3% of all facilities. In conclusion, the proposed method effectively balances spatial constraints and economic efficiency, providing a scientific decision-making tool for governments and enterprises planning origin-based cold chain infrastructure.
