Smoking and the risk of neurodegenerative diseases in a Chinese case–control study
Authors: Jia Nie, Susan Peters, Calvin B Ge, Lützen Portengen, Nathaniel Rothman, Wei Hu, Ruwei Ou, QianQian Wei, Lingyu Zhang, Yanbing Hou, Qing Lan, Hui-Fang Shang, Roel Vermeulen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile smoking is inversely associated with Parkinson’s disease (PD) risk, its relationship with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and multiple system atrophy (MSA) remains unclear, particularly in Asian populations. We investigated these associations in a Chinese case–control study.MethodsWe recruited newly diagnosed ALS (n=430), MSA (n=271), PD (n=523) cases and hospital-based controls (n=1033) in Sichuan, China. Logistic regression models were used to evaluate associations between smoking and disease risks, adjusting for demographic, lifestyle and occupational factors.ResultsCompared with never-smokers, the adjusted ORs and 95% CIs of ALS for current and former smokers were 1.00 (0.61 to 1.65) and 1.79 (1.01 to 3.17), respectively. For MSA, ORs were 1.27 (0.73 to 2.23) for current smokers and 2.54 (1.41 to 4.60) for former smokers. Individuals who quit within 4 years before diagnosis showed the highest risk of ALS (OR=1.93, 95% CI 0.96 to 3.88) and MSA (OR=2.09, 95% CI 1.11 to 3.93). For both ALS and MSA, no consistent trend was found with increasing smoking duration or pack-years. In contrast, ever-smokers had a significantly lower PD risk (OR=0.49, 95% CI 0.33 to 0.71), particularly current smokers (OR=0.30, 95% CI 0.19 to 0.48). Longer smoking duration and higher cumulative smoking were also linked to PD risk with clear negative exposure-response patterns (P trend=0.039 and 0.029, respectively).ConclusionsConsistent with findings in non-Asian populations, smoking was inversely associated with PD risks in the Chinese population. For ALS and MSA, we found evidence suggestive of positive relationships with cigarette smoking, but no clear exposure-response relationships were observed.Keywords: ALS, MULTISYSTEM ATROPHY, PARKINSON'S DISEASE

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study is among the first to systematically evaluate and compare the associations between smoking and the risk of ALS, MSA and PD in a Chinese population using a consistent case–control design. Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), multiple system atrophy (MSA) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) are progressive neurodegenerative disorders with overlapping but distinct clinical features and pathologies.1 ALS primarily affects motor neurons, resulting in progressive paralysis, while PD and MSA are synucleinopathies characterised by motor symptoms such as bradykinesia and rigidity. Compared with PD, MSA typically progresses more rapidly and is marked by earlier and more severe autonomic dysfunction. These disorders remain incurable, and their aetiology is not fully understood.2,4

ALS has a crude incidence of 1.8/100 000 worldwide.5 Cigarette smoking might contribute to the risk of ALS either by directly damaging motor neurons or by increasing oxidative stress.6 While several observational studies conducted in European countries7,10 and the USA6 have indicated a weak positive relationship between smoking and the risk of ALS, other studies conducted in the UK11 and the USA12 have found no such association. Additionally, phenotypic differences between ALS patients in Asian and Western populations raise the possibility that environmental and genetic risk factors may differently affect disease risks across populations.13

MSA is a rare neurodegenerative disorder with an estimated annual incidence of 0.6 to 3.0 per 100 000 people globally.14 The association between smoking and MSA risk remains unclear. While nicotine may reduce α-synuclein accumulation and oxidative stress,15 16 prior epidemiologic studies have yielded conflicting results. A meta-analysis of five case–control studies, including one from China, suggested a possible inverse association between smoking and MSA risk, but heterogeneity between studies limits definitive conclusions.17

The global incidence of PD is estimated at 13.4 per 100 000 people per year.18 While cigarette smoking has been consistently associated with a reduced risk of PD, the biological mechanisms remain unclear. Nicotine is hypothesised to exert neuroprotective effects by inhibiting the striatal dopamine transporter, increasing dopamine levels in the synaptic gap.19 A meta-analysis of observational studies worldwide reported a pooled risk ratio of 0.59 (95% CI 0.56 to 0.62) for PD among ever-smokers compared with never-smokers.20 However, the influence of smoking duration and cumulative exposure on PD risk remains in debate, with conflicting findings in previous Chinese studies.21 22

Given the limited epidemiological evidence, especially for ALS and MSA risk factors in Asian population, we conducted a hospital-based case–control study in Sichuan, China. By examining detailed lifetime smoking histories, this study provides additional insights into the relationship between smoking and the risks of ALS, MSA and PD in a Chinese population.

Methods

Cases and controls

We recruited participants with ALS (N=469), MSA (N=361) and PD (N=1403) from West China Hospital of Sichuan University in Chengdu from February 2018 to January 2020. Participants with ALS complied with the revised El Escorial diagnostic criteria, and only definite or probable ALS cases were included.23 Participants with probable MSA complied with the Second Consensus Statement on the Diagnosis of Multiple System Atrophy24 and the Movement Disorder Society Clinical Diagnostic Criteria (2015) were applied to identify participants with PD.25 To reduce potential survival and recall bias, only newly diagnosed cases (within 1 year of diagnosis) were included, resulting in 436 ALS, 275 MSA and 528 PD cases for analysis.

Controls were drawn from a case–control study on lymphoid and myeloid neoplasms.26 A total of 1043 controls free of central neurological diseases were recruited from the same hospital as where we recruited neurodegenerative cases and two other tertiary care hospitals in Chengdu. The diagnoses of hospital admission for controls were: injuries (n=213), circulatory (n=347), digestive (n=200), genitourinary (n=254) and other conditions (n=29).

Exposure assessment

Smoking history was assessed through a standardised, computer-assisted, in-person interview conducted by trained interviewers using a structured questionnaire. The questionnaire contained a complete residential and occupational history, questions about demographic characteristics including age, sex and education, lifestyle factors such as smoking, alcohol, tea and coffee consumption, physical activity from the age of 20 to 10 years before the interview, and anthropometric measures (height and weight 1 year before the interview).

Detailed smoking history included the age or year of smoking initiation and cessation, as well as the average number of cigarettes smoked per day. Ever-smokers were defined as individuals who had smoked at least one cigarette per day for six consecutive months or longer. Smoking status was categorised as never, former or current smoker. Former smokers were those who had quit smoking more than 1 year before the interview, while current smokers included those who were currently smoking and who had quit within 1 year at the interview. Cigarette consumption was categorised based on smoking intensity (≤10, 11–20, ≥21 cigarettes/day) and smoking duration, calculated as the difference between the start and quit age for former smokers and between the start and interview age for current smokers. Cumulative smoking exposure was calculated in pack-years, determined by smoking intensity and duration. Time since quitting was defined as the difference between the quit age and the interview age for former smokers. Tertiles among control participants were used to define categories for smoking duration, pack-years and time since quitting smoking. Reference groups were set as never-smokers or current smokers, where appropriate.

Statistical analysis

Demographic characteristics of cases and controls were summarised using means and SDs for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Unconditional logistic regression models were used to estimate the association between smoking and disease risk, expressed as ORs and 95% CIs.

To account for potential confounding, adjustments were made stepwise. Model 1 adjusted for age at interview, sex (male, female), education (none/primary/middle school, secondary/technical school, college or higher) and residential distance from the hospital (≤5 km, >5–≤25 km, >25–≤100 km, >100 km). Residential distance from the hospital was included in the model to reflect rural versus urban residence and differences in environmental exposures and access to medical services. Model 2 further adjusted for alcohol consumption (no, yes; defined as drinking at least once per month), tea or coffee consumption (no, yes; defined as drinking either tea or coffee at least once per week), pesticide exposure (no, lower-level, higher-level; defined by linking lifetime job titles that participants ever held with the ALOHA+JEM27), general physical activity level from age 20 to 10 years before the interview (highly active, moderately active, moderately inactive, highly inactive) and body mass index (BMI) 1 year before the interview (kg/m²).

Exposure-response relationships were assessed by modelling smoking duration, pack-years and time since quitting as ordinal variables, both including and excluding the reference group. Potential interactions between smoking and sex, education, alcohol consumption, tea or coffee consumption, pesticide exposure, physical activity and BMI were evaluated by incorporating interaction terms into the fully adjusted model.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of our findings and to address potential sources of bias. Specifically, we (1) excluded cases and controls who lived more than 100 km from hospitals to reduce the potential impact of socioeconomic (ie, access to healthcare) or environmental differences; (2) excluded cases and controls who never lived in Sichuan for at least 15 years; (3) restricted cases to those diagnosed within 6 months before the interview to minimise potential survival and recall bias; (4) sequentially excluding control subgroups based on admission diagnosis (injuries, circulatory, digestive, genitourinary or other conditions) to mitigate confounding from conditions potentially related to smoking; and (5) matched cases and controls using propensity score matching to address imbalance in demographic characteristics. Propensity scores were calculated using a logistic regression model, with disease status (case, control) as the dependent variable and covariates adjusted in model 1 as the independent variables. Cases and controls were matched 1:1 based on their propensity scores using the nearest-neighbour matching algorithm with a calliper width of 0.1 SD of the logit of the propensity score. Standardised mean differences (SMDs) less than 0.1 are generally considered indicative of good balance. We evaluated covariate balance post-matching by calculating SMDs for all matched variables and reported the proportion of covariates meeting this threshold. The MatchIt package in R was used to perform propensity score-matching analysis.28 We analysed the association using conditional logistic regression and further adjusted for other unmatched covariates including covariates further adjusted in model 2.

Two-sided p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Due to minimal missing data (1.1% for smoking and key covariates), complete case analyses were performed, leaving 430 ALS cases, 271 MSA cases, 523 PD cases and 1033 controls in the final analysis. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (V.4.4.0).

Results

Table 1 shows the demographic characteristics of 430 incident ALS cases, 271 incident MSA cases, 523 incident PD cases and 1033 controls included in the primary analyses. The mean age at the time of the interview was similar between ALS cases and controls, whereas PD and MSA cases were, on average, 5 years older than controls. The percentage of males in the control group (61.5%) was higher than that of all three case groups (57.4%, 52.8% and 47.4% for ALS, MSA and PD cases, respectively). More than half of the cases resided over 100 kilometers from the hospital, whereas only 9.3% of the controls lived that far. Overall, controls were more highly educated, with 23.0% having a college education, compared with 10.2% of ALS cases, 15.1% of MSA cases, and 18.9% of PD cases. The proportions of ever-smokers in cases were 46.3% for ALS, 43.5% for MSA and 27.0% for PD, in comparison to 46.9% among the control group. The percentages of ever-smokers remained consistent when excluding participants who lived more than 100 km from the hospital (online supplemental Table 1).

Smoking and the risk of ALS

Compared with never-smokers, the estimated ORs and 95% CIs in the fully adjusted model for ever-smokers and current smokers were 1.22 (0.76 to 1.95) and 1.00 (0.61 to 1.65), respectively (table 2). Former smokers had a higher risk of ALS than never-smokers, with an OR of 1.79 (95% CI 1.01 to 3.17). The estimated ORs and 95% CIs for smokers with ≤23, 23–41 and ≥41 pack-years compared with never-smokers were 1.19 (0.67 to 2.09), 1.39 (0.79 to 2.45), and 0.89 (0.48 to 1.61) without a clear trend (Ptrend=0.647). Additionally, former smokers who had quit within the past 4 years showed higher risk of ALS compared with current smokers, with a fully adjusted OR of 1.93 (95% CI 0.96 to 3.88).

Smoking and the risk of MSA

The association analysis between smoking and the risk of MSA is shown in table 3. Compared with never-smokers, associations were found between ever-smoking and MSA risk in the fully adjusted model (OR=1.70, 95% CI 1.03 to 2.85) but not for current smokers (OR=1.27, 95% CI 0.73 to 2.23). However, former smokers had a significantly higher MSA risk than never-smokers (OR=2.54, 95% CI 1.41 to 4.60). Among former smokers, those who had quit within the past 4 years had the highest MSA risk compared with current smokers (OR=2.09, 95% CI 1.11 to 3.93).

In sensitivity analyses, slightly higher OR estimates for ever-smokers were observed when excluding controls who resided more than 100 km from the hospital or controls with circulatory or genitourinary diseases (online supplemental Table 3). Smoking and the risk of PD

Smoking was inversely associated with the risk of PD (table 4). In the fully adjusted logistic regression models, the ORs and 95% CIs for ever-smokers and current smokers compared with never-smokers were 0.49 (0.33 to 0.71) and 0.30 (0.19 to 0.48), respectively. The ORs and 95% CIs for ever-smokers with ≤23, 23–41 and ≥41 pack-years of smoking compared with never-smokers were 0.60 (0.36 to 0.98), 0.48 (0.28 to 0.79) and 0.36 (0.21 to 0.60), respectively, with a significant trend (Ptrend<0.001). Longer smoking duration was also associated with decreased PD risk (Ptrend<0.001). Former smokers had a higher PD risk than current smokers who continued smoking, especially for those with longer cessation duration.

Sensitivity analyses showed minimal variation in estimates after excluding participants residing more than 100 km from the hospital, restricting cases to those diagnosed within 6 months before the interview, or sequentially excluding control groups by admission diagnosis (online supplemental Table 4). After propensity score matching, nearly all matching covariate subgroups (90%) showed adequate balance (SMDs for all <0.1) except for age (SMD=0.15). Discussion

This hospital-based case–control study demonstrated that smoking was associated with a lower risk of PD in the Chinese population, consistent with findings from previous studies in both Asian and non-Asian populations. Using the same control group, evidence suggestive positive associations with cigarette smoking were observed for ALS and MSA, but no clear exposure-response relationships were observed.

We observed a potential positive association between smoking and ALS risk, which is in contrast to a previous case–control study in a Chinese population that reported a significantly lower ALS risk among ever-smokers (OR=0.47, 95% CI 0.27 to 0.82).29 On the other hand, a recent meta-analysis of studies from both Asian and Western populations suggested that smoking may increase ALS risk,30 and Mendelian randomisation studies in Western populations further supported a causal relationship.31 Although our study did not identify a clear dose-response association, most observed ORs were above unity, aligning with identified increased risks.

Our findings on smoking and MSA risk were consistent with prior case–control studies conducted in Korean populations,32 which also report an over unity but non-statistical significant increased risk for ever-smokers. However, we observed an increased MSA risk among former smokers compared with never-smokers. This finding may be influenced by long latency periods and reverse causality, as MSA symptoms often begin years before diagnosis, with an average latency of 4.2±2.6 years.33 In contrast, a prior case–control study in a Chinese population reported a significantly reduced MSA risk among ever-smokers (OR=0.13, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.42).34 The discrepancy between studies may be attributed to the small sample size of the previous study (55 cases and 76 controls) and the use of healthy controls recruited from a health examination centre, which may have introduced selection bias.

We observed an inverse association between smoking and PD risk, with a stronger effect observed among current smokers compared with former smokers. The lower risk of PD among ever-smokers is further supported by the exposure-response relationship of smoking duration with the risk of PD. These findings align with previous longitudinal studies conducted in both Asian22 and Western populations35 36 and support the hypothesis that smoking may have a neuroprotective effect against PD. The underlying mechanisms have been widely investigated, with potential explanations including nicotine-mediated inhibition of the striatal dopamine transporter, as well as the effects of non-nicotine cigarette components such as monoamine oxidase inhibitors and antioxidant response activation.19 37 Alternative non-causative explanations have also been proposed, including premorbid personality and behavioural traits such as lower novelty-seeking, higher harm-avoidance, and reduced risk-taking tendencies in individuals predisposed to PD,38 which may decrease the likelihood of smoking initiation or persistence. However, despite this observed association, smoking remains the leading preventable cause of premature death,39 and its harmful health effects far outweigh any potential benefits in reducing PD risk. Moreover, studies on smoking and PD prognosis have not demonstrated a significant association between smoking and survival in PD patients.40

We highlight several strengths of the current study. First, the sample size is relatively large for neurodegenerative diseases, especially for rare diseases like ALS and MSA in the Chinese population. Second, the sensitivity analyses that excluded control groups one by one according to their diagnosis for admission to the hospital yielded nearly identical results. These analyses added robustness to our primary findings. Third, the study carefully adjusted for potential confounding factors, and residual confounding was also addressed through propensity score matching, and results in the matched analyses were generally in line with the analyses of the total population.

Our study had several limitations. First, the inherent nature of the case–control design is prone to potential recall bias and reverse causality. However, this design is efficient for investigating rare diseases such as ALS and MSA. Second, the long latency periods and complex progression of neurodegenerative diseases may lead to diagnostic challenges, possible misclassification and reverse causality. Nonetheless, all cases in this study were newly diagnosed by experienced neurologists, and diagnostic misclassification was minimised by thorough evaluation of any revision of diagnosis in the period between enrolment and data analyses (>3 years). Third, smoking intensity was based on participants’ self-reported average daily cigarette consumption, without accounting for changes over time, which may have led to exposure misclassification. However, the consistency of our findings for PD with prior robust studies supports the validity of both our exposure assessment and outcome ascertainment. Another limitation is the age difference between cases and controls, as PD and MSA cases were, on average, 5 years older than controls in the main analysis. Propensity score matching generally balanced age and other covariates, and the results from the conditional logistic regression analysis showed minimal differences. Given that strong temporal trends in smoking habits are unlikely, this mismatch is expected to have had a limited impact on the study’s findings. However, the results should be interpreted with caution, as residual confounding or unmeasured confounding (eg, genetic factors, detailed occupational or environmental exposures like passive smoking exposures, or potential interactions between gene and environmental factors) may have distorted the observed associations.

Conclusions

This study supports that smoking is associated with a lower risk of PD in the Chinese population, consistent with findings from non-Asian populations. For ALS and MSA, we found evidence suggestive of positive relationships with cigarette smoking, but no clear exposure-response relationships were observed. Sensitivity analyses, including propensity score matching, generally supported the robustness of our findings. These findings highlight disease-specific effects of smoking and the need for further research in Asian populations.
