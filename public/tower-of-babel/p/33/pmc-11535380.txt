IGF2/IGFBP4 reduces apoptosis and increases free cholesterol of chicken granulosa cells in vitro
Authors: Wenhui Zhang, Xuejiao Chen, Axiu Guo, Zongyi Zhao, Bo Zhang, Fuwei Li, Hao Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollicle selection, a crucial step in maintaining continuous egg production in chickens, is a process that relies on granulosa cells (GCs). In this study, we aimed to identify the key genes that are involved in follicle selection from our previous single-cell transcriptomic data. We used a combination of techniques and assays, including quantitative real-time PCR, immunofluorescence, Oil Red O staining, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), monodansylcadaverine (MDC) assay, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay, cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay, and 5-ethynyl-2-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay. Multiple indices, such as cell proliferation, cell differentiation, progesterone synthesis, lipid droplet production, total and free cholesterol content, apoptosis, and autophagy, were measured to determine the states of GCs in vitro. The results demonstrated that overexpression of genes related to insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF2) or IGF-binding protein 4 (IGFBP4) increases intracellular free cholesterol (progesterone precursors) and lipid droplet production, inhibits apoptosis through increased autophagy, and inhibits cell proliferation. This indicates that IGF2 or IGFBP4 can maintain the survival state and improve differentiation tendency of chicken granulosa cells in vitro. Therefore, this study provides new evidence on the functions of IGFs and IGFBPs in chickens, establishing a crucial experimental foundation for understanding the regulatory mechanisms of follicle selection. In addition, our study contributes to understanding follicular development and improves the egg-laying performance of chickens.Keywords: Chicken, Follicle selection, Single-cell transcriptome, IGF2, IGFBP4

Introduction

Chickens and eggs are important protein sources for daily needs; therefore, increasing egg production has long been a concern for breeders. Commercial hens typically lay nearly one egg daily during their peak laying period, but extending their productive lifespan remains challenging. Furthermore, due to the improved quality of human life, there has been a growing demand for premium local chicken breeds and also for diversification. This trend offers promising prospects for improving the egg production and longevity of egg-laying periods in local chicken breeds.

The egg production performance of poultry is closely tied to follicular development. In each laying cycle (usually ∼ 24 h), a dominant small yellow follicle (SYF) is selected to develop into a hierarchical follicle (F6) in a process known as follicle selection (Johnson, 2015; Onagbesan et al., 2009). This continuous and sequential process is crucial for ensuring high productivity. Granulosa cells (GCs) are the somatic cells that play an important role in determining the fate of follicles. Following selection, the surface of the follicle produces develops dense blood vessels, the rapid deposition of the yolk causes the volume of the follicle to increase rapidly, and the proliferation and steroidogenic ability of GCs are significantly enhanced. To date, numerous genes related to steroid hormone synthesis (Huang et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2021b), angiogenesis (Devesa and Caicedo, 2019; Duffy et al., 2019), and yolk deposition have been identified in follicle selection (Lin et al., 2019). However, our understanding of this complex process still contains many gaps, and the genes that switch follicle selection are currently being investigated.

Insulin-like growth factors (IGFs) and IGF-binding proteins (IGFBPs) serve as primary regulators of cell growth and proliferation. These proteins are reported to be widely involved in physiological and pathological processes in animals (Piau et al., 2023; Song et al., 2021). The role of the IGFs and IGFBPs family in the ovary has attracted close attention in both mammals and poultry (Piau Rodrigues and Paulini, 2023; Yang et al., 2024). However, research on this topic has been more extensive in mammals compared to poultry. To date, the IGFs and IGFBPs family has not been fully elucidated, particularly in chickens. Most studies have primarily focused on IGF1(Ahmadi and Ohkubo, 2022; Ahumada-Solórzano et al., 2016; Hrabia et al., 2012), while other members of this family have received relatively less attention (Mohammadi and Ansari-Pirsaraie, 2016; Sirotkin and Grossmann, 2006). Therefore, the functions and regulatory mechanisms of IGFs (IGF1 and IGF2) and IGFBPs (IGFBP1, IGFBP2, IGFBP3, IGFBP4, IGFBP5, IGFBP7) family remain to be fully characterized.

In our unpublished single-cell transcriptome analysis, numerous genes differentially expressed during follicle selection were found to be enriched in members of the IGFs and IGFBPs families. Importantly, IGF2 and IGFBP4 showed high scores. qRT-PCR results also preliminarily indicate a significant up-regulation of IGF2 and IGFBP4 in GCs after follicle selection, suggesting their potential role in promoting follicle selection. Therefore, in this study, we conducted a preliminary functional exploration of these two genes in vitro to assess the regulatory mechanisms of the IGFs and IGFBPs families. We aimed to provide a scientific basis for understanding follicle selection dynamics and improving egg production performance.

Materials and methods

Detailed reagents are provided in supplementary Table S1

Animals and ethics statement

All chickens used in this experiment were yellow-bearded chickens (Zheng, et al., 2023), which were raised at the Experimental Chicken Farm of China Agricultural University and given free access to food and water. Experiments were performed during the laying period. Ten 45-weeks old hens (healthy, stable laying) with consistent egg production records were selected, and three of them with consistent follicle order were sacrificed for scRNA-Seq. The hens were all sacrificed around 3:00 p.m. to control the variables. All experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Welfare Committee of the State Key Laboratory for Agro-Biotechnology of the China Agricultural University (Permit Number: XK257).

SeekOne® MM single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and data analysis

SeekOne®MM (Microwell & Magnetic Beads) scRNA-seq was performed by Beijing SeekGene BioSciences Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Prehierarchical follicles (small white follicles (SWF)) and (SYF), hierarchical follicles (F6), and atretic follicles (AFs) (atretic small white follicles (ASWF) and atretic small yellow follicles (ASYF)) were collected from 3 hens that were 45 weeks old. All GC layers were peeled off, and single-cell suspensions were prepared for sequencing. The data were clustered using a graph-clustering algorithm (Butler, et al., 2018). Nonlinear dimensionality reduction methods such as UMAP or T-SNE were used to visualize the data. The differentially expressed genes IGF2 and IGFBP4 were screened in our unpublished study.

Construction of plasmid vectors

The vectors were constructed using homologous recombination. The coding sequences (CDs) of IGF2 (NM_001030342.5) and IGFBP4 (NM_204353.1) were amplified from chicken follicle cDNA and inserted between the NheI and HindⅢ restriction sites. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1. The successfully constructed vectors were named pcDNA3.1-IGF2-3×flag and pcDNA3.1-IGFBP4-3×flag. The pcDNA3.1-3× FLAG was stored in our laboratory.

Cell isolation and culture

SYFs were collected and placed in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the GC layers were peeled off under a stereomicroscope to ensure purity. The GC layers were digested in 0.25% type II collagenase (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) (approximately 5 min), filtered, washed with PBS 3 times, and resuspended in a single-cell suspension.

SYF-GC suspensions were seeded in culture plates, which were precoated with rat tail collagen (liquid in 0.02 N acetic acid) (Corning, NY, USA) and cultured at 37°C in 5% CO2. When the cells reached approximately 60% confluence, the plasmids were transfected. The following groups were established: Control (transfected with pcDNA3.1-3×FLAG control plasmid), OE-IGF2 (transfected with pcDNA3.1-IGF2-3×FLAG plasmid), and OE-IGFBP4 (transfected with pcDNA3.1-IGFBP4-3×FLAG plasmid).

Quantitative real time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The cells were collected for RNA extraction every 24 h after transfection. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). The FastKing gDNA Dispelling RT SuperMix Kit (TIANGEN) was used for reverse transcription. The 2× Universal SYBR Green qPCR Premix (Accurate Biotechnology, Co. Ltd., Changsha, China) was used for qRT-PCR. GAPDH was used as the putative reference gene. All primer sequences are shown in Table 2. Data were analyzed using the 2‐ΔΔCt method.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence assays were performed on the SYF-GCs 24 h after seeding. After discarding the medium, the cells were washed 3 times with PBS, and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA)/PBS solution for 15 min at room temperature (RT, ∼25°C), then washed 3 times with PBS for 5 mins each, 0.1% Triton X-100/PBS for 15 mins at RT, blocked for 1 h with 1% BSA/PBS (Beyotime Biotechnology, Nanjing, China) at RT, incubated in primary antibody rabbit anti-FSHR (1:500, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) for overnight at 4°C, washed 3 times with PBS for 5 mins each, incubated in secondary antibody goat anti-rabbit IgG/HRP (FITC labeled, 1:1000) (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 2 h at RT in the dark, washed 3 times with PBS, stained with Hoechst for 15 mins. Green fluorescence was observed under a fluorescence microscope (RVL-100-G, ECHO, California, United States)

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Cells were collected 48 and 72 h after transfection. Progesterone concentration was determined using the Chicken PROG ELISA Kit (JINGMEI Biotechnology, Yancheng, China). Progesterone concentration was measured at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer (Biotek, Winooski, VT, USA), normalized to cell protein concentration, and determined by comparing the optical densities (OD) to the standard curve.

Oil red o staining

The cells were fixed with 4% PFA/PBS for 30 min, washed thrice with PBS, and stained with a 0.3% Oil Red O solution (0.3 g in 100 ml isopropanol) obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) for 15 min at RT. After staining, cells were washed thrice with PBS followed by a brief wash with 60% isopropanol for 10 s. They were then photographed under a microscope (RVL-100-G, ECHO, California, United States) and further washed with 100% isopropanol for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, and the color change was measured spectrophotometrically at wavelengths of 490–500 nm.

Monodansylcadaverine (MDC) assay

Cells collected for autophagy assays at 48 and 72 h after transfection were processed according to the manufacturer's protocol using the Autophagy Staining Assay Kit with MDC (Beyotime Biotechnology, Nanjing, China). Following removal of the cell medium, cells were washed once with PBS and then stained with MDC solution at 37°C in the dark for 60 min. After staining, the Blot MDC solution was removed, and cells were washed 3 times with Assay Buffer. Green fluorescence was observed under a fluorescence microscope (RVL-100-G, ECHO, California, United States).

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay

Cell samples were collected at 48-h timepoint, and cell suspensions were prepared using a One Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Nanjing, China) to quantify apoptosis. The cell suspension was washed once with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for 30 mins, washed again with PBS, and then incubated with the TUNEL solution at 37°C in the dark for 60 mins, washed with PBS twice, resuspended again with PBS, and detected by flow cytometry. The excitation wavelength range was 450–500 nm, and the emission wavelength range was 515–565 nm.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

After removing the medium, the cells were prefixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde, scraped gently, collected by centrifugation, postfixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde, treated with 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated with a gradient of alcohol and acetone, embedded in resin, and sectioned. Autophagosomes were detected by TEM (HT7800; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

A Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was used to detect cell proliferation at 6, 9, 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36 h after transfection, according to the manufacturer's instructions (Beyotime Biotechnology, Nanjing, China). The cells were seeded into 96-well plates, and CCK-8 solution was added to each well at the corresponding time. The hole without cells was set as a blank control, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm after incubation for 2 h.

5-ethynyl-2-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay

5-ethynyl-2-deoxyuridine (EdU) staining was performed at 24 h when proliferation differences were most significant, according to the protocol of the Cell-Light EdU Apollo567 In Vitro Kit (Ribbio, Guangzhou, China). The cells were cultured in 50 μM EdU medium for 5 h and then the medium was discarded. The cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min, incubated with glycine for 5 min for decolorization, washed with PBS for 5 min, incubated with 0.5% Triton X-100/PBS for 10 min, washed with PBS for 5 min, and stained using apollo solution in the dark for 30 min. After washing with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS 3 times, the nuclei were stained with Hoechst stain for 30 min. The cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope (RVL-100-G, ECHO, California, United States).

Total and free cholesterol assay

The concentrations of total and free cholesterol in the cells (in vitro experiments) were assayed using tissue total cholesterol (TC) and tissue-free cholesterol (FC) content assay kits (Applygen Technologies Inc., Beijing, China). All experiments were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical analysis

Values are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean. One-way Analysis of Variance followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test was used to compare the statistical data between groups that conformed to a Gaussian distribution and had equal standard deviation (SDs). Non-parametric tests followed by Dunn's multiple comparisons were applied if the data did not conform to a Gaussian distribution. If the data conformed to a Gaussian distribution, but the SDs were not equal, Brown-Forsythe and Welch ANOVA tests followed by Dunnett's T3 multiple comparisons were used. Student's paired t-tests were used to determine the significance of the differences between 2 groups. GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 software (La Jolla, CA, USA) was used to plot statistical data. P values of <0.05 were considered significant (ns = not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001).

Results

Screening of IGF2/IGFBP4 during follicle selection

Based on the results of the single-cell transcriptome analysis, IGF2 and IGFBP4 were identified as candidate switch genes for follicle selection. In cell clustering, both genes were highly expressed in the clusters of F6 (Fig. 1a–d). The mRNA expression of IGF2 and IGFBP4 was significantly upregulated after follicle selection, whereas the expression levels remained lower in atretic and prehierarchical follicles (Fig. 1, Fig. 1). This observation suggested that IGF2 and IGFBP4 likely play an important role in follicle selection. To verify their roles, IGF2 and IGFBP4 were overexpressed in vitro using transfection vectors in SYF-GCs. qRT-PCR results confirmed significant upregulation of these target genes in the OE-IGF2 and OE-IGFBP4 groups (Fig. 1, Fig. 1), indicating successful overexpression of IGF2 and IGFBP4 using the vectors.

Overexpression of IGF2/IGFBP4 inhibits proliferation and progesterone synthesis of GCs in vitro

The isolated SYF-GCs were identified by positive expression of the marker gene follicle-stimulating hormone receptor (FSHR), indicating that the isolated cells were indeed GCs (Fig. 2a). Over a period of 0, 24, 48, and 72 h post-transfection, there were no observable differences in cell density between the experimental groups (Fig. 2b). Due to the enhanced proliferation and progesterone synthesis ability of GCs post-follicle selection, the mRNA expression of proliferation marker genes cyclin D1 (CCND1) and cyclin B1 (CCNB1) and key genes involved in progesterone synthesis, including steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (StAR), Cytochrome P450 family 11 subfamily A member 1 (Cyp11a1) and Hydroxy-delta-5-steroid dehydrogenase, 3 beta- and steroid delta-isomerase 1 (Hsd3b1) were detected by qRT-PCR. The results showed significant downregulation of these marker genes in the overexpression group at 48 h (Fig. 2c–f). Further confirming these findings, the cell proliferation levels were assessed using the CCK-8 assay at 6, 9, 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36 h post-transfection. The proliferation rate of the OE-IGF2 and OE-IGFBP4 groups was significantly lower compared to that of the control group at 18 and 24 h timepoints (Fig. 3a). Similar results were obtained with EdU staining after 24 h (Fig. 3, Fig. 3). Progesterone detection results showed significantly lower concentrations in the OE groups than that in the control group at 72 h (Fig. 3, Fig. 3). These results confirm that the overexpression of IGF2 or IGFBP4 alone can inhibit the proliferation and differentiation of GCs.

Overexpression of IGF2/IGFBP4 can inhibit apoptosis and increase autophagy of GCs in vitro

To facilitate the observation of cell death, fresh medium was regularly added but not replaced throughout the culture process. Examination of cell morphology revealed that the number of suspended dead cells in the overexpression group at 48 h was lower than that in the control group. However, by 72 h, the number of suspended dead cells was significantly higher (Fig. 4a). This phenotype suggests that the overexpression of IGF2 or IGFBP4 could inhibit cell death. To further validate this result, samples were collected at 24 h and 48 h for qRT-PCR analysis of the apoptosis marker genes Caspase3 and fas cell surface death receptor (Fas), as well as the autophagy marker genes microtubule associated protein 1 light chain 3 beta (Map1lc3b) and Beclin1. The results showed downregulation of apoptosis genes and upregulation of autophagy genes (Fig. 4b–e). Apoptosis was detected using the TUNEL assay, and flow cytometry confirmed that the proportion of apoptotic cells in the OE-IGF2 and OE-IGFBP4 groups was significantly lower compared to that in the control group, which was consistent with the cell morphology analysis (Fig. 4, Fig. 4). The autophagy level of the cells was detected by the MDC assay, which showed a significant increase in autophagy in the overexpression group at 48 h and 72 h (Fig. 5a). TEM results confirmed this observation, showing an increased number of autophagosomes in the overexpression group (Fig. 5b). Furthermore, TEM indicated that the mitochondria in the control group were enlarged, the mitochondrial ridge became blurred, and the nuclear membranes and nucleoli were broken. In contrast, mitochondria in the overexpression groups appeared normal, with clear and intact nuclear membranes and nucleolar boundaries. These observations suggest that the overexpression of IGF2 or IGFBP4 helps cells resist apoptosis by promoting autophagy, including mitochondrial autophagy. Taken together, these results confirm that the overexpression of IGF2 or IGFBP4 can inhibit apoptosis and promote autophagy in GCs.

Overexpression of IGF2/IGFBP4 can increase free cholesterol and lipid droplets in GCs

Previous studies have shown that the overexpression of IGF2 or IGFBP4 reduces progesterone synthesis in GCs. To determine the reason for this phenotype, we measured intracellular cholesterol content and lipid droplet production. Intracellular free cholesterol is a substrate for progesterone synthesis, with excess stored as lipid droplets for cell development after follicle selection. Oil Red O staining confirmed a significant increase in lipid droplet formation in GCs after follicle selection (Fig. 5c). In vitro, lipid droplets also showed a slight increase in the overexpression group (Fig. 5, Fig. 5), but not as pronounced as in F6-GCs. Analysis of TC and FC showed no significant differences in TC across all groups at 48 and 72 h, while FC significantly increased in the overexpression group by 72 h (Fig. 5, Fig. 5). These results indicate that overexpression of IGF2 or IGFBP4 did not affect the content of intracellular total cholesterol but enhanced free cholesterol levels and lipid droplet production.

Discussion

During poultry follicle development, GCs play a crucial role in determining the fate of the follicles. In numerous transcriptome and proteome analyses (Fan et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2021a; Zhou et al., 2020), researchers have attempted to identify key genes that govern follicle selection. Although many genes have been implicated in follicle selection, they often present the outcome rather than the cause of follicle selection. For example, well-known reproductive hormones like estradiol (E2), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), anti-mullerian hormone (AMH) (Huang et al., 2021; Jing et al., 2018; Lemcke et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2021b), and key rate-limiting enzymes for hormone synthesis like StAR, HSD3B1, CYP11A1, and cytochrome P450 family 19 subfamily A member 1 (CYP19A1) (Jing et al., 2018; Sechman et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2021b) are frequently observed in studies of GC differentiation and follicular development. Despite their utility as makers, these genes are limited in pinpointing switch genes due to the significant genetic variability among GCs. To address this issue, our laboratory has previously carried out single-cell transcriptome analysis in the early stages, hoping to find the switch genes of follicle selection more accurately.

The single-cell transcriptome results revealed enrichment of genes in the IGFs and IGFBPs families, which piqued our interest in this family. Previous studies have linked this family to follicle development (Devesa and Caicedo, 2019; Piau et al., 2023), particularly in their role in follicle selection through paracrine mechanisms (Kristensen et al., 2018). Several decades ago, researchers became interested in exploring the role of the IGFs and IGFBPs family in chicken ovaries (Onagbesan et al., 2009; Onagbesan et al., 1999), but most research efforts have predominantly focused on investigating IGF1. In chickens, it has been reported that IGF1 can increase ovulation rate (Kang et al., 2003), promote primordial follicular growth (Ahmadi and Ohkubo, 2022), stimulate estrogen synthesis (Hrabia et al., 2012), maintain granulosa cell and theca externa cell proliferation (Ahumada-Solórzano et al., 2016; Jia et al., 2013; Onagbesan and Peddie, 1995), as well as exhibit gene polymorphism related to reproductive traits (Kim et al., 2004; Li et al., 2009). More recently, a study revealed that differential genes between high- and low-production laying hens' follicles were significantly enriched within the IGFs family (Yang et al., 2024). However, the functions of other members such as IGF2, IGFBP1, IGFBP2, IGFBP3, IGFBP4, IGFBP5 and IGFBP7 have rarely been reported, and their roles remain unclear.

In this study, we conducted a preliminary exploration of the functions of IGF2 and IGFBP4, which scored higher in our analysis. Previous studies have explored the role of IGF2 in various mammal species. In bovines, IGF2 promotes GC proliferation, with mRNA expression in IGF2 in small follicles being 3 times higher than that in large follicles (Spicer and Aad, 2007). Similarly, IGF2 promotes GC proliferation and follicle development in macaque monkeys (Tkachenko et al., 2021). However, in humans, the mRNA expression of IGF2 increased with follicle development (Botkjær et al., 2019), but has no effect on progesterone production in brushtail possums (Juengel et al., 2010). Although there are few studies on IGFBP4, some studies suggest that it is involved in follicle development in mammals (Rubio et al., 2021; Xiong et al., 2017), and its expression patterns differ among different species (Ji et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2003). However, the specific functions of IGF2 and IGFBP4 in chicken follicles have not yet been investigated.

There are significant differences in the developmental processes and regulatory mechanisms of follicles between mammals and birds. For example, researchers have compared the transcriptome data from cattle and chicken follicles and found that cattle follicles at the differentiation stage post-selection were comparable to the SYF in chickens during the selection stage (Jing et al., 2018). In mammals, estrogen is synthesized by granulosa cells, with forkhead box L2 (Foxl2) serving as an early marker and regulator of ovarian granulosa cells (Yoshino et al., 2021). However, in chickens, estrogen is synthesized by the theca cells (TCs), indicating that Foxl2 may not be involved in estrogen biosynthesis in chickens (Wang et al., 2017). These results suggest that many genes involved in follicular development in poultry differ from their mammalian counterparts. Given the importance of the IGFs and IGFBPs families in follicular development and their variability across species, investigating the roles and functions of IGF2 and IGFBP4 in chickens is crucial.

Our in vitro results show that overexpression of either IGF2 or IGFBP4 alone inhibits GCs proliferation and differentiation, which is inconsistent with our sequencing results showing high expression of IGF2 and IGFBP4 in F6 follicles. However, it is worth noting that IGF2 and IGFBP4 can significantly inhibit each other's mRNA expression. Mutual inhibition of these 2 genes is a common phenomenon in organisms (Burda et al., 2016). These observations suggest a potential threshold that balances their respective functions. For example, AMH can affect follicle selection and P4 synthesis by changing the FSH threshold (Huang et al., 2021). Moreover, overexpression of IGF2 and IGFBP4 promotes the accumulation of lipid droplets and intracellular free cholesterol, which are essential precursors for progesterone synthesis. However, progesterone was decreased. This observation indicates that while the overexpression of IGF2 and IGFBP4 tends promote GCs differentiation, other factors affect the conversion of free cholesterol into progesterone, therefore, the concentration of progesterone did not increase. Overcompensation for the sudden stimulation of cells prevents the overexpression of IGF2 or IGFBP4 from promoting GCs differentiation, suggesting that these two genes may be the causative, rather than consequential in follicle selection. In addition, the overexpression of IGF2 or IGFBP4 can inhibit GCs apoptosis and promote autophagy. Autophagy and apoptosis mutually inhibit each other (He et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2022), with autophagy helping cells renew their aging organelles and cytoplasm to resist apoptosis. Autophagy is necessary for maintaining follicular growth and differentiation (Zhang et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2019). These results indicate that although overexpression of IGF2 or IGFBP4 alone may not directly promote GCs differentiation, GCs adept to survive and progress toward differentiation, which also indicates the complexity of follicle development, and the fate of follicles is influenced by multiple factors rather than a single gene. However, the regulatory mechanism between IGF2 and IGFBP4 needs to be further demonstrated, and we will expand the study of the IGFs and IGFBPs families in future investigations to refine the exact regulatory mechanism between them in chickens.

Conclusion

Overall, the results demonstrated that the overexpression of IGF2 or IGFBP4 increased the content of intracellular free cholesterol (progesterone precursors) and the production of lipid droplets. Further, it inhibited cell apoptosis by increasing autophagy and inhibited cell proliferation (Fig. 6). This indicates that IGF2 or IGFBP4 can maintain the survival state and improve differentiation tendency of chicken granulosa cells in vitro. This process provides a novel framework for assessing GCs, indicating that detecting the final differentiation indicators such as progesterone alone may not fully explain GC growth. This study provides new insights into the functional role of IGFs and IGFBPs in chickens and provides an important experimental foundation for investigating regulatory mechanisms. It is expected to explore the mutual regulatory mechanism of IGF2 and IGFBP4 to find the causes for follicle selection. Moreover, it holds significant scientific merit for advancing follicular development and improving chicken egg-laying performance.
