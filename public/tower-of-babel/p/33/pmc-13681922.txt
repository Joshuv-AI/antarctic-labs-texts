Caring while aging: a cross-sectional study in Lebanon on caregiver burden, mental health, and the moderating effects of social and financial resources
Authors: Monique Chaaya, Sawsan Abdulrahim, Aya El Sammak, Alexandra Abi Nassif, Mayssan Kabalan, Suzanne Charbaji, Patricia Arkhondis, Hala Ghattas, Hala Ghattas, Martine Elbejjani, Fredrick Conrad, Stephen J. McCall, Raphael Nishimura, Abla M. Sibai, Carlos F. de Leon Mendes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA growing number of older adults provide care for spouses or relatives, yet the mental health impact of caregiving, particularly while managing their own health conditions, remains poorly understood. In Lebanon, where formal long-term care services remain limited and caregiving is largely family-based, there is a need to better understand how caregiver burden affects the mental health of older adult caregivers. Therefore, this study examined the association between caregiver burden and mental health among older adult caregivers in Lebanon and the moderating role of social and financial resources.MethodsWe used baseline data from the Lebanon Study on Aging and HeAlth (LSAHA), a community-based prospective study of older adults aged ≥ 60 years, which included 434 caregivers. Multivariable linear regression models examined associations between caregiver burden (12-item Zarit Burden Interview) and depression (eight-item Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale) and anxiety (seven-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale), with interaction terms testing the moderating effects of social (emotional and instrumental support) and financial resources.ResultsCaregivers were predominantly female (61.8%), with a mean age of 69 years (± 6.9), and one-third cared for their spouse (33.5%). Those with high caregiver burden, reported by 36.4%, had depression scores that were 7.65 points higher (95% CI: 3.48 to 11.8) and anxiety scores that were 2.84 points higher (95% CI: 0.73 to 4.95) compared to caregivers with low burden. Emotional support significantly attenuated the association between caregiver burden and depression. Financial resources did not moderate the association between caregiver burden and mental health outcomes.ConclusionsThis study underscores the public health need for addressing caregiver burden and mental health among understudied older caregivers. Although financial assistance, psychosocial services, and respite care have reduced caregiver burden in other settings, context-specific services and policies in Lebanon warrant evaluation. Future research should account for care recipients’ characteristics and the multidimensional nature of social and financial resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-08017-1.

Introduction

Background

Family caregiving, often referred to as informal caregiving, is expanding rapidly and has become a public health concern as populations age and long-term care needs rise [1]. Caregiver burden is associated with psychological distress, physical health problems, and reduced quality of life [1, 2]. Among older adult caregivers, these adverse effects may be shaped by caregiving demands, functional limitations, limited social support, and financial hardship. The latter two factors are particularly salient in Lebanon, where older adults often assume caregiving roles within strained economic and social systems, potentially amplifying the mental health impact of caregiver burden.

Globally, the number of adults older than 60 years of age is projected to reach 2.1 billion by 2050, with 80% of this demographic is expected to reside in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [3]. As populations age, the need for care and help with activities of daily living among older adults will increase, with the World Health Organization (WHO) estimating that roughly two thirds of older adults will need caregiving support. In high-income countries, formal social care institutions absorb more of the burden [4, 5]. “Aging in place” policies in high-income countries advance a model in which state services complement care provided to the older adult by family and other informal caregivers [6]. In many LMICs, however, high costs of private institutional care, together with limited or unreliable social protection systems, mean that older adults will continue to rely on family and informal caregivers for social care [7–9].

With population aging, an increasing number of older adults are providing care to spouses, family members, or other relatives [10]. This creates dual demands as older adults must manage their own age-related health challenges while simultaneously responding to the needs of equally or more dependent loved ones. Such dual demands could intensify caregiving strain and heighten susceptibility to psychological distress. Caregiver burden has consistently been associated with poorer mental health outcomes, including depression and anxiety, physical health problems, and reduced quality of life [1, 2]. Although these associations have been documented globally, including in studies conducted in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region [11, 12], relatively few investigations have focused specifically on older adult caregivers [8, 13, 14]. Existing evidence from these studies suggests that higher caregiver burden is associated with increased risk of depression and poorer mental health among older caregivers. Prior research further indicates that the mental health impact of caregiving is not uniform across caregivers. Women, spouses, and those providing more intensive or prolonged care appear to experience a disproportionate burden, with spouses often serving as the primary caregivers in later life [15, 16]. For example, a study from China found that providing more than 10 h per week of spousal care was associated with higher depressive symptoms, particularly among women and those living in rural areas, while higher education and being married were protective [17, 18]. Similar patterns have been observed among Mexican American caregivers aged 65 years and older, underscoring the importance of both caregiving intensity and broader social context in shaping mental health outcomes [13].

Beyond caregiving demands, broader social and economic conditions play a critical role in influencing caregivers’ psychological wellbeing. Particularly, social support and financial stability consistently emerge as key determinants of caregiver mental health [19]. Emotional and instrumental support from family and friends has been shown to reduce psychological distress and buffer the negative effects of caregiving [20, 21]. In contrast, unemployment, low income, financial hardship, and the economic strain associated with caring for individuals with serious health conditions are linked to worse mental health outcomes among caregivers [22–25]. Some studies suggest that social support and financial resources may shape how caregiving stress translates into mental health problems, potentially buffering or exacerbating the effects of caregiver burden [26, 27]. However, relatively few studies have examined these factors as effect modifiers when caregiver burden is treated as the primary exposure, rather than as an outcome [2, 21, 28]. While social support consistently appears to be protective, the role of financial resources remains insufficiently explored, particularly in under-resourced settings.

Caring while aging is therefore an increasingly important public health issue, especially in contexts where mental health services and social protection systems are limited. Lebanon provides a particularly salient context given its rapidly aging population, prolonged economic crisis, constrained mental health infrastructure, and traditionally strong but increasingly strained social networks. Guided by two conceptual models, the Sociocultural Stress and Coping Model and the Pinquart and Sörensen caregiving stress model [29, 30], we conceptualize caregiver burden as a central stressor whose impact on mental health is shaped by caregivers’ background characteristics, caregiving demands, and available coping resources. The Sociocultural Stress and Coping Model emphasizes the indirect role of culture in shaping stress appraisal, coping strategies, and the meaning and availability of social support. The Pinquart and Sörensen model highlights how social and ethnic differences influence background factors, stress exposure, coping mechanisms, and baseline physical and mental health of caregivers.

We conceptualize caregiver burden as an exposure rather than solely as an outcome and account for caregiving characteristics that may confound its association with mental health. These include caregivers’ sociodemographic factors (age, sex assigned at birth, marital status, education, and region), health characteristics (functional limitations and comorbidities), and caregiving patterns (frequency and intensity of care, relationship to and co-residence with the care recipient). While existing models often treat financial and social resources as background variables, we extend this framework by considering both of them as key contextual moderators that may buffer or exacerbate the effects of caregiver burden on depression and anxiety.

Methods

Data source

The population of interest for this study consists of older adult caregivers, defined as participants aged 60 years and above, who provide personal care to household members or to individuals outside their household.

Data was drawn from the baseline assessment of the Lebanon Study on Aging and HeAlth (LSAHA), a prospective, community-based study of older adults residing in two areas in Lebanon: Beirut, the capital city, and Zahle, which is situated in the country’s Eastern region. These two areas were selected a priori to capture Lebanon’s urban and rural contexts and its diverse socioeconomic and religious make-up. The study, carried out in 2023–2024, aimed to establish a comprehensive baseline assessment of older adults’ physical, mental, and cognitive health, as well as a range of aging-related social factors including caregiving and care-receiving. The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board at the American University of Beirut (BIO-2023–0093). Details of the study design, sampling procedures, data collection, and response rates are described elsewhere [31].

Out of a total of 3,027 individuals surveyed, 486 reported providing personal care to household members or to individuals outside their household. We then excluded those who provided care to grandchildren (n = 45), those who did not complete the caregiver burden scale (n = 4), and those who did not answer the mental health scales (n = 3). Measures

Outcome variables

The two mental health outcome measures assessed are depression and anxiety. Depression was assessed using the 8-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression scale (CES-D 8), which was validated in Arabic [32] and among older adults, demonstrating good psychometric properties [33]. Respondents rated how frequently they experienced symptoms such as sadness, loneliness, and restless sleep in the past week on a 4-point Likert scale from 0= rarely/none to 3 = most/all of the time. A sum score was then calculated to create a total CES-D 8 score ranging from 0 to 24 for individuals with at least three non-missing values. Anxiety was measured using the Arabic version of the Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7), which has demonstrated adequate psychometric properties and is considered a reliable tool for assessing generalized anxiety symptoms in Arabic-speaking populations [34]. Participants reported on how often they experienced seven anxiety symptoms (e.g., nervousness or anxiousness, irritability, excessive worry) over the past two weeks using a 4-point Likert scale (0 = "Not at all" to 3 = "Nearly every day") and a sum score was calculated to produce a total GAD-7 score ranging from 0 to 21.

Exposure variable

Caregiver burden was measured using the brief version of the Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI), which consists of 12 items [35]. The short form is widely used for quick assessment of caregiver burden, while retaining a strong correlation with the full ZBI. Older adult caregivers indicated how often they experienced each feeling or situation described, such as feeling that they should do more or a better job when caring for the care recipient, feeling uncertain about how to provide care, lacking privacy or social engagement, and feeling strained or stressed when around the care recipient. Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0= "Never" to 4 = "Nearly always". The total scores range from 0 to 48, with a cut-off score of ≥ 13 indicating high burden, based on a validation study on community-dwelling older caregivers [36].

Moderator variables

Social resources were measured through emotional and instrumental social support, which were assessed using the Arabic validated modified Medical Outcomes Study Social Support Survey (MOS-SSS) [37, 38]. Perceived emotional support was measured by asking the respondents how often someone is available to: (1) have a good time with, (2) offer suggestions for dealing with a personal problem, (3) understand their problems, and (4) show love and make them feel wanted. Instrumental support was measured by asking the respondents how often someone is available to: (1) take you to the doctor if you need it and (2) to help with daily chores if you were sick. Each item was assessed on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “None of the time”, 2 = “A little of the time”, 3 = “Some of the time”, 4 = “Most of the time”, to 5 = “All of the time”). A mean score (range: 1 to 5) was calculated by averaging responses across the four emotional support items and the two instrumental support items, with higher scores indicating greater perceived emotional and instrumental support.

Financial resources were measured through income sufficiency, which was assessed subjectively by asking participants whether their household income was sufficient to meet their essential needs, including housing, food, health, electricity, and other necessary expenses. Response options were categorized into two groups: “fully/mostly sufficient” and “barely/not at all sufficient”.

Covariates

Covariates included caregiver’s sociodemographic and health characteristics and caregiving-related characteristics. Sociodemographic variables were sex assigned at birth, age, region (Beirut or Zahle), marital status (categorized as currently married or not married (never married/widowed/divorced)), and education (university level or higher, intermediate to secondary, and primary or less).

Health characteristics included the presence of chronic diseases, categorized as none, one, or at least two. Chronic conditions included hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, hypercholesterolemia, stroke, asthma, cancer, osteoarthritis, and rheumatism. Functional limitations were measured as a binary variable (yes/no), defined by the presence of at least one of the following: difficulty with Activities of Daily Living (ADL) or in Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL), presence of pain, or use of assistive devices. Caregivers without any ADL or IADL limitations were classified as having no functional limitations.

Caregiving-related characteristics included the caregivers’ relationship to the care recipient (spouse vs. non-spouse), intensity of care (measured by the number of hours of care provided in the past week and dichotomized at the median), frequency of care (at least once a day vs. less frequently), and co-residency with the care recipient (yes/no).

Statistical analyses

Bivariate analyses were conducted to examine associations between the main exposure (caregiver burden) and continuous mental health outcomes (depression anxiety) and all covariates. Independent t-tests were used to compare mean depression and anxiety scores across categories of caregiver burden and other categorical covariates, while chi-square tests were used to assess associations between categorical covariates and caregiver burden. Spearman correlation tests were used to assess the association between depression and anxiety scores with the social support variables. The potential moderating role of emotional support, instrumental support, and income sufficiency in the association between caregiver burden and depression and anxiety was examined by testing interaction terms in the multivariable models and by comparing models with and without the interaction terms using a likelihood ratio test; evidence of interaction was defined as p < 0.05. To further examine effect modification, we fitted a survey weighted linear regression model including an interaction term between caregiver burden and emotional support. When no statistical evidence of interaction was detected, the variables were treated as confounders and adjusted for in the final model, as appropriate.

Separate multivariable linear regression models were fitted for each mental health outcome, with caregiver burden as the primary exposure variable adjusted for relevant covariates. A conservative two-tailed p-value threshold of 0.2 in bivariate analyses was used to select potential confounders for inclusion in the multivariable model [39]. Model 2 A and 2B adjusted for age and sex only, while Model 3 A and 3B adjusted for all identified confounders. Both unadjusted and adjusted regression coefficients (βs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. All statistics were weighted to account for the sampling design, clustering, and non-response bias and were calibrated for age and gender distributions of 60 + individuals in Lebanon, as reported by the Central Administration of Statistics [40]. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata-SE 18 [41]. Two-tailed p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Profile of caregivers and non-caregivers

Characteristics of caregivers and non-caregivers are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Among caregivers, the mean age was 69.0 (± 6.9) years, 61.8% were female, and most were married (72.8%), whereas non-caregivers were older on average (72.1 ± 8.2 years), less likely to be female (52.3%), and a lower proportion were married (64.9%). Three quarters of caregivers resided in Beirut (76.0%) and around two in five had intermediate or secondary education (42.6%), while a similar proportion of non‑caregivers resided in Beirut (74.2%) but a slightly higher proportion had primary education or less (42.2%). With respect to financial resources, the proportions reporting barely or not sufficient income were comparable between caregivers (81.1%) and non‑caregivers (77.3%). The proportion of emotional support reported most or all of the time was slightly higher among non‑caregivers (67.2%) than caregivers (64.5%), whereas instrumental support reported most or all of the time was significantly higher among non‑caregivers (76.6%) than caregivers (61.3%). The proportion of having at least two comorbidities was similar in both groups (62.9% among non‑caregivers vs. 68.7% among caregivers), as was the proportion reporting functional limitations (64.4% vs. 65.1%, respectively). Finally, caregivers had significantly higher mean depression and anxiety scores than non‑caregivers on the CES‑D 8 (10.3 vs. 9.5) and GAD‑7 (8.4 vs. 7.2) scales.

Among care recipient characteristics, co-residence was significantly associated with caregiver burden where 46.7% of caregivers living with the care recipient reported high burden compared to 12.5% of those not co-residing. Caregiver burden was also associated with care intensity, with about half of caregivers providing at least 10 h of care per week reporting high burden (49.6%), versus 27.6% among those providing < 10 h of care.

Perceived income sufficiency was significantly associated with caregiver burden, with 40.6% of caregivers whose income was barely/not sufficient reporting high burden compared to only 22.1% of those whose income was fully/mostly sufficient. Functional limitations were significantly associated with caregiver burden, with 42.9% of caregivers with functional limitations experiencing high burden, compared to 24.3% of those without limitations.

Regarding mental health outcomes, caregivers with high burden had significantly higher depression score on the CES-D 8 compared to those with low burden (13.6 vs 8.2, respectively), as well as significantly higher anxiety scores on the GAD-7 (10.8 vs 6.5, respectively).

No significant associations were observed between caregiver burden and region, level of education, relationship to the care recipient, frequency of care, emotional or instrumental support, or the presence of comorbidities.

Association of caregiver burden and covariates with the mental health outcomes

Bivariate analyses testing the associations between sociodemographic variables and caregiving characteristics with mental health outcomes are presented in Table 3. Female caregivers reported significantly higher depression and anxiety scores, while individuals with lower educational attainment only reported significantly higher depression. No significant associations were observed between region or marital status and either outcome. Self-reported income insufficiency was significantly associated with both higher depression and anxiety scores. Discussion

This study is among the very few examining the caregiver burden among older adult caregivers and its association with mental health outcomes globally and regionally [42]. Older caregivers in Lebanon experience multiple vulnerabilities, including declining physical health, financial instability, and limited social support within a strained social system. Despite these challenges, they remain underrepresented in the caregiving literature. Therefore, this study specifically focused on this population. Our study was also the first to investigate the potential moderating effect of social and financial resources. Our findings indicated that high caregiver burden is strongly linked to higher depressive symptoms and anxiety independently of caregiver sociodemographic and health characteristics. Studies consistently show that caregiving is associated with greater burden and also poorer mental health, particularly depression, which appears to show the most robust association among mental health outcomes. Depression in later life has been linked to worse physical health outcomes, such as increased rates of hospitalizations and cardiovascular events [43]. Importantly, this association has been observed across diverse contexts, including both low- and middle-income countries, and both urban and rural areas [43].

Previous literature established a clear link between caregiver burden and anxiety symptoms in the Arab region, particularly among those caring for individuals with dementia [44, 45]. Similarly, a recent meta-analysis of informal caregiving reported a robust association between caregiver burden and anxiety, regardless of the care recipient’s diagnosis or condition [11]. A plausible explanation for the weaker association with anxiety compared to depression in the current study is the high prevalence (29.7%) of benzodiazepine use among older adults aged 60 + in Lebanon [46]. This number likely underestimates the true use when considering non-prescribed consumption, as it does not take into consideration older adults who self-medicate or obtain benzodiazepines without valid prescriptions. Pharmacological masking may therefore attenuate reported anxiety symptoms.

Our results also support the pre-hypothesized moderating effect of social resources. Previous studies have established the buffering effects of resources, in particular social support, when looking at caregiving responsibility and its effect on experiencing burden. They reported a reduction in the strength of the relationship between caregivers’ status and psychological distress by 40% for full-time caregivers and 60% for part-time caregivers, when social support is present [2, 21, 27].

While our study confirms that a high caregiver burden significantly increased depressive symptomatology among older caregivers, it also highlights the high magnitude of the problem, with a large proportion of caregivers experiencing high burden (36.4%). Other studies have reported similar or even higher levels of high burden, which may be explained by differences in care‑recipient characteristics, such as a higher proportion with chronic diseases or functional limitations, or by the inclusion of younger caregivers, who are known to have higher rates of caregiver burden [42, 47]. The prevalence of high burden in our study reflects the dual burden older adults face as they take care of loved ones while aging themselves and struggling with functional limitations. The characteristics of the caregivers in our study, being mostly female, married, co-residing with the care-recipient, and providing care to their spouse, are aligned with the existing literature in Lebanon and the Arab region on both general and older adult caregivers [42, 48–51].

These findings call for action at different levels for older caregivers. International evidence suggests that policies that provide respite care, financial allowances, flexible and paid caregiving leave, and psychosocial support are effective strategies in addressing the caregiver burden and protecting mental health. However, the degree to which these ideal, long-term, evidence-based policy recommendations can be adopted in low- and middle-income countries is still to be tested [52]. Shorter-term, socioculturally acceptable interventions, such as creating support groups or delivering psychosocial support through primary healthcare centers, NGOs, and community organizations like municipalities, may offer the most feasible way to reducing caregiver burden and mitigating depression risk in our context. Lebanon, like many Mediterranean and Middle Eastern societies, remains strongly patriarchal, with deeply rooted expectations that families, particularly women and especially older women, provide care for aging parents and spouses at home. Mirroring the Lebanese context, a high proportion of caregivers globally feel constrained to provide care because of social pressures and familial obligations rather than choice [53].

This study has several limitations to consider when interpreting its findings. First, its cross-sectional design only allows for the estimation of associations but precludes assessing temporality or causal inference. Consequently, reverse causality cannot be ruled out, with caregivers’ mental health potentially influencing the burden they report. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal sequence and establish causality. Second, the study may be subject to bias from unmeasured confounding, including information on care recipient characteristics and availability of paid support, which are said to influence the relationship between caregiver burden and mental health. Third, the use of self-reported measures, including those on the pattern of care, social support, and physical health, heightens the risk for recall and social desirability biases. The latter is particularly relevant for questions related to caregiver burden and social support. Participants may underreport their burden given the sensitive nature of caring for a loved one and overreport their perceived social support to conform to social norms. Fourth, grandparents who cared only for grandchildren were excluded from the caregiver burden analysis because data on burden of care were not collected for this group, which may have affected the generalizability of the findings to grandparents. Finally, given that financial resources were measured by dichotomizing subjective income sufficiency, it’s possible that the measure may not have captured gradations in financial resources that could have revealed a moderating effect.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. It uses reliable measurement tools validated in Arabic-speaking populations and focuses on older adult caregivers, a population that remains understudied and underserved, particularly as they often experience age-related health declines that may be worsened by caregiving responsibilities. Moreover, the use of a complex sampling design and sampling weights permits generalizability of our findings to the older adult population in Lebanon and enhances the precision of the estimated associations.

Most existing studies on the association between caregiver burden and mental health are cross-sectional. Future studies should prioritize longitudinal cohort study designs to assess the temporal relationship between caregiver burden and mental health outcomes, and to examine potential mediators that can clarify underlying pathways and help inform targeted interventions. Although we did not observe a moderating effect of financial resources on the association between caregiver burden and mental health, this construct is complex and multi-dimensional, encompassing both objective indicators (e.g., income, assets) and subjective experiences (e.g., sufficiency of income). Different dimensions can have different effects on the association between caregiver burden and mental health. Therefore, future studies should examine a broader range of financial resource dimensions to better capture its potential moderating effect.

Conclusion

High caregiver burden among older adult caregivers in Lebanon is strongly associated with exhibiting depressive symptoms, independently of the caregiver’s sociodemographic and health characteristics. Taking into consideration care recipients’ demographic and health characteristics can better elucidate the relationship between caregivers’ burden and their mental health, underscoring the relational and contextual nature of caregiving.

In the Lebanese context, where formal long-term care systems are limited and family members remain the primary providers of care, these findings have important implications.

Strengthening emotional support systems may represent a key intervention target to mitigate the psychological impact of caregiver burden and inform the development of tailored support programs for older adult caregivers.

Moreover, the findings also call for the development of contextualized national policies that formally recognize caregivers as a vulnerable population. Policies could include caregiver-inclusive health strategies, subsidized respite services, mental health coverage within primary care, and collaborations between the Ministry of Public Health and local organizations to build sustainable caregiver support infrastructures.
