Diagnostic performance of deep learning models in differentiating benign and malignant pulmonary nodules: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Fei Chen, Lujiao Chen, Yurui Lv, Yining Song, Zhenhua Zhao, Li Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer is the most lethal malignant tumor globally. Accurate differentiation between benign and malignant pulmonary nodules (PNs) is the core of early screening and diagnosis for lung cancer. Deep learning (DL) models can autonomously extract high-dimensional information from medical images, demonstrating technical advantages in this task. Our study systematically evaluates the accuracy and efficacy of DL models in differentiating benign from malignant PNs, providing systematic, high-quality integrated evidence for their diagnostic performance.MethodsOur study was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Diagnostic Test Accuracy Studies (PRISMA-DTA) reporting checklist. We searched the databases of PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science for relevant studies published up to 24 September 2025. Two researchers independently screened the literature according to inclusion and exclusion criteria, using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) to assess the risk of bias and applicability of the included studies. The pooled sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve (AUC) were used to evaluate the diagnostic performance of DL models. Subgroup analysis and meta-regression were employed to investigate sources of heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis and publication bias tests were conducted concurrently, and a Fagan plot was generated to assess clinical utility.ResultsA total of 21 retrospective studies were ultimately included. The QUADAS-2 quality assessment indicated that the overall risk of bias in the included studies was manageable. The combined sensitivity, specificity, and AUC of DL models for distinguishing between benign and malignant PNs were 0.92 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.90–0.93], 0.90 (95% CI: 0.86–0.92), and 0.96 (95% CI: 0.94–0.98). The pooled sensitivity and specificity were heterogeneous (I2>75%). A threshold effect test confirmed that no significant threshold effect was observed in our study (Spearman’s r=0.221, P=0.336). Subgroup analysis and meta-regression confirmed that model validation method was the primary source of heterogeneity in our study (P=0.00), with cross-validation models demonstrating significantly higher specificity than train–test split models (0.92 vs. 0.84, P=0.00). Models based on the Transformer architecture outperformed convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in terms of both sensitivity (0.93 vs. 0.92, P=0.00) and specificity (0.91 vs. 0.89, P=0.00). Sensitivity analysis confirmed the stability of the pooled results, and the funnel plot revealed no significant publication bias (P=0.56). The Fagan plot indicated that at a pretest probability of 50%, the model achieved a positive predictive post-test probability of 90% and a negative predictive post-test probability of only 8%, demonstrating good clinical utility.ConclusionsDL models demonstrate both high sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing benign from malignant PNs, exhibiting outstanding overall diagnostic performance. With prospective validation and technical optimization in the future, their clinical utility is expected to improve further.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the most prevalent malignant tumor globally in terms of morbidity and mortality, with a global five-year survival rate generally below 20% (1). This is primarily attributed to the fact that most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, missing the optimal window for treatment. The National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) demonstrated that low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening can reduce lung cancer mortality by 20% (2). Pulmonary nodules (PNs) represent a significant precursor to lung cancer, and assessing their malignant risk is a critical step in the early screening and diagnosis of lung cancer (3). If the diagnosis of malignant nodules is delayed, the 5-year survival rate drops from over 70% in early stages to less than 15% in advanced stages (4). Conversely, overtreatment of benign nodules (such as unnecessary biopsies or surgeries) increases patients’ healthcare burden and the risk of complications (5). Additionally, only 29–39% of incidental PNs worldwide complete standardized follow-up care (6). Low guideline adherence further exacerbates diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.

Early artificial intelligence (AI) centered on machine learning (ML) algorithms such as support vector machine and random forest, which required physicians to manually extract radiological features, including nodule size, morphology, and density, for subsequent analysis (7). In recent years, deep learning (DL), as a core branch of AI, has achieved breakthrough developments (8). Unlike ML, DL can autonomously extract high-dimensional information from medical images through multi-layer neural network architectures (9). The core advantage of DL lies in its powerful capabilities for feature learning and pattern recognition. It can not only capture subtle imaging features that are challenging for human visual detection, such as nodule margin morphology, internal textural characteristics, and minimal solid components, but also integrate temporal variation between current and prior computed tomography (CT) images (10). Furthermore, such models exhibit excellent reproducibility and high efficiency, significantly reducing the workload for radiologists and minimizing variations in subjective judgment among observers (11).

Although there has been a growing number of original studies on the use of DL models for distinguishing between benign and malignant PNs, high-quality integrated evidence systematically evaluating their diagnostic efficacy remains limited. Therefore, our study conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis of the diagnostic performance of DL models in distinguishing between benign and malignant PNs. The aim was to comprehensively integrate existing research data, assess the technology’s core diagnostic metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, and the area under the curve (AUC), and thoroughly analyze sources of heterogeneity. Our study can deepen radiologists’ understanding of DL models for distinguishing benign from malignant PNs, thereby aiding imaging assessment of PNs. Simultaneously, it provides high-quality evidence-based for the application of DL models within PN diagnosis and treatment pathways. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA-DTA reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-0581/rc) (12).

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis study protocol has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), with the unique registration number: CRD420251275956.

Search strategies and literature selection

Our study employed a combination of electronic database retrieval and manual searches to comprehensively collect relevant literature investigating the diagnostic efficacy of DL models in distinguishing benign and malignant PNs. The databases searched included PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science. The search period spanned from the inception of each database until 24 September 2025 to fully incorporate the latest research findings in this field and ensure the timeliness and comprehensiveness of the analyzed data. The search strategy combined subject headings (MeSH, Emtree thesauri) with free terms. Specific terms included the following: “deep learning model”, “convolutional neural network”, “transformer”, “artificial Intelligence”, “machine learning”, “pulmonary nodules”, “lung nodules”, and so on. The search strategy and keywords were jointly reviewed and refined by two researchers. Furthermore, the search formula for each database was specifically adapted according to its thesaurus characteristics and search rules to ensure both precision and comprehensiveness of the search. The search was limited to English-language sources. Additionally, to prevent potential literature omission, the reference lists of the final included studies were manually searched, which did not yield any additional literature for inclusion. All retrieved literature was imported into EndNote 2025 (Clarivate, London, UK) for centralized management and organization.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

After removing duplicate records, two researchers independently conducted a tiered screening of the remaining literature. They sequentially assessed each record based on title, abstract, and full text to determine compliance with the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Screening results were cross-checked. Any discrepancies were resolved through joint discussion, with arbitration by a third researcher if necessary. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) study population: patients with benign or malignant PNs confirmed by pathological examination or by a joint diagnosis from experienced doctors; (II) intervention: use of the DL models as the core diagnostic tool for distinguishing benign from malignant PNs; (III) outcomes: AUC as the primary outcome measure, with sensitivity, specificity, and other secondary outcome measures reported; (IV) study design: observational studies (retrospective or prospective cohort studies), randomized controlled trials, or non-randomized controlled trials. Literature was excluded if it met any of the following criteria: (I) does not involve DL models or algorithms; (II) data is incomplete, rendering it impossible to extract or calculate diagnostic fourfold table data [true positive (TP), true negative (TN), false positive (FP), and false negative (FN)]; (III) non-original research such as animal experiments, reviews, or case reports; (IV) not based on chest imaging data; (V) non-English studies. These criteria were adopted to rigorously ensure the quality and reliability of included studies, minimizing the potential impact of bias on the results of the systematic review and meta-analysis.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction adopted a standardized form designed in-house to ensure consistency and accuracy throughout the process. The following three categories of core information were systematically extracted from each included study: (I) basic study information: First author’s surname, publication year, country of study; (II) research models and characteristics: DL model type and architecture, model validation method, study design (retrospective/prospective), data source, number of patients (based on the number of nodules), number of benign/malignant PNs, imaging modality used (e.g., chest CT); (III) outcome metric data: core diagnostic performance metrics (AUC, sensitivity, specificity, etc.), and diagnostic fourfold table data (TP/TN/FP/FN). If a study did not directly provide fourfold table data, corresponding metrics were calculated based on indicators such as AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity provided in the text. If multiple DL models were constructed for the same cohort of patients within a single study, the model with superior diagnostic performance (a higher AUC value) was prioritized for analysis.

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were assessed using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) (13). This tool was the current internationally recognized standard for evaluating the quality of diagnostic studies, covering four core domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. The evaluation content includes “risk of bias” across all domains and “applicability” for the first three domains. Each dimension was assessed using a three-tier classification: “high”, “low”, and “unclear”. The evaluation process was conducted using RevMan 5.3 (Cochrane Collaboration, London, UK) to ensure the standardization of the evaluation procedures.

Statistical analysis

Our study employed Stata 14.2 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) and Meta-Disc 1.4 for statistical data analysis. First, threshold effect testing was conducted using Meta-Disc 1.4 9unit of Clinical Biostatistics, Ramón y Cajal Hospital, Madrid, Spain) to calculate the Spearman’s correlation coefficient between the logarithm of sensitivity and the logarithm of (1 − specificity). A strong positive correlation with P≤0.05 indicated the presence of a threshold effect (14). Heterogeneity among the results of the included studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test in conjunction with the I2 statistic. I2 values were interpreted as follows: 0–40%, no significant heterogeneity; 30–60%, moderate heterogeneity; 50–90% substantial heterogeneity; 75–100%, considerable heterogeneity (15). If heterogeneity was substantial, a random-effects model was used to pool diagnostic performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio (PLR), negative likelihood ratio (NLR), diagnostic score, diagnostic odds ratio (DOR), and their 95% confidence intervals (CI). If heterogeneity was minimal, a fixed-effects model was employed. To investigate sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses and meta-regression analyses were conducted covering six variables: country (China vs. other countries), sample size (>1,000 vs. ≤1,000), data source (multi-center vs. single center), model validation method (train-test split vs. cross-validation), external validation performed or not (yes vs. no), and model architecture [convolutional neural network (CNN) vs. Transformer vs. others]. The overall diagnostic performance of the DL models was comprehensively evaluated by plotting a summary receiver operating characteristic curve (SROC) and calculating the AUC. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plot, and the stability of the pooled results was validated through sensitivity analysis. Additionally, a Fagan plot was constructed to evaluate the model’s clinical utility, and post-test probabilities were calculated based on pretest probabilities to provide a reference for clinical decision-making. All statistical tests were considered statistically significant at P≤0.05.

Results

Literature search and selection

The literature search and screening process strictly adheres to the PRISMA-DTA reporting checklist. The detailed workflow is illustrated in Figure 1. A preliminary search across PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science databases (covering the period from each database’s inception to 24 September 2025) yielded 6,592 relevant publications. Duplicate records were first removed using EndNote 2025, supplemented by manual verification, which resulted in the elimination of 1,285 duplicates. The remaining 5,307 publications proceeded to the initial screening phase. Based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, further screening was conducted by reviewing titles and abstracts. A total of 5,080 articles were excluded for irrelevance to the research topic or mismatched study types (e.g., reviews, case reports, animal experiments). The remaining 227 articles underwent a thorough review of the full text. During the full-text screening phase, 198 studies were excluded because TP, FP, FN, and TN were not available or not used in the DL models. Six studies were excluded because they were not based on chest imaging data, and two were excluded for being non-English publications. Ultimately, 21 studies were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis (16-36).

Study characteristics

The basic characteristics of the included studies are detailed in Tables 1,2. The 21 studies ultimately included were published between 2017 and 2025, collectively reflecting recent research advances in DL models for distinguishing benign from malignant PNs. All included studies were retrospective, comprising 18 multi-center studies (14 of which sourced data from public databases) and 3 single center studies. The sample size ranged from 83 to 56,807 cases, with a total of 110,284 patients enrolled (based on the number of nodules). Only 6 studies explicitly reported patient age distribution. DL model architectures exhibited diverse characteristics: models in 18 studies adopted CNN and their derivative architectures (e.g., ResNet, VGGNet); models in 5 studies adopted Transformer architectures (such as ViT); and other architectures, such as pyramid pooling modules (PPM), were adopted in 5 studies. Regarding model validation method, all studies performed internal validation (including train-test split and cross-validation). Among these, four studies conducted external validation to enhance the reliability of model generalization assessment. In terms of imaging modalities, all included studies utilized chest CT images.

Quality assessment

Our study employed the QUADAS-2 to assess the risk of bias and applicability of the included studies, with results presented in Figure 2. Regarding risk of bias, the patient selection domain exhibited an overall low risk of bias. However, three studies were rated as having unclear risk of bias because they did not explicitly state whether consecutive or randomly selected cases were included. One study was rated as having unclear risk of bias because it did not clearly describe the case inclusion method or specify the exclusion criteria during data screening. Another study was rated as having unclear risk of bias because it did not clearly describe the original case inclusion method. The index test domain showed a predominantly low risk of bias across most studies, with three exceptions being rated as unclear because they did not specify whether classification thresholds were pre-established before analysis or whether the interpretation of model diagnostic performance was conducted in a blinded manner. In the reference standard domain, only one study was rated as high risk of bias due to inconsistent gold standards, where diagnosis was based partly on pathology and partly on clinical follow-up. For the flow and timing domain, only one study was rated as unclear risk of bias, also due to inconsistency in the applied reference standard. In terms of applicability concerns, the patient selection, index test, and reference standard domains all demonstrated low applicability concerns. Overall, the quality of the included studies was satisfactory with an acceptable risk of bias.

Meta-analysis

Our study presents the combined diagnostic performance of the DL models in distinguishing between benign and malignant PNs through forest plots (Figures 3-5) and the SROC curve (Figure 6). The pooled analysis results showed that the pooled sensitivity of the DL models was 0.92 (95% CI: 0.90–0.93), and the pooled specificity was 0.90 (95% CI: 0.86–0.92). The PLR was 8.80 (95% CI: 6.55–11.82), indicating a significantly increased likelihood of malignant nodules when a positive result was obtained. The NLR was 0.09 (95% CI: 0.07–0.11), suggesting that a negative result effectively excludes the risk of malignancy. The diagnostic score was 4.57 (95% CI: 4.16–4.98), reflecting the model’s strong overall discriminatory capability. Furthermore, the DOR was 96.64 (95% CI: 64.13–145.63), indicating a highly strong association between positive results identified by the DL models and malignant PNs. The SROC curve showed an AUC of 0.96 (95% CI: 0.94–0.98), indicating that the DL models exhibit high diagnostic performance in distinguishing between benign and malignant PNs. Additionally, the heterogeneity test revealed that the I2 values for all diagnostic performance metrics exceeded 90% (sensitivity I2=90.34%, specificity I2=96.08%, PLR I2=95.23%, NLR I2=90.89%, diagnostic score I2=93.29%, DOR I2=100%), indicating substantial heterogeneity among the included studies. Therefore, a random-effects model was employed to pool the diagnostic performance metrics.

In the heterogeneity analysis, the Spearman’s correlation coefficient for the threshold effect testing was 0.221, indicating a weak positive correlation that was not statistically significant (P=0.336). This suggests that there is no substantial threshold effect in our study, thereby allowing the use of conventional pooled effect size models for subsequent analyses. To further investigate the sources of high heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup analyses and used meta-regression to examine the influence of various factors on heterogeneity (Table 3). Meta-regression analysis revealed that country (P=0.11), sample size (P=0.12), data source (P=0.21), whether external validation was performed or not (P=0.90), and model architecture (P=0.56/0.69/0.12) did not significantly influence study heterogeneity (all P>0.05). However, the I2 statistic for the model validation method subgroup reached 82% with P=0.00, suggesting this factor may be a significant contributor to overall heterogeneity. In subgroup analysis, the sensitivity of models constructed from Chinese studies (0.92) was comparable to that of models from other countries (0.92), but specificity was significantly lower (0.87 vs. 0.94, P=0.00). Studies with sample size >1,000 demonstrated higher model sensitivity (0.93 vs. 0.90, P=0.00) but lower specificity (0.87 vs. 0.91, P=0.00). Multi-center studies yielded higher model sensitivity than single center studies (0.92 vs. 0.88, P=0.00). Models trained using a train–test split demonstrated higher sensitivity than those trained via cross-validation (0.94 vs. 0.91, P=0.00), whereas cross-validation models exhibited significantly higher specificity than train–test split models (0.92 vs. 0.84, P=0.00). The sensitivity (0.90 vs. 0.92, P=0.00) and specificity (0.89 vs. 0.90, P=0.01) of the model from the study with external validation were both lower than those of the model from the study without external validation. Furthermore, models employing the Transformer architecture demonstrated superior sensitivity (0.93 vs. 0.92, P=0.00) and specificity (0.91 vs. 0.89, P=0.00) compared to those using the CNN architecture. Models utilizing other architectures exhibited significantly lower specificity than the aforementioned two types (0.83, P=0.00).

To validate the robustness of the pooled results in this meta-analysis, we conducted a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis (Figure 7). The results showed that after sequentially excluding any single study, the point estimates of the pooled effect size consistently remained stable within a narrow range of 0.99 to 1.01. And the 95% CI for all pooled effect sizes overlapped substantially. This indicates that the pooled results of our study were not unduly influenced by any single study, demonstrating robust stability and reliability. Since data from 11 of the 21 included studies were drawn from the same public database, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using only the study with the largest sample size and most complete data among these 11 studies to eliminate this redundancy bias (Figure S1). The results showed that the pooled AUC for 11 studies after duplicate removal was 0.93 (95% CI: 0.90–0.95). The 95% CI before and after duplicate removal overlapped, and the AUC remained consistently above the clinical threshold for high diagnostic performance (AUC =0.9). This suggests that the core conclusions of our study remain robust even after accounting for the bias caused by duplicate counts resulting from the exclusion of duplicate databases. In addition, we employed funnel plot asymmetry tests to assess publication bias in the included studies (Figure 8). The results showed a symmetrical distribution of regression lines in the funnel plot, with a corresponding P value of 0.56. This indicates no significant publication bias in the included studies (P>0.05). This finding suggests that the pooled effect size of the current meta-analysis is minimally affected by publication bias, lending high reliability to its conclusions.

Research indicates that the pretest probability in Fagan plot is typically directly correlated with the prevalence of the target disease (37). To ensure that the target population used to establish the pretest probability aligns with the characteristics of the target population in the included studies of this meta-analysis, we calculated the median malignant prevalence rate of PNs across all included studies and adopted this median as the pretest probability, setting it at 50% (38). Based on the likelihood ratios (LR) obtained from our study’s pooled analysis (LR+ =9, LR− =0.09), the Fagan plot (Figure 9) clearly demonstrated that when the DL models predict a positive result, the patient’s post-test probability increases significantly from 50% to 90%. This indicates that a positive result substantially heightens radiologists’ suspicion of malignant PNs. Conversely, when the models predict a negative result, the post-test probability drops significantly from 50% to 8%, indicating that negative results can effectively rule out malignant risk. These findings confirm that the DL models substantially alter radiologists’ assessments of the probability of benign versus malignant PNs, demonstrating significant practical value in aiding clinical decision-making.

Qualitative synthesis

To investigate the diagnostic performance of DL models in distinguishing PNs based on different imaging features, our study extracted nodule feature information from the included studies (Table S1). Due to significant inconsistencies in the stratification of nodule size and type, as well as severe data missingness, which precluded meeting the statistical prerequisites for quantitative subgroup analysis, a qualitative synthesis was conducted instead. Of the 21 studies, only 4 did not report nodule size, and the data were relatively complete. The most common size range was 3–30 mm, and the diagnostic performance of DL models was best supported within this range, with AUC values generally above 0.94. Regarding micro-nodules <10 mm, only the Liu 2024 (AUC: 0.96) was included. Although the results were encouraging, the evidence was extremely limited. Although Huang 2023 (AUC: 0.90), Wang 2020 (AUC: 0.95), and Zhang 2025 (AUC: 0.91) involved large nodules (>30 mm), they did not provide diagnostic performance data for corresponding layers. Therefore, the strength of this evidence is limited.

With regard to nodule type, a comparison was conducted of three studies that included only solid nodules (Feng 2020, AUC: 0.89; Lin 2021, AUC: 0.91; Liu 2024, AUC: 0.96) with studies that did not restrict nodule type (median AUC: 0.94). The results showed that the overall performance for solid nodules is lower, suggesting that the models may be better at capturing malignant features associated with ground-glass opacities (GGOs), whereas the same imaging appearance of different histopathologies in some benign solid nodules poses a challenge to the models. However, the AUC of Liu 2024 reached 0.96, suggesting that when the nodule diameter shrinks to <10 mm, the malignant features of solid nodules may become more uniform, leading to an interaction between size and type. However, this inference is based on a single study and requires further validation with more stratified data. In summary, the current evidence supports the excellent diagnostic performance of DL models for PNs of typical sizes ranging from 3 to 30 mm. However, further stratified studies are needed to validate their performance for micro-nodules, large nodules, and different nodule types.

Discussion

Our study systematically evaluated the diagnostic efficacy of DL models in distinguishing between benign and malignant PNs, incorporating 21 studies that covered 110,284 nodule samples. The pooled analysis results indicate that the DL models demonstrated a pooled sensitivity of 0.92, a pooled specificity of 0.90, and an SROC-AUC as high as 0.96, demonstrating excellent performance. Their core advantage stems from their autonomous capability to extract high-dimensional latent features from images, thereby avoiding the subjective biases and feature extraction constraints inherent in traditional methods.

Clinical risk prediction models, such as Brock and Mayo models, are the most commonly used diagnostic aids among radiologists. However, the combined AUC of the DL models in our study (0.96) significantly exceeded that of the combined AUC for the Brock model (0.80) (39) and Mayo model (0.73) (40). Moreover, the Brock model achieved an AUC of only 0.74 in Asian populations, whereas that of the Mayo model was 0.70 (39,40). In contrast, the DL models in our study maintained an AUC of 0.96, even when applied to a sample containing a substantial proportion of Asian individuals. This advantage is closely linked to the DL model’s adaptive learning capability for imaging features across diverse populations. Evidence (41) has shown that the proportion of part-solid nodules and GGOs is higher in Asian populations compared to American populations, and the malignancy rate of such nodules is also higher. Clinical risk prediction models, trained primarily on United States populations, exhibit limited adaptability to nodule characteristics in Asian populations. Meanwhile, DL models can leverage transfer learning to fine-tune pre-trained parameters, enabling efficient learning of common features and specific radiographic manifestations across different populations. This approach significantly enhances diagnostic consistency across populations. The current imaging diagnosis of PNs primarily relies on radiologists’ subjective judgment, with substantial inter-observer variability. Ewals et al. (42) demonstrated that even experienced radiologists exhibited a misdiagnosis rate (1 − specificity) of approximately 10–18% in predicting PN malignancy without the aid of AI. Less experienced radiologists exhibited even lower diagnostic performance, with misdiagnosis rates ranging from 20% to 58%. DL models can effectively reduce human bias and improve the efficiency of PN diagnosis by automating feature extraction and analysis (43). This advantage is particularly crucial in large-scale lung cancer screening scenarios.

Thanks to its key advantage of significantly reducing radiation exposure while maintaining high detection performance for early-stage lung cancer, LDCT has become a cornerstone of lung cancer screening and holds an irreplaceable clinical position (2,44). Although radiation control yields significant benefits, dose reduction is often accompanied by inherent drawbacks, such as increased image noise and reduced spatial resolution. This directly increases the difficulty for human readers, hindering the localization of PNs and the assessment of features. Consequently, it leads to shifts in Lung Imaging Reporting and Data System (Lung-RADS) scores, which in turn affect patient management decisions (45). DL models can overcome this challenge to some extent through adaptive feature learning and targeted optimization. However, it exhibits a dose–threshold effect, resulting in a significant increase in the FP rate under extremely low-dose conditions (46). Furthermore, model performance degrades as image quality deteriorates excessively, making it difficult to completely eliminate the need for manual review. Photon-counting CT (PCCT), leveraging core technologies such as direct photon counting, effective noise reduction, and ultra-high spatial resolution, can significantly reduce radiation dose while maintaining or even improving image quality, thereby achieving an optimal balance between radiation dose and image quality (47). PCCT can overcome the limitations of energy-integrating detector CT (EID-CT), providing DL models with higher-quality images and richer information on lesions, thereby enhancing the models’ ability to detect and characterize difficult and complicated lesions (48).

Meta-regression analysis revealed that model validation method was the key factor contributing to the overall heterogeneity in our study (I2=82%, P=0.00). The train-test split validation method divides the dataset into training and test sets using a single fixed proportion, making it susceptible to data distribution differences and random partitioning deviation (49). In contrast, cross-validation employs multiple iterative rounds to partition the dataset. This approach not only maximizes the utilization of limited sample information and reduces random error but also effectively mitigates the interference of data distribution bias on results, yielding more robust model performance evaluations. This core difference in validation logic is a major reason for the significant model heterogeneity observed across different validation methods in our study. Moreover, details in variations in dataset partitioning can further exacerbate heterogeneity. Among studies employing train-test split validation, 2/9 used a 3:1 split, 2/9 adopted an 8:2 split, whereas 5/9 utilized other ratios or failed to report specific partitioning proportions. Similarly, studies employing cross-validation utilized varying fold counts for dataset partitioning, such as 5-fold and 10-fold cross-validation. These differences in dataset partitioning can lead to disparities in the available sample size during model training versus the intensity of generalization testing during evaluation, thereby resulting in performance metric deviation. Our study marks the systematic comparison of DL models based on CNN and Transformer architectures for subgroup analysis, contributing to refining the research perspective of previous studies regarding emerging architectures. The model architecture’s impact on heterogeneity lacks statistical significance. Although this finding may appear contradictory to architectural differences, it aligns with current trends in DL model optimization (50). CNN architectures rely on the advantages of local receptive field and weight sharing, possessing inherent strengths in capturing nodular edge morphology, internal density, and subtle structural differences (51). They are the mainstream DL architectures in PN diagnosis. Meanwhile, the Transformer architecture, which utilizes the self-attention mechanism and multi-head self-attention (MHSA), efficiently models long-range dependencies between the nodules’ global features and the lung tissue background (52). Its lack of local receptive field constraints enables comprehensive integration of nodule morphology, density distribution, and surrounding anatomical structure information. However, most models in the research undergo targeted optimization and parameter tuning. DL models primarily based on CNNs often compensate for their inability to capture global features by incorporating multi-scale feature fusion strategies and attention enhancement modules (51). DL models primarily based on Transformers achieve a dual-directional balance between performance and efficiency by introducing local attention mechanisms and lightweight architectures (52). This dual-directional optimization makes the overall diagnostic efficacy of models with different architectures closer to parity. Müller-Franzes et al. (53) showed that the AUC difference between optimized CNN and Transformer architecture models in PN diagnosis was only 0.03 and not statistically significant (P=0.13), confirming that the performance gap between reasonably optimized DL models of different architectures in PN diagnosis can be kept within a narrow range.

The 21 studies included in our review differed in key aspects such as model architecture, data preprocessing methods, and gold standards. From a methodological perspective, pooling studies with high heterogeneity typically undermines the reliability of the pooled effect size (54). Although the results of the quality assessment, sensitivity analysis, and publication bias tests all confirm the robustness of the pooled results from our study, they also suggest that there are inherent limitations to the generalizability of our conclusions. Due to objective differences in study designs, pooled results may not fully reflect the complex and variable clinical scenarios encountered in the real world. Even if statistical analysis can identify the primary sources of heterogeneity, it cannot completely eliminate the effects of methodological differences between studies. Therefore, the conclusions of our study should be interpreted with caution within the context of the available evidence and should not be unconditionally generalized to all populations, devices, and clinical settings. This interpretation, grounded in heterogeneity, does not diminish the study’s value. Rather, it better aligns with the rigorous standards of evidence-based medicine. While acknowledging the overall diagnostic advantages of DL models, clearly identifying the limitations of their application stemming from methodological diversity helps clinicians evaluate the true value of these models more rationally and provides a sound basis for standardizing methodological protocols and conducting prospective validation in future research.

Based on high-dimensional feature extraction capabilities, DL models can automatically identify hundreds to thousands of imaging features related to nodule malignancy that are difficult for human vision to discern. Consistent with the perspective of Ewals et al. (42), their high sensitivity aids in the early interventions of malignant nodules, reducing missed diagnoses. Their high specificity effectively distinguishes benign nodules, thereby avoiding unnecessary invasive examinations and surgeries, and consequently decreasing the incidence of complications such as pneumothorax and hemorrhage. Additionally, DL models can enhance the efficiency of lung cancer screening. In large-scale LDCT screening scenarios, DL models enable rapid remote batch assessment of nodules, addressing the issue of limited experience among primary physicians. This approach is particularly suitable for community hospitals and medical institutions in remote areas. Pilot applications in medically underserved regions of western China (55) have demonstrated that mobile CT lung cancer screening based on AI assistance improves the convenience and timeliness of follow-up for patients with PNs. This approach enabled timely treatment for most (62.98%) of the high-risk PN patients, fully validating its practical value and feasibility in primary healthcare settings. Meanwhile, when DL models are used for the initial screening of PNs to distinguish benign from malignant lesions and for risk stratification, they can automatically rule out low-risk nodules, thereby significantly reducing radiologists’ CT-reading workload (56). This allows senior physicians to focus more on high-risk and complex cases, thereby improving the efficiency of medical resource utilization.

Our study has certain limitations. First, all included studies were retrospective, lacking validation from prospective research. Retrospective studies tend to prioritize cases with typical features and complete data, potentially resulting in overestimation of model performance. In real-world settings, nodule characteristics exhibit greater diversity. Second, some studies did not provide detailed reports on nodule characteristics such as size and type, making it impossible to conduct quantitative subgroup analyses. This limits the depth of heterogeneity analysis and, consequently, restricts the practical value of DL models in specific clinical settings. Furthermore, only four studies conducted external validation, lacking multi-center, cross-regional, and cross-device large-scale external validation data. External validation is a critical step for clinical translation of models, and the current insufficient validation evidence means that the practical clinical utility of our study’s conclusions requires further verification. Fourth, the geographic distribution of included studies was uneven, with 16/21 studies originating from China. The impact of regional bias on the global generalizability of our study’s findings should be considered. Finally, most models in the included studies were trained on public databases (e.g., LIDC-IDRI). However, imaging data from public databases may undergo preprocessing and standardization, differing from raw clinical images. This discrepancy could potentially reduce model performance in real-world clinical settings.

Conclusions

Our study confirms that DL models demonstrate both high sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing between benign and malignant PNs, with excellent overall diagnostic performance. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to limitations such as the retrospective studies, insufficient number of external validation studies, and methodological heterogeneity across studies. Future studies should prioritize prospective study designs and actively conduct multi-center, cross-device external validation to further enhance the clinical utility of DL models in distinguishing between benign and malignant PNs.
