Proposal on incorporating consolidation-to-tumor ratio into the clinical T1 classification in patients with invasive lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Molin Zhang, Chaoqiang Deng, Fangqiu Fu, Ting Ye, Akif Turna, Yuan Li, Yang Zhang, Haiquan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prognostic implication of consolidation-to-tumor ratio (CTR) in patients with part-solid nodules (PSNs) remains ambiguous. The aim of this study is to explore the potential of CTR for refining prognostic stratification within the current clinical T1 classificationMethodsThere were 1,340 N0M0 lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) patients with PSNs (solid size ≤3 cm) enrolled. We constructed a novel risk stratification model based on both solid component size and CTR. The lung cancer-specific survival (LCSS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) rates of the patients in the current and modified staging groups were evaluated.ResultsAt a median follow-up of 80.5 months, there were 89 recurrences and 40 lung cancer-associated deaths. The 5-year LCSS and RFS rates differed significantly in patients divided by CTR (0< CTR ≤0.5 vs. 0.5< CTR ≤0.8, LCSS: P<0.001, RFS: P<0.001; 0.5< CTR ≤0.8 vs. 0.8< CTR <1, LCSS: P=0.002, RFS: P<0.001). Subgroup analysis showed that a larger CTR was associated with worse prognosis in each clinical T1 classification (T1a: CTR ≤0.5 vs. CTR >0.5, P=0.03; T1b: CTR ≤0.5 vs. CTR >0.5, P=0.006; T1c: CTR ≤0.8 vs. CTR >0.8, P=0.04). The CTR-incorporated stratification scheme clearly classified the prognosis of PSNs (LCSS: T1mi: 100%, T1a: 99.1%, T1b: 89.4%, T1c: 75.3%; RFS: T1mi: 100%, T1a: 98.2%, T1b: 87.6%, T1c: 66.9%).ConclusionsCTR had a significant effect on the prognosis of PSNs, even when the solid component sizes were similar. An exploratory staging model incorporating CTR showed potential for improving risk stratification over the current T1 classification, but warrants external validation in future studies.

Introduction

With the wide application of low-dose computed tomography (LDCT), an increase in the detection of lung ground-glass opacity (GGO) lesions, especially subsolid nodules, has been observed (1-5). Previous studies have revealed that part-solid lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is a clinically special subtype with significantly different prognosis from pure GGO and pure solid nodules (6,7). A GGO component is a well-established, favorable prognostic factor in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (8,9). Furthermore, within clinical stage IA, radiologically invasive NSCLCs with a ground-glass component are associated with significantly improved survival compared to purely solid tumors (10). Several studies have confirmed that GGO lesions exhibit a distinct indolent biological behavior, characterized by slow progression and favorable prognosis. Compared to solid nodules, these tumors also demonstrated low activity in both the tumor immune microenvironment and key immune pathways (11,12). In the eighth edition of the tumor node metastasis (TNM) classification of lung cancer, the size of the solid component is used to determine the clinical T stage for part-solid nodules (PSNs) (13). However, substantially different outcomes have been observed among early-stage LUAD with similar solid component sizes, indicating significant prognostic heterogeneity within the same clinical T stage.

As an important feature of PSNs, consolidation-to-tumor ratio (CTR) has been considered to be associated with clinical outcomes in some retrospective studies (14-16). Some researchers believed that CTR was a prognostic factor for early-stage LUAD presenting as GGO lesions (17,18). Aoki et al. found that patients with a CTR of less than 0.5 had a significantly better prognosis than those with a CTR of 0.5 or higher (19). A systematic review indicated that the median disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) rates were higher for PSNs with a CTR <80% than those with a CTR ≥80% (8). The survival outcomes of JCOG0201 revealed that the criteria of nodules ≤2 cm with CTR ≤0.25 identified a group of patients with radiological noninvasive peripheral LUAD (14). However, the prognostic value of CTR in the current clinical T classification of PSNs is yet to be fully elucidated. Therefore, it is important to investigate whether PSNs with similar solid component sizes but different CTRs have different prognoses, and ways to classify the part-solid tumors require further investigation. To improve the accuracy of subsequent revisions of the clinical T classification in PSNs, detailed validation analyses on the basis of adequate follow-up time and large-scale data sets are required.

In this study, we collected a large sample of cT1N0M0 LUAD patients with PSNs who underwent surgical resection to investigate the prognostic value of CTR. Adequate follow-up time ensured that long-term outcomes of patients were recorded. We evaluated the prognostic stratification of CTR and explored its potential implications within the clinical T-stage framework. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2025-aw-1274/rc).

Methods

Patients

We retrospectively evaluated patients with PSNs with surgically resected c-T1N0M0 LUAD who underwent surgical resection at Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (FUSCC) between January 2008 and December 2019. Inclusion criteria were PSNs with a solid size ≤3 cm determined by thin-section CT and clinically no lymph node metastasis or distant metastasis. Patients having synchronous subsolid and pure solid nodules, not having received a TS-CT scan in our institution, or not having complete radiological or pathological information were excluded. Finally, 1,054 patients were included in the analyses for lung cancer-specific survival (LCSS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) (Figure 1). The following clinicopathologic data were collected: sex, age, smoking history, histology type, solid component size, tumor size, CTR, visceral pleural invasion (VPI), and lymphovascular invasion (LVI). The VPI and LVI status used in the analysis was based on the final postoperative pathological examination.

Flowchart of patient selection for the study cohort. AIS, adenocarcinoma in situ; CTR, consolidation-to-tumor ratio; LPA, lepidic pattern-predominant adenocarcinoma; LUAD, lung adenocarcinoma; MIA, minimally invasive adenocarcinoma; PSN, part-solid nodule.

This is a retrospective cohort study utilizing a single-center database. Therefore, the sample size was not based on an a priori power calculation but was determined by all consecutive patients who met the predefined inclusion criteria during the study period. A post-hoc power analysis was conducted for the primary comparison of survival outcomes across the three CTR-based prognostic groups using Pearson’s chi-squared test. Given an observed effect size (Cramér’s V =0.2285), a total sample size of 1,054, and an alpha level of 0.05 (two-sided), the achieved statistical power exceeded 99.9%. The detailed results are provided in Table S1.

Radiological and histologic evaluation

TSCT scans were reviewed by two radiologists independently for GGO appearance. PSN was defined as a lung lesion with both a GGO and a solid component. Tumor size on CT scan was defined as the maximum diameter in the axial plane. When the solid part was multiple or irregular, multiple-plane reconstruction was used and only the largest diameter was analyzed. CTR was defined as the ratio of the maximum size of consolidation to the maximum tumor size on the TSCT scan in the axial plane (0< CTR <1). For cases with different results, the two radiologists reached agreement through discussion or consulted the third senior radiologist.

Postoperative pathologic diagnosis was made according to the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC)/American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society classification as adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA), and invasive adenocarcinoma (IAD), which was further divided into lepidic predominant, acinar predominant, papillary predominant, micropapillary predominant, solid predominant, and invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma. The predominant pattern was defined as the pattern with the largest percentage (not necessarily 50% or higher) (20).

Follow-up protocol

Chest CT scans and ultrasonography of abdominal and cervical/supraclavicular regions were performed every 6 months for the first 3 years after surgery and annually after 3 years. We also performed telephone follow-up and recommended imaging tests for patients who reported clinical symptoms. The distinction between recurrent and second primary lung cancers was made according to the criteria delineated by Martini and Melamed. LCSS was defined as the time from initial resection to death resulting from lung cancer. RFS was defined as the time from the day of surgery to the day of first recurrence or last follow-up. Patients without evidence of recurrence were censored at the time of last negative follow-up. Patients who never came to follow-up and could not be reached by telephone were deemed “lost to follow-up”. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (IRB2008223-9, 14 July 2020). Informed consent was waived because this was a retrospective study.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed by using Stata/SE software (version SE/11.1; StataCorp) and R Statistical Language (version 4.3.3), including the ‘survival’ and ‘survminer’ packages. Surv_cutpoint function from the survminer R package was used to determine an optimal statistical cutoff of CTR based on survival outcomes. The potential recurrent risk factors were obtained by univariate analysis of Cox proportional hazard models. Both CTR and solid component size were evaluated as continuous variables. To assess potential multicollinearity between the CTR and solid size, we calculated the variance inflation factor (VIF), which was 1.645. To avoid multicollinearity in the Cox proportional hazards models, we assessed their independent prognostic contributions through separate multivariable models. Factors with P values less than 0.05 in univariate analysis were analyzed by multivariate Cox regression models to identify independent risk factors for LCSS and RFS. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to analyze LCSS and RFS, and the log-rank test was used to compare survival curves. All P values were two-sided with a significance level of 0.05. We performed internal validation of the exploratory CTR-incorporated staging model using bootstrap resampling to assess its stability and the reproducibility of prognostic discrimination across simulated samples.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 1,340 cT1N0M0 LUAD patients with PSNs were included with a median follow-up of 80.5 months. Of these patients, 845 (63.1%) were female and 495 (36.9%) were male. Among the patients, there were 516 nodules with a CTR less than or equal to 0.5 (0< CTR ≤0.5), 622 nodules with CTR larger than 0.5 but less than or equal to 0.8 (0.5< CTR ≤0.8), 202 nodules with CTR larger than 0.8 but less than 1 (0.8< CTR <1). There were 286 patients with AIS, MIA or lepidic pattern-predominant adenocarcinoma (LPA) The mean solid area diameter was 11.7 mm. Furthermore, 110 (8.2%) patients had VPI and 39 (2.9) patients had LVI. Detailed clinicopathologic characteristics of patients are listed in Table 1.

CTR was a prognostic factor in patients with PSNs

We investigated independent predictors associated with LCSS and RFS of patients with PSNs. Because of the excellent survival of AIS/MIA/LPA (the 5-year LCSS and 5-year RFS were 100%, 100%, respectively), we excluded 286 patients with AIS/MIA/LPA from the Cox proportional hazard analyses. Beside of solid size, a multivariate analysis revealed that CTR was also an independently significant prognostic factor of LCSS [hazard ratio (HR) =18.119, 95% CI: 4.132–79.455, P<0.001] and RFS (HR =106.631, 95% CI: 28.342–401.179, P < 0.001) (Table 2, Table S2).

Identification of prognostic CTR thresholds

In our specific cohort of 1,054 clinical cT1 IADs, we observed that patients with a CTR ≤0.5 universally exhibited 100% 5-year RFS and LCSS (Tables S3,S4). For patients with CTR >0.5, we employed the surv_cutpoint function from the survminer R package to determine an optimal statistical cutoff based on lung cancer associated deaths (Figure 2A) and recurrences (Figure 2B). The derived value was subsequently rounded to 0.8 for clinical practicality and ease of application.

CTR had significant implications for prognostic stratification

We further evaluated the prognostic influence of the solid size and CTR among the 1,054 patients with stage cT1 IADs. Patients were divided into 3 groups according to CTR (0< CTR ≤0.5, 0.5< CTR ≤0.8, 0.8< CTR <1). There were significant differences in 5-year LCSS or RFS among patients with the different categories of CTR. Comparison between adjacent groups yielded significant LCSS and RFS differences at the 0.5 (LCSS: P<0.001, RFS: P<0.001) and 0.8 (LCSS: P=0.002, RFS: P<0.001) cut points (Figure 3).

Lung cancer-specific survival (A) and recurrence-free survival (B) of patients with part-solid nodules stratified by CTR thresholds (0.5 and 0.8). CTR, consolidation-to-tumor ratio; LCSS, lung cancer-specific survival; RFS, recurrence-free survival.

Furthermore, we investigated the prognostic significance of CTR in clinical T1 stage PSNs using different stratification thresholds. Notably, the CTR demonstrated significant prognostic discrimination across all T1 substages. Using a CTR threshold of 0.5, we observed statistically significant differences in RFS for both T1a (0< CTR ≤0.5: 100%, 0.5< CTR <1: 98.1%, P=0.03) and T1b (0< CTR ≤0.5: 100%, 0.5< CTR <1: 88.1%, P=0.006) patients. Similarly, when applying a CTR threshold of 0.8, T1c patients also showed significant prognostic stratification (0< CTR ≤0.5: 84.1%, 0.5< CTR <1: 66.9%, P=0.04) (Figure 4).

Kaplan-Meier curves comparing recurrence-free survival among patients with part-solid nodules stratified by CTR thresholds (0.5 or 0.8) within each clinical T category: T1a (A), T1b (B), and T1c (C). The clinical T1 classification incorporating CTR revealed a clear prognostic stratification

We stratified 1,054 cT1 patients based on CTR and solid component size and evaluated the 5-year LCSS rate and 5-year RFS rate (Tables S3,S4). 5-year LCSS rate and 5-year RFS rate were 100% in patients with solid component size no more than 15 mm and CTR less than or equal to 0.5 (solid size ≤15 mm & CTR ≤0.5). Then we merged patients with similar 5-year LCSS rate and 5-year RFS rate to divide the PSNs into 4 modified clinical T classifications: cT1mi, PSNs with “solid size ≤5 mm” or “CTR ≤0.5 & solid size ≤15 mm”; cT1a, PSNs with “5 mm < solid size ≤10 mm & CTR >0.5” or “15 mm < solid size ≤20 mm & CTR ≤0.5”; cT1b, PSNs with “10 mm < solid size ≤20 mm & CTR >0.5” or “20 mm < solid size ≤30 mm & CTR ≤0.8”; cT1c, PSNs with “20 mm < solid size ≤30 mm & CTR >0.8” (Figure 5). Compared to the current clinical T1 classification, clinical T1 classification incorporating CTR may provide better prognostic prediction for patients with PSNs (LCSS: T1mi: 100%, T1a: 99.1%, T1b: 89.4%, T1c: 75.3%; RFS: T1mi: 100%, T1a: 98.2%, T1b: 87.6%, T1c: 66.9%) (Figure 6). Under the clinical T1 classification incorporating CTR, there was significant difference in 5-year RFS rate between groups T1mi and T1a (P=0.02), which was not found under current clinical T1 classification (P=0.76).

Bootstrap internal validation demonstrated excellent stability of the proposed staging system. The survival difference across the four T stages remained statistically significant (P<0.05) in all 100 bootstrap iterations (100%). Stage distribution was highly consistent across resamples: T1mi [median 31.8%, interquartile range (IQR): 30.8–32.7%)], T1a (median 16.1%, IQR: 15.6–16.9%), T1b (median 46.0%, IQR: 45.1–47.2%), and T1c (5.9%, IQR: 5.5–6.4%) (Table S5).

Discussion

Pulmonary nodules are radiologically categorized into three groups according to CT features: pure ground-glass opacities, PSNs, and solid nodules. Presence of the feature GGO always suggested favorable surgical outcomes for LUAD (10,21-23). Our previous study showed that pure GGO-featured LUAD should be distinguished from other LUAD because of the excellent survival (6,7). Both 10-year LCSS and RFS were 100% in patients with radiologic pure GGO-featured LUAD (24). PSNs have a better prognosis than solid nodules without GGO components (25). But there remains recurrence and lung cancer-specific death in patients with PSNs. Therefore, the appropriate prognostic stratification of PSNs may help guide treatment strategies and postoperative surveillance strategies.

Currently, in the eighth TNM staging system, the clinical T stage of part-solid GGO is suggested to be determined by the solid component because the solid component is regarded as the invasive part (13). The prognostic implications of CTR in PSNs and the cutoff value of CTR for distinguishing different prognosis remains controversial. We acknowledge that the 0.25 threshold from JCOG0201 is a pivotal benchmark for defining radiologic non-invasiveness. However, in our specific cohort of invasive T1 adenocarcinomas, we observed that patients with CTR ≤0.5 all exhibited 100% 5-year RFS and LCSS. This indicated that within our study population, the 0.25 cutoff lacked further prognostic discriminative power, whereas 0.5 emerged as a more functionally relevant threshold to delineate a subgroup with excellent prognosis. The results of JCOG1211 trial indicated that tumors that were predominantly GGO (CTR ≤0.5) and 3 cm or less in maximum tumor size had a favorable prognosis, with postoperative pathology revealing that 58% of them were AIS/MIA (26). For patients with CTR >0.5, an optimal statistical cutoff was derived using the surv_cutpoint function and clinically rounded to 0.8.

Our results indicated that, in addition to the solid component size, CTR also had a distinguishing effect on the prognosis of PSNs. Radiologic cutoffs of CTR 0.5 and 0.8 were able to subdivide patients with different prognosis. In 2016, Hattori et al. reported that the CTR was not a prognostic factor for GGNs (27). They retrospectively analyzed 448 PSNs, in which 199 (44.4%) nodules were AIS/MIA/LPA. The median follow-up period was 43 months. The 5-year LCSS rate and 5-year RFS rate of patients with AIS/MIA/LPA were 100%, regardless of the CTR value. Because of the excellent survival of AIS/MIA/LPA patients with PSNs, the impact of CTR could be weakened, which might account for the insignificant finding. Besides, regarding the favorable prognosis of PSNs, a longer follow-up period might be required to identify the prognostic implications of CTR value.

Although solid component size is a better T descriptor than tumor size, the current clinical T classification may still be inadequate for PSNs. As CTR is an independent prognostic factor for PSNs, it is then necessary to explore how to divide PSNs with CTR and solid component size. Our findings indicated that within PSNs with a solid component size greater than 5 mm, differences in CTR may significantly altered prognosis, even when the solid component sizes were similar. When we further stratified PSNs by combining the solid component size with the CTR, we observed that patients in certain groups had similar 5-year LCSS rate or 5-year RFS rate. By consolidating groups with similar 5-year LCSS rate and 5-year RFS rate, we proposed modified clinical T1 classification considering the solid component size and CTR. Under the modified clinical T1 classification, 336 patients were classified as T1mi, demonstrating a 5-year LCSS rate and 5-year RFS rate of 100%. Among them, 226 patients would have been categorized as T1a or T1b under the current clinical T1 classification. Comparison of LCSS and RFS curves among T1 subgroups revealed more significant differences when the clinical T1 classification incorporating CTR was applied. Notably, the modified clinical T1 classification identified a statistically significant difference in RFS between the T1mi and T1a subgroups (P=0.02), which was not observed under the current clinical T1 classification (P=0.76). The proposed system showed remarkable stability in internal validation, though external validation remains essential to confirm its generalizability.

Our study has several limitations that must be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the proposed prognostic model, which integrates CTR with tumor size to refine T1 stratification, is exploratory in nature. While the resulting Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated clear separation, the specific subgroup amalgamations were derived from and tested on the same single-center, retrospective cohort. This approach carries a risk of overfitting, and the model’s generalizability, particularly to non-Chinese populations and clinical settings with differing patient characteristics, requires rigorous external validation in independent, prospective cohorts. Furthermore, the clinical implications of specific reclassifications remain hypothetical and must be validated before any consideration for clinical implementation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we identified CTR as an independent prognostic factor in PSNs. Thus, we proposed incorporating CTR into the clinical T1 classification. Our results demonstrated that the modified clinical T1 classification incorporating both CTR and solid component size demonstrated enhanced prognostic discrimination for PSNs.
