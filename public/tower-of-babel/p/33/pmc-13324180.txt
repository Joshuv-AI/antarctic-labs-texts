Clinical drug report generation using multi-phase prompt large language models
Authors: Shaian Khan, Mohammadreza Akbari Lor, Morgan L Sperry, Yifei Liu, Cameron C Lindsey, Shu-Ching Chen, Mei-Ling Shyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate and timely synthesis of clinical drug information is essential for pharmacists engaged in evidence-based practice and formulary evaluation. However, generating structured summaries from diverse data sources remains time-intensive. Advances in large language models (LLMs) offer new opportunities to automate this process. To establish and evaluate a pilot inference framework that automatically generates structured preliminary clinical drug reports by integrating outputs from multiple prompt-specific language models. The framework was designed for compatibility with transformer-based LLMs, including domain-adapted and instruction-tuned variants. Nine independent prompts were implemented to extract key clinical sections: FDA-approved indications, efficacy evidence, summary of clinical findings, dosing recommendations, and adverse reaction profiles. Each section was processed individually, standardized, and automatically merged into a cohesive, human-readable report. Output reproducibility, formatting consistency, and clinical usability were qualitatively assessed across models. The framework generated structured, readable reports that preserved section-level accuracy and coherence across all evaluated models. Outputs demonstrated uniform formatting, reproducibility, and clinical interpretability, independent of the underlying LLM. The automated process effectively reduced manual synthesis time and maintained fidelity to primary clinical content. This inference framework demonstrates the feasibility of using multiple prompt-specific LLMs to automate generation of preliminary clinical drug reports. Its modular, reproducible design supports integration into pharmacy informatics and drug evaluation workflows, offering a scalable tool to enhance clinical decision-making and streamline literature synthesis.Keywords: Large Language Models (LLMs), Prompt Engineering, Pharmacy Artificial Intelligence, Clinical Drug Report, Natural Language Processing (NLP)Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLaMA) have demonstrated transformative capabilities across various domains, including clinical documentation generation, medical question answering, and biomedical knowledge synthesis1,2. LLMs such as LlaMA3, Gemma4, and BioGPT5 have opened new avenues for generating details and context-sensitive output. Despite these advancements, most LLM implementations are optimized for single-output generation tasks, with limited infrastructure to support standardization, multi-section clinical report generation. In highly regulated fields such as efficacy and safety of prescription drugs, structured output is essential for maintaining readability, reproducibility, and clinical utility.

LLMs have impacted clinical natural language processing (NLP) by enabling the generation and understanding of domain-specific medical texts. BioGPT, PubMedGPT6, and ClinicalBERT7 have been tailored for biomedical applications, including abstract summarization, question answering, and evidence-based report generation. These models often undergo pretraining or fine-tuning PubMed8 and clinical notes. Although advancements in general-purpose foundation models such as LLaMA and Gemma have broadened the applicability of transformer-based architecture in medicine, most existing pipelines are designed for isolated query-answering or unstructured summarization9.

Ensuring consistency in model output remains a challenge in clinical NLP due to variability in generation patterns, particularly when multiple models are used across batch inference jobs. Prior work explored techniques to control hallucinations and formatting drift using instruction tuning and syntactic constraints10,11. Recent research has started to emphasize the importance of model-agnostic inferences that support integration across various LLMs, such as LangChain12 and Haystack13. In contrast, task-specific pipelines for clinical NLP remain tightly coupled with the models they were trained on. Example fine-tuned implementations for electronic health records (EHR) summarization, where the inference logic is embedded into a model-specific API14,15. This coupling reduces the flexibility and scalability, particularly when multiple LLMs need to be evaluated or compared under identical inference conditions.

A pilot inference framework was developed in this study to support structured report generation through a nine-prompt architecture. Each prompt corresponds to a clinically relevant section, such as FDA-approved indication, safety profiles, efficiency, and structure markdown tables. The inference architecture analyzed in this work provides a reusable interface that decouples prompt logic from model architecture. By leveraging Hugging Face-compatible tokenizers and model wrappers, the system allows language models such as LLaMA 3 8B, Gemma 7B, and OpenChat 3.5 7B to be tested interchangeably using identical input structures. This design aligns with recent trends in zero-shot and few-shot evaluations16, where modular interfaces are essential for benchmarking across different tasks and domains. Furthermore, the emphasis on post-inference formatting and output consolidation aligns with the growing body of work in the reliability of Artificial Intelligence (AI), which highlights the need for human-verifiable model outputs17. The objective of this study was to establish and evaluate this framework.

Inference optimization and pipeline engineering

The pipeline is designed for efficient performance, consistent reproducibility, and adaptability across different computational environments. Inference is conducted in mixed-precision (FP16) mode to reduce system memory usage and improve throughput, enabling faster processing of large clinical datasets. Mixed-precision execution significantly reduces computational overhead while preserving output accuracy, which is critical for batch processing of documents spanning multiple therapeutic classes and drug profiles. To mitigate tensor reinitialization costs, memory buffers are pre-allocated prior to batched inference, allowing for reuse across prompt cycles and reducing memory fragmentation19.

Prompt engineering in the system is designed for token efficiency. Each prompt is stripped of boilerplate language and reformulated into a minimalist template that conveys semantic intent without redundancy. Contextual placeholders are inserted for document variables, and each prompt is pre-tokenized and cached in memory using a persistent token cache. This caching mechanism reduces runtime tokenization overhead and prevents repetitive decoding, especially in evaluation loops involving multiple LLMs and prompt permutations20.

The output management system enforces a structured and navigable file hierarchy, enabling traceable organization of results. Each model variant has its own directory under the Output/ is a directory. Within each model folder, text files corresponding to specific prompt responses are saved using consistent filenames, such as 1_Table1_ModelX.txt. Once all prompt responses are generated, they are aggregated into a full report and saved in the Combined_Report/ directory using LaTeX-based PDF rendering or markdown-to-PDF conversion tools. This file structure simplifies downstream comparison across LLMs and supports automated statistical pipelines.

Batch inference sessions are controlled by robust logging and input validation routines. Each step in the inference workflow is logged with timestamps, token counts, and model parameters to facilitate debugging and result replication. These design choices enable a scalable and efficient framework that maintains reproducibility while supporting large-scale biomedical report generation across diverse model architectures and datasets.

Results and discussion

Generation quality: structured table reproduction

The inference pipeline was evaluated for its capability to reproduce structured drug safety and efficacy tables from clinical literature on anti-migraine serotonin receptor agonists (Triptans), a therapeutic class widely used for acute migraine treatment. The benchmark reference was a manually curated clinical summary derived from FDA-approved labeling and established medical guidelines. This reference contained critical fields, including drug indications, contraindications, adverse effects, and clinical usage guidelines.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the generated Table 1: FDA Approved Indications closely mirrored the original reference in both structure and content. All primary Triptan agents were represented, including distinctions between “Acute Treatment of Migraine With or Without Aura” and “Acute Cluster Headache.” While minor differences in row ordering and phrasing were observed, the semantic integrity and logical organization were preserved. The generated Table 3: Safety Quick Reference also demonstrated accurate reproduction of four key columns: Drug Interactions, Monitoring, Contraindications, and Significant Adverse Effects. The system generated clinically coherent content without direct access to structured annotations, indicating that the tokenizer configuration, domain-specific prompt templates, and post-processing steps effectively guided output generation toward structured clinical formatting.

Accuracy and fidelity of textual output

Qualitative analysis was performed on descriptive narrative sections, specifically Differentiating Characteristics and Efficacy, comparing the generated outputs to manually authored summaries from peer-reviewed literature on Triptan therapies. The generated Differentiating Characteristics section correctly identified mechanistic properties such as 5-HT1B/1D receptor agonism, clinical administration guidelines (e.g., timing of dose after symptom onset), and relevant safety precautions (e.g., avoidance with nitrate therapy). Adverse effect listings, including dizziness, nausea, and muscle weakness, were accurately reproduced. The preservation of bullet-point formatting was notable, as many LLM-generated medical outputs typically lose structured formatting without specific constraints.

In the Efficacy section (Fig. 4), the generated text captured comparative trial outcomes, such as Eletriptan demonstrating statistically significant superiority to Naratriptan in headache relief at 2 h. Summarized findings included both randomized controlled trials and retrospective analyses, maintaining clinical validity and readability. However, certain limitations were noted, including omission of sample sizes and specific statistical measures in multiple-treatment studies. This indicates that while semantic content alignment was achieved, enhanced extraction logic may be required for precise quantitative reporting.

Reference dataset construction and evaluation metrics

The evaluation of clinical drug report generation requires a domain-specific benchmarking framework capable of producing consistent, reproducible, and semantically aligned comparisons between generated outputs and authoritative references. Existing publicly available corpora generally lack structural consistency, factual accuracy, and section-level granularity required for reliable evaluation in the biomedical domain. To address this limitation, a curated reference dataset was developed, integrating structured clinical knowledge from peer-reviewed literature and official regulatory documentation on Triptans. The objective was to create a structured, section-aligned resource that could serve as a benchmark for evaluating natural language generation models in the pharmacy context.

The dataset construction process began with source selection from two authoritative channels21,22. All retrieved documents were screened for relevance to Triptans and further filtered for their ability to populate defined clinical sections with source-faithful content. Extracted text was normalized, verified against original publications, and mapped to a fixed schema designed to maintain structural integrity across entries. During curation, a section-specific factuality checklist was applied to ensure that each reference field contains only source-supported clinical statements with consistent terminology and scope.

The schema was modeled on the organizational standards of professional pharmacy references, ensuring that each record mirrored the format used in clinical practice. Sections included FDA-approved indications, dosage guidance, contraindications, adverse effects, efficacy outcomes, safety considerations, and differentiating characteristics between drugs. Each field was carefully curated to remain concise yet fully representative of the clinical context, which is shown in Table 1, thereby allowing direct alignment with model-generated content. The schema was intentionally designed to be extensible so that future iterations of the dataset could incorporate other therapeutic classes and additional document types without requiring changes to the evaluation framework.

A total of 100 triptan-related articles were included in the efficacy evaluation subset, and all five LLMs produced outputs for every evaluated item, yielding 100.0% file-level coverage across models. The non-empty coverage metric reports the proportion of generated efficacy outputs that contain usable content, excluding empty or placeholder responses, and ranges from 93.0% to 100.0% across models. Once the dataset was populated, all entries underwent a normalization and preprocessing phase to standardize text formatting and reduce noise. This included harmonizing punctuation, capitalization, whitespace, and abbreviations, as well as applying alignment rules to resolve synonymous headings and remove near-duplicate entries caused by variations in source formatting. These preprocessing steps reduced inconsistencies that could impact semantic similarity calculations while preserving clinically important details.

The evaluation pipeline was implemented to enable flexible, reproducible benchmarking. The pipeline processes model-generated outputs in batches, aligns them with the corresponding sections in the reference dataset, and applies semantic evaluation metrics to quantify agreement. The configuration is modular, allowing both selective evaluation of specific drug categories and adjustment of field weights and matching thresholds to emphasize criteria such as safety relevance, dosage accuracy, or efficacy reporting.

The evaluation framework applies three established semantic and factual alignment metrics:

BERTScore_F1 Measures token-level semantic similarity between model-generated text and reference entries by computing cosine similarity over contextual embeddings derived from a pretrained BERT model23. The F1 formulation balances precision and recall, making it suitable for evaluating clinical statements that vary lexically but preserve underlying meaning.

SBERT Cosine Similarity Utilizes Sentence-BERT24 to compute sentence-level embeddings and assess semantic similarity between generated outputs and reference sentences. This method captures higher-order relationships across clinical sentences, such as paraphrasing or variations in medical terminology.

COMET COMET25(Cross-lingual Optimized Metric for Evaluation of Translation) evaluates fluency, coherence, and adequacy by leveraging pretrained encoder-decoder models trained on human-rated examples. In this evaluation pipeline, COMET scores were computed at the section level to reflect quality in fluency and factual consistency across structured medical content.

These metrics were selected to evaluate the intended behavior of the proposed prompt-engineering pipeline under a fixed, section-aligned schema. The system is designed to generate clinically coherent content that often differs lexically from the reference while preserving the same clinical meaning and section placement. BERTScore_F1 provides token-level sensitivity to clinically meaningful phrasing differences, SBERT cosine similarity captures sentence-level equivalence under paraphrasing and terminology variation, and COMET reflects section-level adequacy and coherence relative to the curated reference structure. Together, these measures quantify how well the prompt bank and deterministic post-processing reproduce a standardized clinical representation rather than rewarding superficial n-gram overlap. However, these scores remain reference-alignment measures and do not constitute direct claim-level evidence verification against source spans or explicit hallucination rate estimation.

All evaluations were executed through a custom-designed inference pipeline that manages input-output alignment, model scoring, and result aggregation. The evaluation outputs were logged and visualized primarily on the efficacy prompt, as it represents a structured and content-rich section. The inference logic for adverse effects, dosage information, and tabular summaries shares a common architectural backbone with only minor differences in the prompt design and post-processing routines. Within each table-specific prompt, article-level information is embedded using predefined formatting logic. The unique functionality lies in how the inference mechanism dynamically organizes and inserts extracted content into the structured table layout, preserving consistency across different clinical dimensions. This targeted approach enables modular evaluation and serves as a foundation for robust benchmarking in pharmacological text generation tasks.

Evaluation metrics for factual overlap

Performance was assessed using BERTScore_F1, SBERT cosine similarity, and COMET metrics, replacing BLEU and ROUGE evaluations to emphasize semantic similarity and reference-faithful alignment under a checklist-curated schema. Structural fidelity is enforced by schema-constrained prompting and deterministic post-processing, and representative outputs are illustrated in Figs. 3 and 4.

The evaluation dataset comprised a curated CSV reference containing field-level tabular and narrative ground truth entries. In this experiment, a custom LLaMA 3 8B model was developed and trained on PubMed data, with specialization in biomedical literature processing. The objective was to evaluate the capability of this domain-adapted model against a set of nine independent prompts when compared with four benchmark models: OpenChat 3.5 7B26, Mistral 7B v0.127, Meta LLaMA 3 8B (Base), and Gemma 7B Instruction. This comparison aimed to measure how domain-specific fine-tuned LLM influences the structured reproduction of clinical drug reports within a domain-specific context.

The OpenChat 3.5 7B model achieved the highest BERTScore_F1 (81.12) and SBERT cosine similarity (79.52), indicating the strongest semantic alignment with the curated reference outputs. The Custom LLaMA 3 8B model ranked closely behind with a BERTScore_F1 of 80.58 and a competitive COMET score of 50, demonstrating strong reference alignment, with COMET reflecting section-level adequacy and coherence relative to the curated reference. Mistral 7B v0.1 and Meta LLaMA 3 8B (Base) showed similar performance, each achieving BERTScore_F1 values slightly above 80, while Gemma 7B Instruction scored the lowest across all metrics, with a BERTScore_F1 of 77.81, SBERT cosine similarity of 60.83, and a COMET score of 49. These results indicate limited suitability for domain-specific structured text reproduction without fine-tuning.

Limitation (quantitative extraction and evidence grounding)

The evaluation framework in Section III.C quantifies section-level reference alignment under a checklist-curated schema; however, several limitations remain for quantitative extraction and evidence grounding. Despite the structure reported in the table-oriented prompts and the high semantic alignment scores in Table 4, the current framework has measurable limitations in quantitative extraction. As noted in the Efficacy section analysis (Fig. 4), outputs can omit sample sizes and specific statistical measures in multiple-treatment studies, even when the narrative summary remains clinically valid and readable.

The primary cause is the variability in how quantitative evidence is expressed across biomedical sources. Key statistics are frequently embedded in heterogeneous formats, including dense prose, multi-row tables, and subgroup analyses that reference multiple cohorts or treatment arms. Ambiguity is further introduced when publications report outcomes across stratified populations, crossover designs, pooled analyses, or secondary endpoints without consistently repeating the associated denominators and arm identifiers. Under these conditions, a single prompt-driven generation strategy can summarize comparative findings while failing to reliably bind numeric values to the correct treatment arm, endpoint, and cohort context. That is why the Multi-Phase Prompt is to get a readable and structured report, which has dense prose, multi-row tables, and subgroup analyses.

A related limitation is the lack of evidence grounding at the claim level. The pipeline supports traceability through structured schema alignment, standardized output sections, and reproducible file naming; however, the generated narratives do not currently carry explicit evidence alignments to source spans that justify individual quantitative claims. As a result, semantic metrics such as BERTScore_F1, SBERT cosine similarity, and COMET can indicate overall similarity while remaining insensitive to unsupported or weakly supported numeric statements. Addressing these limitations requires technical augmentation beyond prompt and formatting control. A practical extension is to integrate entity and relation extraction for quantitative variables (arm, endpoint, measure, statistic) alongside table parsing for structured recovery of denominators and arm-level outcomes. Evidence alignment can then be enforced by mapping each extracted quantitative claim to supporting source text spans, enabling automated validation layers for hallucination detection and factual consistency, and supporting downstream expert-in-the-loop review for safety-critical sections. Claim-level hallucination rates, numeric verification against source spans, and formal structural fidelity scoring were not computed and are prioritized as future validation steps enabled by evidence-span alignment.

Multilingual extension and regulatory document integration

Although the present evaluation focuses on English-language clinical sources and a curated reference derived from authoritative clinical and regulatory materials, the modular design of the prompt bank , the document interface , and the schema-aware transformation functions supports multilingual extension and expanded regulatory integration without altering the core pipeline. Within this framework, multilingual scalability can be formulated as an input normalization and evidence alignment problem, where heterogeneous source languages are mapped into the same fixed clinical schema used for section-level generation and deterministic post-processing.

Two implementation configurations are technically compatible with the existing architecture. The first is a translate-then-extract workflow, in which non-English inputs are translated into English prior to executing the established prompt templates, post-processing rules, and table alignment logic. The second is direct multilingual extraction, in which prompts are localized and executed on the original language text using a multilingual-capable model while enforcing the same output schema and formatting constraints. In both configurations, reliable multilingual performance requires controlled normalization of drug names, adverse event terminology, endpoint phrasing, units, and abbreviations, since surface-form variation can propagate section inconsistency and degrade quantitative fidelity. While COMET is included in the evaluation pipeline as a cross-lingual, translation-oriented metric, multilingual deployment requires verification beyond semantic similarity, particularly for safety-critical statements and numeric expressions that are sensitive to translation artifacts, negation, and cohort qualifiers.

Regulatory document integration provides a complementary extension that strengthens authority, traceability, and deployment relevance. The current reference construction incorporates regulatory documentation for alignment and benchmarking in the triptan domain, and the pipeline can be extended by treating regulatory artifacts as first-class evidence sources. This includes structured ingestion of regulatory documents such as risk management plans and clinical protocols, as well as jurisdiction-specific materials that encode indications, contraindications, warnings, and adverse reaction profiles in standardized language. Technically, regulatory integration can be implemented as a retrieval-conditioned evidence layer attached to each prompt , where relevant regulatory passages are retrieved, segmented, and aligned to schema fields prior to generation and deterministic formatting. This design supports evidence grounding by constraining section outputs to verifiable source spans, enabling consistency.

checks for contraindications, monitoring requirements, and major safety statements, and reducing unsupported content in narrative summaries. Regulatory integration also introduces requirements for version control and source-precedence rules, since discrepancies can occur between peer-reviewed trial reporting and jurisdictional labeling. Section-level governance can prioritize regulatory authority for indications and safety claims while preserving literature-grounded synthesis for comparative efficacy narratives.

From an application perspective, multilingual scalability and regulatory integration extend utility to multinational formulary evaluation, cross-jurisdiction safety synthesis, and pharmacy informatics workflows where authoritative labeling and protocol constraints must be represented consistently. When combined with the quantitative extraction and evidence grounding improvements identified in the limitations analysis, these extensions provide a pathway toward globally applicable, regulator-aware, and auditable clinical report generation under a unified schema and reproducible inference pipeline.

Conclusion and future work

The inference framework presented in this study demonstrates the potential to generate structured, clinically relevant drug reports from unstructured biomedical literature using a modular, multi-prompt architecture. This design enables standardized output, section-level reproducibility, and interoperability across multiple LLMs without retraining28. Through the integration of targeted prompts, domain-specific schema alignment, and deterministic post-processing, the system consistently produced outputs that maintained both semantic and structural fidelity with reference documents, supporting its use in drug evaluation workflows.

Evaluation results confirm that LLMs, when paired with schema-aware formatting, can generate FDA-compliant sections, safety references, and efficacy summaries that align with clinical practice. Models such as OpenChat 3.5 7B and the domain-trained Custom LLaMA 3 8B achieved higher semantic alignment scores, as shown by BERTScore F1, SBERT cosine similarity, and COMET metrics. Performance variations across models emphasize the role of optimized prompting, post-processing strategies, and curated datasets in ensuring factual accuracy.

Future development will target expanding the dataset to cover and incorporate regulatory documents such as risk management plans and clinical protocols. Improving quantitative extraction capabilities will address current limitations in capturing sample sizes, statistical parameters, and detailed trial results. Additionally, integrating automated validation layers for hallucination detection and factual consistency will enhance output reliability. Efforts will also be directed toward multi-lingual scalability to promote global accessibility and adoption in diverse healthcare settings. Furthermore, clinician evaluation and expert-in-the-loop feedback can be introduced to assess the interpretability and trustworthiness of generated outputs. These evaluations can be incorporated into continuous feedback and alignment pipelines designed to improve system performance without dependence on a specific model architecture29.
