Optimization of metabolizable energy prediction models for maize in laying hens by incorporating anti-nutritional factors
Authors: Zhonghao Liu, Jinsheng Qin, Shimeng Huang, Lihong Zhao, Qiugang Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to improve the prediction of AME and AMEn of maize for laying hens by evaluating the contribution of anti-nutritional factors (ANF) as predictor variables. Prediction models were developed using two sets of independent variables: a nutrient-only set (starch, crude protein, and ether extract; IVC1) and an extended set additionally including ANF (crude fiber, total arabinoxylan, ash and phytic acid; IVC2). Five algorithms (Linear Regression, Ridge, LASSO, Elastic Net, and Random Forest, RF) were trained with Bayesian hyperparameter optimization and evaluated using repeated nested cross-validation (3 × 10-fold) to obtain unbiased performance estimates. With nutrient-only inputs, RF achieved the highest accuracy, with R²_CV values of 0.645 for AME and 0.669 for AMEn, and RMSE_CV values of 22.9 and 21.3 kcal/kg DM, respectively. Including ANF further improved RF performance to R²_CV = 0.748 (RMSE_CV = 19.1 kcal/kg DM) for AME and R²_CV = 0.758 (RMSE_CV = 18.1 kcal/kg DM) for AMEn. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirmed that the improvements from IVC1 to IVC2 were consistent for all algorithms (P < 0.001).In conclusion, incorporating ANF as predictor variables substantially improves the prediction of maize AME and AMEn for laying hens beyond conventional nutrient composition. Among the tested algorithms, RF combined with Bayesian hyperparameter optimization and repeated nested cross-validation provided the most accurate and robust models.Keywords: Maize, laying hen, Anti-nutritional factor, Metabolizable energy, Prediction-model

Introduction

Maize is a main energy feedstuff in livestock diets (Liu et al., 2024), with over 50% of the world's maize used for this purpose. To balance animal requirements with feed cost management, accurate measurement of the available energy in maize is essential for formulating optimal livestock and poultry diets (Zaefarian et al., 2021). Since the 1950s, the AME and AMEn system has been the universally accepted standard for assessing poultry energy requirements and the usable energy content of various feed ingredients (Lopez and Leeson, 2008; Tay-Zar et al., 2024). However, traditional animal experiments to determine AME and AMEn are time-consuming, labor-intensive and costly (Meloche et al., 2014; Zaefarian et al., 2021). Therefore, developing reliable predictive models for maize AME and AMEn is crucial for optimizing diet formulations to maintain poultry health and achieve optimal production performance.

In previous models to predict AME and AMEn, most approaches have used nutritional indicators such as enzymatic hydrolysate gross energy, crude protein (CP), ether extract (EE), and starch to develop simple linear prediction models (Rochell et al., 2011; Meloche et al., 2014; Latifi et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2024). Although these models have provided valuable insights, their accuracy may be compromised by the omission of dietary anti-nutritional factors (ANF), such as crude fiber (CF), total arabinoxylan (TAX) and phytic acid (PA), which are known to impair the digestion and absorption of proteins, minerals, and energy substrates in poultry (Choct and Annison, 1990; Woyengo and Nyachoti, 2013; Liu et al., 2016). Therefore, ANF also contain valuable information for the prediction of AME as well as AMEn; we hypothesized that incorporating ANF into predictive models can improve the accuracy and stability of estimating AME and AMEn in maize for laying hens.

Mathematical models are widely used in precision animal nutrition, translating biological knowledge and empirical data into a quantitative framework to explain complex processes and predict outcomes (Dumas et al., 2008; Torres and Santos, 2015; Zhang et al., 2025). This approach is critical for evaluating feed ingredients like maize, where models must account for the complex interactions between nutrients and ANF to accurately predict metabolizable energy.

Linear regression modeling is a prevalent analytical method in the field of agriculture, particularly in the context of studies pertaining to livestock and poultry nutritional requirements, meat quality, and feed nutrition (Jie et al., 2013; Souza et al., 2017; Jo et al., 2022; Goethals et al., 2025). In all real-world data, there is a degree of collinearity between the predictor variables (Dormann et al., 2012). When the composition of maize itself (whether nutritional or ANF) is used as independent variables to predict its energy, the individual independent variables cannot avoid strong linear correlations (i.e., multicollinearity). This can make the linear regression model unstable and reduce its predictive performance (Giacalone et al., 2017). To address multicollinearity, advanced modeling techniques can be employed. These include regularized regression models, such as ridge, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), and elastic net, which mitigate the issue by penalizing correlated features (Cleophas and Zwinderman, 2013). In parallel, ensemble machine learning models such as Random Forest (RF) are inherently robust to this problem due to their ability to handle correlated features through random feature selection and bootstrap aggregation (Kaveh et al., 2023).

Beyond model selection, hyperparameter optimization plays a critical role in maximizing model performance. Traditional grid search methods are computationally expensive, particularly for models with multiple hyperparameters. Bayesian optimization, which uses probabilistic surrogate models to guide the search for optimal hyperparameters, is an efficient alternative in machine learning applications (Song et al., 2021). Furthermore, robust model validation is essential to avoid overfitting and ensure generalizability. Nested cross-validation provides unbiased performance estimates by separating hyperparameter tuning from model evaluation, while repeated k-fold cross-validation reduces variance in performance metrics (Song et al., 2021; Yates et al., 2023).

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate whether incorporating ANF (CF, TAX, ash, PA) as predictor variables can improve the accuracy of maize AME and AMEn prediction models for laying hens. To evaluate model generalizability, we employed multiple regression approaches including linear regression, regularized regression (Ridge, LASSO, Elastic Net), and machine learning algorithms (RF). All models were optimized using Bayesian optimization and validated through repeated nested cross-validation (3 × 10-fold). The predictive performance between models with (IVC2) and without ANF (IVC1) was statistically compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All experimental procedures were approved by the China Agricultural University Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval No. Aw32214202-1-1) and conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Agricultural Animals in Research and Teaching (Federation of Animal Science Societies, 2020).

Experimental materials

A total of 247 maize samples were collected from the maize producing area in Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, China, with the help of Xinjiang Tiankang Feed Co., Ltd. The samples were stored in sealed vacuum bags at 4°C until further analysis.

Detection methods of AME and AMEn contents

AME values were determined using the total excreta collection method (Wu et al., 2020). A total of 2,223 healthy female Hy-Line brown laying hens (2.00 ± 0.05 kg) at 30 weeks of age were used to determine the AME and AMEn of 247 maize samples. For each sample, 9 hens (one hen per cage) were assigned as replicates for metabolic balance trials. They were trained to consume the diet provided as soon as possible for 1 week prior to the experiment. Each sample of maize was ground using a hammer mill with a sieve of 4 mm official diameter. During the 7-day test period, each hen was fed 80 g/day of the specific maize sample only, divided equally into two feedings at 0800 and 1600 h. To avoid vitamin and mineral deficiencies, an electrolyte and vitamin complex product was administered in the drinking water according to the commercial manual. After an adaptation period of 3 d, the mixture of feces and urine of the test birds was collected continuously for 48 h using a collection tray. Feces were also collected from 9 hens that had been fasted for 24 h in order to simultaneously estimate basal endogenous energy. The fecal sample was transferred to a plastic bag and then frozen at −20°C until further analysis.

Chemical analyses

The excreta samples were thoroughly homogenized, and subsamples were taken for analysis. The contents of DM, CP, EE, CF, and ash in maize and excreta samples were analyzed according to Chinese national standards (China National Standard, 2006, China National Standard, 2007, China National Standard, 2014, China National Standard, 2018, China National Standard, 2022), and starch was determined by the enzymatic method (China National Standard, 2023). Total arabinoxylan content was measured using the phloroglucinol colorimetric method (Douglas, 1981; Rouau and Surget, 1994), and PA content was determined using a phytic acid assay kit (K-PHYT, Megazyme International, Bray, Ireland; Perera et al., 2019). Results were expressed on a DM basis.

Data preprocessing

Outliers were identified on a variable-by-variable basis using the Z-score method (Chandola et al., 2009; Hair, 2011). For each variable, observations with absolute Z-scores exceeding 3 were flagged. Samples flagged as outliers for any variable were entirely excluded from subsequent analyses, as partial data imputation was considered inappropriate for this study. After outlier removal, 230 complete samples remained for model development.

Statistical analysis, modeling, and software

This experiment used a completely randomized design. The dataset was compiled and organized using Microsoft Excel 2021 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA). All statistical analyses, including Pearson correlation analysis, model development, and visualization, were performed using Python (version 3.13.2).

The data included two outcome variables: AME (kcal/kg, DM) and AMEn (kcal/kg, DM), and we considered two feature combinations: Independent Variable Combination 1 (IVC1), containing only nutrients (starch, crude protein [CP], ether extract [EE]); and Independent Variable Combination 2 (IVC2), containing nutrients and ANF (starch, CP, EE, CF, TAX, ash, PA). To evaluate whether incorporating ANF improves prediction accuracy, we employed a multi-model framework including linear regression, regularized regression (Ridge, LASSO, Elastic Net), and RF. All features were standardized using StandardScaler to ensure consistent scaling, which is required for the regularized linear models (LASSO, Ridge, ElasticNet). Although tree-based models such as Random Forest are invariant to monotonic feature transformations and do not require standardization, we applied uniform preprocessing across all models for pipeline consistency. This does not affect the Random Forest predictions, as verified by the mathematical properties of decision tree splits.

Several Python libraries facilitated data processing, analysis, visualization and machine learning tasks. Pandas and NumPy handled data manipulation and numerical computation. Matplotlib and Seaborn were used for visualization. Scikit-learn provided machine learning tools including cross-validation, regression models, and model evaluation. Optuna was employed for Bayesian hyperparameter optimization. SciPy provided statistical tests including Pearson correlation and Wilcoxon signed-rank test. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) was used for model interpretability analysis. Joblib was used for model saving and loading. MAPIE was used for conformal prediction to calculate prediction intervals.

For hyperparameter optimization, we employed Bayesian optimization using the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) algorithm implemented in Optuna. For model evaluation, we implemented repeated nested cross-validation with 3 repetitions of 10-fold outer cross-validation, yielding 30 outer test folds in total. Within each outer training fold, hyperparameter optimization was performed using 5-fold inner cross-validation with 30 Bayesian optimization trials per model. The inner loop selected optimal hyperparameters for each algorithm, whereas the outer loop provided unbiased estimates of generalization performance by testing on held-out data unseen during hyperparameter tuning. Performance metrics including coefficient of determination (R²), root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) were calculated with 95% confidence intervals. To statistically compare the predictive performance between IVC1 and IVC2, we performed the Wilcoxon signed-rank test on paired cross-validation scores (n = 30 per model), with statistical significance set at α = 0.05. Model performance evaluation metrics

The prediction models were evaluated using multiple performance metrics calculated through repeated nested cross-validation (3 repeats × 10-fold outer CV, totaling 30 evaluations) to obtain unbiased estimates. The coefficient of determination (R²) quantified the proportion of variance explained by the model. The root mean squared error (RMSE) measured average prediction deviations in original units (kcal/kg DM). The mean absolute error (MAE) provided average absolute deviations, whereas the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) expressed errors as percentages for scale-independent assessment. For prediction reliability, we calculated 95% prediction intervals using conformal prediction methods. The prediction interval coverage probability (PICP) measured the percentage of observed values falling within predicted intervals, with values close to 95% indicating well-calibrated uncertainty estimates. The mean prediction interval width (MPIW) quantified average interval width in kcal/kg DM, where narrower intervals indicate higher prediction precision. All metrics were reported with 95% confidence intervals derived from the repeated CV procedure. Statistical comparison between IVC1 and IVC2 feature sets was performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with effect sizes reported as Cohen's d. The detailed mathematical formulas and calculations for each metric are available in supplemental file 1.

Feature importance of independent variables

Feature importance (FI) was assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values, which provide theoretically grounded, model-agnostic explanations based on cooperative game theory (Lundberg and Lee, 2017). SHAP values quantify each feature's contribution to individual predictions by calculating the average marginal contribution across all possible feature combinations. For the RF model, TreeExplainer was applied for computationally efficient exact SHAP value calculation (Lundberg et al., 2020). For linear models (multiple linear regression, Ridge, LASSO, and ElasticNet), SHAP values were computed using LinearExplainer, which exploits the linear model structure to derive exact Shapley values. LinearExplainer computes SHAP values directly from the fitted model coefficients. For regularized models (Ridge, LASSO, and ElasticNet), these coefficients correspond to the penalized estimates obtained during model training. Therefore, the resulting SHAP values quantify each feature's contribution to the model's predictions as learned by the regularized model, rather than representing unbiased estimates of the underlying feature effects. The mean absolute SHAP value for each feature across all samples indicates its overall importance to the model output and SHAP beeswarm plots visualize both feature importance ranking and the direction of effects on predictions (Ponce‐Bobadilla et al., 2024). Additionally, SHAP dependence plots reveal how individual feature values relate to their SHAP contributions, identifying potential nonlinear relationships and interaction effects (Nohara et al., 2022).

ANF integration improves the performance of maize metabolizable energy prediction model

Using two alternative combinations of independent variables (IVC1 and IVC2), a total of 20 prediction models were developed (5 algorithms × 2 feature sets × 2 targets). Model performance was evaluated using 3 × 10 repeated cross-validation, and the reported R²_CV, RMSE_CV, MAE_CV and MAPE_CV values represent the means across all cross-validation iterations. Conformal prediction was used to derive prediction interval coverage probability (Conformal PICP) and mean prediction interval width (Conformal MPIW).

For maize AME prediction, models based on IVC1 (starch, CP and EE) showed moderate performance (Table 2; Fig. 3). Across the four linear models (LinearRegression, LASSO, Ridge and ElasticNet), R²_CV values ranged from 0.607 to 0.619 with RMSE_CV values of 23.14 to 23.63 kcal/kg DM and MAPE_CV values of 0.45 to 0.46%, whereas the RF model achieved an R²_CV of 0.645, an RMSE_CV of 22.9 kcal/kg DM, an MAE_CV of 16.89 kcal/kg DM and a MAPE_CV of 0.44%, with Conformal PICP of 91.30% and MPIW of 90.31 kcal/kg DM.

Among all five algorithms tested, RF consistently achieved the highest R²_CV in every prediction scenario and was therefore selected as the best model for both AME and AMEn (Table 5). A direct comparison of the RF models revealed clear benefits from including ANF as additional inputs. For AME, switching from IVC1 to IVC2 raised R²_CV from 0.645 to 0.748, lowered RMSE_CV from 22.9 to 19.1 kcal/kg DM and reduced MAPE_CV from 0.44% to 0.36%. For AMEn, the same transition improved R²_CV from 0.669 to 0.758, decreased RMSE_CV from 21.3 to 18.1 kcal/kg DM and reduced MAPE_CV from 0.42% to 0.35%. Both model configurations maintained adequate prediction interval coverage (Conformal PICP > 91%) with stable interval widths.

Discussion

The nutrient content and ME levels of maize are variable, depending on the variety, origin, moisture content, storage time, and grain size (Li et al., 2014; Park et al., 2014; Huart et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2020; Niu et al., 2023; Stefanello et al., 2023). Compared to the National Research Council (National Research Council, 1994) data, our study found higher mean AMEn and starch concentrations, but lower mean DM, CP, EE, CF, and PA levels. The mean ash concentration was also lower than previous study (Li et al., 2024). In comparison to the latest reported values for ME and nutrient composition of maize from the recent feeding tables (CVB, 2022; INRAE-CIRAD-AFZ, 2019), our maize samples exhibited higher levels of AME. However, the contents of CP, ash, EE, and CF remained similar. Furthermore, compared to other studies, the AME and AMEn of the maize samples were similar to the values reported by Barzegar et al. (2019b), which indicated an AME of 3791 kcal/kg and an AMEn of 3722 kcal/kg. More recent results from Li et al. indicate that the AME of maize ranges from 3458 to 3601 kcal/kg (Li et al., 2024). These variations are likely to be due to differences in maize variety, feed formulation, particle size, age of birds, poultry breeds and production stage, as these factors have been shown to influence nutrient content and ME values (Li et al., 2014; Stefanello et al., 2016; Mateos et al., 2019; Noblet et al., 2022). Most variables showed CoV values within 10%, while CF and PA exhibited slightly higher variability. This is likely because our samples were all collected from Xinjiang, where the crop varieties are relatively similar and the climatic conditions on the farms are fairly consistent. Starch showed the lowest CoV (0.56%), corresponding to a narrow absolute range of 72.88 to 75.34% DM, consistent with the inherently high and stable starch content of maize grain. Both PDP and SHAP analyses confirmed that starch remained among the most influential predictors in the present models despite this limited variability, indicating that small absolute differences in starch content carried meaningful predictive information for AME and AMEn. The CoV of AME and AMEn was also low, and the models were trained and validated within a narrow energy range. Achieving R² values of 0.748 and 0.758 within such a restricted range indicates that the models captured genuine compositional-energy relationships at a fine resolution, rather than relying on large-scale variation to inflate predictive accuracy. The low MAPE values indicate that the model has good practical predictive performance within a similar compositional range. However, the relatively narrow variation in both predictor and target variables may constrain its generalizability. Future studies should validate the model using maize samples from broader geographic origins to further evaluate its robustness and applicability.

Compared to other grains, maize possesses a higher starch content and has long been widely used as an energy source in livestock and poultry feed (Liu et al., 2020). Lasek et al. predicted the AMEn of maize kernels based on crude nutrient content, revealing a highly positive correlation between the starch content in maize and AMEn (Lasek et al., 2020). Other studies have shown a positive correlation between EE and ME (Shi et al., 2015). Yang et al. observed that ME was negatively correlated with CP, neutral detergent fiber (NDF), and acid detergent fiber (ADF), which is consistent with recent findings showing a negative correlation between AME and CP, ash, NDF and ADF (P < 0.01) (Yang et al., 2020; Li et al., 2024). These correlation patterns are consistent with previous reports showing positive associations of starch and EE with ME (Ball et al., 2013; Aderibigbe et al., 2020; Phuah et al., 2022) and negative associations of fibrous components with energy availability (Wu et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2020; Li et al., 2024). PA showed no significant correlation with either AME or AMEn in our study, despite being traditionally considered an anti-nutritional factor due to its strong metal-chelating properties (Humer et al., 2015). This lack of correlation may be attributed to the relatively low and consistent PA content in our maize samples, which may not have been sufficient to exert detectable effects on energy availability. Recent studies suggest that gut microorganisms such as Mitsuokella jalaludinii can efficiently degrade phytic acid (De Vos et al., 2024), since SCFAs serve as an energy source for the host (Cani et al., 2019), which may explain the lack of correlation between PA and metabolizable energy in our samples.

Previous studies have focused on the nutrients content of maize, including CP, EE, and starch, to predict its AME (Li et al., 2014, 2024). However, these studies often neglect the effects of ANF such as crude fiber, total arabinoxylan, ash, and phytic acid, which can affect nutrient absorption and utilization. In our study, incorporating ANF as independent variables improved the performance of all models across both target variables, with RF consistently achieving the highest absolute performance under both feature set conditions. This improvement indicates that ANF capture additional variance in energy availability beyond what conventional nutrients alone can explain, likely because ANF directly interfere with the digestion and absorption of energy-yielding substrates (Choct and Annison, 1990; Liu et al., 2016; Woyengo and Nyachoti, 2013). Both model configurations maintained adequate prediction interval coverage with stable interval widths, supporting the robustness of these findings.

Utilizing regression equations to predict AME and AMEn can reduce the time and cost of animal metabolism experiments and enhance the accuracy of energy value estimation (Noblet and Perez, 1993). Linear regression is widely used to predict the AME of feed stuffs or feed ingredients (Jie et al., 2013; Li et al., 2014; Barzegar et al., 2019a; Yang et al., 2020). However, few studies have considered the impact of multicollinearity on models. In our research, we compared the performance of simple linear regression models, regularized linear regression models (Ridge, LASSO, ElasticNet), and more complex machine learning model (RF) in predicting maize AME and AMEn. The results showed that although regularized models introduced penalty terms to mitigate the impact of multicollinearity, they had no effect on the predictive performance of the models. Compared with linear regression models, using the RF model allowed us to develop prediction models with better performance, which may indicate that more complex nonlinear relationships exist between the nutritional components and ANF of maize and AME and AMEn.

The positive effects of starch and EE on AME and AMEn are consistent with their roles as primary energy-providing nutrients in maize. Starch, as the major carbohydrate component, is efficiently digested and absorbed in poultry, directly contributing to metabolizable energy (Moran, 1982). Fat has an extra caloric effect, contributing more metabolizable energy to the diet than expected from its gross energy value. This may explain the strong positive effect of EE on predicted AME and AMEn (Wu et al., 2020; National Research Council, 1994; Ravindran et al., 2016). CP exhibited a positive partial dependence on predicted AME and AMEn in the IVC1 models, despite showing a negative bivariate correlation with AME and AMEn. This reversal can be explained by the strong negative inter-correlation between CP and starch. In the bivariate analysis, higher CP effectively acts as a proxy for lower starch content, the dominant energy-providing substrate, resulting in a negative marginal association with AME. a negative marginal association with AME. When the model conditions on starch and EE simultaneously, this confounding pathway is removed, and the residual effect of CP becomes positive. This is biologically plausible, as digested protein contributes to metabolizable energy through deamination and oxidation of amino acids. However, this conditional effect was small, consistent with the low SHAP importance of CP in IVC1 models. The negative effects of CF, TAX, and ash observed in IVC2 models align with their known anti-nutritional properties. The negative effect of CF, ash and TAX may act as a diluent, thus lowering the energy content of maize (Li et al., 2014; Jha and Mishra, 2021). PA displayed a weak positive effect on predicted energy values, which appears counterintuitive given its known anti-nutritional properties. This weak positive signal may be partly attributable to the positive correlation between PA and EE. If the model does not fully partition the shared variance between PA and EE, part of EE’s positive contribution to predicted energy may be inadvertently attributed to PA, the low SHAP importance of PA supports this interpretation. The narrow and low PA concentration range in our samples may also have been insufficient to produce a detectable negative effect on energy availability.

In linear regression models, the coefficients of independent variables directly indicate their contributions to the model, providing clear interpretability. However, in complex machine learning models such as RF, interpretability is often compromised due to their higher complexity and non-linear interactions between variables (Linardatos et al., 2021; Rengasamy et al., 2022). SHAP analysis revealed that EE and starch were the dominant predictors across all model configurations, which aligns with their established roles as the primary energy-contributing nutrients in maize. The inclusion of ANF in IVC2 redistributed feature importance, the TAX and ash gained substantial explanatory roles, whereas the contributions of EE and starch were attenuated. This redistribution suggests that in IVC1 models, EE and starch partially absorbed variance attributable to the omitted ANF, and that including ANF allowed the model to assign attribution more precisely to the features directly responsible for energy reduction. The directional patterns identified by SHAP were consistent with the PDP results, reinforcing the biological plausibility of the learned relationships. SHAP dependence analysis further revealed that the positive contribution of EE was amplified in high-starch samples, and the negative impact of TAX was stronger in samples with higher EE, suggesting that energy utilization in maize is governed not only by individual component levels but also by interactions among nutrients and ANF.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study shows that including anti-nutritional factors (ANF) as predictor variables improves the estimation of maize AME and AMEn for laying hens. With nutrient-only inputs (starch, crude protein, and ether extract; IVC1), the RF model outperformed linear and regularized regression, achieving R²_CV values of 0.645 for AME and 0.669 for AMEn, with RMSE_CV of 22.9 and 21.3 kcal/kg DM, respectively. The addition of ANF improved RF performance, increasing R²_CV = 0.748 for AME and 0.758 for AMEn and reduced RMSE_CV to 19.1 and 18.1 kcal/kg DM, and these improvements were confirmed (P < 0.001). These results indicate that ANF explain additional variation in energy availability beyond conventional nutrients, and future prediction equations for maize fed to laying hens should include key ANF. Among the tested methods, RF combined with Bayesian hyperparameter optimization and repeated nested cross-validation provided the most accurate and robust predictions.
