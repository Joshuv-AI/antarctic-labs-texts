Understanding the effectiveness of mHealth interventions incorporating behaviour change techniques in reducing sitting time in older adults: a protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Siqing Chen, Chen Wu, Carol Ewing Garber, Matthew Stults-Kolehmainen, Edward Giovannucci, Lili Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo address the growing prevalence of sedentary behaviours among older adults and their associated adverse health outcomes, there is an urgent need to prioritise effective and accessible interventions. Mobile health (mHealth) delivers healthcare services and health-related information through portable electronic devices, enabling interventions to be administered directly in home settings. However, the evidence on the effectiveness of mHealth interventions in reducing sitting time among older adults remains inconsistent. To derive literature-based estimates of the effectiveness of mHealth interventions, we will systematically review and meta-analyse the impact of these interventions on sitting time in adults aged 55 years and older.Methods and analysisAn electronic search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and Cochrane will be conducted from database inception to March 2025 to identify randomised controlled trials evaluating the effects of mHealth interventions on sitting time during waking hours, excluding sleep duration. Subgroup analyses will explore potential moderators (eg, participant characteristics, intervention specifics). Studies from all settings (eg, community, long-term care facilities, etc) will be considered. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines will be explicitly applied for structuring this report. Methodological quality will be assessed using the Cochrane Handbook tool. Two independent reviewers will screen the studies and extracted data, with methodological quality to be assessed using established criteria. Meta-analyses will be performed using Review Manager v.5.4 software.Ethics and disseminationThis study uses secondary data and, therefore, does not require ethics approval. The findings will be communicated through presentations at international conferences and published in peer-reviewed journals. The results will inform the development of future mHealth interventions aimed at reducing sedentary behaviour in older adults and provide benchmarks for the effectiveness of technology-driven public health strategies. This protocol adheres to the PRISMA guidelines and follows the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions for methodological rigour.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42023443926.

Introduction

In recent years, the global population of older adults has been rapidly increasing, with projections indicating a doubling of the older adult population to approximately 2 billion by 2050.1 This trend is particularly pronounced in China, where the ageing population forms a significant and rapidly expanding segment.1 Statistics show that individuals aged ≥60 years account for 18.9% of the total population globally.1 Within this demographic, 75% of older people have at least one chronic health condition, and 43% are experiencing multiple comorbidities, highlighting a widespread challenge in global public health.2 The ‘Healthy China 2030’ initiative emphasises the vital importance of cultivating healthy lifestyles to enhance the population’s overall well-being. It advocates for tailored health interventions aimed at key demographic groups, particularly older adults, to promote healthy ageing.

Sedentary behaviour, characterised by activities with low energy expenditure (<1.5 metabolic equivalents), while in a sitting, reclining or lying posture,3 is a significant concern. Currently, there is no universally accepted standard for classifying sedentary behaviour. Previous studies47 have defined individuals as those with ≥5 to ≥8 hours of daily sitting time. Building on this evidence, Chinese scholars further proposed criteria of ≥6 hours of sitting per day, ≥1.5 hours of uninterrupted sitting and insufficient physical activity (<150 minutes/week or <5000 steps/day).5

Prolonged sedentary behaviour is associated with an increased risk of chronic diseases, such as cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes and chronic liver disease, as well as a higher all-cause mortality rate.68 However, current strategies to promote healthy ageing often prioritise physical activity, overlooking the adverse effects of a sedentary lifestyle,911 an independent risk factor for adverse health outcomes, separate from physical activity.10 12 Sedentary behaviour negatively impacts health (especially cardiometabolic health), such as increasing their risk of chronic diseases, contributing to cognitive decline, muscle loss and premature death.9 13 14

Older adults have the highest duration of daily sedentary time and the least physical activity compared with younger adults.1315 Screen-based entertainment is highly prevalent among older adults, accounting for up to 80% of their waking hours spent in sedentary behaviours.15 Studies show that nearly 70% of older adults are sedentary up to 8.5 hours per day.16 17 18 The issues of lower physical activity levels and higher sedentary behaviours in older adults have become increasingly prominent in public health efforts.9 14 19 Effective interventions to reduce sedentary time are crucial for older adults to maintain their health and independence. Against this backdrop, reducing sedentary behaviour in older adults has been listed as a priority item on the public health agenda by the WHO, calling for countries to formulate targeted intervention measures.13

Traditional interventions for sedentary behaviour have focused on reducing overall sedentary time and increasing the frequency of interruptions to sedentary behaviour.20 These interventions often involve advice from healthcare professionals and the use of educational materials for health education. However, these methods have limitations due to personal/internal barriers (such as mobility difficulties for older adults, lack of time and motivation) and external barriers (such as limited opportunities for older adults to interact with professionals, limited intervention coverage and high cost of monitoring and feedback to healthcare professionals).21 Currently, many face-to-face traditional interventions face implementation difficulties and cannot achieve positive changes in sedentary behaviour.22 23

WHO defines mobile health (mHealth) as providing healthcare and health information to healthcare professionals and the public through various mobile electronic devices.22 With the rapid development of information technology and the widespread use of smart devices, mHealth interventions based on behavioural principles have emerged and become prominent tools for rehabilitation and health behaviour promotion.20 Although mHealth interventions generally prioritise younger demographics and tend to underrepresent older adults, the increasing functionality of smartphones and user-friendliness has made smartphones increasingly popular among older adults. In developed countries, 61% of people aged 65 and over own a smartphone, and the number of users is still increasing.23

The integration of behaviour change techniques (BCTs) into mHealth interventions has emerged as a promising approach to reduce sedentary behaviour among older adults, leveraging technological accessibility and behavioural science principles to foster sustained health behaviour change. BCTs are a systematic set of 93 strategies based on health behaviour change theories that can be used to develop mHealth applications (MHAs) and implement various interventions to reduce sedentary behaviour.24 Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have highlighted the effectiveness of mHealth interventions integrating BCTs in promoting physical activity among older adults.232527 However, these reviews do not specifically focus on sedentary behaviour among older adults. These techniques are commonly used to enhance self-efficacy in older adults, helping them reduce sedentary behaviour and promote physical activity.28 Compared with traditional intervention methods, mHealth interventions combine MHAs, wearable devices, the internet and psychological factors of behaviour change (such as goal setting, planning, feedback, rewards, social support, social comparison), enabling continuous and real-time assessment and monitoring of changes in older adults’ sedentary behaviour, medication use, social interaction and physiological indicators throughout the day.2931

Although mHealth interventions have been increasingly studied in older populations, most evidence has focused on promoting physical activity rather than explicitly reducing sedentary behaviour. However, the existing evidence on the effectiveness of mHealth interventions in reducing sedentary behaviour among older adults remains insufficient and controversial, with inconsistent findings across studies.32 33 Furthermore, the underlying mechanisms through which these interventions exert their effects are poorly understood, as prior studies have rarely systematically explored the theoretical frameworks and BCTs applied.10 20 In addition, existing studies32 33 have distinguished between different types of sedentary behaviour or considered their role in moderating intervention effectiveness and have often failed to incorporate a broader range of secondary outcomes relevant to older adults’ health and well-being. Thus, this study primarily aimed to (1) Conduct a comprehensive systematic review to compare study characteristics, including country, participant demographics, disease types, sample sizes, intervention settings, duration and frequency, monitoring devices, tools for assessing sedentary behaviour, behaviour change theories and BCTs, (2) Explore the effects of mHealth interventions on reducing sedentary behaviour in this demographic through a meta-analysis of mHealth interventions and (3) To examine the influence of potential moderators on mHealth interventions’ effectiveness by conducting subgroup analysis according to participants’ characteristics and intervention specifics. Specifically, we will compare study characteristics (eg, country, participant demographics, disease types, sample sizes, intervention settings, duration and frequency, monitoring devices and tools for assessing sedentary behaviour), and synthesise their effects through quantitative analyses. Building on this, our review will place particular emphasis on systematically analysing the theoretical underpinnings and mechanisms of action by examining the integration and application of BCTs within mHealth interventions, classifying different types of sedentary behaviour and incorporating them into subgroup analyses alongside other moderators, and extending the scope of outcomes to include a broad range of secondary measures. This comprehensive approach is expected to generate novel insights into how mHealth interventions can be optimised and tailored to effectively reduce sedentary behaviour in older adults.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis will synthesise current evidence on the effectiveness of mHealth interventions in reducing sitting time among older adults aged 55 years and older. We recognise that the variability in intervention designs, delivery methods and outcome measures may pose challenges in synthesising data across studies. However, we aim to address these challenges by including a broad range of mHealth interventions and employing rigorous methodological standards. The review will adhere to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols guidelines34 (online supplemental file 1). This review is registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD42023443926.

Systematic review clinical question

Our systematic review aims to answer the following clinical question, organised by Population, Intervention/Exposure, Comparator, Outcome and Design.

Population, intervention/exposure, comparator and outcome

Our population of interest will include older adults (aged 55 years and above) who are sedentary or engage in prolonged sitting time, without specific restrictions on gender, race or comorbidities. Studies involving participants who have severe cognitive impairments will be excluded. The intervention of interest will be mHealth interventions, defined as the use of electronic devices such as smartphones, smartwatches, tablets and related digital technologies to promote health services. These interventions aim to reduce sitting time among older adults and may include apps, wearable devices and other digital tools that encourage physical activity, track sitting behaviours or promote active lifestyle habits. The comparator will be individuals not exposed to mHealth interventions or those receiving conventional care, with qualitative studies and pre-post designs being exempt from this criterion. Studies from all settings (eg, community, long-term care facilities, etc) will be considered.

Outcomes

We will evaluate the effectiveness of mHealth interventions on the primary outcomes: reduction in sitting time (measured by self-reported sitting time or device-based assessments, such as an accelerometer). Secondary outcomes will include behavioural outcomes (changes in physical activity levels, such as step counts and time spent in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity), physical health outcomes (physical function, including balance and mobility, and anthropometric and clinical indicators such as body mass index and blood pressure), psychological outcomes (eg, depression, anxiety, psychological distress) and overall quality of life.

Design

This systematic review will include randomised controlled trials (RCTs), pilot studies, pre-post studies (studies comparing outcomes before and after an intervention within the same group), and study protocols for qualitative synthesis; RCTs and pilot studies for quantitative synthesis.

Methods of searching, screening, extraction and analysis for systematic review search strategy

Two members with expertise in systematic reviews, with guidance from the principal investigators, developed the initial search strategy for this study. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and title/abstract keywords will be used to identify studies related to mHealth interventions, sedentary behaviour and older adults. Terms will be tailored to capture relevant studies from international databases. Language restrictions will not be applied in the search, and studies published globally will be considered, with a focus on high-quality evidence. An electronic search of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library and Web of Science will be performed to identify relevant studies. The databases will be searched from database inception to March 2025. We anticipate completing study selection and data extraction by December 2025, with data analysis finalised by April 2026. Reference lists of key review articles will also be screened for additional studies. The search methodology combined specific keywords and MeSH terms, such as ‘mHealth interventions’, ‘sedentary behaviour’ and ‘older adults’, to ensure a comprehensive retrieval of relevant studies. The detailed search strategy is provided in online supplemental file 2.

Study selection and data extraction

In the systematic review process, the initial phase will involve the lead researcher conducting a comprehensive search and screening titles and abstracts using Endnote X9 reference management software to manage references and eliminate duplicates. Screening will follow predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, with irrelevant articles being discarded. Two independent reviewers will conduct the screening of titles, abstracts and full-text articles in duplicate to ensure thoroughness and accuracy. Eligible studies will be selected according to the following criteria: (1) Population: older adults (age ≥55 years), based on previous studies,20 35 36 (2) Intervention: mHealth intervention, as defined as using any forms of electronic devices such as smartphones, smartwatches, iPads and related digital technology to promote health service, (3) Comparison: not exposed to any mHealth interventions or conventional care (qualitative studies and pre-post designs are exempt from this criterion), (4) Primary outcomes: total sitting time during waking hours, explicitly excluding sleep duration (such as the number of minutes spent sitting per day or week for sedentary behaviour) and (5) Study design: RCTs, pilot studies, pre-post studies (studies comparing outcomes before and after an intervention within the same group), and study protocols for qualitative synthesis; RCTs and pilot studies for quantitative synthesis. Studies will be excluded if they (1) Are not published in English, (2) Are duplicates of the same research and (3) Are incomplete studies (such as study protocols or ongoing studies or lack sufficient or necessary data for calculating an effect size for the meta-analysis procedure).

Data extraction

An Excel-based data extraction template will be developed to standardise the process of data collection. The lead author will review the full texts of the selected articles and extract key details, including: author, years, country, age (years), gender, disease, study type, sample size, intervention setting, intervention duration, intervention device, sedentary behaviour assessment tools, main assessment metrics, intervention outcomes, the theories and BCTs, sedentary behaviour types (eg, recreational screen time, total sedentary time), used in the study. To characterise intervention mechanisms, BCTs will be coded using the 93-item taxonomy,29 and their theoretical associations will be documented in a standardised table.

To ensure data accuracy and consistency, two authors will cross-check the extracted data. Any discrepancies will be resolved through collaborative discussion until consensus is reached. Should disagreements persist, a third reviewer (LY) will be involved to mediate and ensure an agreement is established. Additionally, the methodological quality of the included studies will be evaluated, and any conflicts or variances during the screening and extraction process will be addressed collaboratively.

Risk of bias assessment

Two researchers used the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool to assess the risk of bias in RCTs, following the guidelines provided in the Cochrane Handbook.37 The evaluation was based on five domains: randomisation process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome and selection of the reported result. Each study was rated as low, high or some concerns for each domain. A study was considered to have a high risk of bias if any domain was rated as high risk, and rated as ‘some concerns’ if predominantly unclear ratings were given without any high-risk domains. A study was rated as low risk if all domains were rated as low risk. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or third-party consultation. For non-randomised intervention studies, the Risk of bias in non-randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool will be applied.38 For observational studies (eg, cohort or cross-sectional studies), the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tools will be used.39

Data synthesis

The data in this systematic review and meta-analysis will be synthesised in two stages: qualitative and quantitative synthesis. In the qualitative synthesis, studies will be grouped based on intervention type, outcomes and study design. Descriptive statistics will summarise key characteristics such as sample size, age group and outcomes. In the quantitative synthesis, data will be pooled to estimate the overall effect of mHealth interventions on reducing sitting time. Results will be presented in narrative form, tables and forest plots.

Statistical analysis

The review sourced its statistical data from various outcomes related to sedentary behaviour, such as the number of minutes spent sitting per day or week for sedentary behaviour. The inverse variance method was employed using both random-effects and fixed-effects models based on the level of heterogeneity among studies.40 When outcome measures across studies varied in units or methods, Standardised Mean Differences (SMD) with 95% CIs will be calculated to allow for standardised comparisons, using mean values and standard deviations (SD) for sedentary behaviour and physical activity. Conversely, when studies report outcomes using consistent measurement units and methods, Mean Differences (MD) will be used to directly compare intervention effects in terms of size. If mean values and SD are not provided, calculations will first be made using relevant formulas from the Cochrane Handbook37 or by requesting data directly from the original authors. Additionally, based on a comprehensive review of existing literature,10 we were interested in the following subgroup variables: subgroup analyses were conducted for nine moderating factors: mean age, intervention site, intervention duration, intervention equipment, theoretical basis, BCT categories, sedentary behaviour types, total sedentary time, monitoring tools and intervention indicators. Meta-analyses will be performed using Review Manager v.5.4,37 with the I² statistic assessing heterogeneity across studies. A fixed-effect model was employed when heterogeneity was not significant (I²≤50%), while a random-effects model was used in the presence of significant heterogeneity (I²>50%), accompanied by subgroup analyses to explore sources of heterogeneity. Effect sizes will be expressed as weighted mean differences in minutes per day, with 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) calculated.

Limitation

First, this study includes studies with participants aged 55 years and above, which is a common threshold for defining ‘older adults’ in the literature. This age range may potentially include some middle-aged adults, which could affect the generalisability of the findings specifically to older adults. Second, methodologies for measuring sedentary behaviour and intervention outcomes may vary across studies, but these differences will be addressed through rigorous analysis, ensuring that the findings are as accurate and representative as possible for older adult populations.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics approval is not required for this study as it involves only secondary, published data with no attempt to identify individuals. The results will be presented at international conferences and submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal.
