Tuning oxidative stability and digestibility of frozen myofibrillar protein: A combined magnetic field and pH-shifting approach
Authors: Bo Chen, Shanshan Huangfu, Jiayi Cai, Wen Nie, Ke Li, Yu Wang, Yanhong Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the synergistic effects of magnetic field (MF) and pH-shifting (pH 9–11) on the oxidative stability and in vitro digestibility of frozen denatured myofibrillar protein (MP). Results revealed that MF-assisted pH-shifting at pH 9 (MP-9 + MF) exhibited the better synergistic effect, significantly enhancing oxidative stability by promoting a compact, ordered protein conformation that minimized carbonyl formation and preserved sulfhydryl content. However, pH-shifting at pH 10 without MF achieved the highest in vitro digestibility, but this benefit came at the cost of reduced oxidative stability, as evidenced by the significant increase in carbonyl content. These findings demonstrate that this combined approach presents a clear trade-off, allowing for the targeted modulation of MP properties to prioritize either oxidative stability or digestibility, providing a novel strategy to enhance the quality of meat products.Keywords: Myofibrillar protein, Magnetic field, pH-shifting, Oxidative stability, Digestibility

Introduction

Freezing-induced denaturation of muscle proteins is a major factor that impairs the overall quality of meat products. This quality deterioration is primarily attributed to the structural damage of myofibrillar protein (MP), given that it constitutes approximately 55 % of total muscle protein and plays a crucial role in determining key attributes such as tenderness and water-holding capacity. The mechanisms underlying the denaturation by freezing include mechanical stress from ice crystal formation, dehydration, and freeze concentration effects (Chen et al., 2025). Although advanced technologies like ultrasonic-assisted freezing, which aim to mitigate initial damage by promoting the formation of small, uniform ice crystals, can alleviate these issues, prolonged storage still leads to ice recrystallization via mechanisms such as Ostwald ripening, as evidenced by a 42 % increase in the average ice crystal diameter in pork stored at −18 °C for 90 days even with this technique (Zhang et al., 2022).

This process of ice recrystallization not only intensifies freeze concentration and decreases pH but also promotes gradual and irreversible MP denaturation. Such structural destabilization directly impairs processing characteristics and, more critically, increases the susceptibility of protein chains to oxidative modification. This heightened susceptibility arises from two concurrent phenomena: protein chains unfold to expose previously shielded amino acid residues, and cellular compartments are disrupted by ice, releasing pro-oxidants like catalytic iron (Soladoye et al., 2015). When the meat is subsequently thawed and subjected to processing steps such as mincing, salting, or heating, these exposed residues are readily oxidized, resulting in the formation of carbonyls and disulfide-mediated cross-links, which severely impair techno-functional properties like gelation and emulsification.

Severe protein oxidation also markedly impairs digestibility. The primary mechanism involves the oxidative modification of amino acid residues that serve as cleavage sites for digestive enzymes. For example, the activity of trypsin, which hydrolyzes proteins at the carboxyl side of arginine and lysine, is diminished if these residues are carbonylated (Liu et al., 2023). Additionally, oxidation-induced protein aggregation also sterically hinders enzyme-substrate interactions, further reducing digestibility (Wang, Chen, et al., 2024). Such reduced digestibility results in the loss of valuable dietary protein while also posing potential health risks, as undigested proteins fermented by gut microbiota can generate toxic metabolites (e.g., hydrogen sulfide, ammonia, and phenol). Therefore, enhancing the antioxidant capacity of MP in frozen meat is crucial for preserving its techno-functional properties as well as ensuring its nutritional value and digestibility.

In our previous work, a method integrating a magnetic field (MF) was established with pH-shifting to treat freeze-denatured MP (Chen et al., 2024). This approach is applied post-thawing, where the MF is hypothesized to potentiate the structural modification effects of the pH-shifting process, thereby mitigating the decline in processing quality associated with prolonged frozen storage. The findings indicated that as MP unfolds under alkaline conditions, the MF can exert an orienting effect on charged amino acid residues and polar groups along the polypeptide chains. This directional influence could promote a more effective and controlled refolding process of MP upon pH neutralization, leading to significant improvements in key physicochemical properties, including protein solubility, total sulfhydryl content, and structural integrity. While this preliminary work focused on enhancing processing characteristics by reversing protein denaturation, the broader potential of pH-shifting to improve nutritional and health-related attributes is well-documented. For instance, pH-shifting has been shown to effectively enhance the antioxidant activity and digestibility of various proteins. Yildiz (2025) reported a 27 % increase in the antioxidant activity of pumpkin seed protein isolates following pH-shifting treatment. Similarly, Tian et al. (2017) observed that the digestibility of carp protein was significantly enhanced by pH-shifting, reaching levels 7–8 times higher than that of fresh muscle. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that pH-shifting can generate protein hydrolysates with notable bioactivity. Theodore et al. (2008) found that the bioactivity of recovered hydrolysates from channel catfish was positively correlated with the degree of hydrolysis, with higher molecular weight fractions displaying superior ferric reducing power and DPPH radical scavenging capacity. These collective findings prompted the hypothesis that integrating an MF with the pH-shifting process could offer synergistic benefits.

In summary, this study aimed to investigate whether combining a magnetic field with the pH-shifting process could enhance the oxidative stability and subsequent in vitro digestibility of MP from frozen pork. Furthermore, the underlying mechanisms of this combined treatment, particularly the potential synergistic interactions, were systematically explored. Such an outcome would represent a novel strategy for comprehensively improving the quality of meat products.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Fresh porcine longissimus dorsi muscle (six-month-old, male, Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire pig) was purchased from the Da-zhang supermarket (Zhengzhou, Henan, China) within 24 h of slaughter, and transported to the laboratory on ice. After the visible connective tissues and fat were removed, the muscles were wrapped in vacuum bags, and stored at −20 ± 1 °C for up to three months prior to experimental analysis. NaCl, NaOH, HCl, H2O2, FeCl3, ascorbic acid, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), piperazine-1,4-bis (2-ethanesulfonic acid) (PIPES) and sodium (Na₂HPO₄/NaH₂PO₄) phosphates were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co, Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Pepsin and pancreatin were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co, Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All of the chemicals and reagents in the experiments were of analytical grade.

Preparation of samples

The treatments were applied to a minced pork slurry based on our previous study (Chen et al., 2024). Briefly, frozen meat was thawed at 4 °C for 6 h, then churned using a meat grinder to form a mince. This mince was then homogenized with four volumes of buffer (1:4, w/v) (10 mmol/L Na₂HPO₄/NaH₂PO₄, 0.1 mol/L NaCl, pH 6.5) to create the treatment slurries. The slurries were then divided into control, pH-shifting-treated and magnetic field-assisted pH-shifting-treated groups. In the treatment groups, samples were adjusted to pH 9, 10, or 11 using 2 mol/L NaOH with continuous stirring, then held at 4 °C for 20 min. The samples that were exposed to a 3 mT static magnetic field in a magnetic field test chamber (MIF-FI, Indust, Wuxi, China) for 20 min were in the magnetic-field-assisted pH-shifting treatment group, and the rest were in the pH-shifting treatment group. Subsequently, all samples in the treatment groups were neutralized to pH 6.5 using 0.5 mol/L HCl and maintained at 4 °C for 10 min. For the untreated control group, an equivalent volume of buffer was added, and the samples were held under the same conditions (stirring at 4 °C for 20 min, followed by resting for 10 min) as the treatment groups without any pH adjustment. Finally, all samples were subjected to centrifugation at 600 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain the minced meat precipitate.

Extraction of MP

MP was extracted from the above prepared minced meat precipitate utilizing the method described by Yu, Shi, et al. (2023). All extraction procedures were conducted at 4 °C. The minced meat precipitate was resuspended in four volumes of protein extraction solution (1:4, w/v) (0.1 mol/L NaCl, 10 mmol/L Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4, 2 mmol/L MgCl2, 1 mmol/L EGTA, pH 7.0), and homogenized by a homogenizer (UltraTurrax T25, IKA, Staufen, Germany) for 60 s (10,000 r/min). Following this, centrifugation was performed at 2000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was subsequently removed, and the sediment fraction, designated as the MP precipitate, was collected. The above operation was repeated twice. Next, the precipitate was resuspended in protein wash solution (0.1 mol/L NaCl) at a 1:4 (w/v) ratio, and the homogenization and centrifugation processes were repeated three times under the same conditions, and the precipitate was collected. Subsequently, the final MP precipitate was resuspended in buffer solution (0.6 mol/L NaCl, 25 mmol/L PIPES, pH 6.5), and the concentration of MP was assayed using the Biuret method. Finally, the extracted MP was stored at 4 °C and used within 72 h. MP-9, MP-10, and MP-11 represented the pH-shifting-treated samples and MP-9 + MF, MP-10 + MF, and MP-11 + MF represented the magnetic field-assisted pH-shifting-treated samples.

MP oxidation

The treated MP was adjusted to 25 mg/mL protein suspension utilizing buffer solution (25 mmol/L PIPES, 0.6 mol/L NaCl, pH 6.5). The oxidation of MP was simulated based on the method of Xia et al. (2022), with some modifications. Briefly, oxidation of the samples was carried out by adding 0.01 mmol/L FeCl3, 0.1 mmol/L ascorbic acid, and 1 mmol/L H2O2 to the suspension. After 36 h of oxidation at 4 °C in the dark, the reaction was terminated by adding 2 mmol/L EDTA. The resulting oxidized MP samples were used for subsequent analyses.

Determination of carbonyl content

The carbonyl content of oxidized MP was assessed following the method reported by Liu et al. (2024) with slight modifications. One milliliter of oxidized MP suspension (5 mg/mL) was combined with 1 mL of 10 mmol/L DNPH and allowed to react in a dark chamber for 1 h at room temperature. The reaction was stopped by adding 0.5 mL of 20 % (w/v) TCA, followed by centrifugation of the samples at 12,000 ×g for 5 min to remove unreacted reagents and collect protein precipitate. The precipitate was subsequently washed three times with ethanol/ethyl acetate (1:1, v/v). After being resuspended in 6 mol/L guanidine (dissolved in 2 mol/L HCl), the final pellet was incubated at a temperature of 37 °C for 15 min. Utilizing an ultraviolet spectrophotometer (TU-1810, Purification General Instrument Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) with an absorption coefficient of 22,000 M−1 cm−1, the absorbance at 370 nm was measured to estimate the carbonyl value, which was then expressed as nmol/mg protein.

Determination of total sulfhydryl content

The method for determining the total sulfhydryl content of oxidized MP was slightly modified based on the description provided by Wu et al. (2024). Generally, 4.5 mL of buffer (0.2 mol/L Tris-HCl, 8 mol/L urea, 1 % SDS and 3 mmol/L EDTA, pH 8.0) was added to 0.5 mL of the oxidized MP suspension (1.0 mg/mL), mixed thoroughly and centrifuged at 8000 ×g for a duration of 15 min at 4 °C. Then, a volume of 4 mL supernatant was added to Ellman's reagent (0.5 mL, 2 mmol/L DTNB, 0.2 mol/L Tris-HCl, pH 8.0) and the mixture was allowed to react in the dark at a temperature of 40 °C for 25 min. The absorbance at 412 nm was recorded utilizing an ultraviolet spectrophotometer (TU-1810, Purification General Instrument Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Total sulfhydryl content was determined utilizing an extinction coefficient of 13,600 M−1 cm−1 and expressed in nmol/mg protein.

Determination of solubility

The solubility of oxidized MP was examined according to the method described by Zhao, Hei, et al. (2024). An aliquot of 10 mL of oxidized MP suspension (5 mg/mL) was centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for a duration of 20 min, and the supernatant was collected. The Biuret method was utilized to measure protein concentration, with the percentage of MP concentration before and after centrifugation representing the solubility of the protein.

Determination of surface hydrophobicity

The surface hydrophobicity of oxidized MP was determined with reference to the method described by Gu et al. (2022), using bromophenol blue (BPB) as a probe. First, 4 mL of oxidized MP suspension (5 mg/mL) was combined with 80 μL of 1 mg/mL BPB solution with the buffer serving as the blank group. After shaking evenly, the mixture was stored at 20 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was obtained through centrifugation (2000 ×g, 15 min), and the absorbance at 595 nm utilizing an ultraviolet spectrophotometer (TU-1810, Purification General Instrument Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was determined. SDS-PAGE

The oxidized MP was separated by SDS-PAGE with a 4 % stacking gel and a 10 % separating gel (P0468S, Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) following the method of Liu et al. (2024). The Bio-Rad Mini-PROTEAN II System Cell apparatus (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Hercules, America) was employed to conduct SDS-PAGE of protein composition under both non-reducing and reducing conditions. Oxidized MP suspension (1 mg/mL) was mixed at a ratio of 4:1 (v/v) with a 5× loading buffer. The non-reducing loading buffer (5×) contained 0.25 M Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 10 % (w/v) SDS, 50 % (v/v) glycerol, and 0.1 % (w/v) bromophenol blue. For reducing conditions, DTT (to a final concentration of 50 mM) was added to the sample mixture with loading buffer. The mixtures were then boiled at a temperature of 100 °C for 5 min. The protein samples (10 μg) were loaded onto the sample well and electrophoresis was conducted at 80 V to allow proteins through the stacking gel, and then the voltage was raised to 120 V until the sample bands reached 0.5 to 1.0 cm from the bottom of the separating gel. After separation, the gels were stained with 0.1 % (w/v) Coomassie brilliant blue R-250 (BioRad, USA) for 30 min and decolorized using a decolorizing solution (10 % acetic acid, 40 % methanol in water) until the background of the gels was nearly colorless.

Determination of secondary structure

Structural alterations in oxidized MP were analyzed using circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy (Zhao et al., 2021). The oxidized MP suspension (40 mg/mL) were diluted to a protein concentration of 0.2 mg/mL utilizing buffer solution (25 mmol/L PIPES, 0.6 mol/L NaCl, pH 6.5). CD spectra were obtained using a CD spectropolarimeter (Chirascan q100, Applied Photophysics Ltd., London, England). MP samples were loaded into a 1 mm quartz cell. The molecular ellipticity of the samples was assessed from 200 to 260 nm at 60 nm/min scan rate and 1.0 nm bandwidth, with the spectra being the average of three scans.

Amino acid composition analysis

Analysis of amino acid content and composition in the oxidized MP samples was performed using an L-8900 automatic amino acid analyzer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan), following a previous method (Liu et al., 2023). Briefly, oxidized MP samples (10 mL) were hydrolyzed with 6.0 mol/L HCl at 110 °C for 24 h. The hydrolysates were then dried overnight at 42 °C and redissolved in 0.02 mol/L HCl. After centrifugation at 10,000 ×g for 30 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. An aliquot of the prepared sample was then injected into the amino acid analyzer for analysis.

In vitro digestion of MP

The in vitro gastrointestinal digestion of oxidized MP was performed based on the description by Zhang et al. (2024) with some modifications. Oxidized MP samples were dispersed in five volumes (1:5, w/v) of pre-warmed (37 °C) distilled water. The pH of the suspension was adjusted to 2.0 with 6 mol/L HCl. Gastric digestion was initiated by adding pepsin to achieve a final enzyme-to-substrate ratio (E/S) of 2000 U/g protein. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 2 h with continuous shaking (120 r/min). Subsequently, the gastric digestion was terminated by adjusting the pH to 7.0 with 2 mol/L NaOH. Intestinal digestion was then initiated by adding pancreatin (E/S ratio of 100 U/g protein), and the mixture was incubated under the same conditions for another 2 h. Finally, the reaction was terminated by heating the mixture at 95 °C for 15 min. The digests were centrifuged (12,000 ×g, 20 min, 4 °C), and the resulting supernatant was collected and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis.

In accordance with the method described by Zou et al. (2023), the digested samples were mixed with three volumes of ethanol (1:3, v/v), stored at 4 °C for 12 h, and then centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, both the undigested precipitate and the supernatant were collected separately. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Determination of peptide content

The peptide content was determined according to the previous method (Nwachukwu & Aluko, 2019) with some modifications. 5 mL of the digestion product solution was added with 5 mL of 10 % (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA) aqueous solution. The mixture was then mixed evenly on a vortex mixer, and kept at 4 °C for 20 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 ×g at 4 °C for 15 min, the supernatant was collected. A portion of the supernatant was taken, and mixed with copper sulfate reagent at a 3:1 ratio (v/v). The mixture was mixed evenly and reacted in the dark for 10 min. Then, the mixture was centrifuged at 2000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 540 nm, and the result was substituted into the standard curve formula to obtain the peptide content of the enzymatic digestion solution.

Determination of peptide sequences in digestion product

The peptides in the digestion product were analyzed following a previously published method (Huang et al., 2022). The lyophilized digestion product (5 mg) was redissolved in 1 mL of ultrapure water. Equal volumes of this solution and 0.2 % trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) (v/v) were mixed and loaded into HC-C18 solid-phase extraction (SPE) cartridges (ANPEL Laboratory Technologies Inc., Shanghai, China) and pretreated with 5 mL of 0.1 % TFA and acetonitrile (v/v), and subsequently eluted with 5 mL of a solution containing 80 % acetonitrile and 0.1 % TFA (v/v). After drying the peptide-containing eluate in a centrifugal evaporator at 30 °C, it was redissolved and adjusted to the appropriate concentration utilizing 2 % acetonitrile. Subsequent sample analysis was performed utilizing a QExactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA), with MS scans acquired in the range of 350 to 2000 m/z, and MS/MS scans obtained in the range of 200 to 2000 m/z. Data analysis was carried out utilizing MaxQuant software (version 1.5.5.1) with a permissible error rate of 1 %. The primary ion mass tolerance was configured to 20 ppm, and the secondary ion mass tolerance was configured to 0.1 Da. Peptides were identified through searching the Uniprot sus database (https://www.uniprot.org/, accessed 12/06/2025) for Sus scrofa proteins. Proteins with credibility were selected based on a criterion of ≤0.01, with three samples measured per group, and peptides were only included in the results if they were consistently identified in all three replicates.

Statistical analysis

All data represent the means of three independent replicates. The results are expressed as the means ± standard deviation. Prior to performing one-way ANOVA, normality was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test and homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene's test. Data were subjected to a one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's test using SPSS 21.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The significance level was set at p < 0.05. All graphs were generated using Origin 2021 (Origin Lab Inc., Northampton, USA).

Results and discussion

Carbonyl content

The formation of carbonyl groups is a hallmark of protein oxidation, resulting from the attack of reactive oxygen species on the side chains of susceptible amino acids such as arginine, lysine, proline, and threonine (Kehm et al., 2021). Higher carbonyl content generally indicates a greater degree of protein oxidation. As shown in Fig. 1, the different treatments significantly affected the oxidative stability of MP compared to the control group, which had a carbonyl content of approximately 0.71 nmol/mg. At pH 9, the treatments exhibited a protective effect. Specifically, pH-shifting alone (MP-9) reduced the carbonyl content to ∼0.61 nmol/mg, although this difference was not significant (p > 0.05). The combination of a magnetic field with the pH 9 treatment (MP-9 + MF) yielded a significant synergistic effect and lowered the carbonyl content to a minimum of ∼0.50 nmol/mg (p < 0.05). This value represents a reduction of about 29.5 % compared to the control. This result indicates that under mild alkaline conditions (pH 9), the synergistic action of pH-shifting and MF induced favorable structural refolding of the protein into a more compact and ordered conformation. This optimized structure effectively buried oxidizable amino acid residues within the molecular interior, thereby enhancing the protein's antioxidant capacity (Qiu et al., 2022). However, as the pH increased to 10 and 11, the treatment effect shifted from protective to pro-oxidative. Compared to the control, the carbonyl contents of the MP-10, MP-11, MP-10 + MF, and MP-11 + MF groups were all significantly elevated (p < 0.05). This suggests that the higher alkaline environments caused excessive protein unfolding, which could not be fully reversed upon neutralization, leading to the prolonged exposure of pro-oxidative residues and subsequent carbonylation (Qiu et al., 2022). The application of MF at higher pH levels exacerbated this effect, particularly in the MP-11 + MF group, which displayed the highest carbonyl content. This aligns with our previous findings that a magnetic field can potentiate the unfolding effect of alkaline conditions on proteins (Chen et al., 2024). In contrast to the protective effect observed at pH 9, these results collectively demonstrate that the synergistic interaction between MF and pH-shifting is highly pH-dependent.

Total sulfhydryl content

Total sulfhydryl (SH) content is a key indicator for assessing protein oxidation, as its decrease typically signifies the oxidation of cysteine residues to form disulfide bonds, leading to protein cross-linking and aggregation. As shown in Fig. 2, all treated groups exhibited higher total SH content than the control group, indicating that both pH-shifting and MF-assisted treatments effectively protected MP from oxidation. In the pH-shifting groups, the SH content of the MP-9 and MP-11 groups was significantly higher than that of the control (p < 0.05), whereas the increase in the MP-10 group was not significant. This suggests that pH-shifting induced protein refolding, which re-buried the SH groups exposed by freeze-denaturation, thereby reducing their susceptibility to oxidation (Qiu et al., 2022). The non-linear trend in SH content from pH 9 to 11 indicates that the refolded conformations varied subtly under different pH conditions. The introduction of a magnetic field further enhanced the protection of SH groups in a pH-dependent manner. A key finding was the pronounced synergistic effect at pH 9; the MP-9 + MF treatment substantially increased the total SH content from ∼25 nmol/mg (MP-9) to ∼34 nmol/mg, an increase of approximately 36 %. This value was significantly higher than that of all other groups (p < 0.05). This may be because the magnetic field not only promoted a more ordered protein refolding but might have also induced the cleavage of some pre-existing disulfide bonds, thus increasing the detectable SH content (Yang et al., 2021).

Changes in total sulfhydryl content of frozen MP under different pH-shifting and MF-assisted pH-shifting treatments.

However, this synergistic effect diminished at higher pH values. The SH content in the MF-assisted groups was lowest at pH 10 (MP-10 + MF) before recovering slightly at pH 11 (MP-11 + MF), but both remained significantly lower than the level in the MP-9 + MF group. This suggests that at pH 10 and 11, the high alkaline environment may have caused excessive protein unfolding or irreversible chemical modifications (e.g., conversion of SH groups to sulfonic acid), which likely counteracted the positive effects of the magnetic field. Thus, our results confirm that the efficacy of the synergy between MF and pH-shifting is context-specific, with pH 9 providing the optimal condition for maximal protection.

Surface hydrophobicity

Surface hydrophobicity (H₀) is a crucial indicator of changes in protein tertiary structure and conformational stability. Protein oxidation typically leads to structural unfolding, which exposes internal hydrophobic amino acid residues and thus increases H₀ (Bezerra et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 3, the control group exhibited the highest H₀ (∼132 μg), indicating significant structural damage due to oxidation. All pH-shifting treatments effectively reduced the H₀ of oxidized MP, with the effect becoming more pronounced as the pH increased. The H₀ of the MP-9, MP-10, and MP-11 groups were all significantly lower than that of the control (p < 0.05). Specifically, the H₀ of the MP-10 and MP-11 groups was significantly lower than that of the MP-9 group, suggesting that higher alkaline conditions (pH 10 and 11) more effectively promoted refolding into a conformation where the hydrophobic core was efficiently buried. This stronger refolding drive may stem from a greater degree of initial unfolding at higher pH, providing more favorable kinetic conditions for subsequent reconstruction (Chen et al., 2024). However, this trend did not align perfectly with the changes in carbonyl content. This discrepancy reveals that pH-shifting-induced refolding has complex effects on different physicochemical properties. At pH 10 and 11, while the final conformation effectively buried the hydrophobic core (lowering H₀), some easily oxidizable hydrophilic or amphiphilic residues (e.g., lysine, arginine) may have remained exposed, leading to increased carbonyl formation.

Changes in surface hydrophobicity of frozen MP under different pH-shifting and MF-assisted pH-shifting treatments.

The introduction of a magnetic field produced a significant synergistic effect, further reducing H₀. Under all pH conditions, the H₀ of MF-assisted groups was significantly lower than that of their non-MF counterparts (p < 0.05). This synergy was optimal at pH 10, where the MP-10 + MF group exhibited the lowest H₀ value. This suggests that at pH 10, the MF maximally promoted the formation of a structurally optimized conformation, achieving the most effective burial of hydrophobic groups (Zhao, Hei, et al., 2024). As the pH rose from 10 to 11, the H₀ of the MF-assisted group increased (MP-11 + MF > MP-10 + MF) but remained significantly lower than that of non-MF group. This may indicate that under the more intense conditions of pH 11, MF-induced refolding was accompanied by the formation of non-dissociable micro-aggregates, preventing H₀ from reaching the minimum level seen at pH 10. Overall, pH 10 was the better condition for the synergistic action of MF and pH-shifting to maximally reduce surface hydrophobicity.

Solubility

Protein solubility is a critical indicator of denaturation, aggregation, and functional properties, and it is closely related to surface charge and hydrophobicity (Dong et al., 2020). As shown in Fig. 4, the oxidized control group exhibited the lowest solubility, indicating severe oxidation-induced protein aggregation. Compared to the control, all pH-shifting and MF-assisted treatments significantly improved the solubility of oxidized MP (p < 0.05). This is consistent with the general trend of reduced surface hydrophobicity, suggesting that a more ordered, refolded structure improved protein-water interactions. Among the pH-shifting groups without MF, solubility first increased significantly from pH 9 to pH 10 and then plateaued, with no significant difference between the MP-10 and MP-11 groups. This suggests that a higher alkaline environment (pH 10/11) promoted protein unfolding and subsequent refolding into a more hydrophilic conformation, likely due to enhanced electrostatic repulsion (Wang, Zhou, et al., 2024). However, this improvement in solubility did not correlate with enhanced antioxidant capacity, as these conditions were shown to increase protein carbonylation.

Changes in solubility of frozen MP under different pH-shifting and MF-assisted pH-shifting treatments.

The introduction of a magnetic field exhibited a significant synergistic effect on the enhancement of solubility, although this effect was pH-dependent. At all pH levels, the solubility of the MF-assisted groups was significantly higher than that of their corresponding non-MF counterparts (p < 0.05). Notably, the MP-9 + MF treatment group achieved the highest solubility among all groups, showing increases of 91.1 % and 39.7 % compared to the control and MP-9 groups, respectively. However, as the pH rose from 9 to 10 and 11, the solubility in the MF-assisted groups conversely showed a significant decline. This phenomenon reveals the detrimental impact of an excessively alkaline environment. Although the combination of high pH and a magnetic field could effectively reduce surface hydrophobicity, it might also have accelerated irreversible covalent modifications. Following treatment in a harsh alkaline environment, exposed sulfhydryl groups became more reactive, and the carbonylation of amino acid side chains was more severe. These two factors collectively contributed to the formation of insoluble aggregates, which in turn reduced protein solubility. Therefore, the better solubility of the MP-9 + MF group can be attributed to its achievement of a balance between promoting effective protein refolding and avoiding excessive oxidative and cross-linking damage.

SDS-page

SDS-PAGE was performed to visualize the aggregation and degradation of MP, which are hallmarks of protein oxidation. Oxidative stress is known to induce the formation of high-molecular-weight (HMW) protein polymers, which appear as new or intensified bands in the stacking gel and correspond to a decrease in the intensity of specific protein bands like the myosin heavy chain (MHC) (Wu et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 5, the main bands identified were MHC (∼245 kDa) and actin (∼48 kDa).

Effect of different pH-shifting and MF-assisted pH-shifting treatments on the SDS-PAGE patterns of frozen MP.

Under non-reducing conditions (Fig. 5A), HMW aggregates were present at the top of the stacking gel in all samples. These polymers are typically formed through a combination of disulfide and non-disulfide covalent bonds that are stable in the presence of SDS (Chen et al., 2024). Notably, the aggregate bands in the MP-9, MP-10, and MP-9 + MF lanes were fainter compared to the control, indicating that these moderate treatments effectively mitigated oxidation-induced aggregation. In contrast, the more intense aggregation bands in the MP-11, MP-10 + MF, and MP-11 + MF samples suggest that more extreme processing conditions (e.g., high pH or combined treatments) promoted the formation of extensive cross-links.

The distinction between cross-link types becomes evident under reducing conditions (Fig. 5B), where the addition of a reducing agent cleaves disulfide bonds. As expected, the disappearance of the HMW bands and the concurrent intensification of MHC bands in most samples confirmed that disulfide cross-linking was a primary mechanism of aggregation (Gu et al., 2022). However, the MHC bands in the MP-11, MP-10 + MF, and MP-11 + MF samples failed to fully recover their intensity. This crucial observation suggests the presence of stable, non-disulfide covalent bonds that are resistant to reduction. While oxidative reactions can contribute to such cross-linking, the intense alkaline treatment (e.g., in the MP-11 group) may have also promoted the formation of non-reducible covalent bonds through mechanisms independent of oxidation (Yu, Zhang, et al., 2023).

Secondary structure

The Far-UV CD spectroscopy was used to investigate the effects of different treatments on the secondary structure of MP (Fig. 6). All samples displayed characteristic negative peaks at 208 nm and 222 nm, which are indicative of an α-helical structure. The absolute value of the mean residue ellipticity at these wavelengths is proportional to the α-helix content (Wang et al., 2025). Compared to the oxidized control, all pH-shifting and MF-assisted treatments resulted in a significant increase in α-helix content (p < 0.05). This demonstrates that these treatments effectively promoted the refolding of partially denatured peptide chains into more ordered secondary structures, which aligns with the observed lower carbonyl content and higher solubility. Among the pH-shifting treatment groups, the α-helix content exhibited significant pH-dependency, with the recovery effect following the order: MP-10 > MP-11 > MP-9. This result indicates that pH 10 was the optimal condition for inducing the recovery of protein secondary structure in this study. This can be attributed to the fact that at pH 10, the intensity of intermolecular electrostatic repulsion was sufficient to effectively promote the dissociation of protein aggregates and the unfolding of peptide chains, while not reaching a level that would induce significant irreversible chemical modifications, thereby providing the most favorable conformational foundation for ordered refolding during the subsequent neutralization process (Kristinsson & Hultin, 2003). In contrast, the effect of the pH 9 treatment was the least pronounced among the three groups, presumably because the electrostatic repulsion generated at this pH was insufficient to facilitate the complete dissociation and unfolding of the highly aggregated protein molecules, thereby limiting the efficiency of their refolding. The effect of the pH 11 treatment was superior to that of pH 9 but inferior to that of pH 10. This is likely because while the stronger alkaline environment could induce a more thorough protein unfolding, it simultaneously accelerated the occurrence of side reactions, such as the non-disulfide covalent cross-linking indicated by the SDS-PAGE results. These newly formed covalent bonds restricted the conformational flexibility of the peptide chains, thereby impeding them from achieving the level of structural recovery attainable with the pH 10 treatment.

Effect of different pH-shifting and MF-assisted pH-shifting treatments on the secondary structure of frozen MP.

The introduction of a magnetic field exhibited a synergistic enhancing effect at all pH levels, as the α-helix content in the MF-assisted groups was significantly higher than that in their corresponding non-MF counterparts. This indicates that the magnetic field may further promote the ordered refolding of peptide chains by modulating intermolecular interactions. Nevertheless, the trend of α-helix content among the MF-assisted groups followed the order of MP-9 + MF > MP-10 + MF > MP-11 + MF. This reveals that pH remains the key factor governing the final protein conformation, and that the adverse reactions induced by an excessively high pH (pH 11) were the primary reason limiting structural optimization, an effect that could not be entirely eliminated even with magnetic field assistance.

Amino acid composition

The effects of the treatments on the amino acid composition of oxidized MP are shown in Table 1. While no significant differences were observed for most amino acids across the groups (p > 0.05), notable changes were observed in certain oxidation-susceptible amino acids (e.g., threonine, phenylalanine, histidine, and arginine), indicating a protective effect of the pH-shifting treatments. Specifically, the threonine content in the pH-shifting groups was higher than that in the control group, indicating that this treatment likely inhibited the oxidative conversion of threonine into carbonyl-containing compounds (Dominguez et al., 2022). The MP-9 + MF and MP-10 + MF groups exhibited a similar protective effect, but this effect was diminished in the MP-11 + MF group. This could be attributed to the synergistic interaction between the excessively high pH and the magnetic field, which may have intensified the adverse impact of the alkaline environment on amino acid residues, making them more susceptible to oxidative damage. The phenylalanine content in all treatment groups was higher than that in the control group. This phenomenon suggests that the pH-shifting and MF-assisted treatments induced protein refolding, which reduced the exposure of the aromatic ring of phenylalanine side chains, thereby inhibiting their conversion into tyrosine and other hydroxylated derivatives. Similarly, the histidine content was higher in the MP-9 + MF and MP-10 + MF groups than in the control group, whereas it was significantly lower in the MP-11 + MF group. This implies that moderate MF-assisted pH-shifting (pH 9–10) can effectively protect the imidazole ring of histidine, inhibiting its conversion to aspartic acid and asparagine (Liu et al., 2022); however, at an excessively high pH (pH 11), the treatment led to excessive protein unfolding, which increased the exposure of histidine residues and exacerbated their oxidation. Furthermore, the arginine content in all treatment groups was higher than that of the control group. This may be attributed to the treatments improving the protein structure, which diminished the chelating ability of arginine residues with metal ions such as Fe2+, thereby inhibiting their oxidative damage from free radical attacks.

In summary, although the overall amino acid profiles of the various groups did not change significantly following oxidation, the pH-shifting and MF-assisted treatments demonstrated a protective effect on specific, readily oxidizable amino acids such as phenylalanine, histidine, and arginine. Notably, the MF-assisted treatment at pH 10 (MP-10 + MF) was the most effective in maintaining the stability of multiple amino acids. The changing trend of lysine further corroborates this difference: its ε-amino group is highly sensitive to oxidation. The lysine content in the MP-10 + MF group (11.25 %) was significantly higher than that in the oxidized control (11.09 %) and other treatment groups (p < 0.05), while the MP-9 + MF group showed the poorest retention (10.92 %). This suggests that at pH 10, the combination of moderate protein unfolding and magnetic field assistance is more favorable for the burial or reduced reactivity of key residues, thereby minimizing the oxidative loss of amino acids like lysine.

In vitro digestibility

The digestibility of MP is closely associated with their conformational state, which is sensitive to both oxidative damage and processing interventions. It is well-established that protein oxidation impairs digestibility primarily by forming extensive cross-links and aggregates that obstruct or alter the cleavage sites, hindering protease access. This not only slows down the hydrolysis rate but also reduces the bioavailability of essential amino acids, thereby diminishing nutritional value (Chen et al., 2023; Zhao, Yang, et al., 2024). In contrast, controlled structural modifications induced by processing techniques, such as pH adjustments, can enhance digestibility. By promoting a moderate protein unfolding, these methods disrupt aggregates and expose previously hidden cleavage sites, thus increasing protease susceptibility (Wang, Zhou, et al., 2024). However, it is important to note that excessive processing (e.g., raising the pH to 11) may trigger irreversible protein aggregation and nonfunctional refolding, leading to the formation of a protein structure that is less accessible to enzymes, ultimately negating the potential benefits (Chen et al., 2024).

As shown in Fig. 7A, during the gastric digestion stage, the digestibility of the MP-9 group was not significantly different from that of the control (p > 0.05), whereas that of the MP-10 group increased significantly to 57.90 %. This suggests that moderate alkaline treatment (pH 9–10) promoted partial protein unfolding, which enhanced pepsin accessibility. Conversely, the digestibility of the MP-11 group decreased significantly to 50.52 % (p < 0.05), likely due to irreversible aggregation caused by the high alkaline conditions. The gastric digestibility of all MF-assisted groups (MP-9 + MF, MP-10 + MF, and MP-11 + MF) was also significantly lower than that of the control (p < 0.05), suggesting that MF-assisted treatment induced structural changes unfavorable for digestion, consistent with its role in potentiating the effects of the alkaline environment. During the intestinal digestion stage, the trends were similar to those observed in the gastric stage. Notably, the intestinal digestibility of all samples was significantly higher than their gastric digestibility, which is attributed to the broader substrate specificity of pancreatin, a multi-enzyme complex capable of more extensive hydrolysis (Cao et al., 2023).

Number and molecular weight of peptides from in vitro digestion

As shown in Fig. 7B, the total number of peptides released after in vitro digestion varied significantly among the different treatment groups. Compared to the control group, the number of peptides was significantly increased in the MP-9 and MP-10 treatments. Specifically, these two groups yielded the highest peptide numbers, consistent with the protein digestibility results (Fig. 7A). This indicates that moderate pH-shifting treatment (pH 9–10) enhanced hydrolysis efficiency by inducing partial protein unfolding, thereby increasing the accessibility of internal cleavage sites to proteases (Cao et al., 2023). Conversely, a significant decrease in peptide numbers was observed under excessive pH-shifting (MP-11) and with MF treatments at mild alkaline pH (MP-9 + MF and MP-10 + MF). Under these mild alkaline conditions, the MF application led to a significant reduction in the number of peptides released after digestion. During the protein refolding process, the MF exerts a force on the unfolded, charged polypeptide chains, which inhibits disordered unfolding and promotes the formation of a more stable three-dimensional structure. This conformation effectively masks numerous protease cleavage sites within the protein interior, thereby reducing enzyme accessibility. This outcome of an enhanced protein structure is consistent with previous experimental results, such as decreased surface hydrophobicity and increased order in the secondary structure. However, a contrasting phenomenon was observed under the extreme condition of pH 11, where the peptide count in the MP-11 + MF group was significantly higher than that in the MP-11 group. This is primarily attributed to the force exerted by the MF on the unfolded chains, which modulates the protein aggregation pathway in the highly alkaline environment. This modulation prevents complete, disordered entanglement, and instead induces the formation of aggregates with a greater exposure of cleavage sites, resulting in a comparatively higher degree of hydrolysis.

To further investigate the composition of the digested products, the molecular weight (MW) distribution of the peptides was analyzed (Fig. 7C). In all samples, peptides in the 1000–1500 Da range constituted the predominant fraction (43.7 %–47.2 %). A key observation was that the MP-11 group, which exhibited the lowest degree of hydrolysis, had the highest relative proportion of small peptides (<1000 Da) at 17.8 %. This is likely due to protein aggregation limiting protease access to the protein's interior, causing hydrolysis to occur primarily on accessible molecular surfaces. This preferentially generated small peptide fragments while leaving the core protein structure largely undigested. Consequently, although the total number of peptides produced was the lowest (Fig. 7B), the proportion of small peptides among those generated was disproportionately high. In contrast, the MP-9 and MP-10 groups, particularly MP-10 with the highest degree of hydrolysis, produced a greater number of peptides in the 1000–2000 Da range, indicating more comprehensive hydrolysis. These findings demonstrate that different treatments not only affect the extent of hydrolysis but also alter the MW distribution of the resulting products, potentially influencing the composition of bioactive peptides (typically <2000 Da) (Sánchez-Velázquez et al., 2021).

Protein conformation directly influences the recognition of specific cleavage sites by digestive enzymes. Trypsin primarily hydrolyzes peptide bonds at the C-terminus of arginine (Arg) and lysine (Lys) residues (Ballatore et al., 2020); thus, the number of peptides with these C-terminal residues reflects the efficiency of tryptic hydrolysis. As shown in Fig. 7D, in line with the protein digestibility trend (Fig. 7A), the number of peptides with C-terminal Arg and Lys was significantly higher in the MP-9 and MP-10 groups than in the control. These groups exhibited the highest counts, consistent with moderate pH-shifting effectively exposing trypsin's recognition sites by altering protein conformation (Cao et al., 2023). This enhancement aligns with reduced oxidative modification of Arg and Lys (e.g., carbonylation of the ε-amino group of Lys), improving their availability for cleavage. However, in MF-treated groups, a more complex pattern emerged. For peptides with C-terminal Arg, their numbers were lower in all MF-treated groups compared to their non-MF counterparts. In contrast, for peptides with C-terminal Lys, the number in the MP-11 + MF group was significantly higher than in both the MP-11 and control groups. Collectively, these results suggest that excessive pH-shifting reduces enzymatic efficiency by promoting protein aggregation, while the magnetic field differentially affects cleavage site accessibility, likely due to its influence on protein conformation and aggregation dynamics, thereby altering the digestion and degradation pattern of MP.

Discussion

This study systematically elucidates how the structural modification of frozen denatured MP, induced by pH-shifting and MF-assisted treatments, dictates its oxidative stability and in vitro digestibility. A key finding is the seemingly contradictory relationship between different markers of protein oxidation and structure. While higher pH treatments (pH 10 and 11) effectively promoted a more ordered and compact protein conformation, as evidenced by significantly reduced surface hydrophobicity and well-preserved sulfhydryl content, they paradoxically led to an increase in carbonyl content. This discrepancy highlights that the “unfolding–refolding” mechanism is not uniformly protective. The process effectively reconstructs the protein's tertiary structure to shield its hydrophobic core and cysteine residues, thereby enhancing its stability against aggregation and disulfide-mediated cross-linking. However, this same refolding process reconfigures the protein surface and inadvertently exposes other oxidation-susceptible residues (e.g., lysine, arginine) to the environment, making them prime targets for carbonylation.

The introduction of a magnetic field acts as a potentiator of this refolding process. At the mild alkaline condition of pH 9, the MF exhibited a clear synergistic effect, guiding the partially unfolded polypeptide chains into a highly ordered and compact structure. This conformation proved optimal for overall oxidative stability, minimizing both carbonyl formation (residue protection) and sulfhydryl loss (conformational integrity). However, this synergy was highly pH-dependent. At pH 11, the combined stress of extreme alkalinity and the MF appeared to accelerate irreversible modifications and promote the formation of non-functional aggregates, thereby compromising aspects of protein stability, as indicated by SDS-PAGE.

Furthermore, a critical inverse relationship was revealed between maximal oxidative stability and in vitro digestibility. The highly compact and rigid conformation achieved by the MP-9 + MF treatment, while excellent for resisting oxidation, sterically hindered the access of digestive enzymes to their cleavage sites, resulting in suppressed digestibility. In contrast, the MP-10 treatment, without MF, struck an balance between structural stability and conformational flexibility. The resulting conformation was stable enough to reduce oxidation-induced aggregation but retained sufficient flexibility to allow proteases access to their recognition sites, as evidenced by the maximal number of peptides released with C-terminal Arg and Lys. Recent studies emphasize this balance, suggesting that excessive rigidity or aggregation can impede enzymatic digestion by concealing key cleavage sites. For instance, Liu et al. (2025) and Chen et al. (2022) demonstrated that proteins subjected to oxidative modifications or extreme structural stability exhibited reduced digestibility due to limited enzyme-substrate interactions. These findings further indicate the importance of maintaining an optimal balance between oxidative stability and protease accessibility. Therefore, the decline in digestibility would be observed under more intense conditions (e.g., pH 11 or with MF assistance).

Conclusion

This study systematically demonstrated that the combination of MF treatment and pH-shifting can effectively modulate the structural and functional properties of frozen denatured MP. Specifically, MF-assisted pH-shifting at pH 9 significantly improved oxidative stability by promoting compact refolding and reducing the exposure of oxidation-sensitive residues, while pH-shifting at pH 10 without MF maximized enzymatic digestibility by achieving an optimal balance between unfolding and refolding. Notably, these findings reveal a pH-dependent trade-off between oxidative stability and digestibility, indicating that treatment conditions should be carefully tailored to achieve desired functional properties.

From a practical perspective, the results provide a new strategy for the meat industry to mitigate protein denaturation and oxidative deterioration that commonly occur during frozen storage. By fine-tuning pH-shifting conditions and introducing MF assistance, manufacturers can customize meat processing approaches to prioritize either shelf-life extension (via enhanced oxidative stability) or improved nutritional quality (via enhanced digestibility). For example, MF-assisted pH-shifting at pH 9 could be applied to frozen meat products that require longer storage and oxidative resistance, whereas pH-shifting at pH 10 without MF may be better suited for products where digestibility and nutritional release are critical, such as in elderly or clinical nutrition applications.

Overall, this combined strategy offers a flexible and targeted processing tool for improving the quality of frozen meat products, with potential applications in industrial meat preservation, functional food development, and the design of protein-rich products tailored to diverse consumer needs. Future research could focus on optimizing treatment parameters for different meat types and storage conditions, as well as evaluating sensory attributes and in vitro digestibility to support industrial implementation.
