Implementing shorter all-oral treatment durations for multidrug-resistant tuberculosis in the UK: from concept to application
Authors: Aneeka Chavda, Mark Gilchrist, Toby Capstick, Christabelle Chen, Onn Min Kon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) is a global public health threat associated with high mortality and challenging treatment regimens. The UK, like many countries, faces a growing incidence of MDR-TB, with 105 (1.9% of all notified cases) treated as MDR-resistant or rifampicin-resistant TB in 2024. Recent clinical trials have demonstrated the efficacy and safety of shorter, all-oral regimens such as BPaLM (bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid, moxifloxacin), yet their implementation in the UK presents unique challenges, particularly around the antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) agenda, such as the lack of licensed drugs, pharmacokinetic/dynamic concerns, fragile supply chains and high cost of second-line antituberculosis drugs. This narrative review outlines how the UK has addressed implementation hurdles through coordinated efforts by the British Thoracic Society MDR-TB Clinical Advice Service and National Health Service England. This collaboration has facilitated the adoption of new regimens, with 54 out of 60 requests for using pretomanid-containing regimens approved in the UK up to January 2026. Furthermore, resources like the TB Drug Monographs and the shift to video-observed therapy have streamlined care delivery. The UK has effectively navigated the transition to shorter, all-oral MDR-TB regimens, significantly enhancing patient care and operational efficiency. The integration of clinical guidance, AMS principles, policy reform and specialised monitoring tools provides a robust framework for managing evolving treatment landscapes and delivering patient-centred care.

Background

Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB) represents one of the foremost global causes of mortality. According to the WHO, approximately 10.8 million individuals were diagnosed with TB in 2023, resulting in an estimated 1.25 million fatalities.1 In 2015, the WHO launched a global TB strategy with the objective of eradicating the epidemic by reducing both deaths and new cases by 95% by the year 2035.1 2 In England, this objective translates to an annual decline in TB notifications of 18.1% for the next 11 years.3 Unfortunately, the decline in the notification rate has not only decelerated over the past 4 years, but there is now an increasing trend.

Rifampicin-resistant (RR) or multidrug-resistant (MDR) TB poses a significant threat to public health programmes, as it is associated with higher mortality rates.4 This scenario often leads to longer treatment durations, reduced adherence rates and an increased risk of drug toxicity.5 In England, there has been an upward trend in the number and proportion of individuals treated for MDR or RR-TB; increasing from 99 out of 8282 (1.2%) in 2011 to 105 out of 5490 (1.9%) in 2024.3 This demonstrates a growing need for alternative treatment regimens that require a shorter duration and fewer antibiotics that are better tolerated.

In recent years, first-line treatment has shifted to an all-oral regimen used under programmatic conditions,6 consisting of a minimum of 4–5 drugs and a total duration of 18–20 months.7 This regimen was based on expert opinion and pooled analyses of observational studies due to the lack of evidence from contemporary randomised controlled trials.6 7 However, with the emergence of new and repurposed drugs like bedaquiline, delamanid, pretomanid and linezolid, the approach to managing MDR/RR-TB has been transformed, with clinical trials demonstrating that significantly shorter durations are now possible.8 9

In 2024, the WHO issued a rapid communication followed by the updated WHO Consolidated Guidelines on Tuberculosis in 2025,10 recommending the adoption of four new 6–9 month regimens, but their application is limited to selected populations, excluding children, pregnant women, CNS and disseminated disease. The UK faces challenges implementing these regimens due to high drug costs and outdated TB guidelines.11

This narrative review focuses on the evolving landscape of MDR-TB treatment, highlighting advancements in treatment strategies. It explores the shift towards newer oral regimens and how the UK has adapted practices to incorporate emerging study data to deliver the best care for MDR/RR-TB patients.

6-month regimens

Three key studies—Nix,12 ZeNix13 and TB-PRACTECAL14—demonstrated high success rates with treatment regimens comprising bedaquiline, pretomanid and linezolid in adults and adolescents with pulmonary multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB). Nix was a 26-week, open-label trial with 109 patients with MDR-TB or extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB) unresponsive to second-line drugs or who stopped treatment due to side effects.12 Unfavourable outcomes at 6 months were seen in 10% of patients. The combination of bedaquiline, pretomanid and linezolid was effective, though 1200 mg daily linezolid caused high adverse effects—peripheral neuropathy (81%), myelosuppression (48%) and optic neuritis (2%). These were manageable with dose adjustments. ZeNix was a trial to determine the optimal linezolid dose that balanced efficacy and toxicity.13 181 patients with pulmonary XDR, pre-XDR or RR-TB received bedaquiline, pretomanid, plus linezolid (1200 or 600 mg) for 26 or 9 weeks. Favourable outcomes were reported in 93%, 89%, 91% and 84% of participants. Fewer side effects occurred with a 600 mg dose over 26 weeks, establishing this as the recommended dose. TB-PRACTECAL was an open-label trial comparing three 24-week regimens of oral bedaquiline, pretomanid and linezolid, either alone (BPaL), or in combination with clofazimine (BPaLC) or with moxifloxacin (BPaLM) to standard care.14 Among 552 patients, BPaLM had fewer unfavourable outcomes compared with standard care (12% vs 41%) and better tolerability, with fewer high-grade adverse events.

The BEAT-TB trial followed, which evaluated a 6-month treatment regimen of bedaquiline, delamanid, linezolid, along with levofloxacin and/or clofazimine (BDLLfxC), depending on fluoroquinolone resistance for pulmonary RR-TB, compared with the then-current South African 9-month or longer regimens.15 The BDLLfxC regimen demonstrated non-inferiority for effectiveness and safety. Successful outcomes (treatment completion and end-of-follow-up at 76 weeks) were achieved in 86.1% vs 86.0% of patients in the study and control groups, respectively. Severe adverse events during treatment were reported in 31.2% and 37% of patients, respectively.

9-month regimens

The multinational STREAM stage 1 trial demonstrated non-inferiority of a seven-drug, 9-month regimen that included an injectable agent compared with 20-month longer regimens.16 This was endorsed by WHO as an option for patients with no additional drug resistance in the absence of disseminated, meningeal or central nervous system (CNS) TB.17 Limitations with using injectables led to the STREAM 2 trial, which replaced kanamycin with bedaquiline to create a 9-month all-oral regimen.18 It demonstrated favourable outcomes and superiority over the 9-month injectable regimen. Disadvantages of these regimens include the complexity of drug combinations, pill burden and the potential for drug toxicity.

By November 2023, the endTB trial evaluated five 9-month regimens versus standard 18-month treatments. BLMZ (bedaquiline, linezolid, moxifloxacin and pyrazinamide) was preferred over BLLfxCZ (bedaquiline, linezolid, levofloxacin, clofazimine and pyrazinamide), which was preferred over BDLLfxZ (bedaquiline, delamanid, linezolid, levofloxacin and pyrazinamide). Two regimens lacking bedaquiline were associated with higher rates of treatment failure, relapse and acquired resistance.19 Favourable outcomes were reported in 89.0%, 90.4%, 85.2% and 80.7% of participants treated with BLMZ, BLLfxCZ, BDLLfxZ and standard longer regimens, respectively. The incidence of serious adverse events was similar, occurring in 14.3%, 13.1%, 15.7% and 16.7% of participants, respectively.

In summary, these trial results enable shorter and effective oral regimens, and they are likely to continue evolving due to the rapidly changing evidence in this field. For the first time, we have the opportunity to select from multiple all-oral regimens that enable more patient-centred and cost-effective care, improving quality of life and helping to support the UK Antimicrobial Action Plan in preventing antimicrobial resistance.

UK experience with implementing shorter all-oral regimens

Although the WHO rapid communications were issued to inform national TB programmes about key changes in DR-TB treatment prior to the release of the updated consolidated guidelines, the UK experienced a delay in adopting these regimens. Managing MDR-TB has always been challenging in the UK due to factors such as nationally commissioned specialised drugs and adapting to the newly emerging treatment regimens. Additionally, these studies mainly involved non-UK population, and some consideration needs to be applied to the UK demographic. In the UK, the most common TB lineages are Euro-American (lineage 4) and East African-Indian (Lineage 3).20 The Indo-Oceanic (lineage 1) lineages are also present to a lesser extent and may pose challenges for implementing the BPaLM regimen due to the higher minimum inhibitory concentration for pretomanid observed in this phenotype, potentially affecting its efficacy.

The UK was an early adopter of the 6-month BPaLM/BPaL regimen and is now regarded as the preferred standard for MDR-TB where there are no contraindications. To date, the challenges this transition has introduced within the UK have been addressed through coordinated efforts among National Health Service England (NHSE), specialist clinical networks and policy infrastructure. Specialist clinical networks

The British Thoracic Society (BTS) MDR-TB Clinical Advice Service (CAS) is a national expert group with specialists in respiratory medicine, infectious diseases, microbiology, specialist respiratory and infection pharmacy, nursing and public health. They offer comprehensive advice on managing all MDR-TB and complex TB cases, which are often complicated by treatment challenges and new regimens. They also offer consensus guidance and approve high-cost NHSE drugs such as bedaquiline, delamanid and pretomanid. They assist practitioners with interpreting whole genome sequencing data and managing adverse drug reactions (ADRs) to ensure safe, effective treatment. The service operates via a secure online forum and monthly multidisciplinary team (MDT) meetings.21

Policy infrastructure

NHSE commissions highly specialised services and the associated high-cost drugs (bedaquiline, delamanid and pretomanid) for MDR-TB treatment.22 23 The WHO 20249 rapid communication led NHSE to review evidence to support the commissioning of these specialised drugs to ensure equitable accessibility to eligible patients and promote widespread adoption across England. This was implemented through policies specifying funded treatments based on clinical effectiveness, cost-efficiency and ethical considerations. Additionally, they establish criteria for use, monitoring parameters and duration. NHSE monitors adherence to policy via a central database (Blueteq) which is mandatory for approval of patient-specific use of the high-cost MDR-TB drugs. Further clinical governance supporting the use of these regimens is achieved through the additional requirement for BTS MDR-TB CAS approval.21

Monitoring: TB drug monographs

To transition from theoretical safety profiles into clinical practice, the UK has adopted a stratified monitoring approach. The UK-based TB drug monographs24 are an open-access guidance on both initial and ongoing monitoring for all antituberculosis drugs, especially those used in MDR-TB regimens. The guidance supports antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) principles and aids in providing guidance on dosing, common and serious adverse effects, drug interactions and provides practical and routine monitoring schedules (see table 3). The monographs are vital tools for staff working within TB, ensuring safe prescribing and early detection of toxicity.

As one example, linezolid requires a monitoring schedule aligned with its pharmacokinetics and cumulative toxicity, particularly myelosuppression and peripheral or optic neuropathy. This has been incorporated into the TB monograph guidance, with a proactive approach to enable personalised dosing, such as reducing linezolid from 600 to 300 mg in the event of linezolid-specific toxicity (WHO),10 although we would recommend confirming therapeutic dosing with therapeutic drug monitoring.

National TB programmes and monitoring ADR drug safety

WHO recommends that National TB programmes implement an active TB drug-safety monitoring and management (aDSM) framework to detect, manage and report suspected or confirmed ADRs, particularly where new TB medicines or novel MDR-TB regimens are used.25

We are not aware of any systematic aDSM schemes currently operational in the UK, although clinicians are encouraged to report adverse events to the BTS MDR-TB CAS.21 Furthermore, pharmacovigilance in the UK requires clinicians to report new and serious ADRs to the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) via the yellow card system. These systems are limited in the UK due to the relatively low level of prescribing of second-line antituberculosis drugs, such that low-frequency or serious ADRs may not be easily identified. The aDSM framework allows enhanced pharmacovigilance towards these second-line antituberculosis drugs and early identification of drug toxicity by pooling data across nations, and future work could focus on implementing this across the UK.

Sourcing and fragile supply chains of MDR-TB treatment

Like many countries, the UK faces medication supply disruptions, especially for antituberculosis therapy, affecting TB services including AMS programmes and patients care. In the UK, sourcing and supply chain fragility were addressed through three primary mechanisms: the NHSE Interim Clinical Commissioning Policy, centralised BTS MDR-TB CAS oversight and a hub-and-spoke procurement model. For example, pretomanid initially needed unlicensed imports through the MHRA’s unlicensed medicines route.26 However, the 2024 WHO recommendation prompted an expedited procurement process timeline. National responses to antituberculosis drug shortages, such as those formalised in the National Patient Safety Alert,27 strengthened these mechanisms. These safety alerts required coordinated action among stakeholders, including NHSE, UKHSA, clinicians and pharmacists, BTS and Pharmacy Procurement Specialists, to secure a national volume commitment. For MDR-TB, this allows regional specialist centres to hold buffer stocks of unlicensed agents such as pretomanid, ensuring that supply disruptions, which typically impact first-line therapy, do not delay the initiation of BPaL(M) regimens. Furthermore, the BTS MDR-TB CAS standardises and streamlines the informed consent process, ensuring regulatory barriers do not cause clinical inertia or unfair access.

Impact for patients

The introduction of shorter 6–9-month all-oral regimens is expected to improve the patient experience within the UK, as shorter regimens confer substantial benefits, including significantly lower pill burden and fewer ADRs. Furthermore, all-oral regimens have the advantage over historical longer regimens that included injectable aminoglycosides, as these avoid the risk of high toxicity rates experienced with these drugs. However, the risk of drug–drug interactions (DDIs) and ADRs still exists. Despite these risks, the shortened regimens improve the opportunity for greater adherence and thus treatment completion.

Impact for TB services

The adoption of novel shorter regimens over longer regimens facilitates a recalibration of TB services, due to the reduction in the number of patients at any one time requiring in-person directly observed therapy or video-observed therapy (VOT). This frees up existing nursing capacity, enabling more efficient care pathways, increasing capacity for enhanced contact tracing, latent TB management and health promotion.

The reduction in duration of disease and safety monitoring for shorter regimens compared with longer 18-month regimens is cost-saving, with BPaLM saving US$80–US$904 per person, with savings having the potential to free up resources for investment in other areas of TB programmes, such as improving patient support and expanding TB case-finding efforts.28

Social determinants and programmatic management

Implementation in the UK recognises that shorter regimens are only effective if the social determinants of health are addressed concurrently. Under the National Health Service (Charges to Overseas Visitors) Regulations 2015, TB treatment is exempt from all charges, ensuring that legal status is not a barrier to care.29 This statutory support is operationalised through the TB Action Plan for England 2021–2026,30 which prioritises ‘Inclusion Health’ groups,31 providing the social scaffolding, such as medical housing and the use of VOT to maintain adherence. This has proven essential to ensure treatment adherence in populations with social risk factors, such as those experiencing homelessness or precarious housing. This policy lever is essential for the success of shorter regimens among Inclusion Health groups, as it mitigates the fear of financial or legal repercussions—a known driver of treatment interruption in underserved populations.31 Furthermore, shorter regimens reduce the time a patient needs to be ‘visible’ to the health system, lowering the risk of treatment interruption because of the transient nature of these groups.

Outcomes

Since the implementation of the NHSE commissioning policy, the BTS MDR-TB CAS has supported 54 out of 60 requests to use pretomanid as part of a BPaL/BPaLM regimen up to 6 January 2026. Prescription data across the NHS in England show that prescribing of pretomanid has increased from an average of 9 patients per month in the first 6 months of 2024 to 45 patients in the same period of 2025.32

Conclusion

It is important that practitioners are aware of the latest evidence-based approach to care when treating MDR-TB. This includes utilising the expertise of all members of the TB MDT (medical, nursing, pharmacy). Targeted therapeutics, standardised eligibility, shorter durations and a move to more evidence-based oral therapy with improved outcomes are now the mainstay of MDR-TB management and further support the approach of the UK AMR Action Plan in minimising resistance.
