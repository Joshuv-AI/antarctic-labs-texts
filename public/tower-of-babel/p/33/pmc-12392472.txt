D-S-Net: an efficient dual-stage strategy for high-precision segmentation of gross tumor volumes in lung cancer CT images
Authors: Chen Yi, Shaofeng Jiang, Liangli Xiong, Jun Yang, Huanhuan Shi, Qiliang Xiong, Bo Hu, Huaiwen Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate delineation of Gross Tumor Volume (GTV) in lung cancer is critical for effective radiotherapy and surgical planning. However, segmentation of GTV in high-resolution CT images remains challenging, particularly when tumors are small or have indistinct boundaries.MethodsWe propose D-S-Net, a novel dual-stage strategy to enhance both the accuracy and efficiency of lung cancer GTV segmentation. In the first stage, a simplified detection network is used to locate candidate regions in high-resolution (512×512) CT slices, reducing input size and computational demand. In the second stage, a modified U-Net variant is applied to perform fine segmentation within the detected regions. The architecture incorporates a spatial attention mechanism and employs a combined loss function (binary cross-entropy and Dice loss) to address class imbalance.ResultsOn the lung cancer GTV dataset, D-S-Net achieved a Dice coefficient of 78.52%, representing an improvement of 5.49% over SwinU-Net and outperforming several mainstream models. On the second dataset, D-S-Net reached a Dice coefficient of 86.56%, surpassing the second-best model by 13.19%. Ablation studies demonstrated that the detection stage, spatial attention, and combined loss function effectively improved performance, while computational complexity analysis confirmed the model’s efficiency.ConclusionThe proposed D-S-Net offers a robust and efficient solution for the segmentation of lung cancer GTV in CT images. Its dual-stage design and attention-based enhancements contribute to both accuracy and computational gains, highlighting its potential for clinical applications in radiotherapy planning.

Introduction

In the field of medical imaging analysis, accurately delineating the Gross Tumor Volume (GTV) in lung cancer is crucial for ensuring precise radiotherapy and surgical planning. As one of the most common malignancies worldwide, lung cancer accounts for over 2.2 million new cases and approximately 1.7 million deaths annually, making it the cancer type with the highest number of new cases and the second highest mortality rate [1]. According to statistics from the World Health Organization's International Agency for Research on Cancer, there were as many as 1.8 million lung cancer deaths globally in 2021, making it the deadliest cancer type [2]. In China, lung cancer is not only the most prevalent malignant tumor but also the leading cause of cancer-related deaths [3].

Despite significant progress in multimodal therapy over the past decade, lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related deaths, accounting for 25% of all cancer-related deaths [4]. Radiotherapy, particularly Stereotactic Body Radiation Therapy (SBRT), is one of the main methods for treating lung cancer, with approximately 60%−70% of patients requiring this type of treatment. SBRT is suitable for patients with early-stage non-small cell lung cancer, delivering high doses of radiation to small targets through multiple conformal coplanar and non-coplanar beams [5–7]. In radiotherapy planning, accurately delineating Gross Tumor Volumes (GTVs) is essential for precise Clinical Target Volume (CTVs) and Planning Target Volume (PTVs) formulation [8].

Traditionally, radiation oncologists rely on manual methods to extract tumor areas in Computed Tomography (CT) images [9, 10]. However, manual delineation can lead to observer variability in GTV contours, affecting the accuracy of treatment planning, random and systematic variations in treatment plans, and the distribution of treatment doses [11, 12]. Such contouring errors propagate directly into the Planning Target Volume (PTV) margin; inter-observer variability therefore widens the margin and unnecessarily irradiates more healthy tissue, whereas automated segmentation can curb these errors, enabling tighter margins and improved clinical outcomes [13, 14]. Consequently, robust automated segmentation methods have become indispensable in contemporary SBRT workflows [15].

With advancements in medical imaging and computer technologies, tumor radiotherapy has entered a high-precision era guided by images [16]. In radiotherapy planning, the accurate delineation of target areas and Organs At Risk (OARs) is particularly critical [17]. Despite uniform principles and consensus, the traditional manual delineation method for radiotherapy targets still relies on the practitioner's experience, a method that is not only variable and time-consuming but can also affect the efficacy of radiotherapy [18]. In recent years, the application of artificial intelligence technologies, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), has shown significant advantages in improving the efficiency and consistency of target delineation in radiotherapy [19]. CNNs are insensitive to image noise, blurring, and contrast, and perform excellently in medical image segmentation [20].

However, challenges still exist in the automatic segmentation of GTV for lung cancer radiotherapy planning. The low contrast between tumors and surrounding tissues in medical images increases the difficulty of automatic recognition and segmentation (Fig. 1A). The small proportion of tumor areas in lung CT images leads to class imbalance, affecting the performance of automatic segmentation algorithms (Fig. 1B). In addition, respiration-induced tumor motion is another considerable difficulty in lung tumor segmentation, as the use of 4DCT can help mitigate this issue to some extent, but motion artifacts may still be present even with respiratory-correlated imaging. Finally, the high heterogeneity of internal structures within lung cancer tumors, such as cystic areas, calcifications, and necrosis, adds further complexity to the segmentation task (Fig. 1C).Fig. 1Examples of GTV regions in three lung samples

Examples of GTV regions in three lung samples

To address these challenges, this paper proposes a two-stage D-S-Net network. In the first stage, the network uses a target detection method to preprocess images to be segmented, effectively reducing image size and easing the computational burden of subsequent processing. In the second stage, our improved U-Net variant model is used for more refined image segmentation, achieving more precise tumor area localization. Through this innovative approach, D-S-Net effectively overcomes the limitations of traditional methods, improves segmentation accuracy, and provides strong technical support for clinical radiotherapy planning.

Related work

In the field of medical image analysis and image segmentation tasks, the development of the U-net architecture and its derivative models marks an important milestone in image segmentation technology. Since the introduction of U-net [21], its basic structure has been widely used and continuously improved in medical image processing. For instance, UNet++ [22], with its deep supervision encoder-decoder network and redesigned dense skip pathways, significantly reduces the semantic gap between the encoder and decoder subnetworks, demonstrating superior performance over the original U-Net and Wide U-Net in various medical image segmentation tasks. Swin-Unet [23] integrates Swin Transformer blocks into the classic U-shaped encoder–decoder framework with skip connections, effectively capturing both local and global semantic features. By leveraging this hybrid design, Swin-Unet achieves superior performance in multi-organ and cardiac segmentation tasks compared to traditional convolution-based methods. The nn-UNet [24] specifically addresses medical image segmentation challenges and demonstrated its superiority at the 2021 International MICCAI Brainlesion Workshop. This study builds upon the previous year's winning entry, including network size adjustments, replacing batch normalization with group normalization, and applying axial attention in the decoder, achieving significant accomplishments in brain tumor segmentation with Dice scores of 88.35%, 88.78%, and 93.19% for the enhancing tumor, tumor core, and whole tumor, respectively.

Furthermore, attention mechanisms in deep learning, mimicking human visual attention, can identify and focus on key information in a vast amount of data while ignoring irrelevant details. This process involves two steps: first, computing the attention distribution over the entire set of information; second, calculating the weighted average of the input information based on the attention distribution. Various attention models have been proposed in fields such as natural language processing [25], image classification [26], and machine translation [27]. For example, Hu [28] introduced the SE module focusing on channel-level feature correlations; Woo [29] developed CBAM, combining spatial and channel attention to capture global feature dependencies; Wu [30] implemented self-attention mechanisms, analyzing relationships between pixels at different distances; Gao [31] proposed GCNet, merging the advantages of Non-local [32] and SENet [33], effectively integrating global context while maintaining a lightweight design. Attention mechanisms, by computing interconnections among different positions, not only bring long-range dependencies and an expanded receptive field to the model but also enhance target area representation and optimize local constraints during the convolution process, thus improving performance and interpretability.

As network depth increases, the richness of extractable feature information also increases; however, excessive depth can lead to issues like network degradation and gradient vanishing. To optimize the training of deep networks, Residual Networks (Resnet) [33] were proposed, significantly enhancing model depth and accuracy through the introduction of skip connections. Resnet's skip connections rapidly transfer input data directly to the output, preserving information integrity and simplifying learning objectives and difficulties, thereby mitigating network degradation and gradient vanishing issues. The simple and easily optimized structure of residual blocks has led to their widespread adoption in deep convolutional networks. Diakogiannis [34] and Ibtehaz [35] proposed Resnet variants optimized for specific datasets, such as ResUNet-a and the improved U-Net residual path. Researchers can choose the appropriate residual block structure according to task requirements, such as a sequence of two 3×3 convolutions or 1×1, 3×3, and 1×1 convolutions.

In practical applications, U-net and its variants are widely used for various medical image segmentation tasks. Şaban Öztürk and Kemal Polat introduced a novel polyp segmentation method combining U-Net with visual saliency feature fusion, surpassing existing state-of-the-art methods and significantly improving the accuracy of colonoscopic polyp detection [36]. Based on the capabilities of U-net, their research represents further development in the field of polyp segmentation, emphasizing the importance of improving the accuracy of colonoscopic polyp detection [37]. Additionally, in the context of automatic diatom image segmentation, H GÜNDÜZ and colleagues developed a new model combining image processing and deep learning. Their model's performance surpassed previous methods and provided a publicly available diatom image dataset [38]. In the field of retinal vessel segmentation, M SHABANI and their team proposed an improved active contour model combined with discrete wavelet transform, with performance on par with state-of-the-art methods across multiple databases [39]. These studies collectively emphasize the versatility and efficacy of the U-net architecture and its variants in medical image analysis, particularly in image segmentation, achieving significant results against various challenges.

Methodology

D-S-Net

This study aims to address the image segmentation task of GTV regions in 512×512 pixel CT axial 2D images, targeting small and morphologically diverse target areas. To this end, we have drawn inspiration from the field of object detection and designed the D-S-Net (Object Detection Network (D-Net) and Image Segmentation Network (S-Net)). The core design of this network is divided into two main stages, as shown in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Structure of D-S-Net

Structure of D-S-Net

In the first stage, we introduce a target segmentation strategy. Specifically, through a customized Region of Interest (ROI) extraction network, we achieve precise localization of target bounding box. In the operational process, we first input the training set images and their corresponding target detection boxes into our self-designed Object Detection Network (D-Net). Notably, to demonstrate the effectiveness of our design philosophy, we did not choose existing advanced object detection networks but constructed a simple dual-layer CNN network to perform the object detection task. After training this network, we can draw predicted target bounding box on the images of the test set. Next, based on these predicted frames, we construct an optimal rectangular frame that covers all predicted frames, ensuring that all labels in the test set are included. To adapt to the subsequent fine segmentation network, we set the size of the rectangular frame to be a multiple of 25. In this study, the final rectangular frame was set to 160 × 224 pixels with coordinates (197, 142, 357, 366) for the lung-cancer dataset, whereas for the liver-cancer dataset we used a 384 × 384-pixel frame positioned at (47, 0, 431, 384). By aligning all images with this rectangular frame and performing precise cropping, we can effectively focus on the key areas to be segmented. This step not only highlights the target areas but also notably reduces background noise in the images, providing clear and accurate input for subsequent image segmentation tasks. This method offers the possibility of enhancing the precision and efficiency of segmentation algorithms, thereby ensuring the high quality and reliability of segmentation results, which is particularly crucial for processing complex and variable target areas.

In the second stage, we crop the area to be segmented based on the target frame determined in the first stage, and apply the innovative AU-net model for fine segmentation. This model adopts an encoder-decoder architecture and integrates an attention mechanism to enhance accuracy and detail restoration. After segmentation, we also remap the results back to the original 512×512 size to ensure data consistency. The encoder of AU-net is responsible for feature extraction, the decoder for image reconstruction, and the attention module focuses on key features, optimizing the overall segmentation effect. Encoder

As shown in the upper half of Fig. 3, we designed five convolutional units, each consisting of an 8x8 convolutional layer, batch normalization (BN), and ReLU activation. This design aims to expand the range of the receptive field. To minimize the loss of feature information, we use convolutional downsampling with a stride of 2 instead of traditional pooling techniques. The number of convolutional layers in the encoder is the same as in the standard U-Net, catering to the need for multi-scale features in medical image segmentation. Through hierarchical convolutional processing, the encoder captures features at different scales: shallow feature maps focus on capturing detailed information, such as attributes of small targets and boundary features, while deeper feature maps concentrate on rough localization, although they are not as detailed as the shallow layers in processing details. The design focus of the decoder is on effectively integrating these multi-scale feature maps to improve the accuracy of image segmentation.

Decoder

As shown in the lower half of Fig. 3, the decoder forms a symmetrical structure with the encoder. Each time the encoder performs downsampling, the decoder correspondingly executes an upsampling step to restore the image resolution. Compared to the standard U-Net model, our decoder employs an improved strategy in the upsampling process. Traditionally, the U-Net model enlarges the scale of deep feature maps through methods like nearest neighbor interpolation and then fuses these enlarged feature maps with feature maps of the same scale from the encoding path, serving as the input for the next step of upsampling. However, due to the significant semantic difference between deep and shallow feature maps and the potential loss of image details with increased network depth, the traditional simple skip connection approach performs poorly in addressing these issues. In response to this challenge, our AU-net model innovatively introduces a parallel skip connection path in the decoder. This design effectively bridges the semantic gap between deep and shallow feature maps while retaining more image details, significantly enhancing the precision and efficiency of image segmentation.

In Fig. 4, we present the detailed structure of the attention module in the AU-net model, which was initially introduced in Fig. 3. The attention module primarily processes two types of feature maps: the deep feature map G and the shallow feature map X. During the module's processing, the deep feature map G is first upscaled using a deconvolution with a 4x4 kernel size and a stride of 2. This step allows G to guide the reallocation of feature weights in the attention path for the shallow feature map X, based on its rich deep semantic information. After this processing, the resulting weighted shallow feature map X'is then fused with the corresponding feature map at the same level in the encoding region to form the next-level attention path input G. This design strategy effectively avoids directly connecting shallow and deep feature maps, solving the feature fusion problem caused by semantic differences in the traditional U-Net model. Additionally, it compensates for small target features that the attention module might overlook by incorporating shallow feature maps. This not only preserves the semantic information of small targets from being diluted but also retains more details and shallow semantic information by utilizing multi-scale features, thus achieving higher accuracy and detail fidelity in image segmentation.Fig. 4The image is the internal structure diagram of the attention module (Att Block)

The image is the internal structure diagram of the attention module (Att Block)

Att Block

The newly added attention path plays a dual role: it not only achieves effective integration of deep semantic information with the decoding path but also significantly reduces noise interference in medical images. Through a detailed analysis of deep semantics, this path precisely adjusts the weight distribution between target and non-target areas, making the target area more prominent and providing the model with key feature information. Specifically, in the process shown in Fig. 4, the shallow feature map X and the deep feature map G are first processed by a 1x1 convolution kernel, followed by channel adjustment and feature cross-fusion in the BN layer. Subsequently, these two types of feature maps are superimposed to highlight the areas the model needs to learn, while suppressing the influence of non-target areas through the ReLU activation function. Further, after processing through another convolution layer, BN layer, and Sigmoid activation layer, the attention coefficients are precisely adjusted within the range of 0 to 1. Finally, this adjusted attention coefficient matrix is multiplied by the original X feature map, forming a new feature map. The weights of this new feature map are optimized to effectively enhance the model's ability to recognize important features.

Training

In this study, the segmentation model for lung cancer was developed using the Pytorch framework and executed on an NVIDIA RTX 3080ti GPU. The setup included Cuda 11.7 and Pytorch 2.0.1, operating on a Windows 10 platform with coding conducted in VsCode 1.75.1. The dataset, composed of axial 2D CT images, was divided into training and validation subsets in a 4:1 ratio, resulting in 1139 images for training and 302 for validation, each with a 512×512 pixel resolution. For the optimization, the Adam optimizer was used. The starting learning rate was set at 1×10−3, with a minimum threshold of 1×10−8, and a weight decay rate of 1×10−5, coupled with a momentum of 0.9. Our training strategy involved halving the learning rate each time there was no reduction in the validation set loss over two successive training periods. The training process extended across 200 epochs, with each batch containing 32 images. Utilizing our dataset, the D-S-Net model successfully completed its training in roughly three hours.

CT scanning parameters

The images used for analysis were obtained from CT scans for patients'radiation therapy positioning The enhanced CT scans were performed using the SOMATOM Definition AS 20 slice CT simulator(Siemens, Germany) system with the following scanning parameters: tube voltage, 120 kVp; tube current,165 mAs; and the upper boundary of the scanning range is the middle neck, and the lower boundary is the upper abdomen (including esophagus and whole lung). The acquired image pixel size was 512×512, and the scanning slice thickness was set at 5 mm with a field of view (FOV) of 400 mm. After scanning, the reconstructed CT images were transferred to the specialized radiation three-dimensional treatment planning system Pinnacle3 version 9.16.

Loss function

In order to optimize the AU-Net model, we adopted a hybrid loss function combining Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) and the Dice loss function. This decision was based on several key considerations: Firstly, the BCE loss function is highly effective in handling pixel-level binary classification issues, as it accurately assesses the discrepancy between model output probabilities and the actual labels. Secondly, the Dice loss function is particularly suitable for dealing with imbalanced datasets like medical images, especially in identifying small areas or rare events. It enhances the model's precision in recognizing small target areas by quantifying the overlap between the predicted segmentation and the actual segmentation. By combining these two loss functions, we can not only leverage their respective strengths but also enable them to complement each other in enhancing the model's performance, which is crucial for handling complex medical image segmentation tasks. Evaluation Indicators

In our study, we evaluated the performance of various methods using the Dice coefficient [40], along with Specificity and Sensitivity metrics [41]. These measures collectively provide a robust framework for quantitatively assessing the accuracy and reliability of medical image segmentation. The Dice coefficient is crucial for evaluating the agreement between segmentation results and actual images, while Specificity and Sensitivity yield insights into the model's ability to accurately identify negative and positive samples, respectively.

Dice Coefficient: This metric, used to gauge the similarity between two datasets, is a common measure in sample comparison. Discussion

To address the notable computational burden of high-resolution (512×512) 2D transverse CT images, this study devised an innovative two-stage processing strategy. In the first stage, we developed a simplified target detection network aimed at roughly locating the areas that need segmentation. Instead of opting for existing complex network architectures, we specifically designed a simple convolutional network for this task. In practical applications, we tried two approaches to complete the first task.

The first approach was to directly detect bounding boxes for the target regions, but this method proved ineffective for two main reasons. First, the accuracy of the detection boxes could not reach 100%, resulting in false positives and false negatives (even when using the yolov8 model, this issue persisted), which led to inaccurate segmentation regions and hindered subsequent segmentation tasks. Second, even when the target regions were detected, the excessive removal of edge information during the detection process made it difficult to accurately identify the GTV targets using only a small portion of the segmented area.

To address these issues, we adopted the second approach: finding the union of all detection boxes and then selecting the smallest bounding box that could enclose all GTV targets. This method avoided over-reliance on the accuracy of a single detection box and effectively achieved the preliminary identification of regions of interest. Although this custom target detection network did not achieve extremely high detection accuracy, our strategy did not depend entirely on the accuracy of the detection boxes, thereby effectively realizing the initial identification of the regions of interest. This method notably reduced image size, optimizing computational load and providing clearer features for the subsequent segmentation network.

Experimental results validated the effectiveness of this strategy. By comparing network with (D) and without (N-D) the target detection phase, we found that the introduction of the D phase notably improved model performance in terms of Dice coefficient and sensitivity in most cases. For instance, in the baseline model, the inclusion of the D phase increased the Dice coefficient and sensitivity from 48.79% and 57.42% to 68.45% and 65.74%, respectively, achieving improvements of 19.66% and 8.32%. Finding a balance between precision and efficiency is key in medical image processing, especially in the segmentation of high-resolution images. Through this innovative two-stage processing strategy, we not only optimized computational resources but also improved the overall performance of the segmentation task.

In this study, we systematically compared four attention mechanisms—Spatial Attention (Att), Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE), Coordinate Attention Gate (CAG), and Non-Local (NL)—for small-target segmentation tasks. Mechanistically, Att weights the contextual features at each pixel location to precisely focus on the edges and textures of tiny tumors, greatly enhancing high-resolution detail representation; SE recalibrates channel-wise feature responses, which strengthens overall semantic representation but lacks fine-grained spatial localization; NL captures long-range dependencies to restore structural coherence, yet its uniformly distributed weights tend to dilute focus on critical small-target pixels; and CAG injects height- and width-wise coordinate encodings into channel gating to combine positional and feature information, but its “separate-then-aggregate” strategy still coarsely modulates extremely small regions. Thus, the Att module achieves the best performance in capturing spatial details and delineating boundaries of small targets, whereas the other mechanisms, due to their differing emphases, exhibit limitations in fine-grained segmentation of very small structures.

The choice of loss function in image segmentation tasks is also crucial for model efficacy. Our model initially used Dice Loss directly, but this approach had shortcomings in handling small target segmentation. When facing scenarios containing only foreground and background, any slight prediction error in small targets can cause drastic fluctuations in the Dice coefficient, leading to severe gradient changes and negatively impacting training stability. To address this, we adopted a strategy combining multiple loss functions. We introduced a combined loss function, CLoss, which integrates Dice Loss with binary cross-entropy loss. The rationale was to utilize the more stable gradient characteristics of binary cross-entropy loss to counter the potential instability brought by Dice Loss. In this new loss function system, we set the weight of binary cross-entropy loss to 0.9 and Dice Loss to 0.1. Through this approach, we took advantage of the stability of cross-entropy loss while enhancing sensitivity to small targets with Dice Loss. Meanwhile, the experimental results indicate that not only does the Baseline model exhibit a marked performance improvement, but even when combined with the attention mechanism (Baseline + Att + Closs), the same improvement trend persists. This demonstrates the strong generalizability of Closs, which is especially important for clinical applications that require precise detection of small lesion targets.

However, D-S-Net still has certain limitations. The most significant issue lies in its potentially limited generalizability, which stems from the fact that the training dataset is sourced from a single medical center. Consequently, D-S-Net may not maintain the same level of accuracy when applied to data from other centers or to different types of medical images [45]. In addition, even after automatic segmentation, it is essential that the contours generated by D-S-Net be verified by qualified clinicians prior to use in therapy planning, ensuring patient safety and bridging the gap between research findings and clinical applications [46]. The current model is also primarily designed for 2D image segmentation tasks. For more complex 3D segmentation, particularly involving small targets, further optimization and modification of D-S-Net are needed. Moreover, as shown in Table 3, D-S-Net demonstrates a favorable balance between computational efficiency and model performance, which is expected to become even more crucial when dealing with larger 3D or 4D datasets [47]. In future research, we plan to incorporate multi-center data and employ 4D imaging or other respiration-correlated imaging techniques to more comprehensively capture tumor motion during the respiratory cycle, thereby improving the accuracy of small-target segmentation and enhancing its clinical utility.

Conclusion

In this study, we introduced D-S-Net—a customized and enhanced U-Net model specifically designed for medical image segmentation. The core of this model lies in its integration of attention mechanisms and a two-stage process for image handling: initial target detection followed by image segmentation. The aim of this approach was to achieve notable improvements in accuracy and efficiency in medical image segmentation. In experiments on two datasets, D-S-Net demonstrated outstanding performance, particularly in precisely delineating lesion areas, notably outperforming existing segmentation models. These results clearly indicate that D-S-Net holds broad application potential and significant clinical value in complex medical image analysis, especially in the precise identification and segmentation of lesion areas.
