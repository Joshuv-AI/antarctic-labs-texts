Integrin αVβ3 mediates porcine deltacoronavirus infection and inflammatory response through activation of the FAK-PI3K-AKT-nf-κB signalling pathway
Authors: Xiaohui Jin, Xingyi Wu, Zehui Li, Yixin Hu, Lu Xia, Shaopo Zu, Gaiping Zhang, Hui Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPorcine deltacoronavirus (PDCoV) is an emerging porcine enteropathogenic coronavirus that causes acute watery diarrhoea in piglets, resulting in significant economic losses to the global swine industry. However, the underlying mechanism of PDCoV infection is not well defined, which seriously hinders the development of effective drugs and vaccines. Integrins (ITG) are heterodimeric transmembrane glycoproteins that play important roles in the life cycle of many viruses. In the current study, the viral entry pathways of PDCoV were explored and the role of ITGαVβ3 was investigated during PDCoV infection. Our results showed that the lysosomal acidification inhibitor bafilomycin-A1 (Baf-A1) significantly reduced PDCoV infection, while exogenous protease facilitated PDCoV infection and even allowed PDCoV entry to bypass the endosomal pathway, suggesting PDCoV entry into cells via the endocytic pathway and the exogenous protease-mediated pathway simultaneously. Furthermore, ITGαVβ3 was identified to be involved in PDCoV infection, especially during viral entry stages. PDCoV infection triggers the activation of the focal adhesion kinase (FAK)-phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-serine/threonine-specific protein kinase (AKT) signalling pathway, and this activation is ITGαVβ3-dependent, suggesting that the activation of the FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway during PDCoV infection is mediated by ITGαVβ3. Our results further demonstrated that PDCoV infection induced the expression of inflammatory cytokines, which was mediated by activation of the ITGαVβ3-FAK-PI3K-AKT-nuclear transcription factor-κB (NF-κB) signalling pathway. Overall, the results revealed that ITGαVβ3 is an essential host factor for PDCoV infection and can serve as a supplementary receptor to facilitate PDCoV infection, which can help us to explore the molecular mechanism of PDCoV infection.

Introduction

Coronaviruses (CoVs) are a large group of RNA viruses that cause respiratory and enteric diseases in humans and animals. To date, at least three CoVs have caused widespread deadly outbreaks, including the highly pathogenic severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV) in 2003, the Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV) in 2012, and the ongoing SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, posing a serious threat to public health worldwide [1,2]. In addition to epidemics in humans, CoVs have also been detected in swine herds. Porcine deltacoronavirus (PDCoV) is a viral pathogen that causes acute watery diarrhoea, dehydration, and vomiting in piglets, with clinical signs similar to those of porcine epidemic diarrhoea virus (PEDV) and transmissible gastroenteritis virus (TGEV) infections [3]. Since 2012, PDCoV was first reported in Hong Kong, China, and has subsequently been reported in many countries, such as the United States, Canada, South Korea, and Vietnam. PDCoV was first isolated in swine testicular (ST) and LLC porcine kidney (LLC-PK) cells by adding exogenous proteases (trypsin or pancreatin) [4]. Although exogenous proteases have been used for PDCoV isolation and propagation, their role in the viral lifecycle remains unclear.

Apart from infected pigs in the clinic, PDCoV broadly infects porcine, human, and chicken cells in vitro, and the potential cross-species transmission of PDCoV has been experimentally evaluated in calf, chicken, and mouse models [5–7]. A recent study showed that PDCoV was identified in plasma samples of three Haidi children with acute undifferentiated febrile disease, which has aroused extensive attention for the risk of PDCoV transmission among humans [8]. Cellular receptors recognized by viruses are important determinants of the viral host range and cross-species infection. Identification of cellular functional receptors used for PDCoV entry into host cells is critical for understanding the pathogenesis and development of antiviral targets. Several studies have shown that porcine aminopeptidase N (pAPN) facilitates PDCoV entry into non-susceptible cells and promotes viral replication [9–11], but knocking out the pAPN gene from susceptible cells or pigs only slightly decreased PDCoV infection in vitro and in vivo [12,13], indicating that pAPN is not the only functional receptor, and there might exist other unidentified receptors, co-receptors, or supplementary receptors during PDCoV infection. Our previous study showed that sialic acid is a novel attachment receptor for PDCoV [14]. However, the key functional receptor of PDCoV is yet to be identified.

Integrins (ITG) are a large family of heterodimeric transmembrane glycoproteins consisting of non-covalent associations between α and β subunits. In mammals, there are 18 α and 8 β subunits that yield 24 distinct integrin dimers with different binding properties and tissue distributions [15]. ITG participates in numerous biological activities, including extravasation, cell-to-cell adhesion, cell migration, cytokine activation and release, and functions as a receptor for several viruses. As reported, ITGβ1 can mediate vaccinia virus entry into HeLa cells [16,17], the human adenovirus type 26 (HAdV26) uses ITGαVβ3 as receptor for infecting epithelial cells [18], and ITGαVβ5 serves as an internalization factor for Zika virus infection [19].

Recent studies have shown that ITG can also act as a receptor or co-factor for CoV entry. Gao et al. [20] found that both αV and β5 subtypes of ITG can inhibit SARS-CoV-2 entry by binding to the spike (S) protein and angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2). Proteomic analysis of PEDV-infected Vero E6 cells showed that the expression levels of ITGβ2 and ITGβ3 were up-regulated, and the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-serine/threonine-specific protein kinase (AKT) signalling pathway was activated after PEDV infection, suggesting that ITGβ2 and ITGβ3 might be potential factors for PEDV infection [21]. The ITGαV and ITGβ3 subunits are both involved in PEDV infection. The Arg – Gly – Asp peptides targeting ITGαVβ3 significantly inhibited PEDV replication, and co-expression of ITGαVβ3 with pAPN significantly enhanced PEDV replication [22]. Lv et al. [23] confirmed that the ITGα5β1 inhibitor ATN-161 significantly inhibited virus proliferation in PHEV-infected mice by interfering with the ITGα5β1-focal adhesion kinase (FAK) signalling pathway. Our previous study on the PDCoV–host interaction indicated that ITGαVβ3 was involved in the infection of PDCoV using a virus overlay protein blot assay (VOPBA) and proteomics assays. We also found that ITGαVβ3 is broadly expressed in swine intestinal epithelial cells and that the target organ or cells of PDCoV infection are the swine intestine and intestinal epithelial cells. Therefore, we deduced that ITGαVβ3 May play an important role in PDCoV infection.

In the present study, the cell entry pathways of PDCoV were first investigated, and our data demonstrated that PDCoV uses an endosomal pathway and protease-mediated pathway for its cellular entry and infection. Next, we systematically investigated the role of porcine ITGαVβ3 in PDCoV infection. The results demonstrated that ITGαVβ3 can promote viral absorption, invasion, and release during PDCoV infection, and that PDCoV infection activates the ITGαVβ3-FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway. In addition, PDCoV infection subsequently activates the expression of inflammatory cytokines [interleukin 8 (IL-8), IL-10, and tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)] via the ITGαVβ3-FAK-PI3K-AKT-nuclear transcription factor-κB (NF-κB) signalling axis. These results have important implications for understanding the mechanism of PDCoV infection.

Materials and methods

Cells and virus

The swine testicle (ST), LLC-porcine kidney (LLC-PK), and porcine intestinal epithelial (IPEC-J2) cells were purchased from the Institute of China Veterinary Medicine Inspection. ST cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Boster, Wuhan, China) containing 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Invitrogen) and antibiotic-antimycotic solutions (100×, Gibco) in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. LLC-PK cells were cultured in Modified Eagle’s medium (MEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 5% FBS (Gibco, USA), 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution, 1% MEM non-essential amino acids (NEAA, Gibco), 1% HEPES (Gibco, USA) in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. IPEC-J2 were cultured in Modified Eagle’s Medium (MEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA), 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. ST-ITGαVKO and ST-ITGβ3KO cell lines constructed by the CRISPR/Cas9 system in our laboratory were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS and antibiotic-antimycotic solutions (100×; Gibco) in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. The PDCoV strain HNZK-02 (GenBank accession No. MF095123) was isolated and identified in our laboratory, and passage 30 was used in the current study [24]. The maintenance medium for PDCoV-infected ST cells was DMEM supplemented with antibiotic-antimycotic solutions (100×) and 25 μg/mL pancreatin (Gibco, USA). The maintenance medium for PDCoV infection of LLC-PK cells was MEM supplemented with 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution, 1% NEAA, 1% HEPES, and 5 μg/mL trypsin (Gibco, USA). The maintenance medium for PDCoV-infected IPEC-J2 cells was MEM supplemented with 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution and 4 μg/mL trypsin (Gibco, USA).

Cytotoxicity assays

The cytotoxicity of the lysosomal acidification inhibitor bafilomycin-A1 (Baf-A1) (Sigma, USA), ITGαVβ3 inhibitor (TargetMol, USA), PI3K inhibitor (LY294002, Sigma, USA), and AKT inhibitor (Sigma, USA) were evaluated in vitro using 3-(4, 5-dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)-2, 5-diphenyl-2-H-tetrazolium bromide (MTT, Solarbio, China) assays according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, ST, LLC-PK, and IPEC-J2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and grown to 100% confluence for 24 h. After washing three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the cells were exposed to varying concentrations of Baf-A1 inhibitor, ITGαVβ3 inhibitor, PI3K inhibitor, and AKT inhibitors in triplicate for 36 h. DMEM and MEM containing 0.5% DMSO were served as control groups. After removing the supernatant from each well and washing twice with PBS, the MTT solution was added to the cultures to a final concentration of 0.1 mg/mL and the cells were further incubated at 37 °C for another 4 h. The supernatant was discarded and 110 µL of formazan solution was added to each well. The plates were oscillated in a shaker at low speed for 10 min to fully dissolve the crystals. Finally, the absorbance (Abs) was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader (Gallop, MR-96T, China). Cytotoxicity was expressed according to the following formula: cytotoxicity (%) = [(Abs sample)−(Abs blank)]/[(Abs negative control)−(Abs blank)] ×100.

Viral titration

A 50% tissue culture infective dose (TCID50) assay was performed to assess infectious viral titres. Briefly, LLC-PK cells were seeded in 96-well plates and grown to 90% confluence for 24 h. Then, Viral samples were serially 10-fold diluted in MEM and added to the LLC-PK cells in eight replicates per dilution at 37 °C for 1 h. After washing with PBS, the 200 μL of maintenance medium was added to each well and cells were cultured continuously at 37°C in 5% CO2. The expected cytopathic effects (CPE) were observed daily for 3–5 days, and the TCID50 value was calculated using the Reed–Muench method.

Viral infected assays

For the inhibitory effects of Baf-A1, ITGαVβ3, PI3K, and AKT inhibitor assays, ST, LLC-PK, and IPEC-J2 cells were exposed to different concentrations of Baf-A1 and ITGαVβ3 inhibitors or 5 ‰ DMSO at 37 °C for 2 h, then infected with PDCoV in ST cells (MOI = 1), LLC-PK cells (MOI = 0.01), and IPEC-J2 cells (MOI = 1) at 4 °C for 1 h, respectively. Subsequently, the cells were washed three times with PBS, fresh maintenance media containing appropriate concentrations of inhibitors were added, and the cells were further cultured. Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), TCID50, immunofluorescence assay (IFA), and plaque assays were used to analyse the inhibitory effects of the inhibitors on PDCoV infection.

For viral binding assays, ITGαVβ3 inhibitor was used for pretreatment of ST and LLC-PK cells at 37 °C for 2 h. After washed three times with PBS, cells were treated with ITGαVβ3 inhibitor concurrently with PDCoV (MOI = 60) at 4 °C for 1 h. As a control, cells were infected with the same amount of PDCoV without inhibitor treatment. After washing three times with cold PBS, the cells were harvested for qRT-PCR and western blotting.

For viral internalization assays, ST and LLC-PK cells were seeded in 12-well plates, and when they reached 90% confluence, cells were infected with PDCoV in ST cells (MOI = 1) and LLC-PK cells (MOI = 0.01), respectively. Cells were incubated at 4 °C for 1 h to allow virus binding. After washing three times with PBS, fresh maintenance media mixed with ITGαVβ3 inhibitor were subsequently added to the cells, followed by a shift to 37 °C to facilitate virus entry. At 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 36 h after shifting to 37 °C, the cells were harvested for qRT-PCR, western blotting, and TCID50 analyses.

For viral entry assays, ST and LLC-PK cells were infected with PDCoV (MOI = 60) at 4 °C for 1 h to allow virus binding. Unbound viruses were removed by washing with cold PBS. Subsequently, fresh maintenance media mixed with ITGαVβ3 inhibitor were added to the cells at 37 °C to facilitate virus entry for 1 h. As a control, another set of cells was infected with the same amount of PDCoV without inhibitor treatment. After removing the uninternalized viral particles on the cell surface by washing with acidic PBS-HCl (pH 4.0), the cells were harvested for qRT-PCR and western blotting analysis.

For viral release assays, ST cells were infected with PDCoV (MOI = 1) at 4 °C for 1 h to allow for virus binding. After washing three times with cold PBS, fresh maintenance medium were subsequently added to the cells, followed by a shift at 37 °C for 12 h. ST cells were then incubated with medium containing 1‰ DMSO or ITGαVβ3 inhibitor for 2, 4, 6, and 8 h, respectively. The supernatants and cells were collected separately, and viral titres were determined by qRT-PCR and TCID50 assays.

Immunofluorescence assay

ST, LLC-PK, and IPEC-J2 cells were seeded onto coverslips and grown at 37 °C with 5% CO2. At 6 and 12 h post PDCoV infection, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. After three times of washing, cells were then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min and blocked with PBS containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at 37 °C. Cells were then incubated overnight with mouse monoclonal antibody against PDCoV N protein (prepared in our laboratory using standard methods, 1:100 dilution) or mouse monoclonal antibody against integrin αVβ3 protein (1:100 dilution, Sigma) in PBS containing 1% BSA at 4 °C. The cells were then washed and incubated with Fluor-labelled polyclonal goat anti-mouse IgG antibody (1:100 dilution, CST) or AlexaFluor 594–conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG antibody (1:400 dilution, CST) for 1 h at 37 °C. After five washes with PBST, the cells were further counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen) for an additional 5 min at room temperature. After washing five times with PBST, the coverslips were mounted onto microscope slides and observed under a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany) or confocal microscope (Olympus Fluoview FV3000, Germany).

Total cellular RNA was extracted from ST, LLC-PK or IPEC-J2 cells using TRIzol™ reagent (Invitrogen). Cytoplasmic and nuclear protein extraction

Briefly, 6-well plates containing ST cells were infected with PDCoV (multiplicity of infection [MOI] = 0.01). Twelve hours post-infection (hpi), cells were collected with a cell scraper and washed twice with cold PBS. The cytoplasmic and nuclear protein extracts from cells were prepared using a nuclear and cytoplasmic extraction kit (Boster, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The protein concentration was measured using Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Boster, China) and was stored at −80 °C until use.

Western blotting analysis

Cells were lysed on ice using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Boster, China) containing 1 mm PMSF for 15 min. A specific monoclonal antibody against PDCoV N (prepared in our laboratory using standard methods, diluted 1:1000) was used to detect PDCoV N protein expression. Other primary antibodies were anti-ITGαV antibody (Proteintech, China, diluted 1:500), anti-ITGβ3 antibody (prepared in our laboratory using standard methods, diluted 1:1000), anti-phosphorylation-FAK (p-FAK-Tyr397), anti-FAK, anti-p-Akt-S473, anti-AKT, anti- p-p65-S536 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, USA, diluted 1:1000). Anti-TBP and anti-β-actin antibodies (Proteintech, China, diluted 1:1000) were used to confirm equal loading of the protein samples. The proteins were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Boster, China). Images were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health) for quantitative image analysis.

The siNC sequence was used as the control siRNA (Table 2). ST and LLC-PK cells grown to 60% confluence in 12-well cell culture plates were either control transfected or transfected with siITGav and siITGβ3 using Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After incubation at room temperature for 5 min, siRNA or siNC and Lipofectamine were mixed and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The cell culture medium was removed, replaced with 300 μL of OPTI-MEM containing the transfection complex, and further cultured at 37 °C. Determination of PDCoV titers by plaque assay

LLC-PK cells were grown as a monolayer in six-well tissue culture plates, washed three times with D-Hanks, then serum-free MEM was replaced and cultured at 37 °C for 2 h. After washing three times, the cells were infected for 1 h with 10-fold serial dilutions of PDCoV-containing samples (400 μL/well). After washing three times with PBS, the cells were covered with overlay medium [2× MEM containing 2% (w/v) agarose, 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution, 1% NEAA, 1% HEPES, and 5 μg/mL trypsin, 2 mL each well], and incubated at 37 °C for 2–3 days. Then the plates were stained with 0.01% neutral red (Sigma) for 2 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The average number of plaques and standard deviation were calculated from three independent experiments. Viral titres are expressed as plaque-forming units (pfu)/mL.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent replicates. Data were analysed using SPSS version 17.0 software for Windows (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). p values were calculated using Student’s t-test. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01.

Results

PDCoV uses endosomal pathway to entry into ST and LLC-PK cells

The permissive cell lines ST, LLC-PK, and IPEC-J2 are susceptible to PDCoV infection, but the viral entry pathway in these cells remains poorly understood. The endocytic pathway is thought to be pH-dependent. Therefore, to determine whether PDCoV can enter cells via endocytosis, the lysosomal acidification inhibitor Baf-A1 was used. First, the cytotoxic effects of Baf-A1 inhibitor at different concentrations (5, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400, and 600 nM) were determined in ST (Figure S1a), LLC-PK (Figure S1b) and IPEC-J2 (Figure S1c) cells using the MTT assay. The results indicated that the Baf-A1 inhibitor exhibited cellular toxicity following an increase in the drug concentration over 100 nM in ST and LLC-PK cells and over 50 nM in IPEC-J2 cells. Based on these results, 5, 25, 50, and 100 nM were selected as non-toxic Baf-A1 concentrations for subsequent experiments.

We next tested the interfernce ability of Baf-A1 inhibitor at concentrations of 5, 25, 50, or 100 nM for PDCoV infection in ST, LLC-PK, and IPEC cells. Briefly, ST, LLC-PK, and IPEC-J2 cells were exposed to different concentrations (5, 25, 50, or 100 nM) of Baf-A1 inhibitor at 37 °C for 2 h and then incubated with PDCoV (MOI = 1 in ST and IPEC-J2 cells, and MOI = 0.01 in LLC-PK cells, respectively) at 4 °C for 1 h. Subsequently cells were shifted to 37 °C with or without the appropriate concentrations of Baf-A1 inhibitor. qRT-PCR, TCID50 and IFA assays were performed after the cells were infected with PDCoV at 6, 12, 16, or 24 h. The results showed that the Baf-A1 inhibitor could inhibit virus replication at 6 hpi in IPEC-J2 cells, and at 12, 16, or 24 hpi in ST and LLC-PK cells. PDCoV viral RNA titres in the Baf-A1 inhibitor treatment groups at 25, 50, and 100 nM were significantly inhibited in ST (Figure 1a) and LLC-PK (Figure 1c) cells at 12, 16, and 24 hpi in a dose-dependent manner. The TCID50 assay showed similar results, the virus titres of the Baf-A1 inhibitor treatment groups at 25, 50, and 100 nM were significantly inhibited in ST (Figure 1b) and LLC-PK (Figure 1d) cells at 12 hpi, and 5 nM Baf-A1 inhibitor also inhibited the virus titre in LLC-PK cells (p < 0.01). The IFA (Figure 1e) results showed that the number of PDCoV-positive cells decreased with the treatment of Baf-A1 inhibitor in a dose-dependent manner. A similar inhibitory effect on IPEC-J2 cells is shown in Supplementary Figure S2. These data indicated that the Baf-A1 inhibitor treatment efficiently blocked virus replication in a dose-dependent manner, 5 nM of Baf-A1 inhibitor slightly inhibited the replication of PDCoV, while 25, 50, and 100 nM significantly inhibited the replication of PDCoV in ST and LLC-PK cells (p < 0.05), suggesting that PDCoV entry into cells via a pH-dependent endocytic pathway. Trypsin significantly promotes PDCoV replication in ST and LLC-PK cells

Exogenous supplementation of trypsin and pancreatin could facilitate PDCoV replication in LLC-PK, IPEC-J2, and ST cells [25]. Herein, to determine whether PDCoV can enter host cells directly from cell surface via fusion of the virus envelope and the cell membrane, ST, LLC-PK, and IPEC-J2 cells were pre-treated with 100 or 50 nM Baf-A1 inhibitor for 2 h at 37 °C to inhibit the endosomal pathway, and then infected with PDCoV at MOI of 1 for ST and IPEC-J2 cells, and MOI of 0.01 for LLC-PK cells. After incubation for 1 h at 4 °C, the maintenance media containing Baf-A1 inhibitor and pancreatin/trypsin were added for further culture; meanwhile, the inhibitor untreated and treated groups were set as controls without pancreatin/trypsin. The effects of pancreatin/trypsin and Baf-A1 inhibitor on the entry and replication of PDCoV were analysed by qRT-PCR, TCID50 and IFA at 6 and 12 hpi.

PDCoV viral titres in the pancreatin/trypsin-treated groups were increased in ST and LLC-PK cells at 6 and 12 h post-infection. Meanwhile, the viral titres of Baf-A1 inhibitor treatment groups were significantly inhibited as shown in Figure 1c,d (Figure 2a–f). The results of IFA (Figure 2g,h) showed that exogenous pancreatin/trypsin could promote PDCoV infection, whereas treatment with Baf-A1 inhibitor decreased the number of PDCoV-positive cells. However, the addition of pancreatin/trypsin reversed the decrease in PDCoV infection induced by Baf-A1 inhibitor in ST and LLC-PK cells. A similar inhibitory effect on IPEC-J2 cells is shown in Supplementary Figure S3. These results strongly suggest that pancreatin/trypsin significantly promotes PDCoV entry and replication in ST, LLC-PK, and IPEC-J2 cells, and pancreatin/trypsin could reverse the Baf-A1 inhibitory effect of the inhibitors and allow PDCoV entry to bypass the endosomal pathway. ITGαVβ3 could affect the infection of PDCoV

Regardless of the way PDCoV enters host cells, it requires the participation of receptors or co-factors on the cell surface. In our previous studies on the interaction between PDCoV and host cells using a virus overlay protein blot assay (VOPBA) and proteomics assay, we found that ITGαVβ3 was involved in PDCoV infection (data not shown). Therefore, we investigated the function of the cellular membrane protein ITGαVβ3 during PDCoV infection. Briefly, ST and LLC-PK cells were infected with PDCoV at MOI of 1 and 0.01, and the expression levels of ITGαV and ITGβ3 in ST, LLC-PK, and IPEC-J2 cells were investigated by western blotting. Consistent with the quantitative proteomics results, there was an increase in the protein expression levels of ITGαV and ITGβ3 in ST (Figure 3a), LLC-PK (Figure 3b), and IPEC-J2 (Figure S4b) cells with PDCoV infection when compared with the levels in uninfected cells, suggesting that PDCoV infection upregulates ITGαV and ITGβ3 endogenous expression in host cells. In addition, confocal microscopy revealed that PDCoV and ITGαVβ3 were located together in ST and LLC-PK cells (Figure 3c). These results confirmed that the expression of ITGαVβ3 was stimulated by PDCoV infection, and that there may be an interaction between PDCoV and host ITGαVβ3 proteins during viral infection. ITGαVβ3 involved in the infection of PDCoV.

Next, we further investigated the role of ITGαVβ3 during PDCoV infection by downregulating ITGαVβ3 with its specific inhibitor. In this study, we selected three susceptible cell lines (ST, LLC-PK, and IPEC-J2) to evaluate the effects of ITGαVβ3 specific inhibitor on PDCoV replication. The concentration of the ITGαVβ3 inhibitor at 5 μM showed no significant cytotoxicity to ST (Figure S1d), LLC-PK cells (Figure S1e) and IPEC-J2 cells (Figure S1f). No obvious inhibitory effects were observed on PDCoV replication when 0.5 μM ITGαVβ3 inhibitor was added to ST cells. While ITGαVβ3 inhibitor at the concentrations of 1 and 5 μM obviously suppressed PDCoV replication in ST and LLC-PK cells, and the inhibitory effect was in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 3d,e). As expected, plaque assay and IFA also showed that treatment with 5 μM ITGαVβ3 inhibitor reduced PDCoV infection (Figure 3f,g). A similar inhibitory effect was also found in IPEC-J2 cells, as shown in Supplementary Figure S4. These results indicated that blocking cellular ITGαVβ3 significantly decreased the replication of PDCoV in ST, LLC-PK, and IPEC-J2 cells.

ITGαVβ3 knockdown and knockout decrease PDCoV infection

To further examine the potential role of ITGαVβ3 in PDCoV infection, siRNA-mediated ITGαV and ITGβ3 gene knockdown experiments were performed in ST and LLC-PK cells, respectively. Two pairs of siRNAs were designed for the ITGαV (siITGαV-1 and siITGαV-2) and ITGβ3 (siITGβ3-1 and siITGβ3-2) genes. From the siRNA-mediated silencing results, we observed that siITGαV-1, siITGαV-2, siITGβ3-1, and siITGβ3-2 significantly knocked down the total amounts of ITGαV and ITGβ3 in ST and LLC-PK cells and that siITGαV-2 and siITGβ3-2 had the best inhibitory effects. siITGαV-2 and siITGβ3-2 were used for subsequent experiments (Figure S5).

Next, ST cells were transfected with the siRNAs for 24 h and then infected with PDCoV at an MOI of 1 and an MOI of 0.01 on LLC-PK cells. After 1 h of PDCoV infection, the cells were incubated with the maintenance medium until 24 hpi. The cells were harvested to detect the PDCoV S gene by qRT-PCR, and the supernatants were collected for the TCID50 assay. The results showed that the PDCoV viral RNA titres in the ITGαVβ3 knockdown groups were significantly lower than those in the negative control groups in the ST and LLC-PK cells (p < 0.05) (Figure 3h,i). Knockdown of ITGαV, ITGβ3, and ITGαV+ITGβ3 was also sufficient to reduce viral production in ST and LLC-PK cells (Figure 3j,k). These results confirmed that specific inhibition of ITGαVβ3 synthesis affects PDCoV replication and leads to a reduction in viral titres, suggesting that cellular ITGαVβ3 is involved in PDCoV replication.

The siRNA could not completely inhibit the expression of ITGαVβ3 gene, in order to effectively block ITGαV and ITGβ3 genes expression, ITGαV and ITGβ3 knockout cell lines (ST-ITGαVKO and ST-ITGβ3KO) were constructed by CRISPR/Cas9 system and used to functionally assess the roles of endogenous ITGαV and ITGβ3 during PDCoV infection. Briefly, ST, ST-ITGαVKO and ST-ITGβ3KO cells were inoculated with PDCoV (MOI = 1) at 4 °C for 1 h, then cells were washed with PBS and cultured in maintenance medium at 37°C. The cultures were harvested at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 hpi, and virus titres were detected by qRT-PCR, plaque assay, or TCID50. The results showed that the viral titres in the ST-ITGαVKO and ST-ITGβ3KO cell lines were significantly decreased at 1, 3, 12, and 24 hpi compared to the viral titres in ST cells (p < 0.05) (Figure 3l,m). We also analysed the adsorptive capacity of PDCoV on ST-ITGαVKO and ST-ITGβ3KO cells using plaque assays. Consistently, PDCoV produced fewer virus plaques in ST-ITGαVKO and ST-ITGβ3KO cells than in ST cells (Figure 3n), implying that ITGαVβ3 is possibly involved in PDCoV adsorption. Taken together, these results demonstrate that cell surface presentation of ITGαVβ3 is essential for PDCoV infection.

To further determine which steps of the PDCoV life cycle are affected by ITGαVβ3, we conducted ITGαVβ3 inhibitor treatment assays at different stages (binding, entry, internalization, and release) of virus infection. The schematic of a timeline pattern was used to distinguish the different phases in Figure 4a. ST and LLC-PK cells were incubated with PDCoV (MOI = 60) at 4 °C for 1 h, with or without ITGαVβ3 inhibitor (5 μM). PDCoV mRNA and protein levels after ITGαVβ3 inhibitor treatment were evaluated using qRT-PCR and western blotting assays. From the virus-binding assays (Figure 4b,c), we can see that the expression of viral mRNA and protein levels of PDCoV in ST and LLC-PK cells treated with the ITGαVβ3 inhibitor was significantly inhibited when compared with the PDCoV-only infection group (p < 0.01). ITGαVβ3 is involved in the adsorption, entry and release of PDCoV infection.

For viral internalization assays, the viral RNA titres in cell mixture and intracellular, viral titres in cell mixture, and the expression levels of PDCoV N protein in cells were examined at 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 36 h after PDCoV binding to ST and LLC-PK cells using qRT-PCR, TCID50 and western blotting assays. The qRT-PCR results showed that the cell mixture and intracellular PDCoV RNA titre in the ITGαVβ3 inhibitor-treated group were significantly inhibited when compared to the virus-only groups in ST (Figure 4d) and LLC-PK (Figure 4e) cells (p < 0.01). The virus titres obtained by TCID50 in the ITGαVβ3 inhibitor-treated group were markedly reduced at 12, 24, and 36 hpi when compared with the virus-only group, and over 1 and 4 log10 TCID50 decreased in the inhibitor-treated groups at 24 and 36 hpi in ST cells, and over 3 and 2 log10 TCID50 decreased in LLC-PK cells (Figure 4f). Western blotting analysis showed that PDCoV N protein expression was reduced at 1, 3, 12, and 36 hpi in ITGαVβ3 inhibitor-treated ST cells (Figure 4g), and was reduced at 1 and 12 hpi in LLC-PK cells treated with the inhibitor (Figure 4h). These data suggest that ITGαVβ3 inhibitors can block the internalization of PDCoV.

Next, we identified the role of ITGαVβ3 during the entry phase of PDCoV infection. ST and LLC-PK cells were first incubated with PDCoV at 4 °C for 1 h and then shifted to 37 °C for 1 h to allow viral entry with or without the ITGαVβ3 inhibitor. Compared with the virus only group, the copy number of viral mRNA decreased about 10-fold (Figure 4i), and the expression of PDCoV N protein was reduced by approximately 40% after ITGαVβ3 inhibitor treatment (Figure 4j). These results indicate that ITGαVβ3 inhibitors can block PDCoV entry.

For the viral release assays, ST cells were infected with PDCoV (MOI = 1) at 37 °C for 12 h, and then cells were incubated with medium containing ITGαVβ3 inhibitor (5 μM) for 2, 4, 6, and 8 h, respectively. The supernatants and cells were collected separately, and viral titres were determined by qRT-PCR and TCID50 assays. The results showed that there was also a significant inhibition of viral release when the ITGαVβ3 inhibitor was added to the PDCoV post-infection phase. The mRNA levels of PDCoV in the supernatants of ST cells treated with inhibitors for 2, 4, and 8 h were significantly lower than those in the PDCoV-only infection group (p < 0.05) (Figure 4k). TCID50 results showed that the virus titres in cell supernatants after inhibitor treatment for 8 h were significantly reduced when compared with the virus-only group, and there was over 2 log10 TCID50 decrease in the inhibitor-treated groups. There was no significant difference intracellular virus titres between the ITGαVβ3 inhibitor-treated and non-treated groups (Figure 4l). Together, these data provide evidence that ITGαVβ3 is involved in the early-entry and late-release processes of PDCoV infection.

PDCoV infection induced high expression of FAK, PI3K, and AKT protein in a dose-dependence manner

Viruses support efficient replication by activating intracellular signalling pathways. Integrin was previously shown to mediate multiple inside-out and outside-in signalling pathways and to play crucial roles in cell-to-cell and cell-to-matrix communications [26,27]. FAK-PI3K-AKT is the most common signalling pathway mediated by ITGαVβ3 during viral infections, and FAK, PI3K, and AKT are critical downstream regulators of integrin. Thus, whether PDCoV binding to ITGαVβ3 can activate the FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway and whether such kinase activation is critical for PDCoV infection were investigated in the following experiments.

First, the expression of FAK, PI3K, and AKT in ST cells during PDCoV infection was evaluated by qRT-PCR and western blotting, and the expression of phosphorylated FAK (p-FAK), p-AKT, and PDCoV N protein was detected by western blotting. As expected, PDCoV infection increased the mRNA expression of FAK, PI3K, and AKT in the ST cells (Figure 5a). Western blotting showed that the PDCoV N protein was detectable in the cell lysate from 3 hpi, with the level increasing from 6 hpi and peaking at 12 hpi (Figure 5b). PDCoV infection induced the phosphorylation of FAK and AKT, which were significantly activated at 1 hpi and then gradually increased, reaching a peak at 3 hpi (p < 0.01). In addition, the p-AKT protein level increased at 1 and 12 hpi. These results show that the levels of p-FAK and p-AKT proteins increased after PDCoV infection in a time-dependent manner.

The expression of FAK, PI3K, and AKT was further evaluated by qRT-PCR and western blotting after PDCoV infection with different MOIs (0.1, 1, and 10). As shown in Figure 5c, with increasing virus dosage, the upregulation of FAK, PI3K, and AKT mRNA expression markedly increased (p < 0.05). The same pattern of p-FAK and p-AKT upregulation by PDCoV infection at different MOIs was confirmed by western blotting, which showed that the phosphorylation levels of FAK and AKT proteins gradually increased with increasing MOI of PDCoV infection (Figure 5d). These data indicate that p-FAK and p-AKT proteins were notably up-regulated after PDCoV infection in an MOI-dependent manner.

PDCoV infection activated the FAK-PI3K-AKT signaling pathway

AKT phosphorylation is the key kinase for activation of the FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway, while PI3K can activate multiple downstream adapter proteins. To determine whether the activation of AKT by PDCoV infection was PI3K-dependent, a PI3K inhibitor (LY294002) was used to block the PI3K/AKT pathway. First, the cytotoxicity of LY294002 was assessed in ST cells using the MTT assay. No cytotoxicity was observed in ST cells when the concentration of the PI3K inhibitor was lower than 25 μM after treatment for 36 h (Figure S1d). Then ST cells were exposed to the 25 μM concentration of PI3K inhibitor for 1 h at 37 °C, and then infected with PDCoV (MOI = 1) containing PI3K inhibitor (25 μM). After 1 h incubation at 37 °C, maintenance medium containing 25 μM PI3K inhibitor was subsequently added to the cells. p-AKT protein levels were measured at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 hpi. Western blotting showed that PDCoV infection promoted the expression of p-AKT, while PI3K inhibitor treatment markedly reduced p-AKT expression levels at 1, 3, 6, and 12 hpi (Figure 5e), demonstrating that the activation of AKT by PDCoV infection is PI3K-dependent and that PDCoV infection can activate the FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway.

To further explore whether activation of the FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway is related to PDCoV replication, the inhibitory effects of PI3K and AKT inhibitors on PDCoV replication were analysed. ST cells were pretreated with PI3K inhibitors at concentrations of 10 and 25 μM for 1 h at 37 °C, followed infection with PDCoV (MOI = 1) for 1 h at 4 °C. The cells were incubated in the maintenance medium containing PI3K inhibitor at 37 °C for 12 and 24 h, and the inhibitory effect of PI3K inhibitor on PDCoV infection was evaluated. The results showed that PDCoV RNA titres were decreased in the presence of the PI3K inhibitor at concentrations of 10 and 25 μM at 12 hpi and 24 hpi (Figure 5f). Compared with the mock-treated PDCoV-infected cells, the viral titres of PDCoV in the 10 μM or 25 μM PI3K inhibitor-treated groups were also decreased at 12 and 24 hpi (Figure 5g), respectively. The 25 μM PI3K inhibitor-treated group showed a higher inhibitory effect than the 10 μM group (p < 0.05). IFA also showed that treatment with a PI3K inhibitor reduced PDCoV infection (Figure 5h). All results indicated that PI3K inhibitor treatment resulted in a significant dose-dependent decrease in PDCoV infection.

The cytotoxicity assay of the AKT inhibitor indicated that AKT inhibitor at concentrations of no more than 100 nM showed no cytotoxicity in ST cells (Figure S1e). Therefore, ST cells were exposed to the 25, 50, and 100 nM concentrations of AKT inhibitor for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by infection with PDCoV (MOI = 1) containing AKT inhibitor for 1 h at 37 °C. Cells were further cultured in a maintenance medium containing AKT inhibitor at 37 °C for 6 and 12 h. The inhibitory effects of the AKT inhibitor on PDCoV infection were confirmed using qRT-PCR. The results showed that PDCoV RNA titres decreased after treatment with different concentrations of the AKT inhibitor at 6 and 12 hpi (Figure 5i). This indicated that AKT inhibitor treatment resulted in significant dose-dependent inhibition of PDCoV infection. These results indicated that PDCoV infection activated the FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway.

PDCoV infection activated FAK-PI3K-AKT signaling pathway through ITGαVβ3 interaction

The above experiment confirmed that PDCoV infection activated the FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway by upregulating the expression of FAK, PI3K, AKT, and the corresponding phosphorylation proteins. To explore whether ITGαVβ3 is involved in the activation of the FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway by interacting with PDCoV, the possible influence on FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway activation was assessed using an ITGαVβ3 inhibitor in PDCoV-infected ST cells. Briefly, ST cells were pre-treated with 5 nM ITGαVβ3 inhibitor for 1 h at 37 °C, and then infected with PDCoV (MOI = 1). Cells were harvested at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 hpi to detect FAK, PI3K, and AKT expression using qRT-PCR and western blotting. Our results showed that the ITGαVβ3 inhibitor reduced the mRNA expression levels of FAK, PI3K, and AKT compared to those in PDCoV-infected cells (Figure 5j). The FAK and PI3K mRNA levels in the ITGαVβ3 inhibitor group decreased 3.5- and 2.7-fold at 24 hpi, and the mRNA expression levels of AKT decreased by 1.3-, 2.1-, and 1.4-fold at 3, 12, and 24 hpi, respectively, compared to the PDCoV-infected group. Western blotting confirmed that the ITGαVβ3 inhibitor inhibited PDCoV replication and FAK and AKT phosphorylation (Figure 5k). Taken together, these results demonstrated that the activation of FAK-PI3K-AKT induced by PDCoV is related to ITGαVβ3.

PDCoV promotes its entry through ITGαVβ3-FAK-PI3K-AKT signaling pathway activation

It has been confirmed that integrin mediates porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV) [28] and vaccinia virus [17] entry through the activation of FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling. These results indicate that PDCoV infection can activate the FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway through ITGαVβ3. Therefore, we speculated that PDCoV may also require involvement of the ITGαVβ3-FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway during virus entry. Briefly, ST cells were infected with PDCoV (MOI = 60) for 15, 30, 45, 60, and 120 min, and the cells were collected for western blotting analysis. The results showed that PDCoV infection induced the phosphorylation of FAK and AKT, and p-FAK and p-AKT proteins were significantly induced 15 min after PDCoV infection (p < 0.01) (Figure 6a). To verify whether PDCoV requires the ITGαVβ3-FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway during cell entry, ST cells were pre-incubated with 25 μM PI3K inhibitor (Figure S1g), 100 nM AKT (Figure S1h) inhibitor, or 5 μM ITGαVβ3 inhibitor for 2 h, and then infected with PDCoV (MOI = 60) containing the corresponding inhibitors. Compared with the PDCoV control group, the PDCoV RNA titres in the ITGαVβ3, PI3K, and AKT inhibitor groups were obviously decreased after 15 min of PDCoV infection (Figure 6d). This indicates that ITGαVβ3, PI3K, and AKT inhibitors could inhibit the early entry of PDCoV. Taken together, these results suggest that PDCoV promotes its entry into the cell through activation of the ITGαVβ3-FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway.

PDCoV induces inflammation by activating the ITGαVβ3-FAK-PI3K-AKT-nf-κB signaling axis

It has been reported that integrin can activate the expression of inflammatory cytokines and that the nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) is required for this induction [29]. Our previous study showed that PDCoV infection activates the NF-κB signalling pathway and leads to the expression of inflammatory cytokines [24]. Therefore, we further investigated whether activation of the NF-κB signalling pathway to promote the expression of inflammatory cytokines during PDCoV infection is mediated by activation of the cell surface ITGαVβ3 mediating FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway. First, ST cells were, respectively, treated with ITGαVβ3, PI3K, and AKT inhibitors for 2 h at 37 °C, and then infected with PDCoV (MOI = 1) containing the corresponding inhibitors for 1 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, fresh maintenance medium containing the related inhibitors was added. The mRNA expression levels of IL-8, IL-10, and TNF-α were detected by qRT-PCR at 3, 6, 12, and 24 hpi. The results showed that the mRNA expression levels of IL-8, IL-10, and TNF-α were upregulated after PDCoV infection, consistent with previous laboratory results [24]. The addition of ITGαVβ3, AKT, and PI3K inhibitors decreased the mRNA expression of IL-8 (Figure 7a), IL-10 (Figure 7b), and TNF-α (Figure 7c) during the PDCoV infection. These results suggest that the inflammation induced by PDCoV infection is related to the ITGαVβ3-FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway. The effects of ITGαVβ3, AKT, and PI3K inhibitors on the NF-κB signalling pathway activated by PDCoV infection were detected. After PDCoV infection for 3, 6, 12, and 24 h, the mRNA levels of NF-κB in the ITGαVβ3, AKT, and PI3K inhibitor treated and untreated groups were detected by qRT-PCR. However, ITGαVβ3, PI3K, and AKT inhibitors inhibited the phosphorylation of p65 protein in the cytoplasm and nucleus, suggesting that these three inhibitors can inhibit the nucleation of p65 induced by PDCoV (Figure 7f). Discussion

The threat of emerging and re-emerging CoVs highlights the need for further research on the molecular mechanisms underlying CoV invasion. PDCoV has recently emerged as a swine enteropathogenic coronavirus that causes severe diarrhoea, vomiting, and dehydration in suckling piglets [4]. Growing evidence has shown that PDCoV can cross species transmission. It can be used the aminopeptidase N (APN) protein to infect cell lines derived from multiple species [6]. Our previous studies have verified that PDCoV has the potential to infect other animals (including cattle, mice, and chickens) via oral inoculation of cell-culture-adapted PDCoV [5,7]. Most importantly, reports of PDCoV infection in children have aroused extensive attention [8]. Currently, the mechanism of PDCoV infection remains largely unclear, and effective strategies to prevent or treat PDCoV infection are still unavailable. In this study, we found that PDCoV uses two pathways (endocytosis and exogenous proteases) for its entry. Our study also confirmed that ITGαVβ3 could promote early entry and late release-related processes of PDCoV. Most importantly, PDCoV promotes viral infection by activating the ITGαVβ3-FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway and induces inflammation by activating the ITGαVβ3-FAK-PI3K-AKT-NF-κB signalling pathway (Figure 8). This study provides insights into the infection mechanism and the development of appropriate anti-viral drugs for PDCoV. PDCoV promotes viral infection by activating ITGαVβ3-FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway, and induces inflammation by activating ITGαVβ3-FAK-PI3K-AKT-nf-κB signalling axis.

To understand the mechanism of PDCoV infection, the viral entry pathway is the first key point that needs to be studied. Herein, we found that the endocytic inhibitor Baf-A1 can inhibit PDCoV infection in ST and LLC-PK cells, suggesting that PDCoV could infect host cells through the endocrine pathway. These results were consistent with those reported by Zhang et al. [30]. In fact, the PDCoV isolation rate from clinical specimens is very low, and our previous studies suggest that the addition of exogenous protease or swine small intestinal contents is necessary for PDCoV isolation or propagation in vitro [25]. However, whether exogenous proteases play an important role in viral entry remains unclear. In this study, we found that PDCoV could also infect host cells without exogenous proteases, but the exogenous protease dramatically increased the infection efficiency and viral titre of PDCoV. Furthermore, the endocytic pathway partially, but not completely, inhibited PDCoV replication when the endocytic inhibitor Baf-A1 was added, which could be alleviated by adding exogenous protease. These results indicate that PDCoV can infect host cells through endocytosis and exogenous protease-mediated pathways. PDCoV is enteropathogenic and causes tissue damage in the jejunum and ileum. The intestinal contents of swine contain many digestive enzymes including trypsin and other proteases. These proteases may contribute to viral infection during the entry of PDCoV.

Viral entry factors are promising targets for antiviral therapy and for the development of pathogen-resistant animals. To date, the host cell molecules that can serve as receptors for PDCoV infection are APN and sialic acid [9–11,14]. Our laboratory identified sialic acid as a newly discovered attachment receptor for PDCoV invasion, which facilitated its infection [14]. Several studies have indicated that PDCoV uses pAPN as an entry receptor; however, pAPN-knockout cells or pigs are not resistant to PDCoV, suggesting that pAPN might not be a critical functional receptor [12,13], and there are other unidentified receptors or co-receptors for PDCoV infection.

ITG are transmembrane receptors that transduce biochemical and mechanical signals across the plasma membrane and promote cell adhesion and migration. Our previous study found that ITGαVβ3 was involved in PDCoV infection using VOPBA and proteomics assays. Therefore, the role of ITGαVβ3 in PDCoV infection was investigated in the present study. We demonstrated the role of cellular protein ITGαVβ3 in PDCoV infection using several experimental criteria. First, we showed that the mRNA and protein levels of ITGαVβ3 increased after PDCoV infection and that PDCoV could co-localize with ITGαVβ3 on the cell membrane. Second, knockdown and knockout of ITGαVβ3 in ST cells reduced PDCoV replication. Further research has shown that ITGαVβ3 is involved in the early entry and late release-related processes of PDCoV. However, similar to the studies of sialic acid and pAPN receptors on PDCoV infection, knockout of the ITGαV and ITGβ3 genes cannot completely block PDCoV infection, suggesting that ITGαVβ3 is not a functional receptor and may be one of the co-receptors for PDCoV infection. This phenomenon has also been observed in other viruses that can simultaneously bind to ITG and other protein receptors. The classical swine fever virus (CSFV) uses heparan sulphate and laminin as receptors [31,32]. Vaccinia virus binds to cell surface glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) [33] and the extracellular matrix protein laminin [34], and subsequently enters cells. However, the removal of ITG cannot completely block infections by these viruses. Thus, the binding of PDCoV to ITGαVβ3 May require other protein receptors during infection.

Inhibitors of integrin functions have been successfully tested as drugs to treat several pathological conditions. PSK1404, a nonpeptide antagonist of ITGαVβ3, inhibited osteoclast-mediated bone resorption in a cancer animal model of bone loss [35]. ATN-161, an ITGα5β1 inhibitor, reduced symptoms in PHEV-infected mice and inhibited the proliferation of PHEV in mice [23]. Although many viruses having been shown to utilize integrins to enter cells, we have not made a significant breakthrough in targeting integrins for the treatment of virus infections. It may be due to that viruses utilize multiple-receptor molecules to enter the same cell, and that the receptors used by the same virus to enter cells in vitro and in vivo may be different. To overcome this challenge, we may need to decipher the key elements in the motifs that interact with integrins on the virus, and conduct detailed comparative studies to outline the ways in which the virus enters cells under in vitro and in vivo conditions. This will be crucial for a comprehensive understanding of the receptors utilized by viruses to infect cells. We hope that these studies will get us one step closer to developing treatment strategies targeting integrins to combat viral infections.

ITG transduces both outside-in and inside-out signals through multiple pathways. Among the signalling events triggered by several ITG, p-FAK is a central kinase. Several important signalling intermediates, including Src family kinases, PI3K, AKT, MAP kinases, and Rho GTPases, can be activated in an FAK-dependent manner during ITG stimulation by extracellular signals [36]. Since FAK, PI3K, and AKT are critical regulators downstream of ITG [37], we tested whether PDCoV binding to ITGαVβ3 activates FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling and whether such kinase activation is critical for PDCoV infection. As expected, PDCoV infection induced FAK, PI3K, and AKT expression in a dose-dependent manner. PI3K and AKT inhibitor treatments resulted in a significant dose-dependent decrease in PDCoV infection, indicating that the activation of the FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway is necessary for PDCoV replication. ST cells were further treated with an ITGαVβ3 inhibitor, which could inhibit both the expression of three kinases (FAK, PI3K, and AKT) and the replication of PDCoV. These results demonstrated that ITGαVβ3 mediated the activation of the FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway for subsequent PDCoV infection. We found that the ITGαVβ3-FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway was activated after 15 min of PDCoV infection, and ITGαVβ3, PI3K, and AKT inhibitor treatments inhibited viral entry into the cell. Taken together, these results demonstrate that PDCoV-induced FAK-PI3K-AKT activation is mediated through ITGαVβ3, and that such activation is required for PDCoV entry and replication.

Zhu, et al. demonstrated that the SOX2-ITGαVβ5 axis marks glioblastoma stem cells that are preferentially targeted by ZIKV in association with the suppression of immune response genes and a molecular complex involved in viral internalization [38]. Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) binds to ITGα5β1 on nasal mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue memory B cells and initiates activation of the integrin-FAK-c-Src-PI3K-cofilin signal transduction pathway, leading to depolymerization of the actin cytoskeleton and thus to a more efficient nuclear translocation of the EBV capsid [39]. Our results also confirmed that PDCoV infection could activate the integrin-FAK-AKT signalling pathway to promote virus entry. Whether PDCoV further promotes the depolymerization of the actin cytoskeleton and thus its entry into cells, remains to be further investigated.

One of the most important functions of integrin-mediated cell adhesion is the production of inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1, IL-8, and TNF-α. All integrin-induced cytokines contain NF-κB-binding sites in their 5’-regulatory regions. Therefore, NF-κB activation is an important indicator of integrin-induced inflammatory response. Published reports have shown that PI3K mediates integrin-dependent NF-κB and MAPK activation through separate signalling pathways, and then induces an inflammatory response [40]. However, it has also been shown that integrin regulates TLR-induced inflammation by inhibiting activation of the NF-κB signalling pathway and promoting phosphorylation of p38 MAPK [41]. In virus-related studies, HSV infection promotes the recruitment of TLR2 and phosphorylation of IRAK4 by MYD88 through ITGαVβ3, which leads to the phosphorylation of AKT, activation of NF-κB, and upregulation of interferon and cytokine [42]. Our laboratory reported that high levels of inflammatory cytokines were induced after PDCoV infection in vitro and in vivo, and also confirmed that PDCoV promoted the expression of inflammatory cytokines by activating the NF-κB signalling pathway in vivo [24,43]. In this study, we found that ST cells treated with ITGαVβ3, PI3K, and AKT inhibitors inhibited the expression of inflammatory factors and the NF-κB signalling pathway, as well as inhibited the phosphorylation and nucleation of p65. These results indicate that PDCoV could upregulate the expression of inflammatory factors by activating the ITGαVβ3-FAK-PI3K-AKT-NF-κB signalling axis. These findings highlight that the branch of the innate response dependent on the ITGαVβ3 axis significantly contributes to the production of inflammatory cytokines in PDCoV-infected cells.

In summary, our study found that PDCoV can use the endosomal and protease-mediated pathways for its cellular entry and infection. Moreover, ITGαVβ3 plays a key role in the adsorption, entry, and release stages of PDCoV. PDCoV promotes viral infection by activating the ITGαVβ3-FAK-PI3K-AKT signalling pathway and induces inflammation by activating the ITGαVβ3-FAK-PI3K-AKT-NF-κB signalling axis. Our study provides an important theoretical basis for further studies on immune pathogenesis and for the search for drug treatment targets for PDCoV.
