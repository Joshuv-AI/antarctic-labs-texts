Bilateral Ballismus: An Unusual Clinical Sign With an Unexpected Origin
Authors: S Reichert, A Knoflach, I Wiedl, K Hoskin, C Ganser, M Wertli, T Cosma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe spectrum of neurologic symptoms caused by hypoglycaemia can be remarkably broad. Thus, hypoglycemia should be considered early in the diagnostic workup of neurologic symptoms, including those that may appear atypical. Prompt glucose administration can result in rapid symptom resolution, potentially preventing costly or unnecessary invasive testing.Keywords: Ballismus, hypoglycemia, insulinoma, Whipple's triad

Introduction

Ballismus is a rare hyperkinetic movement disorder characterized by involuntary, forceful and uncoordinated flinging movements, typically affecting one side of the body [1]. Many authors consider ballismus and chorea to be distinct manifestations of the same underlying pathophysiologic processes [1, 2]. Compared to chorea, ballistic movements involve the proximal limb and trunk muscles, are more violent and exhibit a wider amplitude. They may be accompanied by abnormal posturing or facial grimacing [1, 2]. Historically associated with damage in the subthalamic nucleus, more recent imaging studies have revealed that lesions in other brain structures—usually within the basal ganglia—are more frequent causes of hyperkinetic movement disorders [1]. Specifically, chorea and ballism have been associated with (vascular) lesions in the caudate nucleus, putamen, or thalamus [3].

While stroke is the most common cause of ballismus, any focal lesion involving the basal ganglia can produce this movement disorder. Such lesions may result from neoplasms, demyelinating plaques, vascular malformations, tuberculomas, or toxoplasmosis [1]. Further, a number of systemic diseases can present with hemiballismus or bilateral ballismus, including metabolic conditions (e.g., hyperglycemia and less commonly hypoglycemia, or hypocalcemia secondary to hypoparathyreoidism), neurodegenerative diseases (Huntington's disease), autoimmune diseases (lupus erythematosus, Sydenham's chorea, vasculitis), infections (HIV, Influenza A), toxin exposure, or head trauma [1, 2]. In predisposed individuals, symptoms can also be exacerbated by certain medications, such as dopamine agonists and antiseizure medication [1, 2]. The causes of ballism can also be differentiated into acute vs. subacute: acute causes of ballismus include vascular events such as stroke and metabolic disturbances like hyperglycemia, while subacute causes are more often related to neoplastic, degenerative, parainfectious/autoimmune, or functional etiologies [1].

We report a case highlighting one of the less common causes of ballismus.

Case Presentation

Case History/Examination

A 42‐year‐old male presented to the emergency department with acute psychomotor agitation. His wife noted that he had been confused the previous day but recovered quickly. On the day of presentation, he failed to return home from work and was found in a parking lot, confused and agitated, and unable to drive his car. She further reported that he had experienced increased appetite and a cumulative weight gain of 20 kg over the past 3 months. His past medical history was notable only for obesity.

On presentation, the obtained vital signs showed a blood pressure of 130/70 mmHg, a pulse rate of 88/min, an oxygen saturation of 96%, and a body temperature of 36.9°C. The patient answered questions inadequately and exhibited forceful bilateral involuntary arm movements and intermittent facial twitching. The remainder of the physical examination was unremarkable. Due to limited cooperation, a full neurological examination could not be completed. Muscle strength was full (5/5) and symmetrical in all extremities. Pupils were equal, round and reactive to light and accommodation. There were no signs of meningeal irritation.

Investigations, Diagnosis, and Treatment

Initial laboratory evaluation showed no systemic inflammation, normal liver and kidney values, and a hypoglycemia with a blood glucose level of 1.8 mmol/L.

Administration of 20% glucose led to a marked improvement of the bilateral arm movements and facial twitching, with persistence of anxiety and confusion. During the further evaluation, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis and cerebral magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were normal. Given the documented hypoglycemia, neuroglycopenic symptoms, and (partial) symptom resolution following glucose administration, we considered the Whipple's triad fulfilled and conducted further investigations to identify the cause of hypoglycemia. The findings of an inappropriately high serum insulin and C‐peptide levels were consistent with endogenous hyperinsulinism (Table 1). Normal cortisol levels and adequate increase following ACTH stimulation ruled out hypocortisolism as a potential differential diagnosis.

Laboratory work‐up.

To identify the source of excess insulin production, we performed a Ga‐DOTA‐exendin‐4 PET/CT scan, which revealed a 20 × 24 mm mass in the cranial part of the pancreatic head, consistent with an insulinoma (Figure 1). At this stage, the treatment of hypoglycemia consisted of symptomatic management with food such as fruit juices and bars, and glucose infusions. Subsequently, treatment with diazoxide was initiated. This led to stabilization of blood glucose levels and complete relief of the patient's symptoms.

The patient subsequently underwent surgical enucleation of the pancreatic mass. Histopathological examination showed a well‐differentiated pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor, staged as pT2, pN0 (0/2), G2, Pn0. The case was reviewed at our hospitals' interdisciplinary tumor board, where no additional therapy was deemed necessary. A surveillance plan was established, consisting of alternating annual MRI and PET/CT scans.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

This is a case of a 42‐year‐old male patient, presenting with psychomotor agitation and bilateral ballismus of the upper extremities, proved to have an insulinoma. Postoperatively, the patient's blood glucose levels remained within the normal range, and he remained asymptomatic without requiring further treatment at the 1‐year follow‐up.

Discussion

Insulinomas are rare endocrine tumors that cause hypoglycemia due to excessive secretion of insulin. They are typically small, benign, and solitary, with an incidence of up to four cases per million people annually [4]. Approximately 5%–10% of cases occur in association with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) syndrome. In contrast to sporadic insulinomas, MEN1‐associated insulinomas are more likely to present as multiple tumors and at a younger age [4].

The hallmark symptoms of insulinomas are related to hypoglycemia caused by unregulated insulin secretion. Hypoglycemic symptoms are typically divided into neuroglycopenic and autonomic (including adrenergic and cholinergic) symptoms. Neuroglycopenic symptoms are almost universally present in patients with insulinoma [5] and arise when the brain is deprived of glucose, its primary energy source. They most commonly include confusion, agitation, blurred vision, dizziness, and in severe cases, seizures and loss of consciousness [5]. Autonomic symptoms—including sweating, tachycardia, anxiety, and palpitations—also manifest in most patients and result from increased secretion of catecholamines in response to low blood glucose levels [6].

Hypoglycemia can present with focal neurological deficits—such as hemiparesis or aphasia—which may lead to misdiagnosis. In one retrospective study, 64% of patients with insulinoma were initially misdiagnosed with a neurological disorder such as stroke or epilepsy, 8% received a psychiatric diagnosis and 8% were suspected of alcohol intoxication due to the manifestations of the insulinoma [5]. Although less common, several case reports have described hyperkinetic movement disorders associated with hypoglycemia [7, 8, 9, 10]. While the pathophysiologic mechanism of this manifestation is unknown, multiple hypotheses have been advanced, including an imbalance in excitatory and inhibitory signaling within the basal ganglia [7], a dysbalance in blood flow to the basal ganglia and contralateral thalamus, cytotoxic edema leading to hypoxia [8], or ischemia caused by reduced nitric oxide levels and subsequent thrombocyte activation [9]. One hypothesis suggests sensitization during hyperglycemia leading to consumption of inhibitory neurotransmitters in patients with diabetes mellitus, followed by symptoms during a hypoglycemic episode [8, 9]. Given the lack of hyperglycemia, this specific mechanism does not apply to this case. Cerebral imaging findings in patients with hyperkinetic movement disorders due to hypoglycemia are variable. While most case reports describe lesions in different regions of the basal ganglia or the cortex on Computed Tomography or MRI [9, 10, 11], some patients present no abnormalities [8]. Apart from the adrenergic and neuroglycopenic symptoms of hypoglycemia, many patients with insulinoma also report increased hunger and significant weight gain [5]. Surgical enucleation is the preferred treatment for solitary insulinomas and usually leads to complete symptom resolution. Medical therapy with diazoxide—which increases blood glucose by inhibiting insulin release from the pancreatic β cells and by stimulating glycogenolysis [16]—can be used to control symptoms in patients who are not surgical candidates or those awaiting surgery [4].

In conclusion, blood glucose measurement should be part of the primary assessment in most patients with acute neurologic presentations. While hemiballismus is usually caused by stroke, bilateral ballismus is rarer and is often caused by systemic conditions. A comprehensive evaluation—including thorough clinical assessment, neuroimaging, and extensive laboratory testing—is typically required to assess ballismus. However, recognizing well‐documented metabolic causes may prevent unnecessary diagnostic procedures. This case underscores the importance of recognizing the wide range of symptoms associated with hypoglycemia.
