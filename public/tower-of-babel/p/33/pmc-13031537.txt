Investigating the effects of Fe, Ni, or Cu additions on the microstructure and mechanical properties of W₂CoB₂ cermets
Authors: Xingyu Zhu, Yingjun Pan, Deqing Ke, Fan Qu, Lian Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
W₂CoB₂ is a type of ternary boride-based cermet. Endowed with prominent advantages including high hardness, high melting point, excellent wear resistance and superior corrosion resistance, it has been extensively applied in various industrial fields such as cutting processing, surface protection and mold manufacturing. In this study, the toughening of W₂CoB₂ cermet was achieved by means of binder phase strengthening, thereby further expanding its application scope. Considering that the mechanical properties of metal ceramics are affected by the bonding performance between the hard phase and binder phase, this study primarily focused on the toughening of cermets. Accordingly, first-principles calculations were used to investigate the effects of doping with transition metals Fe, Ni, or Cu on the interfacial bonding performance of the hard/binder phase. The results showed that doping with Fe or Ni enhanced the interfacial bonding performance, while Cu doping reduced the bonding performance. To verify the calculation results, W2AB2/A metal ceramic samples were prepared by vacuum liquid-phase sintering. It was found that adding Fe resulted in the best indentation toughness, which is consistent with the calculated results; however, the microhardness decreased. The microhardness and indentation toughness of the samples without and with Fe, Ni, and Cu added were determined to be 1983.5 HV0.5 and 9.5 MPa·m1/2, 1806.5 HV0.5 and 12.4 MPa·m1/2, 2123.3 HV0.5 and 11.2 MPa·m1/2, 2006.2 HV0.5 and 10 MPa·m1/2, respectively, and the coefficient of friction(COF) were 0.63, 0.47, 0.41, and 0.51, respectively. The addition of Fe and Ni can form a solid solution in the binder phase, thereby enhancing the interfacial bonding performance between the hard and binder phases. This significantly improves the indentation toughness of W2CoB2. During the wear process, an oxide layer forms on the surface of the binder phase, which reduces the friction coefficient of the sample and enhances its wear resistance. This study demonstrates that the addition of an appropriate amount of Ni can effectively enhance the fracture toughness and wear resistance of W₂CoB₂, while simultaneously maintaining its high hardness.

Introduction

In recent years, boride-based ceramics, especially ternary boride ceramics, have attracted much attention due to their excellent high-temperature oxidation resistance, high hardness, perfect chemical stability, and high strength1–4. Consequently, ternary boride ceramics have been widely utilized in fields such as molds, cutting tools, industrial parts, and coatings5. Among these ternary boride ceramics, typical ones are Mo₂NiB₂, Mo₂FeB₂, and W₂CoB₂. Among these three types of ternary boride ceramics, W₂CoB₂ possesses extremely high hardness, excellent oxidation resistance, and high thermodynamic stability. However, due to its high brittleness, its expansion in industrial applications is limited.

Takagi6 successfully prepared ternary boride ceramics such as Mo₂FeB₂, Mo₂NiB₂, and W₂CoB₂ through in-situ synthesis. Among them, W₂CoB₂/Co exhibits the highest high-temperature resistance performance. Zhang7–9 studied the effects of in-situ synthesized inhibitors such as NbC, WC, and TaC, as well as self-lubricating materials such as WS₂, MoS₂, and h-BN on the microstructure and properties of Mo₂FeB₂ coatings and obtained Mo₂FeB₂/WC coatings with good wear resistance.

Recent advancements in ceramic toughening have predominantly centered on the incorporation of metal carbides, whereas investigations into enhancing the bonding phase through metallic additions remain relatively scarce. Huan Xu10 examined the influence of varying Co content on the microstructure and mechanical properties of TiCN-TiB2 ceramics. Their findings indicated that while Co addition had minimal impact on the phase composition of the sintered ceramics, it significantly influenced densification, microstructure evolution, and mechanical performance. Abdul Basit11 explored the effects of Fe-Ni as a bonding phase on NbC ceramics. Their research demonstrated that Fe-Ni effectively suppressed grain growth, with partial substitution of Ni by Fe in the bonding phase leading to enhanced bond strength, thereby contributing to improved overall mechanical properties of the ceramic material.

Zhang12 studied the in-situ synthesis of NbC in WCoB coatings and found that Nb can homogenize the microstructure of the cermet, thereby effectively enhancing its comprehensive properties. Usually, metal elements are added to materials in small amounts, but there are few reports on the addition of large amounts of metal elements in W₂CoB₂ metal ceramics. The interface structure plays a decisive role in material properties, and the solid solution of metal elements in the hard phase and bonding phase can enhance the bonding between the two phases13. In this study, the influence of doping different transition metals (Fe, Ni, or Cu) on the interfacial properties of W₂CoB₂/Co was investigated. Additionally, the stability of the interface was analyzed through first principles calculations of interface bonding energy, density of states (DOS), and charge difference density. Finally, the reliability of the calculation was verified through experiments. The results of this study have constructive significance for the theoretical calculation of W₂CoB₂/Co metal ceramic heterojunction structures. The heterojunction structure model is closer to the experimental results, providing theoretical guidance for W₂CoB₂/Co series metal ceramics.

The main objective of this study was to toughen W₂CoB₂ cermets through binder phase strengthening. Specifically, the effects of Fe, Ni, and Cu doping on the microstructure, microhardness, fracture toughness, and wear resistance of W₂CoB₂ cermets were systematically investigated by combining first-principles calculations. The ultimate goal of this research is to provide valuable theoretical references and practical technical guidance for the application of ternary boride cermets that are prone to fracture. Eventually, the prepared strengthened W₂CoB₂ cermets can be applied in mold manufacturing and cutting processing, thereby achieving improvements in their service life and service strength.

Materials and methods

Models and calculations

The interface model of W2AB2/A (in this experiment, A was composed of four binder phases, namely Co, Co/Fe, Co/Ni, or Co/Cu) comprises the hard phase W2AB2 and binder phase A. Generally speaking, β-Co has better strength and toughness than α-Co. Therefore, face-centered cubic β-Co was selected as the binder phase for interface bonding with the W2CoB2 hard phase. The interface bonding method is divided into a B-terminal hard phase and a doped atom terminal hard phase and a Co terminal bonding phase and a doped atom terminal bonding phase based on the types of interface terminal atoms. It is divided into top position, bridge position, and hollow position based on the relative positions of atoms. W₂CoB₂ possesses an orthorhombic crystal structure, and among all its low-index planes, the (001) plane exhibits the densest atomic arrangement and the lowest surface energy. Similarly, the (111) plane of A is a close-packed plane with the lowest surface energy. Therefore, a W2AB2 (011)/A (111) heterojunction model was constructed based on this surface. To ensure the stability and reliability of the interface model, the degree of mismatch should not exceed 5%. Where A1 is the surface area of the hard phase (W2AB2 (001)) and A2 is the surface area of the binder phase (A (111)).

According to the mismatch formula, W2AB2 (001) and A (111) are 5 × 1 and 4 × 1, respectively. Based on this, a W2AB2 (001)/A (111) interface model was constructed by selecting three layers from the top and bottom of a supercell. The binder phase is composed of Fe, Ni, or Cu doped with Co at the vertex and face-center positions via atomic substitution. The hard phase is doped with Fe, Ni, or Cu through the atomic substitution of Co at the vertex and body center positions of W2CoB2. After structural optimization, four interface models are obtained by constructing heterojunctions in the cross-section, including W2CoB2/Co, W2(Co, Fe)B2/(Co, Fe), W2(Co, Ni)B2/(Co, Ni), and W2(Co, Cu)B2/(Co, Cu).

To ensure computational accuracy, geometric optimization is performed again after heterojunction construction. To simplify the experiment, a lower-energy and more stable doping method is selected to construct the interface. Finally, the hard phase is selected as vertex-doped with Fe, Ni, or Cu, while the binder phase is face-centered and doped with Fe, Ni, or Cu. The interface is composed of the doped atomic terminal hard phase and the doped atomic terminal binder phase, with the bonding method at the top position. The final heterojunction models selected for construction are shown in Fig. 1. The doping concentration of the model corresponds to the experimental ratio of Co to Fe, Ni, and Cu of 1:1.

All calculations were performed using the CASTEP software package based on density functional theory (DFT) developed by Cambridge University’s Materials Studio15,16. The exchange-correlation functional used in the calculation adopted the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)17. The numerical calculations were supported by the High-Performance Computing Center of Wuhan University of Science and Technology.

Density of states

As the electronic properties of materials are mainly related to changes in the electronic density of states (DOS) near the Fermi level and the influence of low-energy electronic states is insignificant, this study mainly focuses on the DOS near the Fermi level21. The partial density of states (PDOS) of each group of interfaces after bonding is shown in Fig. 3, where the dashed lines indicate the Fermi level.

As shown in Fig. 3, each partial density of states (PDOS) group is continuous at the Fermi level. The PDOS of the W2CoB2/Co interface without transition-metal doping is shown in Fig. 3a. The d orbitals of W and Co are the main contributors to the density of states (DOS) of the hard phase, while the d orbitals of Co are the main contributors to the DOS of the binder phase. In the range of -6 to -3 eV, the peak variation trend of W and B in the hard phase is basically consistent, indicating the occurrence of p-d orbital hybridization and the formation of covalent bonds. Within the range of -5 to 0 eV, the peak variation trend of Co in the hard phase is consistent with that in the binder phase, indicating the formation of hybrid covalent bonds at the interface.

The partial density of states (PDOS) of the W2CoB2/Co interface doped with Fe, Ni, or Cu is shown in Fig. 3b-d, respectively. The d orbitals of W after doping are the main contributors to the density of states (DOS) of the hard phase, whereas those of Co are the main contributors to the DOS of the binder phase. W and B form hybrid peaks in the range of -6 to -3 eV in Fig. 3b-d, and the DOS curves of W and B do not show significant changes, indicating that the doping element only affects the Co element. As shown in Fig. 3b and c, Fe, Ni, and Co in the hard and bonding phases form hybrid peaks in the range of -5 to 0 eV, respectively. As shown in Fig. 3d, only Cu and Co in the hard phase undergo hybridization. Thus, from the perspective of density of states (DOS), the influence of various doping elements on the interface can be given as Fe ≈ Ni > Cu.

Red and blue respectively represent areas of high and low charge density. It is obvious that the doping of transition metals has little impact on charge density.

The charge difference density plot further revealed the charge transfer trend of the interface model; its calculation formula can be given as22,23:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varDelta\:\rho\:={\rho\:}_{interface}-{\rho\:}_{{W}_{2}A{B}_{2}}-{\rho\:}_{A}$$\end{document}

where ρinterface is the total charge density of interface W2AB2/A, while ρW2AB2 and ρA represent the charge density of the separate hard phase layer W2AB2 and binder phase layer A, respectively.

The isosurfaces of the charge difference density at the interface before and after the addition of transition metals are shown in Fig. 5. Here, red and blue represent the accumulation and depletion of charges, respectively. Before the addition of transition metals (Fig. 5a), the charge around the W atom in the hard phase is depleted and accumulates around the B and Co atoms. Moreover, the charge near the interface mainly accumulates around the B atom. After the addition of transition metals, the hard-phase charge mainly accumulates around the W atom. It should be noted that the charge is transferred from the transition metal atom to the W atom. The charge at the interface mainly accumulates around the transition metal atoms. The charge of the binder phase accumulates around the Fe atoms in the second layer of the binder phase (Fig. 5b). The charge also accumulates around the Co atoms in the second (Fig. 5c) and first (Fig. 5d) layers of the binder phase. The amount of charge transferred in the binder and hard phases is Fe > Ni> Cu, indicating the compatibility of Fe, Ni, and Cu, which is consistent with the interfacial bonding energy results.

The total charge density reflects the actual overall electron distribution of the system, and the charge density difference reflects the electron redistribution following the formation of the system. The doping of Fe, Ni, and Cu to replace Co is a monoatomic non-isoelectronic doping mode that maintains the electroneutrality of the system; since the total number of electrons is conserved, the difference in the total charge density is insignificant. However, differences in electronegativity and orbital hybridization between the doped atoms and the matrix lead to the rearrangement of local electrons.

Microstructure

The microstructures of W2CoB2 before and after the addition of different transition metals are shown in Fig. 8. Each sample is mainly composed of three phases. Figure 9 shows the frequency distribution histograms of grain sizes for S1, S2, S3, and S4. The average sizes of grains without abnormal growth are 1.66, 1.68, 2.45 and 2.65 μm, respectively. After the addition of Fe, Ni, and Cu, these elements form a solid solution with the binder phase Co. This improves the bonding property between the binder phase and the hard phase, leading to the growth of the hard phase to varying degrees. As shown in Fig. 8, black and white represent the hard phase and gray represents the binder phase. Minimal change is observed in the microstructure after the addition of Cu, and the hard phase is equiaxed. However, after the addition of Fe or Ni, numerous small particles appear in the binder phase. Moreover, some grains are elongated, and the grain distribution is relatively uniform after the addition of Fe or Ni. The EDS mapping analysis of the corresponding metal added to the sample is shown on the right side of the microstructure. A small amount of Fe or Ni is distributed in the elongated hard phase, while Cu is only distributed in the binder phase.

The atomic ratios of the corresponding phases in Fig. 8a-d are listed in Table 5. Combining Table 5; Fig. 8, it can be seen that the W/Co/B ratio in the white phase (S1P1, S2P1, S2P5, S3P2, S3P3, S4P2, S4P4) is close to 2:1:2, mainly composed of W₂CoB₂. The Ti/B ratio in the black phase (S1P4, S2P2, S3P1, S4P1) is close to 1:2, mainly TiB₂. There is a large amount of Co mixed with Fe, Ni, and Cu in the gray phase (S1P2, S2P3, S3P5, S4P3), mainly serving as the bonding phase. The proportion of (W, Ti)/C in the light gray phase (S1P3, S2P4, S3P4, S4P5) is close to 1:1, mainly composed of solid solution phases of WC and TiC. The grains grew slightly, as shown in Fig. 8b and c. Some W2CoB2 grew into long strips. The content of Fe or Ni in these grains was higher than that in the equiaxed hard phase. This is because a solid solution of Fe or Ni increases the interfacial energy between the hard and binder phases, leading to grain growth. W₂CoB₂ crystallizes in a tetragonal system with remarkable crystallographic anisotropy, facilitating the facile realization of directional growth through interfacial energy regulation. Although Fe and Ni possess atomic radii highly matching that of Co, the three elements exhibit distinct differences in electronegativity. The substitution of Co atoms with Fe and Ni atoms gives rise to a local electronegativity difference within the solid solution, subsequently resulting in slight lattice distortion and uneven charge distribution among atoms at the interface, thereby further enhancing the interfacial energy. Notably, as a structural constituent in W₂CoB₂ instead of a mere binder phase, Co endows the Fe/Ni-Co solid solution with the capability to not only modulate the properties of the binder phase but also exert an influence on the hard phase W₂CoB₂, which further intensifies the anisotropy of interfacial energy. Moreover, fine TiC grains appeared in the binder phase, as shown in Fig. 8d. This is because Cu dissolved in the binder phase in a solid solution manner, thereby reducing the solubility of TiC in the binder phase and resulting in the precipitation of small grains.

Microhardness and indentation toughness

The microhardness and indentation toughness results of W2CoB2 before and after the addition of different transition metals are shown in Fig. 10; the microhardness of the samples was ranked as S3 > S4> S1> S2, whereas the indentation toughness was ranked as S2 > S3> S4> S1. Based on Fig. 8, it can be seen that adding iron leads to grain growth, which results in a decrease in material microhardness and an increase in indentation toughness. At the same time, the addition of nickel enhances the bonding between the hard phase and the bonding phase, thereby improving the microhardness and indentation toughness of the material. In addition, the addition of Cu reduces the solubility of TiC in the bonding phase and increases the precipitation of TiC small particles, thus playing a role in dispersion strengthening and grain refinement strengthening. The increase in the number of grain boundaries hinders the propagation of cracks, thereby improving the microhardness and indentation toughness of the material.

Compared with S1, S3 (the sample with the optimal performance) exhibits a 7% increase in microhardness and a 17.9% increase in indentation toughness. In existing studies, Zhang et al. prepared Cr-doped WCoB cermets via spark plasma sintering (SPS), achieving an optimal microhardness of 1751HV0.525. The microhardness of S3 is 21.3% higher than this value. Shang et al. prepared ultrafine-grained WC-Co via Sinter-HIPing, and its indentation toughness reached 10.8MPa·m1/226. The indentation toughness of S3 is 4.6% higher than this value.

Most classic toughening mechanisms in cermets belong to the structure-modified type, and some even achieve the synergy of strengthening and toughening. In contrast, doping-induced toughening enhances the intrinsic resistance of materials to crack propagation by regulating the bonding characteristics and electron distribution between atoms in the boride matrix. Subsequent studies will focus on investigating the combined toughening effect of these two approaches on cermets.

Crack formation in samples

The indentation crack patterns of W2CoB2 before and after the addition of different transition metals are shown in Fig. 11. As shown in Fig. 11a, the cracks were relatively flat and occurred along the grain boundaries of hard phases W2CoB2 and TiB2 with an intergranular fracture mode. The cracks in Fig. 11b and c penetrate the hard phase and undergo deflection, exhibiting a mixed mode of intergranular and transgranular fracture. It can be seen that the transgranular fractures in Fig. 11b and c both pass through larger hard phases and then deflect. According to Table 5, it can be observed that these larger hard phases contain more Fe and Ni elements, indicating that the addition of Fe and Ni can cause some hard phases to grow. This growth leads to the deflection of cracks and thus has a toughening effect. In Fig. 11d, a small amount of transgranular fracture can be observed. However, due to the precipitation of TiC grains, the number of grain boundaries increased. As a result, cracks deflect between grains and crack propagation is hindered.

Fracture morphology of samples

Figure 12 shows the fracture morphology of W2CoB2 before and after the addition of different transition metals. The morphology mainly consists of brittle fracture, with obvious pits and large plateaus on the fracture surface. The pits can be attributed to the detachment of the hard phase during the fracture process, and the plateaus are flat interfaces left by brittle fracture in the binder phase. Numerous pits and plate-like morphologies can be observed in Fig. 12a. However, the binder phase does not exhibit plastic deformation, which is a typical intergranular brittle fracture. The toughness of the material significantly increases after the addition of Fe or Ni. A small number of tearing edges are formed by the plastic deformation of the binder phase, as evident in Fig. 12b and c. The appearance of smaller pits in Fig. 12d compared with other fracture surfaces can be attributed to the detachment of TiC particles precipitated in the binder phase. Further, Fe and Ni show a better solid-solution effect on Co than on Cu. Additionally, the addition of Fe and Ni enhances the bonding between the hard and binder phases, thereby enhancing material toughness. However, the addition of Cu leads to the precipitation of the brittle TiC phase, which increases the material microhardness, and the effect on toughness improvement is not significant.

Tribological properties of the samples

Figure 13 shows the wear morphology of the sintered sample obtained from a 2-hour wear test at 25 °C. It can be seen that in Fig. 13a, the wear morphology is rough. It mostly consists of hard phases and pits with detached hard phases. A small amount of binder phase debris undergoes cold welding on the surface during the wear process, forming a sheet-like structure attached to the material surface. After adding transition metals, the plastic deformation generated by the binder phase and debris during the wear process increases. Evidently, in Fig. 13b, the binder phase undergoes plastic deformation in the direction of wear, with a large number of sheet-like structures formed under pressure and pits caused by partial tearing. The sheet-like structure in Fig. 13c is composed of binder phase debris that undergoes secondary wear and cold welding and adheres to the sample surface. At the same time, a small amount of pits left by the detachment of the hard phase can be seen on the surface. In Fig. 13d, a plane formed by binder phase debris can be seen. Some sheet-like structures peel off during the wear process, leaving grooves in the direction of wear. Before adding transition metals, the sample is mainly composed of brittle hard phases, which are prone to detachment and fragmentation during the wear process, resulting in three-body abrasive wear. After the addition of transition metals, the content of the binder phase is increased, and the wear debris generated during the wear process could be oxidized to form an oxide layer that adheres to the sample surface, resulting in a varying degree of improvement in the wear resistance of the material27,28.

Figure 14 shows the coefficient of friction curve. It can be seen from the Fig. 14 that after about 2000s of wear time, the COF tends to stabilize. The COF of S1, S2, S3, and S4 stabilize at 0.63, 0.47, 0.41, and 0.51 respectively. The addition of Fe, Ni, and Cu can remarkably reduce the COF. In S1, the hard phase is prone to peeling off, which leads to an increase in the COF. After adding Fe, Ni, and Cu, the hard phase and the binder phase show good wetting, thereby enhancing the wear resistance. At the same time, the debris generated by the binder phase during wear adheres to the material surface, further reducing the COF. The results indicate that S3 has the optimal wear resistance. Zhang et al. prepared a WCoB cladding layer via laser cladding technology and added NbC to improve the wear resistance of the cladding layer, achieving an optimal friction coefficient of 0.4912. The friction coefficient of S3 (the sample with the best wear performance) is 0.41, indicating that the wear resistance of S3 is superior to that of the aforementioned WCoB cladding layer.

Conclusions

The interfacial bonding energies of W2AB2/A metal ceramics were determined using first-principles calculations. Fe or Ni doping improved the bonding between the hard and binder phases; Fe demonstrated a better effect, while Cu doping reduced the bonding performance.The bonding strength at the interface was primarily determined by the d-orbitals of interfacial atoms. From the perspective of DOS and charge difference density, Fe had the greatest impact on the interfacial bonding performance, followed by Ni and Cu.W2AB2/A metal ceramics were prepared via vacuum liquid-phase sintering. The room temperature mechanical performance test results showed that the optimal microhardness was 2123.3 HV0.5 upon the addition of Ni, the optimal indentation toughness was 12.4 MPa · m1/2 upon the addition of Fe, the minimum COF was 0.41 upon the addition of Ni. After comprehensive consideration, adding Ni can improve the indentation toughness and wear resistance of W2CoB2 while maintaining a high hardness. The experimental results demonstrated the reliability of first-principles calculation results.Fe and Ni doping exhibit prominent and continuous hybrid electronic states at the Fermi level, indicating that a large number of electrons are available for energy redistribution. This not only makes crack initiation difficult but also generates significant resistance to crack propagation, which is manifested as high fracture toughness and excellent wear resistance at the macroscopic level. In contrast, Cu doping shows a pseudogap characteristic at the Fermi level, which rigidifies its electronic structure and leads to brittle interfacial behavior. According to the Griffith brittle fracture criterion, this phenomenon causes cracks to easily propagate rapidly along the interface.
