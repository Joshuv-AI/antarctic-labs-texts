Neurofilament light chain levels in serum among a large mixed memory clinic cohort: Confounders and diagnostic usefulness
Authors: Anja Hviid Simonsen, Helena Sophia Gleerup, Christian Sandøe Musaeus, Finn Sellebjerg, Malene Bredahl Hansen, Helle Bach Søndergaard, Gunhild Waldemar, Steen Gregers Hasselbalch
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONEarly and accurate diagnosis of neurocognitive disorders including neurodegenerative dementia remains challenging. This study explores the impact of biological factors on serum neurofilament light chain (NfL) levels and clinical usefulness for the detection of neurocognitive disorders in a mixed memory clinic.METHODSSerum samples and clinical data were obtained from 1188 patients who underwent diagnostic investigations for memory complaints between January 2018 and September 2019. Serum NfL was measured using single molecule array technology.RESULTSNfL exhibited a moderate association with age, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and Fazekas score. NfL was able to differentiate between patients with neurocognitive disorders and those without with a sensitivity and specificity of 80%. NfL could, however, not distinguish between different dementia etiologies.DISCUSSIONSerum NfL could aid early diagnostic triage by identifying patients requiring further diagnostic procedures and therefore aid in a more focused use of health‐care resources.Keywords: blood biomarker, dementia, diagnosis, neurodegeneration, neurofilament light chain

BACKGROUND

Distinguishing between patients with a neurodegenerative disease and those without poses a major challenge in memory clinics. Due to the increasing aging population and the possibility of disease‐modifying drugs for the treatment of Alzheimer's disease (AD), a considerably higher number of patients will undergo diagnostic work‐up at specialized memory clinics in the upcoming years. 1 Hence, a pressing need arises for the development of cost‐ and time‐effective tools to assist clinicians in the identification of patients with neurocognitive disorders. In this context, the use of blood‐based biomarkers appears promising.

AD is the most common cause of dementia and may contribute to > 60% of the cases. Other significant forms include vascular dementia (VaD), dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), and frontotemporal dementia (FTD). The distinction among these various types of dementia is not always clear‐cut and it is common for mixed types to occur. 2 In all the mentioned types, neuronal damage is a pathological hallmark, irrespective of the primary pathology.

The use of imaging and fluid biomarkers for the diagnosis of neurodegenerative diseases is well established. For the diagnosis of AD, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers reflecting the levels of amyloid beta and tau species are implemented. 3 However, CSF biomarkers are not ideal due to perceived invasiveness and cost, which limit their use as large‐scale diagnostic tools. A blood‐based test that could identify or rule out the presence of neurodegenerative processes, by examining neuronal damage in a mixed patient population, would be useful for the clinical management of patients, and aid in a more focused use of health‐care resources.

Neurofilaments are scaffolding proteins expressed in neurons. They enable the radial growth of axons and are abundantly expressed in the larger myelinated axons but also in synapses. 4 Neurofilaments are classified based on their molecular weight in neurofilament heavy (NfH), neurofilament medium (NfM), and neurofilament light (NfL). 5 , 6 Low levels of neurofilaments are constantly released from axons in an age‐dependent manner. 6 In response to damage to the central nervous system (CNS), CSF levels increase, making NfL a biomarker of axonal degeneration. 5 , 7 Indeed, the monoclonal antibody pair used in the most prevalent NFL assays may specifically recognize a peptide stretch only expressed in damaged axons. 8

Increased levels of CSF NfL have been described in numerous neurodegenerative, inflammatory, vascular, and traumatic diseases. 5 , 9 , 10 The perceived invasiveness of a lumbar puncture (LP) limits the usefulness and widespread use of CSF NfL as a biomarker. Recent advances in analytical methods, such as the ultrasensitive single molecule array (Simoa), have enabled quantification of NfL in blood. 11 A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis 12 showed a moderate to strong correlation between CSF and blood NfL. The strongest correlation was seen when using the Simoa technology, supporting the use of blood NfL measurements as an alternative to CSF NfL. However, more data are needed to implement blood NfL in clinical practice. Two recent reviews 13 , 14 pointed out the need for more data regarding clinical confounders and biological factors such as renal disease, liver disease, and other comorbidities in a real‐world setting to aid in the interpretation of NfL and other blood‐based biomarker values in a clinical context.

Our overarching objective was to investigate the potential utility of serum NfL as a biomarker for identifying individuals with neurocognitive disorders within a diverse population of elderly individuals referred to a memory clinic. If used as a primary triage biomarker, serum NfL will require a high accuracy, preferentially with a high sensitivity to rule in neurodegenerative diseases (NDD). However, a trade‐off could be lower specificity and secondary investigations may be relevant to rule out NDD in false positive cases.

The aim of this study was three‐fold: First, to explore the influence of surrogate markers for kidney function, liver function, as well as cardiovascular and metabolic conditions on serum NfL levels in a consecutive and mixed memory clinic population; second, to investigate the clinical usefulness of serum NfL levels for the differentiation between patients with a neurocognitive disorder, including neurodegenerative or vascular brain disease, from patients without any of these conditions; and third, to investigate the diagnostic ability of serum NfL for the differentiation among different etiological dementia diagnoses.

At their first visit, patients underwent a standard diagnostic work‐up including medical and neurological examination, cognitive examination, informant‐based history, and standard laboratory blood work. When deemed necessary, LP, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or 18fluorodeoxyglucosepositron emission tomography (FDG‐PET) were performed later. The etiological diagnosis was established at a multidisciplinary consensus conference following international criteria with a probable, not a possible, level of confidence.

The patients diagnosed with AD fulfilled the criteria suggested by the National Institute on Aging and the Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA); 3 , 15 alcoholic dementia and ataxia were diagnosed according to the International Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision (ICD‐10) 16 with diagnostic codes F10.6 and R27.0, respectively. The patients diagnosed with VaD fulfilled the criteria by the International Society of Vascular Behavioural and Cognitive Disorders (VasCog), 17 and the patients with mixed dementia both fulfilled the NIA‐AA criteria and the VasCog criteria. 3 , 17 Patients diagnosed with FTD fulfilled the criteria for the behavioral variant 18 or the criteria for non‐fluent aphasia or the semantic variant of FTD. 19 The DLB diagnosis was established according to the fourth report of the DLB consortium, 20 and the diagnosis of normal‐pressure hydrocephalus (NPH) was established according to the international guideline criteria for idiopathic NPH. 21 Atypical Parkinson's syndromes (APD), specifically progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), and Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD) fulfilled the criteria suggested by the International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society. 22 , 23

All patients under diagnostic evaluation at the Copenhagen University Hospital Memory Clinic, Rigshospitalet underwent standard structural neuroimaging, typically in the form of MRI or, in specific instances, computed tomography scans, alongside FDG‐PET scans.

A subset of the patients, for whom the clinical team were less confident in the etiological diagnosis, were classified with an uncertain diagnosis. This group comprised patients who decided to interrupt their diagnostic assessment or patients for whom the clinicians were not able to provide an etiological diagnosis. This group of patients was not included in substudies two and three.

To test NfL's performance in substudies two and three, the remaining patients were divided into two groups: The patients with neurocognitive disorders (ND) 24 included patients with neurodegenerative or vascular brain disease (the ND group) and the non‐ND group including patients without neurodegenerative or vascular disease (the non‐ND group). The ND group included patients with the following diagnoses: alcohol dementia, AD, ataxia disorders, FTD, DLB, mixed dementia (AD plus vascular dementia), NPH, PD, and VaD. The non‐ND group was also referred to the Copenhagen University Hospital Memory Clinic for diagnostic evaluation and included patients with subjective cognitive impairment (SCI) but no cognitive impairment after clinical investigations and patients with cognitive dysfunction due to mild psychiatric disease such as depression, but for whom there was no suspicion of an underlying NDD.

NfL analysis

Serum from the patients was stored in polypropylene tubes at −80°C in the Danish Dementia Biobank within 2 hours of sampling. NfL concentrations in serum were measured using the Simoa® NF‐light™ Advantage Kit (Cat. No 103186, LOT#502342) (Quanterix) per the manufacturer's instructions. Measurements were performed on a Quanterix SR‐X Biomarker Detection System at the Danish Multiple Sclerosis Center, Copenhagen University Hospital—Rigshospitalet Glostrup. A maximum intra‐assay percent coefficient of variation (%CV) of 20 was accepted for all calibrator standards and controls. Two manufacturer‐provided assay‐specific controls were used for intra‐assay and inter‐assay quality control (QC), yielding an average intra‐assay %CV of 5.01 and inter‐assay %CV of 6.24.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.1.3).

To understand the association between NfL and other blood‐based biomarkers, we first performed linear regression between NfL and each covariate (age, eGFR, ALT, HDL, LDL, cholesterol, and Hb1Ac) separately. Next, we performed stepwise linear regression with both forward and backward model selection using the MASS toolbox. This was done to understand if any combination of biomarkers affected NfL in combination with age. Due to missing values, this was only performed on a subset of the data (n = 844). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compute the difference in NfL for the FAZ scores (0–3).

To compare age between ND and non‐ND, a Welch two‐sample t test (unequal variance using an F test) was used between the two groups, while a chi‐squared test was used to compare the sex between the groups. Comparisons of the mean NfL levels in the ND and non‐ND groups were calculated using an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). The covariates were age and eGFR for all comparisons due to the confounding effect seen in the first part of the study. Missing values were found for 10 of the participants with eGFR, and the missForest package was used to impute the missing values. We did not use LDL as a covariate, because it was missing in 24% of the participants. If the ANCOVA was significant, post hoc Tukey comparisons with single‐step method adjustment were performed as implemented in the multcomp toolbox. NfL was log‐transformed before comparisons between groups due to unequal distribution of the data.

To understand whether NfL could be used as a diagnostic marker to differentiate between ND and non‐ND, we performed logistic regression between the non‐ND and patients with a neurodegenerative or vascular brain disease (ND). First, we divided the dataset into a training and test set (70%/30%). The first model was trained using NfL, age as a continuous variable, and eGFR. Due to the large age difference between the two groups, we calculated a second model with age as a categorical variable being either below 60, above 85, or divided into 5‐year intervals in the age groups between 60 and 85. To estimate the area under the curve (AUC), we used the pROC package. To compute the confidence intervals of coordinates on the receiver operating characteristic curve, we used 2000 stratified bootstrap replicates as implemented in the ci.coods function for a predefined sensitivity of 80%. We set the sensitivity at 80% as our goal was to detect NDD in a very diverse clinical population and we do not believe that serum NfL will be a standalone test.

To compare patient characteristics among all groups, we performed ANOVA for continuous variables and chi‐squared tests for categorical data. To investigate the role of NfL as a biomarker for differentiation among dementia of different etiologies, an ANCOVA was used among all disease groups with post hoc Tukey comparisons. All groups had to have at least 10 participants; therefore, 5 patients were eliminated from the subcohort. This was repeated without the non‐ND group. Here, the same covariates were used between ND and non‐ND, though sex was also included due to the difference between groups.

The code and output for the statistical calculations can be found in supporting information.

The total cohort comprised 1190 patients, age range 25 to 96, 561 females and 629 males. Of these, 751 patients were diagnosed with a neurodegenerative or cerebrovascular disease (the ND group), and 272 patients were classified as cognitively unimpaired or having a mild psychiatric disease (the non‐ND group). A total of 167 patients remained diagnostically unresolved and were not included in the subsequent analyses of NfL as a biomarker. NfL for differential diagnosis

In substudy 3, the cohort of 751 patients was reduced to 746 patients because a minimum group size of 10 patients per group was deemed acceptable. A significant difference in NfL levels among the nine disease groups (alcohol dementia, AD, ataxia, VaD, FTD, DLB, mixed dementia, NPH, and PD+) was found when adjusting for age, sex, and eGFR (F[8,733] = 2.3187, P = 0.018), see figure 5 and table 2. Additionally, a supplementary analysis revealed that patients with AD dementia exhibited significantly higher levels of NfL compared to patients with MCI due to AD (P = 0.0003). DISCUSSION

Blood‐based biomarkers are potentially relevant in NDD diagnosis due to being less invasive, more cost effective, and more accessible than the currently used CSF and imaging markers. One of the key challenges at this point is the need for large, diverse, and well‐characterized cohorts to further explore the validity of the proposed blood‐based biomarkers.

It is well known that somatic comorbidities are very common in NDD. 26 , 27 Consequently, it is important to consider potential confounders in the interpretation of NfL levels. In addition to analyzing the correlation between eGFR and circulating NfL levels, we incorporated surrogate markers for liver dysfunction, dyslipidemia, and diabetes into the analyses.

In this study, we analyzed NfL in serum in a consecutive mixed memory clinic cohort to elucidate its relevance for differentiating between patients with a neurodegenerative or cerebrovascular disease and those without. NfL levels were associated with surrogate markers of kidney function, liver function, as well as markers for cardiovascular and metabolic conditions, and to patients’ diagnoses.

In our study we observed that several biological variables had an impact on serum NfL levels. Notably, age and eGFR displayed moderate association, while NfL levels showed a weaker association with elevated levels of HbA1C as well as with decreased levels of ALT and LDL. The connection between lower levels of LDL was somewhat unexpected, possibly influenced by patients taking lipid‐lowering drugs as well as the presence of outliers. Furthermore, we also found an association between cerebrovascular burden operationalized by Fazekas score and NfL levels.

Age has been described previously as highly correlated with increasing NfL levels in both CSF 7 and blood, 10 whereas results on other biological factors in large real‐world elderly populations are scarce. One previous study identified age and body mass index (BMI) as confounding factors 28 and another study identified age, creatinine, and HbA1C as major confounding factors in control subjects without neurological disease. 29 Kidney structure and function decline with age irrespective of kidney disease and co‐morbidities 30 and NfL in blood is likely to be cleared by the kidneys. 31 Hence, our study corroborates that interpretation of NfL values in serum need to be accompanied with data on kidney function and age, especially in a memory clinic setting.

Second, we investigated the ability of serum NfL to differentiate patients who had a neurodegenerative or vascular etiology from those who did not. Using a model with age and eGFR as covariates, we found that serum NfL could differentiate the two groups with a sensitivity and specificity of 80%. Another study investigating the ability of serum NfL to differentiate between cases and controls in a research cohort only including patients with AD, FTD, and healthy controls found an AUC of 0.94. 32

The NfL values of our study are in line with newly published NfL values in healthy individuals. 33 , 34 Hence, our results show that serum NfL could aid in the detection of neurodegenerative or cerebrovascular processes in a mixed patient population and therefore aid in a more focused use of resources for the patients who need them the most.

Finally, we investigated the ability of serum NfL to differentiate between the different neurodegenerative or cerebrovascular diseases in our cohort. Even if the all‐group comparisons were significant, none of the post hoc tests were significant. The highest serum NfL levels were seen in patients with mixed dementia, which could mean that patients with dual (vascular and AD) pathology also have higher levels of neurodegeneration, which is in line with our initial finding of NfL being associated with FAZ. Most studies find that among the diseases studied in this work, the highest levels are seen in patients with FTD followed by AD. 10 , 35 However, these studies did not include patients with mixed AD and cerebrovascular pathology. Furthermore, the FTD group in our study may be more heterogeneous due to the consecutive nature of our study design. Earlier studies in non‐AD neurodegenerative disorders have shown higher levels of NfL in atypical PD compared to PD with dementia. 36 , 37 In our sample, this could not be replicated, most probably due to the small sample size.

Our results corroborate that serum NfL is a biomarker of general brain disease, regardless of whether caused by neurodegenerative or cerebrovascular etiologies, and thus serum NfL is unable to reliably differentiate among these diseases. Hence, serum NfL might be used in the early stages of the diagnostic evaluation in memory clinics to triage the patients into those that would need more in‐depth diagnostic procedures, and those who should be referred to other clinical specialties.

The strength of this study is the large real‐world cohort of patients, which translates into the real clinical setting for blood‐based biomarkers. However, the same nature of our cohort leads to limitations, including missing data for some routine blood results as well as unavailability of BMI. Furthermore, all serum NfL levels were measured in one batch, thus minimizing variability, which will not be the case in clinical routine settings. Due to the consecutive nature of the cohort, a subset of the included patients remained diagnostically unresolved, and some were lost to follow‐up. Hence, longitudinal data were not available for all patients.

To corroborate the validity of our findings, studies with consecutive recruitment as well as consecutive NfL measurements need to be undertaken. If these future studies are positive, serum NfL could potentially be used as an addition to the memory clinics’ repertoire of screening tests. Due to the increased focus on tests performed in primary care, it would be of interest to understand if NfL could applied in this setting. However, screening in a primary care setting would require much higher accuracy due to the heterogeneous nature of the patients. However, a combination of serum NfL with other blood‐based biomarkers and a brief cognitive assessment 38 may hold promise for implementation in primary care settings. This also requires that primary care providers are trained in interpretation and communication of the results. If successful, this approach could streamline the process of patient triage using a stepwise diagnostic algorithm, ensuring relevant patients are referred to more extensive diagnostic evaluations at specialized clinics. Simultaneously, this approach would offer assurance to individuals for whom further investigations are deemed unnecessary. This scenario holds significant implications for health‐care costs at a time when we can expect increased health resource use in the world's aging population.
