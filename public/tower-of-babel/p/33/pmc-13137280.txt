Association of glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonist use with clinical outcomes in patients with rheumatoid arthritis and type 2 diabetes
Authors: Yu‐Ting Yu, Ying Chun Lee, Yu‐Wei Fang, Wen‐Hui Hsieh, Ming‐Hsien Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo evaluate whether glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) are associated with improved survival and renal outcomes in patients with rheumatoid arthritis and type 2 diabetes, a population at high cardiometabolic risk.Materials and MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using an active‐comparator, new‐user design within the TriNetX US Collaborative Network. Adults with rheumatoid arthritis and type 2 diabetes initiating GLP‐1 RAs or dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors (DPP‐4is) between 2016 and 2023 were included. The primary outcome was all‐cause mortality; secondary outcomes were major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), major adverse kidney events (MAKE), and all‐cause hospitalization. Hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated using Cox regression after 1:1 propensity score matching.ResultsA total of 4,607 patients per group were followed for up to 4 years. GLP‐1 RA use was associated with lower all‐cause mortality (HR 0.68; 95% confidence intervals (CI), 0.58–0.80), reduced MAKE (HR 0.89; 95% CI, 0.82–0.97), and fewer hospitalizations (HR 0.92; 95% CI, 0.86–0.98), compared with DPP‐4is. No significant difference was observed for MACE (HR 0.96; 95% CI, 0.89–1.04). Results were consistent across prespecified subgroups and sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsIn patients with rheumatoid arthritis and type 2 diabetes, GLP‐1 RA therapy was associated with 32% lower mortality, 11% fewer kidney events, and 8% fewer hospitalizations vs DPP‐4is. These findings support GLP‐1 RAs as a promising therapeutic option for this high‐risk population.Keywords: Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists, Rheumatoid arthritis, Type 2 diabetes mellitus

INTRODUCTION

Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic systemic autoimmune disease characterized by progressive joint destruction and systemic involvement 1 , 2 . Patients with rheumatoid arthritis have a markedly increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus, driven by chronic inflammation, altered adipokine and cytokine profiles, reduced physical activity, and prolonged glucocorticoid exposure, all of which contribute to insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction 3 , 4 , 5 . The coexistence of rheumatoid arthritis and type 2 diabetes creates a high‐risk clinical state in which metabolic disturbances amplify the detrimental effects of persistent inflammation, accelerating atherosclerosis and multi‐organ damage 6 , 7 . Consequently, individuals with both rheumatoid arthritis and type 2 diabetes experience substantially higher rates of mortality, cardiovascular disease, renal impairment, and serious infections compared with those with either condition alone or the general population 8 , 9 , 10 . This intersection of autoimmune and metabolic disease identifies rheumatoid arthritis patients with type 2 diabetes as a particularly vulnerable subgroup, underscoring the urgent need for optimized therapeutic strategies to mitigate morbidity and mortality.

In type 2 diabetes management, glucose‐lowering agents differ widely in their impact on cardiorenal‐metabolic health. Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) reduce all‐cause mortality, improve cardiovascular outcomes, and slow chronic kidney disease progression 11 , 12 . Beyond glycemic control, they promote weight loss through appetite suppression and delayed gastric emptying, attenuate systemic inflammation, and improve lipid and blood pressure profiles 13 , 14 , 15 . Additional evidence suggests neuroprotective, hepatoprotective, and musculoskeletal benefits, as well as efficacy in inflammatory conditions 16 , 17 , 18 . These pleiotropic effects make GLP‐1 RAs uniquely suited to optimize health in type 2 diabetes. Accordingly, both the American Diabetes Association and the European Association for the Study of Diabetes recommend GLP‐1 RAs for patients with type 2 diabetes and established cardiovascular disease or multiple risk factors, often as the preferred option after metformin 19 .

Whether the benefits of GLP‐1 RAs observed in the general type 2 diabetes population extend to patients with rheumatoid arthritis remains unclear. The systemic inflammation characteristic of RA, frequent corticosteroid use, disease‐modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), and a high burden of comorbidities may modify both the efficacy and safety of these therapies 20 , 21 . Notably, a recent study reported that GLP‐1 RA use, compared with dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors (DPP‐4is), was linked to increased risks of certain autoimmune diseases—including rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and ankylosing spondylitis—raising potential safety concerns in patients predisposed to these conditions. Moreover, the absence of direct studies evaluating clinical outcomes of GLP‐1 RAs in individuals with both rheumatoid arthritis and type 2 diabetes contributes to uncertainty in selecting optimal glucose‐lowering strategies for this high‐risk population, highlighting the need for targeted research to guide best practices.

To address this clinical uncertainty, we conducted a large, population‐based retrospective cohort study evaluating GLP‐1 RA use, with DPP‐4is as an active comparator, in patients with rheumatoid arthritis and type 2 diabetes. Our objective was to assess whether the survival, cardiovascular, renal, and hospitalization benefits observed in the general type 2 diabetes population also extend to this high‐risk subgroup.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data sources

This study utilized the TriNetX Global Health Research Network, a federated, real‐world data platform linking healthcare organizations, research institutions, and industry partners to accelerate clinical research and enhance patient care. The network aggregates de‐identified electronic health records and insurance claims from academic medical centers, community hospitals, and specialty practices across >20 countries, encompassing hundreds of millions of patient records. Data include demographics, diagnoses (ICD‐10‐CM), procedures (CPT), medications (RxNorm), laboratory results, and, in certain datasets, oncology and genomic variables. The database is continuously updated, allowing researchers to identify patient cohorts, evaluate study feasibility, and apply built‐in statistical tools, such as propensity score matching (PSM), survival analysis, and outcome visualization, for robust comparisons. Its intuitive interface supports rapid cohort creation, trend analysis, and site identification, for both observational studies and clinical trial recruitment. TriNetX harmonizes data using standardized terminologies, ensuring consistency across diverse sources, while adhering strictly to privacy regulations. By combining comprehensive real‐world data with powerful analytics, TriNetX enables data‐driven decisions that improve patient outcomes and advance medical research 22 , 23 , 24 .

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shin Kong Wu Ho‐Su Memorial Hospital (IRB number: 20250104R). Informed consent was waived, as TriNetX provides access to de‐identified patient data.

Study design and study population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using a new‐user, intention‐to‐treat, active‐comparator design to evaluate clinical outcomes in adults with type 2 diabetes and rheumatoid arthritis within the TriNetX US Collaborative Network. DPP‐4is were chosen as the active comparator because they are widely recommended for glycemic control in type 2 diabetes and share a similar incretin‐based mechanism with GLP‐1 RAs. The patient selection process is illustrated in Figure 1. Eligible patients were ≥18 years old; had at least two recorded encounters between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2023; and met diagnostic criteria for both rheumatoid arthritis and type 2 diabetes before treatment initiation (n = 123,982). Two exposure groups were defined: initiation of a GLP‐1 RA (n = 18,188) or initiation of a DPP‐4i (n = 10,877). The index date was defined as the date of the first prescription for the assigned medication during the study period. To ensure a strict new‐user design, patients were required to have a 12‐month baseline period with no prior exposure to either drug class. We excluded individuals with pregnancy, dialysis, solid‐organ or stem‐cell transplantation, or any neoplasm in the year preceding the index date to minimize confounding from severe conditions. The generation of two cohorts is shown in Method S1.

To address confounding by indication, we created balanced groups using 1:1 PSM on baseline covariates, with balance assessed via standardized mean differences (SMDs). This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational research 25 .

Covariates

Baseline covariates were prespecified and extracted from electronic health records during the year preceding the index date. Variables were grouped into demographics, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, medication use, and laboratory results, as detailed in Table 1. Demographic variables included age at index, body mass index (BMI), sex, and race (White, African American, Asian, or other). Lifestyle factors comprised alcohol‐related disorders, nicotine dependence, and tobacco use. Comorbidities included hypertensive diseases, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, cardiomyopathy, chronic rheumatic heart disease, pulmonary heart disease, peripheral artery disease, aortic aneurysm and dissection, arterial embolism and thrombosis, fatty liver, liver cirrhosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, dyslipidemia, thyroid disorders, gout, cerebrovascular diseases, and anxiety disorders. Baseline medication use included insulin, biguanides (metformin), sodium–glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2is), sulfonylureas, thiazolidinediones, antithrombotic agents, beta‐blockers, calcium channel blockers, renin–angiotensin system inhibitors (angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers), peripheral vasodilators, statins, fibrates, immunosuppressants, immunostimulants, anti‐inflammatory and antirheumatic agents, and antidepressants. Laboratory parameters included electrolytes (sodium, potassium, and calcium), lipid profiles (triglycerides, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol), kidney function markers (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] and blood urea nitrogen), liver enzymes (alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, and alkaline phosphatase), nutritional and hematologic indices (albumin, hemoglobin, leukocyte count, and lymphocyte percentage), and glycemic and inflammatory markers (hemoglobin [Hb] A1c and C‐reactive protein [CRP]). Together, these covariates provided a comprehensive baseline profile for risk adjustment and PSM. Diagnosis codes are provided in Method S1.

Study outcomes

Outcomes were assessed from 1 day after the index date up to a maximum of 4 years, with administrative censoring on August 5, 2025, under an intention‐to‐treat framework. The primary outcome was all‐cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), a composite of myocardial infarction, stroke, and death, and major adverse kidney events (MAKE), a composite of acute kidney injury, end‐stage renal disease, dialysis, and death. All‐cause hospitalization was also evaluated as a measure of overall healthcare utilization. A prespecified specificity analysis examined individual clinical endpoints to contextualize composite outcomes, including acute myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke, end‐stage renal disease, emergency department visits, sepsis, and pneumonia. Outcomes were identified using standardized diagnosis and procedure codes within the TriNetX electronic health record system and were measured consistently across exposure groups. Observation ended at the earliest of the occurrence of an outcome, death, loss to follow‐up in the network, 4 years after the index date, or the study end date of August 5, 2025. Diagnosis codes are provided in Method S1.

Sensitivity analysis

All sensitivity analyses applied the same eligibility criteria, exposure definitions, outcomes, covariates, and PSM strategy as the main analysis. First, alternative postmatching specifications were evaluated to probe residual confounding. Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, race, lifestyle factors, BMI, blood pressure, and proxies of economic status; Model 2 additionally included baseline comorbidities; and Model 3 further incorporated baseline medication use. Second, to account for potential violations of the proportional hazards assumption, period‐specific effects were estimated by segmenting follow‐up into three intervals: 1 day–1 year, 1 day–2 years, and 1 day–3 years, while maintaining risk sets and covariate balance. This time‐stratified approach allows effect estimates to vary across clinically relevant periods and reduces bias from nonproportional hazards. Third, to address potential protopathic bias, early outcome misclassification, and immortal‐time bias around treatment initiation, an index‐lag analysis was performed. The index date was shifted by 90 days after the first prescription, with follow‐up commencing 1 day after this lagged index, while preserving all inclusion/exclusion criteria and matching procedures. Fourth, we compared the effects of GLP‐1 RA with other antidiabetic medications, including insulin, biguanides, SGLT2 inhibitors, and thiazolidinediones. For all sensitivity analyses, the same outcome set was assessed as in the main analysis. Finally, to address potential calendar‐time confounding and changes in drug adoption, patients were stratified into three sub‐cohorts based on the year of index treatment initiation: 2016–2018, 2019–2021, and 2022–2023. We applied the same inclusion/exclusion criteria and PSM protocol used in the primary analysis for each period to ensure balanced baseline characteristics between GLP‐1 RA and DPP‐4i users.

Statistical analysis

Baseline variables from the 2 years preceding the index date were summarized as counts (percentages) for categorical variables and means (standard deviations) for continuous variables. Between‐group differences were quantified using SMDs. For descriptive interpretation, conventional thresholds were applied (~0.20 small, 0.21–0.50 moderate, 0.51–0.80 large) 26 ; for matching diagnostics, SMD <0.10 was considered indicative of adequate balance 27 . Propensity scores were estimated via logistic regression and matched using greedy nearest‐neighbor matching with a 0.1 pooled SMD caliper. Matching procedures were implemented using Python and R (version 3.4.4; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria); further details are provided in Method S2.

Time‐to‐event associations were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models to obtain hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals within the TriNetX platform. Kaplan–Meier curves and log‐rank tests were used to compare survival functions between exposure groups. To assess absolute clinical benefit beyond relative measures, several complementary statistical metrics were calculated alongside traditional hazard ratios. The absolute risk reduction (ARR) was calculated as the difference in Kaplan–Meier survival probabilities between the GLP‐1 RA and DPP‐4i cohorts at the yearly time point. This value was multiplied by 1,000 to determine events prevented per 1,000 patients, providing a population‐level impact measure. The number needed to treat (NNT) was calculated as the reciprocal of the ARR, representing how many patients would need treatment with GLP‐1 RAs instead of DPP‐4is to prevent one outcome event over the study duration. To provide assessment independent of proportional hazards assumptions, we used Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST), calculated as the area under the Kaplan–Meier curve from index date to 4‐year follow‐up. The RMST difference between groups represents the average outcome‐free time gained per patient from GLP‐1 RA therapy.

Subgroup analyses were prespecified based on sex (female, male), age (≥65 years, <65 years), race (Asian, African American, White), BMI (≥30 kg/m2, <30 kg/m2), HbA1c (≥7%, <7%), urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (≥30 mg/g, <30 mg/g), eGFR (≥60 mL/min/m2, <60 mL/min/m2), heart failure (yes, no), coronary artery disease (yes, no), ischemic heart disease (yes, no), and cerebrovascular disease (yes, no). All subgroups were re‐matched using PSM on all baseline variables to create well‐balanced, comparable pairs.

All statistical analyses were performed within the TriNetX platform. Two‐sided P‐values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Figures were generated in R (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics of patients

The study cohort included 9,173 GLP‐1 RA users and 5,744 DPP‐4i users before matching. After 1:1 PSM, 4,607 patients were included in each group. Before matching, GLP‐1 RA users were younger (mean age 59 vs 64.8 years) and had higher BMI (37.3 vs 32.6 kg/m2) with a slightly lower proportion of males. They had lower prevalence of several cardiometabolic comorbidities, including heart failure (10.5% vs 14.4%), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (10% vs 13.3%), and cerebrovascular disease (3.9% vs 6.3%), but higher prevalence of fatty liver (7.9% vs 5.4%) and anxiety (21.8% vs 17.1%). Baseline medication use differed: GLP‐1 RA users had lower use of biguanides, sulfonylureas, statins, beta‐blockers, calcium channel blockers, and antithrombotic agents but higher use of SGLT2is. Laboratory values indicated better kidney function (eGFR 76.6 vs 70.3 mL/min/1.73 m2), higher albumin and Hb, and lower CRP among GLP‐1 RA users. After matching, groups were well balanced across demographics, comorbidities, medications, and laboratory measures, with all SMDs <0.1 (a love plot is shown in Figure S1).

All‐cause mortality of GLP‐1 RA use in patients with RA

The mean follow‐up was 957 ± 438 days for the GLP‐1 RA group and 1,088 ± 462 days for the DPP‐4i group (Figure S2). Kaplan–Meier curves (Figure 2) demonstrate lower all‐cause mortality in GLP‐1 RA users compared with DPP‐4i users over 4 years. Cumulative event‐free survival remained consistently higher for GLP‐1 RA, with narrower CIs. The log‐rank test indicated a highly significant difference (P < 0.001). Numbers at risk declined similarly between groups, supporting a sustained survival advantage with GLP‐1 RA. During follow‐up, all‐cause mortality occurred in 5.1% of GLP‐1 RA users vs 8.5% of DPP‐4i users, corresponding to an HR of 0.68 (95% CI, 0.58–0.80).

Kaplan–Meier cumulative event‐free plots for all‐cause mortality. CI, confidence interval; DPP‐4i, dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitor; GLP‐1 RA, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonist.

Secondary outcomes of GLP‐1 RA use in patients with RA

Kaplan–Meier plots for secondary outcomes (Figure S3) indicated similar rates of MACE between GLP‐1 RA and DPP‐4i users (log‐rank P = 0.345). GLP‐1 RA use modestly reduced the risk of MAKE (P = 0.009) and significantly decreased all‐cause hospitalization (P = 0.013).

As shown in Table 2, MACE occurred in 27.13% of GLP‐1 RA users vs 30.15% of DPP‐4i users (HR 0.96; 95% CI, 0.89–1.04). MAKE occurred in 20.49% vs 24.79% of patients (HR 0.89; 95% CI, 0.82–0.97), and all‐cause hospitalization was reported in 34.21% vs 38.98% of patients (HR 0.92; 95% CI, 0.86–0.98). In prespecified specificity analyses, GLP‐1 RA use was associated with lower risks of end‐stage renal disease (HR 0.65; 95% CI, 0.51–0.83) and pneumonia (HR 0.87; 95% CI, 0.77–0.98). No significant differences were observed for acute myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke, emergency department visits, or sepsis.

Clinical application metrics for key outcomes

Table 3 demonstrated significant benefits across multiple outcomes over 4 years. All‐cause mortality showed progressive improvement with ARR increasing from 1.39% (NNT 71.9) in Year 1 to 2.92% (NNT 34.2) by Year 4, preventing approximately 29 events per 1,000 patients annually by Year 4. MAKE exhibited substantial early benefit with an ARR of 3.13% (NNT 32.0) in Year 1, though this effect attenuated to 1.34% by Year 4. All‐cause hospitalization demonstrated consistent reductions in Years 1–2 with ARRs around 2.9% (NNT approximately 34–35), but statistical significance was not maintained in later years. RMST differences indicated cumulative clinical benefits, with gains of 28.64 days for all‐cause mortality, 35 days for MAKE, and 36 days for all‐cause hospitalization by Year 4.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses comparing GLP‐1 RA and DPP‐4i users demonstrated that the mortality benefit of GLP‐1 RA (HR 0.68; 95% CI, 0.58–0.80) was generally consistent across key categories, including age ≥ 65 years, HbA1c ≥7%, BMI ≥30 kg/m2, and eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (Figure 3). Similar patterns were observed across most subgroups for MAKE (Figure S3) and all‐cause hospitalization (Figure S4). Notably, in patients with chronic kidney disease defined by baseline eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, GLP‐1 RA use was associated with a lower risk of major adverse kidney events (HR 0.87; 95% CI, 0.77–0.98; Figure S4) and a lower risk of all‐cause hospitalization (HR 0.83; 95% CI, 0.74–0.93; Figure S5). These findings indicate that the favorable associations of GLP‐1 RA use extended to patients with impaired kidney function. A directionally favorable effect for GLP‐1 RA use persisted across these subgroups, even in instances where the estimated HR did not reach statistical significance, suggesting broadly consistent benefits across clinically relevant patient strata.

Impact of calendar‐time confounding on outcomes

We analyzed three enrollment periods: 2016–2018 (n = 846), 2019–2021 (n = 1,495), and 2022–2023 (n = 731). The findings in Figure 5 show that GLP‐1 RAs consistently demonstrated beneficial effects, with mortality hazard ratios favoring them (0.68 to 0.73). Hospitalization risk reduction was highly significant in 2022–2023 (HR = 0.73, P < 0.001). Even with wider confidence intervals in the smaller cohort, estimates for key events like MAKE (HR = 0.79, P = 0.036) remained stable. These results indicate that clinical benefits persist despite changes in drug use and care standards.

Sensitivity analysis

Findings were robust across all prespecified sensitivity checks. Alternative postmatching specifications (Table S1) produced consistent estimates: all‐cause mortality remained lower with GLP‐1 RAs (HRs ~0.65–0.69), while MAKE and all‐cause hospitalization continued to favor GLP‐1 RAs. MACE remained near the null across models. Time‐segmented analyses revealed the greatest relative benefit within the first year, with attenuation but persistent significance through Years 2–3 (Table S2). An index‐lag analysis, delaying cohort entry by 3 months, yielded similar results (mortality HR 0.72), with kidney outcomes and hospitalization still favoring GLP‐1 RAs and specificity endpoints largely near unity (Table S3).

DISCUSSION

In this real‐world cohort of patients with rheumatoid arthritis and type 2 diabetes, GLP‐1 RA use was associated with significantly lower all‐cause mortality by 32% compared with DPP‐4is. GLP‐1 RA therapy was also linked to reduced risk of MAKE by 11% and all‐cause hospitalization by 8%, whereas rates of MACE were similar between groups. Specificity analyses further indicated lower risks of end‐stage renal disease by 35% and pneumonia by 11%, while outcomes such as acute myocardial infarction, stroke, and sepsis were unaffected. Subgroup analyses indicated that the mortality advantage of GLP‐1 RA was generally consistent across patient strata. This beneficial trend of GLP‐1 RA was also seen compared with other antidiabetic medications. Collectively, these findings suggest that GLP‐1 RA therapy may confer both survival and renal benefits in rheumatoid arthritis patients with type 2 diabetes, a population at particularly high cardiometabolic risk.

Our study indicated that GLP‐1 RA therapy provides superior long‐term outcomes compared with DPP‐4i, characterized by a cumulative mortality benefit that intensifies over 4 years. While the mortality NNT improved significantly by Year 4 (NNT 34.2), the benefits for MAKE and hospitalization were most pronounced early on, suggesting distinct temporal dynamics in treatment efficacy. These NNT values are clinically meaningful and support the cost‐effectiveness of GLP‐1 RAs in high‐risk populations. Furthermore, RMST difference analysis demonstrates that GLP‐1 RA therapy provides a consistent ‘survival gain’ of approximately 1 month (range: 28–36 days) across all primary outcomes. Unlike relative risk metrics, this absolute measure of event‐free time offers a clear, intuitive framework for clinicians to communicate treatment benefits to patients, facilitating more informed shared decision‐making.

Our findings extend evidence from landmark randomized controlled trials, including SUSTAIN‐6 and LEADER, which demonstrated that GLP‐1 RAs significantly reduce major renal outcomes in the general type 2 diabetes population 28 , 29 . Subsequent meta‐analyses and systematic reviews have confirmed that GLP‐1 RAs lower the risk of kidney‐specific composite outcomes by approximately 20%, notably slowing the progression of diabetic kidney disease and improving albuminuria 30 , 31 . Consistent with these observations, our subgroup analysis among patients with chronic kidney disease and coexisting rheumatoid arthritis showed that GLP‐1 RA use was associated with lower risks of major adverse kidney events and all‐cause hospitalization compared with DPP‐4 inhibitors, in line with recent real‐world findings 32 . Furthermore, GLP‐1 RAs have demonstrated renoprotective benefits even in patients with advanced chronic kidney disease and end‐stage renal disease, with favorable effects on glycemic control, weight reduction, and cardiovascular risk modulation that extend to kidney protection 33 .

GLP‐1 RAs exhibit renoprotective effects through multiple mechanisms. Recent research has identified the β‐Klotho protein as a key mediator: treatment with semaglutide activates the cAMP/PKA/CREB pathway, which upregulates β‐Klotho expression and promotes AMPK activation. This molecular cascade inhibits renal ferroptosis—an iron‐dependent form of cell death—while modulating iron metabolism, fatty acid synthesis, and antioxidant defenses, thereby reducing renal inflammation and fibrosis 34 , 35 . Additionally, liraglutide suppresses renal TRPC6 channel expression, decreasing calcium‐dependent NADPH oxidase activity and reactive oxygen species generation, which protects podocytes and mitigates proteinuria and nephron loss 36 . Beyond these direct cellular effects, GLP‐1 RAs exert systemic actions, including improved glycemic control, weight reduction, attenuation of insulin resistance, lower blood pressure, and favorable lipid modulation 37 , 38 . Collectively, these pleiotropic benefits reduce renal hemodynamic stress, sodium retention, and systemic inflammation, further contributing to kidney protection in patients with diabetes.

The absence of a significant reduction in MACE in our analysis warrants careful interpretation. Persistent systemic inflammation in rheumatoid arthritis promotes endothelial dysfunction, a major contributor to excess cardiovascular risk. Elevated levels of interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), and IL‐1β reduce endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) activity, decrease nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, and increase arterial stiffness 39 , 40 . Although GLP‐1 RAs enhance eNOS activity and improve endothelial function under metabolic stress 41 , these protective effects may be insufficient to restore vascular compliance in the context of chronic rheumatic inflammation. Concomitant rheumatoid arthritis therapies may also mitigate GLP‐1‐mediated cardiovascular protection. Long‐term glucocorticoid use at doses exceeding 5 mg/day prednisone equivalent is associated with increased MACE risk, reflecting both metabolic derangements and vascular toxicity 42 , 43 . Similarly, Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors carry boxed warnings for cardiovascular complications in high‐risk patients, suggesting that concurrent use may further obscure any GLP‐1‐associated benefit 44 .

Our study has several notable strengths. First, the use of a large, multi‐institutional database enabled inclusion of a substantial cohort of rheumatoid arthritis patients with type 2 diabetes—a population rarely represented in cardiovascular outcome trials—providing novel, real‐world evidence specific to this high‐risk group. Second, the application of a new‐user, active‐comparator design with PSM allowed direct comparison of GLP‐1 RAs with DPP‐4is, minimizing confounding and enhancing the validity of our head‐to‐head analysis. Third, we evaluated clinically meaningful endpoints, including MACE, renal outcomes, and mortality, which are highly relevant to rheumatoid arthritis patients with overlapping metabolic and inflammatory risk burdens.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, disease activity measures and cumulative exposure to glucocorticoids or DMARDs were not available in the TriNetX database, introducing potential residual confounding. Second, treatment adherence, persistence, dosing, and drug switching could not be assessed, which may have led to underestimation or overestimation of drug effects. Third, outcome definitions in this study relied on diagnosis and procedure codes provided by the TriNetX platform, without independent clinical adjudication, introducing the possibility of misclassification. Rheumatoid arthritis disease activity biomarkers, including C‐reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, rheumatoid factor, and anti‐cyclic citrullinated peptide, were not available in the TriNetX dataset. Therefore, changes in rheumatoid arthritis inflammatory activity after GLP‐1 RA vs DPP‐4i initiation could not be evaluated. Fourth, the relatively limited follow‐up of 4 years may not fully capture the long‐term trajectory of cardiorenal complications, which often develop over extended periods. Fifth, socioeconomic factors (e.g., education, income, insurance) and lifestyle behaviors were not captured in TriNetX, leaving potential residual confounding. Sixth, as an observational study, our findings demonstrate associations rather than causal relationships, underscoring the need for randomized controlled trials to confirm the benefits of GLP‐1 RAs in patients with rheumatoid arthritis and type 2 diabetes. Finally, TriNetX does not provide calendar‐time data, and since GLP‐1 RA adoption lagged DPP‐4i, earlier DPP‐4i enrolment may have exposed patients to less advanced background care, potentially inflating GLP‐1 RA benefits. However, propensity matching balanced baseline use of contemporary guideline‐directed therapies; both cohorts showed similar disease severity, our effect sizes align with randomized trials from similar periods, and the outcome‐specific pattern suggests drug‐specific mechanisms rather than temporal confounding. Nevertheless, residual calendar‐time bias cannot be entirely excluded.

Future research should prioritize prospective cohort studies and randomized trials to validate the mortality and clinical benefits of GLP‐1 RAs. Integration of disease‐specific registries that include rheumatoid arthritis activity measures, inflammatory biomarkers, and treatment adherence data would help clarify the interplay between systemic inflammation, glucose‐lowering therapies, and long‐term outcomes. Comparative effectiveness studies evaluating GLP‐1 RAs in combination with SGLT2i may also be warranted, given the potential for additive cardiovascular and renal benefits.

In patients with rheumatoid arthritis and type 2 diabetes, GLP‐1 RA use was associated with lower all‐cause mortality, reduced risk of MAKE, and fewer hospitalizations compared with DPP‐4i use. These findings highlight GLP‐1 RAs as a promising therapeutic option for this high‐risk population and support their consideration in integrated management strategies for patients with coexisting autoimmune and metabolic disease.
