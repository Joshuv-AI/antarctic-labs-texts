Narcolepsy 2025 (with an Australasian perspective)
Authors: Amelia Lin, Brendon J Yee, Jian Eu Tai, Sheila Sivam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNarcolepsy is a disorder of central hypersomnolence, which, while uncommon, can carry profound implications for affected individuals. Despite this, it remains under‐recognised within the medical community and presents frequent diagnostic and treatment challenges in the Australasian setting. Narcolepsy is subdivided into narcolepsy type 1, characterised by cataplexy and deficiency of the neuropeptide orexin, and narcolepsy type 2, a distinct entity. The latter can be further difficult to distinguish from the separate central hypersomnolence disorder idiopathic hypersomnia. Implementation of diagnostic criteria to distinguish these conditions is not always straightforward, however, with consideration of comorbidities and test conditions required to avoid misdiagnoses. Within Australia and New Zealand, narcolepsy remains relatively ill‐defined on a population level compared to other developed nations, and greater challenges exist in access to diagnostic testing and therapeutic options. Internationally, orexin receptor 2 agonists have displayed promise as oral therapy to target the neurobiological basis of narcolepsy, particularly narcolepsy type 1, with further testing underway. We discuss practical challenges and updates in narcolepsy from an Australasian perspective, with a view to improve awareness of narcolepsy in both primary and specialty medicine to support access to care for individuals with the condition.Keywords: narcolepsy, hypersomnolence, cataplexy, orexin

Introduction

Narcolepsy is a disorder of central hypersomnolence, characterised by excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) and associated with cataplexy, hypnogogic and hypnopompic hallucinations, sleep paralysis and disrupted sleep, to varying degrees. It is uncommon both in primary and specialty practice, but the related symptom burden and functional deficits can be profound. Much of what we understand about narcolepsy has only arisen in recent decades, including the discovery of the neuropeptide orexin in regulating arousal and visceral functions, and its deficiency in narcolepsy type 1 (NT1). 1

Diagnosis requires fulfilment of clinical and polysomnographic or biochemical criteria to distinguish NT1 from narcolepsy type 2 (NT2). Accurate diagnosis is important, given that the natural history and future treatments (such as orexin receptor 2 (OX2R) agonist dosing) can differ between narcolepsy subtypes. 2 However, local implementation of the diagnostic criteria, including access to and interpretation of testing, is not always straightforward; neither is the subsequent navigation of management guidelines. This article reviews the current landscape of narcolepsy in Australasia, in particular, its diagnostic challenges, established and emerging treatment options and the role of orexin as a diagnostic and treatment target in research.

Epidemiology

Narcolepsy remains a rare condition that is incompletely understood and suspected to be under‐diagnosed across the world. 3 It has an estimated prevalence of 200 to 500 per million in Europe and North America, and a higher rate in Japan of 1 600 per million. 4 Rates of the condition in males and females are similar, and there is a bimodal distribution of disease onset, with a peak typically during adolescence and a second peak in the third decade of age, while a small proportion of individuals are diagnosed in childhood. 4 , 5 , 6 NT1 is estimated to have a lower prevalence (12.6 per 100 000) compared to that of NT2 (25.1 per 100 000). 5

Precise epidemiological data related to narcolepsy in Australasia are limited. However, in an Australian study conducted during 2019 to 2021, the mean age at diagnosis for NT1 (21.6 years) was lower compared to NT2 (28.9 years), with a slightly higher male preponderance in NT2. 7 There remains no Australasian data registry for narcolepsy, though a consumer‐driven registry is available, with most participants having idiopathic hypersomnia (IH). 8 , 9 A single hypersomnolence registry for Australasia would allow better assessment of the comorbidities and burden of disease associated with narcolepsy and IH, disease prevalence and impact of existing and future treatments.

Pathophysiology

The main development in our understanding of narcolepsy pathophysiology was the 1998 discovery of the neuropeptide orexin, or hypocretin, and subsequently, its deficiency in NT1. 4 , 10 Neurons in the lateral hypothalamus project to the brain and spinal cord, producing orexin A (hypocretin‐1) and orexin B (hypocretin‐2), which act on orexin receptors OX1R and OX2R on postsynaptic neurons. 10 Orexin signalling plays a crucial role in wakefulness and arousal regulation and maintenance of sleep states, particularly rapid eye movement (REM) sleep. 10 It is also implicated in pathways for reward processing, motivation and metabolism. 10 REM sleep constitutes a characteristic stage of deep sleep featuring dreaming and atonia, normally occurring after appropriate progression through preceding sleep stages. Neurotransmitters other than orexin, such as histamine, have also been proposed to have a role in narcolepsy pathogenesis, but their roles remain less clear compared to orexin, which is proposed to act as the ‘conductor’ or main coordinator of wakefulness via several downstream sleep and wake neurotransmitters. 4

Over the years, NT1 has been linked to the selective loss of orexigenic neurons, which can occur at variable rates. 10 The resultant orexin deficiency is postulated to lead to a destabilisation of sleep states, such that abnormally rapid transitions between wakefulness and sleep occur. 4 Clinical features of NT1 are thought to be manifestations of this dysregulated sleep physiology; most notably, cataplexy is proposed to represent inappropriate REM sleep atonia intrusion into wakefulness, while cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) orexin levels and loss of orexigenic neurons are thought to correlate with the severity of cataplexy and EDS. 4 Indeed, orexin‐producing neurons in the lateral hypothalamus can be greatly reduced by up to 85% to 95% in people with NT1. 11 There is speculation that NT1 and NT2 may exist on a continuum, with NT2 representing milder disease featuring partial loss of orexigenic neurons. 4 This association presently remains undefined, and NT2 and IH remain conceptually different and less understood clinical entities compared to NT1.

Autoimmune hypothesis

Reasons for the loss of orexin‐producing neurons in NT1 remain speculative, though an autoimmune process has been suggested. T cells reactive to orexin have been particularly observed in NT1, though their role in disease pathogenesis remain unclear. 10 Observed increased NT1 rates following certain influenza vaccinations and H1N1 infections have also been considered as lending support to an autoimmune aetiology for NT1. 10

Genetic associations

The HLA DQB1*0602 allele is most strongly linked to narcolepsy, associated with a 200‐fold increased risk for NT1 and a greater than 90% prevalence in people with this condition. 10 It is poorly discriminative, however, being also present in about 40% to 50% of individuals with NT2, and up to 38% of the general population. 4 Despite this, it is estimated to have a high negative predictive value of greater than 99% for NT1. 12 The allele prevalence suggests a distinction between narcolepsy subtypes and IH, with HLA DQB1*0602 having a high reported positive likelihood ratio (LR+) and low negative likelihood ratio (LR−) in NT1 (LR+ 5.94, LR− 0.23), and weaker associations with NT2 (LR+ 3.35, LR− 0.72) and hypersomnia without narcolepsy (LR+ 1.436, LR− 0.903). 13 The monozygotic twin concordance rate of narcolepsy is only approximately 25%, suggesting that further factors are at play in shaping a diagnosis beyond genetics. 4

Associated comorbidities

Narcolepsy symptoms often overlap with those seen in other medical and neuropsychiatric comorbidities, which themselves can also frequently co‐exist with narcolepsy. Mental health and behavioural disorders – including depression, anxiety and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) – are leading comorbidities in narcolepsy, followed by endocrine, nutritional and metabolic disorders. 14 Rates of overweight, obesity and diabetes are higher in the narcolepsy population than the general population. 4 Other sleep disorders can also co‐exist with narcolepsy, including obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), restless legs syndrome and REM sleep behaviour disorder. 3 , 4 These trends are reflected in Australia, with depression observed to be prevalent among central disorders of hypersomnolence, and obesity more prevalent in NT1, while rates of OSA are comparatively lower in patients with NT1 than in NT2 and IH. 7

Symptoms in narcolepsy are frequently shared by these comorbidities. EDS, the prototypical feature of narcolepsy, carries a wide range of differential diagnoses including sleep deprivation, circadian rhythm disorders and shift work, as well as OSA and psychiatric illnesses. 4 It is important for practitioners to have an open mind not only to consider narcolepsy as a potential diagnosis when assessing patients with sleepiness but also, conversely, when assessing patients with narcolepsy, to be aware that many medical and neuropsychiatric conditions can be both differential and comorbid diagnoses.

Patient experiences

Just as there is variability in clinical presentations of narcolepsy, there is heterogeneity in individual patient experiences of the condition. Results from a recent Australian study found that individuals with narcolepsy often gauged disease severity by functional impairment rather than symptom severity, yet there tends to be a greater focus on the latter in the assessment of trial outcomes. 15 Patient interpretations of medical language may also not necessarily align with intended medical usage, and patients may use the term ‘cataplexy’ to describe experiences outside the formal medical definition. 15 Additionally, while much of the discussion around narcolepsy focuses on the impacts of the classical symptoms, the perceived societal stigma associated with narcolepsy has been identified as a common fear for affected individuals who anticipate discrimination. 15 The patient experience of narcolepsy, therefore, extends beyond the physical manifestations of the condition, with social and psychological impacts, which should not be overlooked.

Diagnosis

Under the International Classification of Sleep Disorders, Third Edition text revision (ICSD‐3‐TR), a diagnosis of narcolepsy requires the presence of persistent EDS and a diagnostic multiple sleep latency test (MSLT) or a CSF orexin level meeting criteria (Table 2). 16 An MSLT constitutes an overnight polysomnogram and typically five nap opportunities during the day. 16

NT1 is distinguished from NT2 by the presence of either cataplexy or low CSF orexin in NT1 (Table 2). 16 Distinguishing between NT1 and NT2 is important given that the natural history differs between the subtypes, with NT1 being a life‐long condition, whereas narcolepsy without cataplexy has the potential to remit. 4 Updated ICSD‐3‐TR criteria now allow an NT1 diagnosis to be made where clear cataplexy is present and a sleep‐onset REM period (SOREMP) on nocturnal polysomnography is recorded, without necessarily requiring a mean sleep latency of 8 min or less on an MSLT. 16 However, identifying true cataplexy can be challenging as some presentations can be atypical (Table 1). 6 , 17

It is also important to note that an MSLT does not in itself distinguish between EDS from narcolepsy and that from other conditions, and alternative causes for a reduced sleep latency – such as sleep deprivation, medication usage and shift work – should be considered and appropriately excluded to maintain study validity. 3 , 4 A small proportion of the general population without narcolepsy also can meet MSLT criteria, and MSLT results may also change over time; abnormalities typically persist in NT1, but can potentially improve over time in non‐NT1 hypersomnolence disorders. 4 The test–retest reliability of the MSLT in the non‐cataplectic disorders of hypersomnolence is poorer compared to those with NT1. 18 Antidepressants can also potentially confound MSLT study results by suppressing REM sleep, and hence SOREMPs, but may not be practical to withhold in view of patient comorbidities. 14 An MSLT therefore requires nuanced interpretation and cannot be used in isolation for diagnosis, illustrating the need for further diagnostic tests including future consideration and reimbursement for the use of CSF orexin testing for assessment of NT1 where atypical features or results are present.

CSF orexin testing has become available for Australians in recent years as a diagnostic option in narcolepsy work‐up, but challenges remain; first, it requires a lumbar puncture; second, the sample is sent overseas via select local pathology companies; and third, testing is not covered by Medicare and incurs out‐of‐pocket fees for the patient. 19 The test has the advantage of being able definitively to diagnose NT1 with a single test, differentiate NT1 from NT2 where cataplexy has not been documented and can be valuable where a false‐negative MSLT is suspected. However, current logistical and financial constraints can present barriers for patients and their clinicians in diagnosing narcolepsy in Australia. Consideration of subsidised orexin testing and strategies to improve accessibility to sleep testing would therefore be valuable in supporting an appropriate and timely diagnosis of NT1 and NT2.

As noted earlier, the HLA‐DQB1*0602 allele is associated with narcolepsy, but its utility is limited in making a formal diagnosis. This test does not make up part of the ICSD diagnostic criteria for narcolepsy, though its high negative predictive value in NT1 may be useful to sway against such a diagnosis where the presentation is atypical. 6

Diagnostic challenges

Delayed diagnoses of narcolepsy, or, conversely, inaccurate diagnoses of narcolepsy, are frequent and can have adverse consequences for affected individuals. First, a delay from symptom onset to narcolepsy diagnosis has been well recognised for decades in studies around the world. 3 Median durations from symptom onset to diagnosis of narcolepsy have been estimated to be 6.0 to 10.5 years. 3 Factors implicated in this diagnostic delay include physician unfamiliarity with the classical features of narcolepsy in primary and specialist care settings, as well as symptom overlap between narcolepsy and other sleep disorders such as OSA and neuropsychiatric conditions. 3 , 20 A New Zealand study found that patients diagnosed with narcolepsy often only received a diagnosis after visiting multiple doctors, often across different specialties. 21 Less than half the patients in this study underwent a formal MSLT as part of their diagnostic work‐up, though this was felt to reflect diagnoses made prior to established diagnostic criteria. 21 Delays in obtaining a timely MSLT can also present a further challenge to diagnosis.

Improving accurate and timely diagnoses of narcolepsy is important to support individual access to appropriate treatment, particularly given the typical onset of the disease during young adulthood when education, workforce participation and family planning are particularly relevant. Aside from the functional benefits of treatment itself, an accurate diagnosis provides answers and validation for people with narcolepsy, with reported improvements in individual experiences of anxiety and criticism from family and employers following receipt of a diagnosis. 22 The socioeconomic impact of narcolepsy has also been suggested to be identifiable around a decade prior to formal diagnosis, with increased rates of healthcare contact and medication usage, and lower employment rates for affected individuals. 23

It is also important to consider the potential for misdiagnosis where a narcolepsy diagnosis is incorrectly applied. A United Kingdom study found that half of the patients in their sample population referred with narcolepsy did not have the condition when formally assessed, implying the potential for patients to be incorrectly managed for a chronic illness while potentially having their underlying condition inadequately treated. 24 We do not so far have comparable data in Australasia regarding rates of narcolepsy diagnoses and misdiagnoses.

A diagnosis of narcolepsy can have life‐changing implications but may not always be straightforward. Making a diagnosis requires not just knowledge of diagnostic pathways but also individual context and testing availabilities.

Treatment

The mainstay of treatment for narcolepsy so far has been pharmacotherapy to mitigate symptoms, supported by adjunctive non‐pharmacological strategies. Accessible treatment options in Australia remain decidedly limited compared to other regions with first‐class health systems. 8 Many medications used for narcolepsy treatment carry potential abuse potential and are frequently closely regulated. However, orexin agonists are emerging therapies displaying promise in clinical trials as a way to target the postulated basis of NT1 and are currently also being investigated in hypersomnolence without defined orexin deficiency, such as NT2 and IH. 25

Nonpharmacological therapy

Nonpharmacological strategies to improve symptoms in narcolepsy include a regular sleep routine and scheduled short daytime naps to manage EDS. 6 Psychological support where appropriate, regular exercise and a balanced diet are advised. 4 There is limited evidence for specific dietary changes, though a ketogenic diet has been previously linked to modest symptomatic improvement in patients with narcolepsy already on therapy. 26 Cognitive and behavioural strategies and psychological support are often an adjunctive treatment, though evidence in the area is limited. 27 , 28 Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) for hypersomnia (a counterpoint to CBT for insomnia) has been suggested to improve depressive symptoms and improve function in individuals with hypersomnolence disorders. 29 Additionally, there are consumer support groups available that can be valuable resources for practitioners to direct their patients for information and support. 9

Pharmacotherapy

Pharmacological treatment remains the cornerstone of narcolepsy management, and medication selection depends on individual symptom profile, comorbidities and preferences. 27 Accessible therapeutic options remain limited, and treatment guidelines differ in Australia compared to the rest of the world (Table 3). 8 European and American Academy of Sleep Medicine guidelines favour modafinil as first‐line treatment for EDS‐predominant narcolepsy, with evidence considered also strong for use of pitolisant and solriamfetol, 27 , 28 although the latter two agents remain unavailable in Australia (Table 3). As previously observed, although Australian guidelines recommend use of modafinil and armodafinil for narcolepsy treatment, with dexamphetamine second‐line, access to modafinil and armodafinil on the Australian Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) requires a contraindication to dexamphetamine, rendering dexamphetamine effectively first‐line subsidised treatment despite its less‐favoured status overseas. 8 , 30 In New Zealand, modafinil, dexamphetamine and methylphenidate may be accessed for narcolepsy via Special Authority applications to the Pharmaceutical Management Agency (Pharmac). 31 Although lisdexamfetamine has been associated with symptomatic improvement in narcolepsy, it presently is only publicly funded in Australia and New Zealand for ADHD. 31 , 32 , 33

Pregnancy considerations

The typical onset of narcolepsy in young adulthood renders pregnancy a particularly relevant consideration in treatment, usually requiring significant management changes. Data so far encouragingly suggest that pregnancy outcomes in women with narcolepsy are comparable to those in the general population. 36

Most pharmacological options for narcolepsy management feature either known teratogenicity or have an uncertain safety profile in pregnancy, and, as such, European guidelines strongly recommend discontinuation of medications for narcolepsy prior to pregnancy, acknowledging that symptoms are likely to deteriorate. 27 These guidelines suggest limiting treatment to monotherapy if total medication cessation is not practicable. 27 Modafinil and armodafinil, in particular, are associated with higher rates of congenital anomalies, as well as decreased effectiveness of the oral contraceptive pill. 28 A recent Australian study in the ADHD population suggested that continued use of dexamphetamine in pregnancy in this cohort was not associated with increased adverse neonatal and maternal health outcomes, though there appeared to be lower rates of complications in the group without exposure to the medication in pregnancy. 37 Current Australian pregnancy safety categories for narcolepsy medications are provided in Table 3 and overall reflect international concerns regarding their use in pregnancy.

Results from European and American surveys of female patients with narcolepsy found that most did not use medications during pregnancy. 36 , 38 Despite cessation of medications in pregnancy, the frequency of sleep attacks may not significantly change, and symptoms in NT1 have been in fact noted to be milder during pregnancy. 27 , 36 Patients also frequently feel dissatisfied with counselling received in relation to pregnancy and contraception in narcolepsy, and this should be an area to improve in practice. 38

Orexins and other emerging treatment

While all pharmacological treatments so far target symptomatic manifestations of narcolepsy, OX2R agonists, currently undergoing clinical trials, are displaying promise as a potential means of directly targeting the neurobiological pathway for the disease. The oral OX2R agonist TAK‐994 has demonstrated dose‐dependent improvements in sleepiness and cataplexy frequency, as well as marked improvements in sleep latencies on maintenance of wakefulness test (MWT) results on treatment 39 — an MWT assesses a patient's ability to remain awake in a series of daytime trials. 40 The trial was terminated early due to hepatotoxicity but presented an encouraging new treatment pathway for development. 39 Further research has shown marked clinical benefits associated with the OX2R agonist oveporexton in reducing sleepiness and cataplexy frequency while improving MWT results in NT1. 41 More clinical trials are underway, including in Australia, exploring new orexin molecules in narcolepsy and IH with promising results in phase 1 and/or 2 studies. 25 , 42

Given theories of an autoimmune basis for narcolepsy, there has been an interest in immunotherapy as a potential treatment option (intravenous immunoglobulin, monoclonal antibodies, steroids and plasmapheresis), but evidence in this area is limited and suggests potential risk with limited benefits thus far. 27

Conclusion

Narcolepsy is a chronic condition that is rare but can carry profound lifelong implications for affected individuals. We make a few observations regarding the clinical landscape of narcolepsy in Australasia. First, an accurate diagnosis to characterise subtypes of narcolepsy is crucial to support individual access to appropriate care and prevent misdiagnoses, and this requires awareness of the condition at the level of the individual practitioner. Diagnosis can be complicated by clinical overlap between narcolepsy and other conditions that can frequently also co‐exist, as well as challenges in accessing and interpreting testing. Second, discrepancies persist between treatment guidelines and accessible therapies in Australasia. Treatment for narcolepsy worldwide continues to rely mainly on symptom control, with effective though imperfect options, and these remain comparatively more limited in Australasia. Third, targets for narcolepsy treatment are evolving, and oral OX2R agonists are emerging as promising agents in current clinical trials to address directly the underlying cause of narcolepsy.

Our collective understanding of narcolepsy has changed markedly in recent decades, and continues to do so. While there remain many challenges in making an accurate diagnosis of narcolepsy, and there remains frustration in relation to limited treatment options in Australasia, improving knowledge of narcolepsy and removing some of the obscurity surrounding it as a rare sleep disorder is an important step in advancing care for individuals with the condition.
