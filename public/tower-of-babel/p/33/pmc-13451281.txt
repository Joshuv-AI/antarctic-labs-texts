Post-fire lidar dataset and 3D reconstruction of Las Médulas World Heritage Cultural Landscape (NW Spain)
Authors: Gabriel Navarro, Juan Carlos García Davalillo, Juan Luis Pecharroman, Antonio Tovar-Sánchez, Juan Carlos Gumiel, Javier Madrigal, Inés Galindo, Alejandro Román
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obtaining high-resolution spatial data to monitor post-fire impacts in environmentally and historically sensitive areas is essential for both heritage conservation and environmental assessment. The “Las Médulas Post-Fire lidar dataset” was acquired in September and November 2025, immediately after the wildfire that affected this UNESCO World Heritage Cultural Landscape in northwestern Spain. Las Médulas, the largest Roman gold-mining complex in the ancient world, is characterized by extensive hydraulic infrastructures and a distinctive geomorphology shaped by the ruina montium technique. The dataset includes UAV-based lidar point clouds and derived topographic products such as Digital Elevation Models (DEMs), Digital Surface Models (DSMs) and RGB orthomosaics, suitable for 3D reconstruction of the archaeological and natural landscape. These products document post-fire terrain conditions, vegetation loss and erosional patterns, while preserving fine-scale mining landforms. All files are openly accessible in a structured repository, providing a reproducible resource for research on cultural heritage management, ecological recovery, geomorphology and risk assessment in Mediterranean mountain environments.

Background & Summary

Las Médulas Cultural Landscape (Fig. 1), located in the province of León (northwestern Spain), represents the largest and most remarkable Roman gold-mining complex in the ancient world1–10. The long-term human–environment interaction at Las Médulas provides essential context, but the focus of this dataset is the urgent need to document post-fire topography and vegetation loss after the 2025 wildfire. Prior to our work, no high-resolution lidar dataset existed for immediate post-disturbance monitoring in this UNESCO site. In August 2025, the region was severely affected by a large wildfire that burned significant portions of the surrounding forests and mountainous slopes11. The fire originated in the western sector of the Aquilianos Mount (9th August, 2025 in the Yeres municipality), and rapidly spread toward the archaeological core of Las Médulas, affecting large areas of the protected area, designated as a Cultural Heritage Site (from the Spanish Bien de Interés Cultural, BIC12) (area burned: 1132 ha, 71% of protected area). The event posed a double threat in the short term: to the biodiversity and ecological integrity of the area, and to the preservation of all the elements that create this unique mining archaeological landscape, which are highly sensitive to erosion and slope instability following vegetation loss.

The emergency response highlighted the urgent need to generate detailed spatial data to assess post-fire damage, quantify morphological changes, and support restoration and conservation strategies in coordination with heritage authorities and environmental agencies. In response, the Fundación Las Médulas activated the Scientific-Technical Advisory Group for Emergency Crises (GADE) of the Spanish National Research Council (CSIC) to support the emergency assessment. The GADE-CSIC team deployed a rapid scientific response operation focused on obtaining precise spatial data and evaluating the post-fire impact on both the natural and cultural components of the site. Here, we present “Las Médulas Post-Fire lidar dataset”13, a high-resolution UAV-based topographic record acquired during a few days in September and November 2025, immediately after the wildfire event. The dataset was obtained using the DJI Matrice 300 RTK, DJI Matrice 350 RTK and DJI M400 RTK platforms, equipped with the DJI Zenmuse L1 and L2 lidar sensors. A total of 37 flight missions (Fig. 2) were carried out jointly by three different research teams from ICMAN-CSIC, IGME-CSIC and IH-CSIC. Methods

Site description

Las Médulas Cultural Landscape is located in El Bierzo region, province of León, in northwestern Spain (Fig. 1). The inscribed UNESCO property comprises a core mining zone and surrounding spoil deposits1–3. The site lies at the western foothills of the Teleno Mountains (Montes de León), near the watershed of the Sil River.

Las Médulas stands as the most significant example of Roman hydraulic mining in the Iberian Peninsula. Starting in the 1st century AD after completing the conquest of the Iberian Peninsula, Romans exploited auriferous conglomerates by employing hydraulic means. This led to a landscape shaped by the force of water, of which the most remarkable is the central mining area where the ruina montium technique was channelled through an extensive network of galleries to collapse or erode mountain interiors, thereby releasing gold-bearing sediments1. Over two centuries of mining work, during which about 4000 kg of gold were extracted, and more than 600 km of hydraulic conduit structures, including channels, tunnels and reservoirs, were constructed to capture and transport water from catchments2. The cumulative movement of materials was enormous, creating the characteristic red badlands, ridges, gullies and terraces that define its current landscape. After mining ceased, around the second century AD, the area underwent a long process of ecological succession and stabilization, during which vegetation (principally oak, shrubs and the chestnut introduced by Romans to feed the population) recolonized much of the territory, generating the distinctive interplay between geological forms and living systems that defines Las Médulas as a cultural landscape1–10.

For the present study, lidar acquisition was primarily concentrated within the officially protected area of Las Médulas, designated as a Cultural Heritage Site (BIC12) under Spanish heritage law. The site is legally protected as well as a Natural Monument and designated within the Natura 2000 network (Special Protection Area for Birds) for its biodiversity and habitat value2. The Fundación Las Médulas, a public institution dependent on the regional government, in collaboration with regional authorities, handles visitor management, conservation efforts, and research activities at the site. The survey covered the most affected sectors by the August 2025 wildfire: the Orellán, Yeres, and Carucedo catchment zones. These areas include critical archaeological features (such as former galleries, terraced spoil deposits and canal remains) and zones particularly vulnerable to erosion, slope instability and morphological alteration after vegetation loss, all operating at spatial scales that require sub-metric resolution beyond the capability of satellite sensors routinely used in post-fire emergency mapping14,15, such as Sentinel-2 (10–20 m), SPOT (1.5–6 m) or Pléiades (0.5 m), whose pixel size precludes the detection of fine-scale archaeological structures and individual tree-crown assessment16.

UAV Data acquisition

Data acquisition was conducted across a total of 37 UAV flight missions (Fig. 3), using a grid flight plan at an average altitude of 120 m above ground level (terrain-following mode). The raw lidar point clouds were processed using DJI Terra v.5.1.0, applying a standardised workflow to generate point clouds, Digital Elevation Models (DEMs), Digital Surface Models (DSMs), and 3D meshes. RGB imagery was processed using Agisoft Metashape v.2.2.1 to generate Digital Orthomosaic Models (DOMs). It should be noted that flight parameters were optimised for lidar acquisition rather than photogrammetric RGB capture; since the RGB imagery was acquired by the integrated camera of the DJI Zenmuse L1/L2 lidar sensor, Agisoft Metashape was preferred over DJI Terra for DOM generation, as it provides more robust image mosaicking performance under the suboptimal overlap conditions inherent to lidar-optimised flight planning. These products allowed for accurate visualisation and quantification of post-fire topographic and vegetative changes, as well as the identification of erosional processes threatening archaeological and ecological features. All flights were executed in RTK (Real-Time Kinematic) position mode, using correction data from the CERCANA3 GNSS network, managed by the National Geographic Institute (IGN, Spain). In one specific mission (Flight ID #9), temporary network instability prevented real-time correction; this flight was therefore post-processed using a PPK (Post-Processed Kinematic) workflow to ensure consistent centimetre-level georeferencing accuracy across the full dataset. No additional Ground Control Points (GCPs) were deployed, as RTK/PPK positioning guaranteed sufficient absolute accuracy. This dual-campaign operation provided a comprehensive and spatially consistent lidar dataset that captures both the immediate post-fire conditions and later landscape stabilization phases, forming a unique topographic baseline for long-term monitoring.

The M300/M350 and M400 quadcopters have a maximum take-off weight of approximately 9 kg and 12 kg, respectively, and a flight autonomy of 30–40 minutes under standard payload conditions, which varies according to weather conditions (i.e. wind, temperature, etc.). Their operational specifications (wind resistance up to 15 m s−1 and temperature range from −20 °C to 40 °C) allowed stable performance in the variable terrain and topographic conditions found at Las Médulas.

The DJI Zenmuse L2 operates at a pulse repetition rate of 240 kHz, supports up to five returns per pulse, and achieves a detection range from 2 cm to 450 m at 50% reflectivity. It integrates a high-precision IMU (200 Hz) and a 20 MP RGB camera with a 4/3 CMOS sensor and mechanical shutter (1/2000 s to 1/8000 s). The Zenmuse L1 offers similar specifications with an equivalent detection range (450 m) and a 20 MP CMOS RGB sensor stabilized on a 3-axis gimbal, though it supports up to three returns per pulse compared to five for the L2. According to manufacturer data, both lidar systems achieve vertical accuracies of ~5 cm and horizontal accuracies of ~10 cm under optimal conditions, with IMU orientation precision of 0.025° (roll/pitch) and 0.15° (yaw).

Lidar processing and data structuring

The UAV-acquired lidar data was processed using DJI Terra (v.5.1.0) and Agisoft Metashape (v2.2.1) software. All raw flight files, including positional and attitude metadata, were imported into the software to generate georeferenced 3D point clouds (compressed LAZ format), as well as the derived DEMs, DSMs, and DOMs. Each individual flight (Figs. 2, 3) was first processed independently to ensure accurate georeferencing and correct positional data integration. RTK georeferencing was applied to all missions except for one flight where adverse connectivity conditions necessitated the use of a PPK workflow to refine point-level positioning, taking advantage of control stations established in areas with optimal satellite coverage. All final topographic products were processed and delivered in the ETRS89/UTM Zone 29 N projection (EPSG:25829) and referenced vertically to the Alicante Height datum (EPSG:5782). The DEM was generated at 0.5 m spatial resolution, corresponding to the 1:500 scale option, the maximum resolution available in DJI Terra for lidar-derived terrain products and is fully supported by the measured lidar point density (249 ± 76 points/m²). During processing, the Ground Point Classification function was enabled, resulting in a bare-earth terrain product functionally equivalent to a Digital Terrain Model (DTM). The DSM was produced at 0.0675 ± 0.0035 m resolution, determined automatically by DJI Terra based on the mean flight altitude and lidar point density. Strip alignment was validated and corrected using DJI Terra’s automatic adjustment function, with visual review of all overlaps. The following settings were used: Tie-point density: High; Strip fitting threshold: 0.04 m; Iterative refinement steps: 5. These parameters optimised alignment consistency across both RTK and PPK-derived flights.

Following the generation of these primary products, we implemented a post-processing and tiling workflow to facilitate data handling and open dissemination. All lidar-derived layers (3D model, DEM, DSM, and DOM) were spatially subdivided into regular tiles using the Python programming language. This approach ensured standardised spatial indexing and improved accessibility. The DEM and DSM products were segmented into 2 × 2 km tiles, while the LAZ and DOM datasets were divided into 1 × 1 km tiles to preserve fine-scale detail (Fig. 4). Data Records

“Las Médulas Post-Fire lidar dataset” is openly available for download from the DIGITAL.CSIC repository13. The processed products folder contains the final topographic outputs derived from the lidar processing workflow in DJI Terra software for 3D point clouds, DEMs and DSMs. The DOMs were processed using Agisoft Metashape from the RGB imagery captured by the integrated camera of the DJI Zenmuse L1/L2 lidar sensor. These include: (i) DEMs and DSMs, both provided as GeoTIFF (.tif) rasters segmented into 2 × 2 km tiles (Fig. 5), (ii) true-colour DOMs generated from the RGB imagery captures, available as GeoTIFF (.tif) files with 1 × 1 km tiling (Fig. 5), and (iii) lidar point clouds (LAZ format) subdivided into 1 × 1 km tiles (Fig. 6), preserving the original point density and classification levels (ground, vegetation, unclassified). The filename convention (Product_LasMedulas_Tile_NameOfTile.ext) explicitly links each topographic product to its corresponding spatial tile, allowing users to easily identify the data type and geographic extent covered by every file. The full repository contains approximately 121 GB of LAZ point-cloud tiles, 33 GB of DEM and DSM products, and 119 GB of RGB orthomosaics. Technical Validation

We conducted a comprehensive technical validation of Las Médulas Post-Fire lidar dataset to ensure positional accuracy, internal consistency, and data reliability across all missions and products (Fig. 7). Validation was conducted at three levels: (i) assessment of the lidar point cloud quality, (ii) evaluation of georeferencing precision through RTK/PPK control, and (iii) visual and statistical verification of derived topographic products (DEM, DSM, and DOM) for each tile. The internal quality of the point clouds was assessed through density, overlap, and classification accuracy. Mean point density was 249 ± 76 points/m², (Fig. 7b), with local variations associated with canopy openings or steep slopes. RTK positioning performed exceptionally well, achieving a 100% FIX ratio for the vast majority of flights, ensuring consistent centimetre-level georeferencing (Fig. 7c). IMU trajectory errors stayed below 0.7 cm, indicating strong IMU-GNSS consistency and confirming stable flight dynamics, correct calibration, low vibration, and reliable georeferencing performance (Fig. 7d). Lateral overlap for lidar strips was approximately 20%, while RGB imagery achieved ~37% lateral overlap and ~70% forward overlap, ensuring sufficient redundancy for error minimisation during strip adjustment. Raster derivatives (DEMs and DSMs) generated in DJI Terra were visually compared against shaded-relief models and DOMs generated with Agisoft Metashape to detect inconsistencies. Quantitative assessment of the spatial alignment between lidar-derived products and DOMs confirmed very high geometric coherence, with mean positional differences of 4.55 ± 1.78 cm across the survey area, consistent with the centimetre-level RTK georeferencing accuracy specifications of the DJI Zenmuse L1/L2 sensors and well within the accuracy range reported by DJI Terra for RTK-positioned acquisitions. Usage Notes

Software

Although commercial software packages are available for visualising and post-processing of lidar and photogrammetric data, it is recommended to use open-source alternatives to ensure full accessibility and reproducibility. For visualisation and analysis of DOMs and topographic models (DEM/DSM), QGIS and SAGA GIS are recommended. For exploring and editing lidar point clouds (compressed LAZ format), the open-source applications CloudCompare and LAStools are particularly suitable. These tools allow visualisation of 3D structures, filtering of point classes, and surface generation with centimetric precision (Fig. 6).

Dataset application

Beyond documenting the immediate post-fire landscape conditions of Las Médulas, this dataset offers broad potential for research and management. In terms of site management, it provides a valuable resource for the entities responsible for the stewardship of the BIC, including the Fundación Las Médulas and associated heritage and environmental authorities (UNESCO), supporting decision-making in conservation planning, risk assessment, restoration actions and long-term monitoring (Autonomous Region of Castilla y León and Municipalities). The high-resolution lidar and RGB-derived products enable monitoring of morphological and ecological changes in a UNESCO World Heritage Cultural Landscape at spatial scales unattainable by satellite sensors, while supporting risk assessment and restoration planning following wildfire disturbance. It is worth noting that the primary value of this dataset is not change detection per se, but rather the establishment of a high-resolution post-fire baseline, an irreplaceable spatial record that no satellite archive can provide at this level of detail, and which will serve as the reference for all future monitoring campaigns at this site. The practical value of this dataset has already been recognised at the highest management level: the Post-Disaster State of Conservation Report submitted to the UNESCO World Heritage Centre17 explicitly identifies the lidar data acquired by the GADE-CSIC team as a key input for the potential revision of the UNESCO protection polygon, including the possible incorporation of newly observable archaeological elements identified from the post-fire lidar survey, and as the spatial baseline for the monitoring programme established at 6, 12 and 24 months within the Post-Disaster Recovery Plan approved by the regional government.

Specifically, the dataset can be applied to geological and archaeological heritage monitoring and conservation, enabling precise 3D reconstruction and volumetric change detection of ancient Roman mining structures, spoil heaps, terrain movements and canal systems. It also supports environmental and ecological restoration, through quantification of vegetation loss, soil erosion, and natural regeneration dynamics. In this context, the chestnut (Castanea sativa) groves represent a defining ecological and socioeconomic feature of Las Médulas: introduced by the Romans to sustain the mining population, chestnut cultivation remains today a cornerstone of the local agricultural economy, providing livelihoods for many families in the region. Assessing fire damage and recovery at the individual tree level is essential for guiding replanting strategies and economic compensation, and is only feasible at the centimetric resolution provided by the UAV-derived DSM and DOM, where individual tree crowns can be delineated and their structural condition assessed across the burned area. In hydrology and geomorphology, the DEM and DSM layers can be used to simulate runoff pathways, slope instability, and sediment redistribution. Furthermore, the elevation models serve as base layers for planning future UAV missions or emergency operations. Finally, the dataset is a valuable tool for education and outreach, allowing the general public to explore an accurate 3D representation of one of Europe’s most significant cultural landscapes.
