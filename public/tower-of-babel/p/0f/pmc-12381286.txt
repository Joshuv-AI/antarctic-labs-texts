Screening of hub genes and immunocytes related to tendon injury based on bioinformatics and machine learning models
Authors: Shulong Sun, Hua Li, Yan Li, Liubing Yang, Juanjuan Zhang, Yujing Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Tendon injury is a common and challenging clinical problem, and its healing process involves complex cellular and biological factors. Therefore, this study aims to reveal the mechanism of tendon healing and provide theoretical basis for clinical treatment. We first selected GSE26051 dataset from the GEO database and used R language to obtain 721 DEGs (459 up-regulated and 262 down-regulated). Subsequently, the 7378 genes of tendon injury obtained from the GeneCards database were intersected with DEGs to obtain 228 common genes. We constructed a PPI network of common genes using the STRING database, visualized it using Cytoscape software, and selected the top 10 (MYH6, MYL3, MYH1, MYH8, MYL1, TTN, TCAP, PKP2, ACTN2, CSRP3) genes through the CytoHubba plugin. We further identified hub genes (MYH1, MYH6, PKP2, MYH8) via machine learning models. Afterwards, the cytoskeleton in muscle cells and IL-17 signaling pathways were obtained by GO and KEGG analysis of common genes. Finally, the macrophages M2 was screened through immune infiltration analysis. This study revealed that hub genes such as MYH1, MYH6, MYH8 and PKP2 were mainly enriched in the cytoskeleton in muscle cells signaling pathway, and macrophage M2 played an important role in the inflammatory phase of tendon healing.

Introduction

Tendon injury is common in clinical practice, leading to severe disability, pain, medical costs, and loss of productivity1. The most common cause of tendon injuries is tearing, with the highest incidence rate occurring in the 20–29 age group. The incidence is higher in males than in females2. However, compared to other tissues, the healing process of tendons tends to be longer, mainly due to factors such as low blood perfusion and slow regeneration rates3. Tendon healing is a multifaceted process influenced by the severity of the tear and the stability of the microenvironment, lasting from several months to years. The inflammatory cascade, initiated after the hemostasis and coagulation stages, plays a crucial role in coordinating the healing process4.

Tendon tissue is the extension of muscles and also serves as a bridge between the muscle and skeletal systems. Muscle fibers are located within a three-dimensional cytoskeleton composed of various collagen, glycoproteins, proteoglycans, and elastin proteins, commonly referred to as extracellular matrix (ECM). ECM interacts with other cells, such as fibroblasts or immune system cells, to regulate muscle development, growth, and repair, which is crucial for effective muscle contraction and strength transfer5. The mechanisms of tendon repair and the involved cellular biology are still a subject of controversy6.

Tendon healing involves complex factors such as cellular and biomechanical aspects, presenting a significant challenge in clinical practice. The repair of tendon injuries typically goes through a brief inflammatory phase, lasting about a week, followed by a proliferative phase lasting several weeks, and then a remodeling phase lasting several months. During the inflammation stage, increased vascular permeability allows immunocytes to infiltrate the damaged area. These cells simultaneously produce a variety of cytokines and growth factors, leading to the recruitment and proliferation of macrophages and fibroblasts. In the proliferation and remodeling stages, fibroblasts further proliferate and begin to produce, deposit, orient, and crosslink fibrous collagen7. An imbalance in the inflammatory response may disrupt the immune microenvironment of the damaged tendon tissue, hindering the healing process8. Excessive inflammatory response can lead to excessive fibrosis of tissues instead of regeneration. Tendon healing typically involves specific cellular events and sources, including the infiltration of immunocytes, fibroblasts from the tendon surface, mesenchymal stem cells derived from the bone marrow, and even osteoclasts, among others9. Clinical observations and animal model studies further confirm the occurrence and progression of inflammation in different tendon injuries10,11. Inflammation is a necessary stage for tendon healing. The inflammatory microenvironment attracts immunocytes such as macrophages, which help clear debris and release growth factors that initiate the healing process12. Therefore, the regulation of the inflammatory response is crucial for promoting tendon repair.

The study of the immune environment in tendon injury repair reveals the enormous potential of targeted anti-inflammatory treatments. Traditional anti-inflammatory treatments show short-term advantages, but prolonged use may damage tendon structural integrity and increase the risk of tendon rupture. In recent years, novel treatment methods based on biomedical materials have garnered attention in anti-inflammatory treatments for tendon diseases, showing promising results13. Macrophages are a component of the immune system and play a crucial role in tendon healing14. During the repair phase following tendon injury, small molecules can be used to regulate macrophage polarization and the inflammatory response15. Regulating the polarization state of macrophages is a potential therapeutic strategy for promoting tendon injury repair. Magnesium pretreated periosteum prevents bone loss by enhancing the polarization of M2 macrophages around the tendon-bone interface, showing promise in promoting tendon-bone healing16,17. Therefore, it is crucial to gain an in-depth understanding of the key immune cells, signaling pathways, and their mechanisms involved in the inflammatory response, as this knowledge provides valuable guidance for the development of novel anti-inflammatory drugs.

Materials and methods

Data sources and criteria for selection

The dataset involved in this research was downloaded from the GEO (Gene Expression Omnibus) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds/). For dataset filtration, we complied with these inclusion criteria: (1) The dataset featured Homo sapiens as the species under study, characterized by an array expression profile as the data type. (2) All the datasets used in this study are accessible to the public18.

DEGs screening

The matrix file was accessed for data management and statistical evaluation in differentially expressed genes (DEGs) screening using R software (version 4.2.2) (https://www.r-project.org/) in the following manners: (1) Initially, the “removeBatchEffect” function was employed to remove batch effects from the data, resulting in a box plot created through “boxplot” function. Additionally, we examined the dataset’s heterogeneity after removing batch effects through principal component analysis (PCA) utilizing the R packages FactoMineR, Stats, and Factoextra19. (2) Following this, the matrix underwent analysis and comparison using the “limma” and “lmtest” R packages. (3) Ultimately, DEGs were acquired by establishing a threshold value of |log2 Fold Change|≥ 1 and P value < 0.0520. The up-regulated and down-regulated genes were screened, and the volcano maps were charted using “ggplot2” and “ggrepel” R packages. And the top 20 genes were further screened, and heat maps were drawn using the “heat map” R package21.

PPI network construction and GO/KEGG enrichment analysis

Genes associated with tendon injury were acquired from the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) database22. The online software Venny 2.0 (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index2.0.2.html) was employed to take the intersection of the genes acquired from GeneCards with DEGs, resulting in common genes, and a Venny plot was created23. We uploaded the common genes into the STRING (https://cn.string-db.org/cgi/input?sessionId=bsQYL4mNUkld&input_page_show_search=on) database, configuring the parameters as follows: (1) Search type set to: Multiple proteins, Organisms to Homo sapiens; (2) the minimum required interaction score: high confidence (0.700)24. Utilizing Cytoscape 3.9.1 (https://cytoscape.org/) software, we developed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network for result visualization and additionally acquired hub genes via the CytoHubba plugin25,26.

And the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed on the common genes27,28. Initially, a “Custom analysis” was conducted on the common genes, assigning the “Analysis species” as “H.sapiens” in the Metascape (https://metascape.org/gp/index.html#/main/step1) database29. Subsequently, the analysis of common genes for GO and KEGG enrichment was conducted by adjusting the parameters to Min Overlap = 3, P value Cutoff = 0.01, and Min Enrichment = 1.5. Ultimately, the findings were displayed in the form of bar, circle and Sankey plots through the use of bioinformatics (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/), an online graphing tool30.

Construction of three machine learning models for screening hub genes

We screen the genes acquired from the earlier PPI network through machine learning algorithms. Utilizing the R package glmnet, DALEX, randomForest, and e1071, we developed three consistent and dependable machine learning models to enhance the identification of hub genes. The key steps for building the model and the optimization process are as follows: (1) We imported the common gene dataset obtained from the preliminary analysis into R software for data cleaning. (2) We used the “caret” R package to create training (70%) and testing (30%) sets, and performed tenfold cross-validation to build three models. (3) We treated the difference between observed probabilities and predicted probabilities as residuals. Using the DALEX package, we evaluated the model’s performance based on the residual distribution31–33. In the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression model, the lambda (λ) value was optimized using tenfold cross-validation with the R package “glmnet”, while the model’s performance was evaluated using a binomial distribution. By selecting the optimal λ parameter, we extracted the coefficients from the LASSO model and identified the candidate hub genes34. The Support Vector Machine (SVM) model identified the feature genes through linear prediction. These were ranked based on the ratio of mean square error to the square of standard deviation. To enhance robustness, a subsampling with k = 10 was used, and the feature genes were selected35. We used the “randomForest” R package to develop a Random Forest (RF) model, constructing 500 trees by minimizing the error rate across the entire dataset36. The importance of genes was ranked using the Gini index, and the top 10 genes were selected37. The genes from three machine learning models are crossbred to obtain the hub genes and create a Venn diagram38.

Immune infiltration analysis of DEGs

The infiltration level of 22 types of immunocytes in the DEGs were examined using the R language-based CIBERSORT(v.1.0 ) package39. To evaluate differences in immunocyte ratios, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was utilized, considering P < 0.05 as statistically significant. Correlation studies were carried out to examine the relationship between the pinpointed hub genes and 22 varieties of immunocytes. R software was employed to exhibit the outcomes for graphical representation. To depict the ratios of different immunocytes, bar stacked plots were employed, whereas box plots were utilized for contrasting the cell proportions between the TI group and the control group40.

Results

Dataset collection and preprocessing

The MeSH search strategy was employed to identify this study’s disease as tendon injury41. Based on the criteria for selection, GSE26051 dataset was chosen for this research, encompassing gene expression profiles of tendon tissues from 23 patients with tendon injuries and 23 healthy tendons. The dataset collects information about patients’ demographics, diagnoses, past treatments, current procedures, and surgical outcomes (10.1186/1471-2474-12-86/tables/1). Patients diagnosed with inflammatory arthritis (such as rheumatoid arthritis or lupus) or those who have previously undergone tendon surgery were excluded. Patients who have previously received corticosteroid injections are included, and this information is recorded42. The series matrix file for GSE26051 and the GPL570-5599.txt information file for the GPL570 platform were downloaded from the GEO database. The data underwent preprocessing through the application of R software (version 4.2.2). Utilizing the “GEO query” package, the platform annotation information file GPL570_Annotation was acquired, and the gene IDs were merged via the “dplyr” and “tidyr” packages. Duplicates and NA values were eliminated using the “limma” package to obtain the matrix file (Table S1).

Identification of hub genes linked to tendon injury via machine learning

For additional identification of hub genes, three machine learning algorithms (LASSO, RF, and SVM) were employed to examine the hub genes derived from the earlier PPI network. First, we evaluate the performance of the three models by comparing their distributions of residuals. Overall, the performance of the three models was similar. However, based on the results of the residual reverse cumulative distribution, we found that compared to the other two models, the SVM model has more residuals in the left tail of the residual distribution. In the right tail of the residual distribution, the LASSO model showed more residuals than the other models (Fig. 3a). The RF model, as shown in the boxplot and histogram of residual, exhibited the minimal median absolute residual value, suggesting its superior performance when considering the median absolute residuals (Fig. 3b, c). The ROC curve analysis revealed that the AUC (Area Under the Curve) values of the three models were identical, indicating no difference in predictive accuracy among the models (Fig. 3d). By examining the Feature Importance graphs of the three models, we could understand which predictors had a greater impact on the outcome variable. Overall, the SVM model has the lowest root mean square error (RMSE), while the RF model has the highest RMSE (Fig. 3e). Through the LASSO regression algorithm, we identified 5 hub genes related to tendon injury based on the optimal lambda value (Fig. 3f and Table 3). And the top 5 most significant genes were determined based on the lowest discrimination error rate and average ranking via the SVM method (Fig. 3g and Table 4). Then, we constructed 500 random forests to rank the genes and extracted the top 7 most important genes from them (Fig. 3h, i). Discussion

A tendon is a type of connective tissue structure that serves as a bridge in transmitting force between muscles and bones43. It is a common type of motor system damage in clinical practice. Due to limited understanding of its complex healing mechanism, there is currently a lack of effective treatment methods to promote healing. Based on this, we screened DEGs related to tendon injury by mining the GEO database, overlapped them with genes from the GeneCards database, and constructing a PPI network. Further, we identified the hub genes through machine learning algorithms. This study found that MYH1, MYH6, MYH8, and PKP2 genes are hub genes in tendon repair. MYH1, MYH8, and MYH6 genes all play a role in encoding the myosin heavy chain isoforms in adult skeletal muscle44,45, while the head domain of the myosin heavy chain is crucial for generating molecular forces and movement46. A study on the fusion of motor neurons and muscle fibers found that neurovascular cells can upregulate MYH8, MYH1, MYH2, and MYH4 to promote muscle fiber fusion and increase their length47. Another study on mice suggests that running exercise can enhance muscle strength and endurance by broadly promoting the expression of Myh1/2/4/7 fast and slow muscle fibers in mdx mice. MYH1 is a type of conventional myosin that encodes a major adult myofibrillar protein involved in muscle contraction functions48. Research has found that MYH1 is the main gene responsible for rhabdomyolysis49. These studies indicate that the MYH1, MYH6, and MYH8 genes improve tendon repair by affecting myosin and vascular remodeling at the injury site. We found that during this healing process, PKP2 exerted adhesion by altering the desmosomes. Research has shown that silencing PKP2 expression negatively impacts tissue healing50. Cells are interconnected by desmosomes (a type of intercellular junction) formed through Armadillo (Arm) proteins and Plakophilin (PKP) 1–3. Among them, Plakophilin 2 not only plays an adhesive role but is also crucial for transferring the precursor to the membrane51. However, desmosomes are dynamic structures, and their assembly state is sensitive to the changes in signals that occur during the tissue healing process52. After cell–cell contact, PKP2 rapidly accumulates on the membrane in stage 1, and then forms a bridge precursor containing cytoplasmic PKP2 without vesicles in stage 2. In stage 3, these membrane-free precursors move toward and fuse with the newly formed cell–cell contacts in an actin- and myosin-dependent manner53,54. In conclusion, during tendon repair, PKP2 facilitates adhesion by altering desmosomes, thereby promoting the healing of injured tendons.

Through KEGG analysis, it was found that the core genes were mainly enriched in the cytoskeleton in muscle cells signaling pathway. The cytoskeleton in muscle cells is a complex network structure composed of collagen, glycoproteins, proteoglycans, and elastin55. The cytoskeleton in muscle cells is the structural and supportive scaffold of muscle segments, playing an important role in muscle plasticity. Each muscle fiber (which represents a muscle cell) forms muscle fiber bundles that make up muscle tissue, supported by a network of cytoskeletal structures in the extracellular matrix. The precise coordination between these components is crucial for shaping the state of muscle health and related motor activity56. The cytoskeleton-ECM reticular junction has been proven to be crucial for providing necessary biomechanical support and handling contraction (tension) pressure within muscles, serving as a key regulator for maintaining mechanical homeostasis within muscles57. The cytoskeleton in muscle cells connect sarcomeres to organelles such as mitochondria and the nucleus through intermediate filaments, forming a scaffold that maintains cellular integrity. The coordination of the cytoskeleton network is crucial for the contraction activity of striated muscles58. The cytoskeleton in muscle cells provides mechanical support for nerves and blood vessels, and is also crucial for the further growth and repair of muscle tissue59. Skeletal tendons rely on satellite cells for regeneration and repair. These stem cells are located in a special ecological niche between the muscle fiber membrane and the basement membrane it envelops. In fact, as a dynamic environment, the stem cell niche transmits mechanical and chemical signals that protect quiescent stem cells or induce their activation, proliferation, and differentiation5. The cytoskeleton in muscle cells has been shown to affect stem cell division. Specifically, fibrinogen, collagen VI, and glycoproteins have been identified as ecological niche components that affect the balance between differentiation and self-renewal, thereby maintaining the regenerative capacity of skeletal tendons60–62. During tendon injury, most quiescent satellite cells are usually activated and differentiate into fibroblasts, ultimately fusing into mature fibers. This process requires timely expression of various transcription factors, and research has identified the role of cytoskeleton in these steps63. ECM interacts with other cells, such as fibroblasts or immune cells, to regulate muscle development, growth, and repair. We found another immune related IL-17 signaling pathway. The cytokine IL-17 family is a subset of cytokines that plays a critical role in acute and chronic inflammatory responses and has a broad impact on the pathogenesis of various inflammatory diseases64. By regulating inflammation-related signaling pathways and macrophage immune function, it leads to the release of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, thereby maintaining macrophage immune homeostasis. It has been confirmed that regulating IL-17-NF-κB signaling enhances macrophage phagocytosis and M2 polarization, thereby promoting cell proliferation and tendon healing65. Research has shown that activating the IL-17 signaling pathway can drive the polarization of M2 macrophages, thereby improving healing66. Additionally, through KEGG enrichment analysis, we also identified the motor proteins, focal adhesion, and calcium signaling pathways. The motor protein signaling pathway plays a crucial role in many cellular functions, primarily involving the regulation of cell motility as well as the maintenance of cell shape and polarity. The interaction between filamentous actin and myosin forms the basis of muscle contraction67. From a biological perspective, the mechanical load generated by muscle contraction helps promote the synthesis of the tendon extracellular matrix68,69. Moreover, mechanical load can restore the mechanical properties of tendons70,71. Studies have found that, after a tendon injury, compared to traditional plaster fixation, a higher muscle stretch-induced load improves tendon healing72. Another study on mouse rotator cuff repair found that eccentric contraction is more effective than other muscle contraction modes in promoting the healing of the mouse rotator cuff tendon73. These findings suggest that the motor proteins signaling pathway may promote tendon healing through mechanical loading generated by the interaction between filamentous actin and myosin. Focal adhesions (FA) are dynamic multiprotein complexes located in the plasma membrane, comprising scaffold proteins, phosphatases, kinases, and actin-binding proteins74. FA can connect actin filaments in the cytoskeleton with the extracellular matrix (ECM) and serve as a central signaling hub within the cell75. Moreover, the mechanotransduction mediated by the focal adhesion pathway can activate signaling cascades such as MAPK/ERK and PI3K/Akt, driving tendon cell gene expression and the remodeling of the ECM of tendons, thereby promoting tendon repair76. Tendons are specialized connective tissues in terms of biomechanics, capable of maintaining joint stability and guiding movement under complex loading conditions. At the cellular and molecular levels, the focal adhesion pathway regulates tendon homeostasis by influencing cytoskeletal remodeling77. As an interface between the intracellular and extracellular environments, FA is capable of sensing external (the intensity of mechanical stress and the composition of the ECM) and internal (actin myosin contractile force) microenvironments. Based on this, it can coordinate both internal and external signal transduction78–80. In conclusion, the formation of focal adhesion has a positive impact on the formation of actin cytoskeletal fibers81. Therefore, the focal adhesion signaling pathway primarily constructs a bridge between the actin fibers in the cytoskeleton and the ECM through focal adhesions, regulating tendon homeostasis and ultimately promoting tendon healing. Ca2+ is an essential second messenger in almost all types of cells and biological systems82. The activation of the intracellular calcium signaling pathway plays a crucial role in muscle contraction responses and is also involved in ECM remodeling83. The role of calcium is tightly regulated to ensure precise cellular responses, maintaining cellular homeostasis and function. This calcium homeostasis is crucial for many physiological processes, including muscle contraction and nerve transmission. Imbalances in calcium levels can lead to calcific tendinopathy, which is characterized by tendon degeneration, pain, and impaired function84.

After a tendon injury, three overlapping stages are required for healing, including inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling85. During the inflammatory phase, blood first accumulates and coagulates at the site of injury, triggering platelet degranulation and the release of cytokines and growth factors. Subsequently, the inflammatory phase lasts for 3 to 7 days, attracting inflammatory cells such as neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages, which are crucial for clearing debris and promoting tendon healing86. Within 24 h of tendon injury, a large number of inflammatory cells infiltrate the subcutaneous tissue and the site of the tendon damage87. Studies have observed the infiltration of macrophages at the site of damaged tendons, coordinating local inflammation and regulating the healing process88. Damaged tendon tissue releases chemokines, thereby recruiting immune cells, primarily macrophages89. These studies highlight the pivotal role of macrophages in tendon healing. Research on Achilles tendon injury models has found that the influx of macrophages also leads to the further release of chemokines and cytokines, which in turn causes a further increase in macrophages. This indicates that there is a reciprocal positive regulatory relationship between chemokines and macrophages90. Through immune infiltration analysis, it was found that macrophages (M2) are continuously expressed throughout the entire healing process, further emphasizing the critical role of macrophages (M2) in tendon injury repair. Macrophages play a crucial role in the early stages of tendon repair by regulating the inflammatory response91. When macrophages respond to local stimuli, significant phenotypic changes occur92. Macrophages are broadly divided into two groups: classically activated (M1) or selectively activated (M2) cells, with each phenotype driven by specific activation conditions. From a functional perspective, macrophages exhibit significant functional diversity, classified into pro-inflammatory M1 and anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes93–95. The balance between M1 and M2 macrophages is crucial for tissue repair. Macrophage polarization disorder can lead to chronic inflammation, impaired healing, and excessive scar formation. In the early stages of tendon healing, macrophages infiltrating the injury site are predominantly of the M1 phenotype96. During the tissue repair process, they undergo a phenotypic transformation from M1 pro-inflammatory macrophages to M2 anti-inflammatory macrophages, releasing various cytokines that contribute to tissue regeneration and wound healing97,98. However, M2 macrophages mainly participate in the later stages of healing, demonstrating the ability to eliminate intracellular pathogens and promote tendon regeneration95,99. 28 days after tendon injury, M2 macrophages significantly increase and function as the main macrophage phenotype at the site of injury89. In a previous study, by examining the interface between the long toe flexor tendons and the bone tunnel, it was observed that M1 macrophages were present in the early postoperative phase and persisted for 4 weeks. In contrast, M2 macrophages were not detected until 11 days post-surgery and showed high expression levels for up to 4 weeks11. M1 macrophages initially exhibit pro-inflammatory functions and are gradually replaced by the anti-inflammatory functions of M2 macrophages. Accelerating this process can promote tissue repair. On the contrary, when macrophages fail to promptly transition into the M2 phenotype, it can lead to prolonged local inflammation and delayed healing100. Similarly, a study using a mouse model of Achilles tendon rupture demonstrated that a decreased M1/M2 ratio promoted tendon healing101. M2 macrophages primarily participate in and promote the proliferation of fibroblasts and the deposition of new tissue, which is crucial for the regeneration and remodeling of tendon tissue102. Therefore, regulating the transition of macrophages from the M1 to M2 state and the balance between them is a promising research direction for improving tendon healing.

In the later stages of tendon healing, the concentration of M2 macrophages in the tendon’s excess extracellular matrix (ECM) area significantly increases103. M2 macrophages induced by Th2 cytokines possess anti-inflammatory effects (by releasing IL-10 and TGFβ1)104, which can subsequently inhibit inflammation and clear extracellular matrix in scar tissue92. Therefore, in tendon injuries, a reasonable assumption is that M2 macrophages in the later stages can suppress inflammation and clear excess ECM, a concept that aligns with experimental evidence. In this context, the role of macrophages, particularly their involvement in regulating inflammatory responses and tendon repair processes, has garnered increasing attention. Understanding how macrophages regulate the inflammatory and proliferative phases, as well as their potential impact on the subsequent remodeling phase, is crucial for unraveling the complexities of tendon healing. The polarization of M2 macrophages plays a crucial role in promoting fibroblast proliferation and stimulating new tissue deposition105. Studies have found that when macrophages are subjected to appropriate mechanical stimulation, they can promote polarization toward the M2 phenotype by activating the IL-4/JAK/STAT signaling pathway106, while simultaneously secreting high levels of TGF-β1 to facilitate the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), ultimately promoting tendon repair107. TGF-β1 can also stimulate the formation of fibroblasts, leading to tendon adhesion108. Clinically, low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) can accelerate the accumulation of macrophages in the early stages, promoting the polarization of M2 macrophages, thereby aiding in the repair of tendon injuries109. These findings confirm that the therapeutic effect of LIPUS treatment on tendon healing largely depends on its regulatory effect on macrophage polarization and function. The role of M2 macrophage polarization in modulating inflammation during tendon repair is multifaceted. A study revealed that the polarization of M2 macrophages can promote tendon healing by stimulating the secretion of phospholipase A1 member A (Pla1a). The secretion of Pla1a not only enhances tendon cell proliferation and reduces apoptosis but also decreases Etv1 expression. This dual role of Pla1a, regulated by M2 macrophages, plays a critical part in reducing tendon adhesion and enhancing cell vitality110. Research has found that during the process of M1 to M2 conversion, the depletion of MSC-derived exosomes leads to a reduction in the M2 phenotype of macrophages111. This suggests that MSCs can promote wound healing by inducing M2 polarization of macrophages through the transfer of microRNAs within exosomes. In addition, miR-29a promotes M2 polarization and suppresses inflammation by down regulating the expression and nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 at the injury site112. These studies highlight the importance of targeting molecular pathways to regulate macrophage behavior, providing insights into the complex interactions between cellular and molecular mechanisms in tendon healing. As people have become increasingly concerned about the inflammatory responses associated with biomaterials, a substantial amount of research has been dedicated to developing various materials. Each of these materials possesses unique properties and mechanisms that play a crucial role in influencing macrophage polarization, thereby highlighting their importance in the tendon healing process. Research shows that biologically derived materials (such as decellularized surgical mesh) can affect macrophage polarization113. In addition, functional biomaterials developed from naturally derived polysaccharides for tissue regeneration and pharmaceutical applications have demonstrated their ability to alter macrophage phenotypes and influence immune responses and tissue healing by targeting cell membrane receptors114. These findings indicate the potential of biomaterials in improving tendon repair by modulating macrophage activity and phenotype to promote better healing and function, while simultaneously reducing the risk of adhesions. However, recent research trends suggest that, compared to naturally derived biomaterials, there is an increasing focus on synthetic biomaterials. A novel high-strength nanofiber woven scaffold, featuring a structure similar to natural anisotropy and immune modulation capabilities, is designed for tendon tissue engineering applications. The scaffold is made from polylactic acid and silk fibroin, and effectively regulates the polarization of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype, demonstrating significant immune modulation ability115. In addition, a study involving Wnt3a-modified nanofibers scaffolds further highlights the potential of material modulation in macrophage M2 polarization116. Extracellular vesicles can effectively regulate the M2 polarization of macrophages, helping to reduce inflammation and promote tendon healing, which provides a complementary biological approach for material design strategies117. These studies reflect the versatility and customizability of synthetic materials, offering broader possibilities for the application of macrophage M2 polarization in tendon healing.

The complexity of tendon repair mechanisms and the lack of understanding have long been major obstacles hindering the development of current treatments. Therefore, identifying the key genes and signaling pathways involved in the repair process is crucial for formulating effective treatment strategies and promoting tissue regeneration118. Based on this, this study analyzed the GSE26051 dataset to identify hub genes and signaling pathways related to tendon repair. Although promising treatments are influenced by various fields, including biomechanics, cell and growth factor therapies, and tissue engineering119, understanding the central genes and signaling pathways involved in tendon repair and homeostasis, as well as their interconnections, is a prerequisite for developing effective treatment strategies, such as the development of new targeted therapies and drug delivery systems.

This study emphasizes the critical role of inflammation in tendon healing and its potential mechanisms, further discussing the possibility of promoting healing strategies through the improvement of the inflammatory response. In the immune microenvironment, the synergistic action of cytokines and immune cells promotes debris clearance, tendon cell proliferation, and collagen fiber formation. However, excessive inflammatory responses often backfire, leading to tissue damage and tendon adhesions, hindering tendon healing and promoting scar formation. Therefore, balancing the inflammatory response is crucial for achieving optimal healing outcomes. The role of macrophages is multifaceted, including their involvement in mediating inflammation, cell proliferation, tissue deposition, and scar formation. Notably, current clinical approaches to improving tendon injury repair, including the use of LIPUS and biomaterials, are based on in-depth research into the regulatory role of M2 macrophage polarization in inflammation during tendon healing. Of course, we note that the dataset included in this study does not cover various types of tendon injury cases, such as acute open injuries or immune-related diseases. Additionally, the limited sample size may introduce some bias in the results. There is still room for improvement in enhancing the dataset in future studies, and we will continue striving toward that goal. The hub genes identified in this study are primarily based on the analysis of a single dataset GSE26051. Although the hub genes were selected by combining DEGs and PPI with three machine learning models, their robustness may raise concerns. A study on DPEBic has provided us with some inspiration120. By using a dual-clustering algorithm and encoding each protein to detect important proteins, it offers valuable insights for our future research. Due to the changes and complexity in cell phenotypes, functions, and the stages of tendon healing, further refinement of animal experiments and clinical evidence is required to deepen our understanding of macrophage functions, as well as the cellular and molecular mechanisms behind tendon healing. Further research in these areas could provide new insights into the development of targeted treatments for tendon injuries.

Conclusion

We used bioinformatics analysis combined with machine learning to screen hub genes and signaling pathways related to tendon healing. The results showed that MYH1, MYH6, and MYH8 can improve tendon repair by affecting myosin and vascular remodeling at the site of injury. PKP2 can promote adhesion by altering desmosomes, thereby promoting the healing of damaged tendons. The cytoskeleton in muscle cells signaling pathway and FA signaling pathway work synergistically, linking myofibrils to organelles such as mitochondria and the cell nucleus via intermediate filaments, constructing the cellular scaffold, and building a bridge between actin filaments and the ECM, thereby regulating tendon homeostasis and ultimately promoting tendon healing. Meanwhile, the motor protein signaling pathway can enhance tendon healing through the mechanical load generated by the interaction between filamentous actin and myosin. In addition, we obtained M2 macrophages through immune infiltration analysis. It was found that the polarization of M2 macrophages plays an important role in promoting fibroblast proliferation, inhibiting inflammation, and clearing excess ECM. These findings can serve as potential research directions and therapeutic targets for tendon injury, providing a theoretical basis for subsequent studies.
