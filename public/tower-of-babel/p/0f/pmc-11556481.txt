A 3-year-old Tanzanian Female with Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase A- and a Novel Heterozygous PIEZO1 Mutation (2744A>G, N915S) Presenting with Severe Hemolytic Anemia
Authors: Nicholas John Fustino, Raven Beck
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A 3-year-old Tanzanian female presented with severe hemolytic anemia of unknown etiology, necessitating multiple red blood cell transfusions. The patient was found to have glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency A- and a heterozygous Piezo-type mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 (PIEZO1) mutation (2744A>G, N915S). This case identifies a novel PIEZO1 mutation implicated in erythrocyte channelopathies occurring in conjunction with an X-linked enzymopathy in a female patient. This underscores the importance of keeping X-linked disorders in the differential diagnosis of hemolytic anemia in females, as well as presents the possibility for novel coexisting mutations to augment the phenotype.

INTRODUCTION

Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, the most common enzymopathy worldwide, is an X-linked disorder affecting >500 million humans globally.[1] We report a 3-year-old female presenting with severe hemolytic anemia and subsequently discovered to have G6PD A-, with coexistence of a novel heterozygous Piezo Type Mechanosensitive Ion Channel Component 1 (PIEZO1) 2744A>G (N915S) mutation not previously reported in the literature. PIEZO1, a mechanically activated ion channel expressed at the surface of erythrocytes, affects cellular hydration in response to mechanical forces.[2] PIEZO1 mutations have been reported to be associated with mild hemolytic anemia.[3]

CASE REPORT

A 3-year-old Tanzanian female presented with a history of generalized weakness, pallor, and darkened urine. Past medical history was remarkable for cerebral malaria at 1 year of age and two hospitalizations necessitating erythrocyte transfusion. There was no known family history of blood disorders. Physical examination was remarkable for an SpO2 86% in room air, pale conjunctivae, scleral icterus, and no hepatosplenomegaly.

Hemoglobin was 3.4 g/dL (decreased from 11.7 g/dL 6 months prior), reticulocytes 7.7%, mean corpuscular volume 99 fL, red blood cell (RBC) 1.03 × 106/μL, nucleated RBCs 10%, white blood cells 15.07 × 103/μL, and platelets 227 × 103/μL. Peripheral blood smear showed anemia with microspherocytes and degmacytes. Coombs test was negative, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) 1336 U/L, and total bilirubin 2.1 mg/dL. Hemoglobin electrophoresis was normal.

She received 30 ml/kg of packed RBCs over 2 days with the improvement of hemoglobin to 11.5 g/dL. Gene sequencing (Invitae Corporation, San Francisco, CA, USA) revealed two homozygous G6PD variants (in cis) constituting G6PD A- (202G>A and 376A>G on X chromosome, N126D and V68M) and a heterozygous PIEZO1 mutation (2744A>G and N915S). Further history revealed parental consanguinity. There was no reported ingestion of new medications, fava beans, or naphthalene-containing products, although she was handling mothballs in the days prior. G6PD enzyme activity obtained after the hemolytic episode was 1.2 g/U hemoglobin (12% of mean normal).

DISCUSSION

In humans, the pentose phosphate pathway is the only mechanism by which to produce nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) in erythrocytes and can cause profound effects if disrupted. G6PD converts glucose-6-phosphate to 6-phosphogluconolactone with concurrent reduction of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP+) to NADPH, a cellular protectant against oxidative stress. The sulfhydryl groups of hemoglobin are often susceptible to such stressors, but NADPH helps produce reduced glutathione which acts as a protectant in unaffected individuals. In G6PD-deficient erythrocytes, levels are not able to increase in the face of oxidative stressors, resulting in acute hemolysis.[4]

The G6PD gene is located on the long arm of the X chromosome, band Xq28,[5] and at least 230 mutations in G6PD have been identified.[1] Prevalence can exceed 20% in tropical Africa, subtropical Asia, and the Mediterranean region.[15] G6PD A- is a World Health Organization Class III variant associated with mild to moderate acute hemolysis.[6] The G6PD A enzyme contains two amino acid substitutions, N126D and V68M[7] resulting in an enzyme activity level ranging from 10% to 23% of normal.[1] Clinically, most individuals remain asymptomatic until exposed to an oxidative threat. Fava beans and naphthalene ingestion (i.e. moth balls),[8] as well as numerous medications have been implicated as inducers of hemolysis, most commonly trimethoprim sulfamethoxazole, primaquine, tafenoquine, dapsone, rasburicase, nitrofurantoin, and phenazopyridine.[4] Laboratory and clinical findings of hemolysis, such as jaundice, fatigue, and pallor, occur after an implicated medication or food ingestion. Anemia with reticulocytosis, negative Coombs, unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia, elevated LDH, and low haptoglobin also typically occur. Splenomegaly rarely occurs and rapid hematologic recovery is typical in most cases.[1] Management involves removing the source of oxidative stress along with supportive or observational care, though occasionally erythrocyte transfusion is indicated.[8]

Our patient required significant blood product support during hospitalization and on at least two other occasions in Africa. A heterozygous mutation was detected in PIEZO1, 2744A>G, p.(N915S) – a variant not previously reported in the literature – and it remains uncertain if this is a pathogenic mutation. Known PIEZO1 mutations

PIEZO1 mutations are not uncommon in the African population and gain of function mutations may be associated with resistance to malaria.[3] Mutations are often inherited in an autosomal dominant fashion and implicated in hereditary xerocytosis in up to 90% of cases,[2] which often present with mild hemolysis and splenomegaly.[3] The majority of reported variants are gain-of-function mutations, resulting in hereditary xerocytosis.[10] The possibility exists that this mutation, implicated in erythrocyte channelopathies, could have augmented her hemolytic process in conjunction with her enzymopathy. While its role in mechanotransduction has been established over the past decade, recent research is further elucidating the role of PIEZO1 in RBC turnover, erythropoiesis, and iron metabolism.[2] We hypothesize our patient may have an activating mutation which is more consistent with erythrocyte channelopathies previously reported.

Given that the patient was found to be homozygous for X-linked mutations causing G6PD A-, it is probable the patient’s father likely has G6PD deficiency while the mother is presumably a carrier of the mutation, with spontaneous mutations unlikely given the consanguinity of the parents. Interestingly, neither of her parents reported they had ever received a blood transfusion or had episodes of jaundice or acute hemolysis. It is unknown whether the patient’s mother or father harbors the PIEZO1 mutation, or if the mutation was spontaneous in the patient. Further research is needed to determine conclusively if the novel PIEZO1 mutation could have contributed to her phenotype.

CONCLUSION

This case highlights a novel PIEZO1 mutation implicated in erythrocyte channelopathies occurring in conjunction with an X-linked enzymopathy in a female patient. This underscores the importance of keeping X-linked disorders in the differential diagnosis of hemolytic anemia in females (and eliciting a history of consanguinity), as well as presents the possibility for novel coexisting mutations to augment the phenotype. Future studies should pursue genotyping of the parents for the PIEZO1 mutation with corresponding analysis of erythrocyte properties through ektacytometry and screened for evidence of chronic hemolysis, thus more definitively characterizing the role of this mutation in erythrocyte stability.
