Risk conditions for invasive pneumococcal disease in adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Kim Ditzel, Federica Giardina, Jaap ten Oever, Amelieke J.H. Cremers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe burden of invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) in adults remains high despite vaccination programs. Age is currently the prime criterion used to define target groups for prevention. To support effective intervention programs, we investigated the relative importance of risk conditions associated with adult IPD.MethodsWe conducted a systematic search in PubMed and Embase and included all original studies published before March 24th 2025 that reported risk estimates for adult IPD in individuals with risk conditions, compared to the general population (PROSPERO ID 417980). Risk estimates were extracted and meta-analyses were performed for conditions supported by more than one study. Pooled incidence rate ratios with 95% confidence intervals (IRRs (95% CIs)) were calculated as the primary outcome using random-effects models. The risk of bias and the certainty of the evidence were assessed using the ROBINS-E tool and GRADE system respectively.FindingsOf the 2983 reports screened, 58 were included in the systematic review, and 46 supported the meta-analyses. More than 50 different risk conditions for adult IPD were identified, which we synthesized in 22 risk domains for meta-analyses. The highest IRRs (95% CIs) for adult IPD were reported in immunocompromising conditions: transplant recipients 30.75 (17.64–53.60), asplenia 24.29 (18.63–31.65), people living with human immunodeficiency virus (PLWH) 21.87 (15.72–30.43), and haematological malignancy 19.63 (11.10–34.71). Increasing age itself mediated minor risk sizes. At lower age the risk conditions conferred relatively higher risk ratios. Chronic kidney disease in adults <65 years old posed an IRR (95% CI) of 18.40 (11.38–29.74), compared to 5.12 (2.08–12.63) among those over 65. IRRs >10 were also observed for compromised cerebrospinal fluid barrier and Down syndrome. The overall quality of evidence was very low, mainly due to high risk of bias and considerable between-study heterogeneity. Several studies suggested cumulative risk in individuals with multimorbidity, although data on interactions between conditions were limited.InterpretationThis systematic review and meta-analysis quantify the relative importance of risk conditions for adult IPD. Understanding how these risk conditions interact in cases of multimorbidity remains an important area for future research.FundingRadboudumc Community for Infectious Diseases encouragement Grant.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Current evidence on risk conditions for invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) in adults is largely fragmented across individual observational studies or limited to reviews focusing on specific host characteristics. No comprehensive synthesis exists that systematically quantifies the relative importance of risk conditions across adult populations.

Added value of this study

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides a comprehensive overview of the evidence supporting clinical risk conditions for adult IPD. We quantify the relative risk associated with 22 defined risk domains and highlight several important findings: risk conditions often confer substantially higher relative risks in younger adults compared with older adults; the presence of multiple coexisting conditions is associated with further increased risk; and healthy ageing may be associated with reduced susceptibility. Our findings also highlight significant gaps in the quality and consistency of available evidence, particularly regarding adjustment for confounders and reporting by age.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings suggest that younger adults living with risk conditions - especially chronic kidney disease, Down syndrome, or unstable living conditions–may warrant greater prioritisation in IPD prevention strategies. The reported relative risks, when interpreted alongside absolute risk estimates, can support the refinement of vaccination policies and the targeting of other preventive interventions. Furthermore, these data can inform the design of future studies evaluating the effectiveness of such strategies in vulnerable adult populations.

Introduction

The bacterium Streptococcus pneumoniae (the pneumococcus) remains the major cause of respiratory sepsis and its related mortality.1,2 Each year, pneumococcal infections are responsible for an estimated 8.1 million disability-adjusted life-years, 829,000 deaths, and 40.3 million life years lost globally.3,4 Also in bacterial meningitis, the highest proportion of deaths remains attributable to S. pneumoniae.5 Invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) represents a subset of pneumococcal infections in which the pneumococcus is identified in a human specimen collected from a normally sterile body site, using laboratory methods. This case definition ascertains the pneumococcal aetiology of a given infection and delineates a population for epidemiology purposes.

Individuals at the youngest and oldest age groups bear the largest IPD burden. Between 2000 and 2024 the conjugated pneumococcal vaccine (PCV) was introduced into 169 paediatric national immunization programmes, with the primary objective to protect children below the age of five from IPD.6,7 In this age category high efficacy was demonstrated for protection from IPD caused by serotypes included in the heptavalent vaccine.8 Serotype-based vaccination not only altered the pneumococcal population that infected the target group itself, but similarly changed the population infecting adults living in the same area.9 This phenomenon is called “herd protection” and it suggests that the paediatric nasopharyngeal niche is a relevant reservoir for pneumococcal acquisition in adults.10 However, what differentiated adults from the paediatric target group, was the more comprehensive replacement of the adult IPD case load by non-vaccines serotypes in many areas in the world.11,12 The largely sustained burden of IPD among adults substantiates the potential value of preventive measures that target this specific age category.

The replacing pneumococcal population in adult IPD is characterized by a diversification of pneumococcal serotypes.13,14 This relative lack of dominant serotypes not only complicates the design and coverage of next generation vaccines that target adults.15,16 It also suggests that in adults, the permissiveness of the human host to infection could be a stronger determinant of IPD compared to children where the range of potentially pathogenic pneumococcal serotypes seems notably more confined thus far.

Over the past years several novel pneumococcal vaccines with extended serotype valency were submitted for licensure to the medical regulatory authorities. In adults, age is currently the primary criterion used to define target groups for interventions aimed at preventing adult IPD. However, if additional risk conditions significantly contribute to adults’ susceptibility to IPD, risk prediction could be improved. In addition, the effectiveness of preventive interventions may depend on the specific vulnerability that mediates the risk of IPD, ranging from f.e. narrow immunodeficiencies to crude breaches in protective tissues. Currently, 54 countries have established indications for adult pneumococcal vaccination in their national immunisation schedule, 38 of which have specified adult risk groups.17 In practice, these specified target groups for prevention of adult IPD are regularly derived or extrapolated from evidence generated for more narrow study populations, and associated risk sizes are rarely accounted for. Optimizing the effectiveness of interventions requires the identification and prioritization of adults who carry marked risks for IPD. However, current evidence on potential risk conditions for adult IPD is largely fragmented and does not provide insight in risk sizes across risk conditions.

Therefore, the aim of this systematic review and meta-analyses of observational studies was to provide a comprehensive overview of all reported risk conditions for adult IPD including their relative risk size. Interventional studies are not considered in this systematic review.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This systematic review and the meta-analyses of observational studies were all conducted in accordance with the COSMOS-E guidelines and reported as advised by the PRISMA 2020 statement.18,19

Our study protocol was registered in PROSPERO on April 28th 2023 (ID 417980). The objective of the search was to identify publications that reported relative risk estimates for IPD in adults with and without a risk condition. The search strategy and eligibility criteria for inclusion in the systematic review are detailed in Supplementary Material A. In short, in March 2025 PubMed and Embase were searched for articles published in English language using extensive search terms for IPD in combination with risk factors, comorbidities and immunodeficiencies. To ensure the search strategy was sensitive enough to capture all key studies, it was iteratively refined in collaboration with the library at Radboudumc. In addition, the bibliographies of all relevant review articles were screened for missing, relevant articles. We did not search for grey literature (e.g., conference abstracts, preprints). Trial registries were not searched as the current study on risk conditions relied on cohort studies and case–control studies.

All articles published up to the search date were assessed for eligibility using clear criteria (Supplementary Material A). One of two researchers (K.D. and A.C.) performed a first round of unambiguous exclusions based on screening of titles and abstracts. In case of any doubt on potential eligibility, early exclusion required discussion and consensus between the two researchers. In brief, articles were potentially eligible if they contained primary data that provided summary estimates of a risk condition for adult IPD (not including non-invasive pneumococcal pneumonia) in comparison to the general population. The reason to exclude studies that report on host polymorphisms as a risk condition for IPD is that detailed systematic reviews already emphasized limitations of the existing evidence which is being addressed in a prospective manner.20,21

We excluded studies with ≥10% of the population aged ≤14 years. This represents a post-hoc amendment to the registered PROSPERO protocol, which initially stated an 18-year cutoff. This adjustment prevented the exclusion of some highly relevant studies.

A detailed assessment of eligibility was performed on full-text articles, and studies were included if independently approved by both investigators. If risk conditions were supported by single studies or if determinants were defined too heterogeneously, the results were reported separately and excluded from the meta-analysis.

Data extraction and risk of bias

Data were extracted by one of two researchers (K.D. or A.C.) and cross-checked by the other using a standardised form. The outcome variable was the risk of IPD, expressed as an incidence rate ratio (IRR), risk ratio (RR), hazard ratio (HR) or odds ratio (OR) with their corresponding 95% confidence interval, associated with a given risk condition. Both adjusted and unadjusted effect estimates were extracted, along with those reported for specific subpopulations (e.g., age categories). Additionally extracted data included study design, study population characteristics (e.g., period, country, control population, reported pneumococcal vaccination policy), definition of IPD, number of IPD patients, and sponsoring by commercial entities.

Given the substantial number of identified risk conditions, we grouped them into broader categories—referred to as “risk domains”—based on consensus. For studies with overlapping population and time periods, we selected only one study using the following hierarchical criteria: largest sample size, largest number of risk conditions, most recent cohort, and availability of age-stratified data. Risk estimates based on a combination of risk conditions spanning multiple risk domains (e.g., haematological and solid malignancy combined) were excluded, with the exception for the risk domain secondary immunodeficiency which included immunocompromising therapies. For studies reporting risk estimates across multiple time periods, we prioritized the overall estimate when available. If an overall estimate was not provided, we selected the estimate for the most recent period, as we considered it the most representative. Exclusion of a risk estimate for a confined concern, left the other study estimates eligible.

The risk of bias in included studies was assessed using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies - of Exposure (ROBINS-E) tool.22 This tool considers seven domains with a potential for bias: confounding, measurement of the exposure, selection of participants, post-exposure interventions, missing data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. Two researchers (K.D. and A.C.) independently evaluated the individual studies, resolved any differences at the domain level, and consolidated the risk of bias in the study to be low, of some concern, high, or very high according to the ROBINS-E tool.

Pneumococcal immunization practices in a study setting may affect the reported risk estimates in 2 major ways. Firstly, paediatric vaccination also results in indirect “herd protection” of adult populations. Because this phenomenon is presumably mediated by a decrease in exposition to children carrying pneumococcus, we assumed that it equally affected the adult populations with and without the studied risk condition. Therefore, changes in absolute incidence of adult IPD have probably not affected the relative risk metrics that are reported in this meta-analysis. However, implementation of paediatric immunization programs has also convincingly caused a shift in the distribution of serotypes that cause infection. While pneumococcal serotypes may differently affect the adult populations with and without the studied risk conditions, there is insufficient knowledge or evidence to integrate such alterations in the current analyses. Secondly, the members of the adult study population with the risk condition themselves may have had a different pneumococcal vaccination status than the comparator population. If this were reported as likely, or if the country had issued immunization recommendations for adult risk groups at the time of study, this was included in the risk of bias estimate.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcomes of interest (pooled IRRs and ORs) were presented by age group ‘all ages’, ‘age ≥65’ or ‘age <65’. Age was not modelled as a covariate in the meta-analysis; instead, reported estimates were extracted as published and stratified post hoc according to these categories.

For studies reporting risk estimates disaggregated into more refined categories than our predefined groups (e.g., age <65, ≥65, or all ages), we combined these estimates to align with our unit of analysis. First, the standard error (SE) of each risk estimate (e.g., OR or IRR) was derived from its reported confidence interval (CI) by transforming to the logarithmic scale and assuming a Wald confidence interval. Subsequently, multiple estimates within the same group were combined using standard inverse-variance weighting, under the assumption of independence. This approach utilized weighted least squares to compute a single combined estimate. Calculations were performed using the escalc and aggregate functions in the metafor package23 in R (version 4.4.0).24

Effect estimates were extracted as reported by the original studies, whether adjusted or unadjusted for potential confounders, such as age or other comorbidities. Risk of invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) was expressed as IRRs, RRs, hazard ratios (HRs), or odds ratios (ORs). Where studies reported ORs, these were approximated as RRs under the assumption of rare outcomes (incidence ≤5%). HRs were approximated as IRRs. Because IPD is uncommon in all included populations, the use of IRRs (which account for person-time) was prioritised, to reduce potential bias associated with using ORs, particularly when risk conditions were also rare.

Random-effects models were used to pool effect estimates, accounting for between-study heterogeneity. Pooled estimates were calculated separately for IRRs and ORs, where reported. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, with thresholds interpreted as low (25%), moderate (50%), or high (75%) heterogeneity. Forest plots were generated to visualize pooled estimates.

All statistical analyses were conducted in R using the metafor package. The full analytical workflow, including annotated R scripts and input datasets are publicly available at: https://github.com/Federica-Giardina/IPD_adults_review.

For each risk domain, the certainty of the body of evidence provided by the meta-analyses on IRRs was assessed using the Grades of Recommendation, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system.25 Imprecision was defined as serious if the one-sided confidence interval was >45% of the pooled IRR for all ages, and as very serious if >65%.

Results

Two additional studies were identified through screening of citations. The risk of bias of the included articles ranged from “high” to “very high” based the on ROBINS-E assessment tool. The major sources of bias were an observational study nature, the size of potential confounding, and unknown allocation of post-exposure interventions such as vaccination, prophylactic antibiotics and treatment of the underlying disease. Even though 76% (44/58) of the studies attempted to adjust for confounders like age, in all of those studies the risk of bias was still assessed as “high” (26/44) to “very high” (18/44). A summary of the risk of bias assessment is represented in Supplementary Material E.

The 58 studies reported on >50 potential risk conditions for adult IPD, plus further subgroups. We binned risk conditions into 22 risk domains for which meta-analyses were performed. Supplementary Material F provides an overview of the risk domains formed, and of the definitions of the grouped risk conditions as reported in the original studies. As factor age affected the risk of adult IPD (Fig. 2) and because 38% of included studies reported risk estimates for separate age categories, we presented the extracted risk estimates accordingly. From the 58 studies included in the systematic review 452 eligible risk estimates were extracted; 261 incidence rate ratios (IRRs) and 191 odds ratios (ORs) (complete data provided in Supplementary Material G). The meta-analyses were substantiated by a total of 227 IRRs and 112 ORs. The number of risk estimates available per risk domain varied between 2 and 39 (Fig. 3). One case–control study raised serious concerns regarding the occurrence of a risk condition in the control population, as it relied on discharge diagnoses recorded over a short 12-month period for three low-prevalent risk conditions. As a result, the corresponding risk estimates were excluded from the meta-analyses. Otherwise, no other risk estimates were excluded from the meta-analyses. Risk domains associated with the highest pooled risk estimates for adult IPD (IRRs >10) were immunocompromising conditions such as haematological malignancy, PLWH, transplant recipients and asplenia with an IRR of respectively 19.63 (11.10–34.71), 21.87 (95% CI 15.72–30.43), 30.75 (95% CI 17.64–53.60) and 24.29 (95% CI 18.63–31.65). Other identified risk domains with high pooled risk estimates were compromised cerebrospinal fluid barrier (IRR 16.97, 95% CI 2.77–103.89) and Down syndrome (IRR 47.48, 95% CI 22.49–100.22).

Among other chronic conditions, kidney disease showed notably high IRRs for adult IPD, especially below the age of 65 with an IRR of 18.40 (95% CI 11.38–29.74). The smallest pooled IRR was observed for male sex (IRR 1.89, 95% CI 1.29–2.79). Age itself, after correction for the presence of other risk conditions, posed a relatively small direct risk for IPD (Fig. 2). Rather, below the age of 65 the risk associated with a given condition was generally larger than in the older population, with significant increases observed for diabetes mellitus, African American ethnicity, and chronic liver disease. The only exception to this pattern were younger patients with primary immunodeficiencies, in whom the reported IRR estimate for IPD was lower (not significantly) than that of patients >65 years old.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analyses provide the first comprehensive overview of all reported risk conditions for adult IPD and their relative importance. All 58 included studies had a high risk of bias, and the level composite evidence was very low for all risk conditions analysed. Meta-analyses were performed for 22 risk domains, of which immunocompromising conditions, compromised cerebrospinal fluid barrier, Down syndrome, and chronic kidney disease were associated with the largest relative risk estimates for adult IPD. Below the age of 65 years old, virtually all studied risk conditions mediated a larger relative risk size, compared to in the older population. In addition, a limited number of studies reported on ethnic, demographic and socio-economic factors that may contribute to the risk of adult IPD. Adults with more than one risk condition displayed an accumulated risk for IPD.

To ensure capture of the variety of clinical manifestations that comprise IPD we applied a broad search strategy and performed iterative refinement until all eligible reports listed in relevant review articles were captured. The studies included in the systematic review were relatively old and lacked representation of certain populations like adults from low- and middle-income countries, patients with solid malignancies, and those receiving immunocompromising treatments.

Three major factors that contributed to a relatively low quality of evidence were the observational nature of the included studies, the heterogeneity between studies, and potential bias from infection prevention and control (IPC) practices. Estimating risk in an observational manner is often executed using existing databases that were generated for other purposes, which may come with risks of bias due to missing data or due to insufficient adjustment for potentially confounding factors. With the objective to generate a comprehensive overview we allowed for relatively large heterogeneity within a risk domain in terms of reported study and control populations, and for a low number of supporting risk estimates, both affecting precision. While this study purposefully did not include pneumococcal vaccination as a risk condition, we acknowledge that infection prevention and control (IPC) measures may affect the validity of the reported risk estimates. Relevant IPC parameters for risk of pneumococcal infection do not only concern pneumococcal vaccination practices, but also management of underlying risk conditions, pre-emptive use of antimicrobials, attention for exposition and hygiene, microbiological laboratory support etcetera. Therefore, in future studies that concern risk estimates for infectious diseases, we recommend reporting infection prevention and control (IPC) parameters in the study setting.

The most pronounced risk conditions with large relative risk sizes were in line with the current indications for adult pneumococcal vaccination as recommended by the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices.84 While in most adult IPD cohorts more than half of the cases is male,85 the relative risk size for IPD conferred by male sex was minor, as it was for smoking, African American ethnicity, and diabetes mellitus. Secondary immunodeficiency, a risk domain that included adults who use immunosuppressive medication, mediated a relative risk size that is comparable to those from chronic lung, liver, or heart disease or alcoholism. Conditions not eligible for meta-analysis but with relevant reported risk sizes for IPD were certain ethnicities, lower socio-economic status, and particularly homelessness. Although we excluded studies that reported risk factors for recurrent IPD, one study reported that preceding pneumonia conferred a significantly increased risk for IPD (IRR 5.5 and 2.9 for bacterial and influenza pneumonia respectively). The study that specifically focused on the risk profile for pneumococcal meningitis did not substantiate risk from chronic conditions other than past head or spinal injury.86

Studies that reported age category as risk condition for IPD had all applied adjustment for comorbidities. This indicates that while age may come with comorbidities, ageing in reasonable health only modestly increases one's risk for IPD. The meta-analysis for the 22 risk domains was performed differently, in that individuals with a risk condition were compared to members from the general population in the same age stratum. Therefore, the systematically larger relative risk sizes in the population below 65 years of age, may partially be attributed to a relatively fit comparator population. While age-stratification reduced the between-study heterogeneity, IPD risks likely alter gradually with age87 which may be better appreciated by modelling instead of pre-stratification.

The relevance of multimorbidity leading to risk stacking was previously described for community acquired pneumonia.82 Our analysis confirms this phenomenon, showing substantial increases in IRRs for IPD in the presence of more than 1 risk condition, also among patients below the age of 65 years old.

Compared to current practices, populations that likely benefit from greater attention for prevention of IPD are the younger adults with risk conditions -especially chronic kidney disease, homeless individuals, and those with Down syndrome.

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide an overview that allows for comparison between populations at-risk for developing IPD. These findings may assist policy makers and clinicians in informing patients about their risk of IPD and in recommending or developing intervention strategies, such as vaccination, lifestyle changes, or optimal management of underlying conditions. The risk ratios that we report do not show the absolute risk size for IPD nor the size of the population at risk, which are both relevant to evaluate potential effectiveness of preventive interventions. Future research should focus on quantifying the cumulative risk associated with multiple concurrent risk conditions for developing IPD, as well as substantiating the relative efficacy of vaccination and other preventive measures in vulnerable populations. While serotype prevalence is globally changing, it may be relevant to study whether certain pneumococcal variants variably affect populations with risk conditions.
