Nonnodulating Fagales retain the functional NODULE INCEPTION gene

Introduction

Root nodule symbiosis (RNS) depends on the transcription factor NODULE INCEPTION (NIN), whose loss has been linked to the repeated loss of symbiotic ability in plants. However, its evolutionary role outside legumes remains unclear. Here, we show that Fagales species retain functional NIN orthologs, including in nonnodulating lineages, and that these proteins can fully complement a Lotus japonicus nin mutant, indicating deep conservation of NIN function despite independent losses of RNS.

Some angiosperms and nitrogen‐fixing bacteria form a mutualistic relationship known as RNS. Plants provide carbon compounds, whereas bacteria supply fixed nitrogen within nodules, which are specialized symbiotic lateral organs on the roots. Members of the order Fabales and the genus Parasponia establish rhizobial‐induced RNS, whereas actinorhizal plants form Frankia‐induced RNS (Kistner & Parniske, 2002; Libourel et al., 2023). Actinorhizal species occur in the orders Fagales, Cucurbitales, and Rosales. These three orders, together with Fabales, constitute the monophyletic nitrogen‐fixing clade (NFC). Within the NFC, however, most species outside Fabales lack the ability to establish RNS.

RNS research has been conducted primarily using two model legumes, Lotus japonicus and Medicago truncatula (Roy et al., 2020). During the initiation of legume nodulation, Nod factors secreted by rhizobia are perceived by lysin motif (LysM) receptors (Limpens et al., 2003). Nod factor perception activates downstream kinase cascades and triggers Ca2+ oscillations (Charpentier et al., 2008, 2016; Wong et al., 2019; Cook et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). Subsequently, CYCLOPS/MtIPD3 and cytokinin signaling induce the gene expression of the transcription factor NODULE INCEPTION (NIN) (Yano et al., 2008; Heckmann et al., 2011). NIN activates the expression of its target genes (e.g. NF‐YA) to regulate rhizobial infection and nodule organogenesis (Schauser et al., 1999; Soyano et al., 2013). In Fabales, phylogenomic studies have shown that the loss of the NIN gene correlates strongly with the absence of RNS capability (Griesmann et al., 2018).

NIN belongs to the NLP transcription factor family. NLPs are primarily involved in nitrate responses. In legumes, NIN loses nitrate responsiveness due to mutations in the nitrate‐responsive domain (NRD) and functions specifically in the regulation of RNS (Schauser et al., 2005; Zhang et al., 2024). NIN protein exhibits constitutive nuclear localization. By contrast, the nuclear localization of most NLPs, including MtNLP1, LjNLP4, and AtNLP2, the NIN ortholog in Arabidopsis thaliana, is dependent on nitrate (Lin et al., 2018; Nishida et al., 2018; Durand et al., 2023). Recent studies have begun to reveal evolutionary molecular mechanisms of how NIN emerged from NLPs. A single amino acid substitution in AtNLP2 reduces its nitrate‐dependent nuclear localization, and as a result, its function becomes more similar to that of MtNIN (Liu et al., 2026). In addition, an NLP‐inherited motif confers broad DNA‐binding specificity to NIN, which is crucial for the regulation of RNS‐related genes (Nosaki et al., 2026).

The NIN and NF‐YA1 transcriptional regulatory systems for RNS are also conserved in Parasponia (Bu et al., 2020). With some exceptions, including jujube, nonnodulating plants in Rosales generally lack the NIN gene (van Velzen et al., 2018). In actinorhizal plants, Frankia activates NIN expression through the secretion of root hair deformation factors and a NIN‐activating factor (NINA) (Chabaud et al., 2016; Cissoko et al., 2018; Hocher et al., 2019). In Fagales, knockdown of Casuarina glauca NIN (CgNIN) attenuates RNS (Clavijo et al., 2015). The nonnodulating Juglans regia likely lost a functional NIN gene due to mutations in the predisposition associated cis‐regulatory element (PACE) region of the NIN promoter, including the putative CYCLOPS binding site (Cathebras et al., 2026). However, the correlation between the presence or absence of NIN and RNS capacity in Fagales has not been comprehensively investigated. Furthermore, it has been noted that NIN proteins outside Fabales exhibit fewer mutations in the NRD (Zhang et al., 2024). To address these questions, we investigated the presence of NIN genes in Fagales and examined their functional homology with Fabales NIN proteins.

Results

Nonnodulating species have NIN in Fagales

We first collected the closest homologs of NIN from 12 Fagales species, comprising three nodulating and nine nonnodulating species (Supporting Information Table S1). Previous studies have shown that the NIN/NLP family can be classified into three groups based on duplication events (Mu & Luo, 2019; Liu & Bisseling, 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). A eudicot‐specific duplication of group 3 divided it into the NLP1 subgroup and the NIN subgroup. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that nonnodulating species in Fagales also possess NIN genes belonging to the NIN subgroup (Fig. 1a). Lineage‐specific NIN duplication occurred in Corylus avellana. Therefore, in contrast to nonnodulating legumes (Cercis canadensis, Castanospermum australe, and Nissolia schottii), Fagales maintain NIN genes even after the loss of RNS ability.

CYCLOPS binds to semi‐palindromic nucleotide sequences in PACE to induce NIN expression (Yano et al., 2008; Cathebras et al., 2026). Alignment of Fagales NIN promoters revealed that the PACE was conserved in six of 10 NINs from nonnodulating species as well as in all NINs from nodulating species (Fig. S1). Fagaceae species (Fagus, Castanea, Notholithocarpus, and Quercus) do not establish RNS; however, their NIN promoters retain PACE. By contrast, nonnodulating Juglandaceae (Carya and Juglans) have lost the PACE. Within Betulaceae, Alnus (nodulating) and Corylus (nonnodulating) harbor PACE in their NIN promoters, whereas Betula and Carpinus have lost half and the entire semipalindromic structure of CYCLOPS binding sites, respectively.

In legumes, a conserved cytokinin response elements‐containing (CE) region exists upstream of the NIN coding sequence (CDS), which is required for NIN expression during nodule organogenesis (Liu et al., 2019). As suggested by Zhang et al. (2024), mVISTA analysis (http://genome.lbl.gov/vista/mvista) (Frazer et al., 2004) of 50 kb upstream of the NIN CDS did not identify a region homologous to the leguminous CE in Fagales NIN promoters (Fig. S2). However, the region c. 8 kb upstream of the CgNIN start codon is relatively well conserved across Fagales, except for the upstream regions of CaNINb and the NINs of Juglandaceae. Alignment of these genomic DNA sequences revealed that this conserved region contains several B‐type cytokinin signaling RR binding sites and a putative AP2 binding site, similar to the leguminous CE region (Figs S3, S4). Complementation analysis of Lotus japonicus nin‐9 mutant (Suzaki et al., 2012) suggested that the CE region and the PACE from C. glauca function similar to those of L. japonicus (Figs 1b, S5).

Alignment of amino acid sequences around the NRD indicated that Fagales NINs do not have the extensive mutations observed in legume NINs (Lotus and Medicago) (Fig. 1c). A conserved serine residue phosphorylated by calcium‐sensor protein kinases (CPKs) during nitrate activation in AtNLP7 (Liu et al., 2017) is also conserved in Fagales NINs, except in the NINs of Betulaceae. Taken together, it is likely that NIN is present in both nodulating and nonnodulating species in Fagales. The amino acid sequences of the NRD suggest that Fagales NINs may retain NLP‐like features, such as nitrate‐dependent activity.

Fagales NINs function independently of nitrate

To investigate whether nonnodulating Fagales species retain functional NIN proteins, we cloned the CDSs of C. avellana NINs (CaNINa/b). Because experimental work in woody Fagales is challenging, we conducted functional analyses using L. japonicus. For comparison, CgNIN was used as a representative of nodulating Fagales species.

We performed transactivation assays using L. japonicus mesophyll protoplasts. A reporter construct, 4xYB1, containing four tandem repeats of the NIN‐binding site from the LjNF‐YB promoter, was activated by all tested NINs, including CaNINa/b in the absence of nitrate (Fig. S6a). NITRITE REDUCTASE 1 (LjNIR1) is directly regulated by LjNLP4 in the presence of nitrate (Nishida et al., 2018). However, activation of the LjNIR1 promoter was not induced by Fagales NINs (Fig. S6b). These results suggest that both nodulating and nonnodulating Fagales species possess functional NIN proteins that can activate RNS‐related gene promoters and, like LjNIN, have lost nitrate responsiveness.

CaNINa/b work as RNS regulators

We next overexpressed these Fagales NINs in L. japonicus hairy roots to evaluate their ability to regulate gene expression. Although CaNINa showed relatively weak activity, overexpressed Fagales NINs induced the expression of NIN target genes, including LjNF‐YA, LjNF‐YB, EXOPOLYSACCHARIDE RECEPTOR 3 (LjEPR3), and RHIZOBIUM‐DIRECTED POLAR GROWTH (LjRPG), in the absence of rhizobia (Fig. 2a,b) (Kawaharada et al., 2017; Li et al., 2023). Overexpression of Fagales NINs caused deformations of root structures similar to that of LjNIN (Fig. S7).

To assess the functional equivalence of NINs in regulating RNS, we performed complementation tests of the L. japonicus nin‐9 mutant using NINs from both nodulating and nonnodulating species. Here, NINs from Parasponia andersonii and CgNIN were used as representative NINs of nonlegume nodulating plants. CgNIN and PaNIN rescued the RNS phenotype of the Ljnin‐9. Notably, CaNINa/b also complemented the phenotype, in which total nodule number, maximum nodule diameter, and the proportion of infection thread formation were comparable to those of LjNIN (Figs 2c, S8a,b). The proportion of plants with mature pink nodules was slightly lower in PaNIN and CaNINa (Fig. 2c). DsRED‐labeled rhizobia revealed colonization of rhizobia within nodule cells (Fig. 2c). Acetylene reduction assay, together with shoot growth and nodule coloration, indicated that the induced nodules had nitrogen‐fixing activity (Figs 2c, S8c). These results demonstrate that the CaNINa/b can regulate RNS in a manner similar to LjNIN.

Discussion

In this study, we clarified the correlation between the presence or absence of NIN and RNS ability in Fagales. In contrast to Fabales, the NIN coding region is retained in nonnodulating species of Fagales. NIN genes in nodulating Fagales, as well as in nonnodulating species belonging to Fagaceae and Betulaceae, completely or partially harbor conserved cis‐regulatory elements in their NIN's upstream regions. We found that NIN proteins encoded in the genomes of nodulating C. glauca and nonnodulating C. avellana function similar to LjNIN.

The PACE and CE regions are conserved in NIN promoters of some nodulating species and in certain nonnodulating species, including C. avellana and members of the Fagaceae. The Fagaceae diverged as the second earliest lineage within Fagales (Yang et al., 2021), suggesting that NIN already possessed all known features required for its expression during RNS before this divergence. Despite retaining a functional NIN, some species within Fagales lack the ability to establish RNS, indicating that this trait is independent of NIN and raising the question of which gene(s) contributed to the loss of RNS. One possibility is the loss or mutation of genes essential for RNS, such as RPG or receptors for Frankia‐derived signals. Griesmann et al. (2018) proposed that nonnodulating species within the NFC lack a functional RPG gene. Indeed, in Fagales, RPG is present in nodulating species but is absent or truncated due to premature stop codons in nonnodulating species such as C. avellana and Castanea sativa (Tables S3, S4). The roles of NIN beyond RNS regulation remain unclear; however, its strong conservation in nonnodulating Fagales suggests additional functions. Alternatively, it is possible that NIN has become functionally redundant but has not yet been lost from the genome in nonnodulating Fagales.

Nonlegume NINs, PaNIN, CgNIN, and CaNINa/b could rescue the nodulation phenotype of the Ljnin‐9 mutant. This indicates that the fundamental features of NIN required for RNS regulation are conserved within NFC. In recent studies, AtNLP2, a member of the NIN subclade, could induce infection thread and nodule formation in L. japonicus and M. truncatula nin mutants (Liu et al., 2026; Nosaki et al., 2026). However, AtNLP2 was insufficient for rhizobial accommodation and nitrogen fixation. These findings suggest that members of the NIN subclade differ in their molecular properties depending on whether they belong to the NFC or not. The loss of nitrate responsiveness in NINs of NFC is likely one contributing factor, but it is also probable that other NFC‐specific molecular features of NIN exist. Deciphering the evolutionary process by which NIN emerged will be useful for understanding how and how often NIN and RNS have evolved within NFC.

In the presence of nitrate, AtNLP7 is phosphorylated by activated group III CPKs at a conserved serine residue within its NRD and is subsequently localized to the nucleus (Liu et al., 2017). AtNLP7 additionally functions as a nitrate sensor by directly binding nitrate (Liu et al., 2022). As recent studies have indicated, Fagales NINs retain most of the NRD (Zhang et al., 2024). However, we showed that Fagales NINs have lost nitrate‐dependent activity. The loss of nitrate responsiveness in Fagales NINs suggests that uncharacterized elements, which are absent in Fagales NINs, may contribute to the nitrate responsiveness of NLPs. It is also intriguing why and how the NRD in Fabales NINs is drastically mutated compared to other members of the NFC.

Materials and Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Lotus japonicus (Regel) K.Larsen Miyakojima MG‐20 and Ljnin‐9 mutant were used in this study (Kawaguchi, 2000; Suzaki et al., 2012). Plants were grown with or without Mesorhizobium loti MAFF 303099 in autoclaved vermiculite with Broughton and Dilworth (B&D) solution (Broughton & Dilworth, 1971) under a 16 h : 8 h, light : dark cycle at 24°C.

Phylogenetic analysis

NIN/NLP protein sequences were retrieved from NCBI and Phytozome13 databases using blastp searches (Table S1). A multiple sequence alignment of full‐length NIN/NLPs was generated using the MUSCLE method implemented in MEGA11 (Tamura et al., 2021). The phylogenetic tree was constructed using the maximum likelihood method with IQ‐TREE2 (JTT + F + I + G4 substitution model) (Minh et al., 2020). The optimal tree with the sum of branch length is equal to 16.091. The percentage of replicate trees in which the associated taxa clustered together in the bootstrap test (1000 replicates) is shown next to the branches. The tree was rooted to the outgroup MpNLP and drawn to scale, with branch lengths in the same units as those of the evolutionary distances used to infer the phylogenetic tree. The evolutionary distances were computed using the Poisson correction method and were in the units of the number of amino acid substitutions per site (Nosaki et al., 2026).

Alignment of upstream regions of NIN

To explore PACE, the 1.5‐kb NIN promoter sequences were aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm implemented in MEGA11. A conserved motif corresponding to PACE was manually identified. To explore CE, the 50‐kb upstream DNA sequences from the NIN coding region were aligned using the mVISTA web‐based alignment tool with default settings. CE regions were identified within conserved regions based on the presence of putative AP2 binding motifs.

Constructs

Descriptions of the cloning vectors for transactivation assay reporter plasmids, overexpression experiments or complementation tests of Ljnin‐9 mutant were previously published (Ito et al., 2024; Nosaki et al., 2026). LjUBQ pro :LjNIN and CE (5 kb)‐LjNIN pro (3.3 kb): LjNIN were previously created (Nosaki et al., 2026). To generate chimeric promoters containing CgCE and/or CgPACE, three fragments spanning the regions upstream of LjCE, between LjCE and LjPACE, and downstream of LjPACE were amplified by PCR from a CE (5 kb)–LjNIN pro (3.3 kb) construct. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2. The core region of CgCE was artificially synthesized. These DNA fragments, together with LjNIN ter (Nosaki et al., 2026) were cloned to pCAMBIA1300‐GFP, and the LjNIN CDS was inserted downstream of the promoters by in‐fusion reaction. For transactivation assay reporter plasmids, the 4xYB1 fragment, in which four tandem repeats of LjNIN‐binding site on the LjNF‐YB promoters were fused with 35S minimal promoter, and LjNIR1 pro fragment were cloned upstream of NanoLuc. The CDSs of CgNIN and PaNIN were amplified by PCR using cDNA as the template. The genomic DNA sequences from start codons to stop codons of CaNINa/b were amplified by PCR and cloned downstream of LjUBQ pro . Each exon of the CaNINa/b CDS was individually amplified by PCR using plasmids which harbor genomic sequences of CaNINa/b. PCR products of CDS were cloned downstream of LjUBQ pro or CE (5 kb)‐LjNIN pro (3.3 kb) by in‐fusion reaction.

Transactivation assay

The isolation of L. japonicus mesophyll protoplasts and the transactivation assay were conducted based on the method (Nishida et al., 2021). Specifically, plants were grown for 14 d with 5 mM NH4Cl. Fluorescence and luminescence were measured using a Synergy LX (Biotek, Winooski, VT, USA). The luminescence of Nano‐Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega) was measured. Constructs for overexpression experiments were used as effector plasmids. For the transformation of protoplasts, an equal amount of DNA (2.5 μg each) of effector, reporter, and internal control plasmids was used. 35S pro :GUS was used as the internal control plasmid.

Hairy root transformation of L. japonicus

Seeds were germinated on the germination medium (1/2× Gamborg's B5 salt mixture (Wako, Osaka, Japan), 1/2× Gamborg's vitamin solution (Sigma), 1% sucrose, 1% agar) in a growth cabinet (24°C dark for first 2 d, 24°C 16 h : 8 h, light : dark cycle for next 2 d). Agrobacterium rhizogenes AR1193 strains harboring each construct were streaked on YEP plate with appropriate antibiotics for 2 d at 28°C. Seedlings were placed in the A. rhizogenes suspension and then cut at the base of the hypocotyls. The seedlings with cotyledons were transferred onto hairy root medium (1× Gamborg's B5 salt mixture, 1× Gamborg's vitamin solution, 2% sucrose, 1% agar) and were grown in a growth cabinet (24°C dark for first 1 d, 24°C 16 h light : 8 h dark cycle for next 2 d). Then, the plants were transferred onto fresh hairy root medium containing 12.5 μg ml−1 meropen and were grown for 7 d in a growth cabinet (24°C 16 h : 8 h, light : dark cycle). Transgenic roots were identified by GFP fluorescence, and the plants with transgenic hairy roots were used for further experiments (Ito et al., 2024).

Gene expression analysis

The primers used for quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR) are listed in Table S2. Total RNA was isolated from respective tissues using the Plant Total RNA Mini Kit (Favorgen Biotech, Pingtung, Taiwan). First‐strand cDNA was prepared using the ReverTra Ace quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover (Toyobo). qRT‐PCR was performed using a CFX Opus 384 Real‐Time PCR system (BIO‐RAD) with a Next SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo) following the manufacturer's instructions (Nosaki et al., 2026).

Acetylene reduction assay

The nitrogenase activity of nodules was indirectly determined by measuring the acetylene reductase activity (Nishida et al., 2018). Transformed plants with pink nodules were put into 20 ml vials. Subsequently, acetylene was injected into the vials. After incubation for 10 min, the amount of ethylene production was measured using a GC‐2014 (Shimadzu).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism v.9 (GraphPad Software) (Nosaki et al., 2026). Normality was checked using the Shapiro–Wilk test and P > 0.05 was considered normal distribution. The F‐test was used to test whether the variances of the two populations were equal. Appropriate methods were chosen according to the nature of the data. The criterion of P < 0.05 means a statistically significant difference in this study.
