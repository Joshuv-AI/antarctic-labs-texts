Determination of Mean Energy and Energy Resolution of Standardized Diagnostic X-ray Beams from the Spectra Measured Using a Portable Cadmium Zinc Telluride Spectrometer
Authors: Rahul Kumar Chaudhary, Sudhir Kumar, S. D. Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:The objective of this work was to measure the spectra of standardized diagnostic X-ray beams (DXRBs) using a cadmium zinc telluride (CdZnTe) spectrometer. The mean energy as well as energy resolution were also estimated from the measured spectra.Materials and Methods:The CdZnTe radiation detector of nominal volume 500 mm3 in combination with an in-house-developed tungsten pinhole collimator was used to measure the spectra of standardized DXRBs of nominal energies 40 kV to 100 kV. The detector was placed at a distance of 1000 mm from the focal spot of the X-ray tube, and the field size of 200 mm × 200 mm was opened. The 241Am, 133Ba, and 57Co reference gamma ray sources were used for the calibration of the CdZnTe spectrometer. The measured spectra were corrected using the stripping procedure and the methodology was validated by comparing the corrected and reference spectra. The mean energy values and energy resolution were determined from the corrected spectra. The effective energies of beams were also determined from the first half-value layer measured experimentally.Results:The mean energies estimated from corrected X-ray spectra agreed within 5% for the beam qualities studied. The energy resolution deteriorated with the energy of DXRBs.Conclusions:In-house-developed pinhole collimator, and CdZnTe spectrometer were successfully used for the measurement of DXRBs spectra. The X-ray beams have been fully characterized for the calibration of diagnostic X-ray dosimeters and quality control instruments.

INTRODUCTION

The quality of diagnostic X-ray beams (DXRBs) is specified either by (a) tube voltage and first half-value layer (HVL), (b) mean energy, or (c) effective energy of the X-ray beam spectrum.[1] For the complete characterization of DXRBs, the spectrum of the photon beam emerging out of the X-ray tube is required. The spectrum can be used to determine the beam parameters such as tube voltage,[2] energy fluence rate,[3] homogeneity coefficient,[4] and HVL[5] etc., The most important use of the spectrum of DXRBs is in the determination of dosimetric parameters such as exposure, air-kerma, and absorbed dose distribution inside a tissue or water equivalent phantom.[6] The knowledge of dosimetric parameters provides a correct estimate of the energy dependence of the response of the radiation detectors, a common problem with the radiation dosimeters when used in the diagnostic energy range.[7] Therefore, accurate measurement of the spectrum of DXRBs is important.

Theoretical methods have been used to obtain the spectra of DXRBs.[8910111213] The limitation with theoretically obtained spectra is that the information related to the technical parameters of the diagnostic X-ray tube is known with limited accuracy. This can reduce the accuracy of the calculated spectrum.[7] Therefore, measurement is the best way to get the spectrum of DXRBs. However, the spectrometry of DXRB is complex and time-consuming and requires requisite expertise.[14]

A major problem encountered in the measurement of spectra of DXRBs is the high photon fluence rate. In general radiography, the photon fluence rate can be as high as 107 photons/mm2/s at 2 meters distance from the focal spot of the X-ray tube operated at 100 kV and 15 mA.[15] At such a high photon fluence rate, the spectrometric system (detector and pulse counting electronics) suffers from the problem of pulse pile-up. As a result, the measured spectrum is distorted in the energy distribution of detected photons (peak pile-up) and has worsened energy resolution (tail pile-up).[16] In order to handle the problem of pulse pile-up, the photon fluence rate at the location of the radiation detector needs to be reduced. One way of achieving this is to increase the focal spot to detector distance. But the distance cannot be increased beyond a limit in some applications, for example, mammography, where the source-to-imager distance is fixed (65 cm). Another method of reducing the photon fluence rate is to use the combination of reduced X-ray tube current and a pinhole collimator. The use of a pinhole collimator for the measurement of X-ray spectra is supported by several studies in the past.[1718192021]

The combination of a pinhole collimator with a suitable radiation detector is generally used to measure the spectrum of DXRBs. The requirements for an ideal radiation detector for X-ray spectroscopy are: (a) high count rate capability, (b) good energy resolution, and (c) high photopeak efficiency. Gaseous radiation detectors are known to have poor radiation detection efficiency, medium count rate capability, and fair energy resolution. Inorganic scintillation detectors (NaI, CsI) have good detection efficiency and high-count rate capability. However, their resolution is not sufficient enough to resolve the radiation beam of bremsstrahlung origin.[22] These limitations with gaseous and inorganic scintillators-based radiation detectors hamper their use for the spectroscopy of DXRBs.

Semiconductor detectors offer an acceptable compromise in the performance between gaseous and inorganic scintillator detectors. They have high physical densities, compact size, and relatively fast timing characteristics etc.[16] which makes them suitable for DXRB spectroscopy. High-purity germanium (HPGe) detectors have high radiation detection efficiency due to thick crystals and excellent charge transport properties.[19] However, HPGe detectors require cryogenic cooling to reduce the thermal noise. The cooling system makes this spectroscopy system bulky and limits its use for the spectrum measurements in a clinical setup.

Cadmium zinc telluride (CdZnTe) and cadmium telluride (CdTe) detectors have the advantage over HPGe detectors as they can be operated at room temperature.[192324] The room temperature operation of these detectors is possible due to a high band gap (1.47 eV for CdTe and 1.57 eV for CdZnTe, at 300 K), which results in a small leakage current (a few tens of nA).[22] In the past study, a CdTe detector was operated at low bias voltage (90 V) for the spectrum measurements of DXRBs. This was done to reduce the detector efficiency so that the detector could be used without the requirement of a pinhole collimator. Low voltage operation of the CdTe detector led to the problem of severe charge carrier trapping, resulting in the spectral distortion.[25]

CdZnTe crystals have a high bulk resistivity of 1011 Ω.cm because of that it has low leakage current and low noise characteristics. The presence of high atomic number elements such as Cd0.9(Z = 48), Zn0.1(Z = 30), and Te (Z = 52) results in a high effective atomic number (Zeff = 49.6), which increases the radiation absorption efficiency. The high mass density (ρ = 5.8 g.cm−3) provides high stopping power to the detector crystal.[26] The CdZnTe detector was chosen in the present study due to its favorable properties for the DXRBs spectroscopy, along with the advantages of the general physical properties of a semiconductor detector.

The number of photons per second at the measurement location should be reduced to approximately 104 photons/s, which is within the measuring range of semiconductor detectors.[17] Considering this requirement, a pinhole collimator was specifically designed and fabricated in-house for use with the CdZnTe detector. The combination of a pinhole collimator and CdZnTe detector was used to measure the spectra of DXRBs. The X-ray beams were standardized previously in terms of HVL[27] using an in-house-developed Free Air Ionization Chamber (FAIC), which adds to the novelty of the present work. The measured spectra were corrected for distortions, validated, and used for the determination of mean energy and resolution of standardized DXRBs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cadmium zinc telluride spectrometer

The spectrometry system consists of a CdZnTe crystal (CZT/500(S) [“S” means super grade (detector with high spectroscopy performance)], Ritec, Riga, Latvia) of nominal volume 500 mm3 as radiation detector and a miniature multichannel analyzer module (MCA-166-USB, version 3.0, GBS Elektronic, GmbH, Germany). The spectral data gets collected in the memory of MCA-166-USB and is transferred via a serial interface to the computer. A software (WinSPEC Version 2.08.002, GBS Elektronic, GmbH, Germany) was used to analyze the measured spectra. A miniature, low noise charge-sensitive preamplifier (Model PA 101C, Ritec, Riga, Latvia), along with other associated electronics, enclosed in a separate module, was used with the CdZnTe detector for the spectroscopy of DXRBs.

Design and fabrication of pinhole collimator

The pinhole collimator consists of a hollow open-ended cylindrical tungsten tube (density 19.3 g/cm3) of external and internal diameters 29 mm and 21 mm respectively, and length 60 mm. The end of the tungsten tube facing the radiation source can be fitted with a circular tungsten disc (outer disc) of 21 mm diameter and 4 mm thickness, having a pinhole of 0.8 mm diameter at the center. The outer disc is separated from another similar inner concentric disc having a pinhole of diameter 0.5 mm at the center by an air equivalent material spacer of 25 mm length. The alignment of pinholes at the center of inner and outer discs was ensured at the time of machining the discs. The electrode of the digital machine was moved by equal distances of 10.5 mm from outer to inner direction along the X- and Y-axes of the machine over the tungsten disc of diameter 21 mm. The point of intersection of the movement of the electrode along the two axes gives the position of the pinhole of the disc. The positioning and movement accuracy of the electrode of the machine were of the order of a few micrometers. The tungsten discs and the wall of the cylindrical tube were constructed to provide the attenuation equivalent to four tenth-value layer at 150 kV X-ray energy. The outer disc reduces photon fluence rate to the detection limit of the spectrometer, whereas the inner disc is provided to attenuate the K-fluorescence X-rays generated in the outer disc if the maximum energy of incident X-ray beam exceeds the K-edge energy of the tungsten disc.[1722] The separation between outer and inner discs ensures that only the primary component of the X-ray beam reaches the CdZnTe detector. Three grub screws were provided at 120° apart to hold the CdZnTe detector in place. Figure 2 shows the drawing of a cylindrical tungsten tube (adopter) along with the discs of the pinhole collimator system.

The sequence of components inside the cylindrical tungsten adopter from the direction facing the X-ray beam is: 0.8 mm pinhole disc, followed by an air-equivalent spacer, and then a 0.5 mm disc. Alignment of pinhole collimator

In order to check the alignment of the X-ray source, pinhole collimator, and CdZnTe detector, the methods suggested in the literature were followed.[1829] Initially, the pinhole collimator was aligned with the central X-ray beam using the cross-hair marked on the diaphragm of the X-ray tube collimator. Keeping the field light of the X-ray tube ON, the pinhole collimator was adjusted in such a way that the field light source was visible through the pinholes of the inner and outer discs. The eye was positioned at the location of the detector. Without disturbing the position of the pinhole collimator, the CdZnTe detector was placed in the slot inside the pinhole collimator, and X-ray exposure was given. The pinhole collimator and CdZnTe detector combination was adjusted about the initial position until the maximum count rate was achieved. This position was fixed for the measurement of spectra of DXRBs.

Correction of the measured spectra

Factors causing the distortion of measured spectra

The measured spectra are distorted compared to the actual spectra. The factors contributing to the distortion are (a) transmission of primary X-rays through the detector crystal, this effect is more pronounced at higher X-ray energies, (b) charge carrier trapping reduces the height of the output pulse and results in spectral distortion in both X and γ rays. Tailing appears in the spectra and distortion arises towards the lower energy region of the X-ray spectra. Charge carrier trapping occurs due to the presence of impurities or defects in the crystal lattice, and (c) the K-fluorescent X-rays of cadmium and tellurium possess energy in the range 20-32 keV, they escape readily from the CdZnTe crystal. The K-fluorescent X-rays are generated as a result of photoelectric interaction of incident X-rays with the detector crystal.[23] As a result of the K-fluorescent X-rays escape, a certain fraction of photon interactions is counted in the lower energy channels.

Stripping procedure for the correction of measured spectra

The interaction of radiation in the diagnostic energy range with the detector crystal is mainly by photoelectric mode, hence Compton interaction is negligible. The thickness of the detector crystal was sufficient enough to prevent the escape of secondary electrons produced as a result of interaction. The pulse pile-up was taken care of by pinhole collimator and the application of low tube current for the production of X-rays. A step-by-step procedure, starting from the highest energy channel, was followed for correcting the number of photon counts. The highest energy channel does not have any Compton or K-escape contribution; therefore, dividing the photon counts of this channel directly by full-energy peak efficiency gives the true photon counts in the channel. The K-escape peak of a monoenergetic beam of this energy was subtracted from the remaining measured spectrum. The above two steps were repeated subsequently for all the lower energy channels.[33] The stripping procedure was performed on a simple desktop-type computer. The total K-escape of X-rays and full-energy peak efficiency as a function of photon energy were taken from the literature for the CdZnTe detector for the purpose of stripping.[24] The full-energy peak efficiency was determined experimentally, corresponding to the photon energies from 57Co, 133Ba, 152Eu, and 241Am reference sources.[24]

Validation of corrected spectra

The measured spectra were corrected following the stripping procedure. The corrected spectra were compared with the corresponding reference spectra[34] for RQR2 to RQR8 beams for the validation of the measurement and spectra correction procedure. The mean energies were also compared for the quantification of agreement between the corrected and reference spectra. Mean energy was chosen for validation purposes because it is one of the parameters to characterize the quality of the X-ray beams.[35] For illustrative purpose, the comparisons of spectra for RQR2 (40 kV), RQR6 (81 kV), and RQR8 (100 kV) beams are shown.

DISCUSSIONS

The measurement of spectra of standardized DXRBs was made possible by reducing the tube current and increasing the exposure time to maximum. This brought down the number of photons per second within the measuring limits of the spectrometer, which is generally of the order of 104 photons/sec. For a higher number of photons per second, the diameter of the pinhole of discs should be of the order of a few micrometers. However, there is a practical limitation associated with the conventional machining methods. The smallest pinhole diameter that could be achieved on the hard material, like tungsten, was 0.5 mm. Therefore, the pinhole diameter of the fabricated pinhole collimator was of the order of sub-millimeter; laser technology is specialized for achieving the pinholes of micrometer sizes. Modern MCAs with fast photon-counting electronics are available commercially, thus reducing the dependency on a pinhole collimator for spectrum measurement.

The energy calibration of the spectrometer was performed by placing the calibrated gamma ray sources horizontally in contact with the beryllium (Be) window of the CdZnTe radiation detector. The attenuation of photon beams by the Be window has been neglected in the present study. The maximum count rate during the acquisition of the spectra for calibration was under 1500 counts per second. This ensured that the dead time of the spectroscopy system was under 1%. The nominal beam energy for the RQR6 beam quality in this measurement was 81 kV instead of 80 kV (voltage setting as per TRS-457 document). This is because 80 kV setting was not available in the X-ray machine used in this work.

The difference between mean energies determined from the corrected spectra and the values reported in the literature may be attributed to the use of approximate k-escape and full-energy peak efficiency functions. Furthermore, the spectra were not corrected for the distortions resulting from (a) the use of a pinhole collimator, (b) charge carrier trapping. The stripping procedure employed for the correction of spectra included the corrections for k-escape and full-energy peak efficiency only. Negative values of photon counts appeared in the lower energy channels due to overcorrection. These channels were not considered for further correction of the remaining part of the spectrum.[33] Despite this, the agreement between corrected and reference spectra was better than 5%, which is within the measurement uncertainty. This validates the accuracy of the measurement and correction of the spectra of the standardized DXRBs using the developed pinhole collimator.

CONCLUSIONS

In-house-developed pinhole collimator and CdZnTe spectrometer were successfully used for the measurement of DXRBs spectra. The characteristic parameters of the standardized DXRBs were determined from the corrected spectra. The agreement between corrected and reference spectra validates the procedure of correcting the measured X-ray beam spectra. The X-ray beams have been fully characterized in terms of mean energy, energy resolution, and HVL for the calibration of diagnostic X-ray dosimeters and quality control instruments.
