Research Note: Three-dimensional microstructural basis for chicken eggshell translucency
Authors: Lin Xuan, Xuefeng Shi, Guiyun Xu, Jiangxia Zheng
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Eggshell translucency, an externally observable feature, can influence consumer purchase intentions. The eggshell quality often deteriorates in aging laying hens, leading to increased eggshell translucency. Eggs exchange water vapor through the pores. It is commonly believed that the moisture remaining in the mastoid space contributes to translucent egg formation. However, the precise mechanisms underlying eggshell translucency require further structural investigations. This study aimed to explore the 3-dimensional microstructure of eggshells using micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) to provide insights into the microstructural basis of eggshell translucency. The results revealed that the ratio of pore volume to mastoid space volume was significantly higher in the high-translucency (TH) group than that in the low-translucency (TL) group (P < 0.01); however, the ratio of mastoid space to eggshell volume showed no significant difference. Additionally, measurements of the individual pore morphological features showed that the roundness of the pores in the TH group was significantly higher than that in the TL group, whereas the flatness of the pores in the TH group was significantly lower (P = 0.01). Eggshell porosity directly reflects the water vapor exchange capacity of eggshells, with the TL group exhibiting significantly higher eggshell porosity compared to the TH group (P = 0.02). In conclusion, based on observations of the 3-dimensional microstructure of eggshells, this study elucidated how the volume and morphological characteristics of pores influence the water vapor exchange capacity of eggshells, resulting in eggshell translucency.

INTRODUCTION

Consumers prefer eggs as cost-effective and high-quality sources of protein (Sharaf Eddin et al., 2019). The primary determinant of consumers’ purchasing decisions regarding eggshells is the external quality of the eggs. These external quality factors include the presence of gray spots on the eggshell surface that are visible to the naked eye under natural light conditions. The existence of moisture spots on eggs diminishes their external quality, negatively affecting consumers’ desire to purchase eggs and potentially resulting in economic losses for egg producers (Wang et al., 2017).

Extending the laying period has emerged as a focal point of research in pursuit of improving the efficiency of laying hens and enhancing animal welfare in poultry farming. Sustaining egg quality and production performance in aging laying hens is critical for achieving an extended laying period. Previous studies have demonstrated that peak egg production in commercial laying hens typically persists until approximately 60 wk of age (Molnár et al., 2016). However, production performance, egg quality, and eggshell moisture spot quality tend to thereafter decline with advancing age. This decline in quality attributes could lead to a reduction in the external appearance of eggshells, resulting in potential losses during transportation and a subsequent decrease in consumer purchasing intentions (Molnár et al., 2016).

The primary etiology of eggshell translucency arises from microstructural disparities in the eggshells (Holst et al., 1932). The current understanding posits that alterations in the mammillary layer and eggshell membrane structure contribute to moisture accumulation within the eggshell matrix (Wang et al., 2019). Moreover, it is well established that factors, such as the breed and age of the laying hen, as well as dietary composition and environmental conditions, influence eggshell translucency (Talbot and Tyler, 1974). Additionally, laying hens experiencing thermal stress exhibit changes in eggshell microstructure, thereby augmenting the occurrence of translucent eggs (Shi et al., 2023).

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) is a rapid and high-resolution microscopic in situ 3-dimensional imaging technique that operates at the micrometer level. Micro-CT utilizes variations in X-ray absorption due to differences in sample density to capture 2-dimensional projection images while the sample rotates uniformly through 360° under X-ray irradiation. Micro-CT utilizes variations in X-ray absorption resulting from differences in sample density to capture 2-dimensional projection images. Subsequently, extensive computation and reconstruction of these 2-dimensional images enable the realization of a comprehensive 3-dimensional representation. Analytical applications of micro-CT have been extensively explored in diverse fields, including medicine, food science, geology, materials science, and agriculture. The grayscale images obtained through micro-CT reflect the degree of X-ray attenuation of the sample. For constant X-ray energy, a higher sample density at a given pixel results in greater X-ray attenuation, which corresponds to higher grayscale values in the image (Yu et al., 2016). Theoretically, considering the differences in composition and content of proteins and minerals in eggshells, the use of micro-CT for eggshell analysis is viable.

In current research, the observation of eggshell microstructure is conventionally conducted through scanning electron microscopy (SEM). SEM allows for microstructure observations within a scanning range of approximately 0.6 mm. While SEM imaging results do offer insights into the 3-dimensional aspects of eggshell microstructure to some extent, it is crucial to note that these images inherently remain 2-dimensional representations and may not accurately depict the intricacies of the 3-dimensional eggshell microstructure. In contrast, micro-CT, while maintaining a consistent examination timeframe, has magnified the scanning range by a factor of 1,600 and provides precise, 3-dimensional reconstructions of the eggshell structure. This advancement enables us to achieve a significantly more accurate understanding of the intricacies of eggshell structure.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to use high-performance and high-precision micro-CT technology to observe the 3-dimensional microstructure of eggshells. Furthermore, we systematically measured the volume of eggshells and mastoid spaces in high and low translucency eggs, as well as the morphological characteristics of the pores, to investigate the relationship between translucency and pores.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental Materials and Feeding Management

The experiment was conducted at the Experimental Unit for Poultry Genetic Resources and Breeding of the China Agricultural University, China. Prior to the experiment, we continuously collected 540 eggs from all pink-shell-laying hens for 2 wk and we recorded the identification numbers of the respective hens on the collected eggs. Subsequently, after a storage period of 5 d, we calculated the proportion of translucent areas in these eggs. Based on the calculation results, we selected eggs with translucent area measurements falling within the top 40 and bottom 40 hens for further analysis. Using the identification numbers marked on the eggs, we identified the corresponding laying hens, designating the top 40 hens with a higher translucent area percentage as the TH group and the bottom 40 hens with a lower translucent area percentage as the TL group.

Once the TH and TL groups were assigned, we collected 35 eggs during the experimental period to assess their translucent quality. The experiments were conducted at 72 wk of age.

Eggshell Translucency Measurements

After a storage period of 5 d, an automated identification method was employed to measure the area of the moisture spots, and subsequently, the proportion of the moisture spot area was computed. This method facilitates the full automation of moisture spot area recognition and enables batch processing, thereby expediting and streamlining the measurement of moisture spot characteristics. The experimental procedure involved illuminating chicken eggs with an LED cold light source, followed by capturing random eggshell contours using a digital camera placed at a vertical distance of 15 cm. The obtained images were then subjected to 8-bit conversion using the Origin software to provide raw samples for subsequent image processing. A copy of the original image was created and subjected to Gaussian blur and difference calculations to enhance the moisture spot region contours. Subsequently, the enhanced contour was added to the original image to generate a sample image with amplified moisture spot boundaries. The segmented image samples were imported into the “trainable Weka segmentation” plugin, and 5 different image feature extractors, namely Gaussian blur, Gaussian function differentiation, membrane projection, Hessian matrix, and Sobel filters, were applied to obtain image features. These extracted feature values were utilized as classification criteria for moisture spot automatic recognition using the “trainable Weka segmentation” plugin. Finally, ImageJ was used to measure the overall eggshell contour size. From these data, we obtained the area of the dark spots, as well as the total eggshell area. We then calculated the ratio of the sum of the spot areas on the whole eggshell to the sum of the spot areas on the whole eggshell (RSS). RSS served as our criterion for evaluating the severity of dark spots on eggs, with higher RSS values indicating more pronounced moisture spots on eggshells. This automated approach allows for the efficient and accurate assessment of moisture spot characteristics in a high-throughput manner, enhancing the overall assessment process in poultry science.

Eggshell Microstructure

We selected 3 eggs with the highest RSS and 3 eggs with the lowest RSS from the eggs collected at 72 wk for Eggshell Microstructure analysis. Samples were scanned using a Phoenix V|tome|x M (GE Sensing and Inspection Technologies GmbH, Wunstorf, Germany) microfocus CT system. The scanner was set at 85 kV electron acceleration energy and 160 μA current from the X-ray source with no filter. The settings were kept constant for each scan. A total of 1,800 projection images were collected using detector exposure for each eggshell. Six eggshells, 3 each from the high translucency group (TH) and low translucency group (TL), were selected for scanning.

Volumetric data were reconstructed using the 3Dslicer software. Once the volumetric data were successfully constructed, the threshold module of the segmentation functionality was employed to select the eggshell region automatically. Subsequently, upon completing the eggshell model construction, the “fill holes” module was applied to encompass and model the lacuna region. The resulting models were subjected to subtraction to generate lacuna-interconnected layers. Within the eggshell model, the void spaces were manually filled, and subtraction was performed between the filled model and the original eggshell model, resulting in the identification of all viable pore models. The viable pore models were then processed using the “island” function for noise removal and segmentation, thereby separating all viable pores into individual models. Measurements were conducted for eggshell, lacuna-interconnecting, and independently segmented viable pore models. Using the labelmap mode, relevant data were computed, yielding valuable insights into the characteristics of the identified features, as follows: shell membrane thickness (SMT), eggshell volume (ESV), mastoid space volume (MSV), ratio of mastoid space volume to eggshell volume (RME), pores number (PN), pore volume (PV), ratio of pore volume to mastoid space volume (RPM), pore surface area (PSA), eggshell porosity (EP), pore roundness (PR), pore flatness (PF), and pore angle (PA).

Eggshell porosity, PSA divided by eggshell thickness (EST), serves as a direct indicator of the exchange of metabolic gases and water vapor within the eggshell (Jaeckle et al., 2012). The PR was calculated from the ratio of the area of the sphere calculated from the Feret diameter to the actual area. A value of 100 indicated a spherical structure. The PF was calculated from the square root of the ratio of the second smallest principal moment to the smallest. A value of zero indicates a flat structure. To objectively quantify the PA, we defined the angle of the pores as the principal axis around the Z-axis and XY plane. This allowed us to measure and quantify the orientation of each pore in 3-dimensional space, providing a comprehensive assessment of pore arrangement and distribution within the eggshell structure.

Egg Quality

At 72 wk, egg quality was assessed. Thirty eggs were collected from each group to determine egg quality by measuring egg weight (EW), eggshell strength (ESS), EST, and eggshell color. ESS was measured using a Model-II eggshell strength tester (Robotmation, Tokyo, Japan). EST was performed using a micrometer screw gauge to measure 3 eggshell positions (bottom, middle, and top), and the average value was calculated. The eggshell color is the average of 3 different shell areas (blunt end, middle end, and sharp end) measured using a portable spectrophotometer (CM-2600d, Konica Minolta, Inc., Tokyo, Japan). After obtaining the L*, a*, and b* values, we applied a normalization function to calculate the quantity of protoporphyrin (Qp). The conversion formula used for normalization is as follows (Zeng et al., 2022):Qp=1.042+0.0137La+0.0763ab−0.0014Lab

Statistical Analysis

Outliers—values outside mean ± 3 SD—were excluded. Data were tested for normal distribution. The basic descriptive statistical results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SEM). Before analysis, the percentage data for RSS (<30%) were subjected to data conversion using the following formula:Y=Arcsin(Sqrt(X))where X represents the observed values and Y the values after conversion.

Outliers—values outside mean ± 3 SD—were excluded. Data were tested against a normal distribution. The 2 groups’ data were then analyzed using Student t tests (TTEST Procedure) in the SPSS software (version 25.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL), assuming homogeneity of variance in the sample populations.

Eggshell Microstructure

To further explore the reasons behind eggshell translucency, we measured eggshell microstructure using micro-CT technology. The results obtained through micro-CT were 3-dimensional and each eggshell yielded approximately 2,000 X-ray image samples. Previous studies have employed SEM to observe the microstructure of eggshells. Each SEM image sample is typically around 500 μm in size, enabling accurate measurements of mastoid space height and width, thus providing a reliable microscopic foundation for understanding the formation of eggshell translucency (Wang et al., 2017; Shi et al., 2023). However, the eggshell is a 3-dimensional entity, constituted by a complex interplay of spatial dimensions. In this study, by utilizing micro-CT technology, we significantly increased the observable area of individual samples to 16 mm2, which corresponds to an enlargement of 32,000 times compared with SEM (Lensink et al., 2023). Interestingly, during the construction of the mastoid space model, the mastoid spaces were interconnected rather than forming distinct individual cavity structures (Figure 1A). Subsequently, we computed the eggshell volume, mastoid space volume, and the ratio of mastoid space volume to eggshell volume. No significant differences were found between the TH and TL groups, which align with previous research, where no significant differences in the mastoid space area were observed among the various eggshell translucency groups (P < 0.01). Additionally, eggshell membrane thickness in the TH group was significantly higher than that in the TL group (P < 0.01), consistent with prior studies (Shi et al., 2023). Interestingly, when examining the X-ray image samples, it was noted that in the TH group, many mastoid cores were located on the inner side of the eggshell membrane (i.e., within the internal region of the egg). However, this phenomenon was not observed in the TL group (Figure 1B). This finding suggests a potential link with disturbances in the eggshell mineralization process, necessitating further measurements and experimental verification.

In our study, we considered the significance of pores in facilitating water vapor exchange between the egg and the external environment, leading us to hypothesize that viable pores play a crucial role in water gathering within the mastoid spaces. Thus, we measured the pores; quantified their quantity, volume, and surface area; and evaluated their porosity, roundness, and flatness characteristics, which describe their morphological features (Figure 1C). Additionally, we explored the potential interactions between the pores and other structural elements, as we believe that these interactions may be critical for eggshell translucency. To assess the efficiency of each viable pore in water vapor exchange, we determined the ratio of the pore volume to the mastoid space volume. Furthermore, we quantified the orientation of the individual pores by measuring the angle formed between the principal axes around the Z-axis and the XY plane. Table 1 presents the obtained data.

Notably, our analysis revealed no significant differences in PN between the TH and TL groups. However, the PV in the TH group was significantly lower than that in the TL group (P < 0.01). Moreover, the RPM in the TH group was significantly lower than that in the TL group (P < 0.01). Regarding the morphological characteristics of the viable pores, the roundness and flatness of the pores in the TH group were markedly lower than those in the TL group (P = 0.01). Roundness represents the degree of pore sphericity, with higher roundness values indicating a closer resemblance to a sphere, thus allowing for greater water passage. Flatness refers to the number of branches possessed by a pore. Pores with higher flatness values have more branches. The morphological features of pores with high and low roundness and flatness are showed in models (Figure 1C). In the TH group, most pores were narrow, nonspherical, and had numerous branches that did not connect to the eggshell surface. This configuration might result in prolonged water retention within the pores, subsequently reducing the water vapor exchange efficiency. In contrast, the TL group displayed predominantly spherical pores with fewer branches. Although the differences in the viable pore surface area were not significant, the TH group showed a declining trend in the viable pore surface area. To further substantiate the relationship between pore morphology characteristics and the efficiency of water vapor exchange in eggshells, we calculated the eggshell porosity. Eggshell porosity directly reflects the water vapor exchange capacity of the eggshell (Jaeckle et al., 2012). The results reveal that the eggshell porosity of the TL group is significantly higher than that of the TH group (P = 0.02). This finding suggests that the morphology characteristics of the pores indeed enhance the water vapor exchange efficiency of the eggshell, consequently leading to the occurrence of eggshell translucency.

Talbot and Tyler suggested that the high porosity of the translucent area causes the contents to penetrate the eggshell at a faster rate than water vapor can escape, resulting in water accumulation and eggshell translucency (Talbot and Tyler, 1974). These observations align with our insights into the 3-dimensional microstructure of the eggshell, where we further elucidated the microstructural differences influencing the differences in water vapor evaporation rates. Additionally, our pore orientation measurements did not reveal any significant differences between the groups, with viable pores displaying angles of approximately 1.5° relative to the eggshell surface. Consequently, we concluded that the viable pores were predominantly perpendicular to the eggshell surface.

Productive Performance and Egg Quality

Table 1 lists the production performances. Throughout the experimental period, there were no significant differences in the laying rate, egg weight, or defective egg rate between the TH and TL groups. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies, as the rate of defective eggs generally tends to be higher in late-laying hens (Molnár et al., 2016).

The egg quality is presented in Table 1. Eggshell thickness was significantly greater in the TL group than that in the TH group. The color intensity of eggshells is primarily determined by eggshell pigment deposition, and for pink-shell eggs, the protoporphyrin IX content directly influences the eggshell color depth (Zeng et al., 2022). Therefore, we used a more accurate measurement of protoporphyrin IX content to assess eggshell coloration. The protoporphyrin IX (original porphyrin IX) content in the eggshells was not significantly different between the TH and TL groups, indicating that the eggshell color was similar in both groups. Moreover, there were no significant differences in the eggshell strength between the 2 groups. These results are largely consistent with previous research, indicating that eggshell quality gradually declines with increasing age, likely due to the aging of the hens themselves (Molnár et al., 2016).

In summary, this study utilized micro-CT technology to reconstruct the 3-dimensional microstructure of eggshells. We investigated the relationship between volume, surface area, eggshell porosity, pore volume, mastoid space volume ratio, and eggshell translucency. Our findings revealed that a larger pore volume and a higher pore volume ratio were associated with reduced eggshell translucency. Additionally, the higher roundness and lower flatness of the individual pores were linked with improved pore morphology, ensuring the normal water vapor exchange efficiency of the eggshell, resulting in reduced eggshell translucency. Our results provide a fundamental understanding of the microstructural mechanisms underlying eggshell translucency. Moreover, these findings offer new evidence supporting eggshell translucency as a potential breeding selection criterion for enhancing eggshell appearance. This finding could potentially spark further research on eggshell translucency.
