Development and IND-enabling studies of a novel Cas9 genome-edited autologous CD34+ cell therapy to induce fetal hemoglobin for sickle cell disease
Authors: Varun Katta, Kiera O’Keefe, Yichao Li, Thiyagaraj Mayuranathan, Cicera R. Lazzarotto, Rachael K. Wood, Rachel M. Levine, Alicia Powers, Kalin Mayberry, Garret Manquen, Yu Yao, Jingjing Zhang, Yoonjeong Jang, Nikitha Nimmagadda, Erin A. Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a common, severe genetic blood disorder. Current pharmacotherapies are partially effective and allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is associated with immune toxicities. Genome editing of patient hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) to reactivate fetal hemoglobin (HbF) in erythroid progeny offers an alternative potentially curative approach to treat SCD. Although the FDA released guidelines for evaluating genome editing risks, it remains unclear how best to approach pre-clinical assessment of genome-edited cell products. Here, we describe rigorous pre-clinical development of a therapeutic γ-globin gene promoter editing strategy that supported an investigational new drug application cleared by the FDA. We compared γ-globin promoter and BCL11A enhancer targets, identified a potent HbF-inducing lead candidate, and tested our approach in mobilized CD34+ hematopoietic stem progenitor cells (HSPCs) from SCD patients. We observed efficient editing, HbF induction to predicted therapeutic levels, and reduced sickling. With single-cell analyses, we defined the heterogeneity of HbF induction and HBG1/HBG2 transcription. With CHANGE-seq for sensitive and unbiased off-target discovery followed by targeted sequencing, we did not detect off-target activity in edited HSPCs. Our study provides a blueprint for translating new ex vivo HSC genome editing strategies toward clinical trials for treating SCD and other blood disorders.

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a genetic blood disorder most commonly caused by a missense mutation (c.20T>A, p.Glu6Val) in the HBB gene that encodes the β-globin subunit of adult hemoglobin (HbA) (α2β2).1 This mutation causes production of sickle hemoglobin (HbS) (α2βS2), which polymerizes during hypoxic conditions in venous capillaries, resulting in stiff, brittle sickle-shaped red blood cells (RBCs).2,3 SCD is associated with chronic anemia, severe pain crises, progressive multi-organ damage, and early mortality.4 Currently approved drugs such as hydroxyurea, voxelotor, crizanlizumab, and L-glutamine are only partially effective.5 Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) represents a potential cure but is associated with immune toxicities, including graft-versus-host disease and graft rejection.6,7 Moreover, human leukocyte antigen-matched donors, which optimize HSCT outcomes, are available for less than 20% of SCD patients. These problems can be circumvented by lentiviral vector-based gene therapy or genome editing of autologous hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs).8

Several viral vector gene therapy and genome editing strategies have been developed to genetically modify autologous HSCs for SCD treatment. These include transduction with lentiviral vectors encoding a β-globin transgene,9 disruption of an erythroid-specific enhancer of BCL11A, which represses γ-globin gene transcription,10 correction of the mutant β-globin gene via homology-directed repair (HDR),11 and base editing to convert HbS to a non-pathogenic variant.12 Two of these strategies have shown promising results in clinical trials and received Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for use in SCD patients.9,13 However, the safest and most effective approach(es) remain to be determined through long-term follow-up studies. Each approach has potential genotoxicities, including insertional mutagenesis by lentiviral vectors, unintended off-target activity by genome editing nucleases, bystander edits within the editing window of base editors,14,15,16 genotoxicities associated with base and prime editors, and potential HSC toxicities with high-dose rAAV used to provide DNA template for HDR.17,18,19

Fetal hemoglobin (HbF) (α2γ2), the predominant RBC oxygen carrier during late-stage fetal development, is a well-established modifier of SCD severity.20 Normally around birth, γ-globin expression declines, with a reciprocal increase in the expression of the nearby β-globin gene, leading to a switch from HbF to HbA (α2β2), or HbS in the case of SCD.21 Numerous clinical observations have shown that elevated levels of HbF in postnatal RBCs can alleviate SCD pathologies by inhibiting HbS polymerization.22,23 Individuals who co-inherit mutations that typically cause SCD along with naturally occurring mutations that cause hereditary persistence of fetal hemoglobin (HPFH) remain largely asymptomatic.24,25

HbF can be induced by using genome editing nucleases such as Cas9 to disrupt repressor-binding sites in the γ-globin promoter.26 Although Cas9-mediated HDR can correct the mutant sickle HBB allele directly, this approach is challenging and may impair HSC engraftment and function.18 Moreover, precise edits are accompanied by non-homologous end joining (NHEJ)-mediated insertions and deletions (indels) mutations that can disrupt the HBB reading frame and reduce the β-globin expression. In contrast, NHEJ-mediated repair can be achieved at high frequencies in quiescent HSCs and can be utilized to re-activate silenced HbF expression.

The perinatal switch from HbF to HbA is mediated by the two repressor proteins BCL11A and ZBTB7A, which bind distinct motifs in the γ-globin promoter to inhibit transcription.27,28 Thus, genome editing nuclease-induced indels can be used to re-activate HbF by disrupting an erythroid-specific enhancer in the BCL11A gene or by disrupting BCL11A or ZBTB7A binding motifs in the γ-globin gene (HBG1/HBG2) promoters.29,30,31 A clinical trial examining Cas9-mediated disruption of the BCL11A enhancer has shown promising results in patients with SCD or β-thalassemia, supporting the approval of the first genome editing medicine.10,32,33

Previously, we showed that Cas9 disruption of a BCL11A repressor-binding site (113–118 nucleotides (nt) upstream of the transcription start site in the γ-globin gene promoters) in HSCs resulted in the induction of HbF to potentially therapeutic levels in RBC progeny generated in in vitro and in vivo.30,34 Potential advantages of our approach include transient Cas9 protein expression for ex vivo modification of autologous HSCs and high efficiency of NHEJ-mediated γ-globin promoter editing compared with HDR correction of the HbS mutation. Moreover, disruption of the BCL11A binding site recapitulates several benign HPFH mutations and may have more specific biological effects compared with the global loss of BCL11A expression in RBCs.35,36

Here, we show a set of pre-clinical experiments characterizing and advancing our genome editing approach toward a clinical trial. Cas9 disruption of the −115 BCL11A binding motif in the γ-globin promoter, hereafter referred to as −115 γ-globin promoter, induced HbF more potently than Cas9 disruption of the upstream ZBTB7A binding motif. Similarly, disruption of this BCL11A motif in SCD donor CD34+ hematopoietic stem progenitor cells (HSPCs) achieved consistent, high-frequency editing of bone marrow (BM) repopulating HSCs and substantial induction of RBC HbF. Single-cell analyses by western blotting and RNA sequencing showed that HbF was induced in most donor-derived erythroid cells with significant reductions in sickling after exposure to hypoxia. No off-target indels or genomic re-arrangements were detected using state-of-the-art, sensitive, unbiased genome-wide assays and targeted sequencing. Overall, our pre-clinical studies suggest that Cas9-mediated disruption of the BCL11A repressor binding site in the γ-globin promoters represents a promising approach for genomic therapy of SCD and β-thalassemia.

Results

Disrupting the γ-globin promoter (HBG1/HBG2) BCL11A-binding motif efficiently induces HbF

We compared the magnitude of HbF induction after Cas9 nuclease disruption of the BCL11A or ZBTB7A binding motifs in the γ-globin promoter, which each harbor naturally occurring HPFH mutations.37 We electroporated peripheral-blood mobilized CD34+ HSPCs from normal donors with Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes targeting either binding motif to induce DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) at positions −196 or −115, respectively (Figure 1A). We tested both wild-type (WT) Cas9 and a high-fidelity version (HiFi),38 each containing enhanced nuclear localization signals that improve editing efficiency (SpCas9-3xNLS).39 Four days after electroporation of WT Cas9-3xNLS RNP, we measured average indel frequencies of 76.2% and 85.7% at the ZBTB7A and BCL11A motifs, respectively. Editing with high-fidelity Cas9-3xNLS RNP resulted in 79.7% and 83.3% of indels at the ZBTB7A and BCL11A motifs, respectively (Figure 1B). To quantify HbF induction after editing, we generated late-stage erythroblasts by in vitro differentiation, followed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) of cell lysates. Editing at either γ-globin promoter target resulted in significantly increased HbF compared with unedited controls. Induction of HbF was significantly higher after WT Cas9-3xNLS editing of the −115 γ-globin promoter 35% (1.00), mean (SD) compared with editing at the −196 γ-globin promoter 29% (1.00) (adjusted p value of 0.0002). We obtained similar results with high-fidelity Cas9-3xNLS in vitro (Figure 1C).

To determine editing outcomes in BM repopulating HSCs, which represent a small proportion of human CD34+ cells, we transplanted normal donor CD34+ HSPCs edited with WT Cas9 at the −196 or −115 γ-globin promoter targets, and CD34+ HSPCs edited with high-fidelity Cas9 at −115 γ-globin promoter target into immunodeficient NBSGW mice. We also analyzed CD34+ HSPCs edited at the erythroid-specific enhancer of BCL11A as a positive control, and unedited CD34+ HSPCs as a negative control. At 17 weeks post-transplantation, we collected human donor-derived cells from the BM of transplanted mice and measured engraftment, multi-lineage differentiation, indel frequencies, and erythroid HbF expression. We analyzed erythroblasts and reticulocytes isolated from the BM of transplanted mice because human RBCs have a very short half-life in mouse circulation.40 Indels in input CD34+ HSPCs prior to transplantation were 83.8%–97.9% at the BCL11A enhancer, −115, and −196 γ-globin promoter targets (Figure 1D).

After transplantation, repopulating human HSPCs edited with WT Cas9 retained high indel frequencies: 86.7% (4.37), 88.5% (4.95), and 82.4% (2.00) at the BCL11A enhancer, −196, and −115 γ-globin promoter targets respectively. In contrast, indels were significantly reduced in repopulating CD34+ HSPCs edited at the −115 γ-globin promoter target with high-fidelity Cas9 (45.5% (10.3) vs. input 83.8%, adjusted p < 0.0001) compared with those edited with WT Cas9 (Figures 1D, S1A, and S1B). Thus, WT Cas9 edited the −115 target site in repopulating HSCs more efficiently than HiFi Cas9, despite similar editing frequencies in input CD34+ HSPCs. Moreover, 2-fold higher concentrations of HiFi Cas9 were required to achieve similar levels of editing as WT Cas9 in input CD34+ cells. These observations may reflect the lower enzymatic activity of HiFi Cas9.41 Our findings emphasize the importance of in vivo xenotransplantation studies to assess HSC editing frequencies.

To compare HbF induction after editing the −115 and −196 γ-globin promoter target sites, we analyzed CD235a+ erythroid progeny of transplanted CD34+ HSPCs by HPLC. HbF induction with WT Cas9 at the −115 γ-globin promoter target 32% (3.24) was significantly higher compared with unedited controls (<2%, adjusted p < 0.0001). In contrast, editing at the −196 γ-globin promoter target resulted in significantly less HbF induction 16.8% (2.17) (adjusted p < 0.0001) compared with the −115 γ-globin promoter target. As expected, lower editing observed with high-fidelity Cas9 at the −115 γ-globin promoter target resulted in lower HbF induction 20.4% (4.1) (adjusted p = 0.0001) compared with WT Cas9. Moreover, −115 γ-globin promoter editing induced higher levels of HbF 32% compared with the cells edited at the BCL11A enhancer 27.2% (2.78) (adjusted p = 0.0169) (Figure 1E). These results suggest that −115 γ-globin promoter editing represents an effective alternate strategy for HbF induction with higher biological specificity.

Notably, we found that the potency for HbF induction in vivo is better predicted by editing levels than by HbF induction in vitro. Our study suggests that lead target selection and optimization should be determined by HbF induction measured after xenotransplant (Figures S2A and S2B).

Taken together, we conclude that editing at the −115 γ-globin promoter target with WT Cas9 represents the most potent nuclease-based approach for therapeutic HbF induction and chose this strategy for further evaluation as a therapeutic lead candidate.

Therapeutically relevant levels of HbF with reduction of RBC sickling

To assess the utility of our genome editing approach for SCD, we electroporated plerixafor-mobilized CD34+ HSPCs from three individuals with SCD (two with HbSS and one with HbSC genotype) and one normal donor with Cas9-3xNLS RNP targeting the −115 γ-globin promoter, followed by transplantation into NBSGW mice. After 17 weeks, human cell engraftment in mouse BM was 90.6% (3.32), as measured by expression of the human CD45+ hematopoietic antigen. Engraftment levels of unedited control HSPCs were similar 90.9% (3.65) (Figure 2A). We observed no significant differences in the differentiation of edited or control HSCs to T cell, B cell, myeloid, or erythroid lineages in vivo (Figure 2B). To determine −115 γ-globin promoter editing frequencies before and after NBSGW mouse transplant, and associated levels of HbF induction, we performed targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) and analyzed HbF levels in CD235a+ erythroid cells by HPLC. We achieved consistent high-frequency editing 85.6% (1.87) in CD34+ HSPCs in pre-transplantation input cells from one normal and three SCD donors. Seventeen weeks after transplant, remaining indel frequencies were 73.28% (10.96) in repopulating human cells from SCD donors (Figure 2C), which resulted in 30.6% (1.24) HbF induction in erythroid progeny isolated from mouse BM (Figure 2D).

To assess functional effects of HbF induction in RBCs, we edited the −115 γ-globin promoter target in CD34+ cells from SCD donors, generated reticulocytes by in vitro erythroid differentiation and exposed them to low oxygen concentration (2%) for 8 h. Phase contrast microscopy showed that reticulocytes derived from unedited HSPCs exhibited 66.9% (2.22) sickled morphology, whereas sickling was significantly reduced to 23.8% (5.16) in reticulocytes derived from edited cells from two donors with HbSS genotype. In reticulocytes derived from an HbSC donor HSPCs, the fraction of sickled reticulocytes was 12.9% (5.25) in unedited controls and 1.3% (0.34) after editing (Figures 2E and S4A). These results demonstrate that disruption of the −115 γ-globin promoter target induces HbF expression to levels that ameliorate RBC sickling in vitro, therefore predicting therapeutic efficacy.

Broad induction of HbF in RBC progeny

Optimal therapeutic induction of γ-globin requires that most or all RBCs express HbF above a threshold level that inhibits HbS polymerization.42,43 Thus, ideal genome editing therapies for SCD should induce both high and broad (i.e., pancellular) expression of HbF.44

We used three orthogonal methods to evaluate HbF expression in erythroid cells generated from edited or control SCD donor HSPCs after xenotransplantation. First, we used flow cytometry to measure HbF immunostaining erythroid cells (F cells). In experiments using three different donors, F cells rose from 29.8% (2.26) in unedited control erythroblasts to 74.4% (3.84) after editing (Figure 3A). The relatively high percentage of F cells in unedited SCD patient-derived cells is commonly observed in this assay.34 Second, we performed single-cell western (scWestern) analysis. Pooled CD235a+ erythroid cells from each donor were immobilized on a polyacrylamide gel-chip, lysed, fractionated by sodium dodecyl-sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and probed with anti-γ-globin and control anti-α-globin antibodies. More than 99% of control or Cas9-edited erythroblasts expressed α-globin. Of these, γ-globin protein was detected in 1.2% or 6.1% of unedited control cells and 49.3% or 58.2% of edited cells from SCD donors 1 and 2, respectively (Figure 3B). To better understand the differences in detection of HbF protein in the two single-cell assays, we analyzed the relative levels of γ-globin transcript by single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). Transcription of the γ-globin genes correlates with HbF protein synthesis in erythroid cells.45 γ-globin mRNA was increased in edited erythroid cells, with an average of 88.2% (6.62) expressing more than 30% γ-globin mRNAs relative to all β-like globin transcripts including HBB (β-globin) and HBD (δ-globin). Consistent with flow cytometry determination of F cells, 26.7% (4.59) of unedited erythroid cells derived from SCD donor HSPCs expressed more than 30% γ-globin mRNA. Interestingly, we observed a bimodal distribution of HBG expression that was clearly shifted to higher levels in edited cells compared with unedited ones (Figure 3C). Our results suggest that most edits induce γ-globin transcription into a therapeutically relevant range, but some more potently than others.

To determine whether editing altered the distribution of erythroblast subpopulations, we integrated scRNA-seq data from all cells and performed dimensionality reduction using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP)46 (Figure 3D). We clustered the cells according to standard erythroid maturation stages including proerythroblast, basophilic erythroblast, polychromatophilic erythroblast, orthochromatic erythroblast, and reticulocyte based on gene-specific markers47 (Figure S5A). Editing did not significantly alter the relative proportions of cells at these maturation stages (Figure 3E).

To further determine whether editing of HSCs altered transcription in erythroid progeny, we performed differential expression analysis to compare edited and control cell populations. HBG1/HBG2 mRNA levels were significantly and specifically upregulated in edited cells (Figures 3F and S6A). No differences in other erythroid-expressed mRNAs including SLCA1, ITG4, and GYPA were detected in edited vs. unedited cells. (Figure S7A).

The levels of HbF indicated by our analysis of human SCD donor-derived erythroid cells in mouse BM likely underestimate the levels that would be expressed human subjects who receive autologous edited HSCs, as high levels of HbF reduce HbS polymerization and increase the lifespan of circulating RBCs.48 This positive selection of HbF-expressing RBCs cannot be measured in human-to-mouse xenotransplantation studies because human RBCs are rapidly eliminated from the mouse circulation.40

Overall, single-cell analyses of γ-globin protein and mRNA show that the −115 γ-globin promoter edit induces HbF expression broadly and to levels that are predicted to offer therapeutic benefit.

The single-guide RNA (sgRNA) includes two components: Crispr RNA (crRNA) a 20 bp complementary sequence that binds to the targeted region and an 80 bp trans-activating crRNA that serves as a scaffold for Cas9 RNP complex formation.49 Commercially synthesized sgRNAs used in this study, including GMP HBG-sgRNA, are chemically modified to increase the stability and editing efficacy.50 The quality of sgRNA is critical for therapeutic genome editing, as heterogeneous contaminant RNAs could impair on-target editing or increase off-target edits at cognate target sites. We utilized a modified SMARTer smRNA sequencing protocol to analyze the −115 γ-globin targeting sgRNA.51 A total of 85.1% of the sequencing reads generated from commercially synthesized, GMP HBG-sgRNA aligned perfectly to the −115 γ-globin promoter target sequence, while the remaining 14.9% contained truncations in targeting and/or scaffold regions that could impact the efficacy or specificity of editing (Figure 4C). From the mapped reads, 80.79% of the 5' spacer sequence perfectly matched the targeting sgRNA sequence with complementarity to the HBG promoter and 64.9% matched the full-length product (Figure S8A). Most importantly, we did not detect evidence of contaminating gRNAs that could target other regions of the genome, consistent with full GMP changeover procedures (Figure S8B).

To optimize Cas9 RNP complexation and minimize the amount of free sgRNA that could induce an innate immune response, we titrated the molar ratios of Cas9 to sgRNA. Using the same buffers and protocol as in GMP manufacturing runs, we generated RNP complexes with different Cas9:sgRNA ratios followed by size-exclusion chromatography with HPLC-UV spectroscopy to quantify the levels of RNP complex, Cas9, and free sgRNA. A Cas9:sgRNA ratio of 1:1.2 resulted in complexation of most Cas9 with 10.4% free sgRNA, whereas increasing Cas9:sgRNA ratios from 1:1.5 to 1:3 ratios resulted in 43.5%–78.2% free sgRNA (Figure 4D). This provided a Cas9 complexation range for further cell-based optimization of editing efficiency and cell recovery.

To develop an efficient clinical scale process for editing CD34+ HSPCs at the −115 γ-globin promoter, we first compared electroporation programs for transfecting RNP using the MaxCyte GTx. The HSC3 and HSC4 programs resulted in comparable editing frequencies and cell viabilities (Figures S9A and S9B). Thus, we selected program HSC3, which uses a lower energy program and may reduce electroporation-associated cell toxicity. Next, we performed design of experiments (DOE) to optimize Cas9 concentration, Cas9:sgRNA complexation ratio, and cell concentration during electroporation. Editing efficiency in vitro plateaued at 4.8 μM Cas9 protein and a Cas9:sgRNA ratio of 1:1.2 (Figure 4E). Editing efficiencies and cell survival were similar at cell densities of 10–100 million/mL during electroporation (Figure 4F). In addition, we determined that the editing efficiency was highest after pre-stimulating plerixafor-mobilized CD34+ HSPCs for 1 day before electroporation (Figure S9C).

In principle, residual Cas9 protein in transplanted HPSCs could stimulate a host immune response.52 We performed western blot analysis and measurement of on-target indels across multiple time points to assess the kinetics of Cas9 expression and genome editing. After electroporation of RNP into HSPCs, Cas9 activity measured by indel formation reached a plateau by day 4, and no Cas9 protein was detected after 7 days (Figure S9D). However, in CD34+ HSPCs cryopreserved after 24 h of electroporation, Cas9 was undetectable 72 h later in post-thaw edited cells (Figure S9E). These results show that intracellular Cas9 is transient and fully degraded by day 3.

We combined optimized electroporation parameters and cell purification processes to conduct a large-scale pre-GMP engineering run with plerixafor-mobilized normal donor CD34+ HSPCs (n = 3). For this scale-up, we used GMP sgRNA (Synthego) and GMP Cas9 protein manufactured at our GMP facility. We conducted three pre-GMP engineering runs and transplanted edited and unedited control cells into NBSGW mice for in vivo pharmacology studies. At 17 weeks post-transplantation, the indels in donor-derived bulk BM, CD34+, CD19+, CD33+, and CD235a+ cells were similar to each other and to that of the input CD34+ cells measured 5 days after editing (Figure 4G). In donor-derived CD235a+ erythroid cells, HbF levels were 17.2%–26% after editing compared with 0% in unedited control cells (Figure 4H). The fraction of F cells was 55.1%–73.3% after editing compared with 11.9%–20.2% in unedited cells (Figure S9F). As expected, there was no change in the frequency of enucleation between edited and unedited control cells (Figure S9G).

Altogether, we established a framework for clinical scale-up process to manufacture and characterize critical genome editing reagents including Cas9 and sgRNA and the edited CD34+ cellular drug product. Additionally, we achieved efficient editing in normal CD34+ HSPCs at a large scale to induce similar and effective levels of HbF, further supporting the efficacy of our pre-clinical studies.

To measure the frequency of this 4.9 kb deletion more precisely, we used digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) assay. Gene-specific primers and probes were designed that bind downstream of the HBG2 promoter and intergenic region between the HBG2 gene and the HBG1 promoter. Deletion of the 4.9 kb region is estimated by loss of signal that occurs when the probe cannot bind to the intergenic deleted region compared with a control probe that binds to the HBG2 promoter. Due to genetic variation in the binding sequences of primers and probes, it is important to check assay performance with unedited controls. We observed a frequency of 29.8% (1.27) of the 4.9 kb deletion in clinical-scale bulk edited HSPCs (Figure 5B). Similar frequencies of this deletion were noted, ranging from 20.8% to 37.2% in bulk edited HSPCs and 12.8%–29.7% in the bulk BM derived from three normal and SCD donors after 17 weeks of xenotransplantation (Figure S11A). Also, we did not detect inversions in bulk edited HSPCs and BM cells at 17 weeks post-transplantation, suggesting that these occur infrequently or that they are selected against in repopulating HSCs.

Previously, we showed, by analyzing clonal burst-forming unit-erythroid colonies from CD34+ HSPCs harboring the 4.9 kb deletion, that the resultant HBG2-HBG1 fusion gene retained the capacity to drive HbF expression.34 To further study the impact of this 4.9 kb deletion on HbF expression, we electroporated the HUDEP-2 erythroblast cell line harboring a single intact copy of the extended β-like globin locus including the two tandem γ-globin genes54 with RNP targeting the −115 γ-globin promoter site (Figure S12A). The editing efficiency in bulk edited cells was 84% (1.34) and HbF was 29.4% (0.46) compared with 2.9% (0.46) in unedited control cells (Figures S12B and S12C). The frequency of the 4.9 kb deletion was 66% (1.38) (Figure S12D), higher than what typically occurs in CD34+ HSPCs (refer to Figure S11A). Analysis of edited HUDEP-2 clones revealed that, with intact HBG1 and HBG2 genes, bi-allelic −13 or −4 nt deletions induced γ-globin expression from 76.7% (6.52) to 78.9% (10.6), respectively. Importantly, clones with the 4.9 kb deletion and −13 or −4 nt deletions in the γ-globin fusion gene exhibited high induction of γ-globin (58.6% (6.9) to 62.7% (12.6)), respectively (Figure S12E), albeit to lower levels than clones with no 4.9 kb deletion and the same bi-allelic indels (Figure S12F). Overall, these observations indicate that that erythroid cells harboring the 4.9 kb HBG2-HBG1 deletion retain the capacity for strong γ-globin induction.

To characterize the spectrum of large deletions involving the extended β-globin locus that could be induced by editing the γ-globin genes, we performed long-range PCR and long-read PacBio sequencing (Figure 5C). We observed the major 4.9 kb deletion products and no other unexpected large deletions (Figures S13A and S13B). One limitation of this analysis is that long-range PCR preferentially amplifies smaller DNA segments, which could overestimate the true frequency of the 4.9 kb deletion.

To define the genome-wide activity of Cas9-sgRNA directed at the γ-globin promoter genes, we used experimental and computational approaches to nominate candidate off-target sites. We performed CHANGE-seq, a highly sensitive biochemical assay to define the genome-wide off-target activity of Cas9 in vitro.55 By selective sequencing of Cas9-cleaved genomic DNA, CHANGE-seq identified 678 off-target sites from plerixafor-mobilized normal donors of African American ancestry (pre-GMP engineering runs, n = 3) (Figure 5D). Most of the potential off-targets classified by CHANGE-seq occurred in intergenic and intronic regions of the human genome, as expected based on their relative genomic proportions (Figures 5E and 5F). We selected the 277 CHANGE-seq candidate sites reproducibly identified in 2 or more biological donors and 11 non-overlapping CasOFFinder predicted sites for multiplexed targeted amplicon sequencing (rhAmp-seq, IDT).

In addition, we used two in silico tools, CRISPRme56 and CALITAS,57 to nominate variant aware off-target sites associated with genetic variation in the intended population. A total of 124 off-target sites were predicted by the tools. We designed new rhAmp-seq panels for all (n = 48) that we had not previously evaluated. Overall, 22 off-target sites overlapped with 25 variants and 15 variants were found in donor cells used in pre-GMP engineering and engineering runs (n = 4). At day 5 post-electroporation of edited CD34+ cells mobilized from normal donors, we observed high on-target editing 91.5% (1.52) (adjusted p = 0.000001) indels with no detectable off-target activity (FDR < 0.01) from 325 amplifiable sites detected by CHANGE-seq and in silico tools such as CasOFFinder, CRISPRme, and CALITAS (Figure 5G).

To assess gross genomic structural re-arrangements, edited and control cells from normal CD34+ HSPCs were evaluated using standard G-band karyotyping at day 5 post editing. No karyotypic abnormalities were detected in 100 metaphase spreads analyzed per edited sample (Figures S14A and S14B).

We applied the UDiTaS58 (unidirectional targeted sequencing) approach to determine the genome-wide spectrum and persistence of genomic rearrangements involving the on-target editing sites in normal donor CD34+ HSPCs. At days 5 and 14 post-editing, no reproducible translocations or structural genomic re-arrangements were detected (Figures 5H and S15A).

Furthermore, to investigate chromosomal scale changes like CNVs, we utilized our scRNA-seq analysis after editing for abnormal gene expression. We applied the inferCNV59 method utilizing scRNA-seq results and found that modified expression is in the normal range of 0.85–1.15 with no significant differences between the control and edited cells in all three SCD donors (Figure S16A).

In sum, through rigorous pre-clinical characterization we have found that Cas9-sgRNA complexes specifically target the −115 γ-globin gene promoters with no detectable off-target mutations or genomic re-arrangements, supporting the safety and specificity of our approach.

Discussion

Here, we describe key pre-clinical studies toward advancing a gene editing strategy for SCD to a planned early-phase clinical trial. Typically, interactions with regulatory agencies are confidential and thus there remains no clear developmental path for investigational new drug (IND) submissions. Our study may serve as a roadmap representing one path to performing IND-enabling studies in accord with recently released FDA guidance on the development of human gene therapy products for genome editing.53

Key criteria for advancing an ex vivo genome editing HSC cellular drug product include sufficient potency and safety predicted by pre-clinical efficacy and genotoxicity assays. For our genome editing approach to SCD therapy, these criteria include HbF induction into a therapeutic range with expression detected in most RBCs, significant reduction of RBC sickling in vitro, and no detectable genotoxicities as measured by comprehensive genome-wide assays. First, we compared different γ-globin promoter targets and identified the −115 γ-globin promoter site as our lead target site associated with the most potent induction of HbF. Second, we tested this editing strategy in mobilized, peripheral blood CD34+ HSPCs from individuals with SCD and confirmed high editing rates in BM repopulating HSCs with high-level induction of HbF and reduction of hypoxia-induced sickling in erythroid progeny. Third, we performed complementary single-cell analyses that indicated HbF induction in most erythroid cells. Fourth, we developed a GMP process to edit HSPCs at clinical scale using well-characterized, GMP Cas9 protein and gRNA. Analysis of pre-clinical cellular drug products showed efficient editing and robust HbF induction that predict therapeutic efficacy. Finally, we performed rigorous analysis of genome editing outcomes and potential unintended genotoxicities.

We and others have demonstrated that disruption of ZBTB7A28,31 and BCL11A30,34 repressor binding motifs in the γ-globin promoter can re-activate HbF to potentially therapeutic levels. In this study, we further show that in vitro studies are not sufficient to indicate the potency of editing for HbF induction. For instance, Cas9 disruption of the −196 ZBTB7A binding motif or the −115 BCL11A binding motif in CD34+ HSPCs induced HbF to similar levels in erythroblasts generated by in vitro differentiation. However, comparison of erythroblasts generated from edited CD34+ cells after transplantation into NBSGW mice revealed significantly greater HbF induction after editing of the −115 BCL11A binding site. ScRNA-seq analysis of mouse BM after xenotransplantation of CD34+ HSPCs edited at the −115 BCL11A binding motif showed that more than 80% of human erythroblasts contained greater than 30% γ-globin transcripts as a proportion of all β-globin-like transcripts. Our studies likely underestimate the levels of HbF induction that could be achieved in humans with SCD, because selection for HbF expression that occurs in circulating RBCs of SCD patients cannot be measured in mice.40 In agreement, a clinical trial examining Cas9 disruption of the BCL11A gene erythroid enhancer showed higher HbF percentage in steady-state RBC of human subjects compared with initial in vitro studies, which likely reflects a selective advantage for HbF-expressing RBCs.60

The results of two different approaches utilizing Cas9 NHEJ for HbF induction in SCD patients are reported in peer-reviewed publications. Based on Cas9 disruption of the BCL11A gene erythroid enhancer, exagamglogene autotemcel (exa-cel, also known as Casgevy) became the first FDA-approved CRISPR medicine to treat SCD. In a phase 3 clinical study, 97% of the patients who received exa-cel were free from severe vaso-occlusive crisis (VOC) events for at least 12 months. HbF levels were elevated to an average of approximately 40% over 24 months follow-up. Hemolysis markers such as lactic acid dehydrogenase, absolute reticulocyte counts, indirect bilirubin, and haptoglobin levels were reduced to normal ranges.60 Of note, studies comparing disruption of the −115 BCL11A binding motif in the γ-globin promoter or the BCL11A erythroid enhancer showed comparable induction of RBC HbF in vivo.13 Here, we observed a small but significant increase in HbF induction after targeting the −115 γ-globin promoter compared with the BCL11A enhancer target, similar to what was reported in another recent study.61 Thus, disruption of the −115 γ-globin promoter appears to be comparable in efficacy to the BCL11A-enhancer-targeting approach, with no detectable off-target activity.

Another clinical study showed that Cas9 disruption of the γ-globin promoter at position −247 induced HbF expression in vitro through unknown mechanisms.62 Three SCD patients treated with the HSC product (OTQ923) expressed 21.8% to 25% HbF in RBCs after 6–18 months infusion. The participants continued to experience VOCs and ongoing mild hemolysis, suggesting that the levels of HbF induction achieved were not sufficient to fully inhibit RBC sickling. These suboptimal results are likely due to the relatively low potency of HbF induction associated with the editing of this target region. The clinical trial was discontinued in early 2023.63 The results of this study further reinforce the importance of pre-clinical in vivo efficacy studies to determine the potency of a cellular drug product (rather than relying only on in vitro outcomes) and help to define the parameters of HbF induction that are required to inhibit sickling and produce optimal clinical responses. Therapeutic levels of HbF induction required to fully inhibit RBC sickling are likely to be between that achieved by OTQ923 and exa-cel.

We showed here that WT Cas9 and HiFi Cas9 produced similar editing frequencies at the −115 site in the γ-globin promoter in bulk edited CD34+ HSPCs in vitro. However, WT Cas9 resulted in higher editing frequencies and HbF induction in erythroblasts generated after xenotransplantation, again emphasizing the importance of pre-clinical in vivo studies. We hypothesize that HiFi Cas9 edits repopulating HSCs less efficiently than WT Cas9 at the −115 γ-globin target site. While the mechanisms underlying this observation require further investigation, it is possible that HiFi Cas9 has relatively reduced enzymatic activity at some targets. Overall, our comparative studies have displayed the significance of using in vivo experiments to select effective genome editors and identifying potent target sites to induce therapeutic levels of HbF.

Potential advantages of our approach are its intrinsic biological specificity, straightforward path to characterize genotoxicity, and clinical scale-up. Targeting the repressor-binding site in the γ-globin promoter rather than globally depleting the expression of the repressor itself in erythroid cells may avoid potential side effects of altering non-globin BCL11A target gene expression.36 With Cas9 nuclease, we are able to utilize CHANGE-seq, a sensitive and unbiased biochemical method to characterize the genome-wide off-target activity rather than applying cell-based methods like GUIDE-seq64 in HSCs where tag integration rates may vary in a site-specific manner. Toward manufacturing of patient cellular drug products, we have established GMP production of Cas9 protein and a clinical scale process to edit cells with Cas9 RNP.

There are several limitations to Cas9-mediated NHEJ for HbF induction. First, heterogeneous indels can produce variable outcomes. We observed previously that 1, 2, or 3 nt deletions that disrupt the −115 γ-globin promoter site induced HbF less potently than some larger deletions, for unknown reasons.65 Moreover, most Cas9-induced indels targeting the −115 γ-globin promoter site also disrupt an overlapping motif that recruits transcriptional activator NF-Y. In these cases, including a 13 bp deletion associated with HPFH, NF-Y activates transcription through an identical cognate motif at downstream position −85.54 Thus, relatively large Cas9-mediated deletions of the −115 BCL11A binding motif that destroy both the adjacent and downstream −85 NF-Y motifs are likely to not induce HbF.66 However, we did not detect these larger deletions in long-range PCR PacBio sequencing.

Second, simultaneous DSBs within the tandem HBG1 and HBG2 genes can cause a 4.9 kb deletion resulting in a single HBG1 gene fused to upstream HBG2 promoter sequences (Figure 5A), which we detected both in the pre-transplant infusion product and engrafted BM cells. However, clonal analysis of Cas9-edited HUDEP2 cells indicated that the remaining HBG2-HBG1 fusion gene is activated to induce HbF, as long as the −115 BCL11A binding motif was disrupted at the site of deletion repair.34 Conversely, the single HBG1 fused to the HBG2 promoter is predicted to be transcriptionally silent if the BCL11A binding motif remains intact.

Third, Cas9 editing can result in large-scale partial chromosome loss, as observed in human primary T cells.67,68 However, by applying inferCNV method, we did not observe large-scale chromosomal changes associated with abnormal changes in the gene expression due to Cas9-mediated editing between control and Cas9-edited cells for this target.

Potential limitations of Cas9 nuclease-mediated DNA repair outcomes can be overcome by newer technologies such as base editing and prime editing, which act independent of DSBs and allow for more precise control of editing outcomes.69 While these next-generation editors present their own unique challenges for manufacturing and safety assessments, they hold great promise for the development of therapeutic genetic medicines. Indeed, our group and others have shown that base editing of the γ-globin promoters can induce HbF more potently than Cas9-mediated indels.65,70,71,72,73

In summary, we describe the pre-clinical characterization of a −115 γ-globin promoter editing strategy to induce HbF for treating SCD by demonstrating induction of HbF to therapeutically relevant levels without detectable genotoxicities. We believe that this represents a promising autologous cell therapy for SCD and anticipate that our rigorous approach for characterizing potential safety and efficacy could serve as a blueprint for future ex vivo HSC cell therapies.

Materials and methods

Study design

All research conducted in this study complies with our Institution Biosafety Committee and the St. Jude Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC protocol #192-100553) and experiments were carried out in accordance with relevant ethical regulations.

The main purpose of this study was to demonstrate CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing of CD34+ HSPCs at −115 γ-globin gene promoter can induce therapeutically predictive levels of HbF for SCD therapy, to perform rigorous characterization of potential genotoxicities, and to develop a clinical scale process toward a clinical trial. For in vitro experiments mentioned in the text, we included at least three (biological replicates) from normal donors and individuals with SCD. For in vivo studies, edited CD34+ HSPCs derived from normal and SCD donors were injected into immunodeficient NBSGW mice. Animal research technicians handling mice, injecting edited CD34+ HSPCs, and harvesting BM were blinded to the names of the treatment and unaware of the expected results. Experiments performed in this study with number of replicates, statistical tests, analysis, and p values are reported in the figure legends and statistically significant values are mentioned in the results.

Animal care

Mice were housed and handled according to the guidelines in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health. Experiments were carried out according to the protocol (Expansion, Transduction, and Selection of Hematopoietic Stem Cells) approved by the St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital Institional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Plerixafor mobilization and CD34+ cell isolation

Plerixafor mobilized CD34+ cells were collected under approved protocol “Peripheral Blood Stem Cell Collection for Sickle Cell Disease Patients” (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03226691) by the human subject research institutional review boards at the National Institutes of Health and St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital. All individuals gave written informed consent for the sample donation and consent documents are maintained in the donor’s medical records. The consent document was approved by the institutional review board prior to study initiation and is reviewed and updated annually.

Normal plerixafor-mobilized CD34+ HSPCs were purchased from the Stem Express Stem Cell Collection Center from California. Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (GCSF) mobilized normal HSPCs were obtained from unknown normal adults commercially purchased from Key Biologics, LLC. CD34+ HSPCs were isolated by AutoMACS instrument (Miltenyi Biotec) and further sorted by St. Jude Experimental Cellular Therapeutics Lab.

The process of mobilizing the CD34+ HSPCs from their BM niche into peripheral circulation74 and the details of the apheresis procedure75 have been described in detail previously. Donors underwent RBC exchange with goals of HbS < 30% and Hb > 10 g/dL prior to apheresis, received 240 μg/kg of plerixafor for HSPC mobilization, followed by HSPC collection 2–4 h later with processing up to 4 total blood volumes. Leukapheresis was performed using a Cobe Spectra or Optia instrument via a central venous apheresis catheter. Acid citrate dextrose formula-A was used as the anticoagulant, with prophylactic intravenous calcium infusions used in all procedures to prevent citrate toxicity. Maximal extracorporeal blood volume during the procedure ranged from 300 to 400 mL. We targeted the deep buffy coat collection to improve SCD HSPC collection. Harvested PB apheresis products were cryopreserved using a controlled rate freezer.

Research-grade protein purification

SpCas9-3xNLS and SpCas9-3xNLS-R691A (HiFi) plasmids were transformed into BL21 (DE3) competent cells (MilliporeSigma, 702353) and grown in Terrific broth medium at 37°C until reaching an OD600 of 2.4–2.8. Bacterial cells were induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) for 20 h at 20°C. Cells were pelleted and lysed in 25 mM Tris (pH 7.6), 500 mM NaCl, and 5% glycerol, passed through a homogenizer twice, and then centrifuged at 20,000 rpm for 1 h at 4°C. Protein was purified by Nickel-NTA resin and treated with TEV protease (1 mg lab-made TEV per 40 mg of protein) and benzonase-100 units/mL (Novagen, 70664-3) overnight at 4°C. Subsequently, purified using a size-exclusion column (Amersham Biosciences HiLoad 26/60 Superdex 200 17-1071-01) and ion exchange with a 5 mL SP HP column (GE 17-1151-01) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Proteins were dialyzed in 20 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.5) containing 400 mM KCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP buffer, and contaminants were removed using a Toxin Sensor Chromogenic LAL Endotoxin Assay Kit (GenScript, L00350). Purified proteins were concentrated and filtered using amicon ultra-filter units (MilliporeSigma, UFC903008) and ultra-free MC centrifugal filter (MilliporeSigma, UFC30GV0S). Protein fractions were further assessed on TGX stain free 4%–20% SDS-PAGE (Bio-Rad, 5678093) and quantified by rapid gold BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A53226).

GMP tag-free SpCas9-3xNLS production

A vial of E. coli T7 express cells containing the pGTPc502 T7 Cas9 Optimized T7-express plasmid was thawed and seeded in a 2.5 L flask containing 500 mL of growth medium and 50 μg/mL kanamycin at an OD600 of 0.002. Culture was incubated overnight at 30°C and 325 rpm. A bioreactor (5 L) was seeded at an OD600 of 0.4 in 2.5 L of growth medium (50 μg/mL kanamycin) using overnight culture. Fermenter controls were 900 rpm stir speed, 30% DO, and pH 6.8 (adjusted pH with 20% NH3OH and 1.2 M H3PO4). FoamAway (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added as needed and air sparging was continuous at 1 L/min. Reactor feed with glucose and yeast extract solution began after 5 h of culture and increased exponentially up to 21 h after culture start, after which it remained constant. When the culture OD600 reached 60–80 at 20–24 h, the temperature was decreased to 20°C and induced with IPTG (Sigma) at 0.04 mM/ODU. At 21–23 h after induction, culture was harvested by centrifugation (4,000 × g, 15 min) and the pellet was frozen at −80°C.

Three hundred grams of thawed pellet was resuspended at 10% solids in 50 mM Tris (JT Baker), 350 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol (pH 7.4), with 1 mM TCEP (GoldBio) and 500 U/g Benzonase (Millipore). Resuspended solution was lysed in a microfluidizer at 12,000 psi for two passes and centrifuged at 15,800 × g for 45 min. Supernatant was filtered using a 1,040 cm2 3M Harvest RC filter and passed through a Sepharose SP column (BV, 461 mL) at 45 cm/h. The SP Column was washed with 5 column volumes (CV) of 50 mM Tris, 350 mM NaCl, and 10% glycerol (pH 7.4), with 1 mM TCEP at 75 cm/h followed by 8 CV elution gradient with 50 mM Tris, 10% glycerol (pH 7.4), and 1 mM TCEP from 350–750 mM NaCl. Allantoin was added to Sepharose SP eluate at 100 mg/mL and stirred at 300 rpm for 15 min at room temperature. Solution was centrifuged at 3,700 × g for 15 min and supernatant was filtered through a Sartopore 2 150 filter at 70 mL/min. Next, solution was applied to a CHT column (BV 132 mL) at 125 cm/h. The CHT column was washed for 5 CV with 50 mM Tris (pH 8.5), 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 5 mM NaH2PO4, and 1 mM TCEP. Elution occurred over a 20 CV linear gradient with 50 mM Tris (pH 8.5), 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 5 mM NaH2PO4, and 1 mM TCEP from 300 mM to 2 M NaCl. The eluted fractions were combined and concentrated to 15–20 mL using a pellicon mini 0.1 cm2 biomax 100 kDa cutoff membrane at 80 mL/min and diafiltrated with 20 CV of 20 mM HEPES, 300 mM KCl, and 10% glycerol (pH 7.4). Final concentration measured by UV spectroscopy and protein was diluted to 12 mg/mL with 20 mM HEPES, 300 mM KCl, and 10% glycerol (pH 7.4) before final filtration using a 0.22 μM millex syringe filter.

Cell culture

CD34+ HSPCs were thawed and pre-stimulated for 48 h in X-VIVO 15 (Lonza, 04-418Q), and for pre-clinical and clinical scale-up experiments we used X-VIVO 10 (Lonza, 04-743Q) supplemented with 100 ng/mL of SCF, Flt3-L, TPO, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. SCD-derived and plerixafor-mobilized CD34+ HSPCs were pre-stimulated for 24 h before electroporation. Cells were washed with 1× PBS, counted, and resuspended in P3 solution. Electroporated cells were recovered in X-VIVO medium with SCF, Flt3-L, and TPO at 37°C, 5% CO2. After 24 h, cells were cryopreserved in medium containing 0.5 mL of plasma-Lyste A + 4% human serum albumin (HSA) and 0.5 mL of 2× freezing medium, including final concentrations of 12% pentastarch, (20% USP, Preservation Solutions, PST001), 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and 7.5% HSA. For erythroid differentiation, post-electroporated cells were transferred into phase 1 medium (1–7 days) with Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (IMDM) containing cytokines and penicillin/streptomycin, followed by culturing in phase 2 (8–14 days) and phase 3 (15–21 days). Phase 1 consists of IMDM (Gibco, 12440053) with 2% human blood type AB plasma (SeraCare, 1810-0001), 3% human AB serum (Atlanta Biologicals, S40110), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122), 3 units/mL heparin (Sagent Pharmaceuticals, NDC 25021-401-02), 3 units/mL EPO (Amgen, EPOGEN NDC 55513-144-01), 200 μg/mL holo-transferrin (Millipore Sigma, T0665, 10 ng/mL recombinant human SCF (R&D Systems, 255-SC/CF), and 1 ng/mL recombinant human interleukin IL-3 (R&D Systems, 203-IL/CF). Phase 2 includes phase 1 medium without IL-3 and phase 3 includes phase 1 medium without IL-3 and SCF with holo-transferrin increased to 1 mg/mL final concentration. Cells were maintained at 1.0–2.0 × 105 cell/mL in phase 1 and phase 2, whereas phase 3 seeding density is at 1.0–2.0 × 106 cell/mL. At day 21 in phase 3 medium, fully differentiated cells were collected for flow cytometry and HPLC. Non-electroporated cells are included as controls.

Genome editing

Cas9-3xNLS and Cas9-3xNLS-R691A (HiFi) proteins were purified by St. Jude production core facility and tag-free GMP Cas9-3xNLS purified by St. Jude GMP facility. Synthetic sgRNAs (chemically modified with 2'-O-methyl analogs and 3'-phosphorothioate internucleotide linkages at the first three 5' and 3' terminal RNA residues) were ordered from Synthego (Table S1). Lyophilized gRNAs were reconstituted in nuclease-free water and aliquots were stored at −80°C. Electroporation performed using Lonza 4D Nucleofector with P3 Primary Cell Nucleofector Kit (V4SP-3096) for 20 μL and (V4XP-3024) for 100 μL reaction. Cas9-RNPs complexes were made by combining 50 pmol of SpCas9-3x-NLS and 150 pmol sgRNA kept for 15 min at room temperature to electroporate 2 × 105 cells in 20 μL reaction and 250 pmol of SpCas9-3x-NLS, 750 pmol of sgRNA were used to nucleofect 4 × 106 cells in 100 μL P3 reaction. For SpCas9-3x-NLS (HiFi) 500 pmol used to complex with 1,500 pmol of sgRNA in 100 μL P3 reaction. Optimal programs for Cas9-3xNLS and Cas9-3xNLS (HiFi) using the Lonza 4D nucleofector are DS-130 and CM-137. Electroporated cells were recovered in warmed medium for 5 min and cultured in X-VIVO medium with cytokines at 37°C, 5% CO2. Cells were harvested for NGS at day 4 or 5.

Optimization and clinical scale-up electroporation

For optimization and scale-up, cells were electroporated using the MaxCyte GTx. Apheresis of GCSF or plerixafor-mobilized donors were shipped overnight and purified for CD34+ cells. Where cells were processed according to manufacturer’s instructions with Sepax C-pro and then CliniMACS Plus with CD34+ selection beads. The cells were then either cryopreserved or pre-stimulated overnight in X-VIVO 10 containing 100 ng/mL of SCF, Flt3-L, and TPO. For optimization studies, we used the JMP (SAS) software to conduct DOE studies looking at key parameters for the editing of the HSCs, which include Cas9 concentration, Cas9 to sgRNA ratio, cell concentration, with key outcomes including editing efficiency, cell viability, and cell recovery. After pre-stimulation between 0 and 48 h, the cells were then washed with MaxCyte buffer with 0.1% HSA. The cells were then resuspended with Maxcyte buffer between 20 and 200 million cells/mL. The RNP was formulated between 1 and 9.6 μM of Cas9 protein at 1 to 3 molar ratio of sgRNA. The cells and RNP were combined, mixed and transferred to an OC-100 or CL1.1 cassette and electroporated with programs HSC3, HSC4, or HSC5. After electroporation, cells were transferred to fresh X-VIVO 10 with cytokines at 2E6 cells/mL and cultured overnight. The cells were then cryopreserved at 5 × 106 cells/mL in a freezing medium containing Plasma-Lyte A with 5% DMSO, 6% pentastarch, and 5.75% HSA with a controlled rated freezer. The final large-scale process was finalized with pre-stimulation of HSCs before electroporation for 24 h and cryopreservation after 24 h. Electroporation conditions were locked in at 4.8 mM of Cas9 with 1:2 Cas9 to sgRNA ratio with electroporation of cells between 10 and 100 million cells per mL in a CL1.1 cassette with program HSC3.

High-throughput sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated using a DNAdvance Kit (Agencourt, A48705). Targeted sequencing libraries for NGS were prepared using a two-step PCR protocol. Step one creates targeted sequencing amplicons using gene-specific primers containing a partial illumina adaptor overhang (Table S2). PCR amplifications were performed using phusion hot start flex 2× master mix (NEB, M0536S) and 20–100 ng genomic DNA with the following conditions for HBG-sgRNA target (denaturation, 98°C for 3:00; touchdown [20× cycles], 98°C, 68°C, 72°C for 0:10, 0:10 [0.5°/cycle], 0:07 min; annealing [10× cycles], 98°C, 58°C, 72°C for 0:10, 0:10, 0:07 min; final extension 72°C for 3:00 min; hold 10°C). PCR conditions for BCL11A enhancer or ZBTB7A are denaturation, 98°C for 3:00; annealing (35× cycles), 98°C, 60°C, 72°C for 0:15, 0:15, 0:30 min; final extension, 72°C for 3:00 min; hold 10°C. PCR step one product was purified using AMPureXP beads. PCR step 2 was conducted using 10–15 ng PCR1 product to complete Illumina sequencing adaptors (P5-dual-index. F and P7-dual-index. R) using Kapa HF Hot Start Ready Mix (Roche, KK2602) with the following PCR conditions: denaturation, 98°C for 0:45; annealing (10× cycles), 98°C, 65°C, 72°C for 0:15, 0:30, 0:30 min; final extension, 72°C for 1:00 min; hold 10°C. PCR2 products were purified with Ampure XP beads, each sample pooled to 1,000 nM, and further diluted to 1.5 nM in 1× TE. Library was sequenced using MiSeq (Illumina) and MiSeq micro v.2 kit and sequenced following the manufacturers protocol spiked with 10% Phix control (FC-110-3001). The library was sequenced using paired-end sequencing with 151-8-8-151 cycles.

In vitro sickling assay

CD34+ HSPCs were differentiated as mentioned above using a three-phase protocol. Erythroid cells in phase 3 medium were incubated with Hoechst 33342 (Millipore Sigma) at 1:1,000 dilution for 20 min at 37°C and sorted for Hoechst-negative populations using an SH800 (Sony Biotechnologies) 96-well plate pre-coated with poly-L-lysine (Sigma) for 30 min and washed with 1× PBS. Sorted cells (5 × 104) were seeded into a 96-well plate with 0.1 mL of phase 3 medium under hypoxic conditions (2% oxygen) for 8 h. The IncuCyte S3 Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius) with a 20× objective was used to monitor cell sickling, with images captured after 8 h. The percentage of sickling was measured by manual counting of sickled cells versus normal cells based on morphology. For each sickling assay, more than 300 cells per condition were counted by researchers blinded to that condition.

scWestern blot

MACS-purified erythroid (CD235a+) cells from BM were pooled and 8 × 104 cells were loaded on small scWestern chips (Protein Simple, cat. no. K500). Following the manufacturer’s protocol, chips were processed with lysis for 0 s, electrophoresis for 30 s, voltage at 240 V, and UV exposure for 4 min. Probed with primary antibodies, rabbit anti-α-globin from ResGen (Invitrogen) and mouse anti-γ-globin (Santa Cruz, Sc-21756, 51-7), at dilutions 1:1,500 and 1:10 followed by secondary antibodies, donkey anti-rabbit IgG NorthernLights557 (R&D Systems, NL004) and donkey anti-mouse-Alexa Fluor647 (Fischer, A31571) at 1:40 dilution. sc-Western chips were scanned using an InnoScan710 micro array scanner, and 16-bit TIFF images were analyzed by scout software.

scRNA-seq and analysis

Pooled erythroid (CD235a+) cells were used for scRNA-seq studies. Determined cell count and appropriate volume of cells was loaded on the Chromium Next GEM chip G to run on a Chromium controller according to the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3' reagent kits v.3.1 user guide (cat. no. 1000127, 1000128) for a targeted recovery of 9,000 cells. Post GEM-RT clean-up, cDNA amplification and library construction was performed according to the 10X Genomics Next GEM single-cell protocol and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer was used for cDNA QC, quantification, and post library construction QC. Sequencing using a NovaSeq 6000 with a library concentration 1.3 nM (+1% Phix) loaded on an S1 flow cell, and read length for R1-i7-i5-R2 was 28-8-0-91 cycles. scRNA-seq data were analyzed using cellranger count (v,7.0.0) with default parameters. The transcriptome database was based on cellranger GRCh38-2020-A_build. Since the cellranger algorithm only counted reads that were uniquely assigned to a single gene, to get accurate quantification of HBG genes two novel transcripts in the database (ensembl: ENSG00000284931 and ensembl: ENSG00000239920) that overlapped with HBG1 and HBG2 genes were removed. Seurat v.4.3.0 was used for the downstream analysis. The single-cell data were filtered by (1) number of expressed genes (i.e., nFeature_RNA) between 200 and 6,000, (2) percentage of mitochondrial reads (i.e., percent.mt), less than 10%, and (3) the total read (i.e., nCount_RNA) less than 50,000. The proportion of HBG gene expression was computed as the total read count of HBG1 and HBG2 divided by the total read count of HBG1, HBG2, and HBB genes. The "FindMarkers" function was used for differential gene expression analysis comparing edited and unedited cells. Single-cell data integration was based on the "anchor" method. The UMAP plot was generated using the first 50 PCs. Cell clustering was performed on the UMAP space with a resolution of 0.1. Cell annotation was based on identified gene markers.47 CNV analysis was done using inferCNV (v.1.6.0) with parameter setting (cutoff for the minimum average read counts per gene among reference cells is 0.1), cluster_by_groups = TRUE, HMM = TRUE, and denoise = TRUE.

sgRNA-seq and analysis

Synthetic sgRNAs (1–2 ng) were sequenced using a SMARTer smRNA-seq kit from Takara (cat. no. 635030). An original oligo-dT protocol is modified and first cDNA was synthesized directly with 1 μL of tailed scaffold primer (10 μM) that specifically binds to the gRNA scaffold sequence 5' GTGACTGGAGTTCAGACGTGTGCTCTTCCGATCT TGCCACTTTTTCAAGTTG 3'. The reactant was incubated at 72°C for 3 min and cooled to 4°C for 2 min to anneal the primer at the 3' end of the scaffold gRNA. Reverse transcription master mix was added to the reaction according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Next, full-length Illumina adapters were added by PCR primers with the following conditions, denaturation, 98°C for 1:00; annealing (17× cycles), 98°C, 60°C, 68°C for 0:10, 0:05, 0:10 min; hold 10°C. PCR products were purified using a nucleospin clean-up kit and quantified by Qubit HS. Pooled index library 2.2 nM (+10% Phix) was sequenced on MiSeq (Illumina) with a MiSeq v.2 micro kit using paired-end sequencing with 101-8-8-101 cycles. Paired-end reads were merged using FLASH with an expected fragment length of 100 bp. A scaffold-specific primer (3' end) was trimmed using cutadapt (v.4.1) and reads with length less than 20 bp were removed. The percentage of target gRNA and full-length product (target gRNA + scaffold sequence) were based on an exact string match. Merged reads were aligned to the expected full-length product using bowtie2 to identify indels in the gRNA sequencing data. Source code is available at https://github.com/tsailabSJ/gRNA_sequencing.

ddPCR for 4.9 kb deletion

Specific probes were designed to target HBG1 and HBG2 regions and used with respective forward and reverse primers that amplify both HBG1 and HBG2, resulting in 198 and 106 bp amplicons. Alternate primer sets were designed for donor-specific regions with nucleotide changes (Table S5). The reaction was set up with 100–300 ng of genomic DNA (from bulk BM), 0.9 μM probes, 0.25 μM probes, and ddPCR supermix (no dUTPs) (Bio-Rad, 1863024); droplets were generated using an automated droplet generator (Bio-Rad auto DG) and PCR reaction was performed on a CX1000 thermo cycler with the following steps: 95°C for 10 min; 94°C for 30 s; 56°C for 1 min (ramp rate 2°C/s, go to step 2 for 40 cycles total); 98°C for 10 min; 4°C hold. The droplets were read using a QX200 droplet reader (Bio-Rad) to measure the fluorescence intensity and data were analyzed using QuantaSoft.

PacBio sequencing

Genomic DNA (200 ng) used were isolated with a DNAdvance Kit (Agencourt, A48705) to the PCR 14.8 kb region in the HBB locus using forward and reverse PCR primers with indexes to allow pooling of multiple donor samples (Table S6). HbF measurement

Analytical ion-exchange HPLC quantification of hemoglobin tetramers and individual globin chains was performed using ion-exchange columns on a Prominence HPLC System (Shimadzu). Proteins eluted from the column were identified at 220 and 415 nm with a diode array detector. The relative amounts of hemoglobin or individual globin chains were calculated from the area under the 415-nm peak and normalized based on the DMSO control. Percentage of γ-globin = ((Gγ chain + Aγ chain)/β-like chains (β + Gγ + Aγ) × 100.

CHANGE-seq off-target nomination and analysis

Genomic DNA from human CD34+ cells from three normal adult donors was isolated using a Gentra Puregene Kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. CHANGE-seq was performed as described previously.55 In brief, purified genomic DNA was tagmented with a custom Tn5-transposome to an average length of 600–800 bp, followed by gap repair with Kapa HiFi HotStart Uracil+ DNA Polymerase (KAPA Biosystems) and Taq DNA ligase (NEB). Gap-repaired tagmented DNA was treated with USER enzyme (NEB) and T4 polynucleotide kinase (NEB). Intramolecular circularization of the DNA was performed with T4 DNA ligase (NEB) and residual linear DNA was degraded using a cocktail of exonucleases containing Plasmid-Safe ATP-dependent DNase (Lucigen), Lambda exonuclease (NEB) and exonuclease I (NEB). In vitro cleavage reactions were performed with 125 ng of exonuclease-treated circularized DNA, 90 nM of SpCas9-3x-NLS protein, NEB buffer 3.1 (NEB), and 270 nM of HBG-sgRNA (Synthego), in a 50 μL volume. Cleaved products were A-tailed, ligated with a hairpin adaptor (NEB), treated with USER enzyme (NEB), and amplified by PCR with barcoded universal primers NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (NEB), using Kapa HiFi Polymerase (KAPA Biosystems). Libraries were quantified by qPCR (KAPA Biosystems) and sequenced with 151 bp paired-end reads on an Illumina NextSeq instrument. CHANGE-seq data analyses were performed using open-source CHANGE-seq analysis software (https://github.com/tsailabSJ/changeseq).

Targeted amplicon sequencing by rhAmp-seq

On- and off-target sites identified by CHANGE-seq, CasOFFinder, CRISPRme, and CALITAS were amplified from genomic DNA from edited CD34+ normal cells or unedited control donor cells using the rhAmp-seq system (IDT). Sequencing libraries were generated according to the manufacturer’s instructions and sequenced with 151 bp paired end reads on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 instrument.

Sequencing analysis

Indel sequencing or rhAmp-seq analysis was conducted using custom Python code and open-source bioinformatic tools. First, high-throughput sequencing data files were demultiplexed to generate FASTQ format files. Paired-end high-throughput sequencing reads were processed to remove adapter sequences with trimmomatic76 (v.0.36), merged into a single read with FLASH77 (v.1.2.11), and mapped to human genome reference hg38 using BWA-MEM78 (v.0.7.12). Reads that mapped to on-target or off-target sites were realigned to the intended amplicon region sequence using a striped Smith-Waterman algorithm as implemented in the Python library scikit-bio; indels were counted and reported with total read counts.

Targeted amplicon sequencing data were analyzed using CrisprEsso (v.2.0.41). Microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ) was annotated based on sequence homology between the deleted sequence and its flanking upstream and/or downstream sequence. Specifically, the following two cases were labeled as MMEJ if (1) the first 2 bp of the deleted sequence was the same as the first 2 bp of its downstream sequence or (2) the last 2 bp of the deleted sequence was the same as the last 2 bp of its upstream sequence. Source code is available at https://github.com/tsailabSJ/count_indels_integrations.

In silico and variant-aware off-target analysis

In silico off-target analysis was performed using CasOFFinder with a tolerance either up to three mismatches or up to two mismatches (allowing unlimited G-A mismatches) within the protospacer and protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequences without any bulges. Search criteria allow mismatches within the PAM sequence using NNN as the desired PAM sequence. CRISPRme and CALITAS were used for variant-aware off-target analysis, and the predicted sites were merged for validation. CRISPRme includes variants from 1000 Genome Projects (1000GP, phase 2, 2,548 individuals) and the Human Genome Diversity Project database including 929 individuals. The search parameters were: spCas9-NGG, up to 3 mismatches, up to 2 DNA bulges, and up to 2 RNA bulges. Post-filtering was applied to off-targets with total number of bulges plus mismatches higher than 3, which resulted in 27 total on/off-targets and 13 of them overlapped with variants. CALITAS command-line version was used to search for off-targets based on variants calling from African American donors used in this study (n = 4). The search parameters were “--max-guide-diffs 5 --max-total-diffs 5 --max-pam-mismatches 2 --max-gaps-between-guide-and-pam 2”. The same post-filtering was applied to off targets with a total number of bulges plus mismatches higher than 3 which resulted in 117 total on/off-targets and 6 of them overlapped with variants. Altogether, the in silico analysis identified 57 additional putative off-target sites (this is 9 from casoffinder and 48 from calitas+crisprme).

Analysis of translocations and other structural rearrangements

The UDiTaS protocol was performed to identify translocations with the on-target breakpoint of HBG-sgRNA in the HBG1/HBG2 promoter at days 5 and 14 after editing. A custom Tn5-transposome with the full-length Illumina forward (i5) adapter, a sample barcode, and unique molecule identifier was used for the genomic DNA tagmentation reaction. After genomic DNA tagmentation, two rounds of PCRs were performed. The first PCR was performed using the target-specific HBG promoter anchor primer (Table S7), and the second round of PCR added the reverse (i7) Illumina adapter and an additional sample barcode. Two primers targeting HBG1 and HBG2 gene promoters were used in parallel for the first PCR. Conditions for PCR1 and PCR2 are denaturation, 95°C for 5:00; touchdown (15× cycles), 95°C, 70°C, 72°C for 0:30, 2:00 (1°C/cycle), 0:30 min; annealing (10× cycles), 95°C, 55°C, 72°C for 0:30, 1:00, 0:30 min; 72°C for 5:00 min; hold 4°C. For positive control, gene-specific primers designed for both BCL11A and HBG promoter complete libraries were then size selected for 400–850 bp using PippinHT, quantified by qPCR, and sequenced on Illumina MiSeq with paired-end 301-8-18-301 cycles. Analysis was performed by https://github.com/editasmedicine/uditas.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). For comparative analysis, ordinary one-way ANOVA (Dunnett’s multiple comparison tests and Tukey’s multiple comparison tests) and two-way ANOVA (Šídák’s multiple comparisons tests) were used. Unpaired t-tests were performed for comparison for fewer than two groups. Data points with three or more replicates considered for statistical analyses and two or fewer were excluded. Mean and SD represented as mean (SD); row statistics were performed and presented in the results.

Source code for sequencing data analysis is provided below:CHANGE-seq: https://github.com/tsailabSJ/changeseq.PacBio sequencing: https://github.com/tsailabSJ/Pacbio_HBG.Single-guide RNA sequencing: https://github.com/tsailabSJ/gRNA_sequencing.Targeted sequencing: https://github.com/tsailabSJ/count_indels_integrations.UDiTaS: https://github.com/editasmedicine/uditas.
