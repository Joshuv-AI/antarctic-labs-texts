Resistance to a Novel Clinical Trial Design for Multicancer Detection Tests: Experimental Investigation of Ambiguity Aversion
Authors: Jacob A Rohde, Jackelyn B Payne, Richard P Moser, William M P Klein, Philip E Castle, Paul K J Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are needed to evaluate multicancer detection (MCD) tests. A novel “intended-effect” RCT design in which control group participants receive MCD testing but results remain undisclosed can increase statistical efficiency. Prospective participants may find this design unacceptable due to ambiguity aversion introduced by the existence of undisclosed but theoretically knowable test results. We evaluated this effect and tested if reducing ambiguity by ensuring the unknowability of test results mitigates this bias.Methods:US adults aged 45 to 70 y (N = 1,576) were randomized to read about 1 of 4 RCT designs evaluating MCD testing: 1) RCT with a conventional control group (no testing), 2) RCT with a standard intended-effect control group (ie, control gets testing but not the results), 3) modified intended-effect RCT emphasizing disclosure safeguards to ensure the unknowability of control group test results to participants and researchers, and 4) modified intended-effect RCT with time-delayed testing on control group blood samples after trial completion. Outcomes were study interest (5-point scale, with higher values indicating more interest) and intention to participate (yes/no). Analyses of variance, chi-square tests, and regression-based analyses examined the main effects and potential mediating roles of the perceived net benefit of participating in the study and trust in study researchers.Results:Intention to participate was similar across all conditions (range: 14%–21%). Study interest was higher in the conventional control (M = 3.73) vs the 3 intended-effect designs (P < 0.05), which did not differ (Ms = 3.36–3.45). The negative influence of intended-effect designs on study interest was partially mediated by perceived net benefit.Conclusion:Intended-effect RCT designs lower interest in MCD trial participation, in part by reducing prospective participants’ perceptions of benefiting from the trial. Strategies to reduce ambiguity do not mitigate this effect.Keywords: multicancer detection, randomized clinical trial, participant recruitment

Introduction

Multicancer detection (MCD) tests are emerging blood-based cancer screening tests designed to detect the presence of multiple types of cancer, including some malignancies for which no standard-of-care screening tests exist.1,2 Some MCD tests are being implemented in clinical practice and marketed as cancer screening tools to the public (eg, Galleri); however, no MCD test to date has received US Food and Drug Administration approval. Although some studies have evaluated the preliminary effectiveness of these tests, 3 additional evidence from large-scale randomized clinical trials (RCTs) is still needed to better understand the diagnostic performance and clinical effectiveness of MCD tests in decreasing cancer-specific and overall mortality. This evidence, however, requires large sample sizes and long follow-up periods, and recruiting participants to RCTs is an ongoing problem. 4

This problem has led investigators to explore alternative study designs. One such design, which Katki et al 5 termed the “intended-effect” design, has researchers conduct MCD tests on participants in both treatment and control groups. Test results, however, would only be shared with individuals in the treatment group.6–8 This type of design is advantageous because it increases statistical power while minimizing the requisite sample size of the study by enabling comparison of outcomes among only the participants with positive tests who could have benefitted from screening. By contrast, RCTs using a conventional control group would need to estimate the effects of the MCD test across the entire randomized population, including the majority who would not have a positive cancer screening result.

An intended-effect design, however, may not be acceptable to prospective research participants. In a qualitative study about MCD tests by Samimi and colleagues, 9 laypersons and primary care physicians reported negative attitudes toward an intended-effect RCT design and expressed concerns that nondisclosure of MCD test results would deprive participants of the benefits of testing, even when reminded about the existence of clinical equipoise about the test’s effectiveness. Perceptions of the trial were also negative. For example, several layperson participants perceived that the goals of the study researchers were being prioritized over their well-being, and some physician participants perceived that the intended-effect design would undermine patient trust in research.

Another factor that may contribute to negative perceptions toward an intended effect design is a cognitive bias known as “ambiguity aversion”—a tendency to avoid choices that involve unknown risks. To demonstrate this phenomenon, theorist Ellsberg 10 conducted a game of chance in which study subjects were asked to randomly draw a ball from 1 of 2 urns containing either 1) 100 equally distributed red and black balls (ie, 50 of each) or 2) an unknown distribution of red and black balls. Subjects were told they would win money if they drew a ball of a specified color. Ellsberg found that most subjects favored the first urn with a known probability distribution over the second urn with the unknown probability distribution, despite the expected value of each urn being statistically equivalent, given that all possible distributions of red and black balls in the second urn average to a 50% selection probability of each color ball. This finding showed that people’s choices are based both on the probabilities associated with choice outcomes and on their associated uncertainty of these probabilities.

Ambiguity aversion may bias people against MCD test RCTs using an intended-effect control arm given that testing control group participants introduces an unknown, yet nonzero, probability of benefit (and harm) from testing, even if results are not acted on. 11 By contrast, a conventional RCT design offers control group participants a known and definite probability of benefit and harm given that MCD testing is never performed for this group. Ambiguity aversion may thus explain why prospective MCD test participants in Samimi et al 9 opposed the intended-effect design compared with the conventional design. If this is the case, then perhaps decreasing the potential for ambiguity aversion could heighten people’s interest in participating in such intended-effect RCTs.

Theoretical accounts of the mechanisms of ambiguity aversion suggest specific strategies to accomplish this aim. For example, the “hostile nature” hypothesis11–14 posits that people’s reluctance toward options with unknown outcome probabilities is likely due to people’s perceptions that the choice process is not truly random but instead determined by others. 12 According to this hypothesis, participants completing the Ellsberg task avoid the urn with the unknown probability because they suspect that the experimenter possesses superior knowledge of its contents and may bias results against participants—a condition that Chen 15 has characterized as “disadvantageous asymmetric information.” Applied to the situation of intended-effect RCT designs, the hostile nature hypothesis posits that information asymmetry between researchers and participants would lead participants to distrust the researchers and negatively affect participation. However, Chen has shown that eliminating information asymmetry in the standard Ellsberg task by allowing participants, rather than experimenters, to generate the probability distribution made them prefer choosing the ambiguous urn (vs the known 50/50 distribution option). 16 This finding supports the hostile nature hypothesis and suggests that decreasing information asymmetry may reduce ambiguity aversion in other contexts.

The “comparative ignorance hypothesis” is another theoretical account of ambiguity aversion that supports this possibility. This hypothesis posits that aversion to unknown probabilities is driven by an implicit comparison with either less ambiguous options or with more knowledgeable individuals (eg, study researchers). 16 This comparison leads people to negatively appraise ambiguous options.17–19 The comparative ignorance hypothesis predicts that the aversion of MCD test trial participants to the intended-effect study design is driven by a comparison with either the more familiar conventional RCT or the superior knowledge of study researchers vs participants.

These theoretical accounts suggest that reducing information asymmetry may diminish people’s reluctance to participating in intended-effect study designs in MCD trials, as exhibited by laypersons in Samimi et al.9,17,20 The current study explored this possibility. The goal of this work was to inform future RCT study design efforts by testing whether 2 different theory-based alternative RCT designs could mitigate some of the negative biases and perceptions toward intended-effect MCD trials, as exemplified in prior work. 9 The first alternate design introduces an unknowability component in which security measures prohibit study researchers (as well as participants) from accessing MCD test results of the control group during the trial.17,18 The second alternate design also makes test results unknowable but through time-delayed testing; that is, MCD tests of participants’ previously collected blood samples would be conducted only after the trial is complete, in 5 to 6 y. This study tested the following hypotheses:

H1: Interest in MCD trial participation will be lower for the standard intended-effect design (vs conventional control).

H1: Interest in MCD trial participation will be lower for the standard intended-effect design (vs conventional control).

H2: Alternative intended-effect control group designs that reduce information asymmetry will increase interest in trial participation compared with the standard intended-effect design.

H2: Alternative intended-effect control group designs that reduce information asymmetry will increase interest in trial participation compared with the standard intended-effect design.

Secondary objectives were to test the potential mediating effect of 2 theory-based factors on participants’ interest in participating in alternative MCD test RCT designs: 1) perceived net benefit (ie, perceived benefit minus harm) of participating in the RCT, as informed by the comparative ignorance hypothesis, 16 and 2) trust in study researchers, as informed by the hostile nature hypothesis.11–14,17 Specifically, we predicted the following:

H3: Perceived net benefit and trust in study researchers would be lower in the 3 intended-effect study design conditions vs conventional control.

H3: Perceived net benefit and trust in study researchers would be lower in the 3 intended-effect study design conditions vs conventional control.

H4: The negative impact of the intended effect conditions (vs conventional) on interest in MCD trial participation would be mediated by perceived net benefit and trust.

H4: The negative impact of the intended effect conditions (vs conventional) on interest in MCD trial participation would be mediated by perceived net benefit and trust.

Methods

Participants and Procedures

We conducted a between-subjects, online survey experiment in August 2023 using Prolific, a commercial opt-in crowdsourcing research panel. Prolific vets all panel participants (including a photo ID check) to help safeguard against fraudulent data. 20 Participants (N = 1,580) consisted of panel members reporting US residence and aged 45 to 70 y. Enrolled participants provided informed consent and were randomized to view 1 of 4 participant recruitment flyers for a hypothetical RCT to evaluate MCD tests for cancer screening, after which they completed questionnaires containing various study measures (described below).

Participants were compensated $2.40 USD for completing the survey (~$13.40 per hour). This study was preregistered through aspredicted.org (#140088). To be excluded, participants had to fail 2 attention check items in the survey or complete the survey more than 2 standard deviations faster than the average completion time (~12 min 30 s). These criteria did not exclude any participants; however, 4 people were removed due to providing incomplete survey data, resulting in a final analytic sample of N = 1,576 adults. All procedures were reviewed and approved by the National Institutes of Health Institutional Review Board (#IRB001814).

Experimental Stimuli

The experimental stimulus was a recruitment flyer for a hypothetical RCT. The flyer contained a brief explanation of MCD tests and described researchers’ plans to conduct an RCT to evaluate the effectiveness of these tests. Participants were randomly assigned to see 1 of 4 control group designs in the flyer. The first group was a “conventional” RCT in which participants in the control condition do not receive the MCD test. The remaining 3 groups consisted of alternative intended-effect control conditions: “unknown,” the standard intended effect design in which a control group would have the MCD test but not receive the test results; “unknowable,” similar to the unknown condition but with added safeguards that would make the test results unknowable to both participants and researchers; and “time-delayed,” a control group that would have blood banked at the beginning of the trial and MCD testing would not occur until the conclusion of the study in 5 to 6 y, making the test results unknowable to anyone until then. All other language in the recruitment flyer was the same across conditions. The stimuli were approximately 300 words each (range: 278–340 words). Measures

Primary outcomes

Study interest was assessed with a single item asking, “How interested would you be in participating in this study?” Response options were on the following 5-point scale: not at all (1), slightly (2), somewhat (3), very (4), and extremely (5). Intentions to participate were assessed with a single item asking, “Would you actually participate in this study?” Response options were “yes” and “no.” Both primary outcomes were developed for this study.

Secondary outcomes

The perceived net benefit of participating in the study was assessed with 2 items asking participants how likely they thought it was that participating in the trial would benefit (item 1) and harm them (item 2). Responses to both items were on 5-point scales ranging from not at all (1) to extremely (5). We subtracted 1 from all response values, setting the lowest response value to 0. The 2 items were combined by subtracting participants’ perceived harm scores from their perceived benefit scores, resulting in a net measure (referred to as perceived net benefit for readability) with values ranging from −4 to +4, with 0 representing a neutral perception. We also separately report the perceived benefit and harm single items for exploratory purposes. Trust in study researchers was assessed with 2 items (averaged together) measuring participants’ trust and confidence in the RCT study researchers ([Pearson’s] r = 0.76). Items were, “How much would you trust the researchers who are doing this study?” and “How confident are you that the researchers would act in the best interests of the study participants?” Responses to both trust items were assessed on 5-point scales ranging from not at all (1) to completely/extremely (5). All secondary outcomes were developed for this study.

Participant demographics and covariates

We assessed age (self-reported), sex, educational attainment, Hispanic ethnicity, race, sexual orientation, household income, past cancer history, ambiguity aversion, and attitudes toward taking risks. Ambiguity aversion was assessed on a 5-item scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5) (α = 0.85); example items were “Conflicting expert opinions about a medical test or treatment would make me upset” and “I would avoid making a decision about a medical test or treatment if experts had conflicting opinions about it.” 21 Attitudes toward taking risks was assessed on a 6-item scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5) (α = 0.86); example items were “I enjoy taking risks” and “I try to avoid situations that have uncertain outcomes.” 22

Data Analysis

We used descriptive statistics to characterize participants and 1-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs)/chi-square tests (with pairwise post hoc comparisons) to examine differences in our primary and secondary outcomes grouped by the 4 experimental conditions (conventional control vs unknown vs unknowable vs time-delayed). We also separately assessed whether perceived net benefit and trust in study researchers (Ms) mediated the association between the experimental condition (X) and the study interest and intentions to participate outcomes (Ys). Note that we only tested mediation models comparing the conventional control group vs the intended effect groups (rather than all 6 pairwise comparisons). We made this decision because the primary objective of this study was to assess if different intended effect control group variations attenuated effects relative to the conventional control group. Indirect effects were calculated by multiplying the X→M (path a) and M→Y (path b) adjusted path estimates; direct effects were the X→Y adjusted path estimates. We only computed mediation models for intended-effect experimental conditions (vs conventional control) that exhibited a main effect on the mediator variables in the unadjusted ANOVA/chi-square analyses. Models used a bootstrap method with 1,000 repeated simulations to estimate variance and adjusted for ambiguity aversion, risk-taking attitudes, sex (male vs female), education (college graduate vs not), race (white vs other race), and history of a cancer diagnosis. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.2).

There was no difference in study interest among any of the other 3 individual groups. In addition, there was a null effect of study condition on intentions to participate (range: 14%–21%, P = 0.058).

For secondary outcomes, results exhibited a main effect of study condition on perceived net benefit (F = 7.39, P < 0.001; Table 2), which was higher in the conventional control group (M = 2.25, SD = 1.36) than in the unknown (M = 1.95, SD = 1.51), unknowable (M = 1.79, SD = 1.45), and time-delayed (M = 1.98, SD = 1.41) groups (all Ps < 0.05). The individual perceived benefit (F = 5.10, P = 0.002) and harm (F = 4.37, P = 0.005) items also varied by study condition. Perceived benefit was higher in the conventional control group (M = 2.55, SD = 1.09) than in the unknowable (M = 2.27, SD = 1.06) and time-delayed (M = 2.36, SD = 1.10) groups (both Ps < 0.05). Perceived harm was higher in the conventional control group (M = −0.31, SD = 0.65) than in the unknown (M = −0.47, SD = 0.83) and unknowable (M = −0.48, SD = 0.86) groups (both Ps < 0.05). Note that perceived harm was reverse coded, meaning higher outcomes indicate less of a belief among our sample that enrolling in the RCT would harm them. Finally, there was a main effect of study condition on trust in study researchers (F = 5.25, P = 0.001; Table 2). Specifically, trust was higher in the conventional control group (M = 3.68, SD = 0.88) than the unknowable group (M = 3.43, SD = 0.96) group (P < 0.001). No other pairwise comparisons for these secondary outcomes were significant.

Trust in study researchers partially mediated the effect of the unknowable condition (vs conventional control) on study interest (indirect effect: b = −0.08, P < 0.001; direct effect: b = −0.10, P = 0.007; Table 3). Consistent with our prediction, the unknowable condition reduced study interest through its negative effect on trust, which was positively associated with study interest. There were no main effects of the unknown and time-delayed conditions (vs conventional control) on trust in study researchers, so they were excluded in mediation analyses. In addition, we did not test the mediating effects of trust and perceived net benefit for the intentions to participate outcome due to a lack of a main experimental effect.

Discussion

This study tested people’s perceptions of participating in a hypothetical MCD test trial using novel control group designs. Our results suggest that “intended-effect” designs—in which participants in a control group receive an MCD test but are not given the results—decreased interest in trial participation compared with an RCT using a conventional control group design (ie, no treatment). These findings provide quantitative support for recent qualitative data suggesting people are reluctant and skeptical of intended-effect trial designs evaluating MCD tests. 9

Ambiguity aversion is one potential explanation for our findings. This experiment assessed whether alternative intended-effect designs that address theorized causes of ambiguity aversion (eg, information asymmetry) could mitigate people’s bias against these designs. We tested 2 such alternative designs: an unknowable control group in which safeguards prevent study participants and researchers from knowing MCD test results and a time-delayed control group in which study researchers conduct MCD tests on banked blood only after the trial ends (5–6 y later). Notably, our results did not support these predictions; all primary (study interest and intentions to participate) and secondary outcomes (perceived net benefit and trust in study researchers) were similar between the standard intended effect condition and the 2 alternative control group conditions.

There are a few possible explanations for these findings. First, addressing information asymmetry may simply not reduce ambiguity aversion in the context of this study and experiment. Second, our 2 alternate intended-effect designs emphasizing the unknowability of the test results may not have been effective or strong enough at mitigating information asymmetry or at countering participants’ perceived comparative ignorance that study researchers would know their MCD test results. Participants may have been left with the belief in a nonzero chance that someone on the research team knows (or could find out) the MCD test results, even if difficult. It should be also acknowledged that we did not collect data on participants’ perceptions of the different study design conditions. Thus, it is possible that some participants may not have understood or believed the reassurances implemented to reduce ambiguity aversion in the unknown and time-delayed conditions. Future studies should build on these null effects by testing people’s comprehension of alternative intended-effect designs.

Another explanation for why the alternative intended-effect conditions produced null findings on study interest (vs the standard intended-effect design) could be that ambiguity aversion is not the underlying mechanism driving participants’ perceptions of the intended-effect study design; there may be other reasons for why study participants rejected this design. Finally, it is possible that participants may have perceived controllability over their cancer risk, which might affect their interest in MCD tests in general. Ultimately, minimizing participants’ resistance to intended-effect control group designs for RCTs evaluating MCD tests needs further study and may require different modifications.

We also tested whether 2 factors hypothesized to play a causal role in ambiguity aversion—perceived net benefit from participating in the RCT and trust in study researchers—mediated control group effects on trial interest. We found significant indirect effects for both mediators, indicating that the negative effects of alternative control group conditions on trial interest were, in part, explained by way of their negative associations with trust and perceived study benefit. These findings support theoretical accounts of ambiguity aversion (eg, hostile nature hypothesis, comparative ignorance hypothesis)11–14,16 and suggest that these factors may play a critical role in the decision-making process for those considering enrolling in MCD test trials. These findings further support the notion that positive expectancies about trial participation and trust in study researchers are important to reinforce in efforts to recruit participants to RCTs, regardless of design.

It is important to recognize that our experimental design precludes us from establishing the causal order of the mediation models tested in this study. An alternative model in which study interest (or perhaps some other unmeasured variable) mediates experimental condition effects on perceived net benefit (or trust) is logically plausible. For example, participants’ low interest in, say, the time-delayed condition could have influenced their subsequent perceptions of study benefit. Similarly, some other unmeasured factor could have influenced our study outcomes. Additional experiments testing alternative designs are needed to refute or support any such hypothesis.

One last notable finding is that intentions to participate (1 of our 2 primary outcomes) in our hypothetical MCD test trial did not vary across study conditions, including between the conventional control and the 3 intended-effect designs. In fact, only about 18% of respondents said they would actually participate in the trial. This finding speaks to the challenges that researchers face in recruiting RCT samples 4 and underscores the potential value of intended-effect designs that increase statistical power with smaller requisite samples than conventional RCT designs.

Strengths of this study include the experimental design that tested novel RCT recruitment strategies and the inclusion of theoretically important mediators (ie, perceived net benefit, trust) in evaluating the experimental study effects. However, the study also had some limitations. First, this study used an opt-in commercial survey panel to recruit participants. Although we implemented several data validity checks (ie, attention items, analyzing mean survey completion time), there is a possibility that some responses were completed by bots. Second, this experiment tested a hypothetical RCT recruitment scenario, which minimizes the ecological validity of our findings. Moreover, our data were collected in 2023, and since that time, direct-to-consumer marketing and media coverage of MCD tests (eg, Galleri) have likely increased awareness of these tools. Thus, current public interest in and intentions to participate in MCD test RCTs may differ from what was observed in our sample. A third limitation is that the primary and secondary outcomes were developed for this study (some of which used only single-item responses) and are not validated, preexisting measures. Lastly, there is a possibility that participants did not fully comprehend study design nuances described in our experimental stimuli. Future studies should consider additional survey verifications to boost confidence in the validity of the self-reported outcomes, such as by incorporating open-ended questions assessing reactions to the stimuli.

It should also be noted that some of the study findings exhibited small effect sizes. Moreover, only about 1 in 5 participants, regardless of study condition, indicated they would actually participate in an MCD trial. Although these effect sizes and estimates are low, they are not unexpected given prior evidence about people’s perceptions toward participating in MCD trials. 9 In addition, these results may still be valuable in helping improve recruitment to clinical trials, which is a recognized problem. 4 While this study provides valuable evidence about the mechanisms underlying people’s responses to the intended-effect RCT design, further studies are needed to replicate our findings using other outcome measures and different participant subsamples.

In conclusion, novel intended-effect study designs that can increase efficiency of MCD clinical trials may be unacceptable to trial participants, and ambiguity aversion is one potential cause that may operate through negative effects on participants’ expectations of benefit and trust in study researchers. However, alternative trial designs aimed at mitigating ambiguity aversion by emphasizing the unknowability of trial results may not mitigate this bias. More research is needed to understand the causes of people’s resistance to intended-effect and other novel clinical trial designs and to develop strategies that can address these causes and improve clinical trial recruitment efforts. This need is particularly salient given that recent evidence from the NHS-Galleri RCT did not demonstrate a significant reduction in advanced-stage cancer among participants using the Galleri MCD test; however, findings did show some clinical promise in reducing stage IV cancer diagnoses. 23 As the science behind MCD tests continues to develop, there will be a need to evaluate these (and similar) tools in clinical trials.
