Growth Hormone‐Induced Stridor in a Patient With Prader–Willi Syndrome
Authors: Anthony M. Saad, Haider Zaki, Adarsh Menon, Mark Hausdorff, Brian Manzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile growth hormone therapy has been associated with the potential risk of upper airway obstruction and hypertrophy, it may also cause glottic or subglottic edema and stridor, even in patients without adenotonsillar tissue. Clinicians should consider medication‐induced airway changes in Prader–Willi Syndrome patients with complex airway histories to optimize management.

Introduction

Prader–Willi syndrome (PWS) is a genetic disorder due to lack of expression of paternally inherited genes, leading to multisystemic effects including infantile hypotonia, obesity, and overall failure to thrive [1, 2]. Growth hormone (GH) therapy is standard treatment for children with PWS, improving both motor function and body composition [3]. However, GH therapy has been associated with an increased risk of upper airway obstruction, particularly causing tonsillar and adenoidal hypertrophy [4]. Although there are several cases in the literature describing sudden death in patients with Prader–Willi syndrome (PWS) occurring within months of initiating GH therapy, there are no published reports of GH‐induced glottic or subglottic edema resulting in stridor in this population [5, 6]. GH's role in causing glottic and subglottic edema is even less frequently reported, making it a potentially overlooked contributor to respiratory complications in patients undergoing GH therapy [7, 8]. This report explores this rarely discussed effect, emphasizing the need for cautious use of GH in patients with complex airway conditions.

Case History/Examination

A 4‐year‐old male with a history of Prader–Willi syndrome (PWS), prematurity (born at 27 weeks), seizure disorder, and bronchopulmonary dysplasia presented with significant subglottic stenosis (70%) on fiberoptic imaging (Figure 1). Genetic testing performed in infancy demonstrated a 5.5 Mb deletion of chromosome 15q11.2 on chromosomal microarray, and methylation studies confirmed paternal origin of the deletion, consistent with Prader–Willi syndrome. He had frequent nocturnal desaturations to 70%, persistent stridor, and increased work of breathing over two days. His extensive airway history included direct laryngoscopy and bronchoscopy (DLB), CO2 laser treatment, vocal fold work, supraglottoplasty, adenotonsillectomy, and a laryngotracheal reconstruction (LTR) with anterior cartilage graft. He also underwent tracheal dilation and multiple stoma granuloma excisions. Despite this history, he had been tolerating growth hormone (GH) therapy prior to this episode. The patient had been receiving recombinant human growth hormone therapy for approximately two years at a dose of 0.4 mg subcutaneously nightly.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis and Investigations)

On examination in the Emergency Department, the patient was not in acute distress and maintained adequate oxygenation on room air. He was admitted to the pediatric ICU for monitoring. Soft tissue neck and chest radiographs demonstrated no evidence of tracheal narrowing, foreign body, or pulmonary pathology. No acute infectious symptoms or fevers were present. Given concern for airway decompensation, he was empirically started on systemic corticosteroids, and GH therapy was held at the time of admission.

On hospital day 2, the patient underwent operative evaluation. Direct laryngoscopy revealed a well‐healed anterior cartilage graft with no evidence of recurrent stenosis or tracheomalacia (Figure 2). However, mild supraglottic collapse suggestive of recurrent laryngomalacia was noted (Figure 3). The subglottis and trachea were patent, and no discrete obstructive lesions or inflammation were identified.

Postoperative airway showing laryngotracheal reconstruction (LTR) with anterior cartilage graft with no stenosis or tracheomalacia.

Direct laryngoscopy and bronchoscopy (DLB) during acute hospitalization showing laryngomalacia along with glottic edema.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

In the days following cessation of GH therapy and initiation of corticosteroids, the patient's stridor resolved, and nocturnal oxygen saturations returned to baseline. No further interventions were required, and he was discharged with outpatient follow‐up. At each subsequent outpatient follow‐up, the patient demonstrated sustained resolution of stridor and nocturnal desaturations, with no recurrence of symptoms while growth hormone therapy remained discontinued.

This case of growth hormone‐induced stridor shows the importance of including medication cessation in the treatment differential. Growth hormone increases adenotonsillar tissue as well as can affect laryngeal and tracheal structures. Particularly, in this case, a patient with a complex airway history and absent adenotonsillar tissue precluded the identification of growth hormone‐induced stridor. Growth hormone was attributed to this patient's stridor by causing temporary edema (reversed with IV steroids) as well as decreasing laryngeal support structures.

Discussion

In patients with complex airway anatomy, differentials for stridor and sleep disordered breathing are wide and complex. Common causes include fixed narrowing of the upper airway anatomy, dynamic airway collapse, infectious changes, and iatrogenic effects. This case presentation of a young patient with Prader–Willi syndrome and a complex airway history highlights a critical but possibly overlooked etiology of stridor: growth hormone therapy.

The patient in this case carried an extensive history of airway complications, including significant subglottic stenosis, rightfully placing the initial spotlight on structural causes. The patient's overall treatment included growth hormone therapy, a mainstay of PWS treatment, with benefits for growth and body composition in children who, due to PWS, are at risk for failure to thrive and developmental delays, among other challenges [2]. Despite this benefit, growth hormone is linked in the literature to cases of adenotonsillar hypertrophy and worsening of sleep apnea [3, 9]. GH's role in causing glottic and subglottic edema is less frequently reported, yet a concern nonetheless, especially in this patient who was status‐post adenotonsillectomy. For this reason, at the time of admission to the ICU for monitoring, the patient's growth hormone was held, and steroid treatment was initiated. Chest and soft tissue XR showed no abnormality, and direct laryngoscopy showed no stenosis or tracheomalacia. With no intervention aside from growth hormone cessation, the patient's nocturnal stridor and sleep apnea gradually resolved. With exclusion of infectious and structural etiologies and improvement following GH cessation without surgical intervention, growth hormone therapy was considered the most likely contributing factor. The lack of symptom recurrence for over a year after GH cessation also supports these findings.

Growth hormone therapy's role in causing glottic/subglottic edema and inciting/worsening sleep disordered breathing comes from somatic growth stimulation, causing soft tissue hypertrophy and increased mucosal bulk. Our patient incurred further increased airway risk due to his extensive prior surgical interventions and preexisting decreased physiologic reserve. By presenting this patient, we hope to mitigate future unnecessary exposure to anesthetics and demonstrate the need for more vigilant administration of this medication in patients with complex airway anatomy.
