Xpert bladder cancer detection and monitor in urothelial carcinoma: systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ludovica Cella, Vittorio Fasulo, Alessandro Uleri, Andrea Piccolini, Valerio Mallia, Francesco Sormani, Alessio Finocchiaro, Roberto Contieri, Stefano Moretto, Pier Paolo Avolio, Alberto Saita, Rodolfo Hurle, Paolo Casale, Giovanni Lughezzani, Nicolò Maria Buffi, Massimo Lazzeri, Marco Paciotti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractXpert Bladder Cancer (BC) is an mRNA-based urine assay for diagnosis and surveillance of urothelial carcinoma. Although previous meta-analyses have examined Xpert, none has evaluated its accuracy in both BC detection and non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) surveillance, while also exploring its potential role in guiding repeat transurethral resection (re-TURBT) and in the diagnostic evaluation of upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC). We systematically assessed the diagnostic accuracy of Xpert in all these clinical settings, integrating grade-specific analysis and formal evidence-certainty assessment. PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were searched to January 2026 (PROSPERO: CRD420251184275). Sensitivity and specificity were pooled using bivariate random-effects models; PPV and NPV using univariate models. Grade-specific sensitivity was pooled across 13 studies with tumour-grade-stratified data. Evidence certainty was assessed using GRADE-DTA. Publication bias was evaluated with Deeks’ funnel plots. Twenty-six studies (8613 patients) were included. For Detection (k = 8, where k denotes the number of cohorts pooled), pooled sensitivity was 0.83 (95% CI 0.73–0.90) and specificity 0.77 (0.65–0.86); NPV was 0.96. For Monitor (k = 15), sensitivity was 0.71 (0.63–0.77) and specificity 0.78 (0.70–0.85); NPV was 0.91. Grade-specific pooling showed high-grade (HG) sensitivity of 0.89 (0.83–0.93) in Detection and 0.80 (0.71–0.87) in Monitor, versus 0.60 for low-grade (LG) in both settings; heterogeneity was not statistically significant for HG estimates. Re-TURBT (k = 2) and UTUC (k = 2) were exploratory, with encouraging NPV but limited by sparse events. Evidence certainty was low for sensitivity/NPV and very low for specificity/PPV. No publication bias was detected. Xpert demonstrates high sensitivity and NPV, particularly for HG disease, supporting its role as an adjunctive rule-out tool. Grade-adapted interpretation is warranted. Introduction

Urinary biomarkers are increasingly incorporated into diagnostic and surveillance pathways of urothelial carcinoma, driven by the need for non-invasive alternatives to cystoscopy, which remains invasive, resource-intensive, and associated with patient discomfort1.

Cystoscopy nonetheless remains the cornerstone of both the diagnosis and the surveillance of urothelial carcinoma, and most patients with non-muscle-invasive disease undergo repeated office-based examinations over many years. This recurring follow-up carries a substantial cumulative burden: flexible cystoscopy is frequently associated with procedural discomfort and pre-procedural anxiety, and these factors can negatively affect patient satisfaction and, in some cases, adherence to long-term surveillance schedules2. Reducing the frequency of these examinations without compromising oncological safety is therefore a central goal, and provides a strong rationale for adjunctive non-invasive urinary biomarkers that can help triage which patients require cystoscopy and when.

The Xpert Bladder Cancer (Xpert BC) assay is a fully automated, cartridge-based RT-qPCR test that quantifies the mRNA expression of five bladder-cancer-associated genes (ABL1, CRH, IGF2, UPK1B, ANXA10) from voided urine and provides a dichotomous result within 90 min3. Two configurations serve distinct indications: Xpert BC Detection for patients with suspected BC4, and Xpert BC Monitor for NMIBC surveillance5.

Individual studies have reported high sensitivity and excellent negative predictive value (NPV), particularly for high-grade disease, but specificity remains moderate and the optimal clinical contexts for implementation are uncertain6–9. Meanwhile, competing urinary biomarker platforms (Cxbladder, ADXBLADDER, Bladder EpiCheck, Uromonitor) have broadened the diagnostic landscape, reinforcing the need to define Xpert’s comparative position10–12.

A prior meta-analysis by Pham et al. (2021) pooled 11 surveillance studies (2,896 patients), reporting sensitivity and specificity of 0.73 and 0.77, respectively. However, that review was limited to the Monitor setting, predated three pivotal randomised controlled trials (UroFollow, DaBlaCa-15, VERNAL), and did not assess the Detection assay, evidence certainty, or grade-specific diagnostic performance.

To address these gaps, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis synthesising Xpert performance across four clinical settings—Detection, Monitor, repeat transurethral resection (re-TURBT) and upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC)—incorporating grade-specific subgroup analysis, GRADE-DTA evidence assessment, and publication bias evaluation.

Evidence acquisition

Search strategy

This review was conducted following PRISMA 2020, PRISMA-DTA, and MOOSE recommendations13–15, aligned with the 2025 EAU Guidelines Office methodology16,17. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251184275). The PRISMA checklist is available in Supplementary Table S1. PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were searched without date restrictions. The full search strategy is provided in Supplementary Appendix S1.

Study selection and eligibility criteria

Studies were screened using the PICOS framework14. Eligible studies enrolled adults (≥ 18 years) with urothelial carcinoma evaluated in one of four prespecified clinical contexts: (i) Detection (patients with haematuria or suspected BC without prior history); (ii) Monitor (patients with NMIBC undergoing post-resection surveillance); (iii) re-TURBT (Xpert as a predictor of the need for repeat resection; exploratory) and (iv) UTUC detection (exploratory). The index test was Xpert BC Detection or Monitor, compared against cystoscopy and urine cytology with or without histological confirmation. Studies were eligible if they reported or allowed reconstruction of 2 × 2 data. Randomised, prospective, and retrospective studies were included.

Two reviewers (L.C. and V.F.) independently screened titles and abstracts, assessed full texts for eligibility, and resolved discrepancies through discussion with a third reviewer (M.P.). Only English-language original studies were included. Reviews, editorials, meta-analyses, commentaries, conference abstracts, and case reports were excluded, but their reference lists were screened to identify additional eligible articles. Potential overlap between cohorts was specifically assessed by examining author lists, recruiting centres, enrolment periods, and sample sizes; when multiple reports appeared to describe overlapping or duplicate patient populations, only the most complete or most recent dataset was retained to avoid double-counting.

Data extraction and risk of bias

Two authors (L.C. and V.F.) independently extracted study, patient, and test characteristics, including 2 × 2 contingency tables (TP, FP, FN, TN). Extracted variables included author, year, study design, setting, reference standard, number of participants, patient demographics, and follow-up duration. Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and accuracy were calculated or verified from reconstructed 2 × 2 tables. Risk of bias was assessed using the QUADAS-2 tool18 across four domains (patient selection, index test, reference standard, flow/timing). Disagreements were resolved by consensus or by a third reviewer (M.P.).

Statistical analysis

Separate meta-analyses were conducted for Detection and Monitor. Sensitivity and specificity were pooled using a bivariate random-effects model (Reitsma method, GLMM) on the logit scale, jointly estimating both measures and their correlation19,20. Results were presented as pooled estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), forest plots, and SROC curves with confidence and prediction regions. The between-study variance (τ2) and correlation coefficient (ρ) were reported. Random-effects models were prespecified given the expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was primarily assessed using τ2 and prediction intervals; I2 was reported for univariate models.

PPV and NPV were pooled using univariate random-effects models (DerSimonian–Laird on logit-transformed proportions). Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran’s Q, I2, τ2, and prediction intervals. Leave-one-out (LOO) analyses were performed when significant heterogeneity was detected (p < 0.05)21.

Grade-specific sensitivity was pooled separately for high-grade (HG) and low-grade (LG) tumours in both settings, using univariate random-effects models (DerSimonian–Laird on logit-transformed proportions, metaprop function, R meta package v7.0). For studies with sensitivity of 1.00, a continuity correction of 0.5 was applied. Specificity was not analysed separately by grade because true negatives and false positives are shared across grade categories.

The two re-TURBT studies evaluated different target conditions and were summarised descriptively. The two UTUC studies were methodologically similar, allowing an exploratory pooled analysis.

Evidence certainty was assessed using the GRADE-DTA framework22 across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Publication bias was evaluated using Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test23.

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.2) with the meta, mada, and metafor packages. Two-sided p < 0.05 defined statistical significance.

Evidence synthesis

Study selection and characteristics

The PRISMA flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1. After duplicate removal, 143 records were identified; 29 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 26 studies were included in the quantitative synthesis. Among these, 3 were randomised controlled trials3,6,8, 20 prospective7,9,24–41, and 3 retrospective42–44. By clinical context, 14 studies evaluated Xpert for NMIBC surveillance6,8,9,25,27,29,31,32,34–36,38,40,42, 7 assessed Detection3,7,30,33,37,39,43, and 1 included both28. Two studies assessed re-TURBT24,26 and two UTUC41,44. Among the Monitor cohort, 15 surveillance cohorts were available for sensitivity analysis, and 13 had complete 2 × 2 data for specificity, PPV, and NPV. Because some studies contributed more than one analysable cohort (for example, one study reported separate Detection and Monitor populations), the number of pooled cohorts (k) may exceed the number of studies within a given setting; throughout the manuscript, k denotes the number of analysable cohorts contributing to each pooled estimate. The included studies (2018–2025) enrolled 8613 patients. Baseline characteristics are summarised in Tables 1 and 2, with the corresponding study selection criteria detailed in Supplementary Tables S4–S5. A complete description of the reasons for exclusion after full-text review is provided in Supplementary Appendix S2.

Risk of bias

The QUADAS-2 summary is presented in Supplementary Table S2. Higher risk of bias was most frequently observed in the patient selection and flow/timing domains. The index test was generally well described. Applicability concerns were low for NMIBC studies and moderate for UTUC and re-TURBT analyses.

Xpert BC detection

Across eight cohorts3,7,28,30,33,37,39,43, pooled sensitivity was 0.83 (95% CI 0.73–0.90) and specificity 0.77 (95% CI 0.65–0.86) (Fig. 2a,b). Heterogeneity was substantial for both (p < 0.001); LOO analyses did not identify any single influential study. Pooled PPV was 0.47 (95% CI 0.25–0.70) and NPV 0.96 (95% CI 0.84–0.99) (Fig. 2c,d). The SROC curve (Supplementary Fig. S1) showed a summary operating point at Se ≈ 0.83, Sp ≈ 0.77.

Sensitivity (a), Specificity (b), PPV (c) and NPV (d) Forest plots for detection.

Xpert BC monitor

Across 15 surveillance cohorts6,8,9,25,27–29,31,32,34–36,38,40,42, pooled sensitivity was 0.71 (95% CI 0.63–0.77) (Fig. 3a). Specificity (13 cohorts) was 0.78 (95% CI 0.70–0.85) (Fig. 3b). Heterogeneity was substantial (p = 0.002 for sensitivity; p < 0.001 for specificity); LOO analyses confirmed that no single study was responsible for the observed variability. Pooled PPV was 0.45 (95% CI 0.33–0.59) and NPV 0.91 (95% CI 0.87–0.93) (Fig. 3c,d). The SROC plot (Supplementary Fig. S2) showed a summary operating point at Se ≈ 0.71, Sp ≈ 0.78.

Pooled LG sensitivity was 0.60 (95% CI 0.46–0.73; I2 = 68.0%, τ2 = 0.288, p = 0.003; prediction interval 0.27–0.86). In the Detection setting (392 HG events, 354 LG events), pooled HG sensitivity was 0.89 (95% CI 0.83–0.93; I2 = 21.6%, p = 0.28; prediction interval 0.84–0.93). Pooled LG sensitivity was 0.60 (95% CI 0.53–0.67; I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.49; prediction interval 0.52–0.67) (Supplementary Figs. S5–S6).

Exploratory settings: re-TURBT and UTUC

Two studies evaluated Xpert as a tool to predict the need for re-TURBT24,26 but addressed distinct target conditions, precluding meta-analysis. In the study evaluating residual tumour, sensitivity was 0.86, specificity 0.72, PPV 0.66, and NPV 0.89. In the study assessing clinical need for re-resection, sensitivity was 0.83, specificity 0.53, PPV 0.33, and NPV 0.92.

Two studies41,44 investigated UTUC detection. Pooled sensitivity was 1.00 (95% CI 0.87–1.00) and specificity 0.09 (95% CI 0.00–0.99). The wide confidence intervals reflect sparse data from two small cohorts.

Evidence certainty and publication bias

The GRADE-DTA evidence profile is presented in Supplementary Table S3. Evidence certainty was low for sensitivity and NPV (downgraded for risk of bias and inconsistency) and very low for specificity and PPV (additional downgrading for imprecision and indirectness). Deeks’ funnel plots (Supplementary Fig. S7a,b) showed no significant asymmetry (Detection p = 0.42; Monitor p = 0.31).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis is the most comprehensive synthesis of Xpert BC performance to date, incorporating 26 studies (8613 patients) across four clinical contexts. Compared with the prior meta-analysis by Pham et al., it includes 15 additional studies published since 2020—including three randomised controlled trials (UroFollow, DaBlaCa-15, VERNAL)—conducts separate quantitative syntheses for Detection and Monitor, provides the first grade-specific pooled analysis, applies the GRADE-DTA framework, and assesses publication bias.

Across both primary settings, Xpert demonstrated strong rule-out performance. In Detection, the pooled sensitivity of 0.83 and NPV of 0.96 indicate that a negative result provides substantial reassurance that BC is absent. In Monitor, performance was attenuated (sensitivity 0.71, NPV 0.91), consistent with the lower molecular signal expected from smaller recurrent lesions. This difference likely reflects contrasting population characteristics between the two settings: Detection cohorts typically comprise symptomatic, treatment-naïve patients with a higher pre-test probability and larger, often higher-grade tumours, whereas Monitor cohorts consist of previously treated patients undergoing surveillance, in whom recurrences are frequently smaller, lower-grade, and arise on a background of prior intravesical therapy—all factors that reduce the available molecular signal and lower pooled sensitivity. However, specificity was moderate in both settings (0.77–0.78), resulting in low PPVs (0.45–0.47), suggesting that a notable proportion of positive results may not be confirmed as cancer in cohorts with this case mix. This underscores that Xpert should complement, not replace, cystoscopic verification.

The grade-specific analysis across 13 studies represents a key advance. Pooled HG sensitivity (0.80–0.89) was consistently and substantially higher than LG sensitivity (0.60) across both settings, with an absolute difference of 0.20–0.29. Heterogeneity for HG sensitivity was not statistically significant in either setting (I2 = 10% in Monitor, p = 0.35; I2 = 22% in Detection, p = 0.28), with prediction intervals (0.66–0.89 and 0.84–0.93, respectively) supporting reasonably consistent performance across settings. By contrast, LG sensitivity showed substantial heterogeneity in the Monitor setting (I2 = 68%, p = 0.003), likely reflecting variation in recurrence prevalence, tumour burden, and treatment history across surveillance cohorts; LOO analysis confirmed that no single study drove this variability. This consistent gap between high- and low-grade sensitivity is biologically plausible and aligns with the assay’s molecular basis: the five target genes (ABL1, CRH, IGF2, UPK1B, ANXA10) are more strongly dysregulated in high-grade tumours, which also exhibit greater cellular turnover and urinary shedding, yielding higher transcript levels and a more readily detectable signal. Low-grade tumours, by contrast, are more genomically stable and shed fewer cells, producing a weaker mRNA signal that more often falls below the detection threshold. Clinically, a negative Xpert result provides meaningful reassurance in high-grade surveillance (NPV consistently ≥ 0.95), whereas in low-grade disease, sensitivity of 0.60 is insufficient to safely defer cystoscopy.

Xpert occupies a balanced position among urinary biomarker platforms. Bladder EpiCheck achieves slightly higher specificity (0.84) with comparable sensitivity (0.74)12,45–47. Uromonitor reports higher overall accuracy (sensitivity 0.93, specificity 0.79) but in smaller, more homogeneous cohorts48,49. Cxbladder Monitor achieves excellent sensitivity (0.91) with lower specificity (0.61)50,51. ADXBLADDER shows inferior overall accuracy10,11. Compared with conventional urine cytology, which retains high specificity but limited sensitivity for low-grade disease, molecular and methylation-based assays such as Xpert and Bladder EpiCheck provide improved sensitivity, and their combined use with cytology has been proposed to further enhance diagnostic accuracy in surveillance, including for high-grade lesions and carcinoma in situ52–57. Comparable urinary methylation approaches have also been explored for upper-tract disease58. Direct head-to-head comparisons remain scarce, and biomarker selection should consider local availability, turnaround time, cost, and the specific clinical question.

Recent randomised evidence supports integration of Xpert into surveillance pathways. The DaBlaCa-15 trial demonstrated that in high-grade NMIBC with a negative Xpert result, cystoscopy intervals could be extended with NPV of 0.998. The UroFollow trial showed that marker-guided surveillance in low/intermediate-risk patients is feasible6. These trials provide the first prospective evidence that Xpert-guided strategies are feasible and, in selected high-grade patients, could allow cystoscopy intervals to be extended without compromising oncological outcomes. However, Xpert should not be considered a replacement for cystoscopy; it provides an adjunctive molecular layer to improve surveillance efficiency.

The exploratory analyses in re-TURBT and UTUC illustrate potential and limitations of extending Xpert beyond its established indications. In re-TURBT, two studies with different target conditions precluded formal pooling but suggested encouraging NPV (0.89–0.92). In UTUC, apparent perfect sensitivity was observed but with extremely imprecise specificity driven by sparse events. These settings remain hypothesis-generating and require dedicated prospective evaluation.

The GRADE-DTA assessment rated evidence certainty as low for sensitivity/NPV and very low for specificity/PPV, reflecting risk of bias in patient selection and flow/timing, substantial heterogeneity, and prevalence-dependent variation in predictive values. Deeks’ funnel plots showed no evidence of publication bias, though statistical power was limited with fewer than 15 studies per analysis.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, substantial heterogeneity persisted across most estimates, reflecting differences in design, patient selection, risk composition, and reference standards; in particular, cystoscopy, urine cytology, and histological confirmation are not equivalent comparators, and their variable combination across studies may have influenced the pooled sensitivity and specificity estimates. LOO analyses confirmed this variability was not driven by individual outliers but reflected cumulative population diversity. Second, 2 × 2 tables were reconstructed from aggregate data, potentially introducing minor inaccuracies. Third, grade-specific pooling was limited to sensitivity because specificity cannot be separated by tumour grade, and most studies did not report complete grade-stratified 2 × 2 tables. Fourth, PPV and NPV are prevalence-dependent and varied across cohorts with substantially different baseline disease rates. Fifth, meta-regression was not feasible given the limited number of studies per covariate level. Sixth, cost-effectiveness data were rarely reported. Finally, the exploratory re-TURBT and UTUC analyses are hypothesis-generating only.

Conclusions

Xpert BC is a non-invasive molecular test with high sensitivity and NPV for urothelial carcinoma detection and surveillance, particularly for high-grade disease. Grade-specific pooling across 13 studies demonstrates that HG sensitivity (0.80–0.89) is consistently and substantially higher than LG sensitivity (0.60), supporting grade-adapted interpretation: negative results in high-grade surveillance provide strong rule-out reassurance, while negative results in low-grade disease should not be used to defer cystoscopy. These findings should nonetheless be interpreted in light of the limited certainty of evidence under the GRADE-DTA framework, the moderate specificity, and the substantial heterogeneity across studies. Prospective multicentre trials with standardised grade-specific reporting and cost-effectiveness analyses are needed to define Xpert’s role within guideline-driven, personalised care for urothelial carcinoma.
