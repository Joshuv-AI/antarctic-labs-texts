Targeted antibody-drug conjugates for rhabdomyosarcoma and other FGFR4-expressing cancers
Authors: Meijie Tian, Katrina Jia, Jerry T Wu, Adam T Cheuk, Siteng Fang, Victor T Ojo, Eleanor G Pope, David Milewski, Yong Yean Kim, Hsien-Chao Chou, Avani Kelekar, Soumili Dey, Yunkai Yu, Mohammad Asikur Rahman, R M Naseer Khan, Oluwatobi Arisa, William D Figg, Shyam K Sharan, Zoe Weaver Ohler, Ludmila Szabova, Simone Difilippantonio, Borys Shor, L Nathan Tumey, Jun S Wei, Javed Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCompared with chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy, antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) offer distinct advantages. Here, we report on two FGFR4-targeted ADCs, using the high-affinity monoclonal antibody 3A11, the same binder used in a CAR T cell format that is being evaluated at the NCI (NCT06865664) for patients with relapsed/refractory rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS). These ADCs, conjugated to monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) or an exatecan derivative, are rapidly internalized, causing potent fibroblast growth factor receptor 4 (FGFR4)-dependent cytotoxicity in vitro. In subcutaneous RMS xenograft models, both ADCs demonstrated robust anti-tumor activities, significantly prolonging survival. Notably, exatecan-ADC showed better efficacy, with durable tumor control, in aggressive fusion-negative (FN) RMS559 and fusion-positive (FP) RH4 RMS cell-line-derived xenografts (CDXs) and a patient-derived RMS xenograft (PDX). Furthermore, exatecan-ADC effectively controls tumors in an FGFR4-expressing MDA-MB-453 breast cancer mouse model, eradicating relapsed tumors with retreatment. These findings highlight FGFR4-targeted ADCs as potent therapeutic agents against aggressive FGFR4-expressing malignancies, supporting their further clinical development.Keywords: FGFR4-targeted ADC, exatecan, MMAE, rhabdomyosarcoma, breast cancer, FGFR4-expressing malignancies, antibody-drug conjugate, legumain-cleavable linker, pediatric cancer

Introduction

Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is the most common pediatric sarcoma, accounting for 3%–4% of childhood and adolescent cancers.1 The current standard of care consists of surgery, chemotherapy, and/or radiation therapy, achieving a relapse-free survival rate of over 70%, but with significant toxicity.2 Additionally, the prognosis for patients with relapsed or metastatic disease remains poor, with survival rates of only 20%–30%.2 Despite increased clinical research, treatment strategies for RMS have remained unchanged over the past three decades.3,4 Therefore, there is an urgent need for effective therapies to improve outcomes for high-risk patients and reduce treatment-related toxicity.

Fibroblast growth factor receptor 4 (FGFR4) has emerged as a promising immunotherapeutic target due to its high expression in RMS and minimal presence in normal tissues.5 In fusion-positive (FP) RMS, FGFR4 is a direct transcriptional target of the PAX3-FOXO1 fusion oncogene, leading to its elevated expression.6,7 In fusion-negative (FN) RMS, FGFR4 is constitutively activated by mutations in approximately 10%–15% of cases.1,8,9 These findings provided a rationale for the development of FGFR4-targeted therapeutics. We previously developed a high-affinity FGFR4-specific monoclonal antibody (mAb), 3A11. Using the 3A11 single-chain variable fragment (scFv) in a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) format, FGFR4-targeted CAR T cells exhibited potent anti-RMS activity,5 and a phase I dose-escalation clinical trial using FGFR4-targeted CAR T cells to treat patients with recurrent or refractory RMS has started at NCI (NCT06865664). In addition to RMS, other malignancies express high FGFR4, including pediatric cancers such as hepatoblastoma (HBL), yolk sac tumors (YSTs), and desmoplastic small round cell tumors (DSRCTs), as well as adult cancers like hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), and a subset of breast cancers5

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) represent a promising alternative immune therapy, leveraging the specificity of mAbs to deliver potent cytotoxic agents directly to tumor cells.10 ADCs have demonstrated success in both hematologic and solid malignancies, with 14 of them approved by the FDA in the US.11 Compared with CAR T cell therapy, ADCs offer advantages such as immediate availability, broader patient applicability, fewer immune-related side effects, simpler manufacturing, and the ability to give multiple doses.12 Therefore, developing FGFR4-targeted ADCs using the 3A11 antibody is an attractive approach for immune therapy against RMS and other FGFR4-expressing malignancies.

RMS is considered a chemo-sensitive tumor to both microtubule and topoisomerase I (Topo I) inhibitors, which are used as frontline and relapse therapies.2 Among FDA-approved ADCs, the most common platforms (5 of 14) utilize a valine-citrulline-p-amino-benzylcarbamate (VC-PABC) linker to conjugate with a microtubule inhibitor, monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE).11 Exatecan, a potent Topo I inhibitor, is used in an FDA-approved HER2-targeting ADC, trastuzumab deruxtecan (Enhertu).13 In this study, we developed two 3A11 ADCs by using two different linkers and payloads, one with a protease-cleavable VC-PABC linker conjugated to MMAE (α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE), and the other with a legumain-cleavable mpGlyAsnAsn (βAla) linker14 conjugated to exatecan (α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan). We evaluated the efficacy of these FGFR4 ADCs in several RMS models in vitro and in vivo and found that it correlated with FGFR4 expression and 3A11-mediated internalization. While both ADCs exhibited comparable in vitro efficacy, the exatecan-based ADC demonstrated better anti-tumor activity in multiple aggressive FN RMS mouse models, including an RMS559 cell line-derived xenograft (CDX), FP RMS RH4 CDX, and an FP-RMS patient-derived-xenograft (PDX) model. Furthermore, when evaluated in FGFR4-expressing breast and liver cancer models, both ADCs demonstrated efficaciousness by exhibiting effective tumor control. Notably, the exatecan-based ADC provided additional prolonged tumor suppression in breast cancer models expressing high FGFR4, supporting its potential clinical utility in other malignancies with high FGFR4 expression.

Results

Internalization and trafficking to lysosomes of 3A11 mAb by FGFR4-positive cells

The effectiveness of ADC-based therapy relies on the internalization of receptor-ADC complexes by target cells, which enables the intracellular release of cytotoxic payloads.15 We first evaluated the internalization of 3A11, a mAb against human FGFR4,5 using a lysosomal pH imaging assay. Antibody 3A11 was labeled with a pH-sensitive dye, FabFluor-pH, which only fluoresces in a low pH environment, allowing visualization of endolysosome formation. Significant internalization of 3A11 was observed in RMS cell lines with high FGFR4 expression (RMS559, JR, and RH30, Table S1; Figure 1A) compared with the isotype IgG control. In contrast, lower levels of 3A11 internalization were detected in cell lines with medium FGFR4 expression (SCMC), and no internalization was observed in FGFR4-knockout (KO) cells (RH30-FGFR4 KO or RH4-FGFR4 KO) or human fibroblast 7250 cells without FGFR4 expression, confirming the FGFR4-dependent specificity of 3A11 internalization (Figures 1A and 1B). Notably, a significant correlation between FGFR4 expression levels and 3A11 internalization activity was observed in RMS cells (Figure 1C, p = 0.0002; Table S1), suggesting that the specific binding of 3A11 to cell-surface FGFR4 leads to internalization of the antibody.

Cytotoxic activity of FGFR4-targeted ADCs against RMS cells

Next, we developed FGFR4-targeted ADCs using microtubule and Topo I inhibitors, both of which are standard-of-care agents for RMS, including in the relapsed setting.2 For the microtubule inhibitor-based ADC, we conjugated the FGFR4-specific mAb 3A11 to MMAE via a commonly used protease-cleavable VC-PABC linker, generating α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE (Figure 2A). For the Topo I inhibitor-based ADC, we utilized a legumain-cleavable mpGlyAsnAsn (βAla) linker, a target of lysosomal asparaginyl endopeptidase,14,16 to conjugate 3A11 with a potent Topo I inhibitor, exatecan, resulting in α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan (Figure 2A). Consistent with clinically approved ADC technology, the α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE ADC was designed with an average drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR) of 4, while α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan was designed with an average DAR of 8 (Figure 2A). The selection of the DAR was based on studies showing that high DAR MMAE ADCs (DAR > 4) exhibit rapid clearance and significant toxicity17 while the reduced potency and hydrophobicity of exatecan-based ADCs allow the use of higher DARs (∼8).16,18 ADCs were prepared by stochastic conjugation to endogenous cysteine residues. To generate isotype-matched ADC controls, we conjugated an irrelevant mAb targeting hen egg lysozyme (HEL) to MMAE or exatecan by using the same linkers and DARs. All ADCs were fully characterized by size-exclusion chromatography and mass spectroscopy to ensure loading, homogeneity, and minimal aggregation.

We compared the two ADCs across several human RMS cell lines with varying FGFR4 expression levels (Table S1). Both ADCs exhibited the most significant cytotoxic activity in cell lines with high FGFR4 expression, RMS559 and JR (Figure 2B; Table S1). However, α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan demonstrated significantly higher cytotoxicity than α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE in RH30, JR, and SCMC cell lines (Figure 2B; Table S1). In contrast, the isotype control ADCs showed no cytotoxicity in any FGFR4-expressing RMS cell lines, nor did FGFR4-targeted ADCs have any effect on FGFR4-KO cell lines (RH30 KO and RH4 KO) or the human fibroblast 7250 cell line lacking FGFR4 expression, confirming the target specificity of FGFR4-directed ADCs (Figure 2B; Table S1). Of note, the antibody 3A11 alone had no effect either on cancer growth or on FGFR4 signaling (Figure S1). Furthermore, the potency of ADCs strongly correlated with the expression levels of FGFR4 on cell surface and 3A11 internalizing efficiency. Interestingly, the potency did not depend on their intrinsic drug sensitivity, as these RMS cells were all sensitive to free MMAE (IC50: 0.05–0.54 nM) and exatecan (IC50: 0.19–0.99 nM) (Figures 2C and 2D; Figure S2; Table S1). Thus, these data demonstrate that the observed cytotoxic activity of FGFR4-targeted ADCs is dependent on the specific FGFR4 expression.

Mechanism of cytotoxicity mediated by the FGFR4-targeted ADCs

To investigate the mechanism of cytotoxicity triggered by α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE, we examined PARP cleavage, a hallmark of apoptosis mediated by caspase-3/7. We treated RMS559, RH4, and RH4-FGFR4 KO cell lines with α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE, α-HEL-vc-MMAE, MMAE alone, or unconjugated 3A11 antibody. Western blot analysis revealed increased PARP cleavage in RMS559 and RH4 cells treated with α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE, whereas control ADC (α-HEL-vc-MMAE) and unconjugated 3A11 mAb did not result in PARP cleavage. Furthermore, α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE did not induce PARP cleavage in RH4-FGFR4 KO cells, confirming FGFR4 dependency and specificity of the ADC (Figure 3A). Furthermore, we observed significantly increased caspase-3/7 activity, using a Caspase-Glo assay in RMS559 and RH4 cells treated with α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE. In contrast, no caspase activity was observed in RH4-FGFR4 KO cells (Figure 3B).

For α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan ADC, western blot analysis revealed increased levels of phosphorylated Chk1 (pChk1), phosphorylated H2A.X (pH2A.X/γH2A.X), and cleaved PARP in RMS559 and RH4 cells treated with α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan for 24–72 h, consistent with the DNA damage effects of free exatecan (Figure 3C).19,20 However, these markers were not induced either in the RH4-FGFR4 KO cells or in cells treated with the 3A11 antibody, α-HEL-NNβA-exatecan control, or vehicle control, indicating that DNA damage resulting from exatecan ADC is mediated by FGFR4. These findings were further corroborated by flow cytometry, which showed increased γH2A.X and activated caspase-3 levels in FGFR4-expressing cells treated with α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan, confirming that exatecan released from the ADC exerts Topo I-dependent DNA damage and apoptosis (Figure 3D).

Bystander effects of ADCs amplify the cytotoxic effects on cancer cells lacking the target antigen when the payload is released from nearby apoptotic-targeted cancer cells.21 To assess the bystander killing effects of both ADCs, we co-cultured equal numbers of GFP-expressing RH4 cells with mCherry-expressing RH4-FGFR4 KO cells for 5 days (Figures 3E and 3F), followed by quantification of GFP+ and mCherry+ cells using flow cytometry (Figure 3E). At 11 nM (1.67 μg/mL), α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE exhibited clear bystander killing that suppressed the growth of FGFR4 KO cells, whereas neither α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan at 22 nM (3.33 μg/mL) nor control ADCs showed any significant growth suppression of FGFR4 KO cells (Figure 3F), demonstrating the dependence of FGFR4 expression for the bystander activity of α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE ADC.

Pharmacokinetics profiling of FGFR4-targeted ADCs

To evaluate the stability and systemic behavior of these FGFR4-targeted ADCs, we characterized their pharmacokinetic (PK) properties in NSG mice by measuring serum levels of total IgG, conjugated drug, and free drug after a single intravenous (IV) dose (Figures 4A–4C; Tables S2 and S3). Free MMAE was detected at low concentrations, with a maximum serum level of 4.2 ± 1.51 ng/mL at 24 h post-injection (Figure 4A), consistent with previously reported values for MMAE-based ADCs.22 In contrast, free βAla-exatecan levels were below the detection limit of 1 ng/mL, indicating minimal systemic release of the exatecan payload. In addition, PK analysis revealed a longer half-life (T1/2) and lower clearance (CL) for exatecan-based ADC compared to the MMAE-based ADC, suggesting its prolonged systemic retention (Figure 4D). Conversely, the MMAE-based ADC demonstrated a higher volume of distribution (Vd) and steady-state volume of distribution (Vss), indicative of broader extravascular tissue distribution and more rapid clearance (Figure 4D). Notably, systemic exposure, as measured by area under the concentration-time curve (AUC), was significantly greater for the exatecan-based ADC, reflecting more sustained systemic drug availability in circulation (Figure 4D).

FGFR4-targeted ADCs demonstrate high potency against FN RMS cell line models

To determine the therapeutic potential of FGFR4-targeted ADCs, we evaluated their efficacy in a CDX, RMS559, an aggressive FN RMS with an FGFR4 V550L activation mutation23 and high FGFR4 expression (7,175 molecules/cell in CDX).5,24 A dose-optimization study was first conducted to determine both efficacy and tolerability of α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE (0.3, 1, or 3 mg/kg, administered twice weekly for two weeks; Figure S3A) and α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan (a single dose of 3 or 10 mg/kg; Figure S4A). A single treatment cycle of α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE ADC suppressed tumor growth and improved survival in a dose-dependent manner, with the highest dose (3 mg/kg) resulting in a significant tumor regression (Figures S3B–S3E). No significant weight loss or toxicity was observed, indicating good tolerability (Figures S3F–S3H). The control ADC, α-HEL-vc-MMAE, did not inhibit tumor growth, confirming that the cytotoxicity of α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE depends on ADC binding to FGFR4 (Figures S3B–S3H). In a confirmatory study using the same model (n = 10 per treatment group, Figure 5A), a single cycle of 3 mg/kg α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE (twice weekly for two weeks) delayed tumor growth in 6 mice and completely eradicated tumors in 4 mice (Figure 5B), corresponding to a 70% survival rate (Figure 5D).

For α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan ADC in a pilot study, a single 3 mg/kg dose was not effective; however, a single dose of 10 mg/kg led to transient tumor shrinkage, followed by tumor regrowth within two weeks (Figures S4B–S4D). Due to the rapid growth of RMS559 xenografts and low toxicity observed for this ADC, we gave two doses of 10 mg/kg α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan in a subsequent confirmatory study (Figure 5A). Remarkably, this treatment resulted in a complete tumor eradication and a 100% survival rate without apparent toxicity such as weight loss (Figures 5C and 5E).

These results demonstrated that although both ADCs displayed comparable in vitro efficacy against RMS559 cells, exatecan-based ADCs exhibited better therapeutic efficacy in RMS559 CDX models that MMAE-based ADCs at the tested doses.

When evaluating the molecular effects of ADCs on tumors using immunohistochemistry (IHC), both ADCs induced apoptosis, as evidenced by significantly increased cleaved caspase-3 staining compared with isotype control ADCs or vehicle controls (Figures 5F–5I). However, neither treatment significantly reduced tumor cell proliferation, as indicated by unchanged Ki67+ cell percentages (Figures 5F–5I). Notably, only α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan treatment resulted in a significant reduction in FGFR4+ tumor cells, as reflected by the decreased FGFR4 H-score (Figures 5G and 5I). These findings suggest that consistent with the mechanisms of action of their respective payloads, both FGFR4-targeted ADCs inhibit tumor growth primarily by inducing apoptosis, rather than halting proliferation, with the exatecan-based ADC demonstrating better efficacy.

FGFR4-targeted ADCs are efficacious in FP RMS CDX and PDX models

Anti-tumor activity of both ADCs was further evaluated in FP RH4 xenografts, which have moderate FGFR4 expression (∼3,637 molecules per cell, Figure 6A). A single cycle of α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE (3 mg/kg, twice weekly for two weeks) delayed tumor growth (Figure 6B), improving survival by 30% (Figure 6D). In contrast, a single 10 mg/kg dose of α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan provided much better tumor control (Figure 6C), achieving 100% survival (Figure 6E). These results further demonstrated a superior potency of exatecan-based ADCs relative to MMAE-based ADCs, likely related to the higher DAR, even in tumors with moderate FGFR4 expression.

We next evaluated these ADCs in an FP RMS PDX model, SJRHB013759 (∼1,337 molecules per cell, Figure 6F). A single treatment cycle of α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE (3 mg/kg, twice weekly for two weeks) resulted in complete tumor regression in 4 out of 5 mice, with one mouse experiencing tumor relapse approximately 60 days after the initial treatment (Figure 6G), achieving an 80% survival rate (Figure 6I). In contrast, all mice in the control groups reached tumor size endpoint within 60 days of the initiation of treatments. Furthermore, a single dose (10 mg/kg) of the exatecan-based ADC resulted in complete tumor regression (Figure S5). Given the non-specific effect of the control ADC at this high dose, we tested a lower-dose regimen (3 mg/kg for two doses). This approach effectively controlled tumor growth for 45 days until relapses occurred in 4 out of 8 mice (Figure 6H). Notably, a second cycle of 3 mg/kg treatment successfully regressed large tumors (1,500–2,000 mm3) (Figure 6H), ultimately achieving complete tumor control with a 100% survival rate (Figure 6J). These data demonstrate that both FGFR4-ADCs exhibit robust anti-tumor activity in FP RMS.

Efficacy of FGFR4-targeted ADCs in other FGFR4-expressing cancer models

We previously reported FGFR4 mRNA expression in various pediatric and adult tumors, including HBL, liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), CHOL, and certain breast invasive carcinomas (BRCAs).5 High FGFR4 expression (TPM ≥ 90) can be detected in approximately 75% of RMS, 80% of HBL, 28% of DSRCTs, and 100% of YSTs (Figure 7A). In adult tumors, 63% of CHOLs, 43% of LIHCs, 34% of adrenocortical carcinomas (ACCs), 30% of rectal adenocarcinomas (READs), 29% of colon adenocarcinomas (COADs), 25% of KIRPs, 15% of stomach adenocarcinomas (STADs), and 3% of BRCA exhibited high FGFR4 expression (Figure 7A). To evaluate FGFR4 expression, we performed IHC on tissue microarrays (TMAs) containing breast cancer (n = 76) and HCC (n = 16) samples. Among the breast cancer samples, 3% exhibited moderate FGFR4 staining, while 13% showed minimal signal. In the HCC samples, 13% displayed moderate FGFR4 staining, and another 13% showed minimal staining (Figure 7B). Flow cytometry analysis of a panel of breast cancer (MDA-MB-453, CAMA-1, BT-474, SKBR3, and MDA-MB-134 IV) and liver cancer (Huh7, Hep3B, and HepG2) cell lines revealed high FGFR4 expression in MDA-MB-453, CAMA-1, Hep3B, Huh7, and HepG2 and moderate expression in BT-474 (Figure 7C), suggesting the potential of FGFR4-targeted therapy against these adult malignancies.

We next evaluated the efficacy of FGFR4-targeted ADCs in FGFR4-expressing breast and liver cancer models. The MMAE-based ADC (α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE) effectively inhibited the growth of MDA-MB-453, CAMA-1, and BT-474 cells (Figure 7D; Table S1). While exhibiting minimal activity against BT-474, the α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan ADC showed comparable potency as the α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE for MDA-MB-453 cells. However, the former was significantly more effective against Huh7 cells (Figure 7D; Table S1), and both ADCs had limited efficacy against Hep3B and HepG2 (Figure S6A).

To further evaluate their efficacy in vivo, we tested both ADCs in an ER-negative, PR-negative, and HER2-positive breast cancer xenograft mouse model, MDA-MB-453 (Figure 7E). A single treatment cycle of α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE (3 mg/kg) or α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan (10 mg/kg) resulted in complete tumor control, lasting 59 and 80 days, respectively (Figure 7F). Tumor relapse was observed by day 98, with α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE-treated tumors regrowing to 200–650 mm3 and α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan-treated tumors to 100–160 mm3. To address relapse, a second treatment cycle of α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE (3 mg/kg, four doses over two weeks) initially suppressed tumor regrowth but failed to achieve long-term control, with tumors recurring after day 116. In contrast, a second 10 mg/kg dose of α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan completely eliminated relapsed tumors, achieving 100% survival (Figure 7G). These results suggest that although both FGFR4-targeted ADCs could control tumor growth, α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan demonstrated better therapeutic durability in this breast cancer model.

Discussion

Despite decades of research, therapeutic advancements for high-risk and recurrent RMS remain limited, underscoring the urgent need for effective treatment strategies. We previously validated FGFR4 as a promising therapeutic target for RMS, identified high-affinity FGFR4-specific binders, and developed FGFR4-targeting CAR T cell therapy.5 Preclinical studies have demonstrated the potency of clinical-grade FGFR4-directed CAR T cells in metastatic and orthotopic RMS models,5 and a phase 1 clinical trial has opened at the NCI (NCT06865664) for patients with relapsed/refractory RMS. However, CAR T cell therapy presents several challenges, including personalized manufacturing, high costs, long production timelines, and immune-related toxicities.12 In contrast, ADC therapy offers off-the-shelf availability, broader patient applicability, fewer immune-related side effects, and simpler manufacturing, making it a viable alternative or a complementary immunotherapeutic strategy. Here, we developed two effective FGFR4-targeting ADCs that exhibited FGFR4-dependent cytotoxicity, leading to tumor regression in multiple animal models, including aggressive RMS559, FGFR4-low RH4 CDXs, and an FP RMS PDX, establishing ADCs as a promising therapeutic approach for different RMS subtypes, particularly against those resistant to CAR T cell therapy. Additionally, ADCs offer a controlled dosing regimen allowing the discontinuation of treatment in the event of toxicity, thereby improving safety to patients. This work underscores the potential of FGFR4-directed ADCs as an effective, scalable treatment option for patients with relapsed/refractory RMS, warranting further clinical evaluation.

As expected, a strong correlation was observed between the internalization efficiency of the FGFR4-specific antibody 3A11 and FGFR4 expression levels in RMS, predicting minimal ADC uptake by normal tissues, which typically exhibit low-to-no FGFR4 expression. Additionally, the potency of FGFR4-targeted ADCs was primarily associated with the FGFR4 expression and efficiency of antibody internalization, rather than the dependency on FGFR4 signaling or payloads (MMAE or exatecan) for RMS, likely due to the high efficacy of these payloads in RMS treatment. In contrast, cytotoxicity of both ADCs on a panel of breast and liver cancer cell lines with high FGFR4 expression used in this study was highly variable. This may be the result of the intrinsic cell sensitivities toward the payload drugs, as liver cancer cells are relatively resistant to the free MMAE (IC50: 0.17–0.37 nM, Table S1) compared with RMS cell lines (IC50: 0.05–0.54 nM, Table S1); MDA-MB-453 is more sensitive to the free exatecan than CAMA-1, BT-474, Huh7, Hep3B, and HepG2 (Figure S6B; Table S1). These findings suggest that, beyond FGFR4 surface expression as a predictive biomarker, sensitivity to the payload drugs can also contribute to predicting ADC efficacy in treating FGFR4-expressing cancers.

Although both FGFR4-targeted ADCs exhibited comparable in vitro efficacy in RMS559 and RH4 cell lines, in vivo studies have demonstrated that the exatecan-based ADC provided superior and more durable tumor control despite differences in dosing, leading to improved survival compared with the MMAE-based ADC. In alignment with previous reports that Topo I-based ADCs exhibit more potent tumor inhibition than their microtubule inhibitor-based counterparts,25 these observed differences in efficacy may also be attributed to the differences in linker chemistry, payload stability, and a higher DAR for α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan (DAR = 8) compared with that for α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE (DAR = 4), as well as the drug release efficiency between the two ADCs. The MMAE-based ADC utilized a hydrophobic ValCitPABC linker, which undergoes rapid lysosomal cleavage26 but is also susceptible to premature cleavage by neutrophil elastase and related plasma proteases, potentially contributing to dose-limiting toxicities such as thrombocytopenia and neuropathy.27,28,29,30 In contrast, our exatecan-based ADC incorporated a hydrophilic legumain-cleavable mpGlyAsnAsn (βAla) linker, which is resistant to random hydrolysis and degradation by neutrophil elastase and carboxylesterase,31 thereby enhancing plasma stability and reducing off-target toxicity while facilitating more efficient lysosomal cleavage and improving drug release efficiency.14 It is also important to note that a recent study by our team demonstrated that lysosomal cleavage of the AsnAsn linker is significantly slower than that of the ValCit linker and the βAla-exatecan payload has significantly reduced permeability compared with MMAE, thus facilitating increased intracellular payload retention upon ADC catabolism.16 Moreover, the exatecan-based ADC exhibited a longer half-life, lower clearance, and higher systemic exposure (AUC) in mice, suggesting a prolonged exposure of tumors to the drug payload. In contrast, the MMAE-based ADC displayed higher clearance and a larger Vd, indicating greater tissue distribution (e.g., fat or muscle) with a higher potential for toxicity and faster systemic elimination. Additionally, a higher DAR (8) may have contributed to the efficacy of exatecan ADC. Despite a higher DAR of 8 typically associated with increased clearance,32 the exatecan-based ADC exhibited lower clearance than the MMAE-based ADC (DAR = 4). Therefore, the increased stability and reduced clearance of the exatecan-based ADC may facilitate the accumulation of the drug payload more selectively in FGFR4-expressing cells, making it more specific and potent than the MMAE-based ADC.

Another key distinction between these ADCs was their bystander killing activity. While the MMAE-based ADC exhibited strong bystander killing, which can enhance efficacy in tumors with heterogeneous antigen expression, it also increases the risk of off-target toxicity.21 In contrast, the β-Ala-exatecan-based ADC did not possess any bystander killing activity, offering a potentially safer therapeutic index. The low bystander activity is consistent with our recent findings on analogous TROP2-targeting ADCs employing the same linker-payload and is likely the result of increased stability of the ADC and reduced permeability of the released payload.16 Interestingly, the reduced permeability may allow increased intracellular payload retention, thereby explaining the increased potency of the exatecan-based ADC in various cell lines (Figure 2B). Given these positive findings, further optimization of the linker-payload systems along with future PK and toxicity studies is warranted to expand the therapeutic spectrum, overcome resistance, enhance safety, and establish appropriate human dosing. Additionally, dual-payload strategies33 may further enhance the therapeutic potential and survival benefits of FGFR4-targeted ADCs, reinforcing their promise as a treatment option for FGFR4-expressing cancers.

Breast cancer, the most diagnosed malignancy in women worldwide, accounted for 2.3 million new cases and approximately 670,000 deaths in 2022.34 In this study, we estimate that 3% (equivalent to 69,000 new cases annually worldwide) of breast cancers express moderate to high FGFR4. FGFR4 overexpression has been reported to contribute to tumor progression and resistance to multiple breast cancer therapies including chemotherapy, hormone, and HER2-targeted treatments.8,35,36,37,38 As proof of principle, we demonstrated that FGFR4-targeted ADCs effectively induced tumor regression in a breast cancer model. Thus, FGFR4-targeted therapy presents a promising alternative treatment option for aggressive subtypes that express FGFR4 beyond RMS.

In summary, we report here the therapeutic potential of FGFR4-targeted ADCs for treating aggressive or therapy-resistant RMS, breast cancers, and other malignancies associated with FGFR4 expression. Our findings strongly support the need for comprehensive evaluation of these ADCs, including safety, pharmacotoxicology, and tolerability studies in non-human primates, followed by clinical trials in FGFR4-expressing solid tumors.

Limitations of the study

We acknowledge that toxicity assessment in this study was limited to body weight and clinical observation, without systematic histopathologic evaluation. However, our previous studies showed no detectable FGFR4 expression in normal human liver or other vital organs by IHC.5 Moreover, the same 3A11 binder has supported FDA IND clearance for a first-in-human FGFR4-targeted CAR T cell trial (IND #28682; NCT06865664), based on negative IHC across normal human tissues, and lack of activity against a broad panel of primary human cells. These data support a low likelihood of systemic toxicity, consistent with the stable body weight and normal behavior observed in the treated mice. Nonetheless, comprehensive organ-level toxicology studies are important in future work. This is not feasible in the current mouse models because 3A11 does not cross-react with murine FGFR4, preventing assessment of on-target, off-tumor toxicity. Such studies are, therefore, planned in non-human primates, in which 3A11 is cross-reactive.

Method details

Antibody production

The anti-FGFR4 antibody was engineered by fusing the sequence encoding Fab region of monoclonal antibody (mAb) 3A11 to the human immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) Fc domain. The chimeric human 3A11 antibody was expressed in a CHO (Chinese Hamster Ovary) cell system and purified by Biointron Biological Inc. A human IgG1-Kappa isotype control, V6 α-Hen Egg Lysozyme (HEL), was purchased from Abinvivo (Cat #B117901; Biointron Biological Inc., Metuchen, NJ). Antibody purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE and size-exclusion high-performance liquid chromatography (SEC-HPLC). Only antibodies with less than 5% aggregate content were used for antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) production.

ADC production and purification

The chimeric human 3A11 antibody, prepared in 5 mM EDTA/PBS, was reduced using either 3 or 12 molar equivalents of Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP) at 37°C for 1.5 h to cleave interchain disulfide bonds. Following reduction, 8 or 25 molar equivalents of the drug-linker ValCitPABC-MMAE or mpGlyAsnAsn(β-Ala)-exatecan, respectively, were separately added to the antibody. The mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 3 h to complete conjugation. The reaction products were buffer-exchanged into DPBS using 2.5 mL PD-10 desalting columns packed with Sephadex G-25 resin (Cytiva) and concentrated as required using 30 kDa molecular weight cutoff spin columns. The final ADC products were sterile filtered through 0.2 μm filters. The drug-antibody ratio (DAR) was determined using HPLC-MS, yielding a DAR of approximately 4.0 for α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE and 8.0 for α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan. Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) analysis confirmed minimal aggregation.

Isotype control IgG-ADCs were synthesized following the same protocol as α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE and α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan, achieving comparable DAR values.

Quantitation of FGFR4 molecules on cell lines

FGFR4 expression levels on tumor cells were quantified as previously described.24 Briefly, single-cell suspensions of tumor cell lines were stained with 1 μg/ml of anti-human FGFR4 antibody (clone 3A11) for 30 min at 4°C. After two washes, cells were incubated with R-Phycoerythrin (R-PE) AffiniPure F(ab’)2 Fragment Goat Anti-Human IgG, Fcγ Fragment-specific antibody (1:200 dilution, Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs Cat# 109-116-170, RRID: AB_2337681) in FACS buffer. Following additional washes, the FGFR4 signal was quantified by flow cytometry. To determine the number of FGFR4 molecules per cell, fluorescence intensity was calibrated using the Quantibrite PE Quantitation Kit (BD Biosciences Cat# 340495, RRID: AB_2868736) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Background signal was corrected by subtracting the fluorescence intensity of the corresponding isotype control antibody (Clone QA16A12 for human IgG1, RRID: AB_2927629, BioLegend) to ensure accurate quantification.

Anti-FGFR4 antibody internalization assays

Cells were seeded into clear 96-well plates at appropriate densities and incubated overnight at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. The chimeric anti-FGFR4 antibody (3A11) and human IgG1 isotype control antibodies were labeled using the Incucyte FabFluor-pH Antibody Labeling Reagent (Cat# 4722, RRID: AB_3714617, Sartorius) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The labeled antibodies were then diluted to the desired concentrations and added to the cells. Internalization was quantified every 30 min over 24 h in an Incucyte S3 live-cell imaging system (RRID:SCR_023147, Sartorius). Fluorescence from internalized antibodies and cell confluence was quantified using Incucyte Software (2024 version). Fluorescence signal normalized by cell confluence was analyzed and visualized in the GraphPad Prism (RRID:SCR_002798, version 10.1.1).

Cell viability assay

Tumor cells were seeded into white 96-well plates and incubated overnight at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Cells were then treated with serial dilutions of test agents, including α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE ADC, α-HEL-vc-MMAE control ADC, α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan ADC, α-HEL-NNβA-exatecan control ADC, free MMAE, and free exatecan (ADC concentrations were based on the molarity of antibody, while free drug concentrations as drug molarity). After 120 h of incubation, cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo 2.0 Cell Viability Assay (Cat# G9242, Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Luminescence was measured on a Spark Multimode Microplate Reader (Tecan), and signals were normalized to the respective vehicle controls (PBS or DMSO).

IC50 and Area Under the Curve (AUC) calculations were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.1). The potency of ADCs was determined by calculating 1 minus the fraction of AUC for the FGFR4-targeted ADCs relative to the AUC of the isotype control ADCs (Table S1).

Western blots to assess the action mechanism of FGFR4-targeted ADCs

RMS559, RH4, and RH4 FGFR4 KO cells were individually treated with 1 μg/mL 3A11 mAb, α-HEL-vc-MMAE, α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE, and MMAE for 24 h; or 1 μg/mL 3A11 mAb, α-HEL-NNβA-exatecan, α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan, and exatecan for 24, 48, or 72 h. After treatment, cells were harvested and lysed using RIPA buffer (Cat# 9806, Cell Signaling Technology) supplemented with Halt Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Cat# 78442, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Total protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce Bradford Protein Assay Kit (Cat# 23200, Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Equal amounts of protein were loaded onto Bis-Tris gels, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked using standard blocking buffer and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies specific to the targets of interest. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Chemiluminescent signals were developed and visualized using the ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

Primary antibodies included anti-PARP (1:2000, Cat# 9542, RRID: AB_2160739), anti-phospho-Chk1 (Ser345) (1:1000, clone 133D3, Cat# 2348, RRID: AB_331212), and anti-phospho-Histone H2A.X (Ser139) (1:500, clone 20E3, Cat# 9718S, RRID: AB_2118009), all from Cell Signaling Technology. Loading control antibodies included anti-β-actin (1:1000, clone 8H10D10, Cat# 3700, RRID: AB_2242334), anti-Chk1 (1:1000, clone 2G1D5, Cat# 2360, RRID: AB_2080320), anti-Histone H2A.X (1:1000, Cat# 2595S, RRID: AB_10694556), and anti-Histone H3 (1:1000, Cat# 9715, RRID: AB_331563). Secondary antibodies used were anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (1:5000, Cat# 7074, RRID: AB_2099233) and anti-mouse IgG-HRP (1:5000, Cat# 7076, RRID: AB_330924), also from Cell Signaling Technology.

Flow cytometry analysis of γ-H2A.X and active caspase-3 expression on tumor cells

RMS559, RH4, and RH4 FGFR4 KO cells were treated with 3 μg/mL 3A11 mAb, α-HEL-NNβA-exatecan, α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan, and exatecan for 48 h at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. After treatment, cells were harvested and prepared as single-cell suspensions. Cells were fixed using BD Cytofix Fixation Buffer (Cat# 554655, RRID: AB_2869005, BD Biosciences) and permeabilized with BD Perm/Wash Buffer (Cat# 554723, RRID: AB_2869011, BD Biosciences) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were subsequently stained in BD Perm/Wash Buffer with Alexa Fluor 647 Mouse anti-H2AX (pS139) (Cat# 560447, RRID: AB_1645414, BD Pharmingen) and PE Rabbit Anti-Active Caspase-3 antibody (Cat# 570184, RRID: AB_3685572, BD Pharmingen). Samples were analyzed using either the LSR Fortessa (RRID:SCR_018655) or FACSymphony A5 Cell Analyzer System (RRID:SCR_022538, BD Biosciences). Data acquisition and analysis were conducted with FlowJo software to quantify γ-H2A.X and active caspase-3 expression.

FGFR4-immunohistochemistry (IHC) on TMAs

Breast cancer tissue microarrays (TMA, BR1506) and liver cancer TMA (LV487b) were obtained from Tissuearray.Com (Derwood, MD). FGFR4 IHC was performed using a Leica BOND Rx autostainer (RRID:SCR_025548, Leica Biosystems) with the Bond Polymer Refine Detection Kit (DS 9800, RRID: AB_2891238, Leica Biosystems). Briefly, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) TMA sections underwent antigen retrieval in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) at 95°C for 20 min. Sections were then incubated with a rabbit monoclonal anti-FGFR4 antibody (Clone D3B12; Cat# 8562, RRID: AB_10891199, Cell Signaling Technology, Denver, CA) at a 1:50 dilution for 1 h at room temperature. Detection of bound primary antibodies was achieved using Alkaline Phosphatase AffiniPure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (Cat# 111-055-003, RRID: AB_2337947, Jackson ImmunoResearch) diluted at 1:500. Slides were scanned at 20× magnification using the PhenoImager whole-slide scanner (Akoya Biosciences).

IHC staining results were reviewed and scored by a board-certified pathologist. Staining intensity thresholds were established to classify cells as negative, 1+, 2+, or 3+. H-scores were calculated using the formula: H-score = [1∗(%1+) + 2∗(%2+) + 3∗(%3+)].

Caspase3, Ki67, and FGFR4 immunohistochemistry (IHC)

For IHC study of α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE, NSG mice bearing RMS559 or RH4 tumors (∼250 mm3) received two doses of 3 mg/kg α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE or α-HEL-vc-MMAE and vehicle control over one week. Tumors were harvested 24 h after the final treatment. For the study of α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan, NSG mice bearing RMS559 tumors (∼250 mm3) received a single dose of 10 mg/kg α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan or α-HEL-NNβA-exatecan. Tumors were harvested 72 h post-treatment.

All harvested tumors were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin and processed into paraffin-embedded (FFPE) blocks. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed using the following primary antibodies: anti-human Ki67 (Cat# 12202, RRID:AB_2620142, 1:200 dilution, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-human cleaved caspase-3 (Cat# 9661, RRID:AB_2341188, 1:800 dilution, Cell Signaling Technology), or anti-human FGFR4 antibody (Cat#8562, RRID:AB_10891199, 1:100 dilution, Cell Signaling Technology).

Whole Slide Imaging was conducted using an Aperio ScanScope XT (RRID:SCR_018457, Leica) at 200× magnification in a single z-plane. Tumor tissue was annotated to exclude areas of necrosis and section artifacts during quantification. Thresholds for positivity were determined using positive and negative controls. FGFR4 staining is reported as an H-Score, Ki67 as the % of positive cells, and Cleaved Caspase 3 as the % positive pixels.

Bystander killing assay

Bystander killing effect of FGFR4-targeted ADCs (α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE and α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan) and isotype control IgG-ADCs (α-HEL-vc-MMAE and α-HEL-NNβA-exatecan) was evaluated using an in vitro co-culture system. GFP-expressing FGFR4-positive RH4 cells were co-cultured with mCherry-labeled RH4-FGFR4 KO cells in 6-well plates. After a five-day incubation with the respective ADC treatments, adherent cells were harvested, and cells were quantified using flow cytometry (BD Fortessa SOPRI). The total cell number and the ratio of GFP+ RH4 and mCherry+ RH4-FGFR4 KO cells were determined using CountBright Absolute Counting Beads (Cat# C36950, Thermo Fisher) and analyzed with FlowJo software.

Pharmacokinetic studies

Pharmacokinetic analysis was performed using 5∼8-week-old NSG mice, which were randomly assigned to two groups. Baseline serum samples were collected before injection. Mice received a single intravenous dose of either 3 mg/kg α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE or 10 mg/kg α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan, and then blood was collected at 15 min, 6 h, 24 h, 48 h (2 days), 96 h (4 days), 216 h (9 days), 336 h (14 days), and 21 days post-injection. At each time point, serum was separated by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 10 min and stored at −80°C until analysis.

Electrochemiluminescence (ECL) Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) assays were used for quantification of α-FGFR4-vc-MMAE and α-FGFR4-NNβA-exatecan in serum samples. Briefly, streptavidin assay plates were blocked with blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature with constant shaking. The biotinylated recombinant human FGFR4/CD334 protein (Cat# RP02357, Abclonal) solution (25 μL) was added to the assay microplates. After incubation for 1 h, plates were washed and the serial diluted ADC calibrators (for standard curve construction), diluted mice serum samples, or reference samples (25 μL) were added to further incubate for 1h. After a wash step, the Sulfo-Tagged detection antibody solution (25 μL) (SULFO-TAG anti-human IgG antibody (Cat# D21ADF-3, MSD), SULFO-TAG monoclonal anti-DXD&exatecan antibody (Cat# DXD-M684, Acro Biosystems), or SULFO-TAG monoclonal anti-MMAE&MMAF antibody (Cat# MME-M5252, Acro Biosystems)) was added and incubated for 1 h. After washing, a 2X Read Buffer was added, which was read with a QuickPlex instrument (MSD) within 20 min. The data were analyzed with WorkBench 4.0 software (Meso-Scale Diagnostics), and concentration of the analytes was determined by use of the 5 Parameter Logistic nonlinear regression model. The concentration-time data were analyzed by non-compartment pharmacokinetic analysis using Phoenix WinNonlin Version 8.4 (Certara, NJ, USA).

LC-MS/MS to quantify free MMAE or β-ala-exatecan

The concentrations of free MMAE and β-Ala-exatecan in mouse serum samples were quantified using a validated ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) method.40,41 The assay ranges were 0.2–125 ng/mL for MMAE and 1–500 ng/mL for β-Ala-exatecan. Low (L), mid (M), and high (H) quality control (QC) samples were prepared for both MMAE (LQC 0.8, MQC 25, and HQC 75 ng/mL) and β-Ala-exatecan (LQC 4, MQC 100, and HQC 300 ng/mL). To prepare the samples, 25 μL of standards in duplicate and QCs in quintuplicate were pipetted into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes. Serum proteins were precipitated by adding 100 μL of acetonitrile solution containing the internal standards (IS)—2.5 ng/mL verapamil for MMAE and 50 ng/mL exatecan for β-Ala-exatecan. Serum proteins from 25 μL of serum samples were similarly precipitated using 100 μL of acetonitrile spiked with the respective IS. The standards, QCs, and samples were vortexed and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate, dried under nitrogen, and reconstituted in 70 μL of acetonitrile/water/formic acid solution (MMAE: 80/20/0.1, β-Ala-exatecan: 95/5/0.1). Chromatographic separation was achieved by injecting 4 μL of MMAE or 3 μL of β-Ala-exatecan sample onto a Waters ACQUITY UPLC with a BEH C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 3.0 μm) maintained at 40°C. The gradient elution consisted of water, acetonitrile, and 0.1% formic acid, with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min for MMAE (run time: 2.1 min) and 0.3 mL/min for β-Ala-exatecan (run time: 3.1 min) was used. Detection was performed using the Waters Xevo TQ-Absolute mass spectrometer for MMAE and the Xevo TQ-XS mass spectrometer for β-Ala-exatecan in ESI-positive mode.

Quantification and statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8 was used for graphing and data analysis. Log rank statistical tests were used for survival analyses. An unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test was used to calculate the significant difference between 2 groups. An ordinary one-way ANOVA was used for multiple group comparisons. A two-way ANOVA was used for in vivo tumor growth curves. p-values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis methods are also described in figure legends.
