Recapitulation of patient-specific 3D chromatin conformation using machine learning
Authors: Duo Xu, Andre Neil Forbes, Sandra Cohen, Ann Palladino, Tatiana Karadimitriou, Ekta Khurana
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryRegulatory networks containing enhancer-gene edges define cellular states. Multiple efforts have revealed these networks for reference tissues and cell lines by integrating multi-omics data. However, the methods developed cannot be applied for large patient cohorts due to the infeasibility of chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) for limited biopsy material. We trained machine-learning models using chromatin interaction analysis with paired-end tag sequencing (ChIA-PET) and high-throughput chromosome conformation capture combined with chromatin immunoprecipitation (HiChIP) data that can predict connections using only assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) and RNA-seq data as input, which can be generated from biopsies. Our method overcomes limitations of correlation-based approaches that cannot distinguish between distinct target genes of given enhancers or between active vs. poised states in different samples, a hallmark of network rewiring in cancer. Application of our model on 371 samples across 22 cancer types revealed 1,780 enhancer-gene connections for 602 cancer genes. Using CRISPR interference (CRISPRi), we validated enhancers predicted to regulate ESR1 in estrogen receptor (ER)+ breast cancer and A1CF in liver hepatocellular carcinoma.

Introduction

Enhancers are non-coding regions that play an important role in gene regulation. Transcriptional reprogramming accompanied by enhancer rewiring is a hallmark of cancer. Most studies to characterize enhancers in a high-throughput fashion in cancer have used functional genomics data from cell lines or mouse models. This is because enhancer identification generally requires chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) assays or a transcription-based approach (Cap analysis of gene expression [CAGE]), which both need larger amounts of input material (∼107 cells) than are available from patient biopsies (∼106 cells or less).1,2,3 The advent of assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq), which can be performed with lower input material, has led to the identification of open chromatin regions in individual patient samples.4,5 However, although chromatin accessibility is a key feature of enhancers, not all open chromatin regions correspond to active enhancers.2,5,6,7,8,9,10,11 Different enhancer states, such as active vs. poised, cannot be distinguished based on ATAC-seq signal alone6,12 (Figure 1A). Besides the identification of enhancer regions, identification of their target genes has remained a challenge. In the past few years, multiple methods have been used to connect regulatory elements to their target genes both experimentally and computationally for representative tissues and cell lines. Experimentally, cohesin ChIA-PET and H3K27ac HiChIP have been applied to various cell types to discover physical contacts of regulatory elements to target genes.13,14 CRISPRi screening has also been applied to individual genes to identify enhancer-gene connections.15 Moreover, hybrid methods integrating CRISPRi-fluorescent in situ hybridization coupled with flow cytometry (CRISPRi-FlowFISH) with ChIP-seq and high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C)16 or comprehensive sets of histone modification ChIP-seq with machine learning17,18 have also been used to identify enhancer-gene connections for representative tissues and cell lines. However, these experiments are expensive and difficult to perform at scale, especially given the aforementioned limits on input samples. While previous efforts have used the correlation between ATAC-seq signal and expression of nearby genes across samples to identify target genes, it has been shown that the relationship between regulatory element and gene varies among different cells or tissue types and is also expected to vary among patient samples.14,18 Moreover, one-to-one correlation coefficients between open chromatin sites and gene expression do not capture the combinatorial effects of multiple regulatory elements on expression.19

To resolve the above issues, we developed a machine-learning-based approach called DGTAC (differential gene targets of accessible chromatin), which can identify differential target genes of accessible chromatin regions in a sample-specific manner. DGTAC is trained using three-dimensional (3D) conformation data to predict enhancer-gene connections using only ATAC-seq and RNA-seq as input. The power of sample-specific prediction is demonstrated by its ability to distinguish active vs. poised enhancer states in different samples, often corresponding to different types or subtypes of cancer and resulting in type-/subtype-specific oncogene regulation. Application of DGTAC on chromatin accessibility and transcriptomic data from 371 patients of 22 cancer types revealed cancer-type and -subtype-specific enhancer-gene connections for 637 known cancer genes. Our predicted active enhancers are validated by existing ChIP-seq datasets in cell lines, while enhancer-gene connections are validated using HiChIP as well as CRISPRi for select candidates.

Results

DGTAC: A machine-learning model trained using 3D conformation data to identify patient-specific enhancer-gene interactions

Previous studies have shown that enhancers and their target promoters need to reside in the same loops insulated by the cohesin/CTCF protein complexes bound at the anchor regions.20,21 Thus, we used cohesin/CTCF ChIA-PET loops to represent the 3D conformation of peak-gene physical contacts to define the gold standard for training. First, we used ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data from 371 patients of 22 cancer types to compute the coefficients of ATAC-seq peaks within 0.5 Mbp of each gene’s transcription start site (TSS) to the gene expression using ElasticNet regression (Figure 1B, step I). This allowed the inclusion of combinational effects of peaks to the gene expression across samples as opposed to the one-to-one association of peak accessibility and gene expression used previously.5 Among all potential 6 million peak-gene pairs, 910,116 peak-gene pairs had non-zero coefficients and were used for the next sample-specific calculations. We converted the across-sample coefficients to sample-specific error terms (e) to indicate the sample-specific association of the peak accessibility to the target gene expression (Figure 1B, step II). The smaller the error term, the stronger the contribution of peak accessibility to the expression, and vice versa. A similar error term has been previously used to provide the tissue-/cell-line-specific information on the effect of enhancers to gene expression for ENCODE/Roadmap and FANTOM samples.18

Next, we collected additional information for each peak-gene pair, including the distance to TSS of the gene, the ATAC-seq signal strength, the genomic copy number, and the expression of the target gene to construct a feature matrix for each patient sample. We used the ChIA-PET datasets from a breast cancer cell line (MCF-7) and a cervical cancer cell line (HeLa)22 and trained two random forest models on patient samples matching the same cancer subtype (luminal A [LumA]/estrogen receptor [ER]+ breast invasive carcinoma [BRCA] for MCF-7 and cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma [CESC] for HeLa) (Figures 1B, step II, and S1A). Both the BRCA model and the CESC model achieved, on average, an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 91.1% and an area under the precision recall curve (AUPRC) of 92% in step III in 10-fold cross-validation. To test whether the two models can be applied to cancer types not used for training, we used the BRCA model on patients with CESC and obtained high precision (0.971) and recall (0.886). Similarly, the application of the CESC model on patients with BRCA shows high precision (0.904) and recall (0.975) as well (Figure S1A; Table S1), demonstrating that the rules of enhancer-gene connections are similar across cancer types even though the enhancers themselves may be different. The performance of the model in such a cross-cell-line comparison stays good when the test set is stratified by distance (Figures S1E and S1F) or when the testing is done using chromosome dropout (Figures S1G and S1H).

Close inspection of features contributing to the random forest model shows that the “error term” is the most important sample-specific feature (Figure S1B).6,12 Upon comparison of the frequency of novel element-gene connections detected by DGTAC with the ones detected by a simple correlation-based approach,5 we observe that the novel connections are more sample specific, as expected (Figure 1C; one-tailed Wilcoxon rank-sum test p < 2.22e−308). Consistent with previous studies, DGTAC predicted that an average of 3.35 peaks (min = 1, max = 104) regulate a gene and that a peak regulates 1.33 genes (min = 1, max = 25) (Figures 1D and 1E).5,8 In total, we identified 61,502 peak-gene connections, which are supported by the expected cancer-type and -subtype classifications (Figures 1F, S1C, and S1D).

Besides CTCF/cohesin ChIA-PET, H3K27ac HiChIP datasets that provide the physical contacts between enhancers and promoters can serve as the gold standard for our model. Thus, we trained another model using H3K27ac HiChIP contacts from a breast cancer cell line (MDA-MB-231)13 as the gold standard. To compare the output from models trained on CTCF ChIA-PET and H3K27ac HiChIP data, we evaluated the predictions for each patient based on the ChIA-PET model using HiChIP-model-based predictions as the gold standard. We observe high precision (average = 0.880; Figure S1I) and recall (average = 0.807; Figure S1I) values for all patients, indicating that the results of models trained with H3K27ac HiChIP are similar to the results of the model trained with CTCF/cohesin ChIA-PET, though the model performance is better at shorter distances than longer ones (Figures S1J and S1K). Although we expect that CTCF/cohesin ChIA-PET solely cannot point to enhancer-to-promoter contacts since many enhancers within CTCF/cohesin loops may not interact with promoters within the same loops, it is likely that step I of the model where contacts with zero coefficients are removed helps with the removal of such false positive contacts. Consistent with these observations, we find that DGTAC predictions based on CTCF/cohesin ChIA-PET data are in higher agreement with H3K27ac HiChIP contacts relative to the simple correlation-based approach (Table S1).13

We also evaluated our predictions using the scores from the activity-by-contact (ABC) model.16 The ABC model defines enhancer-gene connections using active enhancer histone modification marks (H3K27ac ChIP-seq) for the activity of the enhancer and the frequency of its contact to the target gene identified using Hi-C. The higher scores indicate more likely connections of given enhancer-gene pairs and are benchmarked using CRISPRi-FlowFISH.16 We observe that the calculated ABC scores are significantly higher for connections predicted true by DGTAC than those predicted by the correlation-based method5 (Wilcoxon rank-sum test one-sided p < 2.31e−224; Figure S1H). These results further show that DGTAC trained with 3D conformational data can recapitulate the enhancer-gene connections calculated from H3K27ac ChIP-seq and Hi-C.

DGTAC identifies non-canonical element-to-gene connections that differentiate active vs. poised enhancers

While open chromatin is a necessary condition for the presence of active regulatory elements, the absence of nucleosomes is not sufficient to allow active transcription of the gene target.23 In fact, when enhancers are in a poised state, instead of being bound by RNA polymerase II (RNAPII) with tri-acetylation of the H3K27 to initiate the transcriptional activity of the target promoter,23,24,25,26 they are bound by repressive complexes such as polycomb repression complex (PRC2) accompanied by tri-methylation of the H3K27 (Figure 1A). This results in repression of the target gene expression, even though poised enhancers also exhibit open chromatin.27,28,29 In addition, enhancers can transition between the active and poised states via deacetylation or demethylation, and such transitions are involved in important differentiation processes and tumor stemness.6,7,30,31 Sites identified by DGTAC where the chromatin is accessible in multiple samples but predicted to target genes in a subset of those samples may point to enhancers in a poised state (Figure 1A). We call those cases “non-canonical” peak-gene connections, while “canonical” connections are the ones where the presence of the peak is sufficient to indicate that a peak-gene connection exists (Figure 1A). All instances where the peak is open and has a target gene are defined as active enhancers, even though the connection may be canonical or non-canonical across samples (Figure S2A).

DGTAC-predicted active enhancers in eleven cell lines show enrichment of H3K27ac and enhancer-like signatures from ENCODE

To test our hypothesis that open chromatin regions with predicted target genes by DGTAC are active regulatory elements, we applied DGTAC on eleven cell lines for which we either generated ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data or used previously published datasets (Figures S2B–S2D; details for the dataset are listed in Table S2).8,32,33 We observe that our predicted active enhancers (open peaks with target genes predicted) show a significantly higher H3K27ac signal compared with open peaks without target gene predicted (Figure 2A). This trend persists when comparing to active enhancers predicted by correlation-based methods (Figure S2E).5 In addition, active enhancers predicted by DGTAC show a stronger correlation between H3K27ac and the ATAC-seq signal compared to all open chromatin regions (Figure S3A). Nine of the eleven cell lines have enhancer-like signatures (ELSs) available based on H3K27ac ChIP-seq and DNase-seq from Search Candidate cis-Regulatory Elements by ENCODE (SCREEN).8 Consistent with H3K27ac enrichment, we find that the predicted active enhancers from our model are enriched for ELSs among all open regions (Fisher’s exact p ≤ 2.22e−31 and average odds ratio = 2.55; Figure 2B). DGTAC identifies novel enhancer-gene connections for cancer genes

Due to the unique ability of DGTAC to identify low-frequency and cancer-specific enhancer-gene connections, we analyzed enhancer-gene connections that are overrepresented in individual cancers. Overall, we identified on average 114 (ranging from 12 to 329, total of 2,271) cancer-type-specific peak-gene connections specific to only one cancer type (Table S3). Among those connections, 74 connections regulate 72 COSMIC cancer genes,34 and 65 of them are novel connections identified by DGTAC, including both canonical and non-canonical interactions5 (Figure 3B). Besides the cancer-type-specific connections, the classification method also enables us to identify connections common to multiple cancer types.

For example, we identified two open chromatin peaks regulating CYP2C8 at the promoter (TCGA_331538) and at the enhancer (TCGA_331533). We observed that while the enhancer peak can be open and linked to CYP2C8 in patients with multiple cancer types (e.g., cholangiocarcinoma [CHOL], bladder urothelial carcinoma [BLCA], lung adenocarcinoma [LUAD], thyroid carcinoma [THCA], and liver hepatocellular carcinoma [LIHC]), the promoter peak is linked to CYP2C8 only in LIHC. This is consistent with the high CYP2C8 expression in these samples (Figures 4A and 4B). We also observe peaks showing differential regulation patterns among different set of patients. For example, the peak TCGA_474671 regulates 5 genes in all 371 samples (STAT3, STAT5A, STAT5B, PTRF, and COASY), and it uniquely regulates STAT5A in 25 patients and STAT5B in another 45 patients (Figure 4C). While there are other peaks regulating STAT5A and STAT5B besides TCGA_474671, the expression levels of STAT5A and STAT5B are significantly higher in samples with TCGA_474671 vs. those without TCGA_474671 regulating the genes (Wilcoxon rank-sum test p = 1.42e−08 and 1.60e−04, respectively; Figure 4D). The STAT protein family contains intracellular transcription factors (TFs) that are involved in the Janus kinase (JAK)/STAT signaling pathway, in which both STAT3 and STAT5 are crucial for carcinogenesis.37,38 STAT5A and STAT5B are the two isoforms of the oncogene STAT5 and promote the transcription of anti-apoptotic genes.39 The differentially regulating peak (TCGA_474671) may provide an alternative explanation besides copy-number gains or gain-of-function mutations for the enhanced expression of STAT5A/B in patients.39

We further analyzed the top non-canonical connections (6,632 connections; Table S3) that are predicted to exhibit active and poised states in specific cancer types. We find that these cancer-type-specific active/poised enhancer states are linked with known cancer genes and are also enriched in the regulation of cell differentiation and apoptotic processes (adjusted p < 0.00118 and 0.0263; Table S4; Figure 4E). Previous studies showing the association of cellular differentiation and tumor stemness with active and poised transitions in enhancer activity support our observations.6,7,30,31 As an example, non-canonical connection (TCGA_298058 → MELK) is predicted to be active in BRCA and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) but poised in kidney carcinomas (kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma [KIRP] and kidney renal clear cell carcinoma [KIRC]) and prostate adenocarcinoma [PRAD] (Figures 4E, 4F, and S5A). MELK is involved in multiple cellular process including apoptosis and tumorigenesis,40 and its expression is positively correlated with tumor progression.41 We find that while four distal sites regulate this gene, the poised vs. the active state of TCGA_298058 is the best predictor of MELK expression (Figures 4G and S5B). Interestingly, we find that the later-stage tumors exhibit a higher fraction of samples with TCGA_298058 in the active state, potentially pointing to the important role of its transition from poised to active state during tumor progression (Figure 4H).41

Another example of cancer-type-specific non-canonical connections is a peak (TCGA_199201) regulating SPDEF, with the enhancer specifically active in prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD) (Figures S5C and S5D) and correlated with high expression of SPDEF. SPDEF has been known as a tumor suppressor for prostate cancer metastasis and other cancers, with a decrease in its expression observed as the tumor progresses,42,43,44,45 raising the possibility that once the active enhancer becomes poised, as in most other cancers, patients with PRAD will lose the expression of SPDEF, leading to the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and bone metastasis.46

Validation of enhancers using CRISPRi

In addition to the above cancer-type-specific examples, we predicted two peaks regulating ESR1 in a cancer-subtype-specific manner. These peaks linked to ESR1 also show different behavior within breast cancer samples. Both peaks are predicted to regulate ESR1 in ER+ breast cancer subtypes but not in the majority of HER2 or triple-negative subtypes (Figures 5A–5C). Although ESR1 has been known as a key TF facilitating the breast epithelial cell division in patients with ER+ breast cancer, only 1% of ER+ cases were previously explained by copy-number alterations of the gene.47,48 In contrast, the two predicted potential enhancer peaks together can explain the high expression of ESR1 in 93% (40/43) of ER+ samples. To experimentally characterize these enhancers, we first generated ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data for eight breast cancer cell lines spanning the four major breast cancer subtypes, LumA, LumB, Her2-enriched (Her2+), and basal/triple-negative-like (triple-negative breast cancer [TNBC]) (Table S2; Figures S7A–S7D), and applied our model on those cell lines to predict the peak-gene connections for each of them. ATAC-seq confirmed that both enhIntron and enhDistal are open in ER+ cell lines, while enhDistal is closed in ER− cell lines. Consistent with the patient data, our model predicted that both enhancers connect to ESR1 only in ER+ cell lines, not ER− cell lines (Figures 5B, S7E, and S7F).

We then tested the function of these two predicted enhancers using CRISPRi49 in T-47D (ER+), MCF-7 (ER+), and MDA-MB-231 (ER−). We find that guides targeting enhDistal and enhIntron result in average decreases of 38.3% in ESR1 expression in T-47D and 23.6% in MCF-7 relative to a random control peak (negative control) that is open chromatin but not predicted to regulate ESR1 by DGTAC (Figures 5D and S8A; STAR Methods). These results validate the function of enhDistal and enhIntron in regulating ESR1 expression in ER+ cell lines. Furthermore, for both peaks, we find no effect on cell growth in MDA-MB-231 but significant reductions in growth rate for MCF-7, indicating that these enhancers are functional in maintaining the cell growth in ER+ breast cancer (Figures 5E, S8B, and S8C).

We further tested another enhancer candidate (TCGA_323941) predicted to regulate Apobec1 complementation factor (A1CF) in patients with LIHC by DGTAC but not by the correlation-based approach. The enhancer TCGA_323941 is located in the first intron of A1CF. A1CF is an RNA-binding protein that regulates post-transcriptional ApoB mRNA editing, and is highly expressed in liver cancer promoting spontaneous steatosis and fibrosis.50 A1CF expression decreased by 57% upon enhancer knock down (KD) in the HepG2 cell line (matching cell line for LIHC cohort) relative to the random control (Figure S8D).

Discussion

We present a model, DGTAC, trained on 3D chromatin conformation data that is able to recapitulate the connections between active regulatory elements and target genes using only ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data as input. It identifies non-canonical connections that differentiate active vs. poised enhancers in a patient-specific manner. Importantly, such non-canonical peak-gene interactions are ignored by correlation-based methods, which do not account for the variable states of open peaks and their connections to genes across samples. DGTAC identified novel enhancers regulating 602 cancer genes. In particular, two enhancers, whose role in ESR1 regulation was validated by CRISPRi, explain the high expression of ESR1 in 93% of ER+ breast cancer samples.

Prediction of accurate enhancer-gene connections in cancer has remained a challenge in the field. This has also hindered the identification of genomic variants that may act as non-coding drivers by dysregulating gene expression.51,52,53 The map of patient-specific enhancer-gene connections provided in this work will enable further analysis of non-coding genetic variants at these regions. In particular, aggregation of mutations at multiple enhancers regulating the same gene is likely to provide increased statistical power for driver discovery.52,54 Furthermore, the identified enhancer-gene connections make it possible for constructing more precise TF to gene networks for the 371 patients with primary cancer to facilitate the discovery of important TFs and potential novel drug targets.55

Currently, DGTAC was developed on the peak set from 371 patients with primary cancer from TCGA.5,56 In this dataset, some cancer types contain less than 5 patient samples, e.g., 2 samples from CHOL and CESC and 4 samples from skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM). The small sample size of these cancer types may not be able to capture all the representative open chromatin regions. As ATAC-seq and RNA-seq datasets from more primary and metastatic cancer samples become available, the regression coefficients can be computed for the first step of DGTAC, and the random forest model already developed here can be used to predict enhancer-gene links. Similar steps can be used when applying DGTAC for other biological samples such as normal tissues. Moreover, as more ChIA-PET and HiChIP datasets from cancer samples become available, they can be used for training and for improving the supervised learning model presented here. Overall, the framework presented here provides a map of the non-coding regulatory elements to genes in patients with cancer, which can facilitate the discovery of novel cancer drivers.

Limitations of the study

As mentioned above, in this dataset, some cancer types contain less than 5 patient samples, which may not capture all the representative open chromatin regions for the given cancer type. In addition, to define a set of enhancer-gene edges for the training step, we used ChIA-PET and HiChIP data from cell lines that match the subtype of the patient with cancer as the best practice for now. As more ChIA-PET and HiChIP datasets for patients become available, retraining the model will yield more accurate predictions.

Method details

Data processing

ATAC-seq of 400 primary cancer patients was from Corces et al.5 and RNA-seq data were extracted from NCI Genomic Data Commons (GDC)59 with matching sample submitter_id. The ATAC-seq counts and RNA-seq counts used in this study were pre-processed by the above studies, Log2 of Transcripts Per Million (TPM) was adopted for RNA-seq counts, while log2 counts are used for ATAC-seq counts. Copy number segments of patients were obtained from GDC with matching sample submitter_id as well.

DGTAC

ElasticNet regression was performed for all peaks within +/−0.5 Mbp of TSS of each gene. This range was defined from previous studies: 1) by ChIA-PET, the median range between enhancer and its targeted TSS is 58–89 kbp18,60; 2) by HiC, the median range is 124 kbp, and >75% of the promoter-based connections are within the distance of 0.5 Mb.61,62 Thus 0.5 Mb is the upper range of potential enhancer-gene connections and would include most of the enhancer-gene connections. By evaluating the CTCF/cohesin ChIA-PET from MCF-7, which was used to train the model, though the length of the loops ranges from 6.9 kb to 142 Mb, 98% of the loops are within 1 Mb. Therefore, extending further beyond 1Mb would introduce more noise than real signal due to the few numbers of positives in the larger range. The coefficients were collected as peaks’ weight to the target gene expression. For each peak of each gene, we calculate the residual (e) term representing the contribution of the single peak to the target gene expression. Peaks with zero coefficient were filtered out by giving them an artificial large error e = 10. Besides inspecting the peak as a single unit, we partitioned each 500 bp peak into five 100 bp windows and repeated the same regression for each of them independently and output the coefficients to calculate the error for each of those windows. This is to consider the potential effect stemming from the presence of TF footprints in individual peaks which is likely indicative of active enhancers.63,64,65 Together with peak height, window heights, peak errors and window errors, we constructed a feature matrix for each patient including the above peak features as well as gene expression, distance to the TSS and copy number alteration of the gene for each peak-gene pair. Next, we utilized ChIA-PET of CTCF/cohesin defined loops of an MCF-7 cell line and a HeLa cell line22 to identify true peak-gene interacting pairs in a matching ER+/HER2-lumA breast cancer and CESC patient respectively. Using the two true peak-gene sets, we trained two random forest models, BRCA-model and CESC-model. 10-fold cross validations showed high AUROC for both models (BRCA-model: 0.9113 ± 0.0192 and CESC-model: 0.9114 ± 0.0094, Figure S1A and Table S1). Cross-validation with chromosome dropout was carried out with the BRCA-model by dropping chromosome from 1 to 22. The mean AUROC = 0.8945 ± 0.0294 and AUPRC = 0.8918 ± 0.0250.

Distance is the most important feature (Figure S1B). Thus, we stratified predictions by distance to evaluate the model performance through both cross-sample and cross-platform comparison. In the cross-sample comparison, we utilized the two models trained separately by the ChIA-PET from an MCF7 cell line (BRCA-model) and a HeLa cell line (CESC-model) as described above. We stratified the performance by distance; the results show that across samples, the performance varies for each distance bin, but all are within the reasonable range (Figures S1E and S1F). In the cross-platform comparison between HiChIP and ChIA-PET, we stratified the performance by the distance between peaks and genes. Taking the prediction of each patient from the HiChIP-model as standard and comparing the prediction from the ChIA-PET model, we do observe a decrease of AUROCs and AUPRCs as the distance becomes longer (Figures S1J and S1K) while the overall performance is still reasonable as shown in Figure S1I.

Finally, we applied the BRCA-model to all 371 primary cancer patients and predicted 61,502 peak-gene connections in total from 371 primary cancer patients.

Application of DGTAC on cell lines from ENCODE

We downloaded the ATAC-seq bam files (from Michael Snyder, Stanford) and RNA-seq tpm (from Barbara Wold, Caltech) from ENCODE and processed them the same way as Corces et al.5 (details of datasets used are in Table S2). For ATAC-seq, we shifted the reads fragment end by “+” stranded +4 bp, “-” stranded −5 bp for the Tn5 offset, and then we counted the insertion sites on the peak set from 371 TCGA primary cancer patients,5 log2cpm was calculated using “cpm” (cpm(count, log = TRUE, prior.count = 5)) from edgeR66 and normalized using “normalize.quantiles” from preprocessCore.67 The copy number alterations for cell lines were downloaded from The Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE).68 ATAC-seq and RNA-seq of cell lines were both batch-corrected to the ATAC-seq and RNA-seq of 371 TCGA primary cancer patients using ComBat69 with their matching cancer type as covariates. K562 and Panc1 don’t have matching cancer types from the primary cancer cohort, they were adjusted by global average.

Defining canonical vs. non-canonical connections

We separated all connections into canonical and non-canonical by a non-canonical score calculated as the frequency of connection among open peaks across all patients. When the score <0.5 (18,616 connections), i.e., less than 50% of samples with the open peak show the target gene, the connection is defined as non-canonical. When the score >0.5, the connection is defined as canonical (Figure S3A). When identifying the “poised” enhancers in each sample, besides the open peaks involved in non-canonical connections, we ensure the peak doesn’t have any target gene in the sample.

To generate top non-canonical connections predicted from DGTAC, we further filtered non-canonical connections by multiple criteria including the minimum number of samples with and without predicted connections is more than 5; the peak doesn’t regulate another gene in the same sample; and the peak signal should not be significantly different between samples with and without the connection predicted (Figure S6).

ChIP-seq and ENCODE data processing and analysis

To evaluate the potential activity of peaks with predicted target genes, we separated peaks into promoter and enhancer peaks. The promoter peaks are defined as within −2,500/+100bp of TSSs for all transcripts of its target gene; the enhancer peaks are the peaks excluding the promoter peaks from the all-peak set. Cell lines are matched by their closest cancer types and subtypes, and all H3K27ac ChIP-seq data (Table S2) analyzed is “fold change over control” download from encodeproject.org,8 and analyzed using deepTools.57

The newest ENCODE annotations for cell lines regulatory elements were downloaded from https://screen.encodeproject.org/.8 The annotated elements were intersected with ATAC-seq peaks by 1 bp. To generate a comprehensive annotation for each peak, a peak with any of the element annotation for pELS/dELS was annotated as ELS, while any elements with annotation PLS was annotated as PLS.

Activity-by-contact score calculation

The Hi-C data was downloaded from GSE13091670 and has been pre-processed with HiC-Pro.71 The data was further normalized by Knight-Ruiz (KR) matrix balancing using gcMapExplorer.72 The H3K27ac ChIP-seq data was downloaded from GSM1693017.73 The ATAC-seq and H3K27ac ChIP-seq were processed using deepTools57 by flanking +/− 1,250 bp from each peak. The ABC scores were calculated using all ATAC-seq peaks from TCGA primary patient samples5 within +/− 0.5 Mb of each gene TSS, and were plotted in log10 scale as described in Fulco et al.16

Besides using the ABC score to represent enhancer activity, we also compared DGTAC performance in predicting active and poised enhancers to the ABC model. Since ABC model uses H3K27ac as one of the features, we calculated a modified ABC score where only the open chromatin and HiC are used. The prediction of both active and poised enhancers by DGTAC outperforms the modified ABC score. The prediction for poised enhancers by DGTAC is significantly better than using the modified “ABC score”. (Figure S5E)

Cancer-specific connections identification using "distal binarization”

To identify cancer-specific peak-gene connections, we first grouped samples by site of origin, and used the “distal binarization” classification.5 For each peak-gene connection, we calculate the intra-group mean and standard deviation of presence of the connection among patients, then we rank each group by the intra-group mean and iterate until the intra-group mean is larger than half of the intra-group standard deviation. On top of the iteration process we also restrict that the peak-gene connection should be present in more than half of the patients from the group (intra-group mean >0.5).

Gene ontology analysis

Gene ontology analysis was done using GOrilla.58 Using protein coding genes involved in non-canonical connections comparing to the genome background, regulation of cell differentiation and apoptotic processes (adjusted p-value = 0.00116 and 0.00128) are enriched. They are also enriched in protein binding function (adjusted p-value = 1.57e-11).

RNA-seq and ATAC-seq data processing

RNA-seq and ATAC-seq data were generated for 8 cell lines (MCF-7, T-47D, ZR-75-1, MDA-MB-361, BT-474, SK-BR-3, AU-565 and MDA-MB-231). Cells were grown to near confluence, pelleted and frozen at −80°C.

RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Plus Mini kit (74134, Qiagen) and sent to the Weill Cornell Medicine Genomics Resources Core Facility, where an Illumina TruSeq stranded mRNA Library prep was made using HiSeq 2500 and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S1 Flow Cell, PE100 cycles. RNA-seq data were aligned to hg38 using STAR,74 counts was calculated using HTSeq75 and TPM was calculated as described by Wagner et al.76

For ATACseq, nuclei were isolated from >95% viable cells in log phase growth and processed at the Weill Cornell Medicine Epigenomics Core using the OMNI-ATAC protocol as detailed in,77 and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000, SP Flow Cell PE50 cycles. ATAC-seq data was mapped to hg38 and processed using the ENCODE ATAC-seq pipeline (https://www.encodeproject.org/atac-seq/).

CRISPRi sgRNA guide design and cloning

We used a modification of the GeCKO Target Guide Sequence Cloning Protocol to generate our clones.78,79 We utilized the software tool ChopChop to design guide RNA sequences for regions corresponding to 6 separate regulatory elements.80 5 elements were enhancer-like elements 500 bp in size and the remaining element consisted of the region 2,500 bp upstream and 100 bp downstream of the TSS of ESR1. Exact locations and sequences are provided in Table S5. In brief, the sgRNA guide oligos targeting our regions of interest were selected to span the fullest width of the regulatory element possible while also maintaining a high guide efficiency score. For each peak, we found a 20-base pair sequence upstream of an NGG PAM sequence, which was identified as the “Forward” sequence. Each forward sequence and its reverse complement were synthesized with CACCG at the 5′ end of the forward strand and AAAC at the 5′ end of the reverse complement strand to make them compatible for cloning using BsmbI. The complete list of oligos used to make the guide clones is found in Table S5. All oligos were ordered using standard desalting conditions. The pCC_09 was digested with BsmBI (Thermo Fisher, FD0454) and treated with alkaline phosphatase (EF0651, Thermo Fisher) at 37°C for 30 min, gel-purified on a 2% agarose gel, and the digested plasmid was extracted using the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (28704, Qiagen). Oligo pairs were phosphorylated, annealed, and ligated with 50ng BsmBI digested plasmid. Ligated plasmids, at least 3 guide clones for each region of interest, were transformed into Stbl3 bacteria (C737303, Invitrogen), grown on Ampicillin selection and plasmid DNA was extraction using HiSpeed Plasmid maxi Kits (12662, Qiagen). Plasmid DNA was quantitated using NanoDrop spectrophotometer.

Production of Lentivirus

Lentivirus was produced by Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000-015, Invitrogen) transfection of HEK293T cells with pCC_09 (139094, Addgene) individual sgRNA clones, packaging plasmid psPAX2 (12260, Addgene) and envelope plasmid pMD2.G (12259, Addgene). Media was changed 24 h post-transfection and virus was harvested 72 h post-transfection. Individual viral supernatants were spun, aliquoted and frozen at −80°C until transduction.

Lentivirus transduction

Cells to be transduced with CRISPRi guide clones were harvested in log phase and plated at 500 × 105 cells per well in 6 well plates with growth media plus 10ug/mL Polybrene (SCBT, sc134220). Lentivirus, at 500ul/well was added and 18 h post-transduction, cells were split 1:2 and Puromycin was added for selection against non-transduced cells. Puromycin concentrations were determined by titration for each cell line, and ranged between 0.5 and 2 μg/mL. To correct for the transduction bias in MCF-7 and T-47D81 the following changes were made to the protocol. For MCF-7, 1mL of virus was used to transduce 500 × 105 cells, with the virus was removed no sooner than 28 h post transduction, and Puromycin was added the following morning. For T-47D, the virus concentration was not increased from the standard 500ul/well, but the virus was removed no sooner than 28 h post-transduction and Puromycin was added 1–2 h after the cells were re-plated. For each transduction, one well of non-transduced cells were subjected to the selection antibiotic to confirm transduction. Cells were maintained on selection antibiotic until they were frozen and/or pelleted for further analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR

MCF-7, T-47D, SK-BR-3, MDA-MB-231 and BT-474 were transduced with the CRISPRi guide clones described above. RNA was isolated from frozen cell pellets using the Qiagen RNeasy plus Mini Kit (74134, Qiagen) and quantitated using NanoDrop. cDNA was synthesized from 1ug RNA using the qScript cDNA SuperMix (101414-106, Quantabio) according to product protocol. Transcript from 15ng cDNA was quantitated with LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I (Roche, 04887352001) using the Roche LightCycler 480 II machine. The qPCR primers used are listed in Table S5.

Expression was evaluated relative to RPL13a in individual replicates then normalized to the water/non-targeting control to evaluate ESR1 knockdown.

Growth Inhibition assays

MCF-7 guide clones were plated at 2500 cells/well in white solid bottom 96 well plates (3917, Corning) and assayed on day 0–4 for growth using CellTiter-Glo (G7570, Promega) per package instructions. Relative Luminescence Units (RLU) were measured on a Biotek Synergy Neo Alpha plate reader. Day 0 rlu confirmed equivalent density among clones at plating.
