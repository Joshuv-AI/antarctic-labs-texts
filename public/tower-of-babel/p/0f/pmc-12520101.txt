“Same principles, different decisions”: A qualitative study exploring the attitudes of COVID-19 vaccination among school staff in Switzerland
Authors: Marta Fadda, Guenda Bernegger, Kleona Bezani, Ilaria Falvo, L. Suzanne Suggs, Maria Caiata-Zufferey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDuring the COVID-19 pandemic, Switzerland prioritized vaccination for school staff to mitigate the economic and societal impacts of school closures. However, there is limited research on vaccine acceptance and hesitancy among school staff in Switzerland. This study aimed to explore the arguments used by school staff to justify COVID-19 vaccination decisions in public and private institutions across Switzerland’s German-, French-, and Italian-speaking regions. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling from a national survey and through snowball techniques. Data were collected from interviews with 47 participants employed in nurseries, kindergartens, primary schools, middle schools, and special education facilities, conducted between November 2021 and March 2022. Interviews were analyzed using a primarily inductive approach, complemented by elements of abductive and deductive reasoning to identify key principles guiding decision-making. Among the participants, 36 had received at least one dose of the COVID-19 vaccine, while 11 remained unvaccinated. We extracted four main themes: respondents considered four principles (i.e. self-protection, protection of others, return to normality, and institutional loyalty) as starting points in their decision-making process and then developed different arguments, either in support or against vaccination. The findings illustrate the complexity of vaccine hesitancy within the school context, where individual decisions are shaped by a combination of rational, moral, political and identity-related factors. This study contributes to the theoretical understanding of vaccination decision-making processes, particularly in school settings. It highlights the importance of considering vaccine hesitancy in the workplace and underscores the need for tailored communication strategies that address specific concerns within professional groups.

Introduction

Traditionally, studies on vaccine hesitancy among professional groups have primarily focused on healthcare providers.1–4 This focus is rooted in the critical role healthcare workers play in influencing vaccine acceptance through their interactions with patients, their high levels of exposure to infectious diseases, and the ethical obligation they hold to protect themselves and others.5,6 Consequently, much of the research has explored factors such as healthcare workers’ knowledge, attitudes, and trust in vaccines, as well as systemic barriers within healthcare institutions that may impact vaccine uptake.7 While this focus has yielded valuable insights and significantly improved understanding of vaccine hesitancy in healthcare settings, it has led to a relative neglect of other professional groups whose vaccination decisions also have a substantial impact on public health outcomes. Expanding research to encompass professional groups beyond healthcare providers is essential for a more comprehensive understanding of vaccine hesitancy, particularly in fields where professionals work in close contact with vulnerable populations. Professions are not merely occupational categories but social structures that shape norms, values, and risk perceptions.8 Professional roles influence access to information, trust in scientific authorities, and ethical frameworks guiding decision-making.9 Examining vaccine hesitancy through the lens of profession allows us to understand how occupational identities shape attitudes toward vaccination, beyond individual beliefs or structural barriers. Extensive research on vaccine hesitancy in the general population has emphasized factors such as misinformation, trust in authorities, and perceived risk, which provide a useful comparative backdrop to this study.10

School staff represent a particularly relevant group, as their role combines educational responsibilities with daily interactions in a community setting, positioning them as potential influencers of vaccine attitudes. Additionally, teachers and school staff hold a distinctive position in mitigating the societal upheaval and inequalities stemming from the COVID-19 pandemic, given their pivotal role in the well-being of both young individuals and, indirectly, the broader economy. The closure of schools and the shift to remote learning during the initial phase of the pandemic exerted substantial adverse effects on the financial stability, mental health, and educational progress of families and young people. These repercussions disproportionately affected economically disadvantaged families and those reliant on essential support services coordinated through the public school system.11,12 Despite many schools remaining open during the second and following waves of the pandemic, exposure events, compulsory use of masks, and quarantine guidelines continued to be disruptive for teachers, students, and families alike. Thus, a high level of COVID-19 vaccine acceptance among school staff was important to minimize disruptions to schooling and ensure the health, safety, and well-being of staff, students, and families.13 Yet, little is known about how school staff, as a professional group, reason about vaccination – a gap this study seeks to address.

While research on school staff is still limited, a few studies from various countries have begun to explore vaccine acceptance in this professional group. Studies conducted in Canada, Greece, Ethiopia, and the USA show variations in vaccine acceptance among school staff. A population-wide cross-sectional online survey administered from August to November 2020 to more than 5’000 Canadian public school teachers asked them how likely they were to receive a COVID-19 vaccine.14 The majority (89.7%) reported they were likely or very likely to accept a COVID-19 vaccine. Sociodemographic predictors of intention to be vaccinated included being male, having an educational background in science or engineering, and using reliable information sources on vaccination such as public health administrations and health care providers. Teachers who reported lower levels of vaccine hesitancy, higher general vaccine knowledge, and a belief that COVID-19 was a serious illness were more likely to intend to receive a COVID-19 vaccine. A more recent study conducted in Canada showed even higher acceptance rates (95.4%), where education about the risks and benefits of COVID-19 vaccines from a trusted source had the strongest relationship with vaccine intentions among this occupational group.15 A study conducted in Greece among elementary school teachers assessed the intention to uptake the COVID-19 vaccine and identified factors associated with vaccine acceptance during the COVID-19 pandemic.16 In this study, only 38.1% reported they intended to get the COVID-19 vaccine when available. Factors independently and significantly associated with the intention to uptake COVID-19 vaccine included more than 15 years of work experience, prior influenza vaccine uptake, believing that COVID-19 vaccine should be mandatory, and that schoolteachers are a high-risk group for COVID-19. A study in Ethiopia assessed the determinants of intention to receive COVID-19 vaccine among school teachers and found that slightly more than half (54.8%) of participants scored above the median of intention to receive COVID-19 vaccine.17 Being affiliated with a religion other than Orthodox or Muslim, having a bachelor degree, higher perceived susceptibility, higher perceived benefit, lower perceived barrier, and cues to action were significantly associated with the intention to receive the COVID-19 vaccine.18 The proportion of school staff who are hesitant to be vaccinated is estimated to be over 12% based on US data.18 However, rates tend to be even higher among minority groups.19 A study conducted in China indicates that a high proportion of college teachers (42.2%) were hesitant to receive COVID-19 vaccination. It also found that primary decision-makers in a teacher’s family are less likely to exhibit vaccine hesitancy compared to non-decision-makers.20 The large majority are quantitative studies focused on COVID-19 vaccination intention, uptake, and respective drivers. Despite existing research in countries such as Greece, the USA, China, and Ethiopia, there remains a lack of qualitative insight into how school staff in decentralized, multilingual contexts reason about vaccination.21 This study addresses that gap.

Switzerland launched its COVID-19 vaccination campaign in January 2021, initially targeting priority groups such as healthcare workers and residents of long-term care facilities. As vaccine supplies increased and the pandemic evolved, the focus expanded to include other critical groups. With the reopening of schools after lockdowns, Swiss cantons emphasized the importance of vaccinating school staff to ensure the continuity of education and minimize disruptions, highlighting their essential role in safeguarding the health and well-being of students and families. Switzerland’s decentralized education and health systems, combined with cultural-linguistic diversity and relatively high levels of institutional trust, offer a distinctive context for examining vaccine decision-making. The country’s federalist structure allows cantonal variation in public health and education policies, which adds an additional layer of complexity to understanding how professional identities and institutional affiliations influence vaccination choices. No study has so far investigated the COVID-19 decision-making process among school staff in Switzerland. This study aims to provide qualitative insights on how school staff employed in Switzerland made their COVID-19 vaccination decision.

Methods

Study design

Building upon the comprehensive sociological framework established by Max Weber,22 we delved into the experiences of a diverse sample of school staff employed in various educational settings across urban and rural regions within the three linguistic areas of Switzerland. Specifically, we drew on Weber’s concept of social action22 to explore how school staff interpret their roles and navigate institutional expectations within different socio-cultural and linguistic contexts. Social action is defined as an action to which the “acting individual attaches a subjective meaning to his behavior. […] The action is ‘social’ insofar as its subjective meaning takes account of the behavior of other and is thereby oriented in its course.”22 This encompassed a wide array of roles, ranging from teaching staff (e.g., teachers, childcare workers, teaching assistants, speech therapists, paraprofessional educators) to administrative staff, transportation personnel, school nurses and counselors, nutrition staff, athletic coaches and trainers, custodians, canteen workers, security personnel, and cleaning staff. Our primary aim was to capture an all-encompassing representation of their experiences rather than offering a comparative analysis.

To ensure a comprehensive representation of experiences, purposive sampling was based on participants’ linguistic region, professional role, type of school (public/private, urban/rural), vaccination status, and demographic variables. We did not consider whether participants were parents or caregivers, or had comorbidities that might influence vaccine attitudes. Our interviews were structured in two parts. The first focused on understanding the professional challenges faced by participants in light of the pandemic and the measures in place, their strategies for addressing these challenges, and any potential benefits arising from managing such difficulties. The results from that inquiry have already been published.23 The second part aimed to elucidate participants’ COVID-19 vaccination decision-making. This study is part of PERSPECTIVES, a larger research project on vaccine hesitancy among school staff in Switzerland.24

Employing a semi-structured, in-depth interview approach, we developed an interview grid (see Appendix). The interview grid was developed collaboratively by the research team, informed by prior qualitative studies on vaccine hesitancy and COVID-19. It was reviewed internally by the research team and piloted with three individuals from the team’s network who shared characteristics with the intended sample (teachers), and revised in response to feedback. A team of four trained qualitative interviewers, experienced in remote qualitative research methods, conducted interviews in the participants’ preferred language – German, French, or Italian. All were female and had experience in secondary education but not in schooling, and none had a prior relationship with the participants. MCZ had 20 years of experience, MF more than 10 years, GB 15 years, and IF 5 years of experience in the field. Data collection and analysis occurred concurrently, allowing emerging themes to shape subsequent interviews. The four interviewers had backgrounds in public health and qualitative research and were fluent in at least one of the national languages. None had prior relationships with participants. The team held regular debriefings to reflect on their influence on the data collection process.

Ethics statement

This study received authorization from the Ethics Committee of the Canton of Ticino (2021-01955) on October 26, 2021.

Recruitment and data collection

Participants for this study were recruited through a twofold approach. First, we used purposive sampling, identifying potential interviewees among individuals who had completed a quantitative online survey addressing COVID-19-related professional challenges and vaccine hesitancy. This survey was part of a broader research project investigating vaccine hesitancy among school staff in Switzerland. It was distributed through institutional collaborations (city and cantonal school offices) to a broad range of school employees, including both teaching and non-teaching staff. Upon concluding the online survey, we extended an invitation to participants, asking if they would be willing to participate in an in-depth interview to further elucidate their perspectives on COVID-19 public health measures including the vaccination. All individuals who expressed their willingness to participate and who met the basic eligibility requirements (being adult school staff, able to provide informed consent, and sufficiently proficient in the study language) were included. No additional exclusion criteria were applied. Second, we employed snowball sampling techniques to supplement our participant pool and reach the point of data saturation. This involved leveraging personal contacts, with a particular focus on the German- and French-speaking regions of Switzerland. We took care to ensure diversity within our sample, considering factors such as age, cantonal residence, employment in both rural and urban school settings, work within both private and public schools, and gender distribution (female/male). The interviews, conducted between November 2021 and March 2022, were performed via telephone, with oral informed consent obtained from each participant. Each interview lasted approximately one hour. Subsequently, all interview recordings were transcribed verbatim to facilitate data analysis.

Data analysis

Our data analysis approach was consistent with the core principles of grounded theory methodology25 and primarily adopted an inductive methodology, complemented by abductive and deductive reasoning elements.26,27 The analytical process unfolded as follows. Initially, the research team collectively analyzed the same sub-set of interviews. Regular weekly meetings were held to facilitate discussions and consensus-building during the initial analysis phase. Subsequently, each researcher created individual analytical summaries based on the interviews they had conducted. To ensure member checking, these summaries were shared within the research group and subjected to validation and feedback from other team members. As individual reports were created and shared, the team took turns creating cross-sectional analytical summaries that drew connections between multiple interviews. These summaries were likewise shared, reviewed, and validated by the research group. The qualitative research team maintained close collaboration, continuously working together to establish consensus on codes and their interrelationships throughout the analysis process. As saturation became evident, the researchers compiled a final codebook. This codebook served as a reference guide for deductive recoding of interviews using a software for qualitative research (NVivo), enhancing the analytical rigor and confirming the initial analysis. To further validate data saturation, three additional interviews were conducted at this stage. We used thematic saturation rather than information power as our sampling threshold,28 consistent with our exploratory aims. We relied on the concept of data saturation as our sampling threshold, as it is particularly relevant for studies with larger samples, whereas information power is more commonly applied in research with smaller samples.28,29 Finally, all data underwent a process of anonymization, including the removal of any identifying information. Names were replaced with pseudonyms, and quotations were translated into English.

Our analysis identified four main principles that shaped individuals’ decisions regarding COVID-19 vaccination, each corresponding to a distinct reasoning: the self-protection principle (rational reasoning), the protection of others principle (moral reasoning), the return to normality principle (political reasoning), and the institutional loyalty principle (identity-driven reasoning). These principles resonate with Weber’s types of social action: self-protection aligns with instrumental rationality; protection of others reflects value rationality; return to normality can be interpreted as instrumental rationality oriented toward regaining pre-pandemic routines; and institutional loyalty may draw on value rationality, through commitment to authority and collective norms, as well as elements of traditional action. Each principle will be described in the following sections accompanied by relevant arguments and illustrative quotes. Respondents framed their arguments for or against COVID-19 vaccination around four guiding principles: self-protection, protection of others, return to normality, and institutional loyalty. These principles served as foundational reference points in their decision-making process, ultimately leading to varied arguments either in favor of or against vaccination, as summarized in Table 2. Every principle corresponds to a specific reasoning. When considering self-protection, participants approached their decision-making from a rational perspective, evaluating what seemed most effective for safeguarding their own health. In reflecting on the protection of others, decision-making occurred at a moral level, as participants weighed personal and collective values and responsibilities. The principle of returning to normality involved decisions rooted in a political dimension, reflecting broader views on society and governance. Notably, unvaccinated participants appeared to mobilize a more diverse and elaborated set of arguments, showing a stronger commitment to justifying their stance. Their reasoning was often more deliberate, structured, and assertive in tone. In contrast, vaccinated participants tended to describe their decision as stemming from habit, tradition, or social conformity, rather than from an explicit process of reflection or justification.

Principle #1: self-protection

Arguments of the vaccinated

Fear of COVID-19

Several participants expressed fear of contracting COVID-19 and primarily sought vaccination as a means of self-protection. Their concerns centered around the dread of falling ill, avoiding hospitalization, mitigating the risks of long-COVID, and their trust in the guidance of scientific and public health authorities. Fear [of the disease], surely. (Female, Italian-speaking linguistic region)

Fear [of the disease], surely. (Female, Italian-speaking linguistic region)

Arguments of the unvaccinated

Low perception of COVID-19 severity and susceptibility

Most participants conveyed a lack of fear concerning COVID-19, grounded in the belief that the virus would not significantly affect them. This confidence stemmed from diverse factors, including their young age and overall good health, their adherence to a healthy lifestyle, regular physical activity, the absence of firsthand experience witnessing severe COVID-19 cases, or having personally recovered from COVID-19 without complications.

Participants grappled with the argument that unvaccinated individuals could potentially transmit the virus more easily. They also harbored concerns about potential stigmatization if they were to infect others, leading to moral dilemmas and apprehension of repercussions. Consequently, they employed various strategies to neutralize this argument: questioning the actual risk of transmission, citing instances of vaccinated individuals falling ill, diligently adhering to preventive measures. These included limiting social interactions, consistently wearing masks, practicing thorough hand hygiene, and emphasizing that infections often occurred among children, who were generally at lower risk, rather than as a result of their own actions. I am more responsible [than vaccinated people] because I protect myself. I am truly cautious, keep my distance, and continue to wash my hands. (Female, Italian-speaking linguistic region)

I am more responsible [than vaccinated people] because I protect myself. I am truly cautious, keep my distance, and continue to wash my hands. (Female, Italian-speaking linguistic region)

High risk perception of COVID-19 vaccination

Some participants articulated concerns regarding vaccine side effects, particularly citing instances they had heard about among their acquaintances. They expressed apprehension regarding the perceived experimental nature of the vaccines, their rapid development, distrust in mRNA technology, skepticism toward the pharmaceutical industry, and a reluctance to become “guinea pigs” in the vaccination process. I didnt want to get the first dose, because its like being a guinea pig; so, I waited a little while and let the others go first. (Female, German-speaking linguistic region)

Principle #2: protection of others

Arguments of the vaccinated

Transmission prevention

Some participants chose to receive the vaccine driven by a sense of moral duty, recognizing their interactions with numerous individuals and the risk of infecting them. Others made the decision to vaccinate themselves to protect a loved one. Additionally, some participants embraced vaccination as a reflection of professional ethics, perceiving themselves as role models for both young individuals and society at large, driven by a moral responsibility stemming from their professional role. Arguments of the unvaccinated

Superfluousness of the vaccination

The rationale provided against vaccination as a means of protecting others includes multiple facets: skepticism about vaccine efficacy in reducing infectivity, citing instances of illness among the vaccinated; diligent adherence to health measures as an alternative protective measure; belief that children primarily infect each other; confidence in children’s ability to recover well from the illness; acceptance of the notion that not everyone needs constant protection, with the perspective that the frailty of some individuals aligns with the natural course of life. I know more vaccinated people who caught COVID than those who are unvaccinated. (Female, Italian-speaking linguistic region)

I know more vaccinated people who caught COVID than those who are unvaccinated. (Female, Italian-speaking linguistic region)

Altruism

The altruistic motive played a role in some participants’ explanations for their decision not to get vaccinated. For instance, one participant opted against vaccination to make the vaccine available for those who needed it more urgently than she did. I didnt want to take the vaccine away from sick people or people who needed it more than I did. (Female, German-speaking linguistic region)

I didnt want to take the vaccine away from sick people or people who needed it more than I did. (Female, German-speaking linguistic region)

Principle #3: return to normality

Arguments of the vaccinated

Speeding a return to normality through the vaccine as a societal responsibility

For some participants, getting vaccinated was a pragmatic choice to return to a certain normality, at least from a social point of view. Some participants emphasized that their decision to vaccinate was grounded in a sense of societal responsibility. They highlighted the broader implications, such as reviving the economy, reopening businesses, hosting cultural events, and alleviating the strain on hospitals. They underscored the idea that their individual choice was not solely about personal benefit but had wider significance for the functioning of society and the world at large, with an aim for things to return to a previous state of normalcy and functionality. I think it is also a matter of responsibility to society. That the economy can get back on track, that businesses can open again, that cultural events can take place, that hospitals can be relieved – yes, the whole decongestion of society. I think thats part of peoples responsibility – its not just about me, theres more at stake. For the world to function again, lets say. That it works, yes. (Female, German-speaking linguistic region)

Arguments of the unvaccinated

Affirming a new lifestyle

Participants perceived the push for a return to normalcy as an imposition, characterized by intense pressure, strong encouragement, and at times, what they described as “brainwashing.” While individuals were aware of their freedom to choose, they keenly sensed ongoing and predominantly indirect or implicit pressure, exemplified by tactics such as the distribution of leaflets at schools containing recommendations to get vaccinated from the Federal Office of Public Health (FOPH). For some individuals, COVID-19 served as a sort of societal wake-up call, shedding light on the shortcomings of our way of life. It exposed the stress, relentless pursuit, unsustainable practices, including environmental concerns, excessive work hours, and frequent, unnecessary international travel. Paradoxically, COVID-19 was seen as a beneficial setback, prompting a reevaluation of people’s pace, lifestyles, and priorities. The vaccine, in this context, was viewed as a shortcut to swiftly return to the same societal order, potentially reintroducing environmental problems and sustainability challenges.

Participants who declined the vaccine in favor of advocating for a new way of life grounded their refusal in three distinct arguments. First, their refusal represented a form of civic resistance against the Western lifestyle characterized by excess and resource wastage. Second, their vaccination refusal was seen as an opportunity to educate younger generations about a healthier, more respectful, and more natural way of living. One participant even coined the term “vaccination society” to symbolize a society oblivious to sustainability and incapable of capitalizing on local opportunities. That is what I felt and what still worries me now. […] But its more this need of… I heard young people saying “We are losing our freedoms, we absolutely have to get vaccinated to get all that back.” In fact, it hurt me enormously and made me think that we hadnt understood much of this COVID. […] Instead of learning something from this situation, as soon as there was a vaccine, it was the race to return to the initial square, thats it. (Female, French-speaking linguistic region)

Principle #4: institutional loyalty

Arguments of the vaccinated

A duty of loyalty toward the state

Some participants demonstrated a sense of loyalty toward their employer, particularly when they were employed by the government, which mandated vaccination. Working for the State instilled in them a moral and deontological obligation to receive the vaccine, aligning with the State’s directives. I work for the state, we work in a public space, we work with children who have concerned parents behind them (…) we take responsibility for working in a public space, we take responsibility for working with children, and children are vulnerable. (Female, French-speaking linguistic region) I belong to that group of people who have relatively great trust in the State. So, I think that if the Federal Council says that the problem is solved by getting vaccinated, we need to vaccinate as many as possible. (Female, German-speaking linguistic region) My position on the vaccine is quite clear, I say that if we had all let ourselves be vaccinated, it would have been much easier for the children. Regarding my professional position within the school, I try to understand the teachers arguments, but without dwelling on them too much: its not for me to judge, and the position the teachers have regarding the vaccine is not that important to me. For me it is important that they act according to the schools way of acting, so that we all stay healthy. (Male, German-speaking linguistic region)

Arguments of the unvaccinated

A duty of loyalty toward oneself

For some individuals, their resistance to vaccination stemmed from a desire to assert their principles of self-respect and self-affirmation in the face of external authorities attempting to impose their views and compel them to engage in actions they did not believe in. This resistance was at times intensified by the need to defy institutional mandates, reflecting their commitment to remaining consistent and authentic with their inner convictions. Discussion

This study aimed to provide qualitative insights into how school staff employed in Switzerland made their COVID-19 vaccination decisions. Since professional roles influence norms, values, and perceptions of responsibility,8 it is essential to examine how occupational identities shape vaccination decision-making. We particularly focused on school staff as a population of interest due to their pivotal role in education, the broader societal and economic implications of school closures, and the potential impact of their vaccination decisions on the health and safety of students and the community at large. Our analysis identified four key principles – self-protection, protection of others, return to normality, and institutional loyalty – that guided participants’ reasoning, leading to both convergent and divergent vaccination choices.

Our study highlights the complexity inherent in vaccination choices, which, while grounded in shared principles, are influenced by distinct reasonings. While the rational and moral dimensions of vaccine decision-making have also been observed in other professional groups, such as healthcare providers,30 school staff particularly emphasize the role of political reasoning and the affirmation of personal identity in shaping their vaccination decisions. Unlike healthcare workers whose loyalty is often mediated by biomedical ethics and professional codes, school staff may experience competing norms – educational, civic, and familial – that shape institutional loyalty. Political reasoning among school staff also tends to reflect broader civic ideologies rather than sector-specific concerns. These insights suggest that vaccination decisions are not merely public health choices. Rather, they are deeply intertwined with individual values, political orientations, and the sense of belonging within specific social or professional groups. Our results share similarities with the study by Keshet and Popper-Giveon on vaccine hesitancy in Israel, which also emphasizes the sociopolitical dimensions of vaccine decision-making.31 The Israeli study highlights how vaccine hesitancy reflects resistance to institutional (bio)power, mistrust of governmental motives, and skepticism toward collaborations with pharmaceutical companies. Similarly, our findings reveal that vaccination decisions are influenced by political considerations, with participants navigating their choices based on their vision of societal norms and governance. Furthermore, our analysis illustrates how minority groups, such as those who chose not to get vaccinated, often engage in more vigorous argumentation, likely due to the need to defend and rationalize their decisions within a broader societal context.

This study also highlights that vaccination decisions are far more nuanced than the binary yes/no framework often portrayed. Participants articulated sophisticated rational, moral, political, and identity-based arguments to support their choices, reflecting deeply held values rather than simplistic reasoning. Notably, references to vaccine-critical views or to narratives that significantly diverged from official public health messaging played little to no role in their decisions. The labels “anti-vax sentiments” and “conspiracy theories” seem overly connoted and framed from a pro-vaccine perspective, rather than from a neutral, descriptive one. Those who adopt such views are unlikely to characterize them in these terms themselves. Instead, individuals expressed well-founded and reasoned motivations, grounded in meaningful principles. This aligns with qualitative literature emphasizing the legitimacy of arguments both for and against vaccination, as well as the humanization of vaccination decisions – suggesting that individuals are not algorithmic in their choices but deliberate and consistent in their reasoning.30,32,33 The study also underscores the importance of recognizing this “personal consistency.” Moralizing or stigmatizing vaccine decisions risks being counterproductive, as it overlooks the complex and legitimate concerns individuals navigate. Importantly, both vaccinated and unvaccinated participants started from similar principles. What differed were the conclusions drawn to fulfill these principles, underscoring the shared foundation of their decision-making processes and demonstrating that the vaccination decision is shaped by diverse interpretations, experiences, priorities, and personal values. Efforts to promote vaccination should visibly highlight its multifaceted benefits while addressing specific concerns with well-informed counterarguments. At the same time, these efforts should acknowledge the legitimate concerns individuals may have and acknowledge the role of their lived experiences in shaping their decisions. Our findings offer valuable empirical support to the emerging literature on value-based conversations in vaccination, demonstrating the potential of engaging individuals around their core principles to foster meaningful dialogue and build trust.34–36

A third finding is that the vaccination decision served for some participants as a form of self-affirmation, either through demonstrating loyalty to the state or expressing civic resistance.31 This dimension of institutional loyalty reflects an identity-driven process, wherein individuals define their sense of belonging and alignment. For those aligning with the state, vaccination symbolized trust in public institutions and collective responsibility. Conversely, for those resisting, refusal to vaccinate became a way to challenge institutional authority and assert autonomy, reflecting a broader critique of power structures and societal norms. The findings of this study align with historical analyses of vaccine resistance, which demonstrate how decisions around vaccination are deeply tied to broader societal debates about freedom, identity, and institutional power. For instance, an analysis of antivaccination activism in the United States from the 1880s to the 1920s highlights how resistance was framed around liberty and civil rights, opposing both compulsory vaccination laws and the influence of elite institutions and medical experts.37 Similarly, participants in our study framed their vaccination decisions as identity markers, affirming either loyalty to the state or resistance to institutional control, with some viewing their choices as a critique of societal norms. While this study did not distinguish between government and non-government employees, it is important to note that individuals’ views on vaccination may still be influenced by factors such as personal values, workplace environment, and perceptions of authority. In workplaces where clear health mandates are not enforced, employees might exhibit more diverse attitudes toward vaccination, shaped by personal beliefs or the organizational culture rather than governmental directives. This continuity suggests that vaccine hesitancy is not solely a health-related issue but a means of negotiating autonomy, political vision, and belonging. Understanding the parallels between historical and contemporary vaccine resistance underscores the need for public health efforts to go beyond disseminating scientific evidence and engage with the ideological and identity-based dimensions of vaccine hesitancy. Recognizing these deeper underpinnings can help tailor strategies that address the complex interplay of values, mistrust, and individual autonomy influencing vaccination decisions. The majority of participants were women, reflecting the gender distribution among school staff in Switzerland. No gender-related differences emerged, which may be due to this predominance and to the fact that our study was not designed to explore gendered differences. We also did not observe differences between linguistic regions. Future research may examine how gender intersects with professional roles and vaccination beliefs.

Limitations

This study has limitations. Despite initial collaboration with teacher unions, recruitment challenges arose, potentially impacting the diversity of participants. Selection bias may also be present, as individuals with political dissatisfaction, stronger political views, or critical stances toward vaccination may have been more motivated to participate, potentially influencing the perspectives expressed. While this may introduce selection bias, we attempted to mitigate it by recruiting across all linguistic regions and through snowball sampling to diversify perspectives. Additionally, focusing solely on school staff, while providing in-depth insights, limits the ability to compare their experiences with those of other professional groups, such as grocery store or hospital staff, in navigating the challenges of making a COVID-19 vaccination decision. Despite these constraints, efforts were made to recruit a diverse sample and ensure the accurate representation of participants’ voices. Although the study reached thematic saturation, some participant subgroups (e.g., non-teaching staff) were represented in smaller numbers. This may limit the breadth of perspectives captured from those specific groups. Finally, the absence of data triangulation, such as observational or institutional data, limits the validation of self-reported attitudes and behaviors.

Conclusions

Understanding the factors that influence school staff’s decisions regarding COVID-19 vaccination is crucial for improving public health efforts and ensuring the continuity of education. Our study shows that vaccine decision-making cannot be fully understood in isolation from professional identity and context, as occupational roles shape reasonings, perceptions of responsibility, and responses to public health measures. It also highlights the complexity of vaccination choices, emphasizing that decisions are not merely binary but are embedded within four key reasonings: rational, moral, political, and identity-driven. While shared principles guide vaccination decisions, these diverse reasonings shape individual choices in distinct ways. Furthermore, our findings underscore the importance of personal and political identity, particularly within workplace settings, in influencing these decisions. By examining these factors, our research offers valuable insights for policymakers, enabling them to tailor interventions and communication strategies that address the specific concerns of school staff and promote a safer educational environment. This study provides transferable insights into vaccine decision-making among education professionals in similar decentralized and multicultural contexts. Moreover, although this study focuses on COVID-19, the insights gained into school staff’s vaccination reasoning may also apply to other immunization programs delivered through schools, such as influenza or HPV vaccination. The context of the pandemic was unique – marked by fear, intense media attention, and unprecedented restrictive public health measures. Some motivations or hesitations observed, such as concerns about rapid vaccine development, relate specifically to COVID-19, and the political and social context of the pandemic strongly influenced perceptions of risk and trust. Nonetheless, understanding how professional identity and contextual factors influence decision-making can help design communication strategies that resonate across different public health campaigns.
