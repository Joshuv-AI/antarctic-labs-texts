Association of Composite Metabolic Indices With Incident Carotid Plaque: A Chinese Cohort Study
Authors: Yangxuan He, Fei Xu, Jiayi Deng, Meng Li, Song Leng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComposite metabolic indices are key drivers of carotid plaque (CP) formation. This study aimed to evaluate and compare the predictive performance of 12 composite metabolic indices for incident CP in a large Chinese cohort.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study enrolled 10 407 participants free of CP at baseline from the DHMC (Dalian Health Management Cohort). The primary outcome was incident CP, diagnosed via carotid ultrasound. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to assess the association between 12 indices—the triglyceride‐glucose index (TyG), TyG‐body mass index, TyG‐waist circumference, TyG‐waist‐to‐height ratio, body mass index, weight‐adjusted‐waist index, body roundness index, lipid accumulation product, visceral adiposity index, cardiometabolic index, lipoprotein combined index, and atherogenic index of plasma—and incident CP. Time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic curves were employed to compare their predictive performance. Subgroup analyses were performed to validate the findings.ResultsDuring a median follow‐up of 2.4 years, 1832 participants (17.6%) developed CP. After adjustment for potential confounders, all composite metabolic indices were significantly associated with incident CP. Among these indices, the lipoprotein combined index exhibited the strongest association and the best predictive performance. Time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic analyses further confirmed that lipoprotein combined index consistently achieved the highest predictive accuracy throughout the follow‐up period.ConclusionsAmong 12 metabolic indices, LCI emerged as the strongest predictor for CP development in this Asian cohort, supporting its use as a simple, noninvasive tool to identify high‐risk individuals for subclinical atherosclerosis and improve atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease primary prevention.Keywords: carotid plaque, cohort study, insulin resistance, lipoprotein combined index, triglyceride‐glucose indexSubject Categories: Epidemiology, Primary Prevention, Atherosclerosis

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD)—most commonly manifested as ischemic heart disease and stroke—remains the leading cause of mortality and disability worldwide, imposing a severe burden on public health systems. 1 , 2 , 3 Carotid plaque (CP) objectively reflects systemic atherosclerotic burden and is strongly associated with the risk of ASCVD events. Consequently, CP has emerged as a valuable window for vascular risk assessment. 4

Composite metabolic indices reflect insulin resistance (IR) and related metabolic abnormalities. IR is a central driver of ASCVD development and plays a key role in the formation of atherosclerotic plaques. 5 Although the hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp remains the gold standard for assessing IR, its complexity and high cost limit its utility in large‐scale population studies. 6 In recent years, composite metabolic indices—most notably the triglyceride–glucose index (TyG)—have been validated as reliable surrogate markers for IR. Studies have demonstrated that an elevated TyG index is significantly and positively associated with the presence of carotid atherosclerotic plaques in individuals without diabetes, highlighting its potential utility as an early screening tool. 7 Derived indices—such as TyG‐body mass index (TyG‐BMI) and TyG‐waist circumference (TyG‐WC)—integrate obesity parameters and have demonstrated superior predictive performance over the TyG index alone in other metabolic disease settings. 8 , 9 Beyond the TyG series (TyG, TyG‐BMI, TyG‐WC) and traditional measures such as BMI, several emerging indices have been associated with atherosclerosis risk. The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), for instance, has been shown to correlate closely with CP. 10 Additionally, a study has indicated that anthropometric indices such as the visceral adiposity index (VAI) and lipid accumulation product (LAP) are associated with CP. 11 However, most prior investigations have employed cross‐sectional designs, limiting causal inference. Furthermore, no longitudinal cohort study has systematically compared the predictive performance of a comprehensive panel of these composite metabolic indices for predicting carotid atherosclerosis—including the full TyG series, traditional measures, and emerging indices—in an Asian population.

To address these gaps, our study leverages a large‐scale retrospective cohort from northern China to comprehensively evaluate and compare the predictive value of 12 indices—TyG, TyG‐BMI, TyG‐WC, TyG‐waist‐to‐height ratio (TyG‐WHtR), BMI, the weight‐adjusted‐waist index (WWI), body roundness index (BRI), LAP, VAI, the cardiometabolic index (CMI), the lipoprotein combined index (LCI), and AIP for the development of carotid atherosclerosis. Our aim is to identify the optimal predictor and elucidate its relationship with carotid plaque (CP) risk, thereby informing early detection and prevention strategies for ASCVD.

METHODS

Study Population and Design

This investigation was drawn from the DHMC (Dalian Health Management Cohort; CCC2023112102), a large‐scale retrospective study launched in 2014 at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University. The DHMC was established to evaluate population health, identify determinants of chronic disease risk, and inform targeted prevention strategies. Further details of the cohort design have been published elsewhere. 12 , 13 The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

We initially identified 13 555 adults (≥18 years old) in the database who had completed at least 2 routine health examinations, including carotid ultrasound, between 2014 and 2024. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: presence of CP at baseline (n=1854); missing data on key variables (body weight, waist circumference, lipid profile, or blood glucose) (n=1208); or history of coronary heart disease, uremia, liver failure, or other organ dysfunction (n=86). After applying these criteria, 10 407 individuals remained for analysis (Figure 1). The date of each participant's first examination served as baseline, and follow‐up extended until either the final examination date or the diagnosis of CP, whichever occurred first.

Ethics Statement

This study conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University (Approval No. KY2025‐603‐01). 14 As this was a retrospective study using anonymized data, the requirement for informed consent was waived. 15 All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant regulations. Data security and participant privacy were protected through strict confidentiality protocols and data encryption measures.

Data Collection and Measurement

Anthropometric measurements, including height and weight, were taken with participants in light clothing and without shoes. WC was measured with a measuring tape positioned 1 cm above the navel. Laboratory assessments involved the collection of a venous blood sample after a minimum 8‐hour fasting period, followed by the analysis of various biochemical markers using a fully automated biochemical immunoassay system. The blood parameters measured included fasting plasma glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C). BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of the height in meters, and WHtR was derived by dividing waist circumference by height.

Definition of CP

The outcome of this study was CP measured by Doppler color ultrasound. Trained sonographers examined 3 regions of the carotid artery (common, bifurcation, and internal) using Doppler ultrasound. CP was defined as an intima‐media thickness (IMT) ≥1.5 mm or focal structures encroaching into the arterial lumen of at least 0.5 mm or 50% greater than the surrounding IMT value. Carotid IMTwas defined as IMT ≥1.0 mm in either the right or the left carotid artery. 23 Participants with increased carotid IMT or plaques were defined as having carotid atherosclerosis. 24

Covariates

Demographic data, including sex, medical history, medication history, and smoking and alcohol consumption history, were collected using structured questionnaires. Smoking status was assessed using a single questionnaire item asking participants whether they had ever smoked or were currently smoking. Responses were recorded as yes or no. Participants who answered “yes” were classified as having a history of smoking exposure, whereas those who answered “no” were classified as never smokers. Alcohol consumption was assessed using a questionnaire item asking participants whether they had ever consumed alcohol or were currently consuming alcohol. Responses were recorded as yes or no. Participants who answered “yes” were classified as having a history of alcohol consumption, whereas those who answered “no” were classified as never drinkers. Medication use was additionally reported as any medication use at baseline. After resting for at least 5 minutes, participants' blood pressure was measured using an electronic sphygmomanometer. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, current use of antihypertensive medications, or self‐reported hypertension. 25 Diabetes was considered present if participants were using antidiabetic medications or insulin, had a history of diabetes, or had fasting plasma glucose ≥7 mmol/L or glycated hemoglobin ≥6.5%. 26 Dyslipidemia was defined as triglycerides >1.7 mmol/L (150 mg/dL) or total cholesterol >5.2 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) or LDL‐C >3.4 mmol/L (130 mg/dL) or HDL‐C ≤1.0 mmol/L (40 mg/dL). 27 , 28

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables with a nonnormal distribution were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges, andnormally distributed variables were presented as means ±SDs. Baseline differences between groups were assessed using standardized mean differences, with absolute values >0.1 indicating meaningful imbalance. 29 Categorical variables were described as counts (percentages), and group differences were compared using the chi‐square test. Missing baseline covariate values were imputed using individual follow‐up data. Directed acyclic graphs were constructed to identify potential confounders, 30 including age, sex, diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, medication use, alcohol consumption, and smoking; the corresponding directed acyclic graph is presented in Figure S1. All continuous predictors were standardized to Z‐scores (mean=0, SD=1) to ensure comparability across indices. The reported hazard ratios (HRs) represent the change in risk per 1‐SD increase in each index. Associations between 12 insulin‐resistance indices and incident CP were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression (survival package). Three hierarchical models were specified: Model 1, unadjusted; Model 2, adjusted for age and sex; and Model 3, additionally adjusted for diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, medication use, alcohol consumption, and smoking. Collinearity among covariates was examined using variance inflation factors, with values <5 indicating no significant multicollinearity. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using scaled Schoenfeld residuals. The fully adjusted model did not satisfy the proportional hazards assumption at the global level (Table S1). Therefore, variables that violated the proportional hazards assumption based on residual tests (P<0.05) were incorporated as time‐varying covariates in Cox models with time‐varying coefficients. Dose–response relationships were explored using restricted cubic splines with 4 knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles (rms package). Analyses were implemented using the rms package in R. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age, sex, BMI, hypertensive status, diabetic status, and dyslipidemia status, using the best‐performing indicators identified in the Cox regression models. Time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic curves were used to assess predictive performance (time receiver operating characteristic package). To verify the robustness of the association, 2 sensitivity analyses were performed: First, excluding individuals who developed CP within 2 years of baseline to minimize reverse causality. Second, excluding participants using medications that could directly influence metabolic indices or cardiovascular risk, specifically lipid‐lowering agents (eg, statins), antidiabetic drugs, and antihypertensive medications.

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A 2‐sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Characteristics of Participants

Among 10 407 participants, 6723 (64.6%) were male. Over a median follow‐up of 2.4 years, 1832 participants (17.6%) developed incident CP. Compared with participants without CP, those who developed CP were more likely to be male and older, and had a higher prevalence of medication use, diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and smoking. They also showed a less favorable cardiometabolic profile, with higher WC, BMI, fasting plasma glucose, serum uric acid, Scr, total cholesterol, LDL‐C, and triglycerides, as well as lower HDL‐C. Regarding IR‐related indices, CP cases had higher levels of AIP, LAP, WWI, LCI, TyG, TyG‐BMI, TyG‐WC, TyG‐WHtR, and BRI, whereas differences in CMI and VAI were relatively modest. Drinking status was largely comparable between groups (Table 1).

After adjustment for age and sex (Model 2), and further for diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, medication use, alcohol consumption, and smoking status (Model 3), all associations remained statistically significant, although modestly attenuated. In the fully adjusted model, TyG (HR, 3.56 [95% CI, 3.23–3.91]), TyG‐WC (HR, 3.44 [95% CI, 3.14–3.77]), and AIP (HR, 3.40 [95% CI, 3.09–3.74]) remained the strongest predictors of CP.

Significant interactions with time were observed for all metabolic indices across all models (all P for interaction <0.001). The HRs for the time‐interaction terms were consistently <1.0, indicating a progressive attenuation of the associations between metabolic indices and CP risk over follow‐up. In Model 3, the time‐interaction HRs ranged from 0.40 to 0.57, with the lowest estimates observed for TyG‐WC‐time and TyG‐time.

Restricted cubic spline curves showed significant overall and nonlinear associations with CP for all indices except WWI and BMI. Specifically, WWI showed no overall association (P for overall >0.05), whereas BMI exhibited a significant overall association but no evidence of nonlinearity (P for nonlinearity >0.05). For the remaining indices, both overall and nonlinear components were significant (P for overall <0.05, P for nonlinearity <0.05) (Figure 2).

Predictive Performance

Time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic curves demonstrated that LCI exhibited the highest area under curve for predicting the onset of CP over the entire follow‐up period (Figure 3). As time progressed, LCI consistently showed the strongest predictive ability among all indices, followed by TyG‐WC, TyG‐WHtR, and TyG. Conventional composite metabolic indices such as LAP, CMI, and AIP exhibited moderate predictive value, whereas BMI and WWI showed relatively lower performance. Based on the calculated C‐index values, LCI demonstrated the highest predictive performance for the onset of CP, with a C‐index of 0.685 (95% CI, 0.672–0.697), followed by TyG (0.677 [95% CI, 0.664–0.689), TyG‐WC (0.672 [95% CI, 0.660–0.685]), and TyG‐WHtR (0.670 [95% CI, 0.658–0.683]). Other conventional composite metabolic indices, including LAP (0.665 [95% CI, 0.652–0.677]), CMI (0.664 [95% CI, 0.651–0.676]), and AIP (0.657 [95% CI, 0.644–0.670]), exhibited moderate predictive value. In contrast, TyG‐BMI (0.643 [95% CI, 0.629–0.656]), VAI (0.637 [95% CI, 0.624–0.650]), BRI (0.625 [95% CI, 0.611–0.638]), WWI (0.601 [95% CI, 0.587–0.615]), and BMI (0.600 [95% CI, 0.586–0.614]) showed relatively lower performance. Following DeLong testing (Table S2), LCI significantly outperformed all other indices (P<0.05). More detailed results can be found in Figure 3 and Table S3.

DISCUSSION

This retrospective study tracked 10 407 participants to explore the relationship between 12 composite metabolic indices and the occurrence of CP. Our main findings demonstrated that all indices were positively correlated with the risk of CP, with most exhibiting a nonlinear relationship. Using time‐interaction Cox models, we further observed that the associations between composite metabolic indices and CP risk were time dependent, with effect sizes gradually attenuating over the course of follow‐up. Among these indices, LCI consistently demonstrated the strongest overall predictive performance, showing superior discriminative ability and stability across different follow‐up periods and clinical subgroups. Although TyG and TyG‐related indices exhibited stronger effect sizes in Cox regression models, LCI outperformed other indices in terms of risk discrimination, suggesting that it may better capture the combined metabolic disturbances relevant to CP development. After adjusting for several confounding factors, all indices remained independent predictors of CP.

A notable finding was the nonlinear relationship between several composite metabolic indices and CP risk, demonstrated by restricted cubic spline curves. As levels of composite metabolic dysfunction, reflected by higher values of these indices, increased from low to moderate ranges, the risk of CP rose sharply, which is consistent with the well‐established pathological mechanisms linking IR–related metabolic disturbances to atherosclerosis. However, for some indices, such as CMI and BRI, the risk curve plateaued or slightly declined at very high levels. This “plateau effect” or “S‐shaped” trend may reflect complex biological and statistical phenomena, including a saturation point beyond which further worsening of metabolic dysfunction no longer proportionally raises risk. Selection bias and the “healthy survivor effect” could also play a role; some individuals, despite severe metabolic dysfunction, remain healthy due to unmeasured protective factors. Additionally, excluding participants with baseline CP may influence observed risk at extreme levels. These nonlinear patterns highlight the intricate relationship between composite metabolic indices and CP risk, warranting further mechanistic research.

The superior predictive ability of LCI is supported by its strong biological plausibility and well‐established underlying mechanisms. LCI integrates 2 central drivers of atherosclerosis—IR–related metabolic dysfunction and dyslipidemia—rather than serving as a direct measure of either process alone. Consistent with previous studies, IR, a key pathophysiological process reflected by composite metabolic indices such as LCI, has been positively associated with CP formation. IR promotes the onset and progression of atherosclerosis through various mechanisms, such as inducing endothelial dysfunction, promoting reactive oxygen species release, and mediating systemic inflammation and oxidative stress. 31 , 32 Specifically, IR can impair the vascular endothelial nitric oxide generation system, leading to endothelial cell dysfunction, and further exacerbating CP progression through activation of the IFN‐γ (interferon‐γ)‐macrophage pathway. 31 Additionally, IR is significantly associated with the pathogenesis of coronary atherosclerosis and is considered an independent risk factor for cardiovascular metabolic diseases. 33 IR also promotes vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration, driving plaque progression toward irreversible stages. 34 , 35 Late‐stage vascular smooth muscle cell death may result in plaque rupture. MicroRNAs such as miR‐29c and miR‐204 also influence vascular smooth muscle cell phenotypic changes and plaque stability. 36 Beyond IR–related mechanisms, LCI captures abnormalities in lipoprotein metabolism, characterized by an increased proportion of small dense LDL, which enhances vascular inflammation and foam cell formation, alongside impaired reverse cholesterol transport that accelerates cholesterol accumulation within the arterial intima. 37 Therefore, by comprehensively integrating IR‐related metabolic dysfunction and lipid metabolism disturbances, LCI represents a biologically plausible and powerful indicator for predicting atherosclerosis risk, although its clinical utility warrants further validation in diverse populations.

Previous studies have indicated close associations between BMI and TyG with incident CP. 38 , 39 Similarly, research has shown that the VAI and LAP are linked to CP. 40 Other investigations have reported associations between AIP and LCI with CP, with AIP often demonstrating strong predictive performance. 11 Our results align with this literature, as all indices were associated with CP. However, LCI emerged as the strongest predictor in our analysis, which differs from some previous studies in which AIP appeared superior. Possible reasons include smaller sample sizes in earlier studies and their cross‐sectional designs, contrasting with our retrospective cohort approach, which may account for these discrepancies. 11 , 40 LCI demonstrates superior predictive value for CP compared with BMI, TyG, and other conventional indicators may be attributed to its ability to more directly and comprehensively capture lipid metabolism disturbances, a central pathophysiological process underlying atherosclerosis. 28 BMI and WC, as indirect anthropometric measures of obesity and visceral fat accumulation, are associated with dyslipidemia but fail to directly assess the lipid microenvironment. These metrics overlook the heterogeneity of metabolic phenotypes, such as metabolically healthy obesity, and do not account for lipid dynamic fluctuations independent of body composition. 41 Similarly, IR indices like the TyG index, although reflective of IR, do not adequately capture lipid metabolism health, resulting in suboptimal performance. In contrast, LCI offers a more biologically specific advantage by integrating lipoprotein‐related dysregulation with IR–related metabolic dysfunction, both of which are intrinsic to atherosclerotic plaque initiation and progression. Compared with the indirect pathways of BMI, TyG, or WC, LCI demonstrates a more direct causal link with endothelial damage and CP development. This mechanistic superiority underscores LCI's value as a superior prognostic tool, warranting further validation across diverse populations to optimize ASCVD risk stratification and prevention strategies. 42

Our study results have important clinical translational value. LCI is simple, low‐cost, and does not require insulin measurement or complex tests, making it an ideal noninvasive tool for large‐scale cardiovascular risk screening, especially in resource‐limited areas. Notably, LCI showed the highest HR in subgroups with no significant dyslipidemia (ie, normal traditional lipid indicators), indicating its ability to detect high‐risk individuals with metabolic disorders despite normal lipid panels, who may develop CP in the future. Using LCI for risk stratification could enable early, targeted interventions in seemingly healthy individuals, such as lifestyle changes (diet and exercise) or pharmacotherapy (eg, lipid‐lowering or blood pressure management), to control lipid metabolism abnormalities and proactively manage cardiovascular health before plaque formation.

Strengths and Limitations of This Study

This study strengthens causal evidence through a longitudinal cohort design, enabling the observation of the temporal relationship between 12 composite metabolic indices and CP incidence. By systematically comparing 12 metabolic indices, LCI was identified as the most reliable predictor, providing clinical guidance. However, the study has some limitations. The cohort primarily consisted of adults from northern China, which may have regional and ethnic limitations that could affect the generalizability of the findings. Although we adjusted for major cardiovascular risk factors, unmeasured variables (eg, detailed dietary patterns, physical activity levels, socioeconomic status, or air pollution) may cause residual confounding. Furthermore, as an observational study, reverse causality cannot be entirely ruled out, although sensitivity analyses partially addressed this issue. Lastly, CP diagnosis relied on carotid ultrasonography without plaque composition analysis, and future studies should further address these aspects.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, this study highlights the important role of composite metabolic indices, particularly LCI, in assessing CP risk. LCI emerged as the most reliable predictor in this large Asian cohort study, indicating its potential as a simple, effective, noninvasive tool for identifying high‐risk individuals with subclinical atherosclerosis. Although the study has limitations, particularly in terms of population diversity and potential unmeasured confounders, these findings provide valuable insights for enhancing primary prevention strategies for ASCVD and warrant further validation across different populations.
