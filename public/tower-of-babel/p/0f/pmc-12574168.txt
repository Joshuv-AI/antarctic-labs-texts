The potential of melatonin in the mitigation of adverse effects of salt stress in basil (Ocimum basilicum L.) plants
Authors: Lucía del Sol González Forte, Sebastián Andrés Garita, Valeria Fernanda Bernardo, Marcela Fabiana Ruscitti, María Cecilia Arango, Sonia Zulma Viña
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrought and salinity are among the most critical abiotic stresses affecting crops worldwide. Within this context, melatonin has emerged as a multifunctional signaling molecule that mitigates stress and promotes growth in various plant species. This study aimed to evaluate the physiological and biochemical responses of Ocimum basilicum plants cultivated under hydroponic conditions and exposed to salt stress, following pre-harvest treatment with melatonin. Basil seedlings were immersed in melatonin solutions at concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 μM for 48 hours and subsequently grown for 60 days under saline stress.ResultsMelatonin treatment, particularly at 50 μM (T50), significantly increased the fresh weight of both aerial parts and roots. Aerial biomass nearly doubled, reaching 47.6 g plant⁻¹ compared to 23.8 g plant⁻¹ in untreated plants under salinity (+100%). Root fresh weight also rose markedly, from 29.6 g plant⁻¹ to 50.3 g plant⁻¹ (+69.8%). Leaf area expanded substantially, averaging 660.8 cm² per plant at T50 versus 301.2 cm² in salinity-stressed controls (+119.4%). Total chlorophyll content increased by 3.9–11.2%, with values rising from 43.43 µg cm⁻² in untreated plants under salinity to a maximum of 48.29 µg cm⁻² at 25 μM melatonin. Stomatal conductance showed a significant improvement as well, reaching 169.4 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at T50, which represented a 50.6% increase relative to untreated plants. Biochemical stress indicators declined consistently in melatonin-treated plants. MDA content decreased by 36.2% (from 3.87 to 2.47 nmol mg⁻¹ FW at T50), while foliar proline accumulation was reduced by 28.1% under the same treatment. Leaf total phenol content followed a similar trend, showing reductions ranging from 19.2 to 48.1% across all melatonin doses compared with salt-stressed controls. In most cases, these decreases were statistically significant (P<0.05), indicating improved redox homeostasis and membrane stability under salt stress.ConclusionsAmong the tested concentrations, 50 μM melatonin was the most effective in promoting growth and alleviating stress. These findings highlight its potential as a pre-harvest strategy to enhance basil performance under salinity within the broader context of melatonin-based stress management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07566-8.Keywords: Aromatic plants, Soil-less cropping model, Saline stress, Natural bioregulators, Physiological status

Introduction

Soil salinization is driven by multiple factors, including sea-level rise, windborne sea salt deposition, parent rock weathering, prolonged droughts, use of low-quality irrigation water, sustained irrigation practices, inefficient drainage systems, dominance of shallow-rooted annual crops, and improper use of inorganic fertilizers [1, 2]. Climate change has already intensified both desertification and salinization in arable lands [3], and increasing aridity and global warming are expected to exacerbate the accumulation of water-soluble salts in the soil, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions [4]. Initially, salinity alters the soil microbiome and reduces crop productivity. Over time, it can cause irreversible damage to vegetation, leading to the transformation of fertile lands into barren, desertified areas. Salt stress, defined as the adverse impact of excessive Na⁺ and/or Cl⁻ ions on plant physiology, reduces crop yield by limiting water uptake and inhibiting growth. High salinity levels lead to cellular injury, ion imbalance, toxicity, damage to the photosynthetic machinery, and oxidative stress [5].

Several strategies have been explored to mitigate the negative effects of salinity, with biostimulants receiving particular attention. By activating antioxidant pathways and promoting osmolyte accumulation, these compounds enhance plant tolerance to salinity, drought, and nutrient stress [6].

Melatonin (N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine), a well-known neurotransmitter, has been extensively studied in animals for over six decades since its initial isolation and identification from the pineal gland of cows [7]. In plants, melatonin contributes to environmental stress tolerance by enhancing photosynthetic activity and, consequently, biomass accumulation. It also helps maintain membrane integrity, improves cellular redox homeostasis, and supports osmoregulation. Since its first detection in higher plants in the 1990 s, melatonin has been identified and quantified in over 20 botanical families, including both dicotyledons and monocotyledons [8].

In plants, melatonin functions as a growth regulator and biostimulant, modulating responses to various abiotic and biotic stresses. It influences plant growth and development, postharvest quality, oxidative stress reduction, seed germination, seedling vigor, stress resistance, and circadian rhythm regulation [7, 9]. Although the application of growth regulators to alleviate salt stress has shown promising results, their effectiveness may vary depending on several factors, including plant species, application frequency, timing, method, and concentration [10]. Exogenous melatonin application has demonstrated antioxidant activity at the cellular level. Its role in plant defense responses is mainly attributed to the enhancement of radical scavenging capacity and antioxidant enzyme activities. Furthermore, melatonin is thought to protect embryos and reproductive tissues from oxidative damage caused by environmental stressors and may delay the senescence of vegetative organs [11, 12].

Sweet basil (Ocimum basilicum L.), a member of the Lamiaceae family, is widely used in Mediterranean, Indonesian, Thai, and Vietnamese cuisines due to its distinctive flavor. Fresh and dried basil are commonly used as culinary herbs, while basil extracts and essential oils are employed in the food, fragrance, and cosmetic industries [13]. In addition, basil has been reported to possess pharmacological properties such as lowering blood pressure, reducing serum glucose and cholesterol levels, and alleviating muscle spasms and inflammation [14].

Previous studies have shown that basil is sensitive to salinity. Bione et al. [15] observed that saline water reduced shoot hydration, final plant height, and fresh and dry biomass accumulation. Similarly, Caliskan et al. [16] reported a significant decrease in basil yield with increasing salinity levels, with root growth being more affected than leaf development. More recently, da Silva et al. [17] demonstrated that irrigation water with electrical conductivity exceeding 0.8 dS m⁻¹ negatively impacted basil biomass production.

The present study aimed to determine the optimal concentration of exogenously applied melatonin for mitigating salt-induced stress in O. basilicum, and to validate the effectiveness of a pre-harvest root immersion method as a consistent and practical delivery system. The parameters assessed, ranging from morphological and physiological traits to biochemical stress indicators, were selected for their direct relevance to the overall quality of the marketable product.

Materials and methods

Plant material

Seeds of basil (Ocimum basilicum cv. Genovese) were sown in 72-cell speedling trays at a density of one seed per cell. The growth substrate was prepared by mixing perlite, vermiculite, and peat in equal volumes. The trays were maintained in a greenhouse for 40 days under natural light and ventilation. Nutrient supply was provided through irrigation with a complete Hoagland nutrient solution [18]. Basil seedlings with four expanded leaves were treated by immersing their roots in a solution of melatonin at concentrations of 0, 12.5, 25, 50 and 100 µM for 48 h. Then, non-treated (T0) and treated plants (treatments named T12.5, T25, T50 and T100, according to the applied melatonin dose) were grown for 60 days in hydroponics and subjected to salt stress, by adding NaCl to the Hoagland’s solution (EC = 6000 µS cm−1; pH 6). Also, seedlings immersed for 48 h in water without melatonin were cropped for the same period in Hoagland’s solution without NaCl (EC = 1500 µS.cm−1; pH 6), as non-stressed controls (T0-NS). Plants were grown in a polyethylene greenhouse (200 μm thickness) located at 34.659° S latitude and 58.441° W longitude. The cultivation period lasted from February to March, under natural conditions of temperature, relative humidity, and light.

Physiological and biometric assessments

Sixty days after transplanting, immediately prior to harvest, the SPAD chlorophyll index was measured using a SPAD-502 meter (Minolta, Japan), and stomatal conductance was assessed with a SC-1 porometer (Decagon Devices Inc., USA). After harvest, the fresh weight of roots and shoots was separately determined using a precision balance (Classic Plus model, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). Leaf area was measured with a LI-3000 area meter (LI-COR Biosciences, USA).

Total chlorophyll, total phenol and proline content

Total chlorophyll content was determined spectrophotometrically from leaf discs (1 cm in diameter) extracted with N, N-dimethylformamide as the solvent. Absorbance was recorded at 647 and 664 nm using a UV-160 A spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan), and chlorophyll concentration was calculated according to the method of Wellburn [19]. Results were expressed as µg chlorophyll cm⁻².

Total phenol content was quantified colorimetrically using the Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, following the method described by Singleton and Rossi [20]. For extraction, 500 mg of fresh plant tissue was homogenized in 96% (v/v) ethanol and centrifuged at 9600 × g for 10 min at 10 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected, and aliquots were diluted with distilled water and reacted with 1 N Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and 2% (w/v) Na2CO3 in 0.1 N NaOH. After incubation for 90 min at 25 °C, absorbance was measured at 760 nm. A standard curve was generated using gallic acid, and results were expressed as µg gallic acid g⁻¹ fresh weight (FW).

Proline content was measured in leaf and root tissues (100 mg FW) following the method of Bates et al. [21]. Absorbance was recorded at 520 nm, and proline concentration was calculated and expressed on a fresh weight basis (µmol proline g⁻¹ FW), as follows:

µmol proline g⁻1 FW = [(µg proline/mL x mL toluene)/115.5 µg/µmol]/[(g FW)/2]

Malondialdehyde content

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content, an indicator of lipid peroxidation, was determined following the method of Heath and Packer [22], based on its reaction with thiobarbituric acid (TBA). Fresh tissue (200 mg) was homogenized in 0.1% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA), and a 0.5 mL aliquot of the extract was mixed with 1 mL of TBA in TCA solution containing butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT). The mixture was incubated at 95 °C for 30 min and then rapidly cooled in an ice bath. Samples were subsequently centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 532 and 600 nm using a UV-160 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). MDA concentration was calculated using the following formula:

MDA (nmol mg⁻1) = [(A532 - A600)/155000] × 106.

Antioxidant enzyme activity assays

The activity of nonspecific peroxidases (POX) was measured according to the method of Maehly and Chance [23]. Briefly, 250 mg of fresh tissue were homogenized in 1 mL of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) containing 1 mM disodium EDTA as a chelating agent and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) to inhibit protease activity. The homogenate was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. POX activity was determined by monitoring the increase in absorbance at 470 nm due to guaiacol oxidation to tetraguaiacol, using an extinction coefficient of 26.6 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹. Enzyme activity was expressed as specific activity per milligram of protein.

Catalase (CAT) activity was determined spectrophotometrically following the method described by Aebi [24], based on the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and the associated decrease in absorbance at 240 nm. The reaction mixture, prepared in a quartz cuvette, contained the appropriately diluted enzyme extract, 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100, and 12.5 mM H₂O₂. The mixture was protected from light throughout the assay. CAT activity was calculated from the initial rate of H2O2 decomposition using an extinction coefficient of 40 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹, and expressed as specific activity per milligram of protein.

Statistical analyses

All determinations were carried out at least by triplicate. Results were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by a Fisher’s Least Significant Difference test, at a significance level α = 0.05. Also, a Generalized Procrustes Analysis (GPA) was conducted to investigate the structural similarity between leaf and root measurements in basil plants. The analysis involved rotating, scaling, and translating the leaf and root data matrices to achieve optimal alignment. Two matrices were compared: one representing measurements from leaves and one representing measurements from roots. The consensus between the leaf and root configurations was evaluated using the percentage of agreement, with higher values indicating greater similarity between the two datasets. For all statistical analyses, the Infostat software was used [25].

Results

As shown in Fig. 1, melatonin treatments significantly increased the aboveground fresh weight of basil plants compared to the salt-stressed control (T0). The T50 treatment resulted in a 100% increase relative to T0 and a 39% increase relative to the non-stressed control (T0-NS). Root fresh weight also improved with melatonin application, with T50 surpassing T0 and T0-NS by 69% and 36%, respectively (P < 0.05). Variations in total plant biomass are depicted in Fig. 2. All melatonin-treated plants exhibited higher biomass values than T0, which recorded the lowest performance under stress conditions. Notably, T50 treatment resulted in total biomass exceeding even that of the non-stressed controls, highlighting its growth-promoting effect under saline conditions.

As shown in Table 1, total phenol content in basil leaves increased under saline stress (T0), consistent with previous findings reported by Ciriello et al. [26] for three different basil cultivars. However, phenol levels were significantly reduced in melatonin-treated plants, approaching those observed in non-stressed controls (T0-NS), particularly at 25 and 50 µM. In T25 and T50 treatments, leaf phenol content decreased by 34% and 28%, respectively, compared to T0. Proline accumulation in leaves was observed across all treatments under saline conditions when compared to non-salinized controls, as shown in Table 1. This trend is consistent with the findings of Rouphael et al. [27]. Salt-stressed plants without melatonin application (T0) exhibited significantly higher proline levels than the non-stressed control (T0-NS). However, melatonin treatments at 25 and 50 µM significantly reduced leaf proline content by 25% and 28%, respectively, compared to T0.

The effect of salinity on lipid membrane peroxidation was assessed via malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration. As shown in Table 2, melatonin application markedly reduced lipid peroxidation in basil leaves compared to untreated salt-stressed plants (T0). Specifically, T25 and T50 treatments led to 33–36% reductions in MDA levels relative to T0. In contrast, no significant differences in MDA content were detected in root tissues (Table 2).

With regard to stress-related enzymatic activity, both peroxidase (POX) and catalase (CAT) activities were significantly higher in roots than in leaves across all treatments and controls (Table 2). In basil leaves, CAT activity increased significantly in response to melatonin treatment, reaching 1.95-fold and 2.34-fold increases with 50 and 100 µM doses, respectively, compared to the salt-stressed control (T0). In contrast, no such response was observed in roots: melatonin treatments led to a reduction in CAT activity relative to T0, with values not significantly different from those of the non-stressed control (T0-NS) at 25, 50, and 100 µM.

Concerning foliar POX activity, salinity induced a 26% increase in T0 compared to T0-NS. However, melatonin treatments at 12.5 and 25 µM resulted in 61–62% lower POX activity relative to T0. Interestingly, treatment with 50 µM melatonin led to a significant increase in POX activity—approximately 25% higher than T0 and 58% higher than T0-NS. In roots, POX activity was also enhanced under T50, with increases of 22% and 11% relative to T0 and T0-NS, respectively (Table 2).

Generalized Procrustes Analysis (GPA) was performed to evaluate the consensus between variables measured in leaves (namely, total chlorophyll, fresh weight, proline, MDA, catalase, peroxidase, and total phenols) and those measured in roots, which included all of the above except chlorophyll. The first three principal components (PC1, PC2, and PC3) accounted for 86.5% of the total variance. The consensus between the configurations derived from leaf and root variables reached 97.3% (Supplementary Material), indicating a strong correspondence and suggesting potential correlations between the physiological and biochemical responses observed in both organs of basil plants.

Discussion

Melatonin has emerged as a non-toxic regulatory biomolecule capable of modulating plant growth and enhancing stress tolerance mechanisms [28]. Similar to auxins, it promotes growth in several economically important crops, including wheat, barley, rice, soybean, maize, tomato, pepper, and cucumber, as well as in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana [29]. Its beneficial role under salinity, where productivity is typically compromised, has been consistently documented across diverse plant species [28, 30–34]. In addition, in Thymus vulgaris, a member of the same family, foliar melatonin application significantly improved growth, biomass, and antioxidant traits under water deficit conditions [35]. This provides additional evidence of the relevance of melatonin as a biostimulant across aromatic herbs within the Lamiaceae family.

In the present study, melatonin treatment significantly enhanced root biomass under salt stress (Fig. 1), supporting previous findings that link root development with improved stress resilience. Exogenous melatonin has also been shown to stimulate the regeneration of lateral and adventitious roots in etiolated hypocotyls of Lupinus albus and seedlings of Brassica juncea [36, 37].

However, the concentration of melatonin applied appears to be a critical factor in determining its effectiveness. Erland et al. [38] reported a bell-shaped dose-response curve in which increasing melatonin concentrations promoted root growth up to a threshold, beyond which higher doses became inhibitory. This dose-dependent response has been shown to vary considerably among species within a concentration range of 10 to 500 µM. For instance, melatonin at 1 µM enhanced root number and length in sweet cherry shoot tip explants, whereas 10 µM inhibited root growth [39]. Similar to indole-3-acetic acid, melatonin exhibits a concentration-dependent regulatory effect on root development, as reported for canary grass and wheat [40, 41].

A comparable pattern was observed in our study: root development peaked at 50 µM, while treatment with 100 µM resulted in significantly lower root biomass, with values statistically similar to those of the T12.5 and T25 treatments. These findings confirm the importance of dose optimization when using melatonin as a biostimulant under stress conditions.

With respect to leaf area, melatonin-treated basil plants in this study exhibited values ranging from 366 cm² (T12.5) to 661 cm² (T50). The reduced leaf area observed in non-treated salt-stressed plants (T0) was even lower than that reported for significantly younger basil plants (25 days old) exposed to varying light intensities for 12–18 h [42]. The positive influence of melatonin on leaf expansion has also been documented in other species; for example, Liang et al. [43] reported that melatonin treatment (100 µM) in drought-stressed kiwifruit seedlings significantly improved leaf area, with increases of approximately 88% relative to untreated controls.

It is well established that gas exchange parameters, such as net photosynthetic rate, stomatal conductance, intercellular CO₂ concentration, and transpiration rate, are markedly reduced under salinity stress. This reduction is attributed to ionic toxicity, dehydration of mesophyll cells, inhibition of carbohydrate metabolism enzymes, and stomatal closure [44]. Stomatal conductance (SC) in particular is highly sensitive to salt stress due to disruptions in water balance and salt accumulation in tissues [26].

However, several studies have shown that melatonin can mitigate these adverse effects by functioning as both an antioxidant and an osmoregulatory agent, thereby contributing to the maintenance of gas exchange processes under stress conditions [9]. Moreover, the improvement in stomatal conductance and chlorophyll retention observed at 50 µM melatonin may be related to its interaction with other phytohormones. Recent evidence shows that melatonin enhances salinity resilience via hormonal crosstalk mechanisms that regulate photosynthesis and water relations [45], possibly through interactions with abscisic acid (ABA), a key hormone in stomatal regulation. Consistent with these findings, our results demonstrated that melatonin application significantly increased SC in basil leaves under salinity stress, with the most pronounced effect observed in the T50 treatment (Fig. 3).

Exogenous application of melatonin has been shown to mitigate growth inhibition and enhance photosynthetic capacity under various stress conditions [9]. For instance, Qiao et al. [46] reported that melatonin-treated winter wheat plants under nitrogen deficiency exhibited increased activities of nitrate reductase and glutamine synthetase, along with improved nitrogen uptake, higher nitrogen content, and greater biomass compared to untreated plants. Enhanced nitrogen absorption and assimilation can contribute to increased chlorophyll accumulation, given the central role of nitrogen in chlorophyll biosynthesis. This, in turn, supports improved photosynthetic efficiency and overall plant development.

In the present study, no significant reductions in total chlorophyll content were observed in salt-stressed basil plants treated with melatonin (Table 1). On the contrary, some treatments, particularly T25, T50, and T100, resulted in significantly higher chlorophyll levels compared to the non-stressed control (T0-NS). These findings align with previous reports, such as those of Wang et al. [31], who demonstrated that 100 µM melatonin alleviated NaCl-induced chlorophyll loss in Cucumis sativus exposed to 200 mM salt stress.

Melatonin may contribute to the preservation of chlorophyll content by delaying leaf senescence and protecting chlorophyll from degradation. Several mechanisms have been proposed, including the stimulation of de novo chlorophyll biosynthesis and the downregulation of chlorophyll degradation pathways. For example, melatonin has been shown to modulate the expression of genes associated with chlorophyll degradation (e.g., NYC1, NOL, CLH, PPH, and PAO in broccoli) and biosynthesis (e.g., POR, CAO, and CHLG in tomato) [9]. Moreover, its effect may involve hormonal interactions with abscisic acid and jasmonic acid, which regulate chlorophyll stability and long-term renewal. In Citrullus lanatus exposed to vanadium (V), melatonin not only reduced vanadium accumulation in tissues but also induced the expression of genes involved in chlorophyll synthesis, such as Cla018095 [47, 48].

Phenol compounds are secondary metabolites that play a critical role in plant defense, particularly through their ability to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) [49]. Under salinity and other abiotic stresses, ROS production often exceeds the capacity of enzymatic antioxidant systems. Major sources of ROS in plant cells include electron leakage from the photosynthetic and respiratory electron transport chains, redox cycling, and the decompartmentalization of transition metal ions [50]. To mitigate oxidative damage, plants synthesize and accumulate non-enzymatic antioxidants, among which phenol compounds are well documented for their ability to neutralize free radicals [51, 52].

In the present study, phenol content in basil leaves increased markedly under salt stress (T0), confirming their role as key components of the antioxidative response (Table 1).

However, melatonin treatments attenuated this increase in a dose-dependent manner, suggesting that melatonin may reduce the need for phenol content overaccumulation by modulating oxidative stress more efficiently. These results contrast with those reported by Bahcesular et al. [52], who observed an increase in flavonoids and phenolic acids following seed treatment with 10 µM melatonin under high salinity in O. basilicum. Such discrepancies may reflect differences in genotype, stress severity, and the timing or method of melatonin application [53]. Interestingly, Dabaghkar et al. [54] reported that in Satureja rechingeri, melatonin enhanced the accumulation of rosmarinic acid and carvacrol under drought stress. Although this trend differs from our observation of reduced total phenol content, it highlights the capacity of melatonin to reprogram secondary metabolism, either limiting unspecific phenol compounds overaccumulation or promoting the synthesis of selected antioxidant compounds depending on species and stress context.

Proline is a multifunctional amino acid that plays a pivotal role in plant stress responses, acting as an osmoregulator, stabilizer of subcellular structures, and scavenger of ROS [55, 56]. For instance, in basil subjected to moderate and severe water deficit (60% and 40% field capacity), proline content increased linearly with stress intensity [57]. Among the accessions tested, ‘Italian Genovese’, ‘Particolored’, and ‘Red Rubin’ showed the highest proline accumulation under severe and moderate water stress, respectively [57].

In the present study, the leaves of salt-stressed basil plants accumulated more proline compared to the non-stressed control (T0-NS), as expected. However, melatonin treatments significantly reduced proline content under stress (Table 1), especially at 25 and 50 µM. This suggests that melatonin may have alleviated the stress burden, potentially lowering the need for high proline accumulation. Moreover, this decrease could represent an energy-saving adaptation, whereby melatonin-treated plants redirect resources from osmoprotectant synthesis to growth and recovery processes [58]. Although proline also contributes to root membrane integrity and functions as a stress-responsive antioxidant [59], we did not observe significant differences in root proline content among treatments.

Siddiqui et al. [60] reported that melatonin enhanced the expression of Δ¹-pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthetase (P5CS), leading to increased proline levels in salt-stressed tomato seedlings. This suggests that melatonin modulates proline metabolism in a context-dependent manner, potentially balancing osmotic adjustment with growth maintenance. In our case, the observed reduction in proline levels in leaves may reflect improved water uptake and photosynthetic efficiency in melatonin-treated plants under saline conditions.

Lipid peroxidation, triggered by the excessive accumulation of ROS, compromises membrane integrity and fluidity, ultimately impairing cellular function [5]. In the present study, malondialdehyde (MDA) content, an indicator of membrane lipid peroxidation, was significantly reduced by melatonin application under salinity stress (Table 2). These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that melatonin mitigates oxidative membrane damage by limiting lipid peroxidation and preserving membrane stability [33]. For example, Jiang et al. [61] showed that exogenous melatonin (1 µM) significantly reduced MDA levels and electrolyte leakage in salt-stressed maize plants. Similarly, in Brassica napus, the application of 1 µM melatonin decreased lipid peroxidation and hydrogen peroxide levels while maintaining redox and ionic homeostasis under saline conditions [62].

Plants rely on complex antioxidant systems to mitigate oxidative damage through both enzymatic and non-enzymatic mechanisms [63]. Melatonin contributes to ROS detoxification across multiple cellular compartments by enhancing the activity of key antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POX), glutathione reductase (GR), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX), as well as increasing levels of non-enzymatic antioxidants such as ascorbic acid, carotenoids, and glutathione [9, 34].

The present study supports this antioxidant role of melatonin, as evidenced by the significant modulation of CAT and POX activities under salinity stress (Table 2). Our findings align with those of Oloumi et al. [64], who reported increased CAT activity in O. basilicum following melatonin treatment under copper and zinc-induced stress conditions.

In the current context, the improved growth performance observed in melatonin-treated basil plants may be partially attributed to the activation of antioxidant defense mechanisms, particularly the enhancement of CAT and POX activities. The concurrent reduction in MDA content further indicates a lower degree of oxidative damage, supporting the hypothesis that melatonin alleviates salt-induced stress by modulating ROS levels and improving the cellular redox balance.

Beyond the physiological and biochemical improvements induced by melatonin, one of the main contributions of this study lies in the validation of a practical, pre-harvest root immersion protocol as a delivery method. This approach proved effective in modulating plant responses under saline conditions without requiring foliar application or repeated treatments, which can be labor-intensive or environmentally sensitive. Furthermore, the dose–response pattern identified in this study reinforces the importance of optimizing melatonin concentrations to achieve desirable agronomic outcomes. Notably, the observed divergence in antioxidant responses between leaves and roots, despite the high degree of overall phenotypic consensus, highlights the spatial complexity of stress adaptation mechanisms in plants. These findings suggest that integrating organ-specific analyses may be essential to fully understand the action of phytoregulators like melatonin and to design targeted strategies for crop stress management.

Conclusions

Pre-harvest application of melatonin, particularly at 50 µM, significantly improved basil growth and quality under salinity, enhancing biomass, leaf area, chlorophyll content, stomatal conductance, and redox balance. The root immersion method proved to be a simple, cost-effective, and practical delivery strategy. While leaf and root responses diverged in their antioxidant enzyme activities, pointing to the need for further research on superoxide dismutase, ascorbate peroxidase, glutathione reductase, and glutathione, our results highlight melatonin as a novel and promising tool to enhance basil performance and stress tolerance in salt-affected systems.
