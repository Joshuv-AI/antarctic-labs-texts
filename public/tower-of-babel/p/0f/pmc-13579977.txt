Differences in gut microbiota composition between ischemic stroke patients and healthy individuals
Authors: Mohammadreza Movahedi, Somayeh Ahmadi, Sama Rahnemayan, Kaveh Mehrvar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesIschemic stroke is a leading cause of mortality and disability worldwide. Emerging evidence suggests that the gut microbiota, acting through the gut-brain axis, may play a critical role in stroke pathophysiology. Dysbiosis, an imbalance of gut microbiota, has been associated with neuroinflammation and poor stroke outcomes. This study aimed to compare gut microbiota composition in ischemic stroke patients versus healthy controls to explore potential microbial changes linked to stroke.MethodsA case-control study was conducted on 30 ischemic stroke patients and 30 healthy age- and gender-matched controls. Fecal samples were analyzed using real-time PCR to assess the relative abundance of four key gut bacterial phyla (Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria). Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS, and results were considered significant at p < 0.05.ResultsIschemic stroke patients exhibited significant alterations in gut microbiota composition compared to controls. Firmicutes levels were significantly decreased (p = 0.04), while Proteobacteria levels were significantly elevated (p = 0.003). Although reductions in Bacteroidetes and Actinobacteria were observed, these differences were not statistically significant. The Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio was increased in ischemic stroke patients, indicating dysbiosis associated with stroke.ConclusionsThe study identified gut microbiota dysbiosis in ischemic stroke patients, suggesting its potential role in stroke pathophysiology. Reduced Firmicutes and increased Proteobacteria may contribute to inflammation and oxidative stress, potentially worsening stroke outcomes. These findings highlight the potential for gut microbiota modulation as a therapeutic strategy to improve stroke management and recovery.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, Gut microbiota, Dysbiosis, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria

Introduction

Stroke is characterized by the abrupt emergence of localized—or less commonly, generalized—neurological deficits that persist for more than 24 h or result in death, without any cause other than a vascular event [1]. In 2021, the definition was updated to include cell death within the brain, spinal cord, or retina due to ischemia, confirmed through pathological, imaging, or clinical evidence [2]. As a major global health burden, stroke is generally divided into ischemic stroke (IS) and hemorrhagic stroke (HS), with IS representing the predominant form. IS typically results from arterial occlusion that limits blood supply to brain tissue. Worldwide, IS impacts an estimated 24.9 million individuals annually, contributing substantially to disability and mortality [3, 4]. In 2019 alone, stroke led to approximately 6.55 million deaths and accounted for 143 million years of life lost due to premature death and disability [5].

Multiple risk factors predispose individuals to IS, falling into two main categories: nonmodifiable factors such as age, sex, and ethnicity, and modifiable factors including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disorders, smoking, poor diet, and insufficient physical activity [6, 7]. Among modifiable risks, dietary patterns are notable not only for their direct influence on stroke incidence but also for their impact on gut microbiota—an aspect increasingly recognized as relevant to stroke pathophysiology [8, 9].

The gut microbiota is a complex and dynamic ecosystem, mainly comprising four dominant bacterial phyla: Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria [10]. These microorganisms are integral to several physiological processes, including immune regulation, maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity, carbohydrate metabolism, and production of bioactive metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [11, 12]. Disruption of the normal microbial balance—a condition known as dysbiosis—has been associated with heightened inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune dysfunction, all of which are implicated in the development and progression of stroke (Fig. 1) [13].

Bidirectional interactions of the gut-brain axis via endocrine, nerval, and immune pathways in response to microbial dysbiosis

Communication between the gut and brain occurs through the bidirectional gut–brain axis (GBA), influenced by microbial metabolites such as SCFAs and trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO), bacterial endotoxins like lipopolysaccharides (LPS), and immune cell activity [14]. Aberrant signaling from the gut to the brain can drive neuroinflammation, while neural circuits and the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis allow the brain to modulate gut homeostasis [15]. Experimental animal studies have demonstrated that ischemic stroke disrupts the gut microbiome’s structure, compromises gut barrier function, and exacerbates dysbiosis, which may worsen clinical outcomes [16, 17]. Research using germ-free mice further indicates that the absence of gut microbiota is associated with reduced neuroinflammation and improved recovery, suggesting that microbial alterations may play a causative role in stroke pathogenesis rather than merely representing a secondary consequence [18].

Despite increasing evidence linking gut microbiota to stroke, the precise alterations in microbial composition among patients with IS are not yet fully defined. The present study seeks to characterize and compare the core gut microbiota in individuals with ischemic stroke and healthy controls, aiming to clarify its potential involvement in disease mechanisms.

Methods

Study design and participants

This case–control investigation enrolled 60 participants in total—30 individuals diagnosed with ischemic stroke and 30 healthy volunteers. Stool specimens from healthy controls were collected at the Azar Salamat Provincial Medical Diagnostic Laboratory from individuals with no prior stroke history, while patient samples were obtained from ischemic stroke cases admitted to the Neurology and Stroke Care Unit (SCU) of Imam Reza Hospital in Tabriz between April and June 2024. Eligibility for controls required an age above 18 years and absence of previous ischemic stroke. Patients in the ischemic stroke group were over 18 years old, with diagnoses established through clinical evaluation and confirmed via imaging studies.

Participants from both groups were excluded if they had a history of chronic gastrointestinal illness; had taken antibiotics, probiotics, or prebiotics in the preceding two months; or had conditions such as opioid use, digestive disorders, alcohol or substance dependence, diabetes, cancer, organ transplantation, or active infectious diseases at diagnosis. Pregnancy, corticosteroid therapy, and a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 were also exclusionary. Age and sex were matched between groups to reduce bias.

Stool collection and DNA extraction

Fresh fecal samples were obtained aseptically from each participant, promptly frozen at − 80 °C to preserve DNA quality, and stored until analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted using the Sambio™ Stool DNA Extraction Kit (Azma Gene, Iran), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA yield and purity were measured via UV spectrophotometry (NanoDrop, Epoch, BioTek, USA). For subsequent real-time PCR experiments, concentrations were normalized to 30 ng/µL. All DNA aliquots were preserved at − 20 °C until microbiota profiling.

Real-time PCR quantification of gut microbiota

The bacterial community composition was determined by assessing the relative abundance of predominant intestinal phyla through quantitative real-time PCR. Relative quantitation followed the Livak method [20], employing primers specific to each target phylum (Table 1). Amplification reactions were set up using SYBR Green Master Mix without ROX (Amplicon, Denmark) in 72-well strips, run on a Rotor-Gene RG-6000 Real-Time PCR Analyzer (Corbett, Australia).

Each reaction had a total volume of 20 µL, comprised of 1 µL forward primer, 1 µL reverse primer, 1 µL of template DNA (30 ng/µL), 10 µL SYBR Green Master Mix, and 8 µL nuclease-free water. The thermocycler program began with a 15-minute hold at 95 °C to activate Taq polymerase, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation (95 °C, 20 s), annealing (60 °C, 20 s), and extension (72 °C, 30 s). A melt curve step at 72 °C for 10 min was conducted to verify product specificity. Negative control reactions without template DNA were included in each run to detect any contamination.

Statistical analysis

All statistical procedures were conducted using SPSS (version 20). Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Depending on whether the data were normally distributed, comparisons of bacterial phyla abundance between groups were performed using either the independent-samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Figures and graphs were produced using GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.1).

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of participants

Table 2 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the study participants. The ischemic stroke group had a mean age of 67.33 ± 12 years, whereas the control participants averaged 51.10 ± 16 years. An independent-samples t-test confirmed that this age difference was statistically significant (p < 0.05). Gender distribution was identical in both cohorts, with 15 men and 15 women per group, minimizing the possibility of sex-related bias in gut microbiota comparisons.

Gut Microbiota composition analysis by real-time PCR

Quantitative analysis using real-time PCR was performed to measure the relative abundance of major bacterial phyla in both cohorts. Marked compositional differences emerged between ischemic stroke patients and healthy individuals, as depicted in Fig. 2.

Figure 1 shows that Firmicutes levels were significantly reduced among stroke patients compared to controls (p = 0.04). In contrast, Proteobacteria were significantly enriched in the stroke group (p = 0.003). While downward trends were also observed for Bacteroidetes and Actinobacteria in stroke patients, these differences did not meet statistical significance (p > 0.05). The non-significant p-values for these two phyla indicate that their relative abundances were not meaningfully altered between patient and control groups.

Multivariable linear regression analysis

To address potential confounding, multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted with adjustment for age, sex, BMI, and hypertension. Following adjustment, the association between Firmicutes abundance and ischemic stroke was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (β = 0.578, p = 0.186). Likewise, no significant associations were observed for Bacteroidetes (β = 0.63, p = 0.09) or Actinobacteria (β = −0.295, p = 0.612). In contrast, the association between Proteobacteria abundance and ischemic stroke remained significant after adjustment (β = −4.762, p = 0.021), suggesting that this relationship was not fully explained by differences in age, sex, BMI, or hypertension between the study groups (Table 3).

Discussion

This investigation examined differences in the gut microbiota profile between individuals with ischemic stroke and healthy counterparts. The pathogenesis of ischemic stroke is characterized by a multifaceted interaction of neuropathophysiological and neuropathological mechanisms, which include tau protein abnormalities, excitotoxic injury, neuroinflammatory cascades, oxidative stress, amyloid buildup, and programmed cell death [21]. Recent studies have drawn attention to the gut microbiome as both a potential contributor to stroke susceptibility and a determinant in its course and recovery [22]. Growing evidence suggests that gut microbial dynamics can influence stroke risk, outcomes, and therapeutic responses, while stroke itself can disrupt intestinal microbial communities [23]. Nonetheless, full-scale characterization of these microbial changes in stroke remains an area of ongoing inquiry [24].

In this work, real-time PCR data identified marked microbial dysbiosis by day eight following stroke onset. Compared with healthy control samples, stroke patients demonstrated a pronounced drop in Firmicutes representation and a significant rise in Proteobacteria. Although reductions in Bacteroidetes and Actinobacteria were also recorded, they did not reach statistical significance.

Higher concentrations of lipopolysaccharides (LPS) — a hallmark component of Gram-negative bacteria — have been associated with worsening cerebral edema, breakdown of the blood–brain barrier, and heightened neuroinflammatory responses, all of which can aggravate neurological deficits and hinder recovery [25]. Perturbations in gut microbial balance may also modulate circulating bacterial amyloids and metabolic byproducts, potentially facilitating post-ischemic neurodegeneration. Meta-analytic data indicate that gut-derived metabolites can intensify neuroinflammation and promote tau and amyloid protein accumulation, pathological features shared with several neurodegenerative diseases [26].

The observed pattern of reduced Firmicutes alongside increased Proteobacteria is consistent with prior literature, including the findings of Li et al., who reported similar alterations in ischemic stroke patients, along with decreased Bacteroidetes [27]. Elevated Proteobacteria levels have also been documented in a range of pathological states, stroke among them [28, 29].

Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes are widely recognized for their capacity to produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) — particularly butyrate — which possess anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective functions [30]. Supplementation with butyric acid has been shown to alleviate intestinal leakage and improve functional outcomes in cerebral ischemia. A diminished population of butyrate-producing microbes may be associated with mechanisms involved in ischemic stroke progression [31]; however, the present findings do not establish a causal relationship.

In contrast, many members of the Proteobacteria phylum, which include genera such as Escherichia, Salmonella, Helicobacter, and Yersinia, are pathogenic and can trigger intense intestinal and systemic inflammation [32]. The elevated levels of Proteobacteria detected in stroke patients may thus signal a pro-inflammatory milieu and increased vulnerability to secondary infections.

We also noted a declining trend in the Actinobacteria phylum among stroke patients. Although these microbes are less prevalent numerically, they are integral to gut homeostasis, immune modulation, metabolic regulation, intestinal barrier integrity, and bidirectional gut–brain communication [33, 34]. Shifts in Actinobacteria abundance could therefore disrupt these physiological processes in the post-stroke period.

Our patient cohort exhibited an altered Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio, adding another dimension to the observed dysbiosis. While some neurological diseases are linked to a reduced F/B ratio [35], several studies — including those on ischemic stroke — have reported the opposite pattern [19, 31, 36–39]. Importantly, discrepancies between studies may partly reflect differences in sample collection timing. For example, one investigation found the F/B ratio increased by day seven after stroke onset [40], whereas our analysis focused on day eight.

Animal models further illustrate that microbiota shifts are highly time-dependent in the acute stage of ischemia. In a porcine stroke model, fecal analysis showed a 27% drop in Firmicutes and a 19-fold surge in Proteobacteria three days post-stroke, both of which reverted almost to baseline by day five. Actinobacteria peaked on the third day before returning to pre-stroke levels, and Bacteroidetes displayed a modest upward trend by day three, remaining relatively constant thereafter [41]. Such findings emphasize that the timing of stool sample collection is critical when evaluating gut microbial disturbances during acute ischemic events.

Association between gut microbiota phyla and ischemic stroke after adjustment for potential confounders

between ischemic stroke patients and healthy controls were no longer significant after adjustment for age, sex, BMI, and hypertension. This suggests that the initially observed association may have been influenced, at least in part, by differences in demographic and clinical characteristics between the study groups. Given the substantial age difference between stroke patients and controls, adjustment for potential confounders was essential to better evaluate the independent relationship between gut microbiota composition and ischemic stroke.

In contrast, the association between Proteobacteria abundance and ischemic stroke remained statistically significant after multivariable adjustment. This finding suggests that alterations in Proteobacteria may be independently associated with ischemic stroke and are not fully explained by differences in age, sex, BMI, or hypertension. However, given the observational nature of the study, no causal inferences can be drawn.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The relatively small sample size limits the statistical power and generalizability of the findings and may have reduced the ability to detect modest associations for some bacterial phyla. Additionally, although multivariable analyses were performed to adjust for potential confounding factors, the significant age difference between ischemic stroke patients and healthy controls may still have influenced gut microbiota composition, and residual confounding by unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. Furthermore, the assessment of gut microbiota was limited to four major bacterial phyla using qPCR, which restricted the taxonomic resolution and may not fully capture the complexity of microbial alterations associated with ischemic stroke. Future studies should address these limitations by including larger, age-matched cohorts and employing comprehensive microbiome profiling approaches to improve statistical reliability and provide a more detailed understanding of the relationship between gut microbiota and ischemic stroke. Longitudinal and multi-omics studies are also warranted to further elucidate the dynamic interplay between gut microbiota and ischemic stroke.

Conclusion

This study highlights significant dysbiosis in the gut microbiota composition of ischemic stroke patients on the eighth post-stroke day, underscoring the potential role of gut microbiota in modulating inflammatory responses and recovery processes. These alterations in gut microbiota may provide novel therapeutic targets for the prevention and treatment of stroke.
