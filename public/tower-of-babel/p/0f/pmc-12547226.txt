Review: Factors affecting the growth performance of domestic squab
Authors: Yan Zhang, Seong-ho Choi, Shuang Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs living standards have risen, the popularity of pigeon meat has increased because of its high protein content, low fat content, and soft texture. Accordingly, pigeons are the fourth most popular type of poultry in China. More than 1.6 billion squabs from 111 million pairs of breeding pigeons were processed in 2021. Increasing the growth performance of squabs is crucial to industry development, efficiency, and economic benefits. Squab growth is influenced by genetics, nutrition, disease prevention, environmental conditions, and the gut microbiota, all of which collectively affect squab health. This review examines the effects of these factors on squab growth and provides theoretical support and practical guidance for breeding and research. The development of skeletal muscle is crucial for determining growth potential. Previous studies on genetic regulation have pinpointed genes and microRNAs (miRNAs) associated with the growth of skeletal muscles in squabs. Molecular breeding technologies can target these genetic elements to increase squab growth potential. Adequate nutrition is crucial for squab growth, and pigeon milk provides essential nutrients and immune support; however, feed type, nutritional balance, and feeding method also significantly affect the growth of squabs. Therefore, it is vital to optimize a feeding plan. Understanding disease symptoms and implementing preventive measures are essential to healthy squab growth. Environmental conditions such as temperature, humidity, ventilation, lighting, and stocking density also play significant roles in reducing stress and disease and supporting healthy growth. Moreover, the gut microbial balance, which is influenced by probiotics and functional feeds, is critical to nutrient absorption and immune function. Despite existing research on these factors, their relationships and combined effects on growth require further study; exploring these complex interactions will provide a foundation for improved squab farming.Keywords: Squab growth performance, Nutrition, Genetic regulation, Disease prevention, Gut microbiota

Introduction

Globally, pigeons (Columba livia) are highly valued due to the rich nutritional content of their meat and eggs. Pigeon meat is renowned for its high protein content, low fat content, and tender texture, and it has an estimated digestibility rate as high as 97 % (Liu et al., 2023). As living standards have improved, the demand for pigeons has increased steadily. In China, pigeons have become the fourth most popular poultry product after chickens, ducks, and geese. To meet the growing market demand, the number of pigeons raised for meat has increased, and China’s current production of meat pigeons accounts for more than 80 % of the global total, making it the world’s largest producer of meat pigeons (Kokoszyński, et al., 2020).

The growth performance of squabs directly affects their breeding benefits and meat quality. Despite advancements in modern squab breeding technology, growth is influenced by a combination of factors that cause considerable differences in growth rates, health, and final production performance. Therefore, thoroughly investigating the factors that influence the growth performance of squabs is vital. This research will enable the optimization of breeding and improve production efficiency, thus ensuring squab health and promoting the sustainable development of the squab breeding industry.

The growth performance of squabs is influenced by genetics, nutrition, disease, the environment, and the gut microbiota. Genetic factors determine the growth potential of squabs, and good variety selection serves as the cornerstone for enhancing growth performance (Ye, et al., 2018). Breast muscle comprises up to 30 % of a pigeon’s slaughter weight and is a key growth performance indicator (Kokoszyński et al., 2020). The development of this muscle determines the quality of pigeon meat. Research on the molecular regulation of this process is critical for promoting the development of molecular breeding technology in the pigeon industry and improving the growth performance of squabs. Through molecular breeding technology, targeted regulation of key candidate genes or microRNAs (miRNAs) can improve meat development in pigeons and provide a basis for poultry production. Nutrition is fundamental to squab growth, and in addition to pigeon milk, the type of feed, nutritional balance, and feeding method affect the growth rate and health status of squabs (Peng, et al., 2023; Xiao, et al., 2024; Zhang, et al., 2022a). Disease is a serious threat to the growth performance of squabs (Santos, et al., 2020b). Once an outbreak occurs, whether it is viral, bacterial, or parasitic, it not only directly affects the growth and development of squabs but can also cause mass mortality and economic losses to breeders. Suitable environmental conditions, such as temperature, humidity, ventilation, lighting, and breeding density, are critical for providing squabs with a comfortable living space (Mohamed, et al., 2025; Zhang, et al., 2023), which helps reduce stress, decreases the risk of disease occurrence, and ensures normal growth. Gut microbial communities, breeding management measures (Chen, et al., 2023; Xu, et al., 2024b), and the effective control of stress factors (Xu, et al., 2022a) also affect the growth performance of squabs. As the scale of squab breeding has expanded and market competition has intensified, in-depth research on the factors affecting the growth performance of squabs is necessary to develop an efficient breeding program for meat pigeons.

The goal of this review is to provide a comprehensive analysis of the factors that influence the growth performance of squabs. We systematically examine the genetic determinants of breast muscle development, nutritional supply, disease prevention and control strategies, environmental management , and gut microbiota colonization. On the basis of research in these key areas, this review provides theoretical and practical guidance for pigeon farmers and researchers and supports the development and optimization of the pigeon farming industry.

Genetic factors

The development of skeletal muscle is the most important element of meat performance in pigeons. Research on the molecular regulation of this process is critical to the development of molecular breeding technology in the pigeon industry and improving the growth performance of squabs. However, compared with research on other types of poultry, research on the genetic regulation of skeletal muscle growth and development in pigeons has been limited.

Breast muscle is a key indicator of the meat production performance of pigeons. Through selection signal-based transcriptomic analysis, researchers have identified several candidate genes associated with pigeon breast muscle development. Spindle orientation adaptor protein (INSC) and calcitonin-related polypeptide beta (CALCB) genes are directly associated with the growth and development of pigeon breast muscles, whereas the spectrin repeat-containing nuclear envelope protein 1 (SYNE1) gene is closely related to muscle cell differentiation (Hou, et al., 2021). For the myogenic factor 5 (MYF5) gene, the C5084T and T5127A single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) genotypes were significantly associated with the carcass traits of pigeons (Yin, et al., 2016). The T91G mutation in the melanocortin-3 receptor (MC3R) gene and the A903G mutation in the melanocortin-4 receptor (MC4R) gene significantly impact the growth traits of Shiqi meat pigeons, with these genes also showing a synergistic effect on body weight regulation (Li, et al., 2008). Additionally, genes such as ATP-binding cassette, subfamily A (ABC1), member 8b (Abca8b), von Willebrand factor (VWF), 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase (OGDH), TGF-beta induced factor homeobox 1 (TGIF1), Dickkopf homolog 3 (DKK3), glutamine-fructose-6-phosphate transaminase 1 (Gfpt1), and replication factor C 5 (RFC5), which encode key mRNAs, play crucial roles in skeletal muscle growth and development (Ding et al., 2021a). Moreover, the myostatin (MSTN) gene is significantly more highly expressed in pigeon breast muscle than in other tissues, and its expression level increases with age (Liu, et al., 2019). In a group of edible pigeons characterized by high muscle mass and a high weight-to-muscle mass ratio, the frequency of the MSTN minor allele (MSTNT) is the highest (Dybus, et al., 2013), which suggests that this gene plays a vital regulatory role in the development of pigeon breast muscle. miRNAs are noncoding endogenous RNAs that are approximately 22 nucleotides in length. They regulate gene expression by binding to the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) of target genes and are involved in numerous biological processes, including growth and development (Xia, et al., 2020). Recent studies have highlighted the crucial regulatory role of miRNAs in skeletal muscle development. For example, miR-206 is involved in muscle development, growth, regeneration, and disease regulation (Ma, et al., 2015); Chen et al. reported that miR-1 and miR-133 regulate skeletal muscle cell proliferation and differentiation (Chen, et al., 2006), and Kong et al. confirmed that miR-17 and miR-19 jointly promote skeletal muscle cell differentiation (Kong, et al., 2019). Nevertheless, relatively few studies have investigated the regulation of the growth and development of pigeon skeletal muscle by miRNAs. Next-generation sequencing technology has been used recently to analyze the dynamic expression of miRNAs and mRNAs; 20 miRNAs related to the development and growth of pigeon skeletal muscle have been identified, including cli-miR-20b-5p, miR-130-y, cli-miR-106-5p, cli-miR-181b-5p, miR-1-z, cli-miR-1a-3p, miR-23-y, cli-miR-30d-5p, and miR-1-y (Ding, et al., 2022).

These studies provide a solid foundation for understanding the molecular regulation of pigeon skeletal muscle growth and highlight the potential of using molecular breeding techniques to target key genes and miRNAs. These techniques could increase pigeon meat production and provide strong scientific support for the poultry industry. Future research should focus on verifying the specific functions of these candidate genes and miRNAs in skeletal muscle development through gene knockout, knock-in, and overexpression experiments. Additionally, integrating metabolomics and proteomics may clarify the underlying mechanisms and interaction networks and provide a comprehensive understanding of skeletal muscle development. Expanding this research to other poultry species may open more possibilities for poultry breeding and production.

Nutritional factors

The growth of squabs depends on crude protein (CP), metabolic energy (ME), and a variety of minerals, vitamins, amino acids, fats, and carbohydrates. These nutrients work together to support skeletal development, immune function, energy metabolism, and the overall health of squabs (Cheng, et al., 2025; Ji, et al., 2022; Peng et al., 2023). A careful balance of nutrients in the feed supports optimal squab growth and health.

Pigeon milk

Pigeon milk, also known as crop milk, is a highly nutritious and biologically active substance secreted from the crops of male and female pigeons to nourish their squabs. This unique secretion is essential for the growth and development of squabs because young pigeons are altricial and cannot feed themselves immediately after hatching. They rely entirely on pigeon milk, which is delivered mouth-to-mouth by parent pigeons. As shown in Table 1, during early lactation, pigeon milk (on a dry matter basis) contains high levels of protein (52.6–64 %) (Hu, et al., 2016; Jin, et al., 2023; Shao, et al., 2021) and fat (30–36 %) (Shao et al., 2021; Xie, et al., 2019) and low concentrations of carbohydrates (1–3 %) (Ding, et al., 2021b), essential minerals (5–6 %), and vitamins (Wang, et al., 2023). These components collectively support rapid growth and tissue development in squabs and provide the necessary energy and building blocks. Moreover, pigeon milk contains antibodies and immune-active components that improve the disease resistance of squabs. Pigeon milk also contains a diverse microbial community, which helps establish a gut microbiota that promotes nutrient absorption and gut health (Ding et al., 2021b). The presence of desquamated and sloughed crop epithelial cells in pigeon milk further supports squab growth. In summary, pigeon milk is a crucial nutritional source for squabs, and its quality is a major factor that influences the growth and development of squabs. It is rich in nutrients, promotes the gut microbiome, and has immune-enhancing and antioxidant properties, all of which highlight the importance of pigeon milk in the early life of squabs.

Crop Milk Protein. Pigeon milk is rich in 17 amino acids. During the first three days of lactation, glutamic acid (Glu, 14.96 %) and aspartic acid (Asp, 9.7 %) are particularly abundant, whereas methionine (Met, 1.07), histidine (His, 0.82), and proline (Pro, 0.67) are present at relatively low concentrations (Jin et al., 2023). After the first three days of lactation, the levels of Glu and Asp decrease, and the level of Met decreases from 1.07 to 0.77 % between four and six days (Zeng and Zhang, 2003). Other amino acids, such as valine (Val), serine (Ser), glycine (Gly), alanine (Ala), phenylalanine (Phe), His, and lysine (Lys), are also significantly reduced by day 6 (Jin et al., 2023). During the first 14 days of secretion, essential amino acids (EAAs) make up 57.7–59.97 % of the total amino acids (Zhang, et al., 2017), with an EAA to nonessential amino acid (NEAA) ratio of approximately 59:41 in the first week (Jin et al., 2023). However, from Days 1–25, the levels of EAAs and NEAAs in pigeon milk decrease significantly (Xie, et al., 2020). This shift in the amino acid profile reflects a nutritional change in pigeon milk that meets the needs of squabs during their rapid growth phase. These changes provide valuable insight into the optimization of squab nutrition and growth. Additionally, amino acids play multiple roles in immune system regulation, including modulating immune cell activity, promoting cell proliferation, regulating inflammation, and influencing tumor immunity (Munteanu and Schwartz, 2022). High EAA content in pigeon milk not only provides critical nutrients that support rapid growth but also plays a significant role in maintaining immune function. Balanced amino acid intake is essential for the healthy development of squabs.

Like mammalian colostrum, pigeon milk is rich in bioactive components. In the early stage of development, the activity of digestive enzymes in squabs is relatively low. Pigeon milk contains an array of proteases, peptidases, glutamyl transpeptidase, amylase, lipase, alkaline phosphatase, and acid phosphatase (Jin et al., 2023; Wen, et al., 2022). These enzymes play important roles in breaking down proteins, carbohydrates, and fats in pigeon milk and converting nutrients into absorbable amino acids and energy sources. These exogenous enzymes can compensate for insufficiencies in the digestive systems of squabs, promote the efficient utilization of nutrients, and thus drive the growth and development of squabs. Pigeon milk is also rich in immunoglobulins A (IgA), G (IgG), and Y (IgY), which play crucial roles in enhancing the immune system of squabs (Jin et al., 2023). IgA in pigeon milk can be effectively transferred to squabs aged 1–3 days through pinocytosis, which is beneficial to their overall health (Jin et al., 2023). Furthermore, pigeon milk contains ribosomal proteins, keratin, peroxiredoxin, annexin, and heat shock proteins (Shao et al., 2021). These proteins are essential building blocks for tissue development and offer antioxidant protection and support cellular functions. Together, these bioactive compounds increase the ability of squabs to absorb nutrients and improve their resistance to diseases. Notably, the composition of pigeon milk is not static but changes as squabs age. Studies have shown that in the first 7 days, pigeon milk is particularly rich in immunoglobulins and growth factors, which increase the disease resistance of squabs in the early growth stage and reduce mortality during the immature development stage (Jin et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2020).

Crop Milk Fats. Pigeon milk is remarkably rich in fat (30–36 %), which is essential for the rapid growth and development of squabs. Pigeon milk fat is primarily composed of glycerolipids (81.2 %) and phospholipids (12.2 %), with relatively low amounts of cholesterol, cholesterol esters, free fatty acids, diacylglycerols, and monoacylglycerols (Wang et al., 2023). Unsaturated fatty acids account for 73 % of the total fat; oleic acid is the most abundant of these acids (43 %), followed by linoleic and palmitic acids (Jin et al., 2023). These fatty acids are crucial for the structure of the cell membrane, especially in immune cells, as they regulate the fluidity of cell membranes and the composition of lipid rafts, thereby influencing cell signaling and overall function (Pietrzak-Fiećko and Kamelska-Sadowska, 2020; Radzikowska, et al., 2019). ω−3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (ω−3 PUFAs), such as eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), can increase cell membrane fluidity and immune cell activity (Munteanu and Schwartz, 2022). Therefore, the fatty acids in pigeon milk are crucial for maintaining immune balance, reducing inflammatory responses, and preventing immune-related diseases in squabs. Moreover, fats are vital for nutrient absorption and overall health and assist in the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, and K) that are essential for bone and general health (Xie, et al., 2013). Notably, the fat content in pigeon milk increases early in lactation, peaks on day 3, decreases by day 7 (Jin et al., 2023), and decreases further as squabs switch to solid food (Xie, et al., 2023). Understanding the dynamics and biological importance of fat content in pigeon milk provides valuable insight into the nutritional strategies by which birds nurture their young.

Crop Milk Carbohydrates. The carbohydrate content in pigeon milk is relatively low and accounts for 1–3 % of the dry weight. Studies have suggested that a low carbohydrate content in pigeon milk is due to significantly suppressed expression of the glucose transporters sodium-glucose linked transporter 1 (SGLT1) and glucose transporter type 2 (GLUT2) during secretion, which limits glucose uptake in the crop (Lin and Hardie, 2018; Zhu, et al., 2023). Although pigeon milk has a low carbohydrate content, it still provides the energy needed for the rapid metabolism of squabs. Moreover, carbohydrates positively affect immune system development. Certain carbohydrates in pigeon milk, such as glucosamine and galactose, have been shown to stimulate beneficial bacteria and inhibit harmful bacteria through their prebiotic properties (Wang, et al., 2021). These carbohydrates contribute to the enhancement of gut barrier function in squabs and play crucial roles in maintaining the balance of the gut microbiota and promoting overall gut health.

Crop Milk Minerals and Vitamins. Pigeon milk is a highly nutritious substance that contains a diverse array of vitamins and minerals. The mineral composition of pigeon milk is comparable to that of whole milk; it contains calcium (0.81 % to 1.55 %), phosphorus (0.85 % to 1.04 %), potassium (0.62 %), sodium (0.54 % to 0.79 %), magnesium (0.08 %), and trace elements such as iron, zinc, manganese, and copper (Jin et al., 2023). These minerals are crucial for bone development (Murshed, 2018), nerve conduction (Tinawi, 2021), and immune function (Weyh, et al., 2022). Additionally, pigeon milk is rich in vitamin A (which supports vision and immune function) (Amimo, et al., 2022), vitamin C (which is known for its antioxidant properties and immune-boosting effects) (Higgins, et al., 2020), vitamin B2 (which is involved in energy metabolism) (McNulty, et al., 2023), vitamin D (which is important for calcium absorption and bone health) (Latic and Erben, 2020), vitamin E (which provides antioxidant protection) (Higgins, Izadi and Kaviani, 2020), and vitamin K (which is essential for blood clotting and bone health) (Tsugawa and Shiraki, 2020). Collectively, these nutrients facilitate the rapid growth of squabs and bolster their immune systems and disease resistance.

Crop Milk Microbial Communities. Pigeon milk is rich in microbes that improve the growth, intestinal health, and immune function of squabs. These microbial communities include Lactobacillus, Limosilactobacillus, Ligilactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Aeriscardovia (Xu et al., 2024b). The microbial composition varies with the maturity of pigeon milk. For example, Limosilactobacillus and Ligilactobacillus increase in abundance in the later stages of secretion, whereas Bifidobacterium initially increases and then decreases. Aeriscardovia and Lactobacillus are abundant early and decrease over time (Xu et al., 2024b). These genera are well-known gut probiotics that help maintain the gut microbial balance, enhance immune function, aid in nutrient absorption, and reduce inflammation to improve gut health (Jiang, et al., 2023; Luo, et al., 2023; Mekadim, et al., 2019; van Zyl, et al., 2020; Yao, et al., 2021), and they are used in various health applications. Microbial communities in pigeon milk are transmitted to squabs by parental feeding and play a crucial role in establishing and maturing the intestinal microbiota of squabs. Overall, the microbial communities in pigeon milk have profound effects on the growth, intestinal health, and immune function of squabs. They facilitate the digestion and absorption of nutrients and promote healthy growth by regulating the intestinal microbiome and increasing immune function.

Feed composition and systems

The composition of the feed significantly affects the growth performance of squabs by influencing their nutritional intake and micronutrient levels. Healthy development and reproductive efficiency can be promoted by carefully balancing proteins, carbohydrates, fats, vitamins, minerals, and fiber in the feed and tailoring the formula according to the specific growth stage and physiological state of the squabs. Research has indicated that squab growth rates are highest when the energy‒protein ratio in the diet is 12.8 MJ/kg metabolic energy and 18 % CP (Peng et al., 2023). Furthermore, adding 120 mg/kg zinc to the diet of breeding pigeons has been shown to increase the weight of squabs (Shao, et al., 2023). In contrast, micronutrient deficiency, such as riboflavin deficiency, can have an adverse effect on the growth performance of squabs (Zhang, et al., 2024). The growth performance of squabs is also affected by the feed system (Biedermann, et al., 2012). Pigeons prefer whole grains and seeds; therefore, current feed formulas are based on these ingredients, and minerals, vitamins, and other nutrients are added to ensure a balanced diet. A complete formula of pelleted feed can meet the requirements of large-scale, modern pigeon farming. However, its adoption in pigeon farming has been relatively slow because of the unique digestive and physiological characteristics and feeding patterns of pigeons. One study revealed that compared with whole grain plus pelleted feed, complete formula pelleted feed significantly increased the carcass yield and growth hormone levels of squabs and significantly reduced the feed intake, gizzard index, and malondialdehyde concentration (Zhang et al., 2022a). Previous research has also suggested that birds fed whole grains have heavier gizzards than those fed single compound feed does (Xie, et al., 2016), which may be related to the physical properties of whole grains that promote digestive efficiency and nutrient absorption. Although complete formula-based pelleted feed has advantages in terms of enhancing the growth performance of squabs, it faces some challenges in practical applications. Further optimization of the formulation and feeding patterns for complete formula pelleted feed is necessary to meet the growth needs of pigeons and improve its effectiveness in actual production.

In conclusion, nutrients significantly impact the growth performance of squabs. By providing proper feed and using nutritional supplements appropriately, the growth rate, health status, and survival rate of squabs can be improved. In breeding, the feed and use of supplements should be adjusted according to the different growth stages and needs of squabs to achieve the best production results.

Common diseases

Diseases not only severely damage the immune systems of squabs, leading to decreases in their growth rate and immunity, but also increase the risk of secondary infections. Moreover, diseases negatively affect the intestinal health of squabs and hinder the effective absorption of nutrients, which adversely affects squab growth and development. Common diseases and symptoms in squabs.

Rotavirus targets the gastrointestinal system and causes severe diarrhea, dehydration, weight loss, and potentially death (Adamczyk, et al., 2024). It also disrupts the gut microbiota, which increases the risk of secondary bacterial infections (Schoonheere and Zoller, 2024). Preventive measures include vaccination against rotavirus, providing electrolytes and rehydration therapy, and using antibiotics for secondary infections when necessary. Circovirus can affect the immune system and cause weakness, drowsiness, anorexia, poor competitive performance, and diarrhea in squabs (Silva, et al., 2022). Circovirus infection in meat pigeons in China was first reported in Zhejiang Province in 2009, after which the full viral genome was subsequently sequenced (Yu, et al., 2009). Further studies have shown that circovirus is widespread among meat pigeons in eastern and southern China (Wang, et al., 2017; Zhang, et al., 2015). Although there is no vaccine for circovirus, the administration of other vaccines can help mitigate immune system damage. Additionally, the use of probiotics and interferons has been explored as alternative and/or complementary methods to control the effects of circovirus infection (Santos, et al., 2020a; Tsai, et al., 2021). Ensuring a stress-free, clean environment and providing proper nutrition are crucial for supporting the health of squabs. The main symptoms of adenovirus infection include vomiting, diarrhea, rapid weight loss, and listlessness; furthermore, adenovirus infection is often complicated by secondary infections such as those caused by Escherichia coli (Wei, et al., 2025). Reports on pigeon adenovirus are limited, but studies have shown a high prevalence of avian adenoviruses in pigeons, particularly in eastern China, with a positivity rate of 58.54 % in trading markets from 2021 to 2022 (Zhuang, et al., 2023). These findings indicate the widespread presence of adenovirus in most pigeon flocks. Avian pox is a highly contagious viral disease that affects both domestic poultry and wild birds and causes skin lesions and/or diphtheria-like symptoms. Infections cause significant stunted growth in young birds and increase mortality rates (Abd El Hafez, et al., 2021). Effective prevention and control measures include maintaining a clean breeding environment, controlling mosquito populations, and isolating affected squabs immediately. Implementing strict hygiene practices and administering vaccinations are also recommended to limit the spread of this disease. Newcastle disease is caused by the Newcastle disease virus and is a serious concern for poultry worldwide. This virus belongs to the genus Orthoavulavirus in the subfamily Avulavirinae of the family Paramyxoviridae (Amarasinghe, et al., 2019). Squabs infected with Pigeon paramyxovirus type 1 present neurological, respiratory, and digestive symptoms such as depression, neck twisting, ataxia, crouching, paralysis, eyelid edema, diarrhea, and loose green stools (Zhang, et al., 2022b), which significantly impact squab growth. Preventive measures include regular vaccination, isolation of newly introduced pigeons, and maintenance of good biosecurity practices. Additionally, squabs are prone to trichomoniasis (Borji, et al., 2021), candidiasis (Talazadeh, et al., 2023), coccidiosis (Arafa, et al., 2020), and ascariasis (Al Quraishy, et al., 2020). These infections cause abnormal behavior in pigeons and affect their quality of life.

In conclusion, these diseases pose a significant threat to the growth and health of squabs. Implementing vaccination, maintaining good hygiene and breeding conditions, reducing stress factors, and promptly isolating sick squabs can reduce the negative impact of diseases, thereby improving the growth performance and survival rate of squabs.

Environmental management

The growth performance of squabs is significantly influenced by environmental and management factors. Squabs are sensitive to temperature. Newborn squabs require an ambient temperature of 35°C, which should be reduced by 1°C daily to 30°C, maintained until they are fully feathered, and then gradually lowered to stabilize at 22–24°C as their thermoregulatory capabilities mature (Mohamed, Khattab and Elsagheer, 2025). Excessive temperatures can induce heat stress in young pigeons, leading to diminished appetite and stunted growth (El Shoukary and Mousa, 2018). Conversely, when the ambient temperature is too low, young pigeons must expend a significant amount of energy to maintain their body temperature, which can further exacerbate weight loss. These factors collectively contribute to a decline in overall health and growth performance. To mitigate these effects, heating equipment should be utilized in the winter, and ventilation systems should be used in the summer to maintain appropriate temperatures. Humidity also plays a crucial role in squab health. Both high and low humidity can increase the risk of disease in squabs and have adverse effects on skin and feather health. The optimal humidity range for squabs is between 60 % and 70 % (Xu, et al., 2024a), as this environment promotes the best growth performance and minimizes the incidence of respiratory diseases. When the humidity is below this range, the feathers of squabs may become dry and brittle, and their skin may also dry out. Additionally, the amount of dust in the pigeon loft increases, which can easily trigger respiratory diseases. Conversely, when the humidity exceeds the optimal range, squab feathers may become excessively moist, leading to reduced insulation. Therefore, maintaining humidity within the ideal range is crucial for the health and well-being of squabs. Ventilation management is another critical factor affecting squab growth. Proper ventilation can maintain air quality in the pigeon loft, regulate temperature and humidity, and remove harmful gases such as ammonia, thereby promoting the healthy growth of squabs. Insufficient ventilation can lead to ammonia accumulation, leading to respiratory diseases such as aspergillosis, ornithosis, and chronic respiratory disease (Wigle, 2000). To ensure optimal conditions, the air velocity should be maintained at 0.3 m/s for the first two weeks after poultry hatch; it should be increased to 0.5 m/s in the third week, 1 m/s in the fourth week, and above 1 m/s thereafter; and it should be raised to 2.5 m/s in hot weather for adequate cooling (Küçüktopçu, et al., 2024). Hence, appropriate ventilation systems should be installed and regularly maintained on the basis of the size and population of the pigeon loft. Lighting conditions also play a crucial role in the growth of squabs by regulating their physiological rhythms and facilitating the synthesis of vitamin D. To support normal physiological activities, newborn squabs should be provided with 24 hours of continuous light. As they mature, the lighting duration gradually decreases, and the dark period increases. After 7 days, squabs require 12 hours of daily light exposure (Xu et al., 2024a, 2023). Stocking density is another factor that affects squab growth. Maintaining a reasonable stocking density is crucial for reducing competition and stress among squabs, thereby promoting healthy growth. In single-pair caging (50×65×65 cm3) systems, it is advisable for each pair of parent pigeons to rear no more than three milk pigeons (Zhang et al., 2023). This approach ensures that each squab receives adequate nutrition and care, which is essential for optimal development. Proper planning of the pigeon loft to ensure that each squab has enough space can improve the growth performance of the squabs. High-quality feed, clean drinking water, and good hygiene in pigeon houses also reduce the spread of diseases. In summary, optimizing environmental conditions (temperature, humidity, ventilation, lighting, and stocking density) and strengthening management practices related to feeding, breeding, and stress management may improve the growth rate, health status, and survival rate of squabs. In terms of breeding, management measures should be adjusted according to the different growth stages and needs of squabs to achieve the best production results.

Gut microbiota colonization

The gut microbiota engage in complex symbiotic relationships with their hosts, and the initial microbiome exerts a lifelong effect on the host (Wu, et al., 2021). The diversity and composition of microbiota in the gut influence nutrient absorption, immune system development, and overall health in squabs. The gut microbiota is strongly influenced by that of the parent pigeons, and the diversity and composition change dynamically with the growth stage of the squabs. As squabs age, the diversity of their gut microbiota gradually increases, and the quantities of different types of microorganisms also change (Ding et al., 2021b). Traditionally, the avian gut microbiota is established after hatching; because the digestive tracts of healthy newborn birds are sterile, microbial colonization occurs through external contact. However, recent studies have shown that birds also experience prenatal colonization by the gut microbiota via the environment, eggshell, amniotic fluid, or other mechanisms (Akinyemi, et al., 2020; Dai, et al., 2021). In the first few days after birth, the gut microbiota significantly changes (Ding, et al., 2017).

The meconium of newborn pigeons contains diverse microbes that are distinct from those of other fecal groups. In contrast, 7- to 21-day-old pigeons have less varied fecal microbiota. At the phylum level, the feces were shown to be dominated by Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, and Proteobacteria. At the genus level, Lactobacillus, Limosilactobacillus, and Turicibacter are the dominant genera (Xu et al., 2024b). Moreover, studies have shown that the first week after hatching is critical for ileal microbial colonization and that colonization by Escherichia and Lactobacillus is particularly important for the immune response in squabs (Xu, et al., 2022b). Similarly, early weaning increased the richness of the squab ileal microbiota; the relative abundances of Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Cyanobacteria, and Proteobacteria significantly increased; the relative abundance of Firmicutes decreased; and weaned squabs presented a poor growth rate (Xu et al., 2022a).

In early life, the gut microbiota is susceptible to host, environmental, and dietary factors (Schmidt, et al., 2018), and this time is considered to be the window for intervention in gut microbial colonization. Probiotics, which are active bacteria that colonize an animal’s gut and provide health benefits to the host (Salminen, et al., 2021), have been shown to increase nutrient absorption by modulating the gut microbiota and improving the morphology and function of the intestines. The probiotic strain Ligilactobacillus salivarius S10 enhances the growth performance and gut development of young pigeons (Zhao, et al., 2025). Furthermore, the addition of probiotics such as Enterococcus faecalis and Bacillus subtilis to the diet has been shown to significantly increase the disease resistance of squabs (Ma, et al., 2024). Another notable approach is fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), a method that involves transferring the fecal microbiota from a healthy donor to the gut of a recipient. FMT has been demonstrated to significantly promote the growth of squabs by increasing the abundance of beneficial gut bacteria such as Lactobacillus, Megasphaera, and Muribaculaceae (Ren, et al., 2024).

In recent years, some studies have attempted to improve the growth of squabs with functional additives. For example, feeding 2 milligrams of zinc-methionine to squabs through a gastric tube can increase the abundance of Bacillus, lactic acid bacteria, Enterococcus, and Bifidobacterium and improve growth performance and immune function (Wang, et al., 2014). Tea polyphenols can activate the Nrf2-ARE signaling pathway, which may be associated with improved antioxidant and immune responses and is correlated with an increased abundance of Candida arthritis and Corynebacterium in the ileum; thus, tea polyphenols may enhance the antioxidant capacity and immune response of squabs to maintain intestinal health (Chen et al., 2023).

In summary, colonization by the gut microbiota has a significant positive effect on the growth performance of squabs. Probiotics, functional additives, and FMT may improve the growth rate, nutrient absorption efficiency, and immune function of squabs. Optimizing these methods will enable farmers to meet the growth needs of squabs and enhance their production performance. By studying microbial changes in squabs at different growth stages, the overall health and production performance of squabs may be improved through precise nutritional regulation of the gut microbiota.

Conclusion

The growth performance of squabs is affected by multiple factors. Integrating multivariate statistical models and systems biology approaches has proven to be a powerful tool in poultry research for identifying key factors and optimizing growth conditions. For instance, in chicken research, integrating multidimensional data such as temperature, genome-wide data, and feed composition through multivariate models has successfully improved growth rates and feed conversion efficiency. This success offers valuable insights and methodologies for the pigeon farming industry. Adopting this integrated approach in pigeon research is expected to yield substantial benefits. It enables a detailed analysis of the complex interactions among numerous factors, providing researchers with valuable data and insights. On the basis of these data, researchers can develop more precise management strategies to optimize pigeon growth and health. This not only has the potential to further advance pigeon research but also offers practical guidance to pigeon farmers, helping them improve their breeding and management practices to increase the productivity and economic benefits of pigeons. Additionally, these advanced analytical methods can provide a more comprehensive understanding of the multifactor interaction mechanisms affecting pigeon growth. This understanding can inform more effective and targeted intervention measures, improving productivity while ensuring animal welfare. Therefore, to advance pigeon farming and enhance squab growth, future research should focus on elucidating the interactions among factors and their cumulative impact on squab growth by integrating multidimensional data, developing multivariate models, and employing systems biology approaches. Interdisciplinary collaboration among biologists, statisticians, and poultry farmers is essential for accelerating the application of research findings and driving innovation in the pigeon farming industry.
