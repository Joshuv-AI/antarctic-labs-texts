Global and Regional Burden of Periodontal Disease in Adults (1990-2021)
Authors: Yadong Wang, Yadong Wu, Jukun Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriodontitis is one of the most prevalent chronic inflammatory diseases globally, characterised by progressive destruction of tooth-supporting structures. It has significant implications for oral health and has been associated with systemic conditions such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes and respiratory infections. Despite ongoing public health initiatives, periodontitis remains a substantial burden worldwide. This study aims to utilise the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 2021 data to estimate the incidence and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of severe periodontitis from 1990 to 2021, providing updated epidemiological trends at global and regional levels.MethodsData on the incidence, prevalence and DALY rates of severe periodontitis among adults from 1990 to 2021 were extracted from GBD 2021. The Estimated Annual Percent Change (EAPC) was applied to assess trends in the burden of severe periodontitis over the past 3 decades. Besides, Joinpoint analysis was used to evaluate the temporal changes in the global burden of severe periodontitis.ResultsFrom 1990 to 2021, global DALY and incidence rates of severe periodontitis among adults exhibited a slight increase. The estimated number of global incident cases of severe periodontitis increased significantly, from 50,823,934 cases (95% uncertainty intervals [UI] = 39,615,907-59,174,250) in 1990 to 89,613,533 cases (95% UI = 79,069,090-101,005,641) in 2021. The global EAPC for DALY and incidence rates were 0.077 (95% UI: −0.025 to 0.179) and 0.027 (95% UI: −0.042 to 0.096), respectively.ConclusionsThe global burden of severe periodontitis has steadily increased. To address this growing public health challenge, enhanced prevention, control and intervention strategies are critical. More emphasis on common risk factors, such as poor oral hygiene, smoking and diabetes, is essential for effectively preventing and managing severe periodontitis.Key words: Epidemiology, Global Burden of Disease study, Global health, Severe periodontitis

Introduction

Periodontal disease encompasses diseases occurring within the periodontal tissues, including both gingivitis and periodontitis.1,2 Periodontitis is a complex, chronic inflammatory disease that damages the supporting structures of the teeth, including the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone. This damage can lead to partial or complete tooth loss, significantly impacting the patient’s ability to chew, communicate effectively and maintain a high quality of life.3 In recent years, a growing body of research has demonstrated a close association between periodontal disease and systemic diseases, including diabetes, cardiovascular diseases and malignant tumours.4,5 This association contributes to a significant disease burden and has caused substantial direct and indirect economic losses to society.6,7 According to the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD), approximately 1.1 billion individuals worldwide were affected by severe periodontitis in 2019, representing a prevalence rate of 17%.8 As a type of chronic non-communicable disease (NCD), periodontitis shares risk factors with other NCDs9,10 and exerts bidirectional influences on overall health.11 There is a growing global consensus that enhancing periodontal health can significantly benefit systemic health and overall well-being.12,13

Based on survey data from 2009 to 2012, nearly half of adults and over 60% of those aged 65 years or older exhibited periodontitis.14 Severe periodontitis affected 11.2% of adults worldwide from 1990 to 2010.15 According to GBD 2017, Bernabe et al. reported 796 million cases of periodontitis globally in 2017.16 A high prevalence of periodontitis among adults was also observed in mainland China, according to the 4th National Oral Health Survey conducted from 2015 to 2016, which indicated a positive association between disease severity and age. The aging population, changes in risk factors and increased tooth retention rates further amplify the burden of periodontal diseases.12 The above studies overlap in their assertion that periodontal disease remains a significant global public health issue, highlighting the need for effective control measures.

Therefore, it is crucial to systematically analyse the long-term trends in the incidence, prevalence and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of periodontal disease. Importantly, Joinpoint regression analysis was applied to assess the temporal trends in severe periodontitis burden among adults globally. Our findings aimed to provide a theoretical basis for reducing the burden of periodontal disease and for formulating more comprehensive periodontal disease prevention and control policies.

Methods

Data source

This study employed GBD 2021 data to estimate DALYs for 301 acute and chronic diseases and injuries across 188 countries from 1990 to 2021.17 These countries and territories were categorised into 21 regions based on similarities in epidemiology and geographical proximity. Besides, they were classified into 5 sociodemographic index (SDI) categories: low, low-middle, middle, high-middle and high SDI. Our analysis focused on global trends in severe periodontitis across various age groups, as defined by the GBD 2021 stratification: 20-24, 25-29, 30-34, 35-39, 40-44, 45-49, 50-54, 55-59, 60-64, 65-69, 70-74, 75-79 and 80 years and older.

Definition of severe periodontitis

In the GBD 2021 study, the definition of periodontal disease is based on the Community Periodontal Treatment Needs Index (CPITN). A score of Level IV on this index meets the criteria for defining periodontal disease, which includes conditions such as Attachment Loss (AL) greater than 6 mm or Pocket Depth (PD) greater than 5 mm.17, 18, 19 Besides, periodontal disease is classified under the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), with codes ranging from K05 to K06.9; in the ICD-9, the corresponding codes are 523 to 523.9.

Measures of burden

To address uncertainties arising from primary data sources, model inconsistencies, data errors and potential data manipulation, the GBD studies include calculations to account for these uncertainties in all estimates. These uncertainties are quantitatively expressed through 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) for each estimate and each location. The UIs are derived from 1000 simulations drawn from the models’ posterior distributions. Specifically, the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of these distributions define the boundaries of the 95% UIs.

Sociodemographic index

The SDI measures a country’s or region’s development level based on fertility rate, education level and per capita income.19,20 The SDI scale ranges from 0 to 1, where higher values indicate greater socioeconomic development. The SDI is known to correlate with disease incidence and mortality rates. In this study, countries and geographic regions were categorised into 5 SDI levels – low, low-medium, medium, medium-high and high – to examine the relationship between childhood diabetes burden and socioeconomic development.

Joinpoint Poisson regression

Identifying changes in temporal trends is essential for analysing disease burden, including cancer mortality and incidence data. These trends were analysed using a Joinpoint regression model, which is particularly effective in detecting significant changes in the rate of disease over time. In this model, the rates were log-transformed to normalise the data, and standard errors were estimated using a binomial approximation.21,22 To evaluate the magnitude of these trends in incidence and mortality rates, we calculated the Average Annual Percent Change (AAPC) along with its corresponding 95% CIs through Joinpoint regression analysis. P-values were obtained using Monte Carlo simulations, and the overall significance level was adjusted for multiple comparisons with a Bonferroni correction. The AAPC was calculated as a geometrically weighted average of the annual percent changes identified by the regression model. This analysis was performed using the Joinpoint software developed by the Surveillance Research Program at the US National Cancer Institute.

Estimated Annual Percent Change

The trends in adult periodontal disease were assessed using the Estimated Annual Percent Change (EAPC), which is commonly applied in trend analysis of age-standardised rates (ASR) to evaluate changes over specific time intervals.23 The EAPC is calculated using a linear regression model that fits the natural logarithm of the data. Specifically, the formula for EAPC is 100 × [exp(β) − 1], where β is the regression coefficient derived from the equation y = α + βx + ε, with y representing the natural logarithm of ASR (ln(ASR)) and x indicating the calendar years. The 95% CI for the EAPC is also derived from this linear regression model. A positive EAPC estimate, along with a lower CI greater than 0, indicates an increasing trend in the disease burden, while a negative estimate, with the upper CI less than 0, suggests a decreasing trend. If the 95% CI includes 0, the disease burden is considered stable.

Statistical analysis

In the present study, the disease burden of adult periodontal disease was described using incidence, prevalence and DALY rates, along with their 95% uncertainty intervals. Incidence and prevalence rates in the GBD database were estimated using DisMod-MR 2.1 (WHO, Switzerland), a Bayesian meta-regression modelling tool that ensures consistency between estimates across different groups.16 Moreover, this study analysed the trends in the disease burden of periodontal disease by gender and age group. The study population was stratified into 14 discrete age groups: 20-24, 25-29, 30-34, 35-39, 40-44, 45-49, 50-54, 55-59, 60-64, 65-69, 70-74, 75-79, 80-84 and ≥85 years.

Results

Incidence of severe periodontitis

According to GBD 2021, the estimated number of global incident cases of severe periodontitis increased significantly from 50,823,934 cases (95% UI = 39,615,907-59,174,250) in 1990 to 89,613,533 cases (95% UI = 79,069,090-101,005,641) in 2021 (Table 1), representing a rise of 76.32 % (95% UI = 70.69%-99.58%) over the 31 years. In 2021, the increase in the number of incident cases was most pronounced in the Republic of India, where it surged by 1,540,186 (95% UI = 632,989-3,120,731), followed by significant increases in the People’s Republic of China and the United States of America. Conversely, the Solomon Islands and the Republic of Fiji experienced the largest decrease in incident cases during the same period, as illustrated in Figure 1A. During the study period, the age-standardised rate of incidence (ASRI) of severe periodontitis exhibited a rising trend, increasing from 1069.376 per 100,000 person-years (95% UI = 852.995-1239.586) to 1069.436 per 100,000 person-years (95% UI = 942.708-1204.577) (Figure 1B). In 2021, the highest ASRI was recorded in the Kingdom of Denmark at 1,747.04 per 100,000 person-years (95% UI = 1584.297-1896.279), followed by Bermuda at 1707.52 per 100,000 person-years (95% UI = 1473.429-1904.342) (Figure 1B). The lowest rate was observed in the Solomon Islands at 151.14 per 100,000 person-years (95% UI = 107.701-210.001). Despite having the second-largest population globally, India also exhibited a high ASRI of 1309.863 per 100,000 person-years (95% UI = 1142.924-1464.240). Conversely, China, the most populous country, had a higher ASRI of 1285.896 per 100,000 person-years (95% UI = 1106.873-1457.013) compared to the global average. These findings are detailed in Table S1. During the 1990-2021 period, the percent change in age-standardised rates was higher in the Republic of Turkey (0.697, 95% UI = 0.287-1.455), lower in the Republic of Kiribati (−0.745, 95% UI = −0.825 to 0.629) (Figure 1C).

During the 1990-2021 period, the global ASRI initially decreased until 2010, then sharply increased through 2021 (Figure 2). The lowest ASRI levels were observed in regions with high and high-middle SDI. In contrast, the ASRI in middle SDI regions remained relatively stable, while low SDI regions exhibited a more pronounced decline. In different SDI regions, the ASRI was slightly higher in females than in males. Figure 3A illustrates the incidence rates of severe periodontitis across 204 countries concerning the SDI, using GBD 2021 data. The data revealed a trend where countries with higher SDI generally exhibited lower incidence rates of severe periodontitis, suggesting that nations with better socio-economic status may implement more effective health interventions and public health policies to combat this condition. Similar results were observed across 21 regions, as shown in Figure 3B.

In terms of geographic regions, the ASRI was highest in South Asia at 1288.551 per 100,000 person-years (95% UI = 1138.930-1428.391) in 2021, and lowest in Oceania at 261.572 per 100,000 person-years (95% UI = 196.460-359.874). From 1990 to 2021, the incidence of severe periodontitis increased in all regions, with the most substantial rises observed in Australasia, North Africa and the Middle East. However, over the past 31 years, the ASRI exhibited significant variations among regions (Table 1). The trend in incident cases has shown an increase across all SDI quintiles. Notably, the ASRI increased in all SDI quintiles except for those countries categorised as high SDI. In 2021, the highest ASRI was in low-middle-SDI countries at 1174.583 per 100,000 (95% UI = 1030.709-1314.306), followed by middle SDI countries at 1064.106 per 100,000 (95% UI = 933.752-1186.601). The most significant increase during the period from 1990 to 2021 was observed in low-middle SDI countries (0.992, 95% UI: 0.669-1.448), while the most decreased countries were detected in high SDI countries (0.169, 95% UI: −0.005 to 0.422).

Furthermore, the incidence of severe periodontitis increased with age and was significantly higher in males compared to females, as illustrated in Figure 4A and B. The risk of severe periodontitis demonstrated considerable variation across different age groups. Notably, the incidence of severe periodontitis was higher in males than in females within the 20-45 year age range; however, this trend reversed after 45 years, with females exhibiting a higher risk than males. DALYs attributable to severe periodontitis

Over the study period, DALYs due to severe periodontitis increased by 90.67% (95% UI = 82.73%-95.03%), rising from 3,620,518 (95% UI =1,421,408-7,719,685) in 1990 to 6,903,283 (95% UI = 2,772,283-14,106,182) in 2021 (Table 2). In 2021, the largest number of cases was observed in the Republic of India (1,540,186.623, 95% UI = 632,989.835-3,120,731.914), followed by the People’s Republic of China and the United States of America. Conversely, the most significant decrease occurred in Tokelau (0.221, 95% UI = 0.082-0.487) (Figure 5A). Globally, the age-standardised DALY rate for severe periodontitis remained relatively stable, shifting from 79.620 per 100,000 person-years in 1990 to 80.894 per 100,000 person-years in 2021. In 2021, the highest age-standardised DALY rate was recorded in Republic of Sierra Leone (167.829 per 100,000 person-years, 95% UI = 64.814-339.064), with similarly high rates in the Kingdom of Denmark (136.171 per 100,000 person-years, 95% UI = 53.788-272.215), while the lowest rate was documented in the Republic of Kiribati (11.831 per 100,000 person-years, 95% UI = 4.449-25.636) (Figure 5B and Table S2). During the 1990-2021 period, the percent change in age-standardised DALY rate was highest in the Republic of Turkey (1.062, 95% UI = 0.400-2.269) and lowest in the Republic of Kiribati (−0.801, 95% UI = −0.876 to 0.686 (Figure 5C).

In terms of geographic regions, the age-standardised DALY rate in 2021 was highest in South Asia (113.386 per 100,000 person-years, 95% UI = 46.480-230.172) and lowest in Oceania (14.375 per 100,000 person-years, 95% UI = 5.358-31.847) (Table 2). From 1990 to 2021, while age-standardised DALYs decreased or remained increased in all SDI quintiles, DALYs increased in each quintile (Figure 6C and D). In 2021, the age-standardised DALY was highest in low-middle-SDI countries (98.501 per 100,000 person-years, 95% UI = 39.876-199.409) and low-SDI countries (86.986 per 100,000 person-years, 95% UI = 35.395-176.968), and was lowest in high-SDI countries (65.468 per 100,000 person-years, 95% UI = 26.297-132.991).

Regarding age distribution, the trend in age-standardised DALYs differed from that of age-specific rates due to the inclusion of a time factor in DALY calculations. In 2021, the age-standardised DALY increased in patients younger than 20 years and decreased constantly in those aged 55 years and older. Besides, the age-standardised DALY was higher in males than females within the 20-59 age group; however, beyond 65 years, this trend reversed, with females exhibiting a higher risk than males (Figure 8A and B).

Trends in the burden of periodontal disease from 1990 to 2021

From 1990 to 2021, the incidence, prevalence and DALY rates for periodontal disease generally showed an increasing trend, as illustrated in Table 2 and Figure 9A-C. After age-standardisation, the EAPC for incidence was 0.027 (95% UI: −0.042 to 0.096), for prevalence was 0.078 (95% UI: −0.025 to 0.180), and for DALY rates was 0.077 (95% UI: −0.025 to 0.179). Notably, the most rapid increases in the burden of periodontal disease were observed in the Republic of Sierra Leone and the Republic of Turkey. In these countries, the highest EAPCs were recorded for incidence at 1.666 (95% UI: 1.206-2.128) in Republic of Sierra Leone, for prevalence at 2.548 (95% UI: 1.964-3.134) in Republic of Turkey, and for DALYs at 2.554 (95% UI: 1.967-3.144) in Republic of Turkey. In contrast, the Republic of Kiribati exhibited lower increases in incidence, prevalence and DALY rates.

Discussion

By analysing data from the GBD study, the present research estimated the incidence, mortality and DALYs of severe periodontitis across 204 countries/territories and 21 regions from 1990 to 2021. It examined variations in ASRI and age-standardised DALYs across different geographic areas, documenting how these disease burdens have evolved over the past 31 years. Notably, while the incidence of severe periodontitis has been increasing, DALYs have remained relatively stable, suggesting that improvements in periodontal treatments, public health initiatives, or access to care have mitigated the disease burden despite the rising prevalence. These findings provide vital insights for policymakers in various countries, helping them understand the local and global impact of severe periodontitis and enabling the development of more effective strategies for disease management. This understanding could facilitate the adoption of successful approaches from regions that have effectively managed their disease burden.

The GBD study revealed a substantial increase in global severe periodontitis cases from 1990 to 2021, with a 76.32% rise. Notable increases occurred in India, China and the USA, while decreases were observed in the Solomon Islands and Fiji. The ASRI slightly increased, with the highest rates in Denmark and Bermuda. Regionally, South Asia recorded the highest ASRI, while Oceania exhibited the lowest. Overall, higher socio-economic regions showed lower incidence rates, suggesting effective health interventions. A notable demographic variation was observed, with males generally exhibiting higher severe periodontitis risk than females, particularly in younger age groups. The GBD study found that DALYs due to severe periodontitis increased by 90.67%, with a notable rise from 3.62 million to 6.9 million. The increase was most significant in India, China and the USA, while Tokelau experienced the largest decline. Despite this increase, the age-standardised DALY rate remained relatively stable globally, shifting only slightly from 79.62 to 80.894 per 100,000 person-years. The highest rates in 2021 were observed in Sierra Leone and Denmark, with the lowest in Kiribati. An inverse correlation between the SDI and severe periodontitis rates suggests that higher socioeconomic conditions are associated with more effective healthcare interventions, improved oral hygiene practices and better access to preventive care, all contributing to lower severe periodontitis rates. In contrast, regions with lower SDI tend to face greater challenges in healthcare access, including limited availability of oral health services, lower health literacy and fewer policy interventions to prevent periodontal disease. Regionally, South Asia recorded the highest DALYs in 2021, reflecting both limited access to quality healthcare and challenges in oral hygiene practices. Oceania, with higher socioeconomic development and healthcare accessibility, recorded the lowest DALYs. Overall, DALYs increased across all regions, with significant rises in Australasia, North Africa and the Middle East. These trends highlight the impact of variations in health interventions, socioeconomic conditions and the effectiveness of national oral health policies in addressing periodontal disease burden.

This study highlights a global increase in the incidence and DALYs of severe periodontitis. While the ASRI has significantly risen worldwide, the age-standardised DALYs have either remained stable or decreased in some regions. However, these trends varied significantly across different countries. For instance, nations like the Republic of Sierra Leone, and Lao People’s Democratic Republic, Republic of Turkey witnessed notable increases in incidence, primarily due to economic imbalance and population growth. The Republic of Sierra Leone, with the highest ASRI and age-standardised DALYs, exemplifies how economic imbalance can influence severe periodontitis metrics. Similarly, the Republic of India and China showed comparable trends. Contributory factors in these regions may include dietary habits and larger populations, such as the prevalent consumption of betel nuts in South and Southeast Asia, as well as parts of China like Taiwan, Hunan and Hainan. These findings suggest that national strategies focusing on oral health and dietary practices could be crucial in mitigating periodontitis incidence.

In this study, we observed increasing trends in the burden of severe periodontitis. We found that the ASRI has been rising globally, indicating a worldwide increase in the incidence of severe periodontitis. Notably, Mainland China reported the highest ASRI, suggesting a need for increased governmental investment in healthcare resources to prevent and manage severe periodontitis. Conversely, the APCs in the DALYs have increased globally, especially in male populations. This trend suggests improvements in the mortality rate, treatment effectiveness and patient survival rates of severe periodontitis. However, the ASRM and ASR-DALY continue to exhibit upward trajectories in some developing countries, highlighting a significant health disparity that needs addressing.

To contextualise our findings, we compared our results with those from prior studies using earlier versions of the GBD data.24, 25, 26 While our study utilised GBD 2021 data, comparisons with these prior analyses allowed us to examine trends in disease burden over time, assess regional variations and evaluate the impact of healthcare interventions and socioeconomic factors. Notably, differences in trends observed in our study relative to earlier GBD reports may reflect changes in data collection methods, disease definitions and improvements in healthcare access. In this respect, the GBD 2017 study employed different age-standardisation methods and did not account for some emerging healthcare policies that could have reduced the burden of severe periodontitis in certain regions. Besides, while earlier GBD versions had a relatively limited focus on oral health interventions, the GBD 2021 data incorporated more comprehensive oral health programs, including access to preventive care and changes in public health policy, which could explain some of the observed improvements in disease burden. By situating our findings within the broader context of these earlier studies, we provide a more comprehensive understanding of the evolving burden of severe periodontitis, helping to identify the factors that drive regional differences and trends, and offering insights into effective interventions for disease control.

One of the significant strengths of this study is the ability to assess the temporal trends in the global burden of periodontitis using EAPC analysis. Our results showed consistent patterns in prevalence, incidence and DALYs across 204 countries, providing a comprehensive overview of the global disease burden. This approach is particularly valuable as it enables the identification of broad trends and global comparisons, offering insights into the evolving impact of periodontitis worldwide. However, several limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting the findings of this study. The GBD database, while an invaluable resource, has inherent limitations that may influence the accuracy and generalisability of the results. Firstly, the lack of a universally standardised definition of severe periodontitis used by the GBD study is a significant challenge. The variability in how severe periodontitis is defined across countries could result in discrepancies in diagnosis and case estimation, thereby affecting the reliability of the global burden data. This underscores the need for future studies that aim to establish a more consistent definition and diagnostic criteria for severe periodontitis, potentially improving the accuracy of global estimates. Secondly, the period from 1990 to 2021 coincided with significant economic growth in many countries, which may have influenced public health awareness and diagnostic practices. The increased public awareness, particularly in countries experiencing rapid development, might have led to a higher detection rate of severe periodontitis, thus impacting the observed trends. This contextual factor should be considered when interpreting changes in prevalence and incidence over time. Besides, the GBD study is limited by data gaps, particularly in terms of provincial-level information, which hinders the granularity of regional analyses. The absence of detailed data for specific years also introduces the possibility of missing data or reporting biases, particularly in countries with less robust healthcare systems or lower reporting capacities. To address this, future studies should focus on collecting more complete, regionally specific data to facilitate more localised analyses of the periodontal disease burden.

Moreover, while this study provides valuable insights into the trends of periodontitis, it does not address the specific risk factors that contribute to the disease burden. Understanding the underlying causes of periodontitis, including social determinants of health, access to dental care and regional dietary habits, is crucial for developing effective prevention strategies. Future research should aim to fill this gap by conducting more detailed investigations into the risk factors for severe periodontitis, particularly in high-burden regions.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that while the global ASRI of severe periodontitis has slightly increased, the age-standardised DALYs have either remained stable or decreased over the past three decades. Despite these overall trends, significant regional variations exist, which highlight the need for more tailored public health interventions. Prevention strategies should be reassessed, particularly in countries with high ASRI or DALYs. These strategies may include improving access to dental care, integrating oral health into primary healthcare systems and promoting community-based oral health education and fluoride programs. Moving forward, future research should focus on identifying and evaluating the effectiveness of these interventions, with an emphasis on regions with high disease burden, to inform more targeted and effective prevention and treatment strategies for periodontitis.
