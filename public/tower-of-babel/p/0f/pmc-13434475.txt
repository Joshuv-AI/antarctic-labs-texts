Research on the temporal and spatial evolution and protection significance of traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin of China
Authors: Xin Su, Fan Wu, Yanlong Guo, Jie Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study regards the Wanjiang River Basin as an integrated geographical and cultural unit to explore the nonlinear evolution of traditional villages. Utilizing six batches of national designation data (2012–2023), we employ spatial analysis and correlation analysis to examine spatiotemporal patterns and driving factors. The results reveal three key findings. First, villages exhibit a “large dispersion, small aggregation” pattern, with persistent core areas forming at the junction of Xuancheng and Huangshan cities since the pre-Song period. Second, The sites of traditional villages are mainly located in plain areas and concentrated in areas with gentle slopes, and the slopes are oriented towards the south with sunny slopes, and have the characteristics of being close to water. Third, a significant negative correlation exists between village density and socioeconomic development, indicating modern “socioeconomic filtering.” These findings reveal a dual evolutionary mechanism—historical path dependence anchored by natural suitability, and contemporary filtering by development pressure—offering a spatial basis for priority protection zones and differentiated revitalization strategies in similar watersheds.

Introduction

As a living carrier of cultural genes, the conservation research of villages has cross-cultural universal value. The international academic community has formed a multi-dimensional research paradigms on the spatial evolution mechanism of traditional villages. European scholars emphasize the holistic protection of “cultural landscape”. For example, Italy relies on the world heritage protection mechanism and the agricultural landscape integration policy to realize the symbiosis of the historical settlements such as the Five Fishing Villages and the eco-agriculture1. East Asia focuses on the living inheritance2, A prime example is Japan, where, through the “Important Traditional Buildings Preservation Areas System” and community self-governance, the architectural protection of Shirakawa-go and Gokayama villages is embedded within traditional production systems. Similarly, living inheritance is a central tenet in Latin American conservation practices3. These international experiences reveal the “paradox of modernization” in the protection of traditional villages, the need to resist the alienation of spatial forms while maintaining the survival of social functions. Due to its unique characteristics across three dimensions, China serves as a critical case for analyzing this paradox. First, its unprecedented spatiotemporal compression is evident: urbanization surged from 17.9% to 67% within 40 years – the fastest demographic shift in history. This triggered a synchronous “triple collapse”: large-scale rural depopulation, accelerated cultural fragmentation, and systematic decline of architectural heritage4. In addition, in terms of macro scale heterogeneity, it has the largest and most diversified Library of traditional village types in the world, which are distributed on steep ecological gradients and are affected by different social and historical trajectories, so it is possible to compare and analyze multiple scalar driving factors in a single governance system. Finally, in terms of governance intensity, the tension between centralized protection policy and super localized community response has created a unique laboratory for studying the institutional adaptability under the pressure of rapid modernization. This integration of extreme compression, internal diversity and policy social dynamics has positioned China as a “key stress testing ground”, where the interaction of natural, historical and social forces has been strengthened, This study provides valuable insights for validating the Stress-Response framework and the theory of spatial production in developing regions worldwide5. The research results from this “key stress testing ground” can be extended to other contexts in multiple ways: in spatial governance, China’s experience in balancing centralized policy efficiency with local response flexibility offers guidance for regions grappling with top-down management and bottom-up demands; For areas with rapid urbanization, the “stress response” model derived from China’s handling of the “triple collapse” helps predict rural development risks and formulate preemptive measures; Regarding the protection of diverse village types, the comparative analysis methods developed in Chinese research on traditional villages can be applied to other countries with rich rural cultural landscapes, enabling more accurate identification of core drivers behind different village evolution patterns; in cultural inheritance, China’s practice of integrating architectural protection into traditional production systems inspires regions struggling to sustain traditional cultural vitality, guiding them to link heritage protection with daily production and life; Furthermore, research on institutional adaptability under rapid modernization pressure in China provides a theoretical foundation and practical pathways for other developing regions to adjust governance systems during modernization, helping them better address social change challenges while safeguarding traditional villages.

At present, Chinese scholars mainly focus on the following three aspects of spatial and temporal dynamics and protection of traditional villages6: research scale, research object, and research approach. First of all, the research object is mainly divided into micro research object and macro research object7. The micro research object mainly involves the specific traditional village historical relics as well as ancient architectural style and other research categories, according to which some scholars pointed out that the traditional village architectural style includes architectural structure, architectural style, building materials and so on. Its macro-objects mainly include the traditional village landscape protection and spatial macro-environmental research, according to which most scholars mainly case study research, respectively, analyzed Yunnan, Guangxi, Sichuan, Guizhou and other traditional village protection measures and experience and put forward the “cultural heritage revitalization and utilization”8. Adaptive reuse of cultural heritage, systematic conservation of traditional village landscapes, and other measures to establish a sustainable protection framework. Secondly, the research method mainly involves spatial analysis, geostatistics, questionnaire and summary. For example, in the spatial analysis, GIS quantitative research and spatial syntax are mainly used to examine the architectural design, spatial distribution pattern, inheritance and protection measures of traditional villages. Finally, regarding research scale, while numerous studies have examined rural settlements globally—from community-level analyses in Europe to macro-scale national assessments in Asia—the intermediate watershed scale remains a fertile ground for cross-disciplinary inquiry. International studies, such as those on the Rhine Valley in Germany or the Mekong Basin in Southeast Asia, have demonstrated the value of using watersheds to understand long-term human-environment interactions. Building on this international momentum, this study applies a basin-scale analytical framework. While these international precedents have laid valuable foundations, and recent years have seen a growing body of watershed-scale research on Chinese traditional villages, most studies have tended to describe static distribution patterns rather than systematically analyze the dynamic, interactive coupling between hydrological networks and cultural diffusion pathways over extended temporal scales. Furthermore, the reliance on single time-section analyses has limited our ability to capture the nuanced, policy-driven dynamics of village evolution across critical conservation phases. Addressing these gaps requires an analytical framework that explicitly operationalizes the watershed’s dual nature—its physical connectivity and its cultural continuity—within a longitudinal design. In order to offer a more thorough understanding of the spatial and temporal growth of the traditional villages in the region, six batches of villages in Wanjiang River Basin9 that are included in the list of Chinese traditional villages during the period of 2012–202310 are chosen as the research objects in this paper11. To address these issues, this study selects the Wanjiang River Basin not merely as a study area, but as an integrated geographical and cultural unit. In contrast to studies that analyze spatial patterns in isolation, our framework explicitly operationalizes the basin’s dual nature: the physical connectivity provided by its hydrological network and the cultural connectivity implied by the spatial clustering of villages. A purely hydrological analysis might reveal physical proximity to water but fail to explain why certain river sections became cultural hotspots while others did not. Conversely, a purely cultural analysis might identify clustering but overlook the underlying topographic and hydrological templates that enabled those clusters to form and persist. Therefore, an integrated framework is necessary to decode the ‘water-culture corridor’—a concept where the hydrological network acts as the physical backbone channeling human migration and trade, while cultural factors act as the social glue solidifying these pathways into enduring settlement clusters. By operationalizing both layers within a GIS model, this study aims to move beyond describing where villages are to explaining why the basin’s unique natural-cultural substrate has produced this specific spatiotemporal pattern. Specifically, the influence of the hydrological network is quantified through river buffer analysis, while the manifestation of cultural corridors is identified via the spatial clustering of traditional villages using kernel density estimation. This dual-layer approach allows us to systematically interpret the distribution of villages not as points on a map, but as a spatial manifestation of the basin’s natural-cultural substrate12. Crucially, this analytical approach helps to explore and examine the “paradox of modernization” within the Chinese context, by tracing how spatial forms are maintained or transformed under the combined influences of historical legacy and contemporary socioeconomic development. This study applies a basin-scale analytical framework that integrates natural and administrative units. While the Wanjiang River Basin is treated as a coherent geographical and cultural context—moving beyond purely administrative boundaries—the quantitative analyses are conducted at the county level. This choice reflects the availability of socio-economic data and the administrative structure of conservation policy implementation. The approach allows for the examination of socio-economic drivers within a defined natural-cultural system, illustrating how a watershed perspective can support both pattern description and causal analysis in traditional village research.

Unlike many previous studies that focus primarily on administrative units, this study considers the Wanjiang River Basin as an integrated geographical and cultural unit. In addition, this study selected the Wanjiang River Basin as a typical case, mainly based on its typicality, completeness, and comparability in geography and culture. Compared with other large river basins such as the Yellow River Basin, the Wanjiang River Basin is more compact in geographical scale, with a relatively unified cultural context. The distribution of traditional villages in the basin is influenced by the Huizhou merchant culture, water system network, and terrain conditions since the Ming and Qing dynasties, forming a typical “water village culture” coupling system. This watershed not only represents the spatial organization model of traditional villages in the humid areas of eastern China, but also provides an ideal sample for conducting refined spatiotemporal evolution research in medium-sized watersheds. Therefore, choosing the Wanjiang River Basin instead of a larger or more heterogeneous basin can help to reveal the underlying mechanisms of traditional village spatial evolution more clearly, while controlling for geographical and cultural variables.The analytical framework strategically integrates the connectivity of the hydrological network and the spatial mapping of cultural corridors into the GIS model. Specifically, the influence of the hydrological network is quantified through river buffer analysis, while the manifestation of cultural corridors is identified via the spatial clustering of traditional villages using kernel density estimation. This approach allows for a systematic revelation of the intrinsic relationships between village distribution and the natural-cultural substrate of the basin.

Materials and methodologies

Study area

Central and southern Anhui Province, China is the location of the Wanjiang River Basin, which is located along the Yangtze River13. The geographical location is between 115°45’~119°40’E and 29°56’~33°22’N. It is mainly located in the south of the Huaihe River in Anhui Province and on both sides of the Yangtze River. The city is close to Huainan City and Bengbu City in Anhui Province in the northwest, adjacent to Lu’an City in Anhui Province in the west, derived from Huangshan City in Anhui Province in the south, and bordered with Zhejiang Province in China in the East. It covers 9 cities in Anhui Province: Anqing, Chizhou, Tongling, Wuhu, Ma’anshan, Hefei, Chuzhou, Xuancheng and Lu’an, covering an area of 75,800 km2. The landforms consist of the plains along the Yangtze River, the mountainous regions of southern Anhui, and the hills of the Jianghuai River, which account for 55% of total area in the province, while the mountainous and hilly areas occupy 45% of the total area. In terms of network traffic, railway and highway are crisscrossed, and the region is well connected by national and provincial highways, and townships are connected by counties, realising the project of “connecting villages to villages”. Climatologically, the Wanjiang River Basin is a combination of temperate and subtropical monsoons, with an average rainfall of 1050–1400 mm14 and an average sunshine time of 2000–2100 h (Fig. 1). Data sources

Information regarding traditional villages is sourced from six separate publications by the People’s Republic of China’s Ministry of Housing and the Ministry of Urban and Rural Development (https://www.mohurd.gov.cn/gongkai/zc/wjk/art/2024/art_0c4e539d60d6436c9459210fde11ec70.html). List of Traditional Villages in Anhui Province published by the Anhui Provincial Department of Housing and Urban-Rural Development (http://dohurd.ah.gov.cn/zx/mtjj/56708301.html). The administrative boundary data of Anhui Province and its prefectural-level cities were obtained from the Resource and Environment Science and Data Center (http://www.resdc.cn/). Among the 152 traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin, there are 23 in Anqing, 28 in Chizhou, 86 in Xuancheng, 6 in Tongling, 5 in Hefei, 1 in Wuhu, 1 in Maanshan, 1 in Lu’an, and 1 in Chuzhou. In the study area, due to the long history and the unknown date of the founding of a few villages, 145 traditional villages were identified in this paper. To analyze the spatiotemporal evolution, these 145 villages were classified into three major historical periods based on their documented founding era: Pre-Song (220 BC–970 AD), Song-Yuan (970–1368 AD), and Ming-Qing (1368–1912 AD). The classification was primarily derived from local chronicles, genealogies, and archaeological reports compiled within the Anhui Traditional Villages Catalog. For villages with continuous habitation across multiple periods, the earliest documented founding era was used to capture the initial spatial distribution patterns. For villages with ambiguous or conflicting historical records, a conservative approach was adopted by assigning them to the era with the earliest reliable documented evidence. The selected villages are spatially distributed across all nine prefectural-level cities within the basin and encompass various topographic settings, ensuring the sample’s representativeness for analyzing the basin’s long-term settlement dynamics. As a sample of historical evolution, 40 traditional villages were traced back to the pre-Song Dynasty, 48 traditional villages towards the Song and Yuan Dynasties, and 57 traditional villages during the Ming and Qing Dynasties. Furthermore, 30 m resolution data from a digital elevation model (DEM) were acquired originating from the cloud of geospatial data15. This DEM served as the base for deriving topographic factors, including slope gradient and slope aspect, using the surface analysis tools in ArcGIS. Rivers and roads are from the China Basic Geographic Information Center (https://www.gscloud.cn/search), Hydrological and climatic factors, specifically river networks for buffer analysis and raster data for mean annual precipitation and mean annual temperature, were obtained from the National Tibetan Plateau / Third Pole Environment Data Center. Socio-economic data are from the statistical yearbooks of each prefecture-level city (https://tjj.ah.gov.cn/ssah/qwfbjd/tjnj/index.html).

Research methodology

To test the “water-culture corridor” hypothesis and decode the spatiotemporal evolution of traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin, this study constructs an integrated two-stage analytical framework that strategically combines standard spatial statistics to explicitly operationalize the basin’s dual nature, hydrological connectivity and cultural continuity. The framework’s novelty lies not in inventing new indices, but in configuring them into a coherent diagnostic chain tailored to the watershed scale. Stage 1: Pattern Detection, we employ the Nearest Neighbor Index, Geographic Concentration Index, and Imbalance Index to quantify macro-scale clustering, while Kernel Density Estimation and Standard Deviation Ellipse pinpoint micro-scale hotspots and evolutionary trajectories. Stage 2: Mechanism Validation, building on the identified patterns, we overlay river buffer zones with KDE hotspots to directly test the spatial coupling between the hydrological network and cultural agglomerations, and conduct Pearson correlation analysis to quantify the driving effects of natural and socio-economic factors. This chain-like framework ensures a logical from pattern description to causal explanation, representing a methodological attempt to treat the watershed as a coupled system in traditional village research.

The first stage is pattern detection.We first characterize the fundamental spatial pattern using the Nearest Neighbor Index to establish whether villages exhibit clustering, dispersion, or randomness. The Geographic Concentration Index and Imbalance Index then quantify the macro-scale distributional inequality across counties. To visualize specific agglomeration hotspots, we employ Kernel Density Estimation, and to capture directional evolutionary trends across historical periods, we apply Standard Deviation Ellipse analysis. These methods collectively map the “where” and “when” of village distribution.

The second stage is mechanism analysis. Building on the spatial patterns identified in Stage 1, we then investigate the “why” through two complementary approaches. First, to quantify the influence of the hydrological network, we perform river buffer analysis and overlay these buffers with village locations and KDE-derived hotspots. This directly tests the spatial coupling between water systems and cultural agglomerations. Second, we extract topographic, climatic, and socioeconomic variables at village locations and conduct Pearson correlation analysis to identify key drivers and their interactions. This integrated framework allows us to move from pattern description to causal explanation, explicitly operationalizing the basin’s dual nature—its physical connectivity (hydrological network) and cultural connectivity (spatial clustering)—within a single analytical workflow.

NNI is particularly suitable for characterizing the fundamental spatial pattern of traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin. This is a prerequisite for analyzing the driving factors of space-time evolution. KDE is exceptionally suited for identifying localized density hotspots of traditional villages, enabling precise correlation with remote sensing-derived factors. Its continuous surface output transcends administrative boundaries, visually revealing cluster evolution. Limitations include sensitivity to bandwidth selection and boundary effects. In the current research, we primarily utilized kernel density estimation to calculate the density distribution of traditional villages situated within the Wanjiang River Basin. Comprehensive analytical framework

To systematically decode the spatiotemporal evolution of traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin, this study constructs an integrated analytical framework. This framework does not rely on a single method but strategically combines multiple spatial statistics to address distinct, yet complementary, research questions. Each method is selected to reveal a specific dimension of the villages’ spatial characteristics, and their sequential application builds a comprehensive understanding, moving from basic pattern identification to causal mechanism analysis. The framework’s logic is as follows:

First, to establish the fundamental characteristic of the spatial distribution, the Nearest Neighbor Index (NNI) is employed. The NNI answers the primary question: “Are the traditional villages in the basin randomly dispersed, uniformly distributed, or spatially clustered?” Establishing this baseline clustered pattern is a prerequisite for all subsequent analyses.

Second, once a clustered pattern is confirmed, we quantify the nature of this clustering from a macro perspective using two complementary indices. The Geographic Concentration Index (GCI) and the Imbalance Index (S) are used to answer: “How concentrated is this distribution across the different counties of the basin, and is this distribution equitable or imbalanced?” These indices provide a statistical measure of spatial inequality, identifying which administrative units serve as the primary repositories of these villages.Third, while the GCI and S reveal that the distribution is imbalanced, the Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) is employed to visually and spatially pinpoint where the specific agglomeration hotspots are located. KDE answers the question: “What are the precise core areas and secondary clusters of traditional village density within the basin?” This step transforms abstract statistical indices into concrete spatial patterns, visually confirming the “large dispersion, small aggregation” phenomenon.Fourth, to understand how these spatial patterns have evolved over time, the Standard Deviation Ellipse (SDE) is applied. SDE answers the dynamic questions: “What has been the directional trend (e.g., expansion, rotation) of village distribution across different historical periods? How has the center of gravity of these settlements shifted?” This captures the evolutionary trajectory of the entire village system. Finally, building on the spatial patterns identified by the above methods, we investigate the driving forces. This stage answers the question: “Why do the villages exhibit these specific patterns?” We achieve this through two integrated approaches: (1) Hydrological-Cultural Coupling Analysis: By overlaying the KDE-derived hotspots with multi-ring buffers of the river network, we directly test the spatial coupling between cultural agglomerations and the hydrological system, operationalizing the concept of a ‘water-culture corridor’. (2) Correlation Analysis: We extract values of natural and social factors at village locations and conduct Pearson correlation analysis to quantify their influence on the spatial distribution patterns revealed in the previous steps.This integrated framework ensures a logical progression from fundamental pattern description to causal explanation. By explicitly linking each method to a specific research question, it provides a systematic and multi-dimensional diagnosis of the complex spatiotemporal evolution of traditional villages within the Wanjiang River Basin.

Spatial distribution patterns

The traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin are mainly distributed in the “southeast”, and form the corresponding core gathering radiation circle. The southwestern part of Xuancheng City has the highest nuclear density and is also the core area of traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin. These two cities are the secondary core gathering places: Anqing and Chizhou. In general, the Wanjiang River Basin’s historic villages exhibit agglomeration (Fig. 3). Notably, this high-density cultural agglomeration belt exhibits a strong spatial alignment with the mainstream and major tributaries of the Wanjiang River system, visually underscoring a profound coupling between cultural diffusion pathways and the hydrological network. Driving factors analysis of traditional village distribution

The spatial distribution of traditional villages is not random, but rather a long-term adaptation to the natural environment and socio-economic dynamics. This section will go beyond descriptive patterns and investigate potential driving factors. We analyzed how basic natural factors such as terrain, hydrology, and climate create an “initial suitability template” for settlement. Subsequently, we studied how modern social factors, economy, and urbanization serve as “filters” and reshape this historical pattern. The integration of these viewpoints provides a mechanical understanding of the observed spatial outcomes.

Natural factor

Topographical features

In the study, the traditional villages were investigated and analyzed from three aspects: Elevation, Slope, Direction of Slope. The primary objective is to dissect and understand the geographical and temporal evolution characteristics of these villages within the Wanjiang River Basin (Fig. 6). In order to generate relevant data to visualize the impact of natural factors on the distribution of traditional villages, first gather customary village point data and digital elevation model (DEM) data for deriving elevation, slope, and slope direction, import them into a new project; for elevation analysis (Fig. 6a), use the DEM to extract elevation, reclassify it into mountainous, hilly, and plain zones, perform a spatial join or overlay with village points to count villages per zone, and use symbology and chart tools to generate maps and charts; for slope analysis (Fig. 6b), derive slope from the DEM, reclassify it into slope grades, analyze spatial relations with villages, and create corresponding maps and charts; for slope direction analysis (Fig. 6c), calculate slope direction from the DEM, classify it into compass - based groups, analyze with village points, and generate maps and charts; finally, combine these in the layout view, add legends, north arrows, and scale bars, adjust their positions and styles, and export to form Fig. 6. (1) Elevation. GIS analysis reveals that the distribution of traditional villages is heavily skewed towards low elevations: 97% of villages are located below 500 m, with a total of 141 villages. In addition, 67% of the villages are mainly concentrated on plains below 200 m, with a total of 97 villages. The remaining 30% of villages are distributed in hilly areas, with a total of 44 villages, while only 2% of villages survive in mountainous areas above 500 m, with a total of 4 villages. This dramatic gradient underscores that lower elevations provided pre-industrial societies with critical advantages: reduced geophysical risks, lower energy costs for construction and subsistence, superior accessibility to fertile arable land, and more favorable microclimates, all of which were fundamental to ensuring livelihood security and long-term settlement sustainability (Fig. 6a).

(2) Slope. The analysis of slope gradients shows a decisive preference for gentle terrain, with 54.4% of all village sites located on flat slopes (0°-5°) and an additional 26% on gentle slopes (5°-15°). In contrast, only 1.6% of villages were established on steep slopes exceeding 25°. This overwhelming distribution reflects a clear strategy to minimize environmental challenges and maximize agricultural productivity. Gentle slopes effectively reduce soil erosion, simplify farming practices and building construction, and enhance overall living convenience, thereby providing a stable foundation for community development (Fig. 6b).

(3) Slope direction. A clear orientation bias is observed, with south and southeast-facing slopes collectively hosting the highest proportion of villages about 24%, nearly four times that of the least preferred northeastern slopes about6%. This non-random pattern is a direct manifestation of ecological wisdom aimed at optimizing microclimatic conditions. South-facing slopes maximize solar exposure during winter for natural heating while improving ventilation in summer, significantly enhancing thermal comfort and energy efficiency for dwellings. This practical adaptation is deeply embedded in the traditional cultural concept of “sitting north and facing south,” representing a harmonious integration of environmental pragmatism and cultural ideology (Fig. 6c). Hydrology and climate

To delve deeper into the evolution of space and time characteristics of traditional villages within the Wanjiang River Basin, this paper explores them from three aspects: precipitation, air temperature and water system, and carries out visual analyses of these aspects (Fig. 7). (1) Precipitation. An analysis of precipitation shows that all traditional villages are located within the humid zone, with a striking 94% of them concentrated in areas receiving an optimal annual rainfall between 1080 and 1526 mm. The distribution peaks significantly, with 92 villages situated in the most favorable band of 1277 to 1435 mm. This tight clustering within a specific precipitation range is not coincidental but highlights a critical dependency on reliable water availability for agrarian-based settlements. The basin’s subtropical monsoon climate delivers this predictable, ample rainfall, which was fundamental for sustaining paddy agriculture, ensuring domestic water security, and fostering the ecosystem productivity that directly underpinned community prosperity and stability (Fig. 7a).

(2) Climate. The analysis of thermal conditions reveals a remarkable clustering of traditional villages within a highly specific and optimal climatic niche. About 89.7% of the villages, or 130 villages, are located in areas where the annual average temperature is strictly limited between 15.24 ° C and 16.74 ° C, although the temperature range in the basin is wider, from 12.71 °C to 17.88 °C.This pronounced concentration is a strong indicator of a historically determined ‘thermal comfort zone’ for pre-industrial settlement. Within this range, the basin’s subtropical monsoon climate provides sufficient heat for key agricultural activities while minimizing the energy demands and health risks associated with either extreme cold or heat. This optimal thermal environment was therefore a fundamental prerequisite, directly supporting agricultural productivity, year-round habitation comfort, and the overall viability of long-term community establishment (Fig. 7b).

(3) Rainy season. The relationship between villages and water systems is precise and highly non-random. An overwhelming majority of 81% traditional villages across all historical periods were located within a decisive 20–60 m zone from rivers. This overwhelming concentration reflects a calculated settlement strategy that perfectly balances resource access against hydrological risks. The proximity ensured a readily available supply of water for domestic use, irrigation, and potential transportation. Simultaneously, maintaining this specific distance provided a crucial safety buffer against the threats of seasonal flooding and waterlogging, thereby safeguarding lives, crops, and dwellings (Fig. 7c). Road traffic

An analysis of road network density shows that 77% of 111 villages are located in areas with very low traffic 0-0.6 density. This isolation presents a double-edged sword: on one hand, it has historically formed a passive “cultural shield,” limiting external influences and slowing down modernizing transformations, thereby protecting the villages’ historical authenticity. On the other hand, this same inaccessibility now hinders the development of cultural tourism and the infusion of new economic opportunities, posing a challenge to their contemporary revitalization (Fig. 8a).

Gross domestic product

The Anhui Bureau of Statistics published the 2023 Bulletin of Statistics on National Economic and Social Development Statistics (https://www.ah.gov.cn/site/search/6781961?keywords=nationaleconomy) Bulletin encompassing an analysis of Gross Domestic Product (GDP), per capita GDP, and traditional village distribution within the Wanjiang River Basin. The data reveals a discernible pattern where the preservation and distribution of traditional villages are less prevalent in cities with higher total GDP values, such as Hefei City and Wuhu City, and more densely concentrated in cities with lower total GDP values, including Anqing City and Xuancheng City. Hefei City and Wuhu City lead the regional GDP rankings, boasting GDP figures of 1307.4 billion yuan and 1266.48 billion yuan, respectively. However, only 5 and 2 traditional villages are recorded within these areas. Conversely, Anqing City and Xuancheng City, with GDP values of 695.83 billion yuan and 783.58 billion yuan respectively, occupy lower positions in the Wanjiang River Basin’s GDP ranking. Yet, they house 23 and 86 traditional villages, respectively, with Xuancheng City displaying the highest density of traditional villages. This pattern clearly indicates a negative relevance between the number of traditional villages and economic development, with the density of villages increasing as economic growth slows down (Fig. 8b). This relationship not only highlights the need for preserving traditional culture but also suggests that the maintenance of cultural heritage is particularly pertinent in economically less developed regions.

Urbanization

According to the data on urbanization in the statistical yearbooks of each city (https://tjj.ah.gov.cn/oldfiles/tjj/tjjweb/tjnj/2023/index.html), among the nine cities in the Wanjiang River Basin, Hefei, Wuhu and Maanshan have higher urbanization rates and fewer traditional villages. Anqing and Chizhou cities have lower urbanization rates, while traditional villages are more densely distributed. However, individual urban areas are special, such as Lu’an, which has the lowest urbanization rate and only two traditional villages. In general, Less urbanized regions preserve traditional villages more effectively by allocating adequate physical space (Fig. 8c). This is mainly due to three factors. First, the process of urbanization directly promotes the concentration of population and economic factors in cities, leading to the hollowing out of traditional villages. When the urbanization rate exceeds 60%, the exodus of rural labor accelerates, traditional buildings decay naturally due to lack of maintenance, and some villages are transformed into modern residential areas or industrial parks. Second, regions with high urbanization levels tend to be economically developed, with strong demand for land development, and a large number of traditional villages have been demolished or transformed in the course of urban expansion. Again, low urbanization areas are mostly located in mountainous or remote areas with complex geographical conditions and inconvenient transportation, which objectively slows down the erosion of traditional settlements by modern construction. Because of their low economic activity, these areas have preserved the complete spatial pattern of villages and traditional construction techniques. Such spatial distribution characteristics essentially reflect the dynamic game between social development and tradition survival, reminding the need to balance the relationship between modern development and cultural heritage protection in the process of urbanization. Relevant factor

In this study, Pearson’s correlation coefficient and heat map analysis were used to investigate the correlation between the spatial distribution density of traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin and the humanities including GDP, urbanization, and road traffic, and natural factors including precipitation, temperature, water system, elevation, slope, and slope direction. Therefore, the transportation of traditional villages in the Anhui River Basin is the result of multiple factors working together. These correlations reveal key influencing factors, but to understand how they collectively shape spatial patterns, further analysis of their underlying coupling mechanisms is needed.

Coupling mechanism of natural and social factors

Based on the above correlation analysis, this study proposes a coupling framework that includes three core mechanisms to systematically explain the inherent logic of the spatiotemporal evolution of traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin. The pattern of “large scattered and small settlements” in traditional villages is the result of long-term interaction between natural geographical foundations and human socio-economic development.

Firstly, natural factors laid the “initial foundation” for the distribution of villages and triggered “geographical and cultural inertia”. The preference for low altitude plains, gentle slopes near water, and sunny southeast is a manifestation of the “ecological wisdom” of the pre industrial era, aimed at maximizing agricultural productivity and minimizing environmental risks. This optimal location formed the core area of the village in the early Song and Yuan dynasties, such as the junction of Xuancheng and Mount Huangshan, and formed a strong path dependence through the accumulated clan network, cultural inheritance and trade routes.

Secondly, social factors have played a crucial role as a “socio-economic filter” in modern times. The strong negative correlation between GDP, urbanization, and village density revealed by correlation analysis is a manifestation of this mechanism. In economic highlands and rapidly urbanized areas such as Hefei and Wuhu, strong land development pressure, population outflow, and changes in livelihood patterns have directly “filtered” or transformed a large number of traditional villages. On the contrary, regions with relatively lagging economic development, such as Xuancheng and Chizhou, have unexpectedly become “shelters” for traditional villages due to less development pressure.

Ultimately, the contemporary distribution of traditional villages is the spatial presentation of the dynamic game between “historical basis” and “modern screening”. The initial suitability provided by natural conditions determines the areas where villages may thrive; The pressure of modern socio-economic development determines which regions with a long history can survive to this day. This collectively explains why the current pattern presents a macro paradox of “economically developed areas with few villages, while underdeveloped areas become culturally rich”. Discussion

Given that traditional villages within the Wanjiang River Basin hold significant value as components of Chinese cultural heritage and village development, this study selects the region as a case study to analyze the spatial and spatiotemporal evolution characteristics influencing the geographical distribution pattern of these traditional villages. This approach not only enriches the theoretical framework surrounding traditional villages. The research lays down both theoretical and practical foundations for the conservation and enhancement of Chinese rural culture., and it offers a novel approach to the growth and preservation of traditional villages. The findings of this study advance the theoretical and methodological discourse on traditional villages in three key aspects. Theoretically, this study enriches existing descriptive analyses by exploring the formative mechanisms behind spatiotemporal evolution.Methodologically, it presents an integrated diagnostic framework for basin‑scale research.

Traditional village spatial and temporal evolution methodology

This study responds to concerns regarding fragmented spatial analysis by adopting an integrated analytical framework.It applies indicators in combination to form a diagnostic chain, rather than relying on a series of isolated observations.The efficacy of this approach is as follows: In this study, geographic information technology was used to analyze the data of traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin. ArcGIS 10.7 and Origin software were used to calculate and process each data of the villages so as to derive the interrelationships among the factors and analyze them quantitatively, and then further analyze them qualitatively by combining with the actual situation of the Wanjiang River Basin, so as to explore the inherent logic of the development of the traditional villages. The nearest neighbor index, geographic concentration index, imbalance index, standard deviation ellipse, and kernel density estimation were used as the reference methods for the numerical analysis of the traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin, and the results of the spatial analysis showed that: (1) the nearest neighbor index, confirming the significant clustering and distribution pattern of villages; (2) Core density is the most important factor in the development of traditional villages in the Anhui River Basin. The analysis revealed the distribution pattern of “southeast agglomeration and multi-core radiation”, with the highest core density in the southwest of Xuancheng City, and Anqing and Chizhou forming the sub core areas, presenting the overall characteristics of “large dispersion and small agglomeration”; (3) The imbalance index, which confirms the uneven distribution. The standard deviation ellipse analysis shows the spatiotemporal evolution pattern: before the Song Dynasty, the village core located in Chizhou and the southern part of Xuancheng had a flattening rate of 0.273, while during the Sui and Tang Dynasties, the direction angle concentrated in the western part of Xuancheng was 118.5 degrees, with a flattening rate of 0.047; During the Song and Yuan dynasties, the direction angle of its southwest rotation was 92.08 °, with an E value of 0.912. During the Ming and Qing dynasties, the direction angle of its westward expansion was 97.66 °, with an E value of 0.762. The elliptical parameters evolved over time to reflect the intensity of aggregation. The evolution of parameters reflects a decrease in aggregation intensity over time, but still maintains an aggregation trend. This millennium spatial path dependence reveals the geographical inertia mechanism of cultural landscape formation: although the concentration intensity decreases over time, Nearest Neighbour Index shifts from extremely significant to maintaining a trend, the core area of southeastern Anhui Province always maintains the cultural magnetic attraction effect. This has a direct impact on conservation planning: the core areas of historical gathering areas, such as the southwest of Xuancheng and the Anqing Chizhou sub core area, should be designated as priority protection areas for cultural heritage, as their spatial stability demonstrates the continued applicability of these areas to traditional survival models. At the same time, the “large dispersion, small concentration” model indicates that protection strategies should consider the key protection of the core area and the ecological corridor connection of dispersed villages Through the above analysis, based on GIS buffer zone and core density analysis, the concepts of “hydrological connectivity” and “cultural corridor mapping” are put into practice. This study is not just a simple list of villages. It proves that traditional villages are not isolated entities, but systematically organized along the “water culture corridor” defined by river networks and solidified by historical socio-economic activities. Based on the results of data analysis, this study adopts the method of combining the two disciplines of physical geography and socio-economic to study and analyze the traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin, respectively. Among them and selected natural and social factors as the main reference factors, the natural factors include elevation, slope, slope direction, water system, precipitation, and average temperature, and the socio-economic factors include transportation routes, GDP, and urbanization. This method helps clarify the significance of geospatial elements in the region and supports a comprehensive examination of the spatial patterns and evolutionary characteristics of traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin.The GIS spatial analysis shows that 67% of the villages are distributed at an elevation of less than 200 m, 30% are at 200–500 m, and only 2% are more than 500 m, and all the periods from Song, Yuan, and modern times show the characteristics of low elevation agglomeration. In terms of topography, 57% of the villages are located on flat slopes, 25% are located on gentle slopes, and 20% of the villages located on southeast slopes are significantly higher than 5% of the villages located on north slopes. In terms of climate, all villages are located in the humid zone, 94% of the villages have an annual precipitation of 1080–1526 mm, and 81% of the villages have an annual average temperature of 15.24–16.74℃, reflecting the transitional characteristics of monsoon climate. The influence of water system is significant, 55% of the villages are within 2000 m from the water body, and the gradient decreases with the increase of distance. The results show that traditional village site selection has obvious natural geographic element-dependent characteristics, especially in terms of elevation threshold, hydrological convenience and climatic adaptability. This preference for low altitude, gentle slopes, southeast slopes, and adjacent water systems is essentially a spatial expression of pre industrial era “ecological wisdom”: optimizing agricultural production and minimizing disaster risks by maximizing the use of monsoon climate conditions. These parameters provide historical benchmarks for climate adaptive design of contemporary villages, such as reducing heating energy consumption by enhancing winter sunlight on the southeast slope site, and balancing water use and flood control convenience near water rather than near water. The road traffic density of traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin ranges from 0 to 2.2, of which 111 traditional villages are distributed in the range of traffic density from 0 to 0.6, accounting for 77% of the total number of villages. This indicates that the road traffic density in the Wanjiang River Basin is generally low. The distribution of traditional villages in Anqing and Xuancheng cities is dense, and their GDPs are 695.83 billion yuan and 783.58 billion yuan, respectively; the GDPs of Hefei and Wuhu are at the forefront of the Wanjiang River Basin, which are 1,307.4 billion yuan and 1,266.48 billion yuan, but the number of traditional villages is only 5 and 2. The number of traditional villages is negatively correlated with economic development, and a slow economy makes villages denser, which is conducive to the preservation of traditional culture. There is a negative correlation between the urbanization rate and traditional villages in the nine cities of the Anhui River: He-Wu-Ma has a high urbanization rate and fewer villages, while the opposite is true for Anqing and Chizhou. The exception is Lu’an, which has the lowest urbanization rate with only two villages. In general, slower urbanization is more conducive to the survival of traditional villages. System of spatial variables for traditional villages

Relevance of social factors to traditional villages

In the study, social factors mainly refer to important variables such as GDP and urbanization. The results indicate that there is a negative correlation between social factors and village development, and there is a macroscopic gap between the level of economic growth and distribution density of traditional villages. This macro gap is demonstrated by the fact that there are more traditional villages in less developed areas and fewer traditional villages in more developed areas. The GDP of Xuancheng City is 783.58 billion yuan, ranking seventh among the nine cities in the Wanjiang River Basin, and the urbanization rate is 63.55%, ranking sixth in terms of urbanization. This shows that the social and economic level of this place is relatively lagging behind, however, traditional villages make up the majority of the villages in the Wanjiang River Basin roughly 54% of all the villages and are the most densely populated. Conversely, of the nine cities in the Wanjiang River Basin, Hefei, Wuhu, and Maanshan have the fewest traditional villages only 4.8% of all the villages despite having comparatively high economic levels. A common challenge we are currently facing during the process of swift urbanization is how to balance economic growth with the historical preservation of traditional villages to ensure that the original historical and cultural heritage is preserved. This sharp spatial distribution density reflects the obvious connection between the formation of villages and economic development. This Law of “economic development leads to cultural dissipation” constitutes the core paradox of Rural Governance: when the urbanization rate exceeds 60%, the village preservation rate drops sharply to 3%, while Xuancheng maintains the proportion of 59.3% villages at the medium level of 63.55%, which proves that there is a critical threshold effect. This requires the establishment of a new model of urbanization with cultural protection in the first place, the preset cultural heritage red line area in the GDP growth plan, and the transformation of Xuancheng experience into the paradigm of “cultural continuity under medium development intensity”.

In this paper, the social factors also include the road traffic element, and this element determines the spatial transportation of villages to a certain magnitude26. Through the ArcGIS calculation, we can see that about 77% of the traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin have low traffic density, but the inconvenient traffic environment also provides a certain protection space for the villages, and even more local cultural heritage provides a relatively stable physical space. Nevertheless, an underdeveloped transportation system also entails certain drawbacks. This condition is not conducive to the flourishing of the tourism sector and fails to leverage the distinctive cultural advantages inherent in the locale. Consequently, it contributes to the lagging economic status of the region’s tourism industry27. The dialectical relationship of traffic isolation needs fine regulation: 77% of villages are located in the “cultural shield” formed in the low road network density area, which has become a passive protection mechanism in the context of rapid urbanization. However, Lu’an case warns that it is unsustainable to rely solely on traffic isolation. The solution involves developing a “cultural capillary” system through speed-limited landscape highways. This infrastructure connects core villages while maintaining external interference filtration and activating cultural-economic cycles.

Relevance of natural factors to traditional villages

The research for this paper indicates that the location of traditional communities is mostly determined by natural forces. There are 113 traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin, which makes up 67% of all traditional villages. These villages are primarily found in the plains. Conversely, there are just two communities in the highlands. Furthermore, the majority of these communities are found on mild and level slopes; of the total number of villages, roughly 54.4% are found on flat slopes, whereas just 1 village is found on steep slopes. The slope orientation of the villages is mainly in the south direction, accounting for about 40.7% of the villages. Therefore, the traditional villages are more likely to be located in the south direction, where there is plenty of sunlight, which is also in line with the natural geography and the traditional Chinese cultural concept of facing the south from the north. Finally, about 59% of the villages are located 40 to 60 m away from the water system. This shows that the formation of villages is to a certain extent based on good natural conditions, which is conducive to people’s prosperity and provides stable natural conditions for the development of villages. These parameters together build a “golden niche” selection model: 67% of the villages are concentrated in the plain area<200 m, 54.4% are on a 0–5 ° gentle slope, 40.7% are preferred to the south slope (the maximum sunshine is obtained when the solar altitude angle is 35° in winter), and 59% are accurately controlled 40–60 m away from the water system. This kind of spatial allocation improves the agricultural production efficiency by about 30%, and provides a quantitative standard for the construction of contemporary ecological villages. Especially in the context of climate warming, the cooling and ventilation effect of the south slope site selection is more realistic, and should be included in the technical specifications for village renewal.

Relevance of social and natural factors to village development

Under the correlation analysis of social and natural factors, it is found that the correlation value between GDP28 and temperature is 0.45, and its Pearson’s coefficient29 is in the range of 1 to 0.5. In addition, the correlation between urbanization and precipitation is -0.57 and the a Pearson’s coefficient was − 0.5 to -0.72, suggesting that village development is influenced not only by a single factor, but also by a combination of social and natural factors. In addition, under the study of correlation data, it can be seen that the correlation between urbanization rate and GDP is the strongest and the Pearson’s coefficient is the highest in the range of 1 to 0.5 showing a positive correlation, while the correlation between the altitude and temperature is the strongest and the Pearson’s coefficient is the lowest in the range of -0.5 to -0.72 showing a negative correlation. Thus, the historical evolution of villages30 is the result of two or more factors31, and it is a complex process in which multidimensional factors are intertwined32. The multi factor coupling effect affects the fate of villages through three typical interactive paths: (1) GDP temperature positive correlation chain: economic development → heat island effect → failure of historical temperature adaptation; (2) Urbanization precipitation negative correlation chain (-0.57): cement surface increase → precipitation infiltration decrease → traditional hydrological balance destruction; (3) The strong negative correlation between elevation and temperature conflicts with modern transportation: mountain villages migrate to high altitude to avoid heat, but are limited by the lag of road construction. These intertwined effects require the establishment of a “culture climate economy” triple governance framework: embedding cultural heritage climate risk assessment in GDP planning, and transforming historical ecological wisdom into modern sponge village construction parameters.

Formation mechanisms of the spatiotemporal patterns

The spatiotemporal distribution and evolution of traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin are not random artifacts but the legacies of underlying socio-ecological mechanisms. Our analysis reveals that the observed patterns—specifically the persistent southeastern aggregation, the evolution from discrete to clustered distributions, and the stark negative correlation with economic development are primarily governed by three interrelated formative processes.

First, Geocultural Inertia and Path Dependence have played a fundamental role in stabilizing the core distribution areas. The initial establishment of villages during the pre-Song and Song-Yuan periods was contingent upon favorable topographic and hydrological conditions, exemplified by the plains at the junction of Xuancheng and Huangshan cities. Once established, these settlements generated self-reinforcing advantages through accumulated socio-cultural capital, including entrenched clan networks, established trade routes, and inherited architectural knowledge. This process created a powerful path dependence, whereby subsequent development was preferentially channeled into these existing cores rather than pioneering entirely new, high-cost locations. This mechanism directly explains the remarkable spatial stability of the primary and secondary core areas from the Southern Song to the Ming-Qing periods, despite the overall intensification of clustering, manifesting as a persistent cultural magnetic field in the southeastern part of the basin.

Second, the evolutionary trajectory has been significantly shaped by the mechanism of Socioeconomic Filtering and Developmental Pressure. The strong negative correlation between traditional village density and GDP or urbanization levels is a diagnostic indicator of this process. In regions experiencing rapid economic transformation, such as Hefei and Wuhu, the modern forces of urban expansion, land commodification, and shifts in livelihood structures have physically erased, fragmented, or profoundly altered traditional village landscapes. The high opportunity cost of preserving low-density, agrarian settlements in prime development zones makes their survival untenable. Conversely, the high density of villages in areas like Xuancheng and Chizhou is not merely a historical artifact but a direct outcome of their relative economic lag, which has inadvertently acted as a buffer against these transformative pressures. Thus, the contemporary distribution map is effectively a palimpsest, revealing those areas that have successfully passed through the filter of intense modern development.

Third, underpinning both the initial settlement and their long-term viability is the mechanism of Pre-Industrial Ecological Optimization. The non-random spatial preferences for sites below 200 m in elevation, on gentle zero-to-five-degree south-facing slopes, and within a critical forty-to-sixty-meter proximity to water sources, represent a rational, empirically-derived site selection strategy. This strategy systematically maximized agricultural productivity, minimized environmental risks such as flooding and slope instability, reduced construction effort, and optimized microclimatic conditions for dwelling by maximizing solar gain in winter. Consequently, this ecological wisdom acted as the primary initial filter, channeling early human settlement into the most environmentally amenable corridors of the southeastern basin and providing the foundational conditions for their long-term sustainability, upon which the later processes of inertia and filtering acted.

In synthesis, the spatiotemporal pattern of traditional villages is the product of these mechanisms operating in concert: Ecological Optimization defined the initial set of suitable locations; Geocultural Inertia amplified and stabilized these initial choices over centuries; and Socioeconomic Filtering determined which of these historical clusters survived into the modern era. This explanatory framework moves beyond descriptive correlations to support a deeper understanding of the processes shaping the landscape. It suggests that the preservation of traditional villages may be viewed as a dynamic process, in which historical legacies interact with contemporary developmental pressures.

Implications for the formulation of protection policies

This study explores the spatiotemporal evolution and driving mechanisms of traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin. Its findings may offer useful references for the development of targeted conservation and development policies.Based on the analysis of natural and social factors mentioned above, especially the negative correlation between village distribution and GDP, urbanization rate, as well as their ecological preferences for low altitude, near water sources, and southward slopes, we propose the following three-level protection strategies: Specifically, a “cultural heritage protection red line” should be established in the historical core cluster areas identified by nuclear density analysis, such as the southwest of Xuancheng, to implement strict overall protection centered on maintaining the texture of traditional settlements and landscape corridors. At the same time, it is necessary to rely on the spatiotemporal evolution path from “southeast northwest” to “east-west” revealed by the standard deviation ellipse, as well as the distribution characteristics of villages closely attached to water systems, to plan and construct interconnected “hydrological cultural corridors”. By restoring ancient post roads and constructing ecological trails, isolated village heritage sites can be connected into a network, thus solving the dilemma of “islanding” protection. For sub core areas such as Chizhou and Anqing, as well as other sparsely distributed villages, a strategy of “precise activation” should be adopted to encourage the development of characteristic homestays, intangible cultural heritage workshops and other small and micro businesses based on unique architectural heritage or intangible cultural heritage, in order to stimulate endogenous motivation.

Secondly, it is necessary to establish a dynamic regulatory mechanism that is compatible with the level of regional economic development. This study clearly reveals a significant negative correlation between traditional village density, GDP, and urbanization rate, which requires protection policies to have high situational sensitivity. In economic highlands such as Hefei and Wuhu, policies should focus on rigid constraints, by forcibly incorporating traditional villages and their surrounding environments as “scarce cultural spaces” into national spatial planning, and using policy tools such as development rights transfer and plot ratio rewards to compensate for land development rights restricted by protection. On the contrary, in areas with abundant village resources but relatively lagging economy such as Xuancheng and Chizhou, policies should be more incentive oriented. This can be achieved by establishing a “Traditional Village Revitalization Fund” to guide social capital, develop ecological agriculture and cultural tourism, and make the protection achievements benefit local communities, achieving a virtuous cycle of “developing in protection and better protecting in development”.

Ultimately, the vitality of protective policies lies in their deep integration with sustainable development goals. This study found that the systematic preference of village site selection for low altitude plains, gentle slopes, southward slopes, and near water locations is a direct reflection of the “ecological wisdom” of our ancestors. This provides a valuable paradigm for contemporary rural construction: in the planning of new villages and the improvement of living environments, we should actively draw on these historical wisdom, such as establishing elastic buffer zones in waterfront areas to balance water demand and flood control safety, or promoting passive solar energy design that conforms to the terrain to reduce building energy consumption. More importantly, we should promote the coordinated development of “culture ecology industry”, transform traditional ecological landscapes such as terraced fields, water systems, and feng shui forests into sustainable tourism resources, and make cultural heritage protection the core engine for promoting rural green revitalization, rather than a burden on development. By integrating the wisdom of history into contemporary practice, we can find a sustainable path for traditional villages that can both preserve their cultural heritage and rejuvenate their vitality.

Global experience and China’s path in the protection and revitalization of traditional villages

This study draws on experiences from the Wanjiang River Basin in China, and its findings may offer useful insights for the conservation and revitalization of traditional villages in comparable contexts worldwide. Some of the key challenges observed in this analysis, particularly the spatial pattern of “greater dispersion and smaller aggregation” formed by natural factors, as well as the vulnerability of villages under socio-economic filtering pressure revealed by strong negative correlations with GDP and urbanization, require policy solutions that are spatially clear and contextually adaptive. The protection practice of traditional villages at home and abroad shows different paths and consensus.

In international cases, the successful experience of different types of villages is particularly noteworthy: ecologically sensitive villages, cultural landscape driven villages and economic activation oriented villages. The core of their success depends on the collaborative framework of “community empowerment + institutional flexibility + industrial adaptation”, which forms a cross regional resonance with China’s “government community market” ternary mechanism proposed in this study. These international models may offer useful references for understanding the specific dynamics observed in the Anhui basin. For example, the European “cultural landscape” approach seen in Italy directly validates and improves our proposed strategy of creating a “hydrological cultural corridor”. This method provides a systematic approach to protect the symbiotic relationship between villages and their natural environment, thereby aligning our kernel density analysis that shows village clusters closely with river systems. The spatiotemporal evolution path captured by the standard deviation ellipse, that is, from southeast, northwest to east, and west, reveals the connectivity in practice.

Internationally, European countries have adopted legislation to incorporate traditional villages into the cultural heritage system, emphasizing “living protection” and community autonomy, and have achieved remarkable results in balancing tourism development and cultural heritage33; Japan’s “one village, one product” model also echoes the idea of local exploration in China. In contrast, the protection of traditional villages in China started late, focusing on the static protection of material heritage in the early stage. However, based on a cornerstone of our research, the clear negative correlation between survival and economic development highlights the unique and enormous pressure faced by Chinese villages, which require learning and adapting to international experience. But in recent years, it has gradually explored the dynamic mode of “government led + social participation + market operation”, such as Anhui strengthening the system security through local legislation, and Fujian innovating the “building adoption” mechanism of social capital34. However, in terms of the depth of community participation and the stimulation of endogenous cultural power, domestic practice still lags behind international experience. This gap is particularly critical given the role of community cohesion as a potential counterweight to the “socio-economic filtering” mechanism identified in our study, wherein rapid urbanization and economic pressure threaten village preservation. It is necessary to learn from the Italian community autonomy model and improve the voice of residents in protection decisions through the villagers’ Protection Committee.

The distribution of traditional villages in the Wanjiang River Basin is influenced by natural geographical and socio-economic factors. Our research found that 77% of villages are located in areas with low traffic density, highlighting the double-edged sword of isolation, which provides a passive barrier but also limits development. This precise quantification requires policy learning from international examples of managing accessible environments without sacrificing cultural integrity. In addition, the decision-making power of Chinese communities still lags behind the design of the system. The common “artist villager co creation” model internationally indicates that the cultural subjectivity of residents is the key to endogenous revitalization. This is crucial for translating the “ecological wisdom” of historical site selection into sustainable contemporary community practices, which we quantify as a preference for slopes facing south at an altitude of less than 200 m and a precise distance of 40–60 m from water, to ensure that new developments respect natural parameters that ensure historical sustainability.

In this regard, based on our empirical research results and global wisdom, we suggest promoting it from three aspects in the future. Firstly, improve the regulatory framework. The core area determined by the core density analysis, such as the southwest of Xuancheng, requires a legally designated “priority protected area”, reflecting Europe’s strict protection of core heritage to resist the development pressure quantified by our correlation analysis. The second is to establish a collaborative mechanism of “government community market”. In areas such as Xuancheng and Chizhou, our analysis indicates that due to relative economic backwardness, these regions have high-density clusters, and policies must act as an “activating catalyst” to create incentives for protection, consistent with the community empowerment models in Japan and Italy. Thirdly, strengthen interdisciplinary technical support. The core dynamic database and GIS technology of this research method must become the foundation for continuous and adaptive governance, providing the time granularity required to monitor the spatiotemporal evolution of our records.

Global experience shows that the sustainable protection of traditional villages requires finding a balance between institutional rigidity and community flexibility. The core inspiration of China’s road lies in the unique interaction between natural determinants and socio-economic pressures in our river basin, which can provide reference for rapidly urbanizing regions in East Asia and the world. This framework of “unified principles and diverse strategies”, as shown in our research, is based on the specific quantitative characteristics of a region and is a fundamental requirement for sustainable protection of the global village.

Conclusion

By operationalizing a two-stage spatial analytical framework within the Wanjiang River Basin—first detecting patterns through standard spatial statistics, then testing for hydrological-cultural coupling through overlay and correlation analyses, this study provides systematic evidence for the nonlinear evolutionary mechanisms shaping traditional village distributions. The findings demonstrate that the observed patterns are not random but result from three interacting processes: historical path dependence anchored by natural suitability, amplification through geocultural inertia, and contemporary socioeconomic filtering. This framework of “geographic-cultural inertia” and “socio-economic filtering” offers insights for establishing priority protection zones and designing cultural-ecological corridors in similar watersheds.
