Soil microbial life history strategies covary with ecosystem multifunctionality across aridity gradients
Authors: Tao Zhou, Manuel Delgado-Baquerizo, Chengjie Ren, Nianpeng He, Zhenghu Zhou, Yanghui He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAs global climate change escalates aridity, ecosystem functions face increasing risk of collapse. Understanding functional persistence under aridity, particularly the contribution of soil microbiomes, is crucial for effective conservation strategies. Here, we showed that microbial life history strategies of high yield, resource acquisition, and stress tolerance covary with ecosystem multifunctionality (EMF) across aridity gradients. These connections offer a framework to enhance our understanding of microbial-driven ecosystem processes through trait-based approaches. Additionally, by elucidating the mechanistic links between aridity, microbial strategies, and functions, our large-scale survey reveals an aridity threshold beyond which plot-level EMF declines sharply, highlighting potential vulnerability of dryland ecosystems under climate change.

Aridity, exacerbated by global climate change, poses one of the most significant challenges to terrestrial ecosystems worldwide (1–3). Water scarcity reduces ecosystem services by disrupting ecosystem multifunctionality (EMF), the ability of ecosystems to simultaneously provide multiple functions (4, 5). Emerging evidence indicates that EMF responds nonlinearly to increasing aridity, exhibiting critical thresholds beyond which ecosystem functions decline abruptly (6, 7). Berdugo et al. (7) first reported a sequence of abrupt ecological shifts in global drylands, commencing with reduced plant productivity, followed by impaired soil fertility, and culminating in the loss of plant cover and richness. Furthermore, it is known that aridity thresholds shape the biodiversity and function of soils (8). Soil microbiomes, among the most abundant and functionally diverse biological communities of the planet, support almost all ecosystem functions (9); aridity is known to modulate the relationship between soil microbial diversity and function across local to global scales (6, 10, 11). Yet, less is known about the strategies employed by soil microbes to support EMF across aridity gradients.

Life history strategies, shaped by physiological and evolutionary trade-offs, determine resource allocation for organismal survival, growth, and reproduction (12, 13). The recently developed high yield–resource acquisition–stress tolerance (Y-A-S) life history strategies framework offers valuable insights into microbial adaptation to environmental gradients (14, 15). Y-strategy prevails in resource-rich environments but is less competitive under stressful or resource-limited conditions. A-strategy efficiently exploits limited resources, whereas S-strategy thrives in stressful conditions such as aridity (14, 16). Consequently, shifts in the prevalence of microbial Y-A-S life history strategies along aridity gradients likely offer crucial mechanistic insights into the observed sharp declines in EMF. Advancing our understanding of how aridification alters the capacity of soil microbes to support function is key to anticipating changes in fundamental ecosystem services such as soil fertility, carbon sequestration, and water regulation.

Here, we analyzed 474 soil samples from 158 terrestrial sites spanning a broad aridity gradient (aridity index, the ratio of mean annual precipitation to mean annual potential evapotranspiration: 0.02 to 1.48) across more than 9.6 million square kilometers (Fig. 1A). Metagenomic sequencing combined with physiological assays was employed to assess Y-A-S life history strategies (14). Concurrently, we quantified twenty ecosystem functions related to plant productivity, water regulation, soil fertility, soil carbon sequestration, microbial habitat, and decomposition (SI Appendix, Table S1). Plant productivity was measured by net primary productivity. Water regulation was assessed through soil’s maximum water-holding capacity and moisture. Soil fertility was evaluated based on soil nitrogen and phosphorus availability. Soil carbon sequestration was measured by soil mineral-associated organic carbon and dissolved organic carbon. Microbial habitat serves as an indicator of the soil’s capacity to support microbial life (17, 18), and it was measured by microbial biomass carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus (19–21). Microbial decomposition was assessed by eight exoenzymes responsible for the decay of soil organic matter. While our EMF metric broadly reflects ecosystem functioning, its strong weighting toward soil processes means our inferences apply most directly to soil-related multifunctionality and should be extended to ecosystem-wide multifunctionality with caution. Specifically, our study aimed to (1) identify aridity thresholds for EMF and determine the sequence of threshold responses for individual functions; and (2) evaluate the relative contributions of microbial Y-A-S strategies to EMF across aridity gradient. Our study would improve the comprehensive understanding of how aridification alters microbial strategies to support functions, providing essential insights for developing effective adaptation and mitigation strategies to maintain EMF.

Results and Discussion

Understanding the contribution of different microbial strategies to supporting function is critical to better anticipate changes in terrestrial ecosystems under climate change. Our national-scale study indicates that soil microbial functions are relatively more resistant to aridity than plant productivity and soil fertility. Moreover, microbial life history strategies such as high yield, resource acquisition, and stress tolerance are correlated with EMF across aridity gradients. By linking large-scale EMF patterns to these microbial life history strategies, our study offers potential mechanistic insights into the microbial regulation of EMF under aridification processes.

Responses of Soil Microbial Function to Aridity.

Our analysis supports aridity index as the strongest predictor of EMF variation across 158 sites (Fig. 1B), revealing a nonlinear response characterized by a critical threshold (aridity index: 0.48) beyond which minor increases in aridity trigger precipitous EMF declines (Fig. 2 A and B). The robustness of this nonlinear relationship is further substantiated by multithreshold multifunctionality (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Notably, this aridity threshold of EMF occurs at a higher aridity index (i.e., in more humid conditions) compared to the thresholds previously reported (~0.3) for many individual functions specifically within global drylands (7, 22). This apparent aridity sensitivity of EMF here may arise from three factors. First, the nature of an ecosystem is that it cannot simultaneously maximize all functions because trade-offs between individual ecosystem functions are common (23–25). The principal component analysis further elucidates this, revealing the trade-offs between individual functions (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Specifically, microbial function of the decomposition of recalcitrant organic matter (represented by two oxidases of phenol oxidase and peroxidase) displays a greater value in arid soils, opposing the trend of most other functions (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Therefore, high EMF requires coordination across multiple functions (23, 26), making it susceptible to water-sensitive ones, which can trigger EMF decline even if more resistant functions persist. Second, prior studies focused on chronically adapted dryland ecosystems, where biological communities have likely evolved more effective aridity resistance and functional maintenance mechanisms (27–29). Consequently, drylands likely possess lower aridity sensitivity than the broader ecosystems assessed here. In addition, the specific functions selected may also contribute to such divergency. Here, nineteen out of twenty variables are measurements of soil functions, which differ from global drylands (7, 22), as those studies included many aboveground functions. The identification of such thresholds is critical for understanding ecosystem degradation trajectories. Thus, the observed high aridity thresholds suggest that sampled ecosystems in China may be more sensitive than those in chronically arid regions.

Individual ecosystem functions also exhibit nonlinear responses to increasing aridity, resulting in a sequence of aridity thresholds for functions. This sequence starts with declines in plant productivity, followed by soil disruptions, and finally, disruptions in microbial habitat and decomposition (Fig. 2C). The initial decline in plant productivity preceding soil disruption aligns with and is well discussed in a previous study (7, 22). Briefly, plant physiological adaptations to intensifying aridity, such as altered leaf morphology and stomatal closure, inherently constrain photosynthesis and reduce productivity (30, 31). While soil microbiomes are often considered highly sensitive to water stress (32, 33), we observed that microbial habitat and decomposition functions are among the last to respond to aridity, with thresholds occurring under relatively more arid conditions compared to other functions (Fig. 2C). Despite being ultimately impacted by aridity, their delayed response suggests greater resistance, which may stem from three factors. First, high functional redundancy within diverse microbial communities allows maintenance of habitat and decomposition despite compositional shifts (34). Second, widespread microbial dormancy strategies enable tolerance of prolonged aridity and rapid reactivation upon rewetting (35). Third, soil microbiomes in arid conditions might sustain activity through alternative carbon acquisition pathways, such as autotrophic fixation or the recycling of microbial necromass (36), buffering these microbial functions against the declines of plants and soil.

Y-A-S Life History Strategies Explain EMF Across Aridity Gradients.

Microorganisms face trade-offs in allocating resources between distinct high-yield (Y), resource acquisition (A), and stress tolerance (S) strategies to adapt to diverse environments (14). These life history trade-offs shape their metabolic activities and subsequently influence their contributions to ecosystem functions (15, 37). As aridity increased, soil microbiomes exhibited a marked shift from Y-strategy dominance toward S-strategy dominance (Fig. 3A), indicating microbial aridity adaptation via altered life history strategies. This trend is further supported across distinct aridity zones (SI Appendix, Fig. S3): The S-strategy is predominant in arid zones, declining sharply in semiarid zones, and remaining at low abundance throughout the dry sub-humid and humid zones. Conversely, the Y-strategy exhibits a significant stepwise increase with rising humidity between the arid and dry sub-humid zones. Our study provides evidence that these Y-A-S strategies covary with EMF along aridity gradients. Specifically, Y-strategy shows a consistent positive association with EMF in both arid and humid ecosystems (Fig. 3B). This aligns with findings linking higher microbial growth yield (carbon use efficiency) to enhanced soil organic carbon storage in a recent meta-analysis (38) and experimental study (39). Growth-oriented communities (Y-strategy) support faster carbon/nutrient turnover, greater microbial biomass accumulation, and increased microbial-derived carbon formation, thereby enhancing EMF (38). However, the correlation (both slope and R-squared) between EMF and Y-strategy was stronger in arid than humid ecosystems (Fig. 3B), suggesting water stress amplifies this positive effect of high growth yield on EMF.

Conversely, the S-strategy, prevalent in arid ecosystems (15), has a significant negative correlation with EMF in these ecosystems (Fig. 3D). S-strategy maximizes investment in mechanisms like osmolyte production to maintain cellular osmolarity, requiring substantial resources and energy (14, 15). Therefore, fewer resources and less energy are available for allocation to metabolic activities that directly contribute to EMF in these water- and resource-limited soils. As the resource and energy costs associated with the S-strategy are negligible under humid conditions compared to the rich resources, this strategy showed no significant relationship with EMF there (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, as expected, a consistent negative relationship was observed between the microbial A-strategy and EMF across aridity gradient (Fig. 3C). This relationship likely occurs because the A-strategy necessitates investing metabolically costly materials to enhance resource acquisition, often at the expense of microbial investment in growth yield and EMF (14, 40).

Our results also indicated that rare microbial taxa positively correlate with EMF exclusively in arid ecosystems, a pattern consistent across both fungal and bacterial communities (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Figs. S4, and S5). This differential contribution aligns with the life history strategic differentiation of microbial taxa along aridity gradient. In arid ecosystems, strong environmental pressures favor S-strategy among abundant taxa, prioritizing survival and resistance over EMF maintenance (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Consequently, metabolically active rare taxa (41, 42), potentially adopting Y-strategy under stress conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), may become disproportionately important for maintaining foundational functions (Fig. 4A) (43, 44). This aligns with the observation that rare taxa contribute significantly to low functional thresholds (>10% and >30% of their maximum rates or availabilities; Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Conversely, in humid ecosystems, abundant taxa may possess the biomass and metabolic capacity (45) to sustain high functional thresholds (>80% of their maximum rates or availabilities; Fig. 4D and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). In addition, rare taxa predominantly adopt S-strategy in humid regions (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), contributing to EMF stability below the aridity threshold. Overall, this strategic differentiation between rare and abundant taxa further underscores the central role of microbial life history strategies in regulating EMF under environmental stress.

Limitations of the Current Study.

There are several limitations in the current large-scale study. First, although our large soil survey provides empirical evidence that soil microbial life history strategies covary with EMF across aridity gradients and discusses the potential mechanism behind such relationships, our study did not conduct direct manipulation experiments supporting this causality. The framework of our study needs future experimental validation. Second, the S-strategy was informed by metagenomic reads (14, 46), which are predominantly assigned from bacteria and archaea. However, EMF was supported by multiple elements of soil biodiversity, including fungi and animals (47). Besides metagenomic reads, a realistic proxy of S-strategy is urgently needed. For example, extracellular polymeric substances, a self-produced matrix, which can connect microorganisms and substrates and consequently confer advantages to microorganisms in stress conditions (48, 49). Third, one of the challenges in EMF field is the calculation of EMF and the function selection. The trade-offs between individual functions and functions interact with one another (SI Appendix, Fig. S2) (23–25) are expected to lead to inconsistent conclusions because the conclusions can be influenced by function combination. Fourth, our EMF metric is heavily weighted toward soil functions, meaning our conclusions pertain most directly to soil-related multifunctionality. Generalizations to broader ecosystem-wide multifunctionality, particularly aboveground processes, should therefore be made with caution (23). Fifth, considering the labor, cost, and accessibility, our study just focused on the correlation in growth peak season. In addition, the sampled sites are also uneven spatial distribution, especially in the Tibetan Plateau, which may also introduce uncertainty.

Conclusion

Our large-scale survey identifies a critical aridity threshold that precipitates abrupt declines in EMF and reveals sequential functional thresholds where microbial functions show higher resistance compared to plant productivity and soil fertility as aridity increases. These insights are vital for developing informed climate adaptation strategies, particularly for vulnerable regions facing increased aridity. Notably, we provide large-scale evidence linking microbial Y-A-S life history strategies to EMF along the aridity gradient. The Y-strategy was positively correlated with EMF across aridity gradients, particularly under water stress, whereas the S-strategy is associated with lower EMF in arid ecosystems, consistent with the costs of stress tolerance. Importantly, the trait-based framework would elevate microbial ecology to a more central role in understanding the responses of multiple ecosystem processes to multiple environmental changes beyond EMF and aridity reported here. For example, the mean annual temperature can decrease microbial Y-strategy, while microbial communities could adapt to warming by increasing Y-strategy at higher temperatures. Such an adjustment would alter the fate of soil organic carbon and EMF under a warming world because of a positive correlation between Y-strategy and soil organic carbon (50, 51). Overall, our comprehensive experimental evidence clarified how microbial life history strategies interact with soil-related ecosystem processes and multifunctionality, informing climate adaptation strategies reliant on soil ecological resilience.

Materials and Methods

Field Survey and Soil Sampling.

Between June and August 2022, we conducted a field survey across China, collecting 474 soil samples from 158 sites representing major natural ecosystem types (Fig. 1A). The surveyed ecosystems included alpine forests (11 sites), alpine grasslands (27 sites), northern forests (10 sites), deserts (9 sites), desert grasslands (16 sites), Gobi (10 sites), subtropical forests (27 sites), temperate forests (22 sites), temperate grasslands (17 sites), and tropical forests (9 sites). This design aimed to encompass a broad spectrum of environmental conditions, soil microbiomes, and ecosystem functions.

At each site, located >200 m from roadsides, we established three replicate plots following a standardized protocol. Grassland plots measured 5 m × 5 m and were spaced >10 m apart, whereas forest plots measured 20 m × 20 m with >30 m separating replicates. Within each plot, ten soil cores (0 to 20 cm depth) were randomly collected and combined to form a composite sample. These composite samples were immediately sieved (2 mm mesh), homogenized, stored on ice, and transported to the laboratory within 48 h. In the laboratory, each sample was divided into two subsamples: One was air-dried for biochemical analyses, and the other was immediately frozen at −20 °C for molecular analyses. For each sampling location, we recorded the latitude, longitude, and altitude. Mean annual temperature, mean annual precipitation, annual maximum temperature, annual minimum temperature, precipitation seasonality, and temperature seasonality were obtained from WorldClim 2.0. Aridity index (the ratio of mean annual precipitation to mean annual potential evapotranspiration) was obtained from global aridity index and potential evapotranspiration climate database v2 (52). Soil properties were determined using the air-dried subsamples following standardized protocols (4). Specifically, soil pH was measured using a pH meter in a 1:2.5 soil-to-water suspension (w/v) (53), and soil texture (percentages of clay, silt, and sand) was determined using a Malvern laser particle size analyzer (MS2000; Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK).

Soil Microbial Diversity.

Soil bacterial and fungal diversity were assessed via amplicon sequencing. DNA was extracted from 10 g of frozen soil, previously ground using a mortar and pestle, using a DNA isolation kit. The bacterial 16S rRNA gene V3-V4 region was amplified using primers 338F (ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG) and 806R (GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT), while the fungal ITS1 region was amplified using primers ITS1F (CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA) and ITS2R (GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC). PCR amplification was conducted using TransStart FastPfu DNA Polymerase (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) on a GeneAmp 9700 thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). Thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; followed by 27 cycles (bacteria) or 35 cycles (fungi) of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Amplified PCR products were purified from 2% agarose gels using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq PE250 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Raw sequencing reads were subsequently demultiplexed, quality-filtered, and processed using QIIME2 (54) to generate Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) at 100% sequence identity.

Classification of Abundant and Rare Microbial Taxa.

To minimize noise in identifying rare taxa, ASVs with fewer than 20 reads across all samples were excluded from further analysis. To standardize sequencing depth across samples, the data were rarefied to a common number of sequences (47139 reads for fungi and 24846 reads for bacteria) per sample prior to subsequent analyses (55). Following rarefaction, ASVs within each sample were classified as abundant (relative abundance > 0.1%) or rare (relative abundance < 0.01%) microbial taxa based on their respective relative abundances (56, 57).

Metagenomic Sequencing.

To characterize the functional potential of the soil microbiome, total DNA was extracted from six technical replicates (0.5 g each) per composite soil sample using the FastDNA™ Spin Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA). DNA quality and quantity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Metagenomic sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform (150 bp paired-end), yielding approximately 10 GB of raw data per sample. Raw sequencing reads underwent rigorous quality control. Adapter sequences were removed, and reads were quality-trimmed using Sickle. Reads shorter than 50 bp or containing ambiguous bases (N) were discarded. Subsequently, reads aligning with the human genome were removed. High-quality reads were assembled using MEGAHIT (58), with individual sample assemblies followed by coassembly of unmapped reads across all samples to maximize contig recovery. Gene prediction was performed on contigs exceeding 200 bp using MetaGeneMark, and per-base coverage was calculated by mapping reads back to contigs. Genes associated with stress tolerance were identified using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database to represent microbial stress tolerance traits, referencing previous studies.

Microbial Y-A-S Life History Strategies.

Microbial life history strategies were assessed using the high yield–resource acquisition–stress tolerance (Y-A-S) framework, following the guidance of Malik et al. (14). The Y-strategy emphasizes maximizing growth and yield by prioritizing core carbon metabolism and biosynthesis while minimizing investment in stress tolerance and resource acquisition (14). The Y-strategy was quantified using microbial carbon use efficiency and the ratio of microbial biomass carbon to microbial basal respiration (37). Microbial carbon use efficiency was directly measured via a substrate-independent 18O incorporation protocol. Fresh soil subsamples were adjusted to 20% water content using 18O-H2O, while parallel control samples received unlabeled water. Samples were incubated in sealed tubes for 24 h at 25 °C, and CO2 production was monitored continuously via an infrared gas analyzer (IRGA). Following incubation and DNA extraction, IRMS-TC/EA (Thermo Scientific) at the Laboratory of Ecological Indicators Analysis (Institute of Geographic Sciences and Natural Resources Research, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing, China) was used to determine 18O incorporation and total oxygen content, enabling CUE calculation following the method of Zhou et al. (59). Second, microbial biomass carbon was quantified using the chloroform fumigation-extraction method, while basal respiration was measured by incubating 5 g of fresh soil at 25 °C for 24 h and quantifying evolved CO2. Ratio of microbial biomass carbon to microbial basal respiration reflects microbial energy use efficiency, with higher values indicating greater yield (60, 61). To enable comparison, both microbial carbon use efficiency and the microbial biomass carbon to respiration ratio were normalized using min-max transformation (0-1) and then averaged to derive the Y-strategy. Microbial extracellular enzymatic activities provide estimates of microbial ability to degrade various complex substrates, which serve as critical proxies for the A-strategy (15). We measured the potential activities of soil extracellular enzymes crucial for the carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus acquisition, including phosphatase, β-1,4-glucosidase, leucine aminopeptidase, N-acetylglucosaminidase, phosphorylase, urease, phenol oxidase, and peroxidase. These enzyme activities were measured using fluorimetric and colorimetric methods (62). Specific enzyme activities were calculated by normalizing potential activities to microbial biomass carbon. These specific activities for all measured enzymes were then subjected to min-max transformation (0-1), and the resulting normalized values were averaged to derive the A-strategy.

The S-strategy refers to microbial investment in stress tolerance mechanisms, such as DNA repair, osmotic protection, and maintenance of cell or genome integrity (14). Using the metagenomic data and KEGG annotations outlined above, we identified stress tolerance genes based on the datasets proposed by Li et al. (40). We then calculated the total number of reads associated with stress-tolerance genes. Subsequently, the total number of reads was min-max transformation (ranging from 0 to 1) across all samples to enable comparison. The normalized total number of reads was considered the S-strategy.

Ecosystem Multifunctionality (EMF).

We evaluated EMF by assessing twenty functional attributes (SI Appendix, Table S1) related to six key ecosystem services and functions (47): soil carbon sequestration (mineral-associated organic carbon and dissolved organic carbon), plant productivity (net primary productivity), soil fertility (available phosphorus, ammonium nitrogen, nitrate nitrogen, and dissolved organic nitrogen), microbial decomposition (phosphatase, β-1,4-glucosidase, leucine aminopeptidase, N-acetylglucosaminidase, phosphorylase, urease, phenol oxidase, and peroxidase), microbial habitat (microbial biomass carbon, microbial biomass nitrogen, and microbial biomass phosphorus), and water regulation (soil maximum water holding capacity and moisture). These attributes collectively provide a comprehensive assessment of key ecosystem services and functions.

Mineral-associated organic carbon was determined following the protocol of Lugato et al. (63). Dissolved organic carbon and total dissolved nitrogen were quantified from 0.5 M K2SO4 extracts (1:10 w/v) using a multi-N/C2100 elemental analyzer. Available phosphorus, ammonium nitrogen, and nitrate nitrogen were extracted with 0.5 M NaHCO3 and 2 M KCl, respectively, and quantified in the supernatant using continuous flow analysis. Net primary productivity (NPP), at a resolution of 250 m, was obtained from the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) product. The potential activities of extracellular enzymes (phosphatase, β-1,4-glucosidase, leucine aminopeptidase, N-acetylglucosaminidase, phosphorylase, and urease) were measured using 2.75 g of fresh soil and a microplate fluorescence assay (62). Phenol oxidase and peroxidase activities were determined using colorimetric assays. Microbial biomass carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus were estimated using the chloroform fumigation-extraction method, applying extraction correction factors (KEC = 0.45, KEN = 0.45, KEP = 0.40) (64). Soil moisture was determined gravimetrically after drying samples at 105 °C for 24 h. Soil maximum water holding capacity was determined using composite samples from each plot (with two analytical replicates) following the method described by Veihmeyer and Hendrickson (65).

To quantify the ability of ecosystems to simultaneously provide multiple functions, we employed two commonly used methods for calculating the EMF: the averaging multifunctionality index and the multithreshold multifunctionality index (11, 47, 66). For the averaging multifunctionality index, each functional attribute was first standardized to a scale of 0 to 1 using the min-max normalization. The average of these standardized values across all 20 functions was then calculated to derive the EMF. The multithreshold multifunctionality index was calculated by identifying the number of functions exceeding critical thresholds ranging from 1 to 99% (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). We set the breakpoints of the multithreshold multifunctionality index in relation to AI using the aridity threshold (0.48) derived from the relationship between the averaging multifunctionality index and AI (Fig. 2A), which tracks the shifts in the relationship under varying threshold conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). To clearly visualize changes in this index and analyze its relationship with other variables, including AI and microbial life history strategies, a subset of specific critical thresholds was selected. These thresholds were set at 10, 30, 50, and 80% of the maximum observed value for each respective function across all sites (Fig. 2B).

Statistical Analysis.

The principal component analysis was used to explore the trade-offs between individual functions using the R package of “vegan” (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). To quantify the drivers of EMF, we considered the effects of climate (mean annual temperature, annual maximum temperature, annual minimum temperature, temperature seasonality, aridity index, mean annual precipitation, and precipitation seasonality), soil properties (pH, texture, soil carbon to nitrogen ratio, soil nitrogen to phosphorus ratio), topography (elevation, latitude, longitude), and ecosystem types. The variance inflation factor was used to explore the collinearities of candidate predictors using the R package of “car.” Mean annual precipitation, annual maximum temperature, annual minimum temperature, and latitude with variance inflation factors greater than ten were removed from the model. After that, random forest analysis was performed to identify the relative importances of candidate predictors by permutation method in the R package of “ranger.”

To determine the most suitable model for representing the relationship between EMF or individual ecosystem functions and the aridity index, both linear and nonlinear models were fitted. The nonlinear models included quadratic models (to capture simple nonlinear trends) and generalized additive models (GAMs, which smooth complex trends using smoothing parameters). To ensure a robust selection, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation. First, we assessed goodness-of-fit using multiple metrics, including the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and R2 (SI Appendix, Table S2). Second, we assessed predictive performance using a 10-fold cross-validation procedure to explicitly address the risk of overfitting associated with flexible models. We calculated the cross-validated root mean squared error (RMSE) for each model as a measure of its generalizability to new data (SI Appendix, Table S3). Two nonlinear models consistently provided the best fit and, critically, yielded the lowest cross-validated RMSE, suggesting the potential presence of an aridity threshold in these correlations. The robust selection of this nonlinear model provided the statistical basis for investigating abrupt changes using segmented regression. After that, segmented regression was employed to fit the correlations between EMF or individual ecosystem functions and aridity index, using the R package of “segmented” (67).

In addition, to determine the optimal number of breakpoints (zero, one, two, or three), we performed a formal model selection procedure for each function. We used the BIC to select the most parsimonious model, as it robustly penalizes model complexity to prevent overfitting. For EMF and the majority of individual functions, the single-breakpoint model was unequivocally supported by the lowest BIC value (SI Appendix, Table S4). For the “soil fertility” function, although more complex models had slightly lower BIC values, the single-breakpoint model was retained for statistical robustness and consistency. This decision was based on two lines of evidence: The three-breakpoint model showed signs of overfitting with a higher 10-fold cross-validation error (CV = 0.070) compared to the one-breakpoint model (CV = 0.066), and the first breakpoint in the two-breakpoint model was located at an extreme end of the data gradient (AI ≈ 0.05), and the P-value for the change in slope at this breakpoint was marginally significant (P = 0.048). Therefore, a single-breakpoint analysis was applied consistently across all functions.

To investigate the soil microbial mechanisms regulating EMF across aridity thresholds, we categorized the study sites into two groups based on the aridity threshold of EMF: arid ecosystems (exceeding the threshold of 0.48) and humid ecosystems (below the threshold of 0.48). ANOVA was used to examine differences in EMF, individual ecosystem functions, and Y-A-S life history strategies between arid and humid ecosystems. Furthermore, to investigate whether Y-A-S life history strategies shift represented an abrupt transition or a gradual change, we performed a complementary analysis. We categorized all study sites into four distinct climate zones (arid: AI ≤ 0.2; semiarid: 0.2 < AI ≤ 0.5; dry sub-humid: 0.5 < AI ≤ 0.65; and humid: 0.65 < AI) based on the established global aridity index classification scheme (52). We then compared the Y-A-S strategies among these zones (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

The beta regression models were used to analyze the relationship between EMF (ranging from 0 to 1) and different microbial taxa and Y-A-S life history strategies (ranging from 0 to 1). Beta regression was chosen over linear regression due to its suitability for modeling proportional or bounded response variables, such as EMF and Y-A-S strategies. Linear regression analysis was used to explore the relationship between microbial taxa and the multithreshold multifunctionality index. All statistical analyses were performed in R 4.3.2 (68).
