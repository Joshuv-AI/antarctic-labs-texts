Intra-arrest therapeutic hypothermia with combined helium-mixed gas and cold fluid infusion significantly augments brain cooling efficiency: An experimental study in pigs
Authors: Atsushi Sakurai, Yoshihisa Kato, Haruka Uki, Kana Yagi, Atsushi Watanabe, Machi Atarashi, Kosaku Kinoshita
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to develop a more efficient method for intra-arrest therapeutic hypothermia (IATH) and demonstrate its usefulness in cardiopulmonary cerebral resuscitation.Methods and resultsThe following experiments were performed in pigs while measuring the brain temperature, introducing cardiac arrest, and performing cardiopulmonary resuscitation. In experiment 1, the use of helium gas mixtures in cardiac arrest intra-arrest lung cooling (IALC) increased brain cooling efficiency. When the IATH method was performed by adding cold fluid infusion to IALC, a decrease in brain temperature of approximately 0.8 °C within 10 min was observed. In experiment 2, cerebral microdialysis (CMD) during the IATH method of cardiopulmonary resuscitation showed a slight elevation of glutamate and significantly lower glutamate levels than those in the normally resuscitated group. Finally, the IATH method achieved return of spontaneous circulation equivalent to conventional resuscitation involving adrenaline administration and number of defibrillation attempts, without a statistically significant difference between the groups.ConclusionThe IATH method with IALC using a helium gas mixture and cold fluid infusion rapidly decreased brain temperature by approximately 0.8 °C within 10 min after the start of resuscitation. CMD with IATH showed no increase in glutamate levels, suggesting a possible cerebroprotective effect, and cardiac resuscitation could be performed without complications. These facts suggest that this IATH method of rapidly decreasing brain temperature may be more effective for mitigating post-CA brain injury.Keywords: Post-cardiac arrest brain injury, Cardiopulmonary resuscitation, Intra-arrest lung cooling, Helium, Intravenous cold fluid infusion during cardiopulmonary resuscitation

Introduction

The outcome of patients with post-cardiac arrest (CA) syndrome is still poor,1 with post-CA brain injury (PCABI) being the most common cause of death.2 Targeted temperature management following resuscitation from CA has been suggested to be effective but has yet to sufficiently improve outcomes.3 The development of new brain-protective therapies remains challenging. PCABI occurs during CA and worsens with each passing minute,4 meaning that brain protection therapy should be initiated during CA.

In an experimental cerebral microdialysis (CMD) study, Takata et al. reported that hypothermia initiation during CA led to a reduction in peak glutamate release, whereas the introduction of hypothermia after CA did not yield a significant difference in peak glutamate release compared with the normothermia control group.5 Using CMD in a rat model of CA, Schober et al. have shown that glutamate and lactate/pyruvate ratios increase, and glucose decreases during CA.6 Elevated glutamate levels during CA may indicate cerebral ischemia. Other cerebroprotective effects of hypothermia include decreased brain metabolism, decreased free radicals, and anti-inflammatory effects,7 and these are also effective in preventing cerebral ischemia during cardiac arrest. The use of hypothermia during CA reduces the risk of primary brain injury in patients with PCABI. However, although studies have been conducted, including one on prehospital cold saline infusions, effectiveness could not be proven8; it was suggested that cooling infusion alone was not sufficient to reduce brain temperature and may not have demonstrated cerebroprotective effects. To date, no clinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of intra-arrest therapeutic hypothermia (IATH).9 Since primary brain injury in patients with CA is caused by global cerebral ischemia during CA, it seemed key to reduce PCABI and improve its outcome to develop a method to quickly lower brain temperatures during CA to mitigate it. Clinically, Callaway et al. attempted brain cooling by cooling the head and neck during CA, however were unable to reduce intracranial temperature.10 The effectiveness of total liquid ventilation as a candidate for induction of rapid hypothermia is suggested11; however, development of other methods is warranted.

Cooling the lungs during CA is known as intra-arrest lung cooling (IALC), which may improve the rate of return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC).12 We developed an oxygen-cooling device and reported that lung cooling reduced brain temperature in an experimental study in pigs.13 Optimal cooling efficiency was observed when chest compressions and ventilation were synchronised at a ratio of 5:1, resulting in an approximate brain temperature reduction of 1.5 °C/hour. However, it is unclear whether the rate of temperature reduction provided sufficient cerebral protection against PCABI.

Helium is known to reduce the density of gases and improve ventilation efficiency when mixed with oxygen and has been used in the treatment of asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease as ‘heliox’.14 Considering that the cooling efficiency was increased in a previous study through increasing the ventilation ratio,13 the use of helium gas mixtures to increase the ventilation efficiency is predicted to increase cooling efficiency in IALC.

This study aimed to develop a method to increase the efficiency of brain temperature reduction through IATH using helium for ventilation during IALC and the addition of cold fluid infusion during CA. Experiments in cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) in pigs were used to examine the cerebroprotective effect of glutamate release by CMD using the new cooling method and synchronous and asynchronous methods of chest compressions and ventilation, and to examine whether the newly developed methods prevent ROSC during CPR.

Methods and results

This study was approved by the Nihon University Animal Care and Use Committee (AP22MED018-1).

Animal preparation

Details of the animal procedures were described in a previous paper.13 Briefly, castrated domestic male pigs, 2–5 months of age, weighing 43.6 ± 0.4 kg were fasted overnight before testing, with ad libitum access to water. Once the pig was sufficiently anaesthetized, an endotracheal tube with a cuff was inserted and mechanical ventilation was initiated. Ventilation was provided by a gas-cooling system developed in a previous study using the Jackson–Rees circuit manual ventilation. The inhaled gas was adjusted to 0 °C during lung cooling. Brain (frontal cortex) and tympanic, rectal, venous, and arterial blood temperatures were measured. Ventricular fibrillation was induced using a 9-V direct current, and CPR was initiated after 6 min. Chest compressions (100 per minute) were initiated using a cardiac massage machine (LUCAS 1; Scanfil Åtvidaberg AB, Åtvidaberg, Sweden) and CPR was performed. Two minutes after initiating chest compressions, epinephrine (20 μg/kg) was administered via a Swan–Ganz catheter into the right atrium, followed by additional doses of epinephrine at 3-min intervals. The cold infusion was administered with saline at a rate of 25 mL/min, usually at room temperature but at 0 °C when cooled.

Experiment 1. Brain cooling efficiency using helium gas and cooling infusion solutions

CPR was performed for 60 min, with epinephrine administration every 3 min without a countershock. During CPR with synchronised chest compressions and ventilation at a ratio of 5:1, one pig was cooled and ventilated with a mixture of 80 % helium gas and 20 % oxygen. Temperature changes in the brain, ear canal, rectum, blood, and arteries were measured. The brain temperature reduction in the previous oxygen-only study was about 1.5 °C/h,13 whereas it was approximately 2 °C/h in this study; the first temperature drop was also rapid (Fig. 1). This suggested that IALC with helium gas may increase the cooling efficiency. Therefore, the brain cooling efficiency was studied in the following four groups: group 1 (G1) used pulmonary cooling with helium but no cold fluid infusion; group 2 (G2) used pulmonary cooling with no helium and cold fluid infusion; group 3 (G3) used pulmonary cooling with helium and cold fluid infusion; and group 4 (G4) used no pulmonary cooling, helium, or cold fluid infusion. Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using Excel Microsoft 365 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) statistical software (Bell Curve for Excel ver. 4.07). Differences between groups were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test and the Steel test, with G4 used as the control group. Significance was set at p < 0.05.

At 1 h of CPR, G1 and G3 had significantly lower brain temperatures of 1–1.5 °C compared to G4 (Fig. 2). During the first 10 min, G3 had significantly lower brain temperatures than G4 (Fig. 3). During CA, a decrease in brain temperature of −0.85 (−0.95 – −0.77) (median (IQR)) in the first 10 min was achieved by using a mixture of ventilation gas mixed with helium gas and a cold fluid infusion.

Experiment 2. Brain protection by IATH evaluation using CMD

In the animal setting, before CA, the scalp of the pig's head was incised to expose the skull. A small hole approximately 10 mm in diameter was drilled about 1 cm lateral and 1 cm posterior to the bregma. A probe (FX-I-25-5 type probe, membrane length 5 mm, EICOM, Kyoto, Japan) was inserted into the brain parenchyma. The probe and surrounding skin were securely fixed using a soft tissue adhesive (Aron Alpha A “Sankyo”) and adhesive tape. After probe insertion, sampling started with a perfusion rate of 1–2 µL/min following a 1-h equilibration period. The time constant (Kt) was set at 5 min, based on published reports that microdialysis probes typically reach 90 % response within several minutes.5, 15 Glutamate was quantified via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC: EICOM, Kyoto, Japan). The values of glutamate are relative to the value 10 min before the start of the experiment (as 1), and the rise is a multiple of that value. CPR was performed with the same method as in Experiment 1.

Pigs were divided into three groups: group A (GA) received resuscitation without cooling with an asynchronous method; group B (GB), without cooling with a synchronised method of chest compressions and ventilation at a ratio of 5:1; and group C (GC), IALC with helium-mixed cooling gas and cold fluid infusion. (See Supplementary File 2).

Experiment 3. Resuscitation responsiveness in IALC using cold fluid infusion and helium

We should investigate the potential of CPR using a synchronized method, cold fluid infusion, and helium-mixed IALC to achieve ROSC. In Experiment 3, CPR was performed with epinephrine administration every three minutes and a countershock until ROSC. In the control group (n=6), resuscitation was performed using an asynchronous method with indoor air ventilation. The IATH group (n = 6) was ventilated using a synchronised method of chest compressions and ventilation at a ratio of 5:1 with helium-mixed cooling gas and 500 mL (25 mL/min) of cold saline infusion. One subject in the control group was not resuscitated, while all subjects in the IATH group were resuscitated.

Discussion

During CPR of swine CA, a decrease in brain temperature of approximately 2 °C/hour was observed when helium gas was mixed with lung cooling and cold fluid infusion was used; a further decrease in brain temperature of approximately 0.8 °C was observed in the first 10 min. Using CMD, glutamate was found to be elevated in the absence of IATH, but not in the resuscitation method with IATH, suggesting that IATH may have a brain-protective effect. When resuscitation was performed using this method of IATH, all pigs had ROSC and were resuscitated in a shorter time than with normal resuscitation.

Helium is a noble gas and is pharmacologically inactive, with a density one-eighth that of nitrogen. Helium blended with oxygen (70–80 %; heliox) has a density that is one-third that of room air. Resistance to gas flow is density-dependent, and those with a lower density than air should have easier passage in and out of the lungs.14 This suggests that cooling with helium gas mixtures may be faster owing to their lower specific gravity. In one study of experimental pigs not in CA, cooling efficiency increased when helium mixture was cooled, mixed with perfluorocarbon, and administered during hypothermic induction.16 The increased efficiency of brain temperature cooling when using helium gas during CA in the present study indicates that similar phenomena were observed. The thermal conductivity of helium at 300 K is about six times higher than that of air.17 Because helium has higher thermal conductivity than air, its use in the lungs may contribute to more efficient cooling. In IALC during CA, a helium gas mixture is more suitable for reducing the brain temperature.

PCABI for global cerebral ischaemia due to CA is known to worsen by the minute. In Radovsky et al.'s study on dogs, the area of impairment appeared at 5 min, and had spread to the entire brain at 10 minutes.4 Cerebral protection against PCABI requires rapid induction after CA. The failure of cooling infusions8 and transnasal evaporative cooling18 to show efficacy in clinical studies on IATH was presumed to be partly due to the inability to reduce brain temperature quickly enough. In the present study, a reduction in brain temperature of 0.8 °C could be achieved in 10 min from the start of IATH by combining pulmonary cooling and cold fluid infusion. This rapid cooling suggests that this method may have a cerebroprotective effect against PCABI.

Glutamate is an excitatory amino acid released during cerebral ischaemia, which exhibits excitotoxicity and leads to neuronal death.19 The extent of brain damage can be determined by assessing the extraneuronal release of glutamate using CMD. Clinically, elevated glutamate levels measured by CMD in cases of cerebral infarction of the middle cerebral artery reportedly predict malignant stroke, including increased intracranial pressure.20 Takata et al. performed CMD in rats experiencing CA to measure glutamate levels and reported that the induction of hypothermia after resuscitation was ineffective in protecting the brain from glutamate impairment; only IATH reduced the release of glutamate due to cerebral ischaemia.5 The presence or absence of elevated glutamate levels in CMD is useful in determining the response to PCABI treatment. In the present study, IALC with helium and cold fluid infusion significantly reduced the release of glutamate during CA compared with normal resuscitation, suggesting that this method is effective in protecting the brain from PCABI. Helium is also known to reduce stroke size,21 indicating that future studies on its cerebroprotective effect against PCABI are necessary.

There is a concern that IALC with cooled oxygen-helium gas mixtures may have adverse effects on CPR because of the lower oxygen concentration and temperature compared to normal CPR. Additionally, the synchronised method of chest compressions and ventilation at a ratio of 5:1 used in this study was different from that in general resuscitation guidelines,22 and its effectiveness is disputed. In the present study, CPR performed using this synchronized method and IATH resulted in no significant differences in resuscitation rate, time to resuscitation, number of shocks administered, or adrenaline dosage compared to the usual method, suggesting that this method may be safe and could be used for CPR.

Our study had some limitations. First, the number of experimental animals used was relatively small. In addition, the number of animals was uneven due to severe restrictions on the number of animals, which prevented additional experiments when data could not be obtained due to surgical or other failures. However, statistically significant differences could still be identified for some experiments. Additionally, whether this method of IATH truly reduces the risk of PCABI remains uncertain. Actually, in the present study, we were able to demonstrate that this IATH suppressed the greater rise in glutamate in the brain during cardiac arrest, which suggests that it may have reduced the progression of brain damage in response to cerebral ischemia. However, the lactate/pyruvate (L/P) ratio, glucose, and glycerol could not be measured. It is uncertain whether this effect is truly cerebroprotective. To determine the extent of the actual cerebroprotective effect, further studies are needed, such as examination of resuscitation with normal and IATH at various CA durations and observation of neurological findings after resuscitation to demonstrate that the IATH results in neurologically functional outcomes even at longer CA durations, and examination to reliably confirm cerebral ischemia by measuring the L/P ratio, glucose, and glycerol, in addition to glutamate by CMD during CA.

Conclusion

IATH with helium-mixed cooling gas and cold fluid infusion during CA was able to reduce brain temperature by 0.8 °C within 10 min of resuscitation. Compared to normal resuscitation, this method reduced the increase in glutamate levels as measured by CMD, suggesting that it may have brain-protective effects. In addition, compared with normal resuscitation, this method did not prevent ROSC. This experimental study showed that this method of IATH suggests the possibility of reducing PCABI because of its ability to rapidly cool the brain. Further animal studies may demonstrate that this method can actually extend the time limit of cardiac arrest without brain injury, and thus may be effective in clinical practice.
