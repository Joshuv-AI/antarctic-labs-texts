Heterovalent, Homometallic Rings: Synthesis, Optical, and EPR Studies of {TiIV 7TiIII} Complexes
Authors: Selena J Lockyer, Lubomir Loci, Yingzhao Ma, Matthew P Stevens, Grigore A Timco, George F S Whitehead, Richard E P Winpenny, Alice M Bowen, Eric J L McInnes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report a new class of heterovalent, homometallic ring complexes, with {TiIV 7TiIII} cores: [py‐CH2NH2Et][Ti7TiO8(O2CR)16] (R = tBu and cyclopropyl). The compounds are made from [Ti(OiPr)4] reacted with carboxylic acid and py‐CH2NHEt. We also show that {TiIV 7TiIII} rings can be generated by photoreduction of {TiIV 8} precursors in the presence of a suitable sacrificial electron donor, of relevance to photocatalysts. UV–vis/NIR spectroscopy of the {TiIV 7TiIII} rings reveal Robin and Day Class II mixed‐valent behavior at room temperature, while EPR spectroscopy is consistent with a localized 3d1 (s = 1/2) configuration, with C2 local symmetry, at low temperatures. We compare the EPR behavior of these new rings with the isostructural heterometallic {CrIII 7NiII} rings [py‐CH2NH2Et][Cr7NiF8(O2CR)16], which also have s = ½ ground states, due to strong intramolecular antiferromagnetic exchange interactions. We demonstrate that the electron spin lattice relaxation time constants (T 1) for {TiIV 7TiIII} are over two orders of magnitude longer at low temperatures than for the {Cr7Ni} equivalents, and that phase memory time constants (T m) are maintained to much higher temperatures.Keywords: electron spin relaxation, EPR, metallacycles, mixed valent, titanium oxo clusters

Introduction

Metallacycles are of interest in areas including supramolecular chemistry and molecular magnetism. In the former they have formed structural components of for example complex knots, catenanes, and rotaxanes [1, 2, 3, 4]. In the latter, they were initially studied as finite models of low‐dimensional magnets, but it rapidly became clear that their magnetic properties were important in their own right. Much of the original work was on homometallic and homovalent complexes, for example Lippard's [FeIII 10(OMe)20(O2CCH2Cl)10] [5] and Gerbeleu's [CrIII 8F8(O2CtBu)16] [6, 7]. Further interest came with predictions of interesting behavior that could result from symmetry breaking [8], through odd numbers of metal ions or through a dopant site in the ring. We have studied such systems of general formula [cation][CrIII 7MIIF8(O2CR)16] [9, 10]. The {CrIII 7NiII} family has attracted particular interest because strong antiferromagnetic coupling between the CrIII (s = 3/2) and NiII (s = 1) ions leads to a well‐isolated s = ½ ground state, and have been proposed as potential qubits in quantum information processing [11, 12]. These do not have the coherence times achievable with simpler metal–organic [13, 14, 15, 16] or organometallic compounds [17, 18], but can be linked into supramolecular arrays in multiple ways and with great synthetic control [1, 2, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. As it is straightforward to link qubits based on these polymetallic complexes it would be valuable to improve the coherence times. Other groups are making multi‐qubit molecules, for example based on linked radicals [24], heterometallic lanthanide complexes [25, 26], and on linked porphyrins [27, 28].

We reasoned that reducing the number of unpaired electrons in the ring should influence relaxation, and therefore we targeted {TiIV 7TiIII} rings which would have a single unpaired electron (TiIV, 3d0; TiIII, 3d1) hence s = ½, to contrast with {CrIII 7NiII} rings (CrIII, 3d3; NiII, 3d8) which contain twenty‐three unpaired electrons. A further impetus for such compounds is that {TiIV 8O8} rings are the inorganic nodes in an important class of metal–organic framework (MOF) materials, the parent MOF being MIL‐125 which has the formula Ti8O8(OH)4(O2C‐C6H4‐CO2)12 [29]. MIL‐125 is an efficient photocatalyst which involves the generation of Ti(III) sites on UV–vis excitation; such materials give an avenue to combine the porosity of MOFs with the heterogeneous photocatalytic properties of TiO2. Molecular analogues would have potential application in homogeneous catalysis.

Results and Discussion

Here we compare four single ring compounds: [Cation][CrIII 7NiIIF8(O2CtBu)16] 1, [Cation][CrIII 7NiIIF8(CPC)16] 2, [Cation][TiIV 7TiIIIO8(O2CtBu)16] 3, and [Cation][TiIV 7TiIIIO8(CPC)16] 4, where Cation = (py‐CH2NH2Et)+ (py = pyridyl) and CPC = cyclopropyl‐carboxylate (Figure 1). Complexes 1 and 2 have been reported [21, 23]. The two different carboxylates are chosen to compare the effects of the ligands and of the metal core on the electron spin relaxation properties. We have previously reported heterometallic rings (R2NH2)[TiIV 7MIIIO8(O2CtBu)16] (M = Cr, Fe, Al, Ga, and In) [30] based on the observation that the {TiIVO(O2CR)2} unit is isostructural with, and has the same charge as, {CrIIIF(O2CR)2}. A parent homovalent {TiIV 8} ring is known, namely [Ti8O8(O2CC6F5)16] [31]. Complexes 3 and 4 were made by related reactions as {TiIV 7MIII}, but without the heterometal source: [Ti(iOPr)4] is added to a mixture of carboxylic acid and py‐CH2NHEt under heat, in an inert atmosphere to give blue products in 30%–50% yield (see Supporting Information for details). The blue products are only seen with this specific amine, which must also be the reducing agent (see below). Related alkylaminopyridines have been studied in the context of dye‐sensitized solar cells based on TiO2 [32].

In each of 1‐4 the structures [33] (see Table S1 for crystallography details) contain eight metal sites forming an octagon, with each edge of the octagon bridged by two carboxylates and a monodentate ligand, which is fluoride in 1 and 2 and oxide in 3 and 4 (Figures 1 and S1). In 1 and 2 the divalent metal, and in 3 and 4 the trivalent metal ion, is disordered about the octagon of metals. The Ti‐oxide distances in 3 and 4 are 0.1 Å shorter than the M‐F distances in 1 and 2 (Table S2), consistent with the higher charges. A charge balancing cation is found at the centre of the ring; here it is a protonated secondary ammonium ion with a pyridyl group on one of the side‐chains. We have previously used this thread to bond to other metal complexes via the terminal pyridyl [34]. The bond lengths and angles at the metal sites are given in Table S2 and are unremarkable.

Complexes 1 and 2 are green in colour due to the Cr(III) dd transitions [35]. Complexes 3 and 4 are blue. Solution state UV–vis/NIR spectra show this is due to dominant, very broad transitions at 𝜐max = 13.5 and 13.9 x 103 cm−1 in 3 and 4, respectively, (Figure 2). These bands have extinction coefficients (εmax) of 230 and 680 M−1 cm−1, respectively, with FWHMs of ∆𝜐1/2 ≈ 10 x 103 cm−1 (this is an overestimate if more than one transition is present). Two much weaker and narrower features are observed at ca. 19.2 and 23.8 x 103 cm−1 in both complexes (εmax ca. 100 ‐ 101 M−1 cm−1). The latter are consistent with dd transitions associated with the 3d1 Ti(III) ion. The former are not present in parent all‐Ti(IV) compounds, and the FWHM, extinction coefficients and absorption maxima are consistent with inter‐valence charge transfer (IVCT) transitions: these parameters combine to give a weak electronic coupling parameter Hab=(4.2×10−4εmaxυmaxΔυ1/2)1/2/r where r is the Ti(IV)…Ti(III) separation in Å: here H ab is ca. 10% of 𝜐max [36, 37]. The presence of both dd and IVCT confirms these as Class II mixed‐valence compounds in the Robin and Day classification [38]. Similar IVCT transitions have been observed in MIL‐125 derivatives [29, 39] and Ti alkoxide clusters [40] after UV–vis irradiation in the presence of a sacrificial electron donor (e.g., during substrate photooxidation reactions). It is not clear how many sites in the octametallic ring the unpaired electron is delocalized over, nor how this varies with temperature. It may be of interest to study this in a finite, molecular system with relevance to polaron hopping in photoexcitation of extended lattices.

As seen for other {CrIII 7NiII} rings, the EPR spectra of 1 and 2 at low temperature are dominated by the s = ½ ground state resonance at ca. g = 1.8 that arises from strong intramolecular antiferromagnetic coupling between nearest neighbours [9, 10, 41]. Above ca. 20 K, a broad resonance grows at around g = 1.98 due to population of further and numerous excited states [23].

For 3 and 4, well‐resolved rhombic s = ½ continuous wave (cw) EPR spectra are observed at X‐ and Q‐band in the solution and powder states (Figures 3, S3 and S4); simulation of the solid‐state spectra (Figures S5, S6) gives g = 1.955, 1.880, and 1.770 for 3 and g = 1.963, 1.888, and 1.793 for 4. The observed linewidths are around half those for 1 (Table S3). The g‐values are all below the free electron value (g e = 2.0023) hence consistent with isolated 3d1 Ti(III) sites, and the narrowness of the spectra is consistent with valence‐localized centres. At X‐band, and in solution and powder, there is a noticeable broadening introduced above ca. 100 K (Figures S7–S11), becoming pseudo‐isotropic (and very weak) above ca. 200 K. Little signal is observed at higher temperatures. The high‐temperature behavior is consistent with the onset of rapid relaxation via electron hopping between the Ti sites in the ring, as evidenced by the IVCT band in the room temperature UV–vis/NIR spectra. Similar variable temperature behavior is observed for Ti(III) sites in reduced TiO2 [42].

The cw EPR spectra of 1 ‐ 4 (black to purple lines, respectively) at X‐band and 5 K measured as a 2 mM solution in toluene for 1 and 3, and in CH2Cl2 for 2 and 4.

The low temperature g‐values are much more anisotropic than observed for Ti(III) in regular lattice sites of reduced TiO2 (g⊥,∥= 1.99, 1.96 in anatase; g = 1.97, 1.96, and 1.95 in rutile) [42]. However, they are rather close to those observed for Ti(III) in MgO (g = 1.95, 1.85, and 1.77) where the significant rhombic distortion was assigned to a single cation vacancy neighbouring the coordinated oxides in the local equatorial plane giving local C2v symmetry (where the smallest g‐value defines the local z‐axis) [43]. In 3 and 4, the [TiIV 7TiIIIO8(O2CR)16]− rings (i.e. neglecting the thread) have C2 symmetry where the C2 axis passes through the Ti(III) and the antipodal Ti(IV) ion. Hence, the local symmetry at Ti(III) is C2, but if we just consider the first coordination sphere of cis‐{TiOoxide 2Ocarboxylate 4} then we can approximate to C2v (Figure 4). For convenience, we define the C2 axis as x, with z defining the Ocarboxylate‐Ti‐Ocarboxylate direction that is near normal to the ring (in fact this vector is rotated ca. 15° from the normal to the best {Ti7Ti} plane), leaving y bisecting the equatorial Ocarboxylate‐Ti‐Ooxide angles hence near tangential to the rings (Figure 4). Echo‐detected field‐swept (EDFS) pulsed EPR spectra from dilute (0.2 mM) solutions of 1 ‐ 4 at 5 K are consistent with cw EPR spectra (Figure S12). The spin‐lattice relaxation (T 1) and phase memory (T m) time constants were measured using established pulse sequences (see Figures S12–S26; Tables S4–S12). The two {TiIV 7TiIII} rings (3 and 4) have similar T 1 behavior, with maximum values of 3.0 ± 0.2 ms at 4 K, decreasing to ca. 10 µs at 20 K (Figure 5b). At 4 K, these T 1 values are over two orders of magnitude greater than those of the equivalent {CrIII 7NiII} rings 1 and 2 (for which echoes are only detectable below ca. 5 K; Table S10) [12, 23]. The much longer T 1 for 3 and 4 is due to the localized d1 configuration of the Ti(III) ion at these temperatures. In contrast, although 1 and 2 have well‐isolated s = ½ ground states, they have a high density of magnetic states due to the exchange coupling between the seven Cr(III) and Ni(II) ions [first excited states (s = 3/2) at 10 and 8 cm−1, respectively] [23]. This provides an efficient mechanism for T 1 relaxation [44]. As observed for 1 and 2, there is relatively little difference in T 1 between the CPC and pivalate complexes 3 and 4.

Temperature dependence of (a) Tm and (b) T1 for 1 (black diamonds), 2 (green triangles), 3 (blue diamonds), and 4 (red triangles). T m data for perdeuterated [Et2NH2][CrIII 7NiIIF8(O2CtBu)16] shown for reference (pink diamonds) [46].

For a common carboxylate ligand, the {TiIV 7TiIII} rings have a longer T m than {CrIII 7NiII} by a factor of ca. 2 at a temperature of 3 K, with 3 and 4 having T m of 1.1 ± 0.05 and 5.2 ± 0.2 µs, respectively, (Figure 3a). (0.4 and 0.2 mM solutions give the same T m, hence intermolecular effects are not dominating.) In contrast to the T 1 behavior, the use of CPC instead of pivalate has a large effect on T m, increasing T m in 4 compared to 3 by a factor of ca. 5 at 3 K. The weak temperature dependence of T m in 3 is indicative of dephasing dominated by nuclear spin diffusion [44], and this is partially quenched in 4 by having fewer 1H nuclei per carboxylate ligand (5 vs. 9 in CPC and pivalate, respectively,) [23]. In addition, use of CPC, which has no methyl groups, quenches dephasing induced by Me tunnelling rotation [45]. Measurements using the CPMG sequence give longer time constants for both 3 and 4 (T CMPG/T m = 5.2 for 3, 2.8 for 4, at 3 K; Table S8), indicating that spectral diffusion also contributes to dephasing [44].

We have reported similar trends in T m behavior for the {CrIII 7NiII} rings 1 and 2 [23]. However, for 2 the gain in T m (cf. 1) is only at the lowest temperatures with similar values observed at 5 K because T m becomes limited by T 1 induced spectral diffusion. In contrast, for 3 T m only decreases from 1.1 to 0.7 µs between 3 and 20 K; that for 4 decreases by more but at 20 K is still larger than for 3 by a factor of 2.5. The persistence of T m with increasing temperature is because of the much longer T 1 over the temperature range. The effect of the metal core is illustrated by the fact that, even though relaxation at low temperature is dominated by nuclear spins, 3 has significantly longer T m than a perdeuterated {CrIII 7NiII} complex (Figure 5) [46]. The longer T m times of 3 and 4 can be exploited to perform more complex pulsed EPR experiments and to higher temperature. For example, in transient nutation experiments Rabi oscillations can be observed to much longer nutation times, and to much higher temperatures (we have measured up to 20 K) for compounds 3 and 4 than for 1 and 2 (Figures S31–S38). This study demonstrates that significant differences in relaxation behavior can be observed for polymetallic qubit candidates, achieved both by changing the ligand type and also by changing the electronic structure of the core.

Finally, given the importance of TiO2 and the {Ti8} based MOFs in photocatalysis, we examined whether {TiIV 7TiIII} rings could be formed by irradiation of {TiIV 8} rings. Irradiating a solution of [TiIV 8O8(O2CtBu)16] 5, in the presence of Et2NH and excess pivalic acid (providing a proton source to form Et2NH2 +), with a Xe arc lamp under anaerobic conditions gives blue solutions which have similar EPR signals to 3 and 4 (Figure 6). The EPR signal intensity varies with both the 5:amine ratio and the irradiation power, which confirms that a photochemical reduction takes place although the precise mechanism is not clear. Future work will examine this photochemical process including in the presence of substrates that can be reduced, as in the MOFs.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a new class of homometallic, heterovalent rings based on the {TiIV 7TiIII} core can be prepared directly from monometallic TiIV precursors or photochemically from {TiIV 8} analogues. They have electron spin s = ½ ground states that are delocalized (Robin and Day class II) at room temperature but localized TiIII at low temperatures, with much slower electron spin relaxation properties at low temperatures than their isostructural {Cr7Ni} analogues. Future work will seek to exploit these properties in molecular magnetism and photochemical applications.
