Volume ratio factor: a novel CT-derived biomarker for pre-planning ipsilateral lung dose risk stratification in breast cancer radiotherapy
Authors: Qianqian Meng, Zhongzhi Qi, Zheng Li, Birong Hu, Guijun Xue, Renming Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIpsilateral lung dose in breast cancer radiotherapy is typically evaluated only after treatment planning, limiting early risk stratification and planning optimization. This study aimed to develop and validate a novel computed tomography (CT)-derived volumetric biomarker, the volume ratio factor (VRF), for pre-planning assessment of ipsilateral lung dose risk in breast cancer radiotherapy.MethodsIn this retrospective single-center study, 58 breast cancer patients receiving volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) with regional nodal irradiation (RNI) were analyzed. VRF was defined as the ratio of lung internal volume (LIV) to ipsilateral lung volume (ILV) using simulation CT. Correlation analyses were performed between VRF and lung dose metrics, and predictive performance was compared with conventional anatomical parameters using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and machine learning regression models.ResultsVRF demonstrated the strongest correlation with mean lung dose (MLD) among all evaluated anatomical parameters (r=0.582, P<0.01), with significant correlation also observed for V20 (r=0.485, P<0.01) and V10 (r=0.438, P<0.01). ROC analysis showed that VRF achieved the highest discriminative performance for identifying patients with MLD >13 Gy [area under the curve (AUC) =0.71]. Regression analyses further demonstrated stable predictive performance with low estimation error using repeated cross-validation [mean absolute error (MAE): 0.51±0.10 Gy; mean absolute percentage error (MAPE): 4.22%±0.90%]. Compared with seven conventional anatomical parameters, VRF reduced MAE and MAPE by 19.5% and 20.9%, respectively, across all regression frameworks and lung dose-volume endpoints. In the subgroup without internal mammary node (IMN) irradiation, the correlation between VRF and MLD further increased to r=0.637.ConclusionsVRF is a simple and interpretable CT-derived biomarker that captures three‑dimensional target‑lung spatial interaction. It enables pre‑planning risk stratification of ipsilateral lung dose and may support geometry‑informed, individualized radiotherapy planning.Keywords: Quantitative imaging, CT-derived biomarker, breast cancer radiotherapy, lung dose risk stratification, volumetric anatomical parameters

Introduction

Postoperative radiotherapy (RT) is a fundamental component of breast cancer management and has been shown to significantly improve local control and overall survival (1,2). For patients requiring comprehensive regional nodal irradiation (RNI), including irradiation of the breast or chest wall and regional lymphatic regions, treatment planning is particularly challenging due to the need to balance adequate target coverage against radiation exposure to adjacent organs at risk (OARs), most notably the heart and ipsilateral lung (3-5). These challenges are further amplified by substantial inter-patient anatomical variability, underscoring the importance of patient-specific assessment of normal tissue dose burden.

Radiation-induced lung toxicity, including pneumonitis and pulmonary fibrosis, remains a clinically relevant dose-limiting complication in breast cancer RT (6). Numerous studies have demonstrated strong associations between pulmonary toxicity risk and ipsilateral lung dose-volume metrics, particularly mean lung dose (MLD) and the volume of lung receiving 20 Gy or more (7,8). Consequently, accurate characterization of lung dose distribution is a central objective in contemporary breast RT planning and optimization.

Volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) has been increasingly adopted for breast cancer patients requiring RNI owing to its improved dose conformity and target coverage compared with conventional three-dimensional conformal RT (3D-CRT) (9,10). However, the highly modulated nature of VMAT delivery may lead to increased low-dose lung exposure, such as elevated V5, thereby complicating lung dose management (11,12). This inherent trade-off highlights the need for anatomically meaningful parameters that can inform lung dose expectations across different planning techniques and treatment scenarios.

A major source of uncertainty in lung dose estimation arises from pronounced inter-patient anatomical heterogeneity, including variations in lung volume, target extent, and their three-dimensional spatial relationship (13). Conventional geometric surrogates derived from simulation imaging, such as central lung distance (CLD) or planar lung width, provide only limited representation of this complex target-lung interaction and are therefore insufficient to fully characterize patient-specific lung dose risk (14,15). From a quantitative imaging perspective, these limitations point to the need for volumetric descriptors that directly encode three-dimensional anatomical relationships in a reproducible and interpretable manner.

Accurate estimation of ipsilateral lung dose risk at the imaging stage, prior to treatment planning, would offer substantial clinical value by enabling early identification of high-risk patients and supporting informed planning decisions (16,17). In recent years, knowledge-based planning and deep learning approaches have shown promise for patient-specific dose prediction in breast RT (18,19). However, such methods often rely on large training datasets, complex model development, and substantial computational resources, and many lack transparent anatomical features that reflect fundamental target-lung geometry (20). These factors limit their routine applicability for early, pre-planning risk stratification in clinical workflows. In contrast, existing well-validated dosimetric parameters (e.g., MLD, V20) are inherently plan-dependent outputs that can only be obtained after treatment planning completion, which restricts their use at the simulation stage.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to systematically evaluate a comprehensive set of anatomical and geometric parameters derived from simulation computed tomography (CT) and to introduce a novel dimensionless volumetric descriptor, termed the volume ratio factor (VRF). VRF is designed as a plan‑independent, geometry-driven, pre-planning anatomical biomarker that quantifies the intrinsic spatial relationship between the target and lung based solely on simulation CT, distinct from traditional dosimetric predictors. This work emphasizes feature-level anatomical interpretability and robustness rather than predictive model optimization, with the aim of establishing an imaging-derived quantitative biomarker that supports transparent and clinically meaningful pre-planning assessment of ipsilateral lung dose risk in breast cancer RT. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0068/rc).

Methods

Study design and patient cohort

This single-institution retrospective study included 58 consecutive female patients with histologically confirmed unilateral breast cancer who underwent postoperative radiotherapy using VMAT between January 2025 and December 2025 at West China Hospital, Sichuan University. All patients were treated in the supine position and immobilized using a customized breast vacuum bag on a 15° wedge plate with both arms raised. Skin markers were placed on stable chest wall landmarks to ensure reproducibility during CT simulation.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (No. 2025227), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. To minimize confounding technical variability, all patients were planned using an identical prescription scheme, planning protocol, and optimization strategy throughout the study period. Inclusion criteria comprised unilateral breast cancer treated with VMAT and availability of complete simulation CT images and dosimetric data. Exclusion criteria included prior thoracic RT, bilateral breast cancer, inflammatory breast cancer, and metastatic disease at diagnosis.

CT simulation and treatment planning

Simulation CT imaging was performed under free-breathing conditions using a GE Revolution CT scanner (120 kV, 240 mAs, 3.0-mm slice thickness), with scan coverage extending from the chin to the inferior margin of the liver. All images were imported into the RayStation treatment planning system (version 10B, RaySearch Laboratories AB, Stockholm, Sweden) for contouring and dosimetric analysis.

Clinical target volumes were delineated in accordance with European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology (ESTRO) consensus guidelines (21). Planning clinical target volumes were generated using a uniform 5-mm isotropic expansion and subsequently edited slice by slice to respect anatomical boundaries (e.g., trimming expansions that invaded the lung or heart, while preserving coverage of the breast/chest wall tissue), following Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) 1005 recommendations (22) and institutional practice. The supraclavicular nodal region was defined as PCTV1 (planning clinical target volume for the supraclavicular nodes), and the breast or chest wall target was defined as PCTV2 (planning clinical target volume for the breast or chest wall).

All VMAT plans were prescribed a total dose of 50 Gy delivered in 25 fractions using a standardized dual partial-arc technique. During inverse planning optimization, target coverage was prioritized, with the primary planning objective defined as achieving at least 95% of the prescribed dose covering the planning target volume (PCTV V50 ≥95%). Doses to OARs, particularly the ipsilateral lung and heart, were minimized as much as reasonably achievable while maintaining target coverage, in accordance with the as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA) principle (23). OARs doses were not constrained by strict predefined dose-volume limits; instead, they were minimized as low as reasonably achievable while maintaining adequate target coverage. All plans were clinically acceptable and included in the analysis regardless of the absolute lung dose. This approach allowed us to study the relationship between patient anatomy and lung dose without introducing planning‑induced bias. Two 20-mm virtual boluses (medial/lateral) with water-equivalent density were used during optimization to create skin flash and were removed before final dose calculation, applied identically to all patients.

Anatomical parameter definition

From the simulation CT images, seven conventional anatomical and geometric parameters were extracted as imaging-derived descriptors of target-lung geometry (Figure 1): CLD, maximum lung width (MLW), craniocaudal lung length (CLL), ipsilateral lung volume (ILV), chest wall target volume (CWTV), lung internal volume (LIV), and the ILV/CWTV ratio.

The definitions of CLD, MLW, and CLL were adapted from previously reported geometric descriptors (24) to ensure consistency with established methodologies. Specifically, CLD was defined as the maximum extent of ipsilateral lung beyond the tangential boundary of the breast target, evaluated across all CT slices, and is conceptually consistent with the skin maximum lung distance (sMLD) described in prior studies (14). In contrast, MLW was defined as the chord length of lung tissue intersecting the tangential projection of the breast target in the midplane CT slice, representing the lateral extent of lung involvement rather than a perpendicular distance from the chest wall. CLL was defined as the craniocaudal extent of lung involvement along the superior-inferior axis.

CWTV was directly derived from the clinically defined planning target volume PCTV2. While both represent the same underlying structure, PCTV2 is used in the context of treatment planning, whereas CWTV is used here as an anatomical construct for geometric analysis. LIV was defined as the subvolume of the ipsilateral lung geometrically overlapping with virtual tangential planes derived from the medial and lateral boundaries of both PCTV1 and PCTV2. The superior limit was defined by lung–target tangency at the PCTV1 apex, and the inferior limit by the inferior border of PCTV2. This approach provides a geometry-based approximation of the potential lung involvement region (predominantly from the breast/chest wall target, with a smaller contribution from the lung apex near the supraclavicular target) without reliance on treatment planning parameters. All parameters were extracted based on simulation CT and clinical contours using consistent rules across all cases, without additional manual adjustment.

Dosimetric endpoints and statistical analysis

Ipsilateral lung dose-volume histogram (DVH) parameters were selected as reference dosimetric endpoints, including MLD and lung volumes receiving at least 5 Gy (V5), 10 Gy (V10), and 20 Gy (V20). These endpoints are widely used as clinically relevant surrogates of radiation-induced lung toxicity and were used in this study to quantify the dosimetric impact of target-lung geometry.

Univariate associations between CT-derived anatomical parameters and lung dosimetric endpoints were assessed using Pearson correlation analysis. Correlation coefficients (r) and two-sided P values were calculated, with statistical significance defined as P<0.05.

To evaluate feature robustness and model-independence rather than to develop a clinically deployable dose prediction model, regression analyses were performed using repeated 5-fold cross-validation with 10 repetitions (50 total iterations). Three supervised regression algorithms were evaluated using scikit-learn (version 1.2.0, Python 3.9) (25): support vector regression (SVR), random forest (RF), and gradient boosting (GB). Model hyperparameters were fixed a priori and held constant across all iterations to ensure fair comparison across anatomical parameters and to limit optimistic bias (26).

SVR employed a radial basis function kernel (C=10, γ=0.1, ε=0.1). RF consisted of 100 trees with a maximum depth of 5, and GB used 100 boosting stages with a learning rate of 0.05 and a maximum tree depth of 5. For SVR, all features were standardized using z-score normalization based exclusively on training data within each cross-validation fold.

Results

Patient characteristics and lung dosimetry

All 58 patients achieved protocol-compliant target coverage and organ-at-risk constraints. Substantial inter-patient anatomical variability was observed across the cohort. The CWTV ranged from 260.75 to 1,643.34 cm3, while ILV ranged from 672.01 to 1,849.33 cm3. Correspondingly, the proposed VRF spanned a wide range from 0.04 to 0.25, reflecting marked heterogeneity in three-dimensional target-lung spatial relationships.

The MLD was 12.54 Gy (range, 9.31–14.08 Gy). The V5, V10, and V20 were 48.28%, 33.34%, and 22.91%, respectively (Table 2). A representative dose distribution and corresponding DVH are shown in Figure 2, illustrating typical lung dose characteristics in VMAT-based RNI.

Regression-based robustness analysis of anatomical parameters

Overall estimation stability across RF

All dose-volume metrics were taken from the clinically delivered treatment plans. The regression models were used to estimate these actual values from the anatomical parameters. Across all regression algorithms (SVR, RF, and GB) and all evaluated lung dose metrics (MLD, V5, V10, and V20), VRF consistently demonstrated lower estimation errors than conventional anatomical parameters. When evaluated using MAE, RMSE, and MAPE, VRF-based analyses showed reduced error magnitude and greater stability across repeated cross-validation iterations (Table 4). Estimation of MLD from anatomical parameters

For MLD estimation, regression analyses incorporating VRF yielded the lowest absolute and relative errors among all evaluated anatomical parameters. Using SVR as a representative framework, VRF achieved an MAE of 0.51±0.10 Gy and an RMSE of 0.66±0.13 Gy, corresponding to a MAPE of 4.22%±0.90%. Comparable relative performance was observed using RF and GB, with VRF consistently ranking as the most stable anatomical descriptor for MLD estimation.

Estimation of V5, V10, and V20 from anatomical parameters

VRF-based analyses maintained favorable and stable estimation performance across lung dose-volume metrics. For V5, VRF yielded the lowest relative error, with a MAPE of 3.00%±0.73%. For V10, all three regression frameworks produced MAPE values below 5% when VRF was included, with minimal inter-fold variability.

For V20, VRF-based analyses demonstrated stable estimation performance with consistently positive coefficients of determination across validation iterations. Among the evaluated frameworks, RF combined with VRF achieved an MAE of 1.55%±0.32%, an RMSE of 1.94%±0.42%, and a MAPE of 7.04%±1.61%, reflecting robust estimation behavior despite increased endpoint stringency.

Discriminative performance for clinically relevant dose thresholds

ROC analysis was conducted to assess the discriminative ability of anatomical parameters for clinically relevant lung dose thresholds. For identification of patients with MLD >13 Gy, VRF achieved the highest AUC =0.71 among all evaluated anatomical parameters (Figure 5A). An optimal VRF cut-off value of 0.18 yielded a sensitivity of 0.56 and a specificity of 0.82.

For identification of patients with V20 >24%, LIV demonstrated the highest discriminative performance among all evaluated anatomical parameters (AUC =0.75; optimal threshold: 240.38 cm3), followed by MLW (AUC =0.71; threshold: 8.91 cm) and VRF (AUC =0.69; threshold: 0.16) (Figure 5B). Across both clinically relevant thresholds, VRF and LIV consistently ranked among the most discriminative anatomical parameters.

Comparative robustness of VRF across anatomical parameters

When averaged across all regression frameworks and all lung dose-volume endpoints, VRF consistently demonstrated superior estimation robustness compared with the seven conventional anatomical parameters. Overall, VRF achieved a 19.5% reduction in MAE and a 20.9% reduction in MAPE relative to conventional descriptors. This advantage was observed consistently across SVR, RF, and GB frameworks and was most pronounced in gradient boosting analyses, which yielded the lowest absolute estimation errors (Figure 6).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that VRF is a simple, anatomy-driven volumetric descriptor capable of supporting early stratification of ipsilateral lung dose risk in breast cancer patients treated with VMAT for RNI. This work focuses on whether a transparent, imaging-derived anatomical feature can provide clinically meaningful guidance at the simulation stage. Importantly, VRF is not intended to replace established dosimetric parameters such as MLD or V20, but to provide early-stage risk awareness prior to treatment planning.

Within this context, the observed moderate yet consistent associations between VRF and lung dose metrics are expected. Lung dose in VMAT is influenced not only by target-lung geometry, but also by modulation complexity, arc range, optimization priorities, and physician-specific planning strategies (7). Therefore, strong univariate correlations from a single anatomical parameter are not anticipated. The moderate correlation strength and stability across regression frameworks support VRF as a robust pre-planning anatomical indicator.

Across the cohort, VRF showed the strongest and most consistent associations with clinically relevant endpoints, particularly MLD and V20, compared with conventional geometric surrogates. The correlation between VRF and MLD (r=0.582) exceeded that of CLD (r=0.444) and MLW (r=0.484). In prior studies, Chen et al. (14) reported correlations of approximately r≈0.234–0.247 between geometric parameters and V20 in 3D-CRT, while Kundrát et al. (24) demonstrated that CLD alone explained ~36% of interpatient variability in MLD. In the present VMAT cohort, VRF explained 34% of the variance in MLD (R2=0.34), comparable to multi-parameter models but achieved using a single dimensionless metric. This suggests that VRF efficiently captures volumetric geometric information relevant to lung dose.

The distinction between VRF and traditional geometric rules is important. The 2 cm lung inclusion guideline was developed for 3D-CRT tangential fields, where dose is largely determined by planar beam geometry. In VMAT, dose distribution is driven by volumetric target-lung interaction across multiple beam angles. As a normalized volumetric descriptor, VRF better reflects this geometry and improves inter-patient comparability by accounting for differences in lung size. Consistently, VRF outperformed conventional one- or two-dimensional metrics (e.g., CLD and MLW) across multiple endpoints. This highlights the limitation of distance-based descriptors in capturing three-dimensional spatial relationships.

Subgroup analysis further supports this interpretation. In patients without internal mammary node (IMN) irradiation, the correlation between VRF and MLD increased (r=0.637), suggesting that reduced anatomical and technical variability strengthens the geometry-dose relationship. Given that IMN inclusion significantly alters dose distribution (29,30), this finding reinforces that VRF primarily reflects underlying geometric determinants rather than cohort-specific confounders.

The discriminative performance of VRF was moderate but clinically meaningful. AUC values ranged from 0.69 to 0.71 for relevant lung dose thresholds, comparable to previously reported anatomical predictors such as sMLD (AUC ≈0.672) (14). Compared with CLD, VRF showed consistently higher AUC values across evaluated endpoints. In the pre-planning setting, such performance is sufficient for risk stratification, where the goal is to identify higher-risk patients rather than to provide definitive predictions.

The weaker association between VRF and low-dose exposure (V5) is consistent with known planning physics. Low-dose spread in VMAT is influenced by global delivery characteristics, including arc span and modulation (31), which are less dependent on local geometry. Therefore, the reduced explanatory power for V5 reflects its multifactorial nature rather than a limitation of VRF. For 3D-CRT, where low-dose spread is less influenced by modulation, the correlation between VRF and V5 is expected to be stronger, but this requires separate validation.

Regression analyses were designed to evaluate robustness rather than predictive accuracy. The consistent performance of VRF across SVR (32), RF (33), and GB models supports its model-independent validity as an anatomical feature. Overall, VRF reduced estimation error compared with conventional descriptors, supporting its practical utility as a stable geometric indicator. Overall, VRF provided a 19.5% reduction in MAE and a 20.9% reduction in MAPE averaged across all dose-volume endpoints and regression frameworks, compared with conventional geometric descriptors. This magnitude of improvement supports the practical advantage of VRF as a stable and model-independent anatomical feature.

From a clinical perspective, VRF may serve as an early, pre-planning risk stratification tool. Because it can be calculated immediately after CT simulation using standard contours, it can be readily integrated into routine workflows. Elevated VRF values may prompt consideration of lung-sparing strategies such as deep inspiration breath-hold (DIBH) (34), modified arc arrangements, or stricter optimization constraints.

Unlike conventional descriptors focused on the breast/chest wall, VRF incorporates the full target volume, including regional nodal regions. This makes it particularly relevant for patients receiving comprehensive nodal irradiation, where lung dose is influenced by complex spatial overlap (35). In the context of evolving multimodality treatment strategies (36,37), such pre-planning anatomical assessment may support safer integration of RT. Although VRF is derived from geometric principles and may be conceptually applicable to other techniques, its quantitative relationships are technique-dependent. The thresholds identified in this VMAT cohort should not be directly extrapolated to 3D-CRT or DIBH without validation. Technique-specific studies are required to establish generalizable criteria.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was retrospective and conducted at a single institution with a limited sample size, which precluded a comprehensive subgroup analysis across all combinations of laterality and IMN status. An exploratory analysis restricted to left-sided patients without IMN showed an improved correlation (r=0.637), but formal interaction testing and multiple comparison adjustments were not feasible. These factors likely contributed to variability in association strength and may have attenuated correlation and discriminative metrics.

Second, this study did not include clinical outcome data such as radiation-induced pneumonitis, and the analysis was therefore based on dosimetric surrogates (MLD, V20), which are well established predictors of lung toxicity in breast RT. According to the QUANTEC guidelines (27), thresholds such as MLD <13 Gy and V20 <30% are associated with low rates of symptomatic pneumonitis; these values were used here as clinically relevant reference points to evaluate the discriminative performance of VRF, rather than to redefine dose constraints. In contemporary breast cancer VMAT with RNI, the incidence of clinically significant (grade ≥2) pneumonitis is low, typically 1–5% (38), and mean ipsilateral lung doses generally range from 8.4–14.0 Gy depending on target extent (7), with correspondingly low toxicity rates. Given this low event rate and the modest cohort size (n=58), the expected number of pneumonitis cases would be approximately 1–3, which is insufficient for statistically robust outcome-based analysis and may lead to unstable or biased estimates. Accordingly, this study was designed as a methodological and feature-validation study, focusing on whether a CT-derived anatomical descriptor can reliably capture the geometric determinants of lung dose prior to treatment planning. Within this framework, VRF is intended as an upstream risk stratification tool reflecting geometry-driven dose variation, rather than a direct predictor of pneumonitis. Future studies will incorporate larger, multi-institutional cohorts with prospective collection of pulmonary toxicity outcomes to enable direct clinical validation of VRF.

Third, this study focused exclusively on VMAT under free-breathing conditions. While VRF as a geometric descriptor may conceptually apply to other techniques such as 3D-CRT or DIBH, the quantitative dose-response relationships and optimal thresholds have not been validated for these settings. Therefore, our findings should not be generalized beyond VMAT and free-breathing without caution, and technique‑specific validation is warranted.

Overall, this study provides initial evidence supporting the feasibility and anatomical validity of VRF as a CT-derived descriptor for ipsilateral lung dose risk. While further validation is required, the present results indicate that VRF is sufficiently accurate and robust for integration into CT simulation-based workflows, where early lung risk stratification (39) can meaningfully support planning physics decisions.

Conclusions

This study introduces VRF as a novel, CT-derived volumetric metric for pre-planning assessment of ipsilateral lung dose in breast cancer RT. By capturing the three-dimensional spatial relationship between the target and lung, VRF demonstrates stronger and more consistent associations with key dosimetric endpoints, particularly MLD and V20, compared with conventional geometric parameters. As a simple and geometry-driven descriptor, VRF can be readily derived from routine simulation CT without additional contouring or model training, supporting its practical use for early lung dose risk stratification. Its stable performance across heterogeneous and more anatomically homogeneous subgroups further supports its robustness. While further multi-institutional and outcome-based validation is warranted, the present findings suggest that VRF has potential to serve as a clinically applicable tool for geometry-informed and individualized RT planning.
