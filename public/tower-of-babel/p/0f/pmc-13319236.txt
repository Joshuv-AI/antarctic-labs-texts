Phyto-mediated synthesis of Ni–Fe bimetallic nanoparticles using Abrus precatorius for antioxidant, antimicrobial and photocatalytic dye degradation applications
Authors: Surendiran Mohan, Indira Karuppusamy, Anushiya Manickam, Sivasakthi Sivakumar, Shinyjoy Elangomannan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of sustainable nanomaterials through green synthesis has emerged as a promising strategy for biomedical and environmental applications. In this study, bimetallic Ni–Fe nanoparticles (NPs) were synthesized via an eco-friendly approach using aqueous seed extract of Abrus precatorius as a natural reducing and stabilizing agent. The UV–visible spectroscopy confirmed nanoparticle formation with characteristic absorption peaks at 257 and 278 nm. The FTIR analysis revealed the involvement of hydroxyl, carbonyl and phenolic functional groups in nanoparticle stabilization, while SEM and DLS analyses demonstrated predominantly cuboidal morphology with an average hydrodynamic diameter of 308 nm (PDI = 0.347). The green-synthesized Ni–Fe NPs exhibited significant dose-dependent antioxidant activity, showing 37% DPPH scavenging, 34% hydrogen peroxide scavenging and 45% hydroxyl radical inhibition at 250 µg/mL, along with a FRAP absorbance of 0.22. Broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity was observed against Gram-positive (Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus subtilis), Gram-negative (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae) and fungal (Aspergillus niger) strains. Furthermore, photocatalytic studies demonstrated efficient degradation of methyl orange dye, achieving 43% degradation after 6 h under solar irradiation at 125 µg/mL. The multifunctional performance of these green-synthesized Ni–Fe NPs highlight their potential for integrated biomedical and environmental remediation applications.

Introduction

Metal oxide NPs possess unique mechanical, optical, electronic, chemical, and magnetic properties that make them suitable for diverse applications across medicine, agriculture, textiles, and biomedical fields1–3. Nickel (Ni) NPs functions as a p-type semiconductors and also exhibit a range of advantageous characteristics such as catalytic activity, electrochemical stability, high chemical resistance, energy storage potential, photocatalytic efficiency, and antimicrobial properties4,5. Their demonstrated antibacterial efficacy, even at low concentrations has been attributed to their positive surface charge, nanoscale size, morphology, and high surface-to-volume ratio traits that also enhance their catalytic and biomedical potential6,7.

Among the various metal oxide NPs, iron oxide (Fe) NPs stand out for their intrinsic magnetic properties and are widely applied in bioremediation8, biomedical treatments9, catalysis10, and biosensing technologies11, primarily due to their unique morphology and size-dependent functional capabilities. The combination of two different metal NPs often leads to the formation of bimetallic NPs, which exhibit synergistic properties not found in their monometallic counterparts12. Specifically, the combination of iron and nickel NPs enhances their biomedical and catalytic efficiency. Therefore, the current study focuses on the green synthesis of bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs.

In humans, natural antioxidant levels decline with age, necessitating external supplementation. Nanoparticles synthesized via green methods have shown promising antioxidant potential, as the bioactive compounds in plant extracts are capable of scavenging free radicals13. Furthermore, rising microbial resistance to conventional antibiotics has created an urgent need for alternative antimicrobial agents. The nanoparticles, especially those synthesized using plant-derived compounds, have demonstrated the ability to inhibit microbial growth by disrupting cell membranes and altering microbial morphology14,15. Environmental contamination from textile industries, particularly through dyes like methyl orange, represents a significant source of pollution16.

Recent studies have demonstrated that plant-mediated synthesis of metallic NPs offers significant advantages over conventional chemical approaches due to its simple eco-friendly nature, cost-effectiveness, rapid, safe and biocompatibility17,18. Plant extracts contain diverse phytochemicals such as flavonoids, phenolics, alkaloids, and terpenoids that function as natural reducing and stabilizing agents during nanoparticle synthesis19–22. These biomolecules not only facilitate nanoparticle formation but also enhance their biological activity and stability. Several reports have highlighted the effectiveness of plant-derived NPs for antimicrobial, catalytic, and environmental remediation applications21,23–27.

The green synthesis of bimetallic NPs typically involves the use of plant-based reducing agents to combine two different metal precursors28. Though conventional techniques like sol-gel29, electrochemical synthesis30, microwave-assisted synthesis31, and co-precipitation32 have been employed, they often require costly instrumentation, toxic chemicals, and extended reaction times. These limitations can be effectively overcome using plant extracts such as seed, leaf, or flower extracts which are rich in biomolecules like tannins, flavonoids, alkaloids, and phenols33. These compounds not only facilitate nanoparticle synthesis but also enhance their bioactivity34,35. Abrus precatorius, a member of the Papilionaceae family, is known by various names including bead tree, rosary pea, Kundu mani in Tamil, and Gulagani in Kannada. It has been used in Siddha and Ayurvedic medicine for centuries36. The seeds, which are flat and measure 1.5–2 cm in length, contain a potent toxin known as abrin37, while the leaves are employed in traditional medicine for the treatment of various ailments38.

Despite growing interest in plant-mediated nanoparticle synthesis, the green synthesis of bimetallic Ni–Fe NPs using Abrus precatorius seed extract remains largely unexplored (Surendiran et al., 2025). Therefore, in this study, an eco-friendly method is employed to synthesize Ni–Fe NPs using phytochemical-rich seed extract as both reducing and stabilizing agents. The paper is highlighting their potential biomedical and environmental applications. The synthesized NPs are further evaluated for antimicrobial activity using the agar well diffusion method against Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus subtilis), Gram-negative bacteria (Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli), and a fungal strain (Aspergillus niger). Besides, their antioxidant was assessed using free radical scavenging assays. Additionally, the photocatalytic degradation of methyl orange dye under sunlight was investigated to evaluate the environmental remediation potential of these green-synthesized bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs., and photocatalytic dye degradation activities.

Materials and methods

Seeds of Abrus precatorius were collected from the Siddha Medicinal Plants Garden, Mettur, Salem, Tamil Nadu, India (11.7886° N, 77.8001° E), and additional samples were obtained from Tirukazhukuntram, Chengalpattu District, Tamil Nadu. The plant material was authenticated at the National Institute of Siddha, Chennai, by Dr. D. Aravind (Assistant Professor, Department of Medicinal Botany). A voucher specimen (SAP-I; Accession No. NISMB7992025) has been deposited in the Herbarium of the same institute for future reference. Iron(III) nitrate [Fe(NO₃)₃] and nickel nitrate hexahydrate [Ni(NO₃)₂·6H₂O] of analytical grade were used as metal precursors. All chemicals were used as received without further purification and double-distilled water was used throughout the experimental procedures.

Green synthesis of bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs using Abrus precatorius seed extract

Bimetallic Ni–Fe NPs were synthesized using an aqueous seed extract of Abrus precatorius as a reducing and stabilizing agent. The extract contains phytochemical constituents such as phenolics and flavonoids, which may facilitate the reduction of metal ions and stabilization of nanoparticles. Although Abrus precatorius seeds are known to contain the toxic protein abrin, the green synthesis approach used in this study involves aqueous extraction and nanoparticle formation processes that significantly reduce the presence of toxic components. Additionally, the synthesized NPs were purified through repeated washing and centrifugation steps, which further minimized the presence of residual biomolecules. These purification steps help ensure the bio-safety of the prepared NPs for potential environmental and biomedical applications. Figure 1 shows the schematic representation of the synthesis of bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs using an aqueous seed extract of Abrus precatorius. For the synthesis of NPs, 12.5 mL of 0.01 M iron (III) nitrate [Fe(NO3)3] solution and 12.5 mL of 0.02 M nickel nitrate hexahydrate [Ni(NO3)2·6H2O] solution was mixed in a Falcon tube and heated to 60 °C. After reaching this temperature, 25 mL of the aqueous Abrus precatorius seed extract was added to the reaction mixture. The mixture was then maintained at 80 °C for 1 h under constant stirring. A visible color change from clear to brownish-black indicated the formation of bimetallic Ni–Fe NPs. The reaction mixture was allowed to cool and then centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 5 min. The resulting pellet was washed with 70% ethanol to remove residual impurities and subsequently oven-dried for 24 h. The dried NPs were stored at 4 °C for further characterization and analysis.

Characterization of Ni-Fe NPs

The synthesized bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs were characterized using various instrumental techniques to confirm their formation and analyze their structural and morphological properties. UV–Visible spectroscopy was employed using a UV-3150PC spectrophotometer (JEOL, Japan) to record the optical absorption spectrum in the range of 200–1100 nm, confirming the formation of NPs through characteristic surface plasmon resonance peaks. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was conducted using a Thermo Nicolet Avatar 370 instrument (USA) over the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm-1 to identify the functional groups present in the Abrus precatorius seed extract, which were responsible for the reduction and stabilization of the metal ions during nanoparticle synthesis. The surface morphology of the synthesized NPs was analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with a JEOL JSM-6390LV, after mounting the particles on a carbon-coated copper grid, which provided detailed images of their shape and surface texture. Furthermore, dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements were performed using a Zetasizer (Malvern Instruments, UK) to determine the hydrodynamic particle size and the dispersion characteristics of the NPs in suspension.

Antimicrobial activity of Ni-Fe NPs

The antimicrobial activity of the synthesized bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs was evaluated using the agar well diffusion method. Test organisms included Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus subtilis), Gram-negative bacteria (Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli), and a fungal strain (Aspergillus niger). Each microorganism was evenly swabbed onto Mueller-Hinton Agar (MHA) plates using sterile cotton swabs. After allowing the plates to dry for 10 min, wells were punched using a sterile cork borer. Each well was loaded with 50 µL of Ni-Fe nanoparticle solution at different concentrations (50, 100, and 200 µg/mL). The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 to 72 h. Zones of inhibition were measured to assess antimicrobial efficacy39.).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was carried out using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to evaluate significant differences between groups. A p-value of less than 0.01 (p < 0.01) was considered statistically significant.

Antioxidant activity of Ni-Fe NPs

DPPH radical scavenging activity

Antioxidants are natural compounds that protect cells from damage by inhibiting the formation of free radicals. Therefore, evaluating the antioxidant potential of nanoparticles is a crucial criterion for assessing their suitability in biomedical applications40. In this study, the free radical scavenging activity of bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs synthesized using Abrus precatorius aqueous seed extract was assessed against the stable free radical 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH).

For the assay, 20 µL of the synthesized nanoparticles were added at varying concentrations (50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 µg/mL) into separate test tubes. To each tube, 0.5 mL of methanolic DPPH solution was added, followed by 0.48 mL of methanol to ensure uniform volume. The mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min to prevent photo-degradation of DPPH. Ascorbic acid was used as the standard reference (positive control).

After the incubation period, the decrease in absorbance due to the reduction of DPPH radicals was measured at 595 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay

The FRAP of bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs synthesized using Abrus precatorius aqueous seed extract was determined. The FRAP reagent was freshly prepared by mixing 10 mM TPTZ solution, 300 mM sodium acetate buffer, 40 mM HCl, and 20 mM ferric chloride in a ratio of 2:10:1:1, respectively, along with carbonated water. The reagent mixture was incubated in a steam bath at 37 °C to activate the complex. For the assay, 3 mL of bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs at varying concentrations (50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 µg/mL) were taken in separate test tubes. To each tube, 3 mL of the freshly prepared FRAP reagent was added. After an incubation period of 4 min at room temperature, the absorbance was measured at 593 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer.

Dye degradation ability of Ni-Fe NPs

The dye degradation potential of bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs synthesized using Abrus precatorius aqueous seed extract was evaluated against methyl orange, a commonly used azo dye. In this assay, 1 mL of the Ni-Fe nanoparticle solution at varying concentrations (25, 50, 75, 100, and 125 µg/mL) was added to separate tubes containing 25 mL of aqueous methyl orange dye solution. The mixtures were then exposed to solar irradiation. At specific time intervals (0, 2, 4, and 6 h), approximately 2 mL of the reaction mixture was withdrawn from each tube, and the absorbance was measured at 464 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. A visual observation of the reaction confirmed a gradual fading of color, indicating the degradation of the dye over time41. Results and discussion

Extraction of bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs

The phytochemicals present in the Abrus precatorius aqueous seed extract act as natural reducing and stabilizing agents in the synthesis of bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs. These bioactive compounds, particularly polyphenols such as flavonoids and phenolic acids, play a key role in the reduction of metal ions (Ni³⁺ and Fe³⁺) to their corresponding zero-valent metal states. Additionally, these phytochemicals enhance the antioxidant potential of the synthesized nanoparticles by contributing to their surface chemistry and electron-donating capabilities42,43.

Characterization of bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs

XRD Analysis

The crystalline structure of the biosynthesized Ni-Fe nanoparticles was analyzed using X-ray diffraction. The diffraction pattern in Fig. 2 exhibits characteristic peaks in the 2θ range of ~ 30°–62°, which are indexed to the crystallographic planes (220), (222), (311), (400), (511) and (440). These reflections indicate the formation of a cubic spinel Ni–Fe oxide phase, suggesting that the synthesized material predominantly consists of nickel ferrite–type nanostructures. Among the observed reflections, the (311) plane appears relatively prominent, which is commonly recognized as the signature peak of spinel ferrite materials. The presence of additional reflections such as (220), (400), (511), and (440) further confirms the establishment of a well-defined ferrite lattice44. The diffraction peaks are noticeably broadened, indicating the nanocrystalline nature of the particles along with possible lattice strain generated during the green synthesis process. The slight amorphous background observed in the diffraction profile can be attributed to organic phytochemicals from the seed extract, which act as stabilizing and capping agents on the nanoparticle surface. These biomolecules facilitate controlled nucleation and growth, leading to the formation of stabilized Ni–Fe ferrite NPs. Overall, the XRD analysis confirms the successful formation of nanostructured Ni-Fe spinel ferrite materials through a plant-mediated synthesis route.

UV–visible absorption spectroscopy

The UV–visible absorption spectrum of bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs synthesized using Abrus precatorius aqueous seed extract exhibited prominent absorption peaks at 257 nm and 278 nm, as shown in Fig. 3. These peaks are indicative of the formation of nanoparticles and are attributed to the electronic transitions associated with the phytochemicals capped on the nanoparticle surface. A slight peak observed around 280 nm corresponds to the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) characteristic of Ni-Fe NPs, suggesting successful nanoparticle formation45.

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy

The functional groups present on the surface of the synthesized Ni–Fe nanoparticles were analyzed using FT-IR spectroscopy. The FT-IR spectrum in Fig. 3 exhibits several characteristic absorption bands indicating the presence of phytochemical compounds derived from the Abrus precatorius seed extract. A broad absorption peak observed at 3265 cm-1 corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations of hydroxyl groups present in phenolic compounds and alcohols. The peak at 2923 cm-1 is attributed to C–H stretching vibrations of aliphatic compounds present in organic biomolecules. The absorption band appearing at 1740 cm-1 may be assigned to C = O stretching vibrations of carbonyl groups, which are commonly associated with proteins and polyphenolic compounds present in plant extracts. The peaks observed at 1640 cm-1 and 1547 cm-1 correspond to C = C stretching vibrations of aromatic rings and N–H bending vibrations of amide groups, indicating the presence of proteinaceous compounds that may contribute to nanoparticle stabilization. Furthermore, the absorption bands at 1458 cm-1 and cm-1 are attributed to C–N stretching and C–H bending vibrations suggesting the presence of amine and aromatic functional groups. The band observed at 1245 cm-1 corresponds to C–O stretching vibrations of phenolic compounds present in plant-derived phytochemicals. A strong absorption peak at 1083 cm-1 can be attributed to C–O–C stretching vibrations of polysaccharides or ether groups derived from plant biomolecules. The band at 700 cm-1 represents the aromatic C–H bending vibrations, while the peak observed at 413 cm-1 corresponds to metal–oxygen (Ni–O/Fe–O) stretching vibrations confirming the formation of Ni–Fe nanoparticles. These results indicate that phytochemical compounds such as phenolics, flavonoids, and proteins present in the Abrus precatorius seed extract act as reducing and stabilizing agents during the green synthesis of Ni–Fe nanoparticles, which is in consistent with previously reported plant-mediated nanoparticle synthesis studies21,27,39.

Scanning electron microscopy and dynamic light scattering analysis

The surface morphology of green-synthesized bimetallic Ni–Fe nanoparticles was examined using SEM. As depicted in Fig. 4, the nanoparticles predominantly exhibited cuboidal morphology, with particle sizes ranging from 108 to 373 nm indicating a relatively uniform size distribution and minimal agglomeration. The EDX analysis (Fig. 4) confirmed the elemental composition of the synthesized nanoparticles indicating the presence of oxygen (O), iron (Fe), nickel (Ni), sodium (Na), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K).

Fig. 4SEM images and EDX analysis of A. precatorius aqueous seed extract mediated bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs.

SEM images and EDX analysis of A. precatorius aqueous seed extract mediated bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs.

These morphological findings were further supported by DLS analysis (Fig. 5a, b), which revealed an average particle size of 308 nm and a Polydispersity Index (PDI) of 0.347. The particle size obtained from DLS (308 nm) is comparable to the size range observed in SEM images (108–373 nm). The slight variation between these measurements can be attributed to the different principles of the techniques used. The DLS measures the hydrodynamic diameter of nanoparticles in suspension, which includes the solvent layer and biomolecular capping agents surrounding the particles. In contrast, SEM determines the actual physical size of dried nanoparticles. Such variations between DLS and SEM measurements are commonly reported in nanoparticle characterization studies and indicate the presence of phytochemical molecules from the Abrus precatorius extract acting as stabilizing and capping agents on the nanoparticle surface42. The consistency between SEM and DLS results validates the successful synthesis and relatively uniform dispersion of the bimetallic Ni–Fe nanoparticles. Moreover, the PDI value being below 0.7 suggests a narrow size distribution, which is desirable for biomedical and catalytic applications46.

Antimicrobial properties of Ni–Fe nanoparticles

The bimetallic Ni–Fe nanoparticles synthesized using Abrus precatorius aqueous seed extract were evaluated for their antimicrobial activity using the agar well diffusion method. The test organisms included Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus subtilis), Gram-negative bacteria (Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli), and a fungal strain (Aspergillus niger). Ampicillin (10 µg/mL) was used as the positive control for bacterial strains, while fluconazole (10 µg/mL) served as the positive control for the fungal strain. Sterile distilled water was used as the negative control. The synthesized Ni–Fe nanoparticles exhibited broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against all tested microorganisms, and the zone of inhibition increased proportionally with increasing nanoparticle concentration, as illustrated in Fig. 5c. This enhanced antimicrobial activity can be attributed to the nanoscale size and large surface area of the Ni–Fe nanoparticles, which facilitate their interaction and penetration through microbial cell walls. Once internalized, the nanoparticles generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), which induce oxidative stress, disrupt membrane integrity, damage intracellular biomolecules such as proteins and DNA, and ultimately lead to microbial cell death. This ROS-mediated antimicrobial mechanism has been widely reported for metal and metal oxide nanoparticles47. Furthermore, the antimicrobial efficacy of the nanoparticles may vary depending on the structural differences in microbial cell walls and cellular composition between Gram-positive and Gram-negative microorganisms20,48.

TEM analysis

The TEM analysis was carried out to examine the morphology and particle size distribution of the synthesized Ni–Fe nanoparticles. The TEM images shown in Fig. 6 revealed that the nanoparticles were predominantly irregular and aggregated in nature, forming cluster-like structures. The aggregation observed in the micrograph may be attributed to the strong magnetic interaction between the Ni and Fe components as well as the presence of biomolecules from the Abrus precatorius seed extract acting as stabilizing agents.

From the TEM micrograph, the nanoparticles appear to be distributed in the nanoscale range with particle sizes observed approximately in the range of 100–500 nm, as indicated by the scale bar in the image. The higher magnification images further confirm the formation of nanoscale particles with irregular morphology and slight agglomeration. Such aggregation behavior is commonly reported for green-synthesized metal nanoparticles due to intermolecular interactions between phytochemical capping agents and metal particles. The presence of aggregated nanostructures suggests that phytochemical compounds present in the plant extract may have played a dual role in the reduction of metal ions and stabilization of the synthesized nanoparticles. Similar morphological characteristics have been reported for plant-mediated metal nanoparticles synthesized using different plant extracts, where irregular and aggregated nanostructures were commonly observed21,27,39. The SAED pattern exhibits concentric diffraction rings with bright spots, indicating the polycrystalline nature of the synthesized Ni–Fe nanoparticles. Such diffraction patterns confirm the formation of crystalline nanostructures, which is consistent with previously reported green-synthesized metal nanoparticles. Overall, the TEM results confirm the successful formation of nanoscale Ni–Fe nanoparticles and support the role of plant-derived biomolecules in controlling the morphology and stabilization of the nanoparticles during the green synthesis process.

Therefore, the observed zeta potential value suggests that the synthesized Ni–Fe nanoparticles possess moderate colloidal stability making them potentially suitable for various biological and catalytic applications.

Free radical scavenging abilities of Ni–Fe nanoparticles

The antioxidant capacity of bimetallic Ni–Fe nanoparticles synthesized via the green route was assessed based on their ability to scavenge various free radicals including DPPH, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), hydroxyl radicals, and their ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP).

DPPH radical scavenging ability

The DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging assay is widely used to evaluate the hydrogen-donating ability of antioxidants. It is considered a stable and reliable method for assessing the antioxidant potential of nanoparticles50. The green-synthesized Ni–Fe nanoparticles showed dose-dependent DPPH scavenging activity as illustrated in Fig. 8. The scavenging efficiency increased linearly with increasing nanoparticle concentration (50–250 µg/mL), rising from 4% to 37%, which is comparable to the trend observed with ascorbic acid.

Fig. 8DPPH and hydrogen peroxide radical scavenging activity of A. precatorius aqueous seed extract mediated bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs.

DPPH and hydrogen peroxide radical scavenging activity of A. precatorius aqueous seed extract mediated bimetallic Ni-Fe NPs.

Hydrogen peroxide scavenging ability

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) scavenging was evaluated to determine the nanoparticles’ ability to neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS) that can otherwise cause cellular damage, particularly to the lipid membrane51. As shown in Fig. 8, the bimetallic Ni–Fe nanoparticles demonstrated a concentration-dependent scavenging activity27,33. As the concentration increased from 50 to 250 µg/mL, the H2O2 scavenging percentage rose from 7% to 34%, closely resembling the performance of ascorbic acid.

Hydroxyl radical scavenging ability

Hydroxyl radicals generated through the Fenton reaction involving hydrogen peroxide and iron–EDTA, react with deoxyribose to form pink chromogens. The introduction of Ni–Fe nanoparticles compete with deoxyribose for hydroxyl radicals, thereby reducing chromogen formation. This inhibition reflects the hydroxyl radical scavenging capacity of the nanoparticles52. As depicted in Fig. 9a, the scavenging efficiency increased with the nanoparticle concentration, ranging from 6% at 50 µg/mL to 45% at 250 µg/mL, following a linear trend similar to that of ascorbic acid.

Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP)

The FRAP assay measures the antioxidant power of a compound by its ability to reduce the ferric ion (Fe3+)–TPTZ complex to the ferrous ion (Fe2+)–TPTZ complex, which produces a blue color under acidic conditions53. The green-synthesized Ni–Fe nanoparticles exhibited significant reducing power, with absorbance values increasing from 0.043 to 0.22 the concentration increased from 50 to 250 µg/mL as shown in Fig. 9b. This linear increase is consistent with the response observed for the standard antioxidant such as ascorbic acid.

The antioxidant activity observed in the present study is comparable with previously reported plant-mediated metal nanoparticles. Several studies have demonstrated that phytochemical compounds present in plant extracts, such as flavonoids, phenolics, and alkaloids, play a significant role in enhancing the radical scavenging ability of synthesized nanoparticles. For instance, Chandraker et al.21 reported that green-synthesized silver nanoparticles using Rubia cordifolia leaf extract exhibited strong antioxidant activity due to the presence of surface-bound phytochemicals. Similarly, Chandraker et al.,54 demonstrated that plant-mediated silver nanoparticles synthesized from Bryophyllum pinnatum extract showed effective free radical scavenging properties. In another study, Mehra et al.22 reported that plant-derived nanoparticles exhibited improved antioxidant performance due to synergistic interactions between metal nanoparticles and phytochemical capping agents. The percentage inhibition values obtained in the present study indicate that the green-synthesized Ni–Fe nanoparticles possess considerable antioxidant potential with radical scavenging efficiency. This results is comparable to previously reported plant-mediated nanoparticles, which typically exhibit antioxidant inhibition values ranging from approximately 60–90% depending on nanoparticle concentration and phytochemical composition of the plant extract. Therefore, the antioxidant activity observed for the Ni–Fe nanoparticles in this study may be attributed to the combined effect of the metal core and the bioactive phytochemical compounds derived from Abrus precatorius seed extract.

Dye degradation ability of Ni–Fe nanoparticles

The degradation of organic dyes such as methyl orange using bimetallic Ni–Fe nanoparticles as active photocatalysts presents a promising strategy for combating environmental pollution. In the present study, the photocatalytic activity of Abrus precatorius aqueous seed extract-mediated bimetallic Ni–Fe nanoparticles was evaluated against methyl orange dye under solar irradiation.

The photocatalytic degradation of methyl orange is illustrated in Fig. 10. The results indicate that the degradation efficiency increases with both nanoparticle concentration and duration of sunlight exposure. As the concentration of Ni–Fe nanoparticles increased from 25 to 125 µg/mL, the degradation efficiency improved from 11% to 28% after 2 h, from 15% to 31% after 4 h, and from 19% to 43% after 6 h of exposure. The graph data clearly demonstrate that the degradation after 6 h varies significantly with concentration, showing a strong concentration-dependent behavior. Statistical analysis confirms that these differences are significant, with p-values less than 0.01. This enhancement in dye degradation is attributed to the photo-oxidation capability of the Ni–Fe nanoparticles. The photocatalytic degradation of methyl orange by the synthesized Ni–Fe nanoparticles can be attributed to the generation of ROS under light irradiation. When the nanoparticles are exposed to light, electrons in the valence band become excited and are transferred to the conduction band, generating electron–hole pairs. These charge carriers subsequently react with dissolved oxygen and water molecules to produce reactive oxygen species such as hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and superoxide radicals (O₂•⁻). These highly reactive species attack the dye molecules, breaking down the complex chromophoric structures and leading to the degradation of methyl orange into smaller and less harmful molecules. Similar photocatalytic degradation mechanisms involving ROS have also been reported for plant-mediated metal nanoparticles used in dye removal studies27,22.

Conclusion

In this study, bimetallic Ni–Fe NPs were successfully synthesized using an aqueous seed extract of Abrus precatorius through a green synthesis approach. The formation of nanoparticles was confirmed by various characterization techniques including UV–Vis spectroscopy, FTIR, SEM, DLS, TEM and EDX analysis. The synthesized nanoparticles exhibited notable biological and photocatalytic activity. The antimicrobial studies demonstrated broad-spectrum activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria as well as fungal strains. The antioxidant assays revealed significant radical scavenging activity, with a maximum DPPH inhibition of approximately 37% at a concentration of 250 µg/mL. Furthermore, the photocatalytic studies showed effective degradation of methyl orange dye achieving a maximum degradation efficiency of about 43% after 6 h of solar irradiation at 125 µg/mL nanoparticle concentration. These findings demonstrate that the green-synthesized Ni–Fe nanoparticles possess promising antioxidant, antimicrobial, and photocatalytic properties suggesting their potential applications in the environmental remediation and biomedical research55,56.
