Multitargeted Neuroprotective Strategies of Ethyl Pyruvate: From Molecular Mechanisms to Clinical Perspectives in CNS Disorders
Authors: Seung-Woo Kim, Hong Seok Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ethyl pyruvate (EP), a simple aliphatic ester of pyruvic acid, is a potent neuroprotective compound with diverse properties, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, and metabolic regulatory activities. Given the intricate nature of neurological disorders, the multi-target mechanism of action of EP offers significant therapeutic advantages. Preclinical studies across various models of central nervous system (CNS) injury have consistently shown that EP mitigates neuronal loss, preserves blood-brain barrier integrity, and promotes functional recovery. The therapeutic efficacy of EP is attributed to its multifaceted mechanisms of action. It modulates key signaling pathways, such as nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), activates the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway, and provides metabolic support. Specifically, EP suppresses neuroinflammation by preventing HMGB1 release through calcium chelation and by modulating NF-κB signaling via covalent modification of the p65 subunit and stabilization of IκB-α. Furthermore, it enhances endogenous antioxidant defenses by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and inducing Nrf2 translocation. EP also acts as a bioavailable pyruvate donor, restoring intracellular adenosine triphosphate and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide levels under ischemic or oxidative stress conditions. Comparative studies with other pyruvate derivatives have highlighted the superior efficacy and safety of EP. Additionally, structural isosteres such as N,N-diisopropyl-2-oxopropanamide (DIPOPA) and N,N-diethyl-2-oxopropanamide (DEOPA) demonstrate even greater neuroprotective potency, primarily through enhanced NF-κB inhibition by suppressing IκB-α degradation. EP is a promising therapeutic candidate for addressing oxidative stress, inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation associated with CNS diseases. However, further research is necessary to optimize the appropriate dosage, evaluate its long-term efficacy, and facilitate its clinical translation.

INTRODUCTION

Pyruvate is the end product of glycolysis and is subsequently used as a substrate in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Under aerobic conditions, pyruvate enters the mitochondrial matrix, where it undergoes oxidative decarboxylation in a reaction catalyzed by the pyruvate dehydrogenase enzyme complex to form acetyl coenzyme A, carbon dioxide, and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) (Patel and Roche, 1990). Endogenous pyruvate is a key metabolite in adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production because it links glycolysis to the TCA cycle, whereas exogenous pyruvate can function as an antioxidant and anti-inflammatory molecule (Zilberter et al., 2015). Pyruvate can act as an endogenous scavenger of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reacts with hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) through non-enzymatic oxidative decarboxylation, thereby reducing oxidative stress and scavenging hydroxyl radicals (Desagher et al., 1997; Dobsak et al., 1999). However, pyruvate is unstable in aqueous solution because it readily undergoes condensation reactions, thereby reducing its bioavailability and limiting its therapeutic applicability. To overcome this chemical instability and effectively harness the beneficial properties of pyruvate in vivo, ethyl pyruvate (EP) has been developed.

EP, a simple aliphatic ester of pyruvic acid, exerts anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic effects (Hauser et al., 2005; Wang et al., 2005; Ulloa et al., 2002). In pathological states, EP regulates key signaling pathways, including nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription, thereby suppressing pro-apoptotic and pro-inflammatory gene expression and contributing to reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging and cellular ATP production (Dong et al., 2019; Han et al., 2005; Jang et al., 2012; Kim et al., 2005, 2008). Pyruvate is unstable in solution; therefore, EP has been considered a more suitable therapeutic candidate for in vivo applications. Sims et al. showed that intraperitoneal treatment with EP solution attenuated damage to the intestinal mucosa in a rat model of mesenteric ischemia/reperfusion compared to pyruvate treatment at the same dose (Sims et al., 2001). High mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) is a nuclear protein that maintains nucleosome architecture, regulates gene transcription, and is extracellularly released either passively from necrotic cells or actively from stimulated immune cells (Müller et al., 2001). Once in the extracellular space, HMGB1 induces inflammatory responses attenuated by EP (Ulloa et al., 2002). Some studies have reported that EP inhibits the translocation of HMGB1 from the nucleus to the cytoplasm and suppresses the expression of HMGB1, thereby reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine production (Davé et al., 2009; Luan et al., 2010). Additionally, calcium ions chelated by EP can inhibit the phosphorylation and release of HMGB1 (Shin et al., 2014).

Brain damage and neurodegenerative diseases are not caused by a single mechanism but rather by a complex interplay of multiple pathological processes, including inflammation, oxidative stress, energy metabolism dysregulation, and neuronal cell death. These processes collectively contribute to the development and progression of neurological diseases (Dash et al., 2025). For example, in the post-ischemic brain, the rapid generation of ROS and subsequent inflammatory response represent central pathophysiological mechanisms (Chan, 2001). Activated microglia and astrocytes then release pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), along with various chemokines, thereby establishing a highly neuroinflammatory environment in the brain (Xu et al., 2020). In chronic neurodegenerative disorders, such as Parkinson’s disease, dopaminergic neuron degeneration in the substantia nigra is a defining feature arising from the combined effects of mitochondrial dysfunction, sustained neuroinflammation, and persistent oxidative stress (Henrich et al., 2023). Moreover, during brain injury, the disruption of the blood–brain barrier and intracerebral inflammation reinforce each other in a vicious cycle, contributing to the progression of injury and long-term neurological deficits (Archie et al., 2021). These intricate pathophysiological interactions highlight the limitations of single-target therapies and underscore the need for multifaceted therapeutic strategies. Considering the complex pathophysiology of neurological injuries and diseases, therapeutic strategies that target multiple pathways are essential. EP is a promising candidate that exhibits anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, and metabolic regulatory properties, conferring consistent neuroprotective effects in various experimental models. Previous studies have comprehensively described the anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory effects of EP in various inflammatory and autoimmune diseases. However, they primarily focused on its systemic protective properties and did not adequately address its mechanistic role within the complex pathological environment of neurological diseases. In contrast, this review focuses on the mechanistic interactions between EP and key pathogenic processes, including neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic disorders. By incorporating recent findings, this review presents a focused and up-to-date perspective on the therapeutic potential of EP in neurological diseases.

THERAPEUTIC POTENCY OF EP IN CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM (CNS) DISEASES

EP has been extensively studied as a neuroprotective agent in CNS diseases owing to its cytoprotective properties. Its ability to mitigate the core pathological causes of neuronal damage makes EP a promising therapeutic target. Numerous preclinical studies have demonstrated the neuroprotective effects of EP in CNS disease models. EP, a multifunctional compound, protects neurons, modulates glial cell activation, and promotes functional recovery (Table 1).

Parkinson’s disease

EP exerts neuroprotective effects in various models of Parkinson’s disease. It attenuates 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP)-induced dopaminergic (DA) neuronal death in the substantia nigra of mice and reduces MPP⁺-induced cytotoxicity in SH-SY5Y cells by restoring ERK phosphorylation and decreasing PARP cleavage (Choi et al., 2010). Moreover, in mice, it attenuates MPTP-induced degeneration of nigrostriatal dopaminergic (DA) neurons, inhibits glial activation, prevents striatal dopamine loss, and improves motor function by suppressing the production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species in microglia and astrocytes (Huh et al., 2011). EP ameliorates motor and cognitive impairment in mice with MPTP-induced Parkinson’s disease by preserving mitochondrial function, protecting dopaminergic neurons, and reducing oxidative stress-related damage in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc)- ventral tegmental area (VTA), striatum, and hippocampal CA1 regions (Haga et al., 2019). Additionally, it exerts neuroprotective effects by restoring mitochondrial complex I activity and reducing oxidative stress and α-synuclein accumulation, thereby preserving DA neurons and motor function, highlighting its potential therapeutic value in Parkinson’s disease (Satpute et al., 2013). In cellular models, EP protects PC12 cells from dopamine-induced apoptosis by attenuating oxidative stress and modulating NF-κB, p53, and mitochondrial signaling. It inhibits 6-hydroxydopamine-induced apoptosis in SH-SY5Y cells by suppressing ERK signaling and ROS-mediated neuromelanin synthesis while promoting autophagy (Luo and Sakamoto, 2023; Wang et al., 2005). Moreover, EP suppresses NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation in microglial cells by reducing ROS production, cytokine release, and pyroptosis, while downregulating NF-κB/HMGB1 signaling and modulating miR-223 expression (Olcum et al., 2021). Collectively, EP exerts multifaceted neuroprotective effects in Parkinson’s disease models by reducing oxidative stress, regulating apoptotic and autophagic pathways, and suppressing neuroinflammation.

Cerebral ischemia

Cerebral ischemia, characterized by reduced cerebral blood flow, triggers a cascade of pathological processes, including excitotoxicity, peri-infarct depolarization, apoptosis, and inflammation. EP has demonstrated substantial neuroprotective effects. In a middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) model, EP reduced infarct volume within a wide therapeutic window by suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-1β) and enhancing anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4 and IL-13) in the post-ischemic brain (Yu et al., 2005). Compared with pyruvate, EP provides stronger and longer-lasting neuroprotection by protecting neurons from oxidative stress and attenuating microglial activation via suppression of NF-κB-mediated inflammation (Kim et al., 2005). Furthermore, EP promotes neuronal survival under oxygen-glucose deprivation, reduces brain damage, and ameliorates neurological deficits by inhibiting apoptosis and facilitating neural restoration (Li et al., 2017). These effects are complemented by evidence that EP combined with aspirin in transient focal ischemia yields synergistic or additive neuroprotective effects. The combination therapy reduces infarct volume, neurological deficits, and neuronal injury by modulating NF-κB signaling (Kim et al., 2010). EP confers multifaceted neuroprotection against cerebral ischemia and hypoxic-ischemic brain injury through its anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and antioxidant properties, highlighting its potential as a promising therapeutic agent.

Traumatic brain injury (TBI)

In TBI models, EP has demonstrated therapeutic potential for improving pathological outcomes. Delayed treatment with sodium pyruvate or EP after cortical contusion injury in rats significantly reduces cell death, improves neurobehavioral recovery, enhances metabolism, and suppresses inflammation; in all cases, there are neuroprotective effects even when the treatment is administered for up to 24 h post-injury (Moro and Sutton, 2010). EP treatment reduces HMGB1/ Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/NF-κB-mediated inflammation, alleviates brain edema, improves motor function, and decreases cortical apoptotic cell death, suggesting that its neuroprotective efficacy is largely mediated through anti-inflammatory mechanisms (Su et al., 2011). Consistently, EP attenuates cerebral edema, downregulates HMGB1, TLR4, and receptor for advanced glycation endproducts (RAGE) expression, and restores blood–brain barrier integrity by preserving tight junction proteins, such as occludin, claudin, and ZO-1, in experimental TBI models (Evran et al., 2020). In addition to TBI, EP attenuates mortality in sepsis models by inhibiting HMGB1 release (Ulloa et al., 2002). Long-term studies have revealed that EP confers sustained neuroprotection after TBI by reducing inflammation and preventing blood–brain barrier disruption, ultimately improving sensorimotor and cognitive functions (Shi et al., 2015). Recently, EP treatment has been shown to enhance functional recovery and ameliorate white matter injury by promoting anti-inflammatory microglial polarization, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic strategy for severe TBI (Mao et al., 2021). EP exerts sustained neuroprotective effects after TBI by reducing inflammation, preserving blood–brain barrier integrity, and promoting functional recovery.

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH)

ICH is a life-threatening condition caused by bleeding within the brain parenchyma; however, effective therapeutic options are limited. EP provides neuroprotection in ICH by mitigating HMGB1 release and suppressing NF-κB-mediated inflammatory responses, thereby reducing neuroinflammation, neuronal apoptosis, cerebral edema, and neurological deficits (Lei et al., 2013; Su et al., 2013). Similarly, EP attenuates early brain injury after subarachnoid hemorrhage by improving neurological function, reducing TNF-α expression, inhibiting JNK signaling, and decreasing neuronal apoptosis and neurodegeneration (Lv et al., 2017). EP alleviates inflammation in diabetic ICH by downregulating HMGB1/TLR4 signaling and suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation, further supporting its neuroprotective potential (Wang et al., 2022). Moreover, Fang et al. reported that EP administration suppressed microglial activation, reduced IL-1β and TNF-α expression, stabilized the blood–brain barrier by preserving tight junction proteins, and decreased matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) levels, which improved brain edema and neurological deficits in an ICH mouse model (Fang et al., 2016). EP exerts potent neuroprotective effects in various intracerebral and subarachnoid hemorrhage models by attenuating neuroinflammation, inhibiting apoptotic pathways, stabilizing the blood–brain barrier, and reducing brain edema and neurological deficits, highlighting its potential as a promising therapeutic agent for hemorrhagic brain injury.

Multiple sclerosis (MS)

MS is a chronic inflammatory demyelinating disease of the CNS that remains incurable despite the availability of several therapeutic agents. EP has demonstrated notable efficacy in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), an established animal model of MS, by reducing inflammatory infiltrates and activated macrophages, microglia, and astrocytes within the CNS, as well as by suppressing T-cell and antigen-presenting cell activation in gut-associated lymphoid tissue (Djedović et al., 2017). Additionally, EP induces a tolerogenic phenotype in dendritic cells by downregulating T cell-activating molecules and inhibiting T cell proliferation (Djedović et al., 2019). Its benefits extend beyond immune regulation and have direct effects on demyelination and repair. In a cuprizone-induced demyelination model, EP enhanced remyelination and functional recovery by attenuating neuroinflammation, shifting microglia toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype, promoting oligodendrocyte regeneration, facilitating lesion repair through astrocyte reprogramming, and improving myelin debris clearance (He et al., 2019, 2021). Consistent with these findings, a comparative analysis showed that EP, which shares some properties with the approved MS therapy dimethyl fumarate, effectively reduced inflammation across various immune cell types and suppressed disease progression in an animal model of MS, while exhibiting potential as a therapeutic candidate (Miljković et al., 2015). These studies indicate that EP is a potential therapeutic agent for MS, as it simultaneously targets inflammatory and demyelinating components.

Spinal cord injury (SCI)

SCI results in severe neurological deficits through a cascade of pathological events, including neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, astrogliosis, and edema. In traumatic SCI models, EP administration markedly reduced histological tissue damage, neutrophil infiltration, nitrotyrosine formation, iNOS expression, pro-inflammatory cytokine production, NF-κB activation, ERK1/2 phosphorylation, and apoptosis, accompanied by dose-dependent improvements in motor recovery (Genovese et al., 2009). These beneficial effects are closely associated with the inhibition of HMGB1 release, underscoring the importance of HMGB1-mediated inflammatory pathways in SCI (Wang et al., 2009). Further evidence from rat SCI models demonstrates that EP attenuates reactive astrogliosis, decreases microglial and macrophage activation, enhances neuronal survival, and promotes axonal regeneration. This is further supported by in vitro findings showing reduced astrocytic activation in a scratch wound model, suggesting an indirect inhibitory mechanism in vivo (Yuan et al., 2012). Mechanistically, EP reduces astrocytic swelling and spinal cord edema induced by oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD)/reoxygenation by downregulating HMGB1 and aquaporin 4 (AQP4) expression through the inhibition of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway and modulation of IL-6 signaling (Sun et al., 2017). Consistently, in vivo administration of EP or other HMGB1 inhibitors decreases glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and AQP4 expression, alleviates edema, improves motor outcomes, and suppresses TLR4/NF-κB signaling activation (Sun et al., 2019). EP shows therapeutic potency for SCI by offering multifaceted neuroprotective effects, including the reduction of inflammation and cell death and the promotion of neuronal repair, largely by inhibiting HMGB1-mediated signaling.

Neonatal hypoxic-ischemic (H-I) brain injury

H-I brain injury is the leading cause of mortality and long-term neurological deficits, including neuronal death, white matter damage, and impaired neurodevelopmental outcomes. The pathophysiological mechanisms involve oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, apoptosis, and pyroptosis, which disrupt normal brain development. Despite advances in supportive care, effective pharmacological interventions remain scarce. Preclinical studies in neonatal rodent models have demonstrated that EP considerably reduces brain tissue loss and improves neurological function for up to 2 months by attenuating neuronal cell death through the reduction of calpain activation and calcium dysregulation in H-I models and OGD, while simultaneously inhibiting microglial activation and NF-κB-mediated inflammatory responses to mitigate neuroinflammation (Shen et al., 2010). In models of periventricular leukomalacia, repeated EP administration reduced ventricular enlargement, preserved developing oligodendrocytes, and alleviated hypomyelination, accompanied by the downregulation of pro-apoptotic proteins (cleaved caspase-3 and Bax) and upregulation of Bcl-2, indicating the modulation of apoptotic pathways (Wang et al., 2013). Combination therapy with early EP and delayed insulin-like growth factor-I (IGF-I) further enhances neuronal survival, neurogenesis, and maturation, suggesting additive neuroprotective effects (Rong et al., 2015). A recent study showed that EP suppresses hippocampal neuronal pyroptosis by inhibiting the NLRP3/Caspase-1/Gasdermin D (GSDMD) pathway, reducing neuroinflammation, and promoting neural recovery (Sha et al., 2024). These findings demonstrate that EP confers neuroprotection against neonatal H-I injury through anti-apoptotic, anti-inflammatory, and anti-pyroptotic mechanisms, while supporting oligodendrocyte survival and neurogenesis.

MECHANISM

Neurological diseases caused by oxidative stress and inflammation pose serious treatment challenges. EP is a promising multifunctional agent that can address these issues. It exhibits cytoprotective effects and is a potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory regulator. Through its ability to scavenge ROS, chelate metal ions, and regulate redox-sensitive pathways and energy metabolism, EP provides robust protection against neuronal and systemic injury (Fig. 1, Table 2)

Antioxidant effects

Pyruvate, the simplest of the alpha-keto acids, functions as a scavenger of ROS and non-enzymatically reacts with H₂O₂ in the presence of related keto acids to yield acetate, carbon dioxide, and water (Holleman, 1904). EP, a pyruvic acid derivative, directly decomposes H₂O₂ and reduces dopamine-induced ROS production in PC12 cells (Wang et al., 2005). Moreover, it prevents DNA damage and hydroxyl radical generation caused by peroxynitrite, a reactive molecule implicated in neurodegenerative disorders (Chen et al., 2010). In a lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced endotoxemia model, EP reduced blood nitrate/nitrite and isoprostane concentrations and attenuated nitric oxide release and lipid peroxidation (Hauser et al., 2005). Furthermore, EP alleviates lung injury in rats and decreases inflammatory markers, such as NO and PGE₂, by inhibiting the MAPK signaling pathway, which subsequently downregulates COX-2 and iNOS expression (Chen et al., 2013). Microarray analyses have demonstrated that EP modulates the Nrf2 pathway, leading to the upregulation of antioxidant response element-dependent genes in keratocytes and myofibroblasts (Harvey et al., 2010). EP has been shown to inhibit liver fibrosis in rats with bile duct ligation by enhancing the Nrf2 signaling pathway, thereby increasing the expression of antioxidant enzymes Nrf2, SOD2, NQO1, and glutathione peroxidase, and suppressing the expression of inflammatory genes (Zong et al., 2019). In macrophages, EP induces heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) expression via the p38 MAPK–Nrf2 pathway through GSH depletion, suppressing LPS-induced inflammatory responses and improving survival in cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced septic mice in an HO-1-dependent manner (Jang et al., 2012). Similarly, EP protects against subarachnoid hemorrhage-induced brain injury by activating the Keap1–Nrf2 signaling pathway, thereby reducing oxidative stress, apoptosis, and neuronal damage and improving neurological function (Chen et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). Moreover, EP pre-treatment increases HO-1 expression, which exerts antioxidant effects and promotes the accumulation of glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) and GSH in astrocytes (Shin et al., 2012). Collectively, these findings indicate that EP exerts protective effects through multiple mechanisms, including direct ROS scavenging, modulation of redox-sensitive signaling pathways, and regulation of Nrf2-dependent antioxidant gene expression, highlighting its therapeutic potential in the treatment of inflammatory and neurodegenerative disorders.

Anti-inflammation

EP has been investigated as an anti-inflammatory agent in several disease models. In a sepsis animal model, EP administration reduced mortality and systemic inflammatory responses by markedly reducing TNF-α secretion into the serum and its expression in macrophages, and inhibiting LPS-induced HMGB1 secretion in vivo (Ulloa et al., 2002). The authors suggested that EP is an effective inhibitor of HMGB1 release, although its precise molecular target remains unclear. Most studies have demonstrated that EP suppresses HMGB1 expression and secretion; however, the molecular mechanism underlying its action remains unclear. HMGB1 release is regulated by acetylation, phosphorylation, glycosylation, and the redox state; therefore, EP can inhibit phosphorylation-dependent HMGB1 secretion via direct calcium chelation (Shin et al., 2015). In a sepsis-induced acute renal failure mouse model, EP markedly reduced acute renal and multi-organ injury, even when administered 12 h after surgery, by decreasing pro-inflammatory and pro-coagulant factors, such as TNF-α and PAI-1 (Miyaji et al., 2003). Similarly, EP exerts protective effects against kainic acid-induced neurotoxicity, reducing reactive gliosis and hippocampal levels of COX-2, IL-1β, and TNF-α, even when administered 12 h after injection (Cho et al., 2006). In a clinically relevant awake hemorrhage model, EP significantly improved survival and attenuated systemic inflammation by lowering circulating TNF-α levels, highlighting its therapeutic potential in resuscitation settings (Cai et al., 2009). At the cellular level, EP effectively inhibits LPS- and ATP-induced activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to reduced caspase-1 activation and diminished secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 in microglia (Olcum et al., 2021). Furthermore, metabolomic studies have revealed that EP decreases inflammatory markers, such as IL-6 and HO-1, and significantly alters key metabolic pathways, including phenylalanine and arginine metabolism, in plasma metabolites and immune cells (Kwak et al., 2024). Mechanistic studies have shown that EP directly inhibits NF-κB signaling. In LPS-stimulated macrophages, EP prevents NF-κB DNA binding through covalent modification of the p65 subunit at Cys38, without affecting IκB-α or IκB-β degradation (Han et al., 2005). However, in vivo studies have reported that EP inhibits the degradation of IκB-α and IκB-β, thereby preventing NF-κB translocation (Jang et al., 2014; Johansson et al., 2008; Shen et al., 2014). More recently, the anti-inflammatory and antioxidative effects of EP on neuroprotection have been attributed to a novel mechanism in which EP-induced Nrf2 nuclear translocation predominates over NF-κB binding to the co-activator p300 in LPS-treated microglia, thereby inhibiting inflammatory gene expression. This suggests that Nrf2 activation may underlie the anti-inflammatory effects of other phytochemicals (Kim et al., 2013). Collectively, EP exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects through multiple mechanisms, including the inhibition of HMGB1 secretion, suppression of key pro-inflammatory cytokines, blockade of NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome signaling, and promotion of Nrf2 activation. These findings highlight EP as a promising therapeutic candidate for treating sepsis, neuroinflammation, and other inflammation-related diseases.

Anti-excitotoxic effects

Pyruvate protects against excitotoxic cell death, which involves calcium (Ca²⁺) elevation, ROS production, and energy failure (Ruiz et al., 1998; Tian et al., 2014). In hippocampal slices, N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor activation induces glycolytic impairment and reduces ATP levels by inhibiting glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), an effect blocked by pyruvate (Izumi and Zorumski, 2010). Pyruvate promotes GSH synthesis, thereby protecting cells from ROS during glutamate-induced toxicity (Miao et al., 2011). Additionally, pyruvate-mediated accumulation of citrate and isocitrate acts as a Zn²⁺ chelator, protecting cells from Zn²⁺ toxicity (Sul et al., 2016). EP exhibits additional protective properties. EP can be stabilized in a Ca²⁺-containing balanced salt solution (Ringer-EP), in which two EP molecules were associated with Ca²⁺ (Sims et al., 2001). Unlike pyruvate, EP directly chelates Ca²⁺ under cell-free conditions, suppressing A23187 (a calcium ionophore)-induced intracellular Ca²⁺ surges and NMDA-induced HMGB1 release, which depends on Ca²⁺ elevation (Shin et al., 2014). Spectrophotometric studies further revealed that EP directly chelates Zn²⁺, a finding confirmed in NMDA-treated primary cortical cultures and hippocampal slice cultures subjected to OGD. EP suppresses intracellular Zn²⁺ accumulation and subsequent neuronal cell death (Kim et al., 2016a). Although pyruvate primarily protects against excitotoxicity by restoring energy metabolism, enhancing antioxidant defenses, and indirectly mitigating metal ion toxicity, EP provides additional direct protective effects through Ca²⁺ and Zn²⁺ chelation, thereby offering broader neuroprotection against excitotoxicity and ischemic injury.

Energy supply

As the final product of glycolysis and a substrate of the TCA cycle, pyruvate plays a central role in cellular energy metabolism. Pyruvate rescues Zn²⁺-induced neuronal cell death, in which glycolysis and energy production are suppressed via the inhibition of GAPDH (Sheline et al., 2000). Similarly, EP replenishes NAD⁺ to reduce neuronal cell death under Zn²⁺ toxicity (Kim et al., 2016a). In hindlimb ischemia and reperfusion models, EP treatment, whether administered before or after ischemia, effectively preserves ATP levels (Crawford et al., 2011). Consistently, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) studies have revealed that EP provides pyruvate as a TCA cycle substrate and offers greater protection to H₂O₂-treated neonatal rat cerebrocortical slices (Zeng et al., 2007). These findings indicate that EP serves as a substrate for the TCA cycle and contributes to sustaining cellular energy production under metabolic stress conditions.

EP METABOLISM

In a previous study, 5 min after the intravenous injection of 3-13C pyruvate, approximately 20% of brain glutamate and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) became dose-dependently labeled but were not affected by a glial TCA cycle inhibitor, indicating that exogenous pyruvate is predominantly metabolized by neurons (Gonzalez et al., 2005). In contrast, studies on cultured cortical astrocytes have shown that [U-13C] glutamate-derived pyruvate re-enters the TCA cycle via conversion to acetyl-CoA, as reflected by the labeling of aspartate C-2 and C-3, lactate C-2, and glutamate C-4, demonstrating pyruvate recycling in astrocytes (Håberg et al., 1998). Consistently, a 31P-NMR study revealed that EP treatment significantly restores phosphocreatine levels after ischemia in brain slices. However, when the glial TCA cycle was inhibited, EP no longer exerted neuroprotective effects, suggesting that astrocytic function is essential for its activity (Tokumaru et al., 2009). Supporting its utility in brain imaging, hyperpolarized [1-13C]-EP enables a stable formulation, rapid brain uptake, and more effective metabolic imaging compared to hyperpolarized [1-13C]-pyruvate (Hurd et al., 2010). Moreover, after transient cerebral ischemia in rats, a single intravenous bolus of EP improved mitochondrial function and enhanced the synthesis of key energy metabolites, such as 13C-malate and 13C-isocitrate, although it did not reduce the infarct volume (Nygaard et al., 2020). In a rat model of controlled cortical impact injury, EP, sodium pyruvate, and glucose treatments enhanced lactate labeling and restored astrocyte-specific pyruvate carboxylase activity. However, sodium pyruvate robustly improved oxidative metabolism and neuronal recovery, suggesting the differential metabolic benefits of these fuels in brain injury (Shijo et al., 2017). Furthermore, EP provides the same level of ATP protection as the non-metabolizable antioxidant N-tert-butyl-alpha-phenylnitrone in H2O2-treated brain slices, implying that in this setting, the protective effect of EP is primarily antioxidant rather than metabolic (Liu et al., 2009). Collectively, these findings indicate that while pyruvate and EP contribute to neuroprotection and metabolic support, the effects of EP substantially depend on astrocytic metabolism and may involve direct antioxidant mechanisms, highlighting a dual mode of action that differs from that of sodium pyruvate.

PYRUVATE DERIVATIVES

Despite its potent biological properties, including antioxidant activity and metabolic support, pyruvic acid has substantial limitations in its clinical applications. The principal challenge lies in its pronounced chemical instability in aqueous environments, where it spontaneously undergoes aldol-like condensation reactions, yielding toxic byproducts such as parapyruvate and higher-order oligomers. These condensation reactions, which are concentration- and pH-dependent, become more pronounced under high concentrations and alkaline conditions, generating metabolites that interfere with the TCA cycle and disrupt normal cellular energy metabolism (Lopalco et al., 2019). Consequently, this intrinsic instability significantly limits the clinical utility of pyruvic acid, despite its promising biochemical potential (Fig. 2, Table 3).

Phosphoenolpyruvate

Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) is a high-energy intermediate in the terminal step of glycolysis, where it is converted to pyruvate by pyruvate kinase, generating ATP. Previous studies have demonstrated that PEP administration enhances the cellular energy status following ischemic events in cardiac and skeletal muscle tissues and reduces superoxide production in ischemic skeletal muscle (Hickey et al., 1995; Hultman et al., 1983). In hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury, PEP rapidly restores energy metabolism by promoting early ATP regeneration through its conversion to pyruvate in the liver (Saiki et al., 1997). Similarly, the pre-administration of PEP attenuates lung ischemia-reperfusion injury by preserving vascular integrity and enhancing metabolic recovery through increased pyruvate production (Oshima et al., 2015). The high-energy phosphate bond of PEP enables rapid ATP regeneration in vivo, forming the mechanistic basis for its strong cytoprotective effects under energy-depleted conditions. Furthermore, PEP protects against vesicular stomatitis virus infection in vitro and in vivo by inducing apoptosis-associated tyrosine kinase expression, thereby inhibiting viral replication and inflammatory responses (Zhao et al., 2023). Collectively, these findings highlight PEP as a potent bioenergetic and cytoprotective metabolite capable of mitigating ischemic and inflammatory injuries through rapid ATP regeneration and modulation of cellular stress responses.

3-Bromopyruvate

A pyruvate and lactate analog, 3-bromopyruvate (3-BP), functions as a small-molecule alkylating agent that disrupts tumor metabolism by concurrently inhibiting glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, thereby markedly reducing intracellular oxygen consumption, alleviating tumor hypoxia, and enhancing radiosensitization (Fan et al., 2019; He et al., 2023; Yadav et al., 2017). 3-BP administration prolongs the survival of nude mice with malignant pleural mesothelioma, particularly when combined with cisplatin (Zhang et al., 2009). GAPDH has been identified as the primary intracellular target of 3-BP, and its pyruvylation by 3-BP disrupts its enzymatic function and induces cancer cell death (Ganapathy-Kanniappan et al., 2009). Moreover, 3-BP, which is selectively taken up by cancer cells via monocarboxylate transporters, inactivates key metabolic enzymes and induces DNA damage, specifically double-strand breaks during the S phase in yeast and H2A.X phosphorylation in human cancer cells, likely owing to ROS generation at low concentrations, where most cells remain viable (Cal et al., 2020). In addition to glycolysis, 3-BP exerts inhibitory effects on glutaminolysis and the TCA cycle by targeting key enzymes, such as isocitrate dehydrogenase, alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, and succinate dehydrogenase, thereby disrupting glucose and glutamine metabolism in HepG2 cells and mitochondrial systems (Jardim-Messeder and Moreira-Pacheco, 2016). In breast cancer cells, 3-BP induces caspase-independent apoptosis by generating ROS and downregulating the anti-apoptotic protein Mcl-1 via the inhibition of the phosphatidylinositol 3 kinase (PI3K) /Akt signaling pathway (Liu et al., 2014). Furthermore, combination treatment with 3-BP and TRAIL synergistically enhances apoptosis in nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells by inducing endoplasmic reticulum stress and activating the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)/ C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP) pathway, leading to the upregulation of the death receptor TRAIL-R2 (Can et al., 2017). In addition to its anticancer activity, 3-BP attenuates kidney fibrosis in a unilateral ureteral obstruction mouse model and TGF-β-stimulated fibroblasts in vitro by suppressing aerobic glycolysis and inhibiting myofibroblast activation, potentially mediated by the interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinases (IRAK4)/MYC signaling pathway (Yu et al., 2021). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that 3-BP exerts multifaceted biological effects through the broad inhibition of metabolic and signaling pathways, highlighting its potential as a versatile therapeutic agent for cancer and fibrotic diseases.

Methyl pyruvate

Pyruvate ester derivatives, such as methylpyruvate (MP) and EP, have been developed to overcome the inherent chemical instability of pyruvate and improve its bioavailability for in vivo applications. These esters act as prodrugs that undergo enzymatic hydrolysis by endogenous esterases following administration, thereby regenerating the active metabolite, pyruvic acid. This esterification strategy is pivotal for harnessing pyruvate’s therapeutic potential. MP initiates β-cell membrane depolarization and insulin release through mechanisms that do not directly depend on mitochondrial ATP production, suggesting an extramitochondrial effect or the involvement of non-ATP-coupling factors (Lembert et al., 2001). In vivo, MP enhances the β-cell insulin secretory response to glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) in anesthetized rats and elevates plasma D-glucose concentrations; however, this glucose-raising effect indicates that MP is unsuitable for augmenting the hypoglycemic efficacy of GLP-1 in the treatment of type 2 diabetes (Valverde et al., 2001). Similarly, intravenous administration of MP or the dimethyl ester of L-glutamic acid potentiated and/or prolonged the insulinotropic action of GLP-1 in a type 2 diabetes animal model (neonatally streptozotocin-injected rats), suggesting that suitable non-glucidic nutrients optimize GLP-1 activity by bypassing β-cell glucose recognition defects (Cancelas et al., 2001). Mechanistically, MP stimulates insulin secretion in pancreatic β-cells primarily through the direct inhibition of ATP-sensitive K⁺ channels, rather than serving as a mitochondrial substrate to enhance ATP production (Düfer et al., 2002). In addition to its pancreatic and metabolic functions, MP exerts protective effects against pathological conditions. It rescues mitochondrial dysfunction, proteasome impairment, and pathological mislocalization of TDP-43 caused by the σ1RE102Q mutation associated with familial amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, indicating that ATP supplementation may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for this form of neurodegeneration (Tagashira et al., 2014). Moreover, MP selectively induces cytotoxicity in lung cancer cells (A549) while protecting normal lung fibroblasts (MRC-5) from irinotecan-induced death, highlighting its potential as an adjunctive chemotherapeutic agent that exploits cancer-specific metabolic vulnerabilities (Monchusi and Ntwasa, 2017). In endothelial cells, MP functions as a mitochondrial substrate for ATP synthesis, effectively increasing intracellular ATP levels and alleviating endoplasmic reticulum stress-induced apoptotic and inflammatory pathways (Zou et al., 2025). MP is a versatile bioenergetic modulator that exerts diverse physiological and therapeutic effects by restoring cellular ATP homeostasis, regulating insulin secretion, and protecting against metabolic and oxidative stress-related diseases. However, its glucose-elevating properties may limit its application in the treatment of diabetes.

Antithrombotic agents: Pyruvate ester

Antithrombotic agents play a crucial role in the prevention and management of thromboembolic disorders by mitigating the risk of severe cardiovascular events, such as myocardial and cerebral infarction, by inhibiting thrombus formation and promoting clot dissolution within the vascular system. Accordingly, therapeutic strategies should aim to resolve vascular occlusion and alleviate the secondary cellular damage caused by thrombotic ischemia, including energy metabolic failure, thereby supporting thrombolysis and the recovery of cellular energy metabolism. In this context, the novel multifunctional compound oxopropanoyloxy benzoic acid (OBA-09), a pyruvate/salicylic acid ester designed for slow in vivo release, shows robust multimodal neuroprotection against cerebral ischemic injury and neurological deficits across a wide therapeutic window. OBA-09 exerts direct antioxidant, anti-excitotoxic, and anti-Zn²⁺ toxic effects, whereas its hydrolyzed products, pyruvate and salicylic acid, further promote the recovery of cellular ATP and NAD levels (Kim et al., 2011). Moreover, OBA-09 exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects in post-ischemic and LPS-treated rat brains by suppressing microglial activation, neutrophil infiltration, and the induction of pro-inflammatory markers (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, iNOS, and COX-2) via NF-κB inhibition, demonstrating greater efficacy than the equivalent co-treatment with pyruvate and salicylic acid (Lee et al., 2013). Similarly, triflusal, an antiplatelet drug and an acetylsalicylic acid derivative incorporating a trifluoromethyl group, exhibits distinct pharmacological and pharmacokinetic properties. The novel compound 2-((2-oxopropanoyl)oxy)-4-(trifluoromethyl)benzoic acid (OPTBA), an ester of 2-hydroxy-4-trifluoromethyl benzoic acid (HTB, a triflusal metabolite) and pyruvate, demonstrates potent neuroprotective effects against cerebral ischemic injury and neurological deficits. OPTBA is more effective than the HTB/pyruvate combination, primarily by suppressing microglial activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine induction, while providing multimodal neuroprotection both directly and indirectly through its slowly released hydrolysis products (HTB and pyruvate) (Kim et al., 2016b). Multifunctional pyruvate-based esters, such as OBA-09 and OPTBA, have demonstrated superior neuroprotective effects against cerebral ischemia through antioxidative stress, anti-excitotoxicity, anti-inflammatory, and energy replenishment effects, representing a promising therapeutic strategy for thromboembolic and ischemic brain disorders.

EP isosteres

The neuroprotective effects of four EP isosteres, N-ethyl-2-oxopropanamide, N,N-diethyl-2-oxopropanamide (DEOPA), S-ethyl-2-oxopropanethioate, and hexane-2,3-dione, have been previously demonstrated (Lee et al., 2017). Among them, DEOPA shows the most potent activity in the MCAO animal model, reducing cerebral infarct volume and improving motor function loss compared with EP. Moreover, DEOPA exhibits superior anti-inflammatory effects by more effectively inhibiting NF-κB activity, preventing IκB-α degradation, and suppressing p65 DNA binding, thereby leading to a marked reduction in nitrite, cytokine, and chemokine production in microglia and decreased neutrophil infiltration in the postischemic brain. Lee et al. further developed N,N-diisopropyl-2-oxopropanamide (DIPOPA), a novel DEOPA derivative that demonstrated greater neuroprotective efficacy than EP and DEOPA in cerebral ischemia models by strongly suppressing microglial activation and neutrophil infiltration through NF-κB-mediated inhibition of adhesion molecule expression, highlighting its potential as a promising therapeutic agent for ischemic and inflammation-related brain injuries (Lee et al., 2019).

TOXICOLOGICAL PROFILES AND CLINICAL LIMITATIONS OF PYRUVATE DERIVATIVES

EP is generally regarded as safe and generally exhibits low toxicity at clinically relevant doses; however, several studies have noted certain side effects and limitations in its clinical application. In particular, EP has demonstrated safety in human volunteers and high-risk cardiac surgery patients; however, it failed to significantly improve clinical outcomes in a Phase II multicenter trial (Bennett-Guerrero et al., 2009). In addition, the clinical translation of EP remains complicated by species-specific differences in metabolism and esterase activity, which may significantly influence its pharmacokinetics and therapeutic efficacy in humans (Zwolak, 2025). In a mouse model, the therapeutic effect of EP varied significantly depending on the timing of administration, with late administration of EP worsening histopathological liver damage in acetaminophen-induced liver injury (Yang et al., 2012). Other pyruvate derivatives, such as MP and 3-BP, have demonstrated potent therapeutic potential, particularly in cancer treatment; however, their mechanisms of action and safety profiles differ substantially. MP primarily functions as a radiosensitizer by enhancing mitochondrial metabolism and promoting excessive ROS generation, thereby selectively inducing apoptosis in tumor cells while sparing normal cells that possess more robust antioxidant defenses (Nishida et al., 2014). In contrast, 3-BP is a highly potent antiglycolytic agent that irreversibly inhibits key metabolic enzymes, such as hexokinase II, resulting in rapid ATP depletion and tumor cell death. However, this non-selective disruption of cellular energy metabolism can also affect normal tissues, leading to serious side effects, including hepatotoxicity and local venous irritation (El Sayed et al., 2014; Pan et al., 2016). DEOPA and DIPOPA have shown promising neuroprotective effects; however, their safety profiles warrant careful consideration. Although their strong inhibitory effects on microglial activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production (e.g., TNF-α and IL-1β) are beneficial during acute neuroinflammatory conditions (Lee et al., 2017, 2019), excessive or prolonged immunosuppression may impair essential immune surveillance and tissue repair mechanisms. Moreover, the long-term effects of metabolites on various organ functions are not yet fully understood and require further investigation.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS AND TRANSLATIONAL PERSPECTIVES

The failure of EP in a Phase II clinical trial involving cardiac surgery patients (Bennett Guerrero et al., 2009) should not be interpreted as evidence of a lack of efficacy in CNS diseases. First, the clinical population in that trial does not adequately represent the neuroinflammatory conditions in which EP’s mechanisms of action, such as HMGB1 inhibition, Nrf2 activation, and blood brain barrier stabilization, are most relevant. In addition, the timing of administration was not optimized to reflect the temporal dynamics of secondary brain injury, which may have limited its therapeutic impact. Moreover, improving the efficacy of EP and its analogs will likely require the development of advanced drug delivery systems. At the same time, potent analogs such as DIPOPA and DEOPA require careful long term safety evaluation due to the risk of prolonged immunosuppression. Notably, preclinical studies have demonstrated enhanced therapeutic effects when EP is used in combination with other agents, such as aspirin in ischemic injury models and IGF-I in neonatal ischemic injury, highlighting the potential of combination strategies that enhance robust neuroprotection of EP. Taken together, these findings suggest that the clinical applicability of EP is not limited but rather evolving in new directions. Advances in species specific pharmacokinetic modeling, precision drug delivery, and biomarker driven clinical trial design provide a strong foundation for translating the robust neuroprotective effects observed in preclinical studies into clinical success.

CONCLUSION

EP, a simple aliphatic ester of pyruvate, is a highly promising multifunctional neuroprotective agent for a broad range of CNS diseases. Given the complex and multifactorial nature of neurological diseases, the ability of EP to influence diverse pathological mechanisms including inflammation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and energy metabolism provides a significant therapeutic advantage over single-target drugs by effectively preventing the vicious cycle of pathogenesis (Fig. 3). Consistently, numerous preclinical studies have shown that EP reduces neuronal damage, stabilizes the blood–brain barrier, and promotes functional recovery in stroke, TBI, SCI, MS, and Parkinson’s disease models. The therapeutic effects of EP are mediated through multiple molecular pathways (Fig. 1). EP suppresses neuroinflammation by inhibiting the release of HMGB1 through calcium chelation and the regulation of NF-κB signaling. It strengthens antioxidant defenses by scavenging ROS, promoting the translocation of Nrf2 into the nucleus, and supporting cellular energy balance by restoring ATP and NAD levels under metabolic stress. Although these results are promising, additional studies are required to clarify the pharmacokinetic properties of EP, determine its optimal dosage, and confirm its long-term safety in human subjects. Comparative studies of EP-related compounds, such as DIPOPA and DEOPA, and pyruvate-based derivatives, including OBA-09 and OPTBA, suggest that structural modifications may improve their stability and therapeutic effects. Although studies on EP in Alzheimer’s and Huntington’s diseases are limited, the multiple mechanisms underlying these diseases, including oxidative stress, chronic neuroinflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction, overlap with those of EP. Considering these pathological features, EP and its derivatives may further expand the clinical potential of multi-mechanism neuroprotective agents against Alzheimer’s and Huntington’s diseases. Thus, EP represents a promising foundation for the development of multifunctional neuroprotective drugs that regulate inflammation, oxidative stress, and energy metabolism, making it a promising candidate for future clinical translation in treating complex CNS diseases.
