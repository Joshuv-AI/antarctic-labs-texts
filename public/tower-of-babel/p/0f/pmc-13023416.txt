Neurodegeneration and Homelessness: A Bidirectional Relationship Shaping Health and Vulnerability
Authors: Anna C. F. Stouffer, Sandhya L. Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neurodegeneration and homelessness are deeply interconnected. Many structural, environmental, and interpersonal conditions experienced during homelessness—for example, inconsistent healthcare, forced displacement, chronic stress, and trauma—can contribute to accelerated cognitive decline through multiple pathways. Related neurocognitive impairment can also increase vulnerability to homelessness. Deficits in memory, executive functioning, attention, and impulse control can make it difficult to maintain employment, manage finances, navigate social services, and sustain supportive relationships. Together, these pathways create a reinforcing pattern: neurodegeneration can increase risk of losing housing, and homelessness can intensify the biologic and psychosocial stressors that drive neurodegeneration. Despite growing evidence suggesting a substantial burden of neurodegenerative conditions among people experiencing homelessness, these conditions are frequently underrecognized and underdiagnosed. Fragmented healthcare and symptom overlap with psychiatric conditions or substance use can hinder timely detection. Missed or delayed diagnosis has significant individual and systemic impacts. As neurodegeneration progresses without recognition or treatment, individuals may experience preventable functional decline, difficulty maintaining relationships, and repeated, often avoidable encounters with hospitals, shelters, and carceral settings. Without diagnosis, individuals may also miss opportunities to access disability benefits, supportive housing options, and personalized care planning that could significantly improve health and quality of life. Emerging models grounded in trauma-informed, harm reduction-oriented approaches—such as mobile outreach, shelter-based clinics, and Housing First programs—offer promising strategies to improve recognition of neurocognitive changes and support long-term neurologic health. Understanding and addressing the bidirectional relationship between neurodegeneration and homelessness is essential for reducing suffering, promoting stability, and delivering care that is both effective and dignity-affirming.

Introduction / Epidemiology

Homelessness and neurodegeneration are both growing public health concerns in the United States, and increasing evidence suggests a bidirectional relationship between homelessness and neurodegeneration [1]. On one surveyed night in January 2024, there were more than 770 000 individuals experiencing homelessness across the country [2]. Homelessness emerges from an intricate convergence of structural determinants and individual-level vulnerabilities. Severe housing unaffordability, the enduring effects of structural racism, untreated psychiatric conditions, prior criminal legal system involvement, interpersonal violence, substance use disorders, and forced displacement are common contributing factors. These individual experiences and structural pressures shape overall health trajectories, with people experiencing homelessness (PEH) dying on average two to three decades earlier than the general population in the US [3]. The burden of neurodegenerative disease in this population is substantial, with an estimated 51% higher risk of neurodegenerative disorders compared to stably housed individuals [4]. The link between dementia and homelessness has been recognized for decades. Early research published in 1992 found dementia to be significantly associated with homelessness, low income, living alone, and unstable residential history among older adults in an urban setting [5]. Recent evidence suggests that dementia in particular may be substantially more common among PEH. A 2025 meta-analysis estimated the prevalence of dementia among PEH to be approximately 6.8%, more than double the prevalence observed in population-based samples of similarly aged housed adults, where estimates are typically below 3% [6].

The stressors and exposures associated with homelessness may contribute to the onset or accelerated progression of neurocognitive decline. Conversely, neurodegenerative conditions can increase vulnerability to housing instability. The experience of homelessness further complicates the timely diagnosis and effective management of neurodegenerative disorders (Figure 1).

Methodology

This article is a narrative review intended to synthesize emerging evidence at the intersection of homelessness and neurodegeneration. Given the heterogeneity of study designs and the evolving nature of the literature, a systematic review methodology was not undertaken. Literature was identified through iterative searches of PubMed/MEDLINE using combinations of search terms including homelessness, housing instability, neurodegeneration, cognitive impairment, traumatic brain injury, substance use, chronic stress, and social determinants of health. No formal date restrictions were applied; however, priority was placed on literature published within the past 10 years, where available. Additional relevant sources were identified through citation tracking and review of reference lists. Studies were included if they addressed mechanisms, epidemiology, risk factors, or clinical implications of neurodegenerative processes among individuals experiencing homelessness or housing instability. Due to the limited and heterogeneous nature of the available evidence, formal quality appraisal and risk-of-bias assessment were not performed. This review aims to synthesize emerging evidence and highlight gaps in the evidence base rather than to offer a comprehensive or quantitative assessment of all available studies. Findings should therefore be interpreted in the context of an emerging and developing field.

Risk Factors for Neurodegeneration Among PEH

Individuals with symptoms of neurodegeneration like cognitive impairment, executive dysfunction, and deficits in attention or impulse control have an increased risk of becoming homeless [7-9]. The symptoms of neurodegeneration can impair one’s ability to maintain employment, manage financial and material resources, navigate complex systems like Social Security or housing services, and sustain supportive interpersonal relationships [9-13]. They may also increase vulnerability to substance use disorders, further compounding housing instability [14]. Once homelessness occurs, people are exposed to a constellation of biological, environmental, and structural risk factors that may accelerate or exacerbate neurodegenerative processes (Table 1).

Poor Healthcare Access

Homelessness substantially hinders the management of existing chronic illness and heightens vulnerability to new medical problems, resulting in significant morbidity and mortality. For many PEH, participation in traditional primary care, substance use treatment, and specialty care is constrained by a range of barriers such as mistrust of institutions, competing demands on limited resources, transportation challenges, anticipated or experienced stigma, and difficulty tolerating crowded clinical settings or long waits due to a history of trauma [15,16]. In a recent survey of more than 3200 PEH in California, over one-third reported no ambulatory care visit in the preceding year, and nearly one-quarter reported unmet medication needs despite the majority having some form of health insurance [17]. The absence of consistent care places PEH at heightened risk for uncontrolled medical conditions that, when left untreated, are associated with accelerated neurocognitive decline.

Vascular dysfunction is a well-established contributor to dementia and other neurodegenerative processes [18]. PEH face heightened risk for vascular dysfunction due to the challenges inherent to managing chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and tobacco use; the fragmentation or loss of continuity in medical care; limited access to nutritious food; and the difficulty of maintaining consistent medication routines in the context of housing instability [19].

Homelessness is also associated with high rates of hepatitis C virus (HCV) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) [20]. Chronic HCV infection contributes to neurodegeneration through multiple mechanisms, including chronic systemic and cerebral inflammation, autoimmune-mediated neuronal injury, metabolic derangements, and alterations in neurotransmitter circuits, which collectively lead to cognitive impairment, peripheral neuropathy, and increased risk of cerebrovascular events such as stroke [21]. HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder (HAND) is a process by which HIV contributes to neurodegeneration through chronic neuroinflammation, glial activation, and the release of viral proteins, which together induce synaptodendritic damage and cognitive impairment [22]. The intersection of HIV and homelessness compounds this risk further. In a public hospital psychiatric cohort, patients living with HIV had higher rates of homelessness and dementia compared with patients without HIV [23].

Social Isolation and Loneliness

PEH often experience profound loneliness and social isolation, with limited opportunities for meaningful connection and frequent negative or stigmatizing interactions. Loneliness has been noted to accelerate cognitive decline [24]. Chronic social isolation activates neurobiological stress responses like neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and accumulation of tau and amyloid proteins, all of which accelerate cognitive decline and neurodegeneration [25].

Chronic Stress and Trauma

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs)—such as abuse, neglect, or household dysfunction—are strongly linked to chronic stress across an individual’s lifespan. PEH have disproportionately high exposure: over 90% have experienced at least one ACE, and more than half (54%) have experienced four or more [26]. Once homeless, individuals face compounding ongoing stressors including harsh weather, interpersonal and systemic violence, and pervasive social stigmatization requiring constant vigilance just to maintain safety.

These consistent stressors persistently activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to prolonged elevation of cortisol and other glucocorticoids. Over time, this drives hippocampal atrophy, impaired prefrontal cortex functioning, and dysregulated limbic circuits [27]. Chronic stress also promotes neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired neurogenesis, collectively accelerating neurodegenerative processes [27,28].

Untreated Psychiatric Conditions

The prevalence of psychiatric conditions such as major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is higher among PEH compared to the general population [29]. Untreated psychiatric conditions share common neurobiological phenomena—volumetric reductions in the hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex driven by chronic stress, HPA-axis dysfunction, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and neurotransmitter disturbances—and these collectively accelerate cognitive decline and structural brain deterioration [30-32].

Sleep Disruptions and Circadian Dysregulation

Sleep disruptions and circadian dysregulation are common among PEH due to factors such as lack of consistent place to sleep, safety concerns, history of traumatic brain injury (TBI), and substance use. These irregular sleep patterns impair normal restorative processes in the brain like glymphatic clearance, which is critical for removing neurotoxic waste products such as tau protein and amyloid-beta [33,34]. Disrupted circadian rhythm also causes immune dysregulation and oxidative stress, which may further contribute to neurodegenerative changes [35].

Nutritional Deficiencies

PEH often lack reliable access to nutrition-dense, high-quality food, and nutritional deficiencies are increasingly recognized as contributors to cognitive decline and neurodegeneration. Low vitamin B12 levels are associated with Alzheimer’s disease, vascular dementia, and Parkinson’s disease, and even mild B12 deficiency can impair memory and executive functioning [36]. High plasma folate concentrations may further exacerbate the cognitive consequences of B12 deficiency [37]. In the US, folic acid fortification of grain products is mandated; individuals relying heavily on these foods—common in shelter or outreach settings—may have elevated folate levels despite limited nutritional diversity.

Substance Use

The relationship between homelessness and substance use is complex, as substance use may precede and contribute to housing loss, while the experience of homelessness can initiate, intensify, or perpetuate substance use [38]. Substance use among people experiencing homelessness can function as a coping response to trauma and environmental stressors and is further reinforced by social dynamics and the practical day-to-day demands of surviving in unstable and uncomfortable environments [39,40].

Prolonged substance use is associated with widespread structural and functional brain alterations. Alcohol use is a well-established cause of Korsakoff syndrome and impairs visuospatial processing, executive functioning, and memory [41,42]. Stimulants including methamphetamine and cocaine exert neurotoxic effects on dopaminergic pathways, and chronic opioid exposure and recurrent overdoses can cause hypoxic brain injury and brain matter disruption [43-47]. Across substances, common mechanisms include tau hyperphosphorylation, microglial activation, and axonal injury—alterations which may accelerate neurodegenerative processes [48].

Polysubstance use is highly prevalent among PEH and may occur intentionally or unintentionally due to adulterants and impurities in the unregulated drug supply [49]. Neurotoxic effects may be amplified through synergistic mechanisms such as oxidative stress, hypoxia, mitochondrial dysfunction, altered neurotransmitter signaling, and neurovascular injury [50]. The cumulative neurobiological impact of polysubstance use remains insufficiently characterized.

Traumatic Brain Injury

TBI is a notable contributor to neurodegeneration among PEH. Rates of TBI in this population are high, with an estimated 50% of PEH having sustained a TBI in their lifetime [51]. Structurally, TBIs are linked to diffuse axonal injury, cortical atrophy, reduced brain volume, and white matter disruption, which can accelerate cognitive decline and elevate the risk of developing neurodegenerative conditions [52]. There is an established connection between TBI with subsequent vascular dementia and neurodegenerative conditions like Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and cognitive impairment [53,54]. A history of TBI has been associated with poorer self-reported physical and mental health, higher suicidality, memory impairment, and increased involvement in the health system and criminal-legal systems [51].

Barriers to Diagnosis

Diagnosing neurodegeneration is challenging, as symptoms that might prompt concern for neurodegeneration—such as difficulties with memory, planning, judgment, or emotional regulation—are frequently attributed to co-occurring substance use or symptomatic mental health conditions instead. Additionally, standard screening instruments may be insufficiently sensitive or poorly calibrated for this population. In one study of men experiencing homelessness, an abbreviated neuropsychological test battery identified possible cognitive impairment in 80% of participants, whereas the commonly used Mini-Mental State Examination detected impairment in only 30% of the same sample [55]. In a separate study of adults aged 55 and older residing in homeless shelters, the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) identified possible dementia or mild cognitive impairment in 92.9% of participants using the conventional cutoff score of 26, and in 72.3% when a more conservative threshold of 23 was applied [56]. These findings raise concern that routine cognitive screening tools may underestimate impairment in this population and highlight uncertainty regarding appropriate screening thresholds in socially and medically complex settings.

Additionally, as noted above, PEH often lack sustained, longitudinal contact with the healthcare system, leaving clinicians with fewer chances to observe gradual changes in cognition over time, an important element in diagnosing neurodegeneration [17]. As a result, neurodegenerative conditions are frequently missed, despite many PEH exhibiting signs [9].

Implications / Consequences of Under-Diagnosed and Under-Treated Neurodegenerative Conditions Among PEH

When neurodegenerative conditions proceed undiagnosed in PEH, cognitive decline progresses without intervention and contributes to system-level strain as people cycle repeatedly through shelter, hospital, and the carceral systems that are not equipped to support them. Receiving an accurate diagnosis can change the trajectory—prompting linkage to disability benefits, supportive housing eligibility, tailored case management, and care plans that can accommodate cognitive limitations [57]. It also helps providers, friends, and family members interpret behavior through a clinical lens rather than viewing them as intentional or willful, opening pathways to more appropriate and humane care.

Neurodegeneration as a Driver of Homelessness

Neurodegeneration erodes the cognitive and behavioral capacities needed for independent daily functioning. Declines in memory, attention, judgment, and impulse control impair employment, financial management, and navigation of public systems. Changes in affect and behavior can strain relationships and corrode social support. Together, these difficulties compound, increasing vulnerability to economic instability and homelessness [1]. Evidence for this bidirectional relationship comes primarily from studies of veteran populations. Among veterans, housing instability has been associated with significantly elevated risk of Alzheimer’s disease and related dementia diagnosis, and conversely, veterans with dementia diagnoses have been shown to have higher subsequent rates of homelessness [58]. Additionally, veterans experiencing homelessness admitted to nursing home care have been found to be significantly younger and more likely to carry a dementia diagnosis than their stably housed counterparts, suggesting earlier disease onset in the context of housing instability [59]. Whether these findings generalize to broader populations experiencing homelessness remains an important area for future research, as veterans represent a distinct subpopulation with unique risk profiles, including high rates of TBI and combat-related trauma.

Innovative Models of Care

Tailored models of care—such as in drop-in centers, shelter-based clinics, and mobile outreach programs—have been shown to improve access to treatment and engagement with services, and may support modifiable risk factors like smoking cessation and improve management of hypertension, diabetes, addiction, and mental illness [50]. Expanding training in homeless healthcare, harm reduction, and trauma-informed approaches may further enhance care quality. Programming that provides psychoeducation to patients, their support networks, and clinical teams is an essential element of comprehensive care [51]. Programs that intentionally reduce loneliness—such as peer-support groups, structured social activities, or community-based outreach—can strengthen connectedness, improve psychological wellbeing, and buffer the functional impact of neurodegenerative conditions [52].

Access to stable housing is critical for both preventing and treating neurodegenerative conditions. Low-barrier shelters offer a potential solution by providing support aligned with individual goals, including access to mental health care, substance use services, and employment or income supports [53]. Housing First programs, which provide immediate access to permanent housing without preconditions, have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing hospitalizations and improving overall health outcomes in this population [54]. Supportive housing expands on this model by combining stable housing with individualized services designed to help residents maintain that housing.

Conclusions and Outlook

Neurodegeneration and homelessness are deeply interwoven processes, each accelerating the other and complicating the diagnosis, treatment, and long-term stability for PEH. Underdiagnosis fuels preventable suffering and strains systems that are neither designed nor resourced to manage neurodegenerative conditions. Current research remains limited by small samples, lack of disaggregation by shelter status, and the absence of longitudinal studies—gaps that constrain the conclusions that can be drawn and underscore the need for more rigorous, community-engaged research.

Meaningful progress will require structural investment in trauma-informed and harm-reduction-aligned care models, expanding screening in low-threshold settings, and sustained longitudinal relationships between clinicians and PEH—recognizing that continuity of care is, in itself, a clinical intervention for this population. Developing compassionate, evidence-informed responses that recognize the bidirectional relationship between neurodegeneration and homelessness may allow for earlier intervention, reduced suffering, and more humane and effective pathways to stability and health for individuals experiencing homelessness.
