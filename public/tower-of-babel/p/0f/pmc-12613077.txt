The competitive relationship and regional differences in water use for energy and food production in China
Authors: Shuoguo Yang, Jianqin Ma, Qing Wu, Wen Li, Bifeng Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryChina’s dual carbon goals and increasing water stress underscore the importance of understanding energy-food water competition. Using a water footprint framework, we quantified water use between energy and food production across 31 provinces in China from 2005 to 2020, analyzing spatial patterns, competition intensity, and inequality. Energy water use rose rapidly, particularly from thermal and hydropower, while food water use stayed stable. Competition was most severe in northern and northwestern regions, and inequality in water use widened, reflecting mismatches between resource availability and industrial layout. These findings provide a basis for targeted policies, water right reform, and interregional cooperation to enhance sustainability.Subject areas: energy resources, agricultural resource economics, sustainability aspects of food production

Introduction

Water, energy, and food are fundamental strategic resources for human survival and societal development, serving as essential guarantees for achieving sustainable socio-economic-environmental development.1,2 China’s water use is characterized by a clear pattern of “agriculture-dominated, followed by industry,” with rapidly growing water demand from the energy sector.3 Agricultural water use accounts for more than 60% of the nation’s total, and, from 2000 to 2019, the food blue water footprint decreased by 2.3%, while the energy blue water footprint more than doubled.4 With rapid population growth and socio-economic advancement, the pressure on the supply of water, energy, and food is intensifying. By 2030, global demand for water, energy, and food is projected to increase by approximately 40%, 50%, and 35%, respectively.5 Addressing water shortages while meeting rising energy and food demands has become a global challenge.

At the 2011 Bonn Conference titled “The Water-Energy-Food Security Nexus: Solutions for a Green Economy,” the interrelationship among these three resources was formally conceptualized as a nexus.6 In 2015, the United Nations released 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) as the core framework of the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development. Among them, SDG 2, SDG 6, and SDG 7 specifically highlight the critical role of food, water, and energy in enabling countries to achieve sustainable development in the future.7 Water, energy, and food are interdependent and competitive. For instance, water is essential for both energy production and agriculture; energy is needed to supply water and grow food; and food production depends on both water and energy. A change in one can affect the others, and ignoring these links may lead to serious system-wide consequences.8,9 As pressures on these resources grow, understanding their interactions is vital to improve efficiency, reduce risks, and support regional sustainable development.10

To date, both domestic and international scholars have conducted extensive research on the Water-Energy-Food Nexus (WEF-Nexus) from various perspectives, including security,11,12,13,14 risk,15,16 optimization,17,18 pressure,19,20 and coupling-coordination relationships.21,22,23 In terms of research scale, existing studies span from specific industries to the city, national, and even global levels.24,25,26 Methodologically, research on the WEF-Nexus has primarily employed approaches such as surveys and statistical analysis,27 input-output model,28,29 econometric model,30 ecological network analysis,31,32 life cycle assessment,33,34 and system dynamics model.35 For example, Smajal et al.27 used the Delphi method based on expert survey data to identify intersectoral interactions and constructed a dynamic assessment framework for the WEF-Nexus in the Mekong River Basin. Ozturk,30 using multiple regression analysis, found that widening gaps in water and energy supply and demand pose significant challenges to food security in BRICS countries. Chen et al.31 applied input-output model to examine water and energy consumption across sectors in Beijing. Ravar et al.35 employed a system dynamics model to assess water, food, and energy security and their interlinkages in Iran’s Gavkhuni Basin.

Moreover, many researchers have incorporated external factors into the water-energy-food system. For example, Saray et al.5 developed an agricultural water-energy-food optimization model that accounts for carbon emissions, while Melo et al.36 integrated forest ecosystems into the WEF-Nexus, emphasizing that forest landscape restoration is a promising strategy for improving water, energy, and food security. However, research on regional differences and the competitive relationship in water use between energy and food production remains limited. The competition for water between these two sectors is a key component of the WEF-Nexus. If not effectively addressed, it may lead to conflicts between the agricultural and energy sectors and pose threats to regional energy and food security.37,38

Given the uneven distribution of water resources in China, and the fact that both energy and food production are water-intensive industries, there exists significant competition for water resources across regions. Analyzing these regional differences can help optimize water allocation, balance energy and food security, promote coordinated regional economic development, and reduce regional conflicts arising from water competition. In contrast to existing studies that primarily focus on the WEF-Nexus from perspectives such as security, optimization, or system coordination, this study emphasizes the competition for water use between energy and food production. Using a water footprint-based competition index and inequality metrics, it systematically evaluates the spatiotemporal evolution, regional disparities, and intensifying resource conflicts in energy and food water use in China from 2005 to 2020. By clarifying the competitive patterns and inequality trends, this study aims to improve resource allocation efficiency and support sustainable water governance by providing a scientific basis for cross-sectoral coordination. It also contributes to addressing water resource fluctuations caused by climate change and enhancing ecological and environmental protection.

Results

Spatiotemporal evolution characteristics of water use for China’s energy and food production

From 2005 to 2020, the total water footprint of fossil energy increased from 93.77 to 155.01 × 108 m3, with an average annual growth of 4.4%, showing a continuous upward trend, as shown in Figure 1A. The gray water footprint of fossil energy is predominantly driven by coal, which accounts for over 80%. In 2005, the gray water footprint for coal was 52.92 × 108 m3, increasing to 93.87 × 108 m3 in 2020, showing significant growth. The blue water footprint of oil accounted for the highest proportion, increasing from 23.82 × 108 m3 in 2005 to 25.58 × 108 m3 in 2020. The blue water footprint of natural gas grew the fastest, from 2.23 × 108 m3 in 2005 to 8.52 × 108 m3 in 2020, reflecting the increasing freshwater demand for natural gas extraction.

The water footprint of electricity grew from 143.50 × 108 m3 in 2005 to 433.92 × 108 m3 in 2020, with an average annual growth of 7.8%, significantly higher than that of fossil energy, as shown in Figure 1B. The gray water footprint of thermal power accounted for approximately 15%–20% (69.59 × 108 m3 in 2020), and the blue water footprint of thermal power accounted for about 25%–30% (110.16 × 108 m3 in 2020), indicating the dual pressure on freshwater consumption and pollution from thermal power. The blue water footprint of hydropower increased from 74.47 × 108 m3 in 2005 to 254.18 × 108 m3 in 2020, with an average annual growth of 8.3%, reflecting the growing dependence of hydropower development on water resources.

The blue water footprint showed overall fluctuating growth, as shown in Figure 1C. It increased from 3,270.98 × 108 m3 in 2005 to 3,353.75 × 108 m3 in 2020, with an overall increase of 2.5%. However, the average annual growth rate was only 0.15%. It peaked at 3,593.24 × 108 m3 in 2013 and gradually declined after 2017, indicating improvements in water resource utilization efficiency.

From 2005 to 2020, the total energy-food water footprint in most provinces generally increased, but the growth rates varied significantly, as shown in Figures 2, 3, 4, and 5. For example, in Inner Mongolia, the total increased from 11.45 to 48.45 × 108 m3, with an average annual growth rate of 10.2%, primarily driven by coal mining and thermal power expansion. In Sichuan, the total increased from 16.59 to 70.97 × 108 m3, with an average annual growth rate of 10.5%. The blue water footprint of hydropower accounted for over 90% (66.42 × 108 m3 in 2020), reflecting the dominant hydropower model in southwestern China. In Beijing, the total water footprint increased from 1.07 to 2.00 × 108 m3, showing a moderate growth rate, while the coal water footprint dropped by 90%, reflecting a transition in the energy structure.

Spatiotemporal evolution of the water use competition between energy and food production in China

The competition between energy and food production for water resources in China exhibits pronounced spatial differences and dynamic temporal evolution. Overall, from 2005 to 2020, China’s competition index showed an upward trend (Figure 6), indicating that the competition for water between energy and food production has become increasingly intense. Among regions, North China experienced the most severe competition, followed by Northwest and Northeast China, while Southwest and South China showed relatively weak competition.

Energy-food production water competition index for China and its six regions

In terms of spatial patterns, at the regional level, North China consistently exhibited the highest competition index throughout the study period, peaking at 0.427 in 2007. This can be attributed to the region’s dual role as both a major energy base and a key grain production area, compounded by its limited water resource availability. Northwest China also showed a rising trend, with the index increasing from 0.068 in 2005 to 0.114 in 2020, reflecting the region’s growing water reliance for energy development. In Northeast China, a sharp rise in the competition index was observed after 2007, particularly during 2014–2017, when it remained between 0.12 and 0.16. This suggests that, as a traditional grain-producing area, the region has experienced mounting water stress in the context of expanding energy activities. In contrast, East China, South China, and Southwest China maintained lower competition indices, generally ranging between 0.02 and 0.1, indicating relatively sufficient water resources and less intense competition between energy and food production.

At the provincial level, several hotspots of high competition emerged. Notably, provinces such as Ningxia and Shanxi in Northwest and North China showed pronounced increases in their competition indices, driven by intensive coal and thermal power expansion amid constrained water availability. Similarly, Heilongjiang and Jilin in Northeast China—key grain-producing provinces—also recorded elevated competition indices during periods of concurrent energy development. In contrast, provinces in the Yangtze River Delta, Pearl River Delta, and most parts of Southwest China maintained low and stable indices, underscoring the buffering role of their comparatively abundant water endowments in mitigating intersectoral water use tensions. Qinghai, Tibet, and Hainan maintained competition index values consistently below 0.05, suggesting a low level of resource competition due to either sparse population or limited industrial and agricultural intensity.

From a temporal perspective, China’s energy-food water competition index showed a phased decline after 2011, dropping from 0.082 in 2011 to 0.075 in 2020. This suggests that improved water resource management measures have to some extent alleviated the competitive pressure between energy and food production. However, regional variations remain evident (Figure 7). Some provinces continue to exhibit high competition indices, such as Ningxia, Tianjin, Shanxi, and Hebei, which have long maintained elevated levels. Notably, Ningxia reached a peak competition index of 2.38 in 2014, indicating a strong dependence of its energy production on water resources. From 2010 to 2014, Ningxia’s energy water footprint rose rapidly from 3.99 × 108 m3 to 6.59 × 108 m3, while the food water footprint remained relatively stable around 26–33 × 108 m3. This led to a growing imbalance between energy and food sectors in water use. The sharp increase in energy water use is closely related to Ningxia’s accelerated development of coal power and coal chemical industries during the “Twelfth Five-Year Plan” period. As a key national energy base, large-scale projects were launched, significantly raising water demand. Under limited water availability, this expansion intensified competition between energy and food production for natural water resources. In addition, provinces such as Beijing, Shandong, and Henan experienced fluctuations in their competition indices but still maintained relatively high overall levels. In contrast, regions such as Tibet, Xinjiang, and Hainan exhibited lower competition indices, reflecting relatively minor conflicts between energy and food production in terms of water use.

Spatiotemporal evolution of regional differences in energy-food production water use in China

In terms of spatial patterns, Figure 8 shows that the between-region component of inequality (CBRI) increased significantly over time, rising from 20.29% in 2005 to 42.77% in 2020. This indicates that disparities in water use across China’s major regions have intensified, particularly since 2010. The growing share of between-region inequality suggests increasing divergence in resource allocation and development patterns among different regions. Moreover, substantial within-region disparities also persist (Figure 9). The Eastern region consistently exhibited the highest within-region inequality, with its index rising from 0.908 in 2017 to 0.928 in 2020, reflecting worsening disparities in water use among its provinces. Similarly, North China saw a marked increase in internal inequality, with the index growing from 0.44 in 2005 to 0.58 in 2020, likely driven by intensified water competition in provinces facing both energy development and agricultural demands. In the Northwest region, the inequality index showed a fluctuating upward trend, increasing from 0.599 in 2017 to 0.615 in 2020, indicating emerging internal imbalances. These spatial trends underscore the uneven burden of water resource pressures both across and within China’s major regions.

In terms of temporal dynamics, from 2005 to 2020, the total inequality index of water footprint intensity at the national scale rose steadily from 0.64 to 0.95, reflecting a progressive widening of disparities in water use related to energy and food production. The increase became particularly steep after 2017, with the index surging from 0.80 in 2016 to 1.00 in 2019, suggesting an accelerated divergence in water use intensity among provinces during this later period. Meanwhile, the within-region inequality index also exhibited a modest rise, increasing from 0.51 to 0.55 over the study period. Although this growth was less dramatic than the increase in between-region disparities, it still reflects persistent and expanding water use imbalances within individual regions.

Discussion

Regional driving mechanisms of energy-food water use competition

This study confirms that the core conflict of energy-food water use competition is concentrated in regions where water scarcity overlaps with high production demand, but the specific drivers vary by region. In North China (e.g., Shanxi and Hebei), the main pressure comes from the spatial overlap of coal production and wheat cultivation—coal-related gray water accounts for over 80% of the regional energy water footprint, while agricultural blue water demand remains high (3,270 × 108 m3 annually), intensifying groundwater overexploitation. In Northwest China (e.g., Xinjiang), the dual expansion of oasis agriculture and fossil energy development (oil, gas, and thermal power) has increasingly encroached on ecological water, leading to a sustained rise in the competition index. In Southwest China (e.g., Sichuan and Yunnan), although hydropower development has reduced carbon emissions, large-scale dam construction has disrupted natural runoff and suppressed downstream irrigation and ecological flows, revealing the contradiction of “low carbon, high water.” This finding aligns with existing studies48,49 that suggest energy transition intensifies localized water stress, while further quantifying regional differences in competition intensity, thereby offering a basis for differentiated regulation.

Policy and technological drivers behind the expansion of regional disparities

The widening of regional water footprint disparities (with the total inequality index increasing by 48%) is primarily driven by differences in energy production, especially in provinces like Inner Mongolia and Xinjiang, where coal and thermal power development led to annual energy water footprint growth rates exceeding 8%. However, disparities are also reinforced by intra-sectoral variation in food production: for instance, provinces such as Heilongjiang and Henan increased irrigation intensity to expand grain output, while others like Jiangsu and Zhejiang adopted high-efficiency irrigation and cash crop substitution, leading to differentiated water use patterns across regions. Notably, although China’s competition index declined after 2011, the CBRI rose, indicating that while certain policy interventions have helped mitigate localized resource conflicts, they have not been sufficient to reduce broader regional disparities in water use. For instance, the Action Plan for Water Pollution Prevention and Control (2015) focused primarily on controlling industrial wastewater discharge and improving water quality in heavily polluted areas, particularly in the eastern region. While this policy reduced point-source pollution and promoted stricter local water use regulations, it did not directly address the spatial mismatch between water availability and industrial distribution.50,51,52 As a result, wealthier provinces with stronger regulatory capacity benefited more from cleaner production technologies and investments in water-saving infrastructure, whereas resource-poor or inland provinces continued to struggle with structural water stress. This reflects Zhu et al.'s53 hypothesis of the uneven distribution of technological dividends and underscores the need to enhance cross-regional compensation mechanisms and water governance reforms that explicitly address structural imbalances in resource endowment and industrial development.

Regional coordination and optimal allocation of water resources

The expanding regional disparities in energy-food water use competition call for stronger cross-regional coordination. In high-competition areas such as North and Northwest China, water-saving energy strategies—like recirculating cooling systems in thermal power plants and wastewater reuse from coal mining—should be promoted. Meanwhile, southern regions with abundant water resources could ease northern water stress by moderately adjusting their energy industry layouts.

Policies such as water rights trading and interregional water transfers should be further refined to improve water use efficiency and promote equity. The Yellow River Basin water rights reform pilot offers valuable institutional insights54,55: grounded in total water use control and quota management, it disaggregates provincial quotas to municipal and sectoral levels, allowing for allocation, conservation, and market-based transfers. Annual use monitoring, quota accounting, and third-party verification ensure transparency and enforceability. To enhance the operability of these mechanisms, compensation standards should reflect both economic and ecological values—for instance, by indexing payments to marginal water productivity or ecosystem service losses in the exporting region. A robust regulatory framework is also essential, including (1) unified digital platforms for real-time tracking and transaction settlement, (2) legal mechanisms for penalties and dispute resolution, and (3) vertical coordination between basin and local authorities. Building on such pilot experiences, China can move toward an integrated and incentive-aligned water governance system capable of addressing regional water-energy-food allocation imbalances.

Conclusion

Based on water footprint accounting from 2005 to 2020, this study reveals that China’s energy-food water use competition has intensified significantly, with pronounced regional disparities. While the water footprint of energy production grew rapidly (6.3% annually on average)—especially for thermal (7.8%) and hydropower (8.3%)—food-related water use remained stable (0.15% annual growth), indicating gains in agricultural efficiency. Regionally, Inner Mongolia, Shanxi, Sichuan, and Yunnan saw sharp increases in energy-related water footprints. The water use competition index showed a rising trend nationwide, with North China experiencing the highest levels (peaking at 0.427), followed by notable increases in Northwest and Northeast China, while South and Southwest China remained at low competition levels. Moreover, regional disparities in water footprint intensity widened markedly, with the total inequality index rising from 0.64 to 0.95 and the contribution rate of between-region inequality (CBRI) nearly doubling after 2017. These findings highlight a clear spatial mismatch between energy and food water use, emphasizing the need for regionally differentiated strategies—particularly in high-stress areas—to promote interregional coordination and improve water efficiency for sustainable resource management.

Limitations of the study

This study presents a comprehensive spatiotemporal assessment of energy-food water use competition in China using the water footprint approach. Nonetheless, several aspects could be further refined in future research. First, the sectoral classification applied in the study is static and does not reflect the evolving technological heterogeneity within sectors, such as the gradual adoption of water-efficient practices in agriculture or energy production, which may influence estimated water intensities over time.56 Second, while regional differences and competition intensity are quantitatively analyzed, the study does not directly isolate the impact of specific policy or institutional factors. Exploring these linkages through targeted empirical models could help strengthen the explanatory power of the findings. Third, scenario-based simulations are not included, which limits the study’s ability to explore potential changes in water-energy-food interactions under varying climate or policy pathways.57 Future work may benefit from dynamic modeling of technological progress, empirical validation of institutional impacts, and multi-scenario simulation frameworks. These enhancements would support a more adaptive, forward-looking understanding of water governance under uncertainty.

Method details

This study examines the competitive relationship over water use between energy and food production across 31 provincial-level administrative regions in China (excluding Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Macao) using data from 2005 to 2020 (Figure S1). Fossil energy production data are sourced from the China Energy Statistical Yearbook, while electricity data are obtained from the China Electric Power Yearbook and the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA) (http://www.irena.org/, accessed January 21, 2025). Regional water resource data are derived from the China Statistical Yearbook and the China Water Resources Bulletin for the corresponding years. Data related to blue water use in food production are taken from the China Statistical Yearbook and the China Rural Statistical Yearbook.

Accounting for water use in energy and food production

The energy water footprint model is built upon the ISO standard water footprint methodology,39 employing a life cycle approach to account for the energy water footprint. This includes processes such as resource extraction, processing, and waste management. The energy water footprint consists of both direct water use during the production process and indirect water use associated with various inputs. It is categorized into two components: energy blue water footprint and energy gray water footprint. The energy blue water footprint refers to the consumption of surface and groundwater during production, while the energy gray water footprint denotes the volume of freshwater required to dilute pollutants to meet discharge standards. In this study, “energy” includes fossil energy (coal, oil, and natural gas) and electric energy (thermal power and hydropower). Tables S2 and S3 present water footprint data for energy (2005–2020) and food production, respectively.

Fossil energy water footprint covers coal, oil, and natural gas, involving both direct and indirect blue water use throughout the production process, along with wastewater discharge that forms the gray water footprint. For coal, water use is concentrated in mining and washing stages; for oil, it primarily occurs during crude extraction and refining; and for natural gas, it includes field extraction and liquefaction. Equipment manufacturing and other external inputs contribute to the indirect water footprint, while wastewater generated at various stages constitutes the gray water component.

Electric energy water footprint accounting for thermal and hydropower includes both direct and indirect water use.For thermal power, the direct water footprint primarily stems from water used for cooling during electricity generation. The indirect water footprint includes water consumed throughout the fuel supply chain, such as during the extraction and processing of coal or natural gas. For hydropower, the direct water footprint is mainly associated with the use of water flow to drive turbines for power generation. The indirect water footprint involves water used during dam construction, infrastructure development, and the manufacturing of turbine equipment. Both involve a certain degree of wastewater discharge throughout their life cycles, which constitutes the gray water footprint.

In the formula, Ep represents the water footprint per unit of energy output, m3/GJ; Ed is the direct water footprint per unit of energy output during production, m3/GJ; Eind is the indirect water footprint per unit of energy output during production, m3/GJ; Ed,b is the direct blue water footprint per unit of energy output during production, m3/GJ; Ed,g is the direct gray water footprint per unit of energy output during production, m3/GJ; Eind,b is the indirect blue water footprint per unit of energy input, m3/GJ; Eind,g is the indirect gray water footprint per unit of energy input, m3/GJ; Em(d,b) is the direct blue water footprint per unit of output in the m-th production process, m3/GJ; Em(d,g) is the direct gray water footprint per unit of output in the m-th production process, m3/GJ; En(ind,b) is the indirect blue water footprint per unit of output from the n-th energy input, m3/GJ; En(ind,g) is the indirect gray water footprint per unit of output from the n-th energy input, m3/GJ; m is the production process number; n is the energy category number.

In the formula, E represents the total water footprint of energy production, m3; Ep,n is the water footprint per unit of energy output from the n-th energy category, m3/GJ; Pn is the energy output from the n-th energy category, GJ.

The water footprint of food production is divided into blue water footprint, green water footprint, and gray water footprint. The blue water footprint refers to the amount of surface or groundwater consumed by crops during food production; the green water footprint typically refers to the precipitation and water stored in crops consumed during food production; the gray water footprint represents the volume of freshwater required to dilute pollutants generated during the food production process. This study focuses on the competitive relationship over water use between energy and food production, specifically emphasizing water resources that can be directly managed, allocated, or competed for across sectors. However, green water of food production represents soil moisture derived from rainfall and is not accessible to other sectors such as industry or energy for direct use. Meanwhile, the gray water of food production requires detailed pollutant discharge data, which is difficult to obtain uniformly at the cross-provincial level. Therefore, based on existing research,41,42 this study only considers the blue water footprint for food production (including cereals, legumes, and tubers). Regional water use differences based on the index

The Theil index was proposed by Theil et al. using the concept of entropy from information theory to measure income inequality.45 The Theil index,46 by dividing the overall study area into multiple regions, not only measures the overall level of disparity but also distinguishes differences both between and within regions, thereby analyzing the causes of the disparities and explaining the sources and evolving trends of the gaps.42 A larger Theil index indicates greater disparity. In this study, the Theil index is used to quantify the differences in China’s water footprint intensity (the ratio of water footprint to regional GDP). The calculation formula is as follows

In the formula, Th represents the total Theil index; TBR is the between-region inequality index; TWR is the within-region inequality index; x is the number of regions; y is the number of provinces (autonomous regions) within a region; vi is the share of the water footprint of the i-th region in the national water footprint; vij is the share of the water footprint of the j-th province (autonomous region) within the i-th region in the national water footprint; di is the share of the regional GDP of the i-th region in the national GDP; dij is the share of the GDP of the j-th province (autonomous region) within the i-th region in the national GDP. China is divided into six regions based on the classification from The Data Center for Resources and Environmental Sciences of the Chinese Academy of Science (Table S1).

Evaluation of the competitive relationship in water use for energy and food production

Unlike previous studies that relied on regional water use, which is often affected by human interventions such as water-saving technologies and industrial restructuring,41,47 this study adopts regional water resources as the basis for analyzing water competition. By doing so, we eliminate the influence of water use efficiency and better reflect the objective natural endowment of a region. Specifically, we construct a competition index model based on the contribution rates of energy and food production to water resource pressure. This approach directly reveals the extent to which energy and food production consume the regional natural water resource supply capacity and the pressure they exert on the water resource system. Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted across 31 provincial-level regions (n = 31) in China for the period 2005–2020. Water footprint values for energy and food production were quantified annually (n = 16 years), and the analyses were based on these panel data. Statistical details and indicator values are reported in the results section, figure legends, and supplemental tables. All data processing, inequality decomposition, and visualization were performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft), Origin 2021 (OriginLab), and ArcGIS (ESRI).
