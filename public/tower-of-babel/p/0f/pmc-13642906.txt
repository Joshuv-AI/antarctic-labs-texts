From heterogeneity to translation: data‑driven subtyping of autism in a multilevel framework
Authors: Xing-Ke Wang, Zhou Zhang, Shuang Li, Rui-Xuan Ren, Li-Rui Hao, Yue Cao, Shao-Di Wang, Gaoxiang Ouyang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) exhibits pronounced heterogeneity across genetic, neurobiological, and clinical phenotypic levels, posing substantial challenges for mechanistic elucidation and clinical translation. This review synthesizes advances in data-driven approaches to parsing ASD heterogeneity and centers the discussion on three complementary strata: neural, behavioral, and transdiagnostic subtypes. At the neuroimaging level, studies leveraging features such as functional connectivity and brain structure have consistently identified two core neurosubtypes characterized by increased and decreased neural activity, respectively. These neurosubtypes differ in time-varying dynamics, spatial architecture, and network hierarchy, and they are closely associated with specific symptom dimensions and cognitive functions. At the behavioral level, data-driven methods delineate phenotypes along axes of severity and functional impairment, and further reveal their links to neural circuits. Transdiagnostic investigations indicate that ASD and frequently co-occurring disorders share neurobiological substrates and cognitive endophenotypes. Collectively, these findings argue against a simple one-to-one correspondence between behavioral and neural subtypes; instead, the evidence is more consistent with multi-to-one, one-to-many, or many-to-many mappings that converge on the overall functional impairment. Notwithstanding this progress, major challenges remain, including sample heterogeneity, methodological inconsistency, and the integration of categorical and dimensional models. Future research should prioritize large samples, multi-site collaboration, longitudinal designs, and transdiagnostic frameworks, coupled with reverse validation via intervention response, to build robust evidence for mechanism-informed individualized assessment and intervention in ASD.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) exhibits profound heterogeneity and extensive comorbidity across genetic, neurobiological, and clinical dimensions [1, 2]. Hundreds of risk genes contribute to ASD, and their convergent effects perturb local circuits and large scale networks, giving rise to social communication deficits (SCD) and restricted and repetitive behaviors (RRBs) alongside related symptoms [3, 4]. Diversity in developmental trajectories, intellectual functioning, and co-occurring conditions complicates mechanistic inference and undermines intervention generalizability [5–7]. As prevalence rises [8] and diagnostic boundaries broaden, cohort diversity has expanded and effect sizes in cognitive and neuroimaging studies have declined [9, 10]. Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition (DSM-5) defines ASD as a single diagnostic category, which aggregates distinct etiological and pathophysiological routes [11]. Mechanisms and treatment targets must therefore be inferred within a label that compresses multiple biological pathways into one diagnosis. Refined stratification that links clinical traits to neurobiology is now a major priority [12].

Recent work has moved beyond traditional case control designs toward mechanism-oriented modeling strategies that reduce reliance on diagnostic labels [13]. Data-driven subtyping is central to this shift and aims to extract relatively homogeneous strata from highly heterogeneous ASD samples [14–16]. Typical pipelines quantify interindividual similarity across multidimensional measures, apply supervised or unsupervised learning to derive candidate subtypes, and interpret them using symptom dimensions, cognition, or neurobiological indices. Behavioral phenotyping has yielded clinically coherent clusters. One study identified four subtypes, including social, behavioral, mixed ASD with developmental delay, moderate challenges, and broadly affected profiles, with subtype linked genetic profiles and developmental expression trajectories [17]. Yet behavioral subtypes do not provide a direct map to mechanism. Distinct neural or molecular pathways may converge on similar behavioral phenotypes, reflecting phenotypic convergence, whereas network reorganization may render comparable neural alterations behaviorally divergent, indicative of mechanistic divergence [18–20]. Neurosubtyping therefore increasingly leverages brain structure, functional connectivity (FC), electrophysiology, and related metrics to capture latent strata. Key bottlenecks persist. Sample size variation and the winner’s curse lead effects to attenuate with scale, motivating thousand participant studies and robust statistical strategies [21]. Even within the same datasets, results diverge across modalities, algorithms, and effective sample sizes, limiting reproducibility and cross study concordance [14, 15]. Subtype discovery is also contingent on the scientific question and analytic pipeline [22]. A fundamental question remains whether reported subtypes capture stable, disease relevant structure or reflect analytic variability. The most consequential limitation is integrative. Behavioral phenotyping and neurosubtyping often proceed in parallel, and brain behavior mapping remains weak. It remains unclear whether specific behavioral patterns map to consistent neural mechanisms, and whether neural and behavioral subtypes capture overlapping or distinct dimensions of ASD heterogeneity.

ASD heterogeneity also reflects extensive symptom overlap across psychiatric and neurodevelopmental conditions [23–25]. Convergent manifestations and blurred pathophysiological boundaries are reshaping approaches to psychiatric classification [26]. Genetic evidence supports shared liability across ASD, intellectual disability, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), schizophrenia spectrum disorders (SSD), and bipolar disorder (BD), consistent with a broader neurodevelopmental continuum [24, 27]. Subtyping confined to a single disorder may therefore miss core neurobiological dimensions. Data-driven transdiagnostic subtyping organizes individuals by covariance patterns in behavioral and neural features rather than by a priori labels, and targets reproducible subgroups and latent dimensions that generalize across diagnoses. In contrast to ASD focused subtyping that emphasizes autism related dimensions and their neural representations, transdiagnostic analyses test shared dimensions and seek mechanisms or therapeutic targets that transfer across conditions.

The primary objective in addressing ASD heterogeneity is to improve precision and individualized intervention. Neurobehavioral strata could support targeted intervention matching. In major depression, neural subtypes have shown associations with clinical outcomes and treatment response, offering a transferable paradigm [14, 28, 29]. Evidence in ASD remains emerging, so clinical value should be judged by mechanistic interpretability and its capacity to inform individualized intervention [30]. Behavioral phenotyping focuses on observable symptom patterns and cognitive profiles, whereas neurosubtyping relies on features such as brain structure and FC and can incorporate EEG or ERP measures and MRI derived indices. Neural perturbations shape behavior, and behavioral change can modify neural systems across development, yielding reciprocal and nonlinear interactions [31]. No single behavioral phenotype or neurosubtype can capture the full ASD profile. Establishing principled links between behavioral and neural subtypes is essential for validating subtyping schemes, integrating evidence across levels, and improving translation.

Existing reviews have advanced the field by detailing neurosubtyping workflows and biomarker interpretability [16], contrasting neurosubtyping with dimensional and normative approaches [14], and tracing the evolution of ASD heterogeneity from prototypical diagnoses to spectrum models with recommendations for stratification [22]. Yet a unified synthesis that integrates neural subtypes, behavioral phenotypes, and transdiagnostic dimensions remains limited. This review aims to construct a multilevel framework that integrates research on ASD heterogeneity across neural, behavioral, and cross diagnostic domains. We searched PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar for articles published from January 2005 to December 2025 using combinations of terms related to “autism/ASD”, “heterogeneity”, “subtyping”, “clustering”, “neurosubtype”, “functional connectivity”, “EEG/ERP”, “MRI”, and “transdiagnostic”. Inclusion criteria were as follows: i) data-driven subtyping or clustering of ASD or transdiagnostic samples; ii) explicit use of neural and/or behavioral features for subtype identification at the complementary level; and iii) clear documentation of sample characteristics and analytical methodologies. Studies relying exclusively on investigator-defined grouping strategies or dimensional modeling approaches were excluded. The discussion proceeds along three axes (Fig. 1). First, at brain spatiotemporal scales, we synthesize data-driven subtyping studies based on neural features to identify putative neurosubtyping. Second, at the behavioral level, we summarize behavioral phenotype studies that incorporate neural features for subtype validation, and explore associations between neural variations and clinical symptoms. Third, moving beyond ASD as a single diagnostic category, we examine subtyping approaches grounded in transdiagnostic dimensions to uncover potential neurobiological commonalities shared across diagnostic boundaries. Heterogeneity across pipelines is driven primarily by feature construction. Feature choice defines the mechanism space that clustering can recover. Four strategies recur, each balancing interpretability against discovery. A priori indicator features target circuits with established neurobiological meaning, such as the N170 component or the salience network (SN) [32, 33]. This choice supports mechanistic interpretability and cross study comparability [34, 35]. It also narrows the search space and can miss distributed patterns. Whole brain representations broaden coverage by using high dimensional FC matrices or summary features such as regional homogeneity, followed by feature selection and clustering under regularization [36, 37]. These models are sensitive to unanticipated structure. They also depend strongly on preprocessing decisions such as thresholding and graph density, and subtype boundaries can be difficult to interpret [16, 38, 39]. Semi supervised pipelines increase clinical anchoring by selecting neural features that covary with measures such as Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule Second Edition (ADOS-2) scores before dimensionality reduction and clustering [18, 40]. This design can improve external validity, and it requires strict nested cross validation to reduce circularity. Normative modeling defines each individual by deviation from a typically developing (TD) reference group [41]. Deviation maps or individualized z scores can then be clustered to derive subtypes [42, 43]. It can aid cross site harmonization, yet it inherits bias when matching is imperfect.

Most studies use unsupervised algorithms, including k-means, hierarchical clustering, and spectral clustering. Model order is usually set to two using internal criteria, including the silhouette coefficient [44] and the Davies Bouldin index [45]. Studies often adjust for age, full scale intelligence quotient (IQ), head motion, and site before clustering. Three studies used hybrid supervised and unsupervised workflows [46–48]. These designs can encode theory guided structure, and they place a high premium on independent replication and rigorous validation [15]. From a computer-science perspective, ASD neurosubtyping remains relatively conservative in model order selection and robustness evaluation. Many studies compare only a small number of candidate solutions (e.g., K = 2 or 3) using internal indices such as silhouette and Davies-Bouldin, and rarely report perturbation-based consistency metrics such as adjusted Rand index [49] or normalized mutual information [50], or simulation-based power analyses [51] to quantify the statistical sensitivity for detecting latent subtype structure. These complementary evaluations would strengthen the distinction between reproducible signal and chance clustering driven by noise, batch effects, or preprocessing idiosyncrasies.

Methodological innovation is emerging around individualized deviation features and graph-based representations. Some studies cluster deviation space embeddings derived from normative models [52, 53]. Others build functional hypergraphs from individual deviation matrices and apply community detection to define FC subtypes [54]. These approaches emphasize individualized deviations [55] and can increase stability. They require larger samples and high-quality normative data. Validation practice also remains uneven. Internal indices alone provide limited protection against chance clustering under high dimensional noise. Agreement metrics such as the adjusted Rand index [49] and normalized mutual information [50], together with simulation-based power analyses [51], would strengthen claims of reproducible latent structure.

Representative ASD neurosubtyping and characteristics

Across modalities, many results converge on a small set of neural axes. Prior summaries often describe two recurring patterns, commonly labeled enhancement and reduction, with occasional mixed profiles [14, 16]. These labels capture relative differences in the chosen features and do not imply global neural benefit or deficit. Modality matters because fMRI, structural MRI, and EEG index different spatiotemporal scales (Fig. 2B).

At the large-scale network level, a recurring contrast concerns the balance between integration and segregation. One subtype shows increased cross-network connectivity together with reduced within-network connectivity, whereas the other shows the opposite pattern, suggestive of atypical modular organization [56–58]. Consistent with this organizing axis, hypoconnectivity and hyperconnectivity patterns have been repeatedly observed within and between multiple systems, including the frontoparietal network (FPN), default mode network (DMN), dorsal attention network (DAN), SN, and subcortical network (SUB) [18, 37, 43, 59]. These pathways collectively form a key functional axis spanning sensory processing, subcortical modulation, and higher-order cognitive control [37, 59]. Transcriptomic association work further links globally elevated FC profiles to pathways related to excitation-inhibition imbalance [36]. Such evidence is suggestive of subtype-specific molecular correlates, but it remains indirect and likely sensitive to analytic choices.

Temporal organization offers a complementary axis. At the scale of seconds, SN coordination can diverge in stability and periodicity [60], with recurrence quantification showing high versus low dynamical signatures [32]. At millisecond timescales, EEG work partitions N170 latency into slow, intermediate, and fast profiles [33]. These findings suggest heterogeneity in the timing and coordination of information flow.

Structural neuroimaging further indicates heterogeneity in both local brain morphology and long-range white-matter (WM) pathways. sMRI and DTI studies report subtype differences in gray matter (GM) and WM volume [42, 47, 48, 61] and in cortical thickness (CT) and surface area [52, 62]. Effects often implicate sensorimotor and visual cortices, cingulo insular regions, lateral prefrontal cortex, fusiform gyrus, and superior parietal areas [48, 52, 61, 62]. Several studies also report opposing patterns in the corpus callosum, amygdala and hippocampus, and caudate nucleus [63]. These loci are frequently interpreted in relation to perception and motor coordination [64], cognitive control and socioemotional processing [65], and affective learning and memory.

Developmental timing likely shapes subtype structure. Pediatric work suggests age related differences consistent with early overgrowth followed by later normalization, delay, or compensatory remodeling [66], and an early progressive phase followed by volumetric regression toward typical brain size [67]. Cross sectional clustering cannot separate trait like strata from stage dependent expressions. Longitudinal sampling is therefore essential. Stability also depends on site, age range, and preprocessing. Some studies identify a subtype that is closer to TD profiles, suggesting a continuum from near typical to more divergent neural states [46]. Joint evaluation of categorical and dimensional structure can improve interpretation and translational relevance [15].

Brain-behavior associations

Behavioral validation tests whether neurosubtyping captures clinically meaningful variation beyond group averages. In practice, behavioral validation in this literature often relies on a limited set of core instruments, which constrains coverage of adaptive functioning, context-dependent behavior, and ecologically sampled phenotypes. Furthermore, nearly every identified subtype demonstrates concurrent, though varying, degrees of association with both SCD and RRBs, which complicates the interpretation of symptom specificity and independent effects. Overall, three domains predominated SCD (n = 13), RRBs including sensory anomalies (n = 11), and cognitive impairment (n = 4).

Brain-behavior associations in SCD and RRBs converge on three neural dimensions: i) the balance of network integration versus segregation, ii) disruption of sensory-to-control pathways, and iii) abnormalities in temporal coordination dynamics. Enhanced subtypes related to SCD and RRBs tends to show stronger connectivity between the visual network (VN) and prefrontal network or SN, higher periodicity or stability of SN coordination, and elevations in functional or structural indices within the ventral visual pathway [18, 42, 68, 69]. In these subtypes, both SCD and RRBs are often elevated, although their relative weighting varies across studies. In contrast, reduced subtypes more commonly exhibit lower connectivity within VN to the SN, language and intelligence-related networks, alongside more diffuse reductions in GM/WM volume and decreased connectivity in networks associated with verbal IQ [18, 47, 69]. Clinically, these patterns are frequently associated with poorer SCD and/or higher RRBs. Several studies also report stronger connectivity between subcortical and cortical networks in the reduced subtypes, for example between subcortical systems and the DMN, VN, FPN, and DAN, which suggests possible subcortical compensation when cortical control pathways are compromised [59]. Graph-theoretic and structural covariance analyses further support subtype dissociations by reporting stronger structural covariance or higher nodal centrality in RRB-related subtypes [42]. Future validation efforts would benefit from moving beyond demonstrating broad correlations with core symptoms toward testing specificity, and from aligning behavioral batteries with the hypothesized neural mechanisms to improve interpretability and ecological validity.

Finally, cognitive impairment may represent a distinct axis relevant to neurosubtyping. Cognitive difficulties can remain prominent across development and may become more apparent as environmental demands increase, even when some core symptoms improve [70]. Dysfunction of executive function (EF) is one influential account within ASD, proposing that social-communication difficulties, repetitive behavior, and reduced cognitive flexibility are linked—at least in part—to impaired EF. Cognitive-impairment subtypes show tighter within network connectivity in DMN [71], FPN, and DAN [37] together with lower between network interaction, for example between DMN and attention or perceptual systems [71]. On MRI, these subtypes show cortical thickening in primary somatosensory and motor cortex, the superior parietal lobule, associative visual cortex, and the frontal eye field [52].

Data-driven behavioral phenotypes

Advances and methodology

Behavior-based subtyping has the pragmatic advantage of operating directly in clinically meaningful space, potentially refining assessment dimensions and informing individualized support needs. At the same time, behavioral phenotypes reflect downstream clinical expression, and similar subtypes may stem from distinct neural mechanisms [31]. Caution is therefore needed in their partitioning. Historically, the limited reliability and temporal stability of DSM-IV pervasive developmental disorder subcategories illustrates how behavior-driven partitions can fail to generalize, and may influence diagnostic practice and prevalence estimates when boundaries are porous [9]. Within this context, data-driven behavioral phenotyping is particularly informative.

Behavioral subtype discovery typically draws on multi-level measurements, including standardized rating scales, clinician-administered interviews, performance-based tasks, and (less frequently) expert-derived summaries of functioning. In the present review, eight data-driven behavioral phenotyping studies also incorporated neuroimaging or electrophysiology for subtype validation (Table 2) were included. Of these, five studies (62.5%) classified participants using rating scales, two studies (25%) used behavioral task performance, and one study used expert ratings. Feature selection construction most often centers on core symptom domains of SCD and RRBs, with ADOS-2 and the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised (ADI-R) as the most frequently used instruments (Fig. 2D). Building on these core assessments, some studies further parse subgroups within these symptom domains [72]. To extend phenotypic coverage beyond core symptoms, studies commonly incorporate adaptive functioning (e.g., Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales), EF and attention measures (e.g., continuous performance, stop-signal, delay discounting tasks), and emotion-processing indices (e.g., facial/vocal emotion recognition), aiming to separate behaviorally similar individuals into subgroups that differ in putative information-processing or control profiles. Compared with neurosubtyping, a central challenge for behavioral phenotyping is the breadth of features used for clustering. Studies range from highly aggregated totals on standardized scales to finer grained item level ratings from clinical interviews, and to quantitative metrics derived from specific experimental tasks. This diversity means that different investigations operate in distinct behavioral spaces. Methodologically, Algorithms commonly employ interpretable unsupervised methods such as k-means, hierarchical clustering, or latent profile analysis, often combined with dimensionality reduction and feature selection techniques including principal component analysis or random forest to suppress redundancy and highlight key behavioral signals, with the goal of discovering latent subtypes without reliance on diagnostic labels. Building on these approaches, several large-sample studies have further applied latent profile/class analysis or Gaussian mixture models to jointly model RRBs, social communication, and comorbid psychiatric symptoms, and to determine the optimal number of behavioral subtypes using criteria such as the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) or likelihood ratio tests, often yielding three to five phenotypically distinct subtypes [73–75]. Compared with hard partitioning methods such as k-means, these probabilistic models retain membership probabilities for everyone, which can better handle boundary cases and align more naturally with hybrid structures in which continuous severity and discrete classes co-exist.

However, behavioral phenotyping often requires prior specification of target functional domains. Consequently, the selected measurement battery implicitly defines the behavioral space in which heterogeneity can be detected, and subtype solutions may capture within-domain variability rather than comprehensive ASD heterogeneity. On the other hand, while dimensionality reduction can improve statistical tractability, derived components do not always map cleanly onto established clinical constructs, limiting interpretability and clinical communication. Some studies address this by incorporating expert review of algorithmic partitions or clustering on clinically actionable functional scores to align results with intervention targets [76], although such practices require explicit safeguards against subjective bias and inconsistent scoring.

Representative ASD behavioral phenotypes and characteristics

Despite variation in behavioral measures and methods across studies, behavioral phenotypes are commonly organized into three forms. One prevalent approach adopts a severity-based scheme. Many studies identify mild to severe subtypes anchored by SCD and levels of adaptive functioning [77, 78]. The mild ASD subtype shows relatively limited impairment in daily adaptive and social functioning, whereas the severe subtype shows more pronounced core symptoms. Three studies centered on cognitive control [76, 79] and face recognition [80] also yielded gradations by severity. This approach emphasizes that clinical heterogeneity in ASD often appears as differences in symptom severity. It does not necessarily capture all mechanistic variation. For example, Feczko and colleagues reported task defined subtypes that did not differ on core symptom ratings, which suggests that some cognitive impairments may influence behavior independently of symptom severity [79]. Beyond severity, behavioral phenotypes highlight patterns of impairment in two core symptom (i.e. SCD and RRBs), emotion [78, 81, 82] and cognition [72, 76, 79]. For instance, a study divided individuals with high autistic traits into three phenotypes characterized by emotional dysregulation, ADHD-like features, and anxiety-depressive symptoms [81]. A third motif emphasizes within-domain heterogeneity. For example, RRBs can be partitioned into circumscribed interests (CI), insistence on sameness (IS), and repetitive motor actions (RM) [72]. Collectively, these patterns suggest that behavioral subtyping can reveal both quantitative severity gradients and qualitatively distinct functional profiles, with clinical relevance that may depend on intellectual level, comorbid symptom burden, sex, and developmental timing. Furthermore, these phenotypes may align with transdiagnostic liabilities such as attention and internalizing, motivating cross-diagnostic models in later sections.

Beyond the studies reviewed above, heterogeneity in ASD may also extend to other functional domains. In the domain of social cognition, performance on mentalizing tasks has been used to identify distinct phenotypes in both individuals with ASD and TD, indicating that variation in mentalizing ability can be differentiated at the phenotypic level [83]. Subsequent studies further showed that theory of mind (ToM) phenotypes are associated with support needs and educational placement, and that they differ in adaptive functioning and pragmatic competence in everyday contexts [84, 85]. These findings suggest that ToM-related heterogeneity is meaningful and clinically relevant within ASD, although some variability is also present in TD. Heterogeneity has also been reported in the language domain. In a large-sample study, language comprehension abilities clustered into hierarchically ordered levels that defined distinct language phenotypes in ASD [86]. Related work further showed that phenotypes characterized by stronger language abilities were also associated with better ToM and EF [87]. Other studies likewise indicated that language phenotypes are associated with social communication, symptom severity, family resources, and broader cognitive characteristics [88, 89]. These findings suggest that language heterogeneity in ASD is better understood within an interactive framework encompassing language, social communication, mentalizing, and broader neurocognitive functioning. However, most of these studies still lack convergent neural evidence to support the underlying brain-behavior relationships, or some rely on investigator-defined classification. Future studies should therefore combine behavioral phenotyping with neurobiological data to evaluate the validity, mechanistic specificity, and translational relevance of these phenotypes.

Behavior-brain associations

Behavior-first phenotypes can guide mechanistically targeted neuroimaging analyses and improve interpretability of candidate biomarkers. Validation modalities included EEG (n = 2), MRI (n = 2), and fMRI (n = 4). Across data-driven studies that included neural characterization, recurrent large-scale systems most consistently involve the DMN, visual and somatomotor systems, and salience-related circuitry, while frequently implicated regions include the amygdala, fusiform gyrus, precuneus, occipital visual cortex, and midline cingulate areas.

Specifically, severity-based behavioral phenotypes are often associated with distributed abnormalities in interactions among large-scale networks, with the clearest deviations typically observed in more severe phenotypes. Consistent with this pattern, a symptom-based stratification study further showed that the less severe phenotype was marked by a more focal network abnormality centered on the mid-cingulate cortex [77]. A similar pattern has been reported, where clustering based on symptom severity and adaptive functioning identified dissociable network patterns spanning frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital regions [76]. When autism is examined as a single group relative to TD, some phenotype-specific differences may be obscured by within-group heterogeneity [78].

Behavioral phenotypes defined by functional dimensions or within-symptom structure may also map onto relatively separable neural circuits. Prior work has shown that imbalance between the SCD and RRBs is associated with distinct patterns of large-scale networks [90], suggesting that the internal configuration of core symptom domains has relevance for neural circuit organization. A comparable pattern has been observed for social-perceptual phenotypes that poorer emotion-recognition performance was associated with greater SCD, together with reduced activation in the amygdala and fusiform gyrus [80]. At the level of within-symptom structure, further decomposition of RRBs suggests that components weighted toward CI and IS are more closely related to abnormalities in cognitive control circuit dynamics, whereas RM are more closely linked to motor circuit abnormalities [72]. Additionally, different phenotypes may correspond to distinct cortical morphometric signatures, although these differences often become less apparent when heterogeneous groups are analyzed together [81]. Psychopathology-related phenotypes also appear to show closer links to limbic structures. For example, a high-psychopathology phenotype has been associated with enlarged right amygdala volume, and the relation between this structural marker and internalizing symptoms appears to differ by sex [82]. Taken together, behavioral phenotyping provides a more refined framework for examining correspondences between clinical variation and brain mechanisms.

In addition, language heterogeneity in ASD has a discernible neurodevelopmental basis, and that this heterogeneity may already be expressed as distinct prognostic trajectories early in development. Early neuroimaging work found that autistic children with poorer later language outcomes showed reduced speech-related activation in language-sensitive superior temporal cortices, whereas those with better outcomes displayed more typical activation patterns in these regions [91]. Subsequent multimodal work further showed that autistic children with poor versus good early language outcomes differed in their gene-brain coupling patterns, and that the implicated coexpression modules were enriched for ASD-related, prenatal, and language-relevant genes [92]. Future research should therefore prioritize longitudinal multimodal designs to test whether early neural and molecular variation can prospectively distinguish clinically meaningful language phenotypes in ASD.

In contrast to subtyping strategies based solely on ASD specific features, transdiagnostic work tends to select cross disorder comparable cognitive and behavioral measures, for example EF and emotion and behavior regulation [94, 95], together with indices derived from resting or task fMRI that capture generalized connectivity topology and large scale network integration and segregation [96, 97]. Given the complexity of the transdiagnostic framework, multimodal data fusion becomes even more critical than in single-disorder subtyping studies. Stefanik and colleagues used similarity network fusion (SNF) to integrate sMRI, DTI, and behavioral measures [98] and identified four data-driven groups spanning ASD, SSD, BD, and TD. These groups showed separable differences in WM, CT, and social cognition and EF, whereas traditional diagnosis-based grouping yielded only small effects [99]. Vandewouw and colleagues similarly combined SNF with spectral clustering in a large sample spanning ASD, ADHD, and OCD and identified six to ten highly replicable subtypes based on whole brain connectivity and behavioral features [97]. This pattern suggests that data-driven, multidimensional integration can effectively synthesize information across modalities and disorders, thereby helping to identify common neurobiological mechanisms that transcend traditional diagnostic categories. It also suggests that sources of neurophenotypic variation in ASD often cross diagnostic boundaries and that some aberrant circuits may not sharply separate patients from nonpatients.

To address the limitations of forcing individuals into mutually exclusive categories, recent studies have adopted probabilistic generative models. Bayesian nonparametric approaches, such as the Indian Buffet Process and Latent Dirichlet Allocation, allow for the modeling of mixed membership, where an individual expresses a combination of latent connectivity patterns [96]. Beyond handling this, identifying informative features in high-dimensional neurobehavioral data is also a practical challenge. Cordova and colleagues used a functional random forest approach to handle high-dimensional EF task data and to isolate features that contributed most to subtype separation, supporting transdiagnostic subtypes centered on EF deficits [94]. Vaidya and colleagues applied community detection to correlation networks of behavioral measures, providing convergent support for EF as a robust transdiagnostic dimension [95].

Despite these advances, the stability of transdiagnostic subtyping remains susceptible to dataset heterogeneity, including variability in age, sex, IQ, and medication history. The distinction between categorical subgroups and continuous spectra remains a critical debate. Dajani and colleagues using group iterative multiple model estimation to model individualized effective connectivity, did not obtain stable discrete solutions and the results were more consistent with a continuous distribution [100]. By contrast, Vandewouw and colleagues reported a stable binary split in task fMRI-based consensus clustering [101]. This discrepancy suggests that subtyping stability is likely modality-dependent and sensitive to analytic scale. Furthermore, high-IQ subtypes consistently show distinct neurobiological profiles, such as greater network integration and fewer attentional deficits [96, 101], underscoring the confounding role of general cognitive ability. Therefore, rigorous methodological controls, including site harmonization, regression of covariates, and stringent motion censoring, are prerequisite for translating these data-driven insights into reproducible clinical biomarkers.

Representative subtypes and characteristics

Multimodal studies that incorporate both behavioral and neural measures have consistently shown that subtypes track behavioral dimensions such as social cognition, EF control, and inattention across ASD, ADHD, SSD, BD, and OCD [99, 102, 103]. In one such study, inattentive symptom burden, together with CT within socioemotional and attention/executive networks, ranked among the most influential features driving the subtypes [102]. Another study similarly identified significant differences in autism-related and inattentive symptom dimensions, accompanied by widespread CT variation [103]. These findings suggest that linking behavioral and neural features can substantially improve the external validity and theoretical interpretability of data-driven subtypes. At the same time, behavioral features in transdiagnostic stratification do not all carry the same typological status. Some subtypes more closely capture differences in functional constructs, whereas others are better understood as partitions of symptom severity or symptom configuration [94, 95, 100]. An analysis of individualized effective connectivity for cognitive flexibility did not yield stable categorical solutions and was more consistent with a continuous distribution [100]. Classification frameworks should therefore be matched to the level of data.

At the network level, ASD and ADHD show overlapping abnormalities in connectivity among the DMN, the DAN, and the SN. Data-driven studies of behavioral phenotypes and neurosubtyping converge on abnormal interaction among DMN, DAN, and SN as a shared neural endophenotype rather than one confined to a single diagnosis [94, 96, 97]. In a hierarchical Bayesian framework, Kernbach and colleagues further differentiated the disorders. The ADHD related subtype showed stronger DMN to DAN connectivity together with weaker within network connectivity in DMN and in DAN. Conversely, an ASD-related subtype exhibited stronger within-DMN connectivity alongside weaker DMN-DAN connectivity [96]. These results point to a DMN centered dysfunction that is common across conditions, with clinical differences arising from different combinations and degrees of circuit impairment. Converging evidence from reviews also confirms that ASD and ADHD share genetic factors and common deficits in social and EF, yet exhibit significant phenotypic differences that warrant their classification as separate disorders [23].

Additionally, nearly all studies also implicated IQ. Higher IQ subtypes showed fewer attentional problems [101]. In fMRI, they showed stronger activation in classic ToM regions under social conditions [101] and greater integration among the SN, DAN, limbic, and DMN, together with reduced integration in the frontoparietal control network and subcortical regions [101].

Transdiagnostic subtypes in RDoC

The National Institute of Mental Health Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework was developed to organize transdiagnostic functional constructs in relation to their underlying neural circuitry and observable behavioral manifestations [13, 104, 105]. Within this framework, data-driven subtypes may be interpreted as distinct patterns of alteration across functional domains, which increases the interpretability of transdiagnostic findings within a more explicit psychopathology model.

A subset of studies is most readily situated within the RDoC domain of systems for social processes [105]. Across these studies, transdiagnostic subtype structure is commonly organized around social cognition, ToM, and social meaning attribution [99, 101–103, 106]. The resulting subtypes rarely map onto conventional diagnostic labels and are more consistent with variation along social-process dimensions. For example, task-based work using a social attribution paradigm identified diagnosis-agnostic subtypes spanning ASD, ADHD, and TD, with between-group differences more closely related to social meaning extraction and mental-state understanding [101]. Symptom-based clustering similarly suggests that social impairment and inattentive/hyperactive burden can form separable transdiagnostic subtypes [106].

Another set of studies is more appropriately interpreted within the RDoC domains of cognitive systems and arousal/regulatory systems [105]. In these studies, heterogeneity is expressed through dissociable patterns of EF and regulatory difficulty, including cognitive flexibility with emotion regulation, inhibitory control, and working memory-related dysfunction, with these profiles extending across the normal-to-impaired continuum [95]. Similar phenotypes may also arise from different neural architectures. In a study that clustered individuals using 43 EF measures, mild and severe subtypes were identified, yet the corresponding connectivity findings did not follow a simple severity gradient, indicating that EF abnormalities and behavioral dysregulation are not uniformly coupled [94]. MRI studies further show that subtypes are often more strongly related to intelligence, adaptive functioning, attention problems, and hyperactivity/impulsivity, suggesting that homogeneity in these disorders may be better indexed by functional dimensions than by categorical diagnoses [97, 107].

Findings from these studies suggest that at least part of the observed heterogeneity reflects distinct combinations of functional-domain variation and their neural implementation. From this perspective, transdiagnostic subtypes should not be regarded as direct replacements for existing diagnostic categories. They can be understood as patterned variation across social, cognitive, and regulatory systems, providing a more explicit basis for mechanistic validation, prognostic stratification, and intervention targeting [108].

Cross-level integration and future directions

Mapping relationships between behavioral phenotypes and neurosubtyping

Within a multilevel framework, these subtypes overlap conceptually but are not identical subtypes, since they differ in the criteria used for classification. Both behavioral phenotyping and neurosubtyping often yield subtypes ordered by overall impairment across current studies. Behavioral phenotypes separate individuals by degree of cognitive and social communication limitation, with neural differences that involve abnormal interactions among large scale networks [76, 78, 79]. Connectivity-based neurosubtyping frequently identifies globally increased or decreased FC, and behavioral correlates track language ability and core or overall symptom severity [18, 37, 40, 46]. Despite this severity-related axis, neural and behavioral features remain imperfectly aligned. At the neural level, larger deviations in network organization often cooccur with broader deficits [46, 69], yet comparable biological alterations do not necessarily translate into uniform clinical manifestations, consistent with mechanistic divergence. At the behavioral level, subtypes with greater symptom severity often show widespread shifts in connectivity that constrain segregation and integration and reduce flexibility [56], reflecting phenotypic convergence. This complexity is further underscored by observations that some connectivity-based subtypes show relative preservation in selected cognitive domains [36, 43], whereas some behavioral phenotypes show weak neuroimaging correlates [82].

Transdiagnostic studies further support phenotypic convergence, showing that despite disorder-specific neural heterogeneity, psychiatric conditions can still be partitioned into data-driven subtypes centered on core symptom dimensions, such as social-cognitive and neurocognitive deficits in ASD and SSD [99], and attentional, affective, and inhibitory dysfunction in ADHD [94, 95]. These findings suggest that behavioral phenotypes emphasize observable downstream consequences [31, 109], whereas neurosubtyping emphasizes latent dysfunction across distributed systems. Characterizing heterogeneity therefore requires a framework that accommodates cross-level correspondence, partial overlap, and non-linear coupling. More specific mappings require targeted features and explicit models. Accordingly, related multi-view learning and multimodal fusion frameworks may offer a useful strategy for disentangling these cross-level relationships. Methods such as joint independent component analysis, multimodal canonical correlation analysis, and independent vector analysis have been used to integrate MRI, EEG, genetic, and behavioral data to identify cross-modal covarying or modality-specific signatures [110]. Across SSD [111], BD [112, 113], and ADHD [114], these approaches have revealed multimodal patterns linked to symptom severity, cognition, and treatment response.

Translational pathways toward individualized assessment and intervention

Subtyping has clinical value to the extent that it informs decisions regarding intervention selection, treatment adaptation, and prognostic monitoring. Traditional efficacy studies focus on group-level averages, which can obscure marked individual differences in treatment response. Although studies reviewed above did not directly evaluate differential intervention response across identified subtypes, some intervention-informed subtyping research suggests that treatment response in ASD has a heterogeneous profile [115–117]. In autistic children receiving early intervention, longitudinal cluster analysis identified high-change and low-change groups, with the high-change group already showing lower symptom severity, higher cognitive functioning, and better adaptive behavior prior to intervention [116]. Similarly, in 55 children receiving the Early Start Denver Model, three cognitive outcome subtypes were identified, and 6-month gains in cognition and adaptive behavior already distinguished later trajectories [117]. Early intensive behavioral intervention research further showed that 6-month language change predicted later educational placement and contributed to risk stratification together with baseline language, cognition, symptom severity, and age [115]. Accordingly, a translationally meaningful stratification framework in ASD may need to move beyond static baseline phenotypes toward trajectory-informed models that incorporate early treatment-related change.

Beyond demonstrating heterogeneity in treatment response, a small but growing number of studies have begun to examine whether such variation can be anticipated before treatment initiation. In a trial of intranasal oxytocin in autistic children, pretreatment measures identified two subtypes, one of which showed a significantly higher response rate, while a low-cost marker set achieved performance close to that of the full multivariable model [118]. In children with ASD and comorbid anxiety, the five-factor model of personality differentiated the relative suitability of two approaches [119]. Social skills training research likewise suggests that pretreatment social-communication ability, language level, and parent/teacher involvement are closely related to intervention trajectories across different subtypes [120]. Taken together, these findings suggest that subgroup information may not only help explain post-treatment heterogeneity, but may also hold potential for pretreatment stratification and intervention matching.

Although these findings support individualized intervention, data-driven ASD subtyping remains largely proof-of-concept and still faces a substantial translational chasm. One limitation is that evidence for both internal and external validation remains limited, as discussed further in Section 3.3.2. Another limitation is that individual-level assignability remains insufficiently established, as many reported subtypes are defined by between-group differences rather than stable classification at the level of a single child. More fundamentally, most current subtype studies remain descriptive rather than prognostic. To be clinically useful, a subtype must predict intervention response, prognosis, or developmental course beyond cross-sectional group differences [115, 118].

Another practical barrier to translation is the cost and limited scalability of high-dimensional neuroimaging in routine clinical settings. A more pragmatic route is to derive low-cost proxy markers from readily accessible behavioral or clinical measures. Machine-learning approaches may help extract clinically relevant information from such measures, with eye-tracking providing one illustrative example. In ASD, GeoPref testing identified a more severe geometric-preference subtype, and machine-learning validation improved sensitivity while preserving high specificity [121]. In an intervention context, clustering of pretreatment clinical and eye-tracking features identified an oxytocin-responsive subtype, and a reduced ADOS-2 plus eye-tracking feature set approached full-model performance [118]. More broadly, deep-learning models trained on eye movements have detected executive dysfunction, visuospatial memory impairment, and affective bias across psychiatric conditions [122, 123]. Together, these findings suggest that clinically accessible measures may carry stratification-relevant information and could be leveraged to train scalable proxy markers for translational use. Furthermore, subtype frameworks should incorporate transdiagnostic dimensions, given shared patterns across ASD and other disorders [100]. Intervention studies can provide reverse mechanistic validation when a prespecified neural marker change accompanies behavioral improvement.

Key challenges and mitigation strategies

Sample composition and developmental stage heterogeneity

Developmental stage shapes brain organization and subtype structure. Many studies enroll broad age ranges (Fig. 2A, C) or treat age as a covariate, despite rapid remodeling in early childhood [124, 125]. Subtypes derived from broad age samples often differ in age distribution [33]. Moreover, sex imbalance is pervasive. Male to female ratios in ASD samples often range from 2.3:1 to 7.0:1, and one study reported 10.3:1 in ASD with 2.4:1 in TD [40], a difference of more than fourfold. Such imbalance can bias clustering and confound between group contrasts when controls are not sex matched. Finally, language and intellectual variation, comorbidity, and medication status can reduce subtype generalizability. These factors warrant stratification, matching, and sensitivity analyses, especially for comorbidity. Given meaningful variability in TD [15], ASD features should also be standardized relative to TD distributions to quantify deviation and disorder specificity [16].

Beyond the confounding factors noted above, a deeper methodological limitation lies in the cross-sectional framework itself. ASD is a neurodevelopmental condition whose heterogeneity is embedded in ongoing developmental change. Clinical presentation varies with age, and neural, cognitive, language, and social domains follow partially dissociable developmental trajectories [126, 127]. Take language heterogeneity in ASD as an example, longitudinal studies show that distinct language phenotypes diverge over time in vocabulary, grammar, and pragmatics, and speech-focused work has reported parallel differences in developmental trajectories and phenotype stability [128, 129]. Under these conditions, subtypes derived from a single time point may capture transient developmental states. Cognitive behavioral phenotypes identified in cross-sectional samples may reflect enduring cognitive stratification, developmental stage effects, or both [76, 79]. For instance, in studies spanning different age windows, subtype differences in cognitive performance do not always align with differences in core autistic symptom severity [79], suggesting that cognitive subtype structure may be jointly shaped by developmental level and neurocognitive burden. A related concern applies to neuroimaging-based subtyping. Developmental effects on CT and surface area have been repeatedly documented in autism, particularly in samples spanning childhood and adolescence [130]. Subtypes identified in pediatric cohorts may therefore conflate developmental stage effects with subtype-specific neurobiology [3, 4]. Interpretation of ASD subtypes requires a distinction between trait-like features and developmentally contingent features. Longitudinal designs and trajectory-based subtyping are necessary to test the stability of subtype membership, characterize developmental change, and evaluate prognostic or treatment-related relevance [126, 131].

Methodological heterogeneity and reproducibility

Subtype reproducibility requires validation across samples, cohorts, and studies. Within ABIDE, two fMRI studies of the same pediatric sample identified two SN-related subtypes using k-means clustering on different dynamic features, although the associated symptom dimensions differed across studies, indicating partial consistency at the neuroimaging level with less stable clinical mapping [32, 60]. In contrast, two additional sMRI studies based on different structural features yielded different subtype solutions, yet both linked GM-related abnormalities to lower intelligence and social-communication phenotypes, suggesting that some brain-behavior associations may be more reproducible than the subtype solutions themselves [47, 48]. Across datasets, a study in the EU-AIMS LEAP cohort identified five CT-based subtypes, including three with reduced CT and two with increased CT [52]. One reduced-CT subtype was associated with lower IQ and greater severity in core autistic symptoms, broadly echoing patterns reported in ABIDE MRI studies. Collectively, current evidence points to partial convergence rather than strict correspondence across subtype studies.

In addition to differences in sample composition, differences in imaging modality, feature construction, denoising and harmonization procedures, dimensionality reduction, clustering algorithms, and model order selection can all influence the resulting clustering solution [14–16]. With respect to clustering methods, in the currently reviewed study, some studies using k-means appear more likely to yield relatively simplified binary structures, whereas studies using hierarchical clustering more often identify separable patterns across brain regions or feature profiles. Such differences, however, should not be attributed to the clustering algorithm alone, as the other analytic factors noted above also jointly shape the final clustering solution [14–16]. Accordingly, robustness should be evaluated at multiple stages. Model selection addresses whether the chosen number of clusters or model order is appropriate, typically using indices such as the silhouette coefficient [44], the Davies-Bouldin index [45], the BIC [132], or the elbow criterion [133]. Internal stability evaluates whether a clustering solution remains consistent after bootstrap resampling, subsampling, feature perturbation, site perturbation, or repeated clustering. External validation evaluates whether the same subtype structure can be reproduced in independent cohorts, held-out sites, or cross-consortium datasets. These forms of evidence address distinct questions and should not be treated as interchangeable. Some studies have begun to report robustness-related evidence, including independent-cohort replication, leave-one-site validation, frozen-model transfer, and cross-consortium analysis (Tables 1–3) [43, 46, 68, 77, 95, 97]. However, many studies still rely primarily on model selection or downstream clinical associations [33, 37, 72, 78, 81]. Such analyses may support cluster separation or clinical relevance within a given dataset, but they do not establish stability under perturbation or reproducibility in external samples.

Future studies should report robustness evidence more routinely at each stage. At the model selection stage, the rationale for the selected number of clusters or model order should be stated explicitly, although these criteria should not be taken as evidence of stable subtypes. Internal stability can be quantified using agreement measures such as Adjusted Rand Index, normalized mutual information, the Jaccard index, or variation of information. External validation should test whether subtype membership and its associated clinical or neural correlates remain consistent across independent cohorts or sites. Where such evidence remains limited, dimensional or categorical-dimensional hybrid models may provide a more cautious account of ASD heterogeneity [15, 16].

Relations between categorical subtypes and dimensional continua

Some constructs show continuous structure across individuals, including transdiagnostic dimensions [100]. Hard classification can yield unstable boundary assignments and can obscure gradual developmental change. Dimensional models propose local clustering along continua in ASD [14]. The choice between categorical and dimensional representations should follow observed distributions and support falsifiable criteria. Regression to the mean after intervention for a subtype defined by between trial variability of the P100 supports a continuous interpretation [134]. N170 latency showed a bimodal distribution in a subset of participants and related to sensitive developmental periods and response to social interventions [33]. Future studies should report both categorical and dimensional fits, including Gaussian mixture models, continuum regression, and normative modeling, and compare external validity. Longitudinal data enable trajectory-based subtyping that can detect inflection points and separate developmental delay from stable heterogeneity. Hybrid approaches that extract interpretable dimensions and then apply probabilistic grouping can preserve individual variability while yielding clinically usable categories [18, 71]. Ultimately, neurosubtyping will be most informative when it yields clinically legible categories accompanied by continuous scores and uncertainty estimates, and when these outputs predict developmental trajectories and treatment response in prospective designs.

Conclusions

This review synthesizes recent advances in computational approaches for characterizing ASD heterogeneity across neural, behavioral, and transdiagnostic domains. Current evidence demonstrates that ASD heterogeneity follows structured and measurable neurobehavioral dimensions. Brain and behavioral subtyping methods capture these variations from complementary levels to reveal complex interactions. A specific clinical profile may originate from diverse neural mechanisms. Similarly, shared neural substrates can yield distinct behavioral outcomes across development. Although these algorithmic strategies advance mechanistic classification, critical challenges remain. High demographic variability and inconsistent methodologies currently limit the reproducibility of subtyping results. Furthermore, brain and behavior associations lack sufficient stability for immediate clinical application. Future progress depends on translating robust subtype profiles into actionable interventions. Achieving true precision medicine will require prospective empirical testing and seamless collaboration between researchers and clinicians.
