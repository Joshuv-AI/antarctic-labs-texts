Mindfulness therapy for patients with coronary heart disease: A systematic review and meta‐analysis
Authors: Weina Li, Xiaoqin Liao, Dandan Geng, Jiechao Yang, Hu Chen, Shuqin Hu, Mengqiao Dai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimCoronary heart disease (CHD) is a prevalent cardiovascular disease with high mortality rates worldwide. Patients with CHD often experience adverse psychological stress related to the disease's diagnosis, treatment and recovery phases. This stress can hurt sleep quality and overall quality of life. Mindfulness‐based interventions (MBIs) have been studied as a psychotherapeutic approach to alleviating the psychological stress associated with CHD. This study aimed to determine the effectives of MBIs for health outcomes in patients with CHD.MethodsA total of eight English‐language databases were searched, and eight relevant studies were included in the analysis. The included studies were assessed for literature quality, and data were extracted and analysed using Review Manager 5.3.ResultsA total of eight studies involving 802 participants were included in the analysis. Compared to control groups, MBIs significantly reduced anxiety, depression, perceived stress, and systolic blood pressure. However, there was no significant effect on diastolic blood pressure, quality of life or body mass index. One study reported that MBIs significantly improved sleep quality in patients with acute myocardial infarction after percutaneous coronary intervention but had no significant effect on body mass index.ConclusionMBIs had significant effects on anxiety and depression in patients with CHD, reduced perceived stress and were associated with reductions in systolic blood pressure and improvements in sleep quality. However, they did not significantly affect diastolic blood pressure, quality of life or body mass index.

INTRODUCTION

Effective management of coronary heart disease (CHD), a leading cause of death globally with significant health (GBD 2017 Disease and Injury Incidence and Prevalence Collaborators, 2018 and socioeconomic burden (Bauersachs et al., 2019; Berger et al., 2019), is crucial for improving quality (QoL) of life and life expectancy. Current treatment approaches often focus on medications, surgery and lifestyle modifications, but there is a growing recognition of the significant impact of mental health on CHD outcomes. (Knuuti et al., 2020). Recent studies show (Kang & Malvaso, 2023) a high prevalence of mental health issues, such as anxiety and depression, in patients with CHD. Addressing these psychological concerns is essential for a holistic approach to CHD management.

Stress, a common response to chronic illness like CHD, is a pathological process triggered by threats to homeostasis (Graham, 2003). It induces negative emotions such as anxiety, anger and depression, impacting biological processes and behaviours, contributing to pathophysiological changes and disease development (Chen et al., 2019; Dudek et al., 2021). Psychological stress increases sympathetic nerve activity, platelet aggregation and endothelial dysfunction, elevating cardiovascular disease risk (Ceci et al., 2021; Ghiadoni et al., 2000; Soysal et al., 2020). Additionally, stress can induce coronary artery constriction at atherosclerotic sites, exacerbating outcomes in CHD (Steptoe & Kivimäki, 2012).

Mindfulness, rooted in Buddhist philosophy, is defined as the deliberate and nonjudgmental awareness of present‐moment thoughts, feelings and bodily sensations (Kabat‐Zinn, 2003). Through training, individuals cultivate a heightened awareness of their internal experiences, fostering a nonjudgmental approach towards their thoughts and emotions (Perry‐Parrish et al., 2016). This approach has led to the development of mindfulness‐based interventions (MBIs) (Crane et al., 2017; Kabat‐Zinn, 2003; Segal et al., 2018) that have emerged as a promising strategy to address stress and improve mental well‐being across diverse populations.

In recent years, MBIs has emerged as a promising adjunctive treatment for various psychological disorders and chronic diseases. MBIs may improve CHD outcomes by promoting nonjudgmental awareness of thoughts and emotions, thereby reducing stress reactivity and enhancing emotional regulation (Loucks et al., 2022). Studies (Fjorback et al., 2011; Kabat‐Zinn & Hanh, 2009; Klainin‐Yobas et al., 2012) have shown its effectiveness in reducing the perception of pain severity, stress, anxiety and depression, leading to improved psychosocial adjustment and QoL for patients. While these studies demonstrate the potential of MBIs to improve mental well‐being and manage stress, the specific impact of MBIs on patients with CHD remains less explored. Therefore, investigating the effectiveness of MBIs specifically for this population is crucial. These systematic review and meta‐analysis aim to address this gap in knowledge by synthesizing the existing evidence on the effects of MBIs on health outcomes exclusively in patients with CHD.

METHODS

This study followed the PRISMA reporting framework (Moher et al., 2015) and was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the ID: CRD42023417589 on 26 April 2023.

Search strategy

We searched eight electronic databases (Embase, Ovid Medline, Cochrane, CINAHL, Web of Science, Ebsco, Science Direct and PubMed) from their inception up to April 2023. The search was limited to studies written in English. We used the following keywords and medical subject heading (MeSH) terms: mindfulness, mindful*, mindfulness intervention*, mindfulness‐based intervention*, mindfulness decompression therapy*, mindfulness‐based stress reduction*, mindfulness‐based cognitive therapy*, acceptance and commitment therapy*, MBCT*, MBS*, and Coronary Diseases, Coronary Diseases*, Disease, Coronary*, Diseases, Coronary*, Coronary Heart Disease*, Coronary Heart Diseases*, Disease, Coronary Heart*, Diseases, Coronary Heart*, Heart Disease, Coronary*, Heart Diseases, Coronary*. A sample search strategy in PubMed is illustrated in Table S1. Additionally, we manually screened the reference lists of relevant articles to identify any potential studies.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

The following criteria were used to determine eligibility for selection:

Types of studies

Only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating the effects of MBIs on interventions for patients with CHD were included.

Types of participants

The participants were adults (aged ≥18 years) who met the International Society of Cardiology and WHO clinical criteria for the diagnosis of CHD. This included patients about to undergo coronary intervention (PCI) and those who had undergone PCI confirmed by coronary angiography. Participants with comorbid psychiatric and/or other serious medical conditions were excluded.

Types of interventions

The interventions were based on Mindfulness‐Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) developed by Jon Kabat‐Zinn (Kabat‐Zinn, 2003) or Mindfulness‐Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) developed by Segal (Segal et al., 2018). Adaptations or variations of the mindfulness protocol (e.g. shortened or amended versions) or mindfulness meditation were also considered. There were no restrictions on the type, duration or mode of interventions. The control interventions could include no intervention, conventional treatment, health education or other stress reduction measures (e.g. relaxation therapy).

Types of outcome measures

The primary outcome indicators included anxiety (measured using validated anxiety scales or clinical diagnosis), depression (measured using validated depression scales or clinical diagnosis) and perceived stress (measured using a validated perceived stress scale). The secondary outcome indicators included blood pressure, body mass index (BMI), QoL and quality of sleep.

Search outcomes

Initially, we retrieved 872 studies, which were reduced to 612 studies after removing duplicates. After reviewing the titles and abstracts, 569 studies were excluded. Out of the remaining 43 studies, we excluded 35 studies after reading the full text for the following reasons: inappropriate outcomes (n = 6), mindfulness not being the major focus (n = 3), non‐RCT design (n = 15) and participants not exclusively having CHD (n = 11). Finally, we included eight studies in this meta‐analysis. The detailed screening process is shown in Figure 1.

Quality appraisal

Two reviewers independently assessed the eligibility of the studies using the Cochrane Collaboration tools (Higgins et al., 2011) and conducted a quality assessment of the included studies. Any disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer. The assessment tool included six specific domains: selection bias, performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias, reporting bias and other biases. Other biases encompassed participant recruitment, comparability of baselines and the reliability and validity of outcome measures. As a result, the overall risk of bias for each included study was categorized as low risk (low bias risk for all domains), high risk (high bias risk for one or more domains) or unclear risk (unclear bias risk for one or more domains).

Data abstraction

Two researchers independently screened the title and abstract of each article to evaluate its eligibility. Full‐text articles for all potentially eligible trials were obtained and screened for inclusion. For the included studies, two researchers independently extracted the following information: author, country, year of publication, participant characteristics (diagnosis, sample size, age and gender), intervention programme, intervention details (content, setting, number and length of sessions, delivery, frequency, time and duration), outcomes and follow‐up. The extraction results were cross‐checked, and any discrepancies were resolved by a third investigator.

Synthesis

Meta‐analysis was conducted using Review Manager Version 5.3 software. First, a heterogeneity test was performed using Review Manager Version 5.3 to statistically analyse the included literature. The χ2 test was employed to determine whether there was heterogeneity among the studies. If the p‐value was >0.1 and I2 was <50%, it indicated that the statistical heterogeneity among the studies was small, and a fixed‐effect model was chosen to combine the data. If the p‐value was <0.1 and I2 was ≥50%, it indicated that the statistical heterogeneity among the studies was large, and a random‐effect model was utilized to combine the data and conduct subgroup analysis. Continuous data on intervention effects were analysed by combining the standardized mean difference (SMD) of each study with 95% confidence intervals (CI) of the post‐intervention data.

According to literature recommendations (Sterne et al., 2011), we did not statistically assess publication bias by plotting funnel plots or conducting Egger tests because each outcome included fewer than 10 studies.

RESULTS

Characteristics of the studies

A total of eight RCTs were included in this review, published between 2013 and 2023 and conducted in Asia (six studies) and Europe (two studies). The studies involved a total of 802 participants, with 454 in the intervention group and a mean age ranging from 43.2 to 64.81 years (overall mean age ± SD: 54.13 ± 7.27 years), and 348 in the control group with a mean age ranging from 43.2 to 65.30 years (overall mean age ± SD: 54.44 ± 6.09 years). Intervention characteristics

The interventions in this review primarily consisted of positive meditation (3/8), MBSR (2/8) and MBCT (1/8). The intervention sessions ranged from 1 to 12 weeks, with session lengths varying from 30 to 120 min. The frequency of interventions ranged from every 1 day to every 2 weeks. Six studies reported on intervention settings, with interventions primarily conducted in hospitals (4/8) or via telephone for hospital or home sessions.

Results of quality appraisal

Two studies (Nasiri et al., 2020; Nyklíček et al., 2014) were considered to have an unclear risk of bias for random sequence generation due to the insufficient description of the randomization procedure. All but one study (Younge et al., 2015) had an unclear risk of bias for allocation concealment due to inadequate information. Risk of bias for blinding of participants and personnel, as well as blinding of outcome assessment, was also unclear for most studies. The risk of outcome data completeness and selection reporting bias was low for all studies. Regarding other biases, two studies were considered to have a high risk, one (Nyklíček et al., 2014) due to noncomparability at baseline and one (Parswani et al., 2013) due to inappropriate participant selection. Detailed information on the risk of bias assessment is shown in Figure 2. Therefore, we assessed two studies (Nyklíček et al., 2014; Parswani et al., 2013) as high risk, one (Younge et al., 2015) as low risk, and the remaining five (Demirci et al., 2023; Hou et al., 2019; Jang et al., 2018; Liang et al., 2019; Nasiri et al., 2020) as unclear risk. Intervention effects

Among the eight included studies, six (Hou et al., 2019; Jang et al., 2018; Liang et al., 2019; Nyklíček et al., 2014; Parswani et al., 2013; Younge et al., 2015) reported the effect of orthomolecular therapy on anxiety and depression in patients with CHD. Five studies (Hou et al., 2019; Nasiri et al., 2020; Nyklíček et al., 2014; Parswani et al., 2013; Younge et al., 2015) reported the effect of orthomolecular therapy on perceived stress in patients with CHD. Two studies (Parswani et al., 2013; Younge et al., 2015) reported the effect on blood pressure, two (Demirci et al., 2023; Younge et al., 2015) reported the effect on quality of life, one study (Parswani et al., 2013) reported the effect on body mass index and one study (Liang et al., 2019) reported the effect on sleep quality.

Primary outcome indicators

The pooled results showed that MBIs significantly reduced anxiety (SMD = −0.86, 95% CI [−1.33, −0.38], P = 0.004, I2 = 86%), depression (SMD = −0.73, 95% CI [−1.11, −0.35], P = 0.0001, I2 = 78%) and perceived stress (SMD = −0.64, 95% CI [−1.19, −0.09], P = 0.02, I2 = 87%) levels in patients with CHD compared to the control group. Secondary outcome indicators

The results of the meta‐analysis showed that MBIs were effective in reducing systolic blood pressure, with significant differences compared to the control group (SMD = −0.79, 95% CI [−1.33, −0.24], P = 0.005, I2 = 53%). However, there was no significant effect on diastolic blood pressure (SMD = −0.09, 95% CI [−0.31, 0.13], P = 0.44, I2 = 0%) or quality of life (SMD = −0.43, 95% CI [−0.12, 0.99], P = 0.13, I2 = 73%). One study reported that MBSR improved sleep quality in AMI patients after PCI (P < 0.05) (Liang et al., 2019) but had no significant effect on BMI (Parswani et al., 2013). Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding the outcome indicators of anxiety, depression, perceived stress and systolic blood pressure from each study. The results showed that the combined effect sizes did not change significantly, indicating that the meta‐analysis results were generally stable.

DISCUSSION

In this meta‐analysis, eight studies were included, and the risk of bias varied widely among them, with one study being low risk, five being moderate risk and two being high risk. The main sources of bias were related to incomplete and uncritical design protocols in the specific RCTs of the included studies. The blinding of participants, investigators and outcome measures was uncertain in most studies due to the nature of psychological interventions.

This meta‐analysis suggests that MBIs can be a valuable tool for improving mental well‐being in patients with CHD. Our findings align with previous research in cardiovascular disease, demonstrating significant reductions in anxiety, depression and perceived stress in CHD patients following MBIs (Abbott et al., 2014). These positive effects can be explained by several potential mechanisms explored in the field of mindfulness research (Coffey et al., 2010; Shapiro et al., 2006). First, mindfulness training cultivates nonjudgmental awareness, fostering a detached observation of thoughts and emotions. This allows CHD patients to de‐identify from negative thought patterns and emotional reactivity, potentially reducing anxiety and fear associated with their condition. Second, MBIs equip individuals with skills to manage stress responses more effectively. Chronic stress is a significant burden for CHD patients and can worsen cardiovascular health (Lagraauw et al., 2015). By promoting relaxation techniques and stress awareness, MBIs may help patients cope with stress more effectively, leading to improved emotional well‐being and potentially even cardiovascular health outcomes. Third, MBSR can enhance emotional regulation skills, allowing patients to experience difficult emotions with greater acceptance and composure. This improved emotional regulation can be particularly beneficial for managing the emotional challenges associated with a chronic illness like CHD. While our study and others (Abbott et al., 2014) show positive effects on anxiety, another meta‐analysis (Zou et al., 2021) reported no significant effect of MBIs on anxiety in CHD patients. This discrepancy may be attributed to variations in the type of intervention used, suggesting that different MBI programmes may have different effects. Further research is needed to explore these nuances and optimize MBI protocols for CHD patients.

Our findings align with some previous research (Zou et al., 2021), demonstrating that MBIs can significantly reduce systolic blood pressure in patients with CHD. However, the impact on blood pressure may be influenced by factors like the specific MBI programme and baseline medication use. For instance, Conversano et al. (Conversano et al., 2021) reported that MBSR was more effective in lowering diastolic blood pressure in hypertensive patients, and the effect on systolic blood pressure was more pronounced when a higher percentage of subjects were already taking antihypertensive medication. These findings suggest that MBIs might have an additive effect alongside existing blood pressure management strategies. Further research is needed to explore these nuances and understand the mechanisms by which MBIs influence blood pressure in CHD patients.

Regarding secondary outcome indicators, the present meta‐analysis did not provide sufficient evidence for a significant effect of MBIs on improving quality of life and BMI, which aligns with the findings of two other meta‐analyses (Abbott et al., 2014; Sosa‐Cordobés et al., 2022). This may be due to the primary focus of mindfulness therapy on psychosocial issues rather than physiological outcomes.

Although one study reported improved sleep quality in AMI patients after PCI with MBSR, another study found no significant effect on sleep quality in a sleep‐disordered population compared to specific and non‐specific active control groups (Rusch et al., 2019). This suggests that MBIs may have a positive impact on sleep quality, but further research is needed to establish conclusive evidence.

Limitations and future directions

There are several limitations to this review. First, the number of included studies was small, and the sample sizes were insufficient. The search was limited to mainstream databases in English, potentially missing studies in other languages or published in nonmainstream databases, which could introduce publication bias. Second, all included studies were conducted in Asian or European countries, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Third, most studies lacked long‐term follow‐up, with only one study conducting a 3‐month post‐intervention follow‐up. Therefore, the long‐term effects of MBIs on CHD remain unclear.

CONCLUSION

This meta‐analysis confirms the significant effects of MBIs on psychological issues in patients with CHD. Future studies should consider extending the duration of intervention and follow‐up to more effectively evaluate the long‐term effects of MBIs on CHD patients. Additionally, multicentre, large‐sample, high‐quality RCTs are recommended to validate the effects of MBIs on patients with CHD, providing reliable evidence‐based medical evidence for clinical application.

MBIs can improve anxiety and depression, reduce perceived stress, stabilize blood pressure levels, lower systolic blood pressure and improve sleep quality in patients with CHD. However, no significant effects were found on diastolic blood pressure, BMI or quality of life. This suggests that MBIs have a significant impact not only on psychological indicators but also on physiological indicators in CHD patients. Further research should focus on analysing additional physiological indicators to provide a comprehensive understanding of the effects of MBIs on CHD patients.

MBIs have garnered attention due to their potential relevance in improving CHD in clinical practice. A systematic review and meta‐analysis results indicate that MBIs can effectively improve anxiety and depression, reduce perceived stress, stabilize blood pressure levels, lower systolic blood pressure and enhance sleep quality among patients with CHD. However, no significant effects were observed in terms of diastolic blood pressure, BMI or quality of life. These findings suggest that MBI has the potential to address both the physical and psychological aspects of CHD, thereby improving patient treatment and nursing outcomes and overall cardiovascular health. Therefore, MBI can be used as a complementary approach to conventional psychological nursing care for CHD. To further validate the effects of MBIs on CHD patients, future research should encompass multicentre, large‐sample and high‐quality RCTs. Additionally, extending the duration of the intervention would allow for a more comprehensive evaluation of the long‐term effects of MBIs on individuals with CHD.
