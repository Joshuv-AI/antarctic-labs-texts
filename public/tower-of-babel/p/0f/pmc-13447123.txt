Adverse outcomes between VMAT2 and anticholinergics in tardive dyskinesia: A target trial emulation
Authors: Tien‐Wei Hsu, Chih‐Wei Hsu, Ping‐Tao Tseng, Yi‐Ya Fang, Chuo‐Yu Lee, Yu‐Hsuan Lin, Brendon Stubbs, Trevor Thompson, Andre F Carvalho, Yu‐Chen Kao, Jen‐Ping Chen, Fu‐Chi Yang, Chia‐Kuang Tsai, Chih‐Sung Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimVesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) inhibitors have been approved for the treatment of tardive dyskinesia (TD), whereas anticholinergic agents are still widely used for this condition. This study aimed to compare the risk of major clinical adverse outcomes among patients with TD treated with VMAT2 inhibitors versus anticholinergic agents.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used the TriNetX collaborative network, which aggregates de‐identified electronic health records (EHRs) across the United States. Adults aged≧18 years with TD who initiated anticholinergic agents or VMAT2 inhibitors for the first time between 2019 and 2025 were included. We applied a target trial emulation framework under the intention‐to‐treat principle. 1:1 propensity score matching was applied. Cox hazard regression was used to assess the primary outcomes: incident fall, injury, fracture, and mortality, and secondary outcomes: incident delirium, dementia, and arrythmia.ResultsAfter propensity score matching, 2749 patients (mean age: 59 years; 34% male) were included in each treatment group with well‐balanced demographics. Compared with anticholinergic therapy, VMAT2 inhibitors were associated with significantly lower risks of fall (HR = 0.83, 95% CI = 0.73–0.93), injury (HR = 0.77, 95% CI = 0.71–0.85), fracture (HR = 0.82, 95 % CI = 0.72–0.94), and mortality (HR = 0.71, 95% CI = 0.58–0.87). Secondary outcomes also favored VMAT2 inhibitors, with reduced risks of delirium (HR = 0.39, 95% CI = 0.29–0.52), dementia (HR = 0.55, 95 % CI = 0.45–0.69), and arrhythmia (HR = 0.72, 95% CI = 0.60–0.85).ConclusionThis real‐world study suggests that VMAT2 inhibitors may offer a safer therapeutic option than anticholinergic agents for TD management, supporting their potential clinical advantage and the need for further long‐term validation.Keywords: anticholinergic agents, fall, mortality, tardive dyskinesia, vesicular monoamine transporter 2 inhibitor

Tardive dyskinesia (TD) belongs to the spectrum of extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) that may emerge following exposure to dopamine receptor‐blocking agents (DRBAs), particularly antipsychotics. Unlike acute EPSs such as drug‐induced Parkinsonism or acute dystonia, TD is considered a tardive syndrome characterized by delayed onset and potentially persistent abnormal movements. TD usually develops after at least 3 months of DRBA exposure, though in vulnerable populations such as older adults, symptoms may appear earlier. 1 TD is typically characterized by the insidious onset of involuntary rhythmic, repetitive, stereotypic movements of the face, mouth, and tongue, which may extend to the trunk and extremities. 2 Although the precise mechanisms underlying TD remain unclear, several hypotheses have been proposed. Dopamine receptor hypersensitivity is the most widely cited, suggesting that chronic DRBA exposure leads to compensatory upregulation of striatal D2 receptors. 3 Additional mechanisms include GABAergic neuronal dysfunction within the basal ganglia, oxidative stress‐related striatal neurodegeneration, and maladaptive cortical–striatal plasticity contributing to abnormal motor encoding. 4 A meta‐analytic work (41 studies, 11,493 participants) reported that the global mean prevalence of TD was 25.3% among patients receiving antipsychotics. 5 Given its chronic course, TD may persist even after discontinuation of antipsychotics, causing significant impairment in daily functioning and quality of life. 6 , 7

Anticholinergic drugs have traditionally been prescribed to manage EPSs, particularly drug‐induced Parkinsonism and acute dystonia. 8 Importantly, anticholinergics are not considered evidence‐based treatments for TD and may even worsen tardive symptoms in some cases. A 2018 Cochrane review concluded that there is insufficient evidence to support the use of anticholinergics in the management of TD. 9 , 10 Although treatment guidelines advise against their use in patients with TD, anticholinergic drugs are still frequently prescribed in practice. 11 This is often because reducing or switching antipsychotics is not always feasible for maintaining psychiatric stability, and anticholinergics may already be co‐prescribed for other EPSs. In recent years, vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) inhibitors have emerged as the first evidence‐based pharmacological options for TD. 12 Valbenazine and deutetrabenazine, a deuterated tetrabenazine analogue requiring dose titration, have both demonstrated efficacy in randomized controlled trials. 13 , 14 , 15 These agents represent a major advance in TD treatment; however, their use may still be restricted by cost and access, including limited insurance coverage, prior authorization requirements, specialty pharmacy distribution restrictions, and variability in prescriber familiarity or availability. Such access‐related barriers may influence real‐world treatment allocation and could introduce potential selection bias in observational studies.

Both anticholinergic drugs and VMAT2 inhibitors are associated with adverse effects that require careful monitoring. Anticholinergic burden is particularly concerning in vulnerable populations, as it has been linked to falls, delirium, and cognitive decline. 16 Similarly, VMAT2 inhibitors carry risks such as somnolence, depression, and drug‐induced parkinsonism. 17 These safety concerns underscore the importance of judicious prescribing and highlight the need to understand comparative safety in real‐world practice. To address this issue, we conducted a real‐world cohort study using the TriNetX US collaborative network, a target trial emulation framework to compare several safety outcomes, including incidence of fall, injury, fracture, and mortality of VMAT2 inhibitors versus anticholinergic drugs in patients with TD.

Methods

Data source

Data were obtained from TriNetX, a large federated health research network that integrates deidentified EHRs from more than ~275 million patients across academic medical centers, specialty physician practices, and community hospitals worldwide. 18 The EHR data were collected for clinical practice and administrative purposes, with optional linkage to administrative claims when available. 18 Available data elements include demographic characteristics, diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory tests, genomics, and health care utilization. Importantly, data are acquired in near real time through periodic synchronizations, ensuring that the network reflects up‐to‐date clinical activity. For this analysis, we used the TriNetX US Collaborative Network, the major TriNetX dataset, and a federated EHR platform comprising data from 72 health care organizations across the United States. All diagnoses were identified using ICD‐10 codes, and medications were identified using RxNorm codes. The Kaohsiung Medical University Hospital Institutional Review Board (KMUHIRB‐E(I)‐20250199) approved the research protocol and exempted informed consent. This study followed the TARGET Statement 19 and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies (Table S1). As the TriNetX database is continuously updated in real time, data were retrieved on two occasions (September 6, 2025, and November 16, 2025), which may account for minor discrepancies in cohort sizes.

Study design, cohort, and treatment strategies

We applied a target trial emulation framework (Table S2) to compare the effectiveness of the VMAT2 inhibitor versus anticholinergic drugs in patients with TD on several important clinical outcomes. This approach aims to emulate an RCT using observational data (see Table S2 for the protocol of the target trials). Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older who initiated study drugs for the first time between 1 January 2019 and 30 June 2025. This window was chosen to ensure that both VMAT2 inhibitors approved for TD in the United States were available in routine practice (valbenazine received FDA approval on April 11, 2017, and deutetrabenazine on August 30, 2017) while capturing contemporary prescribing and follow‐up. Two treatment strategies were defined at baseline: (i) initiating FDA‐approved VMAT2 inhibitors (valbenazine or deutetrabenazine); and (ii) initiating anticholinergic drugs (trihexyphenidyl, procyclidine, benztropine, or biperiden). We adopted a new‐user, active‐comparator cohort design within a target trial emulation framework. To identify new users, patients were required to have no recorded prescriptions for either VMAT2 inhibitors or anticholinergic agents during a prespecified washout period of at least 12 months prior to cohort entry. We also excluded patients in either cohort with prior use of tetrabenazine, another VMAT2 inhibitor without FDA approval. Both strategies were analyzed under the intention‐to‐treat principle: once a patient initiated either therapy, they remained in that cohort for all subsequent analyses, regardless of treatment discontinuation or switching. Treatment initiation was defined as receipt of at least one prescription. Eligibility, treatment assignment, and start of follow‐up were aligned at time zero (the date of first prescription), consistent with a target trial design.

Outcomes

The outcomes of interest were established based on a combination of evidence from the literature review, clinical expertise, and their availability within the dataset. The primary outcomes were fall, injury, fracture, and mortality. The secondary outcomes were delirium, dementia, and arrhythmia. Mortality was ascertained from the HER and administrative claims, and the other outcomes were identified using validated ICD‐10 codes. Patients who were alive at the last follow‐up were censored at that date. Each eligible patient was followed from the index date (initiation of a VMAT2 inhibitor or an anticholinergic drug) until death, loss to follow‐up, or June 30, 2025, whichever occurred first.

Emulation of the target trials

We included covariates identified as potentially important prognostic and predictive factors based on a combination of literature review, clinical expertise, and their availability within the dataset. The covariates included demographic characteristics (age, sex, race/ethnicity), neuropsychiatric conditions (substance use disorders, schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders, mood and anxiety disorders, suicide attempt, intellectual and developmental disorders), comorbidities related to metabolic syndrome (diabetes, thyroid disorders, obesity, circulatory diseases), conditions associated with fall and fracture risk (osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, gait and balance disorders, musculoskeletal conditions), dementia‐related diagnoses, concurrent medication use (antidepressants, antipsychotics, anticonvulsants, antiparkinsonian agents, cardiovascular drugs), and frequency of outpatient visits.

Propensity score matching was then employed to balance baseline covariates and emulate the randomization process. A logistic regression model estimated the probability of assignment to the VMAT2 inhibitor versus the anticholinergic cohort. Using the TriNetX built‐in analytics, 1:1 greedy nearest‐neighbor matching without replacement was performed with a caliper of 0.1 pooled standard deviations. To mitigate order dependence inherent to greedy matching, TriNetX randomly shuffles the order of patients in each cohort prior to matching. Patients from the smaller cohort were matched to those in the larger cohort with comparable scores. A standardized mean difference <0.1 was considered indicative of adequate balance. The details of the covariates are listed in Table S3.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were reported as means with SDs or medians with IQRs, as appropriate. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Time‐to‐event outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between treatment groups were assessed with the log‐rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models, and the proportional hazard assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals. For non‐fatal outcomes, patients were censored at death (if recorded) or at the last available follow‐up, consistent with the TriNetX platform's time‐to‐event analyses. Primary analyses were conducted in the overall matched cohort, and subgroup analyses were performed by age (<65 vs. ≥65 years), sex (male vs. female), and type of the VMAT2 inhibitor (valbenazine vs. deutetrabenazine). These subgroup analyses were prespecified to evaluate the consistency of treatment effects.

To assess the robustness of the findings, we conducted several sensitivity analyses: (i) excluding outcomes occurring within the first 30 days after treatment initiation; (ii) restricting the cohort to patients with ≥3 prescriptions; (iii) limiting follow‐up to a fixed 3‐year duration; and (iv) restricting analyses to calendar time between 2022 and 2025. The restriction to patients with ≥3 prescriptions was intended to approximate sustained treatment exposure and to evaluate whether the primary findings were driven primarily by short‐term treatment initiation, in light of limitations in medication supply and adherence data within TriNetX. In addition, we evaluated negative‐control outcomes (benign neoplasms of the skin, hearing loss, psoriasis, gastric ulcer, and osteoarthritis) to test for potential residual confounding. All statistical analyses were performed using the TriNetX Analytics Platform and R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two‐sided p‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 3696 patients with TD initiated VMAT2 inhibitors, and 5816 patients who received anticholinergic therapy were identified. After applying predefined exclusion criteria and 1:1 propensity score matching, 2749 patients were included in each group for analysis (Table 1). All baseline demographics and clinical characteristics were well balanced between the two groups.

Comparative safety outcomes

Compared with anticholinergic therapy (Fig. 1), VMAT2 inhibitors were associated with a significantly lower risk of fall (hazard ratio [HR]: 0.83, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.73 to 0.93), injury (HR: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.71 to 0.85), fracture (HR: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.72 to 0.94), and mortality (HR: 0.71, 95% CI: 0.58 to 0.87). Secondary outcomes, including delirium (HR: 0.39, 95% CI: 0.29 to 0.52), dementia (HR: 0.55, 95% CI: 0.45 to 0.69), and arrhythmia (HR: 0.72, 95% CI: 0.60 to 0.85), similarly favored VMAT2 inhibitors.

Subgroup analyses

In the age‐stratified analysis (Fig. 2A), the protective associations of VMAT2 inhibitors persisted in both younger (<65 years) and older (≥65 years) patients across most outcomes. For falls and injuries, both age groups demonstrated significantly reduced risks, with HRs ranging from 0.77 to 0.86 in younger patients and 0.78 to 0.80 in older patients. A similar trend was observed for mortality, delirium, dementia, and arrhythmia, where the use of VMAT2 inhibitors was associated with substantial reductions in risk in both age strata. For fractures, however, the associations did not reach statistical significance in either age group, with HRs of 0.85 (95% CI, 0.70–1.03) for those <65 years and 0.85 (95% CI, 0.72–1.01) for those ≥65 years.

Anti‐Ch, anticholinergic; CI, confidence interval; Events_A, number of events in the anticholinergic group; Events_V, number of events in the VMAT2 group; HR, hazard ratio; Total_A, total number in the anticholinergic group; Total_V, total number in the VMAT2 group; VMAT2, vesicular monoamine transporter 2.

In the sex‐stratified analysis (Fig. 2B), VMAT2 inhibitors were likewise associated with lower risks across outcomes for both males and females, although the magnitude of risk reduction varied. The protective effects were particularly consistent among females, with significant reductions observed for falls, injuries, fractures, mortality, delirium, dementia, and arrhythmia. In males, significant reductions were seen for falls, injuries, and delirium, whereas the associations with fracture, mortality, dementia, and arrhythmia did not reach statistical significance, though the point estimates still favored VMAT2 inhibitors.

Comparative effectiveness of valbenazine and deutetrabenazine

When examined separately, both valbenazine and deutetrabenazine overall showed protective effects compared with anticholinergic therapy across most outcomes (Fig. 3). However, although both therapies showed quantitatively similar effects for fractures and mortality, valbenazine did not reach statistical significance for fracture (HR: 0.93, 95% CI: 0.79–1.09), and deutetrabenazine did not reach statistical significance for mortality (HR: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.57–1.03). Regarding arrhythmia, neither drug reached statistical significance (valbenazine, HR: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.65–1.00; deutetrabenazine, HR: 0.80, 95% CI: 0.61–1.04).

Negative‐control analyses revealed no significant difference between the VMAT2 inhibitor and anticholinergic therapy on benign neoplasm of skin, hearing loss, psoriasis, gastric ulcer, or osteoarthritis (Fig. 4).

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted four sensitivity analyses, including: (i) exclusion of events occurring within 30 days; (ii) restriction of the cohort to patients with ≥3 prescriptions; (iii) limitation follow‐up to a 3‐year duration; and (iv) restriction of the study period to 2022–2025 (Fig. S1–S4). Compared with anticholinergic therapy, VMAT2 inhibitors were still associated with significantly lower risks across all outcomes in the four sensitivity analyses, except mortality (HR: 0.83, 95% CI: 0.68–1.02) in sensitivity analysis 1, fracture (HR: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.71–1.00) and mortality (HR: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.59–1.00) in sensitivity analysis 2, arrythmia (HR: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.69–1.02) in sensitivity analysis 3, and fracture (HR: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.71–1.03) and mortality (HR: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.58–1.02) in sensitivity analysis 4.

Discussion

In this large real‐world cohort study comparing VMAT2 inhibitors with anticholinergic therapy for patients with TD, we found that VMAT2 inhibitors were consistently associated with lower risks of multiple adverse outcomes, including falls, injuries, fractures, mortality, delirium, dementia, and arrhythmia. Similar patterns of association were observed across prespecified age and sex subgroups, although some subgroup estimates were imprecise and, in certain cases, did not reach conventional statistical significance. Notably, fracture and mortality risk reductions were at the margin of statistical significance in certain subgroups. When valbenazine and deutetrabenazine were analyzed separately, valbenazine showed only a marginally significant trend toward lower fracture risk, whereas deutetrabenazine showed a marginally significant trend toward lower mortality risk.

Our findings align with and extend prior evidence suggesting that VMAT2 inhibitors may offer a safer therapeutic profile than anticholinergic agents in patients with TD. Patients with TD are typically older, receive multiple medications (particularly those with central nervous system properties), impair balance, and have comorbid psychiatric disorders – all of which contribute to a high risk of falls, injury, and fracture. 20 In addition to direct injuries and the increased mortality risk associated with post‐injury immobility, TD‐related symptoms such as difficulty swallowing lead to malnutrition or aspiration pneumonia, as well as reduced ability for self‐care, may also contribute to higher mortality. 20 , 21 Anticholinergic agents pose an even greater risk of falls and mortality in patients with TD by causing sedation, blurred vision, orthostatic hypotension, and cognitive impairment, which together impair balance and alertness. 11 In contrast, VMAT2 inhibitors are more selective in modulating dopaminergic transmission and generally have a more favorable safety profile, and they may provide better therapeutic effects on TD symptoms, thereby improving swallowing difficulties and motor imbalance, leading to a lower impact on fall and mortality risk. 22

Similarly, the lower risks of delirium, dementia, and arrhythmia also reflect the safety advantages of VMAT2 inhibitors compared with anticholinergic agents. Particularly, the risks of delirium and dementia showed the largest effect sizes and the most robust results among the seven outcomes analyzed, consistent across all sensitivity and subgroup analyses. Anticholinergic agents might provoke delirium and contribute to dementia by blocking central muscarinic receptors, thereby disrupting cholinergic neurotransmission essential for attention, memory, and cognitive processing. Chronic exposure to strong anticholinergic activity has been associated with structural brain changes, including cortical atrophy and reduced glucose metabolism in the hippocampus and temporal regions, which may accelerate neurodegeneration. 23 Regarding VMAT2 inhibitors, previous literature suggested inhibition of synaptic monoamines might lead to monoamine deficiency syndrome, with symptoms including irritability, anxiety, apathy, somnolence, and cognitive slowing. 24 In large phase 3 clinical trials, cognition‐related adverse events were rarely reported, whereas anxiety and depression did occur. 13 , 14 , 15 Our findings complement this by providing real‐world information on dementia and delirium.

Valbenazine and deutetrabenazine are both selective VMAT2 inhibitors used for the treatment of TD, but they differ in several pharmacological aspects. Valbenazine is highly selective for VMAT2 with minimal off‐target receptor activity, whereas deutetrabenazine's active metabolites exhibit broader dopaminergic and serotonergic effects. 22 , 25 Structurally, deutetrabenazine is a deuterated form of tetrabenazine, which slows its metabolism and prolongs its half‐life, while valbenazine is a prodrug designed to produce a more stable active metabolite profile. 22 , 25 However, in our analysis, no significant differences were observed between the two drugs across the seven outcomes, with only mortality, fracture, and arrhythmia showing a marginally significant trend. Among these, fracture and mortality were relatively less robust outcomes in our subgroup and sensitivity analyses, likely because they are indirect events – for example, a fall does not always result in a fracture, and an injury does not necessarily lead to death. We may need longer follow‐up periods and more precise study designs to address these issues.

Our study has limitations. First, on the TriNetX platform, we are unable to directly obtain the severity of TD symptoms or the therapeutic effects of medications. Second, the outcomes and diagnoses were derived from an electronic database rather than direct records or structured diagnostic interviews, and the accuracy of outcomes and diagnoses may be limited. Third, for non‐fatal outcomes, death may act as a competing event. Our primary analyses treated death as censoring (as implemented in the TriNetX time‐to‐event workflow), which assumes non‐informative censoring and may overestimate cumulative incidence when mortality differs between groups; competing‐risk methods would be informative in future work. Fourth, anticholinergics may have been initiated for co‐occurring EPSs – most notably drug‐induced parkinsonism – rather than for TD itself. Because drug‐induced parkinsonism is associated with gait instability, functional impairment, and increased fall risk, this clinical context may introduce confounding by indication and could partially explain observed safety differences. We attempted to mitigate this by balancing measurable neurologic comorbidities and concurrent antiparkinsonian medication use via propensity score matching; however, residual confounding due to unmeasured EPS severity likely remains. Fifth, certain clinical data, such as lifestyle behaviors including physical activity, dietary habits, smoking/alcohol use, and environmental factors, could not be accessed. Nevertheless, we performed propensity score matching using a wide range of carefully selected prognostic and predictive factors, which might minimize the concerns of residual confounding. Finally, the generalizability of our findings may be limited because VMAT2 inhibitors are not yet widely available or reimbursed in many countries, and their cost may restrict real‐world utilization outside the United States.

In conclusion, this large real‐world study provides evidence that VMAT2 inhibitors are associated with lower risks of several clinically important adverse outcomes compared with anticholinergic agents in patients with TD. These findings highlight the potential clinical advantage of VMAT2 inhibitors as safer therapeutic options for TD management. Future research with longer follow‐up and more detailed clinical assessments is warranted to confirm these results and guide individualized treatment strategies that optimize both safety and functional outcomes in this vulnerable population.
