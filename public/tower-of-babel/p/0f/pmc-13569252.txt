Arts informed indigenous research: Listening to Indigenous Peoples' knowledges in health professions education
Authors: Karen Adams, Lynne Stuart, Ali Drummond, Linda Deravin, Olivia Bonanno, Vicki Saunders
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextThe 2007 United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples affirms Indigenous Peoples' right to maintain, control, protect and develop their knowledge systems, including knowledges of sciences and technologies of medicine and health. It could, however, be argued that this right is typically not upheld in health professions education (HPE), as the broader HPE community perceives dominant colonial ideologies as supreme. Yet, Indigenous Peoples' knowledges promote futurity. They can protect communities and promote healing for everyone from colonising harms. However, engaging with Indigenous Peoples' knowledges in HPE is not straightforward, as a contested cultural interface exists between knowledge systems. This article shares how Arts Informed Indigenous Research (AIIR) can illuminate ways to grow Indigenous knowledges into HPE. We outline the context and problem, describe AIIR's conceptual frame and praxis and share a praxis example.ApproachInformed by arts‐based research paradigms, AIIR embeds and embodies an Indigenist research agenda. AIIR also incorporates storywork drawing attention to storying complexity. Indigenous Peoples engage AIIR to deeply investigate research questions and solve complex issues often caused by colonisation. Importantly, AIIR is Indigenous Peoples led and engages knowledge holder diversity through sensory and symbolic forms that create reflection. For many research methods, researchers perform data analysis. However, in AIIR knowledge holders make meaning through creativity, searching for ideas and interrelated understanding. Researchers revere created knowledges with honour and show reciprocity by strengthening story impact.ConclusionIncorporating Indigenous knowledge within HPE is not straightforward, as colonial ideologies continue to dominate the field. However, AIIR can support engagement between divergent western and Indigenous knowledge systems by creating ethical, critically reflective and relational spaces that promote anti‐colonial listening. Additionally, AIIR offers methods to generate insights into ways the broader HPE community can relinquish colonial power to make space in HPE for Indigenous Peoples' knowledges of futurity.

INTRODUCTION

By way of introduction, we are a collective of Indigenous researchers with experience in health professions education (HPE) and Arts Informed Indigenous Research (AIIR) and who are Wiradjuri, Wergaia and Wemba‐Wemba, Mandandanji, Gunggari, Meriam, Erubam and Wuthathi (see Fig. 1). In this article, we outline how AIIR can illuminate ways to grow Indigenous Peoples' knowledges and futurity into HPE. The plural term knowledges recognises the diversity and heterogeneity of Indigenous knowledges as a strength. 1 We also draw on the United Nations' understanding of Indigenous Peoples as those who self‐identify with unique social, cultural, economic and political characteristics as the descendants of peoples who inhabited a country or region at the time of arrival of people from different cultures or ethnicities. 2 Readers please be advised this article contains names and images of people who have passed on.

The roots of HPE lie deep in coloniality. 3 Empires and their colonial processes involving resource extraction and wealth hoarding have accelerated global ruination, 4 particularly, gross inequity, political instability and climate change. 4 Various forms of colonisation exist, and many Indigenous Peoples experience the ongoing violence of settler colonisation. Here, settlers arrive and violently replace Indigenous Peoples' knowledges, lives and livelihoods:

‘the logic of elimination can include officially encouraged miscegenation, the breaking‐down of native title into alienable individual freeholds, native citizenship, child abduction, religious conversion, resocialization in total institutions such as missions or boarding schools, and a whole range of cognate biocultural assimilations. All these strategies, including frontier homicide, are characteristic of settler colonialism’. 5 (p.388)

The colonial processes also exclude Indigenous Peoples' knowledges from most contemporary health professions.

The reason for the exclusion is because colonialism reproduces the institutions and associated power relations of its empire, including knowledge systems. 6 Colonial ideology dismisses Indigenous Peoples' knowledges as inferior and not useful for the growth of their empire and settler‐colonial agendas. Simultaneously, colonial logic dismisses the violence of colonial knowledge systems, assuming colonial ways of vetting the value of knowledges and ways of knowing are supreme. 6 The colonial logic sees the destruction of Indigenous Peoples' knowledges and ways of knowing as human progress, not as assimilation under the colonial empire. 7

However, Indigenous Peoples' knowledges and ways of knowing within HPE serve the futurity of Indigenous Peoples and our Communities. Our ways of knowing and knowledges protect and heal us from the harms of colonialism, the leading morbidity and mortality burden for Indigenous Peoples. 8 These knowledges can also protect and promote healing for all people from the harms produced by colonising processes. 8 The United Nations recognises that Indigenous Peoples hold knowledges that contribute to sustained and equitable development and proper management of the environment. 9 It further recognises that Indigenous Peoples have the right to maintain, control, protect and develop our knowledges, including sciences and technologies of medicine and health. 9 Even so, within HPE Indigenous Peoples' knowledges are frequently rendered silent, invisible, marginalised or dismissed as invalid. 10 These issues are causing significant problems for HPE, limiting its capacity to create healing and well‐being.

THE PROBLEM

The following words in the Bidjara language of Southeast Queensland, Australia, remind us of the problems caused by hearing and not listening.

Binan Goonj ‐ they hear but don't listen 11

The act of hearing can be understood as passive exposure to sound. 12 Listening, in contrast, involves more meaningful and responsive engagement with sound and the holistic context for it. 12 Within Indigenous Communities, listening is crucial, for instance ‘In our Aboriginal way, we learnt to listen from our earliest days. We could not live good and useful lives unless we listened’. 13 However, not everyone listens in the same way. This is because listening is influenced by our learnt worldview, such as the teachings we have received about why we listen, how we listen, when we listen and what we think is worth listening to (or not). 14

Within the HPE community, Indigenous knowledges are often heard but rarely truly listened to. This is because the original construction of almost all HPE placed value on listening to Western and colonial worldviews and stories to inform its design. 15 Notably absent was any listening to Indigenous Peoples' knowledges. No one invited Indigenous Peoples to contribute to the vision for HPE, nor its theories, philosophies, pedagogies, educative structures or scopes of practice. The context of HPE was essentially not constructed for Indigenous Peoples, and in effect was designed to keep Indigenous Peoples out. 16

Today, Indigenous Peoples are invited into HPE; however, we are expected to assimilate into HPE's design. It is extremely difficult to fully bring into HPE Indigenous Peoples' knowledges, as ‘to this day colonialism casts a long shadow over all educational institutions’ 17 (p. 2) meaning the institutions are not designed to listen to Indigenous knowledges. Instead, HPE was designed with colonial attitudes, whose engagement with Indigenous Peoples and knowledges is characterised by

‘not only by scopophilia, a drive to look, but also by an urge to penetrate, to traverse, to know, to translate, to own and exploit…The academic branch of the enterprise collects and analyses the experiences and things of others; it transforms story into text and objects‐in‐relation into artifacts to be catalogued and stored or displayed’. 18 (p. 23)

Thus, the current HPE design forces Indigenous Peoples to leave their self‐determination at HPE's at the front door and assimilate into its worldview.

Often, Indigenous Peoples are given a desk in HPE to create some scholarship or bestowed a space for our belongings and activities. Indigenous Peoples' knowledges can momentarily appear in HPE's learning and teaching. Sometimes, HPE goes on excursion and traverses into Indigenous Peoples' Communities. Within institutions, students and educators may knowingly or unknowingly encounter Indigenous Peoples and experience colonial memorials that celebrate a violent past. Ultimately, HPE's marginalisation of Indigenous Peoples and our knowledges prohibits HPE's engagement with Indigenous Peoples' crucial knowledges of futurity.

LISTENING TO INDIGENOUS PEOPLES' KNOWLEDGES

Although Indigenous Peoples are globally diverse, we collectively hold knowledges of futurity.

Here we provide some brief examples. Elders in Indigenous Peoples' Communities, such as Aunties, Uncles and Grandparents, commonly hold respected roles. 19 In this context, Elders promote social cohesion, share generational knowledges and skills and assist in developing younger people's relational abilities, including reciprocity. 19 In North America, the concept of two spirit involves the reverence of gender diversity, 20 and in this tradition, two spirit people often hold important roles in the community as medicine people, healers, mentors and educators. 21 In the area of disability, Indigenous Peoples have diverse values and practices associated with collective responsibilities, 22 which promote human capability, social participation and caregiving. 23 Indigenous Peoples' knowledges of futurity are also relationally holistic, holding relational obligations. 24 These obligations involve localised practices that promote clean air, clean water and sustainable resource management. 24 Consequently, Indigenous Peoples' managed lands are biodiverse rich, and these lands prove critical for global sustainability and human existence. 25

If the HPE community could functionalise meaningful listening to Indigenous Peoples' knowledges, then HPE could engage with Indigenous knowledges of futurity. However, engaging with Indigenous Peoples' knowledges in HPE is not straightforward, as this happens within a contested cultural interface between knowledge systems. 26 Nakata warns that simplistic engagement of Indigenous knowledges in western education programmes can risk: positioning Indigenous knowledges as oppositional to science paradigms or not science; disembodying Indigenous knowledges from the peoples who are its agents and the practices which renew their meanings and facilitating a view of Indigenous knowledges through standpoints or filters that serve colonial educational objectives. 26

One example of the contested cultural interface involves HPE's tendency to see art as inter‐relational understanding, science as biomedicine and a hierarchy where art is a supplement for science. 27 However, some forewarn that ‘Science without a soul is an empty vessel that can sail to dangerous waters’. 28 In contrast, Indigenous Peoples' knowledges value holism, 29 where art and science are kindred and inseperable. 28 It has been argued that ‘Art erects monuments with its sensations. Science constructs states of affairs with its functions’. 30 (p. 199) Art recreates and reimages what is seen or not seen, whereas science measures and quantifies phenomena as a whole and its parts. 30 A rich tissue of correspondence can exist between art and science within networks that share culminating points. 30 This intersection between art and science means collaborations between the pair have the potential to influence each other and create new knowledges and experience. This is one reason why AIIR can meaningfully illuminate ways to include Indigenous peoples' knowledges in HPE.

AIIR CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK

Informed by arts‐based research paradigms, AIIR embeds and embodies an Indigenist research agenda (see Table 1 for relevant texts). Arts‐based research engages ‘the arts as a foundation for creating expressive forms that enlighten’ 31 (p. 11) and a myriad of arts forms can be engaged to generate, interpret and communicate knowledges through ‘evocative and emotionally drenched expression’. 31 (p.10) Arts‐based research typically promotes social justice aims and being participant led can assist to facilitate participatory action and community‐based research. 32

For Indigenous Peoples, art and creative expression commonly involve storywork, which forms ‘part of articulating our world, understanding our knowledge system, naming our experiences, guiding our relationships and most importantly, identifying ourselves’. 33 (p. 5) Storywork highlights the importance of Indigenous stories and the underlying work of Indigenous stories in contemporary spaces, strengthening people's attention to the work of Indigenous stories to inform more meaningful education and research. 33 The term storywork challenges colonial logics that position Indigenous Peoples' stories as less legitimate than Western scholarly knowledge. 33 It also critiques the privileging of colonial values of knowledge, 33 such as rapid knowledge consumption and listening with a ‘tin ear’ and biases to Indigenous Peoples' knowledges. 14 This colonial oppression of Indigenous Peoples' knowledges held in story is why an Indigenous research paradigm is imperative for AIIR.

Indigenous Peoples have always conducted research. Yet, Indigenous Peoples remained largely excluded from settler‐colonial academies until relatively recently. Social activism movements in the 1960s and 1970s strongly influenced the rise of Indigenous academics and scholarship, initially in New Zealand and subsequently in Australia, Canada and the USA. 6 , 34 These Indigenous scholarly contributions informed the creation of Indigenous research paradigms, 6 which are commonly conceived as grounded in relationality and relational accountability. 35 This relationality recognises an interdependence and connection not only between people but also the land, sky, water, ancestors, animals and more. 35 The paradigms are also ‘grounded in the politics, circumstances, and economies of a particular moment, a particular time and place, a particular set of problems, struggles, and desires’. 36 (p. 14) The paradigms acknowledge the ongoing presence of colonisation and promote advances towards Indigenous Peoples' emancipation and self‐determination, 37 particularly through Indigenous governance of research processes, including the determination of research questions, methodologies and the forms of knowledges that are recognised and valued. 38 , 39

A further critical element within AIIR is the researcher(s)' socio‐cultural positioning. This can probably be overlooked in many research domains. Yet, the researcher's position is critical, as their epistemological (how we understand, prioritise and value knowledges) and ontological (how we perceive reality and be in the world) assumptions impact every aspect of the research, 38 guiding chosen actions and perceptions. 14 Transparency of researcher positionality and power alongside continual critical refection can reduce risks of epistemic dominance: where western knowledges are seen as neutral or dominant causing ethical issues and reproduction of colonial ideology. 6 , 26 Indigenous researcher socio‐cultural positions are also diverse, and depending on context, these are often key to an Indigenous research paradigm. This is due to stronger relationality with Indigenous knowledge holders, via shared knowledges, lived experiences and memories, facilitating thicker data and more meaningful data analysis and interpretation. 40 The term Indigenous knowledge holder foregrounds that research processes are not operating within a hierarchical participant‐expert model, flipping the idea of researchers as the experts in research and challenging power structures. 41 This premise is fundamental to participant‐led AIIR praxis.

AIIR PRAXES

Globally, Indigenous Peoples engage with arts praxes to deeply investigate research questions and find solutions to complex issues, often caused by colonising. 42 The methods of AIIR engage Indigenous Peoples in creative activities that resonate with Indigenous Peoples' knowledges 42 and produce multi‐dimensional art forms recognisable by Indigenous families and Communities. 32 The research is highly relational and collaborative. Depending on the research question and context, knowledge holders can include the researchers, who share stories and vulnerabilities to grow relationality. 42 In many research methods, data analysis is primarily conducted by researchers. In AIIR, however, knowledge holders lead meaning making through creativity and search for ‘ideas, seeking an interrelated understanding of historical, political, cultural, social or other contextual impacts … developing, sharing and representing these storied understandings’. 33 (p. 4) This knowledge holder leading also aligns with community‐based, participatory action research and co‐design methods. However, AIIR provides a distinct way to facilitate knowledge holder led analysis through artistic expression. This relational‐based meaning‐making engages ‘the heart (emotions), mind (intellect), body (physical actions), and spirit (spirituality)’ 33 (p. 4) to spark new ideas and knowledges and illuminate a better understanding of a phenomenon for solution generation. 33

As AIIR aims to uphold Indigenous self‐determination, this requires the research be led by Indigenous researchers. However, non‐Indigenous researchers can be invited to join AIIR and critical allyship practices 43 are vital. Knowledge holders in AIIR can be Indigenous or non‐Indigenous, depending upon the research question. Indigenous Peoples' knowledges can be engaged with by all people; though, they can only be produced within Indigenous cultural and relational contexts by Indigenous Peoples themselves. 26 , 44 Ermine argues that engagement between Indigenous and non‐Indigenous knowledge systems requires an ethical space to support respectful, relational, cross‐cultural understanding. 45 Whereas, Nakata conceives a cultural interface where engagement benefits from recognising the tacit assumptions and interpretive filters people bring to Indigenous Peoples' knowledges. 26 AIIR can support engagement between divergent Indigenous and Western knowledge systems by engaging ‘sensual and symbolic forms that elicit reflection, understanding, engagement and conversations’. 46 What can assist this engagement is anti‐colonial listening, where non‐Indigenous and Indigenous (depending upon Indigenous standpoint) knowledge holders can practice attentiveness as guests in Indigenous territories or spaces. Anti‐colonial listening is engaged as a relational action, involving the contextual layers of our positionalities, by ‘becoming aware of normative listening orientations across a range of gendered and racial formations, and developing self‐reflexivity around how these are guided’. 14 (p. 60)

Although AIIR is emergent and responsive, it has been suggested that AIIR requires certain aspects to demonstrate the integrity of the work. Phillips and Bunda highlight the importance of the following storying ways: who the researchers are; where the stories come from; how stories are listened to; how stories are brought to life; and how stories are gifted. 47 Similarly, Archibald et al. suggest that storywork research requires four principles ‘listen to Indigenous peoples' stories with respect, develop story relationships in a responsible manner, treat story knowledge with reverence, and strengthen story impact through reciprocity’. 33 (p. 2) Three further principles—holism, interrelatedness and synergy—enhance story meaning making. 33

For those of us researching in HPE with more positivist research methods, AIIR can seem almost fey. Though qualitative researchers may see some alignments with AIIR. Rather than objectivity, AIIR values relationality. It is interested in engaging holism, relationships and patterns between observation points, rather than measuring or assessing observation points. As such, for HPE researchers aspiring to engage with AIIR, we recommend collaborations with Indigenous artists experienced in art practice as research. Additionally, seeing an illustration of AIIR praxis can help clarify the method, so we now share a praxis example from a current study.

AIIR PRAXIS EXAMPLE

Praxis example context

The praxis example shared here is drawn from a current research project (see Fig. 1) set in Australia. The project aims to grow understanding of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledges of nursing and midwifery and identify ways to include these knowledges in nursing and midwifery education. The aim is informed by the Congress of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Nurses and Midwives, a crucial partner invested in building key stakeholder relationships to advocate for Indigenous nurses and midwives. 48

Important to the project are two central assumptions. First, Indigenous Peoples' knowledges of nursing and midwifery exist and are largely missing from nursing and midwifery education programmes. Second, Indigenous Peoples' knowledges are diverse and held locally and collectively. In contrast, Western research (problematically, from an Indigenous research paradigm) tends to extract, own and centralise knowledges with experts in institutions. 49 A commissioned artwork (see Fig. 2) pays reverence to the second assumption in project communications, aiming to strengthen safety for all involved in the research.

One way to explain AIIR praxis is by making transparent its integrity aspects. In this example we provide two transparencies. First, in Fig. 1 we illustrate a summary of how the broader research project responds to the integrity aspects, such as storying ways. 47 Second, we take a deeper dive into one component of the broader study. To explain how Photoyarn praxis responds to the integrity aspects of storywork principles. 33

Photoyarn praxis example

Photoyarn engages visual‐storying and is an adaptation of the arts based research Photovoice method. 50 Photoyarn invites knowledge holders to create photos relating to an issue and Yarn about the photos and their stories, to make meaning of them. 50 Yarning is a culturally specified process that Indigenous Peoples in Australia have engaged with for millennia. 51 The process upholds storying principles through relational story sharing and making of knowledges. When Indigenous People yarn, there is complex immersion in diverse Indigenous Peoples' worldviews, where there is often shared tacit and explicit knowledges allowing ‘the story to go deeper quicker as concepts do not need explanation’. 40 In contrast, when Indigenous Peoples and non‐Indigenous People yarn, cross‐cultural yarning takes place, drawing people together to be on the same page. 52

In this praxis example, flyers invite Indigenous nurse and midwifery knowledge holders to Photoyarn sessions on truth‐telling about nursing and midwifery and Indigenous Peoples. Creation of a storywork space (online or in‐person) aims to promote respect for knowledge holders. Providing an inviting and welcoming space, for instance, with artworks, materials and circular seating. To build relationality for storying, Social Yarning 51 opens the Photoyarn session. Knowledge holders are welcomed, Country is acknowledged, and everyone shares who their Mob is and where they are from. Collaborative Yarning 51 follows to yarn about the research purpose and invite consent. The consent process reveres people's storying through provision of choice to be named as creators of works.

Next, knowledge holders are invited to create or find existing photos relating to the research topic. Once photos are chosen, people send the photos to the researchers. Although knowledge holders write stories about their photos, researchers generate a photo collage (see Box 1). Research Yarning 51 then begins where knowledge holders point out their photos in the collage and share stories about them. Everyone listens. In keeping with arts‐based methods, knowledge holders lead the analysis, 31 making meaning of the relationships between the photos and stories, upholding storywork principles of holism, interrelatedness and synergy. The collage facilitates this relational thinking by bringing together the multiple images, arresting attention to possible relationships between them. 53 Researchers respectfully listen and take notes, which are transparently written and/or read out by researchers for knowledge holders to confirm or alter (see Box 1 example). The Photoyarn session closes with further yarning about next steps for the project.

My sister recently visited a traditional healer who referred to her as volcano people, not tropical island people. This observance speaks to the truth of our ancient connection to our island homes.

Talking about truth → so much spiritually that exists that we cannot necessarily talk about (lost memory, knowledge holders still scared to share or we live far from home that we do not get to learn, or we are regulated from speaking about them casually). Truth that is deep for us and beyond understanding of our non‐Indigenous colleagues. As Indigenous health professionals, we know about how crucial the spiritual health and well‐being side is to health. Our truth is deep and complex, and, despite having to make sense of and survive colonialism, we maintain this strong link in different ways. Truth of hope. Part of our DNA, storytelling is truth, hijacks us and we feel emotional. Weeping for the past (trauma, loss and longing to reconnect). Connecting to a memory (not always bad). Remembering the truth. Country remembers you and that you belong here, because Country knows those who came before you.

Dad was a passionate supporter of the western suburbs football team the magpies. When I see a magpie, I think of Dad. Dad was not supportive of girls going to uni; uni was costly and did not bring income back into the house. I started nursing because it was free. There were no scholarships in those days, so I was working 5 days a week and going to uni 5 days a week. Moving out of home was challenging financially. Magpies are Black and White; people see me as neither Black nor White, particularly as I have light coloured skin. Notes generated on the relationality between the photos and stories.

Family and place are relational. Just as truth‐telling is relational, not conceptual. We are in this place with connections, belonging, spirituality and memory. The bones of our ancestors are in this place, we are Sovereign, we are here being our Sovereign selves. There are histories that inform how nurses work with mob, form relationality with mob and impact mob. It is frustrating that nurses sometimes do not know these histories. Often working for, or with our mob, is not counted in, or as part of our workload. It is expected to be a side hustle, not core business, something added on as an extra. There are additional dimensions in the work Indigenous academics, midwives and nurses do. Working in colonial structures, ‘we take our tools into the master's house’. At the cultural interface we have Community obligations and have to navigate these in a white world and in non‐relationally oriented worlds.

CONCLUSION

The HPE community is steeped in colonial ideology which often fails to uphold Indigenous Peoples' rights. The consequence is that meaningful engagement with Indigenous Peoples' knowledges of futurity is forfeit. These knowledges can crucially protect and promote healing for all people from the significant harms of ongoing colonising. AIIR can illuminate ways to grow Indigenous Peoples' knowledges and futurity into HPE. AIIR can support complex engagement between Indigenous and Western knowledge systems. It does this by inviting people into ethical, critically reflective and relational spaces, where the tacit assumptions and interpretive filters people bring to Indigenous Peoples' knowledges are acknowledged. It gifts the HPE community normalisation of anti‐colonial listening and critical allyship and a departure from rigid colonial and Western ideas of what constitutes knowledges. The authors offer the HPE community a creative provocation: What if HPE listened to AIIR's invitation and gifts and accepted them?
