Reconsideration of radiographic axial imaging techniques for the evaluation of patellofemoral alignment: skyline view vs. merchant view
Authors: Kyohei Nakata, Masakazu Ishikawa, Atsuo Nakamae, Akinori Nekomoto, Koji Takeda, Junya Tsukisaka, Kanji Goto, Masayoshi Deie, Nobuo Adachi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to compare two common radiographic axial imaging techniques, the skyline view (SV) and the merchant view (MV), and to reveal the variability of acquired images in patients with recurrent patellar dislocation (RPD).MethodsThirty consecutive patients with 34 affected knees of RPD were included in this study (4 males, 26 females; mean age, 26.4 years). Both SV and MV were measured at 30°, and 45°of knee flexion in each patient. The congruence angle (CA) and the lateral patellofemoral angle (LPA) were evaluated for lateral shifts and patellar tilts in each view.ResultThe axial alignment differed significantly between the SV and MV at both 30° and 45° of knee flexion. The MV had higher CA and lower LPA values than the SV. Intraclass correlation coefficient analysis demonstrated excellent-to-good intra- and inter-observer reliabilities. A discrepancy in acquired images between SV and MV was demonstrated, with SV underestimated patellar malalignment in both lateral tilt and shift in patients with RPD.ConclusionSince axial patellar alignment varies significantly depending on the radiographic technique, MV imaging is preferable to prevent the underestimation of patellofemoral instability. Surgeons should be aware of the characteristics of each imaging method and choose the appropriate technique for accurate evaluation.

Introduction

Radiographic axial imaging of the patellofemoral joint is the first-line imaging modality used to evaluate patellofemoral disorders in daily clinical practice 1, 2, 3. Several techniques for acquiring axial images have been introduced; however, multiple factors contribute to the variability of images obtained using these techniques. Furthermore, no gold-standard imaging method is available.2,4

The most widely accepted techniques are performed with patients in the supine position.2 Skyline view(SV) and Merchant view(MV) have been used in clinical practice because of their feasibility.5,6 However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has compared the images acquired using these two imaging techniques. We hypothesized that the ability to demonstrate patellar alignment would differ between the Skyline view, obtained with quadriceps setting, and the Merchant view, obtained with the lower extremity relaxed and the lower leg externally rotated. This study aimed to compare two common axial radiography imaging methods and reveal the variability of the obtained images in patients with recurrent patellar dislocation (RPD).

Methods

Patients

Institutional review board approval was obtained for this retrospective case series. We reviewed 33 patients diagnosed with RPD at our outpatient clinic between 2013 and 2019. According to the definition of RPD, affected knees exhibit more than twice lateral dislocation or subluxation of the patella, and patients who met this criterion were included in this study. Patients with habitual, permanent patellar dislocation and revision cases were excluded. Finally, we identified 30 patients with RPD (4 males, 26 females; mean age, 26.4 years; range 14–42 years). Imaging findings were assessed on the basis of preoperative images obtained before MPFL reconstruction.

Radiographic imaging

In each case, patellofemoral alignment was assessed using two imaging techniques: SV and MV. For the SV, the patient was placed on the imaging table in a sitting position while maintaining a 30°or 45° knee flexion angle. The cassette was placed on the patient's thigh and held by the patient (Fig. 1A). The X-ray beam was directed towards the film from the distal side. For the MV, the patient was placed on the imaging table in the supine position (Fig. 1B). Using a special cassette holder and an adjustable frame (SV-10; Nikko Fines Industries, Co., Ltd., Japan), the knee flexion angle was adjusted to 30°or 45° at the end of the table (Fig. 2). The cassette was held on the leg using a special holder, and the X-ray beam was directed at the film from the proximal side. Radiographs in the Merchant view are obtained with the lower extremity relaxed and the lower leg externally rotated. Evaluation of axial view radiographs in the skyline and merchant views

To evaluate lateralization and lateral tilt of the patellae, axial images of the affected knees obtained at each angle of the SV and MV were analyzed using the indices described below.

Congruence angle (CA)

The CA was measured as an indicator of patellar centralization and subluxation. Following the method described by Merchant et al. the sulcus angle was bisected to establish a reference line, and a second line was projected from the apex of the sulcus to the lowest point on the articular ridge of the patella.5 When this line was lateral to the reference line, the angle was assigned a positive value; when it was medial, a negative value was assigned (Fig. 3A)Fig. 3(A) CA (Congruence Angle)The bisector (dotted line) of the angle formed by the tangents to the femoral trochlear surfaces is used as the reference line. Lateral patellofemoral angle (LPA)

To assess the lateral tilt of the patella, the LPA was measured as described previously.5 The angle formed by the lateral patellar facet and a line drawn across the most prominent aspects of the anterior portion of the femoral trochlea, which opens laterally in the normal patellofemoral joint, was measured (Fig. 3B).

Statistical analysis

All values are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare the SV and MV at each angle of radiographic analysis.

Two orthopedic surgeons performed the measurements of the CA and LPA values in a randomized order, twice by each rater, with at least a 30-day duration between measurements. Each rater was blinded to the other rater's and their previous measurements. Intra-and interobserver reliabilities were then analyzed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). The ICC values were interpreted as follows: ICC <0.40 = poor agreement, 0.40 < ICC <0.75 = fair to good agreement, and ICC >0.75 = excellent agreement.

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad PRISM (GraphPad Software, Inc., CA, USA); p < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Power analyses were performed using G-power statistical software.

Results

At 30° of knee flexion, significant differences were found in both CA and LPA between SV and MV. CA increased significantly from 4.7° ± 25.1° in SV to 28.8° ± 21.4° in the MV (p < 0.0001), indicating greater lateral patellar displacement in MV. Conversely, LPA decreased significantly from 3.0° ± 8.0° in SV to −10.3° ± 12.9° in MV (p < 0.0001), reflecting a more medially tilted patella as visualized on MV. Discussion

The most important finding of this study was that MV demonstrated greater sensitivity in detecting lateral displacement and patellar tilt than SV, as reflected in the higher values of CA and LPA. Lateral tilt of the patella, which may be overlooked in SV, can be appropriately assessed using MV. Elias et al. reported several radiographic series assessing patellar axial alignment.2 They described that the most widely accepted techniques are performed in the supine position; however, no gold standard examination is available. Both the SV and MV compared in this study are widely performed in the supine position and are considered standard radiographic methods. Although comparisons between these imaging techniques have been addressed in previous literature, Smith et al. concluded in a systematic review that it is difficult to assess differences in reliability, sensitivity, and specificity by imaging the axial alignment of the patella and morphological evaluation of femoral trochlear due to poor evidence.7 Nord et al. demonstrated that differences in knee flexion and beam incidence angles exist between facilities, and even when the same photography method is used, the imaging conditions differ by facility.8 To minimize variability, we investigated the imaging techniques in the same patients at a single institution.

The SV is a common imaging modality for evaluating patellar instability.9 It is also routinely used in our hospital to evaluate patellar axial alignment. The advantage of SV is that it does not require special equipment, and PF photography can be easily performed at various angles.

Conversely, MV is widely used as a supine imaging technique, and several reports on the evaluation of patellar alignment exist using this technique.10,11

MV is characterized by its ability to reduce examination errors by the patient and the photographer, achieved by adopting a supine posture where the lower limbs are dropped and relaxed. In our hospital, MV is performed in parallel with SV, and the alignment of the patella is evaluated and compared with these two imaging modalities.

This study showed that the detection sensitivity of MV for CA and LPA was superior to that of SV. Some SV normal-range cases showed abnormal LPA and CA values in the MV. Two possible explanations exist for these discrepancies. The first factor is the knee flexion angle and quadriceps femoris contraction. Obtaining an axial PF view in low knee flexion has more variability in the positioning of both the beam and the knee. However, a lower knee flexion angle reveals more pathology as the patella becomes more centralized with increased knee flexion.2,6

In the low knee flexion position, the effect of bone formation on patellar alignment becomes relatively small. The effect of soft tissue, such as the quadriceps femoris muscle and medial patellofemoral ligament, appears to be more strongly reflected.

In the SV, the patient actively maintains the knee flexion position, meaning the quadriceps muscle is contracted. Conversely, in the MV, the knee joint flexion angle is passively adjusted without quadriceps muscle contraction, and photography is then performed.

Quadriceps muscle contraction leads to differences in the imaging sensitivity of patellar alignments.2,4,12 A previous report noted that knee position and quadriceps muscle contraction in radiographic methods greatly influence each test value and are important factors.8 Removal of the correction by quadriceps contraction caused the lateral displacement and lateral tilt angle to be larger in the MV compared to the SV. MV may detect potential instability of the patella, even when corrected by quadriceps contractions.

The second factor was the external leg position. SV requires patients to position their legs themselves, whereas MV involves dropping the patient's leg into an externally rotated position. External positioning of the tibial tuberosity enhances the lateralized force on the patella.4,13,14 Chassaing et al. reported that external torsion of the tibial tubercle enhances lateral patellar instability.15,16 In MV, laterally stressed patellar instability can be visualized by externally rotating the lower leg, and further dynamic evaluation can be performed by evaluating this imaging method in conjunction with SV.

Leg external rotation during MV may also be associated with quadriceps contraction. However, anatomical features, such as bone morphology, may also be related to differences in sensitivity; therefore, further studies are needed.

Finally, differences in alignment assessment compared with computed tomography (CT) are discussed. Many studies have evaluated patellar alignment using CT and reported its usefulness.17,18 Several studies have measured the anatomical features of patients with patellar instability. However, CT scan evaluation cannot easily determine knee position and flexion angle. Frequent scans are also difficult to perform from the viewpoint of radiation exposure. Planar radiography is more useful for a chronological evaluation. In terms of the medical economy, plain radiographic examination is relatively inexpensive compared to CT. Examining patellar alignment using plain radiography is considered clinically useful, and it is important to recognize the characteristics and differences of each examination method.

Limitation

The limitations of this study include the small sample size and the fact that it focuses on a specific population—patients with recurrent patellar dislocation who underwent surgical treatment—thereby restricting the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, cases with clear bony abnormalities, such as trochlear dysplasia, were not included, and therefore, the results may not be applicable to patients with pronounced osseous factors.

Conclusions

In this study, the SV, which is commonly used in daily clinical practice for axial imaging of the knee, showed a lower degree of lateral shift and patellar tilt. Evaluating the differences in patellar alignment using these two imaging methods—each with distinct limb positions and soft tissue influences—may allow for a more detailed assessment of patellar instability. Orthopedic surgeons should be familiar with the characteristics of each imaging technique when evaluating alignment.
