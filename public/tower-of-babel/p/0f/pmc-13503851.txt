Natural dyeing of wool fabric with Sappan via the atmospheric-pressure non-equilibrium microwave plasma system
Authors: Wittawat Poonthong, Toshifumi Yuji, Shinichi Tashiro, Daisuke Hirotani, Narong Mungkung, Nat Kasayapanand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents an improved natural dyeing method for wool fabric utilizing Sappan wood, achieved by integrating iron mordant treatment with atmospheric-pressure non-equilibrium microwave plasma irradiation. The dyeing process was conducted at 80 °C with a 2.0 wt% Sappan dye solution, and plasma irradiation was applied both during and after the dyeing procedure. There has been found that colorimetric analysis using the CIE Lab* system demonstrated a significant decrease in L* (brightness) and increases in a* and b* chromaticity values for plasma-treated samples compared with untreated dyeing. These results indicate enhanced color depth and chromatic development. In addition, XPS analysis revealed increased Fe 3p and O 1s peak intensities following plasma irradiation, indicating the formation of oxidized iron species and additional oxygen-containing surface groups. These changes result from reactions among plasma-generated oxygen radicals, iron mordant species, and phenolic hydroxyl groups in Sappan dye molecules, which enhance dye fiber interaction. Therefore, the results indicate that atmospheric-pressure microwave plasma irradiation promotes surface chemical activation and enhances natural dye fixation on wool fibers, offering a promising method for improving natural dyeing processes.

Introduction

The Japanese textile industry has developed a sophisticated division of labor, spanning raw yarn production, fabric processing, dyeing, and sewing. Combined with advanced technological capabilities, this system enables the global supply of high-quality materials. However, domestic textile production has recently declined due to the relocation of many manufacturing sites to China and Southeast Asia. This shift presents a significant challenge for the development of high-value-added products utilizing local resources. In particular, demand for products made with natural dyes or sustainable materials with low environmental impact has increased, necessitating the advancement of new dyeing technologies for organic fabrics1–3 and natural dyes. Despite their superior biodegradability and safety, natural dyes4–6 exhibit low dyeing reproducibility, limited color stability, and challenges in controlling color development compared to chemical dyes. Sappan wood (Caesalpinia sappan L.) is recognized as a natural dye source containing brazilin (C16H14O5)7, a primary coloring compound that oxidizes to form brazilein (C16H12O5). The dye undergoes a color change from reddish to purplish upon oxidation. When applied to animal fibers, however, the dye often results in uneven coloring and discoloration, as the high hydrophobicity of protein-based keratin structures in animal fibers impedes effective interaction with dye molecules8. Traditionally, these issues are addressed by stabilizing dye pigments with mordants (metal salts), but such chemical methods have inherent performance limitations.

Interestingly, various dye techniques have been studied by many research groups to develop both extraction and dyeing performance. For example, Shahid et al.9 reported the development of microwave-assisted extraction of Arjun bark, which was found to effectively improve color strength and fastness on silk fabrics; they then utilized ultrasonic treatment to extract tannin-based dye from neem bark, which could improve color strength on silk fabrics through bio-mordanting10. In addition, they used waste tea leaves as a tannin-based dye for wool, combined with bio-mordants11. On the other hand, Shazia et al.12 studied microwave-assisted extraction of hematein using logwood chips for silk dyes. Sadeghi-Kiakhani et al.13 applied the UV pretreatment of wool fibers, which altered the fiber surface, leading to improved dye uptake, color strength, and fastness during dyeing with Cochineal natural dye. While Wu et al.14 enhanced the performance of natural dyeing in supercritical CO2 systems by chemically modifying alizarin to increase its solubility and dyeing performance. Moreover, Alin Ahmd et al.15 applied gamma irradiation at 15 kGy to enhance the extraction of dyes from red calico leaves, resulting in improved dye uptake and fastness on cotton fabrics.

As shown, these techniques have been successfully investigated to improve natural dye extraction and dye efficiency. In spite of that, there are s some limitations, such as controlling surface chemical reactions and dye-fiber interactions. To address these, atmospheric-pressure plasma technology offers advantageous conditions, enabling the generation of reactive species that modify the fiber surface by introducing oxygen-containing functional groups without the need for harsh chemical treatments.

Recently, various works have incorporated plasma techniques to improve the dyeing process. For instance, Abu Naser Md Ahsanul et al.16 studied the plasma-assisted spray-dyeing of wool fabrics with dyes derived from onion skin. They suggest that adjusting the electrical power and plasma irradiation time improved the fiber surface condition, enhancing dyeing ability and color fastness. Can et al.17 reported the effects of air plasma pre-treatment on the dyeability behavior of silk fabric. Their experiment demonstrated that plasma irradiation conditions the fiber surface, increases color intensity, and improves color fastness, especially in raw silk. Somaeya et al.18 studied the effect of a low-pressure glow discharge (LPGD) plasma with oxygen and nitrogen gases on the dyeing process of jute-cotton (JC) blended fabrics by using coffee extract as a natural dye source. The results showed that the increased surface roughness, and created new functional groups on the fabric. This led to significantly improved dyeability (K/S) and color fastness. Saweat et al.19 developed a dyeing technique using an atmospheric-pressure plasma jet to precondition cotton fabric prior to natural indigo dyeing. The experiment showed that plasma treatment enhanced oxygen-containing functional groups and surface roughness, resulting in significantly improved dye absorption (a K/S ratio increase of approximately 20%) and increased color fastness. In addition, Marzhan et al.20 explored the effect of low-temperature plasma on natural fiber composites. Experimental results show that plasma conditioning increases strength (from 18 to 29 MPa), alters fiber microstructure, and accelerates biodegradation by about 7 days, indicating the plasma’s potential to develop strong, eco-friendly materials.

Atmospheric-pressure plasma technologies have recently gained attention for surface modification of materials. Low-temperature plasma generated at atmospheric pressure is increasingly utilized as a modification process with low environmental impact, as it does not require vacuum systems21–28. Among these, atmospheric-pressure microwave discharge plasma technology is recognized for its high electron density and ability to maintain stable low-temperature discharges compared to other plasma technologies. This process generates activated radical species, including O, N, and H, during discharge29–33. These radical species partially cleave C–H or C–N molecular bonds on textile surfaces and introduce hydrophilic functional groups such as carbonyl and hydroxyl34–38. Consequently, this plasma technology is expected to increase binding sites for dye molecules and enhance dye performance39. Previous research has demonstrated that atmospheric-pressure microwave discharge plasma technology can control dyeing effects in chemical fibers by utilizing radical species generated from this plasma. Building on this foundation, the present study proposes a novel dyeing method that applies atmospheric-pressure microwave discharge plasma to the coloring process of wool fibers using the natural dye Sappan wood. Specifically, the study examines the potential for forming new chemical bonds between dye molecules and fiber surfaces through reactions with O radicals and Ar excited species generated in plasma, in combination with pre-mordant dyeing using iron mordant solutions. This approach is distinctive in that it induces oxidation and complex-forming reactions on the fiber surface while simultaneously controlling the color development reaction with natural dyes.

This study presents results from surface condition analyses using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), as well as investigations into the fundamental dyeing characteristics and chemical alterations of Sappan wood. Furthermore, the study examines the mechanism of dyeing wool fiber with natural Sappan wood dye in conjunction with atmospheric-pressure microwave discharge plasma technology.

Experimental setup

Natural dyeing of wool fabric was performed with the assistance of atmospheric-pressure non-equilibrium microwave plasma, utilizing a coaxial plasma system engineered for stable, controllable, and scalable operation under atmospheric conditions. Plasma generation was achieved using a 2.45 GHz microwave power supply (MG0500D-210TC, AET Inc., Japan) operated at 150 W, which was coupled to a compact torch-type coaxial plasma generator (ADTEC Plasma Technology Co., Ltd, Japan) via an RG-393 coaxial cable40–42. This configuration enabled direct and reproducible microwave energy delivery without requiring a resonant cavity. The resonator-free configuration streamlines system architecture and improves process scalability by minimizing geometric constraints and reducing sensitivity to load variations. The plasma torch utilized a copper (Cu) inner electrode (Ø 2.0 mm) and an aluminium (Al) outer electrode (Ø 6.0 mm), which were electrically insulated by a silica tube to maintain discharge stability, electrical isolation, and consistent plasma generation during continuous operation. Wool specimens were pre-treated with iron mordant at 25 wt%, then dyed in a Sappan wood dye solution at a total dye concentration of 2.0 wt% (5.0 × 10−3 mol/L) at 80 °C for 30 min. Microwave plasma irradiation was applied both during and after the dyeing process, with 10 min of irradiation allocated to each phase. The specimen temperature was maintained at 80 ± 2 °C throughout the procedure. The irradiation distance was set at 5.0 mm to ensure consistent plasma exposure and reproducibility across all samples. After treatment, the specimens were rinsed with deionized water and allowed to air dry.

In addition to the experimental procedure, the color properties of the treated wool specimens were quantitatively evaluated using a spectrophotometer (M311R-12HVCN, Shiro Sangyo Co., Ltd, Japan). The color coordinates were expressed in the CIE L*, a*, and b* color space. For each specimen, measurements were conducted at three different positions, and the mean L*, a*, and b* values were calculated to ensure measurement reliability. Surface chemical states were characterized using XPS with a KRATOS ULTRA2 system (KRATOS Analytical) equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα radiation source (hν = 1486.6 eV). The investigation targeted the Fe 3p and O 1s spectral regions to assess modifications in surface chemistry resulting from dyeing and plasma irradiation. Surface morphology and appearance were examined using a digital microscope (VHX series, KEYENCE) to assess modifications induced by plasma treatment and dyeing (Fig. 1).

Results and discussion

Figure 2 presents wool fibers colored with Sappan wood dye, as well as fibers pre-mordanted with iron, subsequently dyed with Sappan wood and subjected to simultaneous plasma irradiation. The fibers treated with Sappan wood dye exhibit a blackish orange color. In contrast, fibers pre-mordanted with iron, subsequently dyed with Sappan wood, and subjected to simultaneous plasma irradiation display a color shift from blackish antique violet to antique violet. This transformation is attributed to a decrease in L* value, indicating reduced brightness, and increases in a* and b* chromaticity values, as measured by a chromometer. The L* value represents color brightness on a scale from 0 to 100, with 0 corresponding to black and 100 to white. Higher L* values indicate greater brightness. The a* and b* values represent the intensity of color shades. Positive a* values correspond to red hues, whereas negative a* values indicate green hues. Similarly, positive b* values denote yellow hues, while negative b* values correspond to blue or purple hues.

The combination of plasma irradiation and pre-mordant dyeing resulted in improved dye retention and surface coordination interactions. This characteristic is not observed in conventional natural dye coloring methods. Therefore, this approach may be applicable to dyeing with other natural dyes, including indigo, madder, and persimmon tannin.

To clarify plasma irradiation’s role in dyeing, the experiment isolated its effects using controlled comparisons. Three processing methods were tested: dyeing with Sappan wood extract alone, dyeing with iron mordant, and dyeing with iron mordant plus atmospheric-pressure microwave plasma irradiation. In all cases, dye concentration, temperature (80 ± 2 °C), dyeing duration (30 min), and specimen geometry stayed constant. This made plasma irradiation the main variable in the third condition, setting it apart from conventional methods.

Under atmospheric-pressure microwave plasma conditions, reactive species such as oxygen radicals and excited argon species form within the discharge region. These species induce surface oxidation and limited bond scission on polymeric and protein-based materials. This process leads to oxygen-containing functional groups, including hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxyl moieties. On wool fibers composed of keratin, such plasma-induced modifications can increase the density of polar surface sites. These polar sites participate in coordination interactions with metal mordants and phenolic dye molecules.

The presented results provide two complementary observations supporting the influence of plasma-assisted surface reactions. First, colorimetric analysis in the CIE L*, a*, and b* space reveals systematic chromatic changes in samples with combined mordant treatment and plasma irradiation. These changes contrast with those observed in samples processed under conventional dyeing conditions. Second, XPS measurements show increased intensities in the Fe 3p and O 1s spectral regions after plasma treatment. This finding indicates the presence of oxidized iron species and more oxygen-containing surface environments. Because the thermal dyeing parameters were identical across all experiments, the observed chemical and optical differences can be attributed to the action of plasma-generated reactive species on the fiber surface.

Taken together, the comparative experimental design and the observed spectroscopic changes support the interpretation that atmospheric-pressure microwave plasma promotes surface oxidation and enhances coordination interaction between iron mordant species and the phenolic hydroxyl groups of Sappan dye molecules. These plasma-induced surface modifications provide a plausible explanation for the enhanced dye retention observed in the plasma-assisted dyeing process.

The comparative experimental design and the observed spectroscopic changes support the idea that atmospheric-pressure microwave plasma promotes surface oxidation. It also enhances the coordination interaction between iron mordant species and the phenolic hydroxyl groups of Sappan dye molecules. These plasma-induced surface changes help explain why dye retention is improved in the plasma-assisted dyeing process.

Conclusions

This study presents a method to significantly enhance the coloring performance of wool fabrics with the natural dye Sappan wood by integrating iron mordant processing and atmospheric-pressure non-equilibrium microwave discharge plasma irradiation. This approach demonstrates low environmental impact and effectiveness as a sustainable dyeing technology that reduces chemical usage. Future work will focus on the practical application of this method as an environmentally friendly, high-performance natural dyeing process, including its adaptation to other natural dyes and fiber types.

In addition to future research that aims to clarify the viability of plasma-dyed natural dyes by focusing on four objectives: (1) elucidating molecular interactions using FTIR and Raman spectroscopy, (2) analyzing fabric surfaces with SEM, (3) evaluating fabric performance in color fastness, tensile strength, and breathability, and (4) quantifying environmental impacts to determine the practical potential of plasma dyeing.
