Development of a Video‐Based Decision Aid for Breast Cancer Screening Among Older Women
Authors: Meghan E. Lindsay, Iyanuoluwa Odole, Sadde Mohamed, Claire King, Nancy L. Schoenborn, Mara A. Schonberg, Ilana B. Richman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWhether to continue breast cancer screening beyond age 74 is uncertain. Decision aids may improve understanding of health information and support informed screening decisions. The goal of this study was to develop a video‐based decision aid for breast cancer screening among older women using patient‐centered design.MethodsFollowing the Framework for Innovation, the research team first used formative focus groups to understand older women's perspectives on mammography. We developed a prototype video based on decision aid best practices and formative focus group findings. We then evaluated the content, clarity, and style of the decision aid in cognitive testing focus groups. We made iterative changes to the video in response to focus group feedback. Focus groups included women age ≥ 70 without a personal history of breast cancer from Connecticut‐area community and clinical settings. We coded and analyzed transcripts using both abductive and deductive approaches.ResultsWe convened 6 formative focus groups and 7 cognitive testing groups with 31 participants (mean age 78 [range 70–93]); 39% Black, 58% White, and 3% Latina. In focus groups, participants perceived screening as largely beneficial and saw overdiagnosis as unfamiliar. Some participants valued quantitative information about risks and benefits of screening, while others relied on experience, perceptions of risk, and beliefs about the efficacy of mammography to make screening decisions. We incorporated these perspectives into the framing, language, and narrative arc of the decision aid. In cognitive testing focus groups, participants found the decision aid informative and engaging.DiscussionUsing a patient‐centered approach, we developed a video‐based decision aid for breast cancer screening for older women. Our design, which drew on the perspectives of older women, was perceived as easy to understand and informative. We will assess the impact of the decision aid on decision quality, decisional conflict, and intention to screen in future work.

Introduction

Although guidelines recommend breast cancer screening for women between the ages of 40–74, whether to continue screening beyond age 74 is uncertain. Clinical trials of screening mammography have not included women over the age of 74, and observational evidence is mixed [1, 2]. Simulation modeling suggests that continued mammography beyond age 74 reduces breast cancer specific mortality [3, 4, 5]. However, cohort studies have not shown a mortality benefit [6, 7]. Harms of screening among older women include false positives and overdiagnosis [6, 8]. Current guidelines from the US Preventive Services Task Force state there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against screening in older women, while guidelines from the American Cancer Society recommend that screening can be continued after age 75, until life expectancy falls below 10 years [1, 9].

In the absence of strong evidence to guide screening decisions, shared decision making can play a central role. Decision aids provide structured information about the risks and benefits associated with screening choices and are designed to help patients and clinicians make informed decisions concordant with their goals and values. Decision aids have been shown to improve knowledge, prepare women to make informed decisions about breast cancer screening, and shape screening decisions [10, 11, 12, 13]. Decision aids developed specifically for older women have likewise been shown to be acceptable, improve decision quality, and reduce low‐value screening [14, 15, 16, 17].

Although decision aids can improve decision quality, adoption has been slow [18]. Barriers to adoption include inadequate time to complete a decision aid and integration into clinical workflows [19]. Decision aids can also be challenging for patients with limited health literacy or numeracy to use [12, 20]. Most existing breast cancer decision aids for older women are paper or web‐based [15, 21, 22]. Video‐based decision aids have the potential to overcome some of these implementation challenges. Video uses narration and imagery, thereby potentially reducing the need for clinician involvement and lowering cognitive demands on patients. Video can also improve understanding compared to text in other contexts [23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Lastly, video is easy to disseminate, creating more options for implementation.

The goal of this work was to develop a video‐based decision aid to support decision‐making for average risk older women considering breast cancer screening. We used an iterative process to first understand perspectives of older women on mammography and to incorporate those perspectives into decision aid design. We then sought patient feedback on the decision aid and iteratively updated the decision aid design with the goal of improving intelligibility and acceptability.

Methods

Decision Aid Development

The Framework for Innovation is a user‐centered design process that seeks to first explore an issue widely and deeply, develop a prototype solution, and iteratively refine the product with users [28, 29]. Following this approach, we developed the video‐based decision aid in three discrete phases: (1) Formative focus groups to understand older women's perspectives on screening and screening decisions, (2) Prototype decision aid development, and (3) Cognitive testing focus groups with iterative refinement of the decision aid (see Supporting Information: Methods and Figure for extended methodologic detail) [28, 29]. We designed formative focus groups to allow for a broad discussion of participants' perspectives on screening mammography while also focusing discussion on topics relevant to the development of a video‐based decision aid. These topics included how to explain overdiagnosis in the context of a video, and preferences around video style, language, use of quantitation, and imagery (Table S1). We analyzed formative focus groups using an abductive approach, building on prior literature and established frameworks of cancer screening decisions among older adults while also identifying new concepts relevant to video development [30, 31, 32, 33].

Next, we developed a prototype video‐based decision aid (Vyond, San Mateo, CA). The prototype decision aid was developed to adhere to best practices of decision‐aid design, including stating the decision at hand, providing balanced information, and describing outcomes as absolute risks [34]. We also incorporated participants' perspectives from formative focus groups into the initial design (Table 1). We aimed to keep the video relatively brief, around 3–4 min. We then assessed the messaging, intelligibility, and acceptability of the video‐based decision aid through cognitive testing focus groups (Table S2). The research team iteratively incorporated findings from cognitive testing groups, making changes and testing updated versions with subsequent groups (Table 2, Figure 1).

Participants

Participants included women 70 and older who had not had breast cancer, who had been screened in the past, and who were able to participate in a group discussion. Participants were recruited from community sources including senior centers, religious organizations, health fairs, and primary care clinics. We used purposive sampling to select recruitment sites, and sites were chosen to reflect a broad range of communities in south‐central Connecticut. Within sites, we recruited a convenience sample of participants.

The study was reviewed and approved by the Yale Institutional Review Board and participants provided informed consent.

Results

We conducted 13 focus groups (n = 6 formative groups and n = 7 cognitive testing groups) with a total of 34 participants with 31 unique individuals. Most participants attended a single focus group. One participant attended both a formative and cognitive testing group, and 2 participants attended two cognitive testing groups (Tables S3 and S4). The median number of participants per focus group was 3 (IQR 2–3). The mean age of participants was 78 (range 70–93). Among participants, 39% were Black, 58% were White, and 3% were Hispanic or Latina. Approximately 32% of participants had a high school degree or less, and 74% reported needing assistance with medical information sometimes or usually.

In formative focus groups, participants conceptualized screening as largely beneficial and important to health. Participants viewed mammography as important not only for its potential health benefits but also because the process of deriving self‐knowledge was seen as valuable. Participants regarded screening as having few harms. The major harm of screening was noted to be discomfort. False positives were not always seen as a harm, but rather as a necessary and tolerable part of the screening process. While some participants valued quantitative information when making screening decisions, for others, decisions were driven by prior perceptions of benefit, desire for self‐knowledge, perceived risk of breast cancer, or risk aversion. In the decision‐making process, participants valued autonomy but also welcomed support and collaboration with trusted clinicians (Table 2, Table S5).

When introduced in focus groups, the concept of overdiagnosis was seen as unfamiliar. Some participants expressed surprise and even skepticism at the concept. Conceptualizing overdiagnosis as the discovery of an indolent cancer was seen as intuitive, while conceptualizing overdiagnosis via the counterfactual (i.e., a cancer that wouldn't have caused harm had it not been discovered) did not resonate. Participants were somewhat skeptical that the possibility of overdiagnosis would influence their own screening decisions because overdiagnosis is inherently uncertain. Participants preferred reducing harms of overdiagnosis by reducing overtreatment rather than simply stopping screening. Specifically, they desired accurate prognostication, so breast cancers that were unlikely to cause harm could be observed, while aggressive cancers could be treated. Lastly, some participants could envision scenarios where it would be “better not to know” about a breast cancer. However, participants felt this would only be the case if they had very significant medical problems or marked functional impairment for example, having advanced dementia or being “bedridden” (Table S5).

When reflecting on optimal video design, participants prioritized clarity, accurate information, delivery from an authoritative source, diverse characters, and ease of use. Participants generally found video acceptable as a medium and were familiar with educational videos from other contexts (e.g., preparation for elective surgery). Participants provided suggestions for video use, including in community groups and prior to office visits. We incorporated these perspectives into initial prototype design (Table 2).

In cognitive testing, participants saw the main purpose of the video as presenting information about breast cancer screening, supporting conversations, or offering choices. Some participants felt the video was slanted against mammography. For example, one participant said, “I kind of get the impression that it's kind of focused a little bit more not to have mammograms when you are older, which to me is a little scary, to tell you the truth.” However, others were simply surprised at the information presented and felt it was contrary to what they had previously understood about mammography. For example, one participant said, “Early detection is so important. I'm not saying I'm shocked, but I'm a little bit shocked. Because that is what's always been told to us.” Participants also felt the video prompted them to think about their own screening choices. One participant said, “I thought it was very interesting and direct and to the point, cause the questions that the patient asked and how the doctor answered and just kind of cleared up in my mind what I want.”

Participants offered suggestions for improvement including improving the clarity of numerical information, adding repetition or a summary, and revising language to remove jargon or difficult vocabulary (Table 2; see Supporting Information: video link). Changes included reordering informational content, changing numerical presentation to allow easier comparison of outcomes with and without screening, including a summary slide, editing language to be more easily understood, and changing the appearance and voices of characters.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a video‐based decision aid for older women who were considering continuing breast cancer screening with mammography. In cognitive testing focus groups, participants reported that the video was informative, effective at conveying the central idea that screening is a choice with risks and benefits and expressed that the video presented new and sometimes surprising information.

Consistent with prior work, we also found that older women often held strongly positive views of mammography, and saw it as important to health, valuable, and even essential [35, 36]. Information that ran contrary to these perceptions was seen as surprising and sometimes generated skepticism. For example, participants expressed surprise at the small mortality benefit of screening, and at the possibility of overdiagnosis. In addition, consistent with prior studies, participants expressed some doubt that the existence of overdiagnosis would dissuade them from pursuing screening [37, 38]. These findings highlight persistent challenges in informed decision‐making for breast cancer screening among older women. Whether video improves understanding of these challenging concepts beyond standard decision aids must be evaluated in future work.

Despite the potential for video to improve understanding and decision‐making, only a few studies have developed video‐based decision aids for breast cancer screening. Investigators from Australia tested video‐based messaging explaining that screening was not routinely recommended after age 75. Participants who received a video in addition to a standard letter were less likely to report plans to continue screening than those who received only a standard letter, though screening intentions did not differ compared to an extended text‐based explanation of screening cessation [39]. Other US‐based studies have evaluated video‐based decision aids in the context of specific screening scenarios, including screening for women at elevated risk of breast cancer, and have shown that video‐based decision aids can reduce decisional conflict [40]. However, whether video‐based decision aids are easier to implement and whether video format could facilitate broader use remains largely unknown.

Our work extends this literature in several ways. First, by developing a video‐based decision aid for older women in the US, we were able to tailor the content and framing to the US context, where screening is common and where there are no strong recommendations for or against screening [41]. Second, our approach incorporated the perspectives of patients into the design of the decision aid, and we made iterative refinements based on participant feedback. Importantly, many participants had lower educational attainment, and a substantial proportion likely had low health literacy. Thus, the decision aid was designed to be intelligible to all participants and may be particularly useful for those with lower educational attainment or health literacy.

Our study has important limitations. First, this work focused on the development of a decision aid, using a patient‐centered, iterative process. We did not test whether the decision aid improves decision quality, knowledge, decisional conflict, or actual screening decisions. Second, although we included a diverse sample of older women in our focus groups, and while we believe we reached thematic saturation, there may be groups or viewpoints not represented in our sample.

Conclusions

Using a patient‐centered approach, we designed a video‐based decision aid to support breast cancer screening decisions among older women. The design includes both features of a typical decision aid while mirroring the language and perspectives of patients. In future work, we will evaluate the impact of the decision aid on decision quality, knowledge of screening, understanding of overdiagnosis, and screening intention.
