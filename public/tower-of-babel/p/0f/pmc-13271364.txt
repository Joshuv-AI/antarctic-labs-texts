Predicting Risk of Plasmodium Vivax Microscopy-Detected Episodes Using Serological Markers in Patients With Plasmodium falciparum Malaria: A Multicountry Diagnostic Performance Evaluation
Authors: Maulina Hafidzah, Tamiru Shibiru Degaga, Michael Christian, Mohammad Shafiul Alam, Mohammad Sharif Hossain, J Kevin Baird, Inge Sutano, Lauren Smith, Dionne C Argyropoulos, Aisah R Amelia, Rintis Noviyanti, Leanne J Robinson, Ric N Price, Ivo Mueller, Kamala Thriemer, Rhea J Longley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Plasmodium vivax presents a significant obstacle to malaria elimination due to its capacity to form dormant liver-stage hypnozoites that can cause relapses. Universal radical cure, which administers hypnozoite-targeting treatment to patients with P. falciparum malaria living in co-endemic areas, has potential to reduce P. vivax relapses. However, its implementation is hindered by the lack of a diagnostic tool for detecting hypnozoite-carriage.Methods P. vivax serological exposure markers (SEMs) were evaluated as a screening tool in P. falciparum patients for predicting risk of P. vivax microscopy-detected episodes over the following 63 days. Analysis was performed using samples from participants in the PRIMA study from Ethiopia, Indonesia, and Bangladesh (NCT03916003). An existing random forest serological classification algorithm was used and evaluated for sensitivity and specificity, then further optimized by re-training a study-specific model.ResultsIgG antibodies were measured in 244 P. falciparum participant samples, of which 22 had a vivax microscopy-detected episode during follow-up. Serological exposure markers showed high sensitivity (82%) but low specificity (27%), likely due to sustained antibody responses in moderate-to-high transmission areas or undetected submicroscopic P. vivax infections during follow-up. A study-specific algorithm increased specificity to 68%, but with a corresponding drop in sensitivity to 68%.ConclusionsAlthough P. vivax SEMs showed limited suitability for guiding radical cure in P. falciparum patients in this study (where the outcome was microscopy-detected vivax episodes), they could play a valuable role in building community trust and acceptance of universal radical cure when supported by strong communication and implementation strategies.Keywords: Plasmodium falciparum, universal radical cure, Plasmodium vivax, serological exposure markers, machine-learning algorithm

Significant progress has been made in reducing the global burden of malaria through expanded access to tools preventing infection and treating parasitemic individuals [1]. However, Plasmodium vivax remains a significant challenge for malaria elimination as it can form liver-stage hypnozoites that reactivate leading to clinical relapses unless treated with specific liver-stage-targeting drugs [2]. Effective clearance of hypnozoites requires use of 8-aminoquinolines, termed “radical cure” [3]. This requires glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) testing to minimize adverse events, such as hemolytic anemia, in patients with G6PD deficiency [3]. Additionally, control efforts are confounded by the lack of commercial diagnostics to identify individuals with hypnozoites [4], occurring in the absence of patent blood-stage infection. With approximately one-third of the world's population at risk of P. vivax [5], there is an urgent need for improved diagnostic and treatment strategies.

PRIMA was a multicenter, open-label, randomized controlled trial conducted in Bangladesh, Indonesia, and Ethiopia, to evaluate primaquine radical cure of patients with P. falciparum malaria as a novel approach to reduce subsequent P. vivax relapses [6]. This intervention was designed to address the high incidence of P. vivax following treatment for P. falciparum in coendemic areas [7, 8]. PRIMA demonstrated that patients treated with high-dose primaquine (1.0 mg/kg per day for 7 days) had fivefold lower risk of P. vivax microscopy-detected recurrence within 63 days compared with those treated with standard care (0.25 mg/kg single-dose primaquine to treat P. falciparum gametocytes) [6]. These findings highlighted the potential to expand the indication for radical cure to patients who present with P. falciparum malaria, an approach termed “universal radical cure.” However, the acceptability of universal radical cure is potentially limited by concerns of exposing patients who are not infected with hypnozoites to the risk of primaquine induced hemolysis and the associated costs of expanded G6PD testing [9]. Better strategies for targeting patients who would benefit most from universal radical cure are needed.

P. vivax serological exposure markers (SEMs) can be used to identify individuals previously exposed to infection with P. vivax [10]. These markers rely on detecting immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies that indicate a history of recent P. vivax infection. Nearly all strains of P. vivax exhibit a primary relapse within the first 9-months after their initial infection [11], hence P. vivax SEMs have potential to act as indirect biomarkers for relapse risk. The P. vivax SEMs have 80% sensitivity and 80% specificity for detecting recent P. vivax blood-stage infections in the prior 9-months [10], and have been validated in multiple geographic regions (Thailand [10, 12], Cambodia [13], Indonesia [14], Solomon Islands [10], Brazil [10], Peru [15]). The ability to identify patients at risk of relapse led to the World Health Organization (WHO) releasing the first preferred product characteristics (PPCs) for diagnosing relapse risk. The PPCs define various use cases including guiding radical cure, with use case 1b specifying acute case management aimed at preventing P. vivax relapses following P. falciparum infection [16]. The SEM classification algorithm has not previously been evaluated in P. falciparum patient samples for this use-case.

P. vivax SEMs and the associated serological classification algorithm could be used in countries co-endemic for both P. falciparum and P. vivax to guide use of universal radical cure. To evaluate the utility of SEMs to classify P. falciparum patients at risk of future P. vivax relapse, we conducted a retrospective study using plasma samples collected from PRIMA control arm patients, who were not treated with primaquine and thus remained at risk of relapse. Plasma samples were analyzed for antibodies against the P. vivax SEMs using a multiplexed assay and associated random forest serological classification algorithm, developed and validated based on prior data [10]. Modified versions of the existing serological classification algorithm were also developed to better match the epidemiology of P. vivax in the trial setting.

METHODS

Ethical Approval

Samples for this study were obtained from PRIMA study conducted between 18/08/2019 and 14/03/2022 in Bangladesh, Ethiopia and Indonesia (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03916003). Ethical approval was granted by the NT Health and Menzies School of Health Research Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC), as well as by independent review boards at each participating site [6]. Written informed consent was obtained from participants or guardians prior to enrollment, and written assent was secured from participants aged 11–18 years. Use of samples in Melbourne was approved by WEHI HREC (23/35).

Study Samples

Day 0 plasma samples from 474 participants from the PRIMA study were utilized. PRIMA recruited patients with uncomplicated P. falciparum mono infection diagnosed by microscopy who had experienced fever or a history of fever within 48 hours (Supplementary Text 1).

During days 1 to 6, patients attended daily clinic visits for directly supervised treatment. Blood-stage treatment was given according to national treatment guidelines, which follow WHO recommendations to use artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) as first-line treatment for uncomplicated P. falciparum malaria. The ACT regimen varied by country: artemether–lumefantrine in Ethiopia and Bangladesh, and dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine in Indonesia [6]. In the standard care arm, patients also received a single oral dose of supervised primaquine (0.25 mg/kg) in conjunction with their schizonticidal treatment, as per national treatment guidelines. Patients were monitored weekly throughout the follow-up period (day 7 to 63). Participants were advised to seek medical attention at the study center should they develop any symptoms outside scheduled visits. Microscopy was performed on all weekly and unscheduled visits. Notably, in prior studies of P. vivax SEMs, the outcome measured has been PCR-detectable P. vivax infection, and not microscopy-detectable P. vivax episodes.

For serological classification algorithm evaluation and training of the dataset-specific algorithm, only control-arm samples were used (n = 244). All 474 samples were used to analyze the impact of epidemiological and demographic factors on antibody levels.

P. vivax positive control samples from Papua New Guinea served as standard references [17], with negative control samples from malaria-naive individuals in Bangkok, Rio de Janeiro, and Melbourne [10].

Total IgG Antibody Assay

The preparation of P. vivax protein-coupled beads followed established protocols [17], with final bead suspensions stored at 4°C until further use (see Supplementary Text 1). The proteins used were selected from a validated P. vivax serology panel for their ability to classify individuals as recently exposed to P. vivax parasites within the preceding 9 months, as described [10].

IgG antibody binding was evaluated using the Luminex MAGPIX instrument, as previously described [17]. 50μL of P. vivax protein-coupled beads were combined with 50μL of diluted plasma (1:100) and incubated for 30 minutes, followed by incubation with anti-human IgG phycoerythrin (1:100) for 15 minutes. Each plate included a log-linear standard curve and 2 blank wells as negative controls. All serology data are available in Supplementary Data 1.

The raw median fluorescence intensity (MFI) values were converted to relative antibody units using a protein-specific standard curve, as previously reported [10], performed with a 5-parameter logistic regression model (https://shaziaruybal.shinyapps.io/COVIDClassifyR/). Assay passed quality control if wells contained >15 beads, and plates with blank wells had MFI values <50.

Serology Classification Analysis

The primary outcome measured (that we were aiming to predict by serology) was microscopy-detected vivax episodes (with or without symptoms). These were detected throughout the 63-day follow-up by monitoring for malaria by microscopy weekly, and for symptoms outside this schedule. This data was already available from the main PRIMA trial. The antibody data generated in this study was used to classify samples through a Random Forest serological classification algorithm, which determined seropositivity (indicative of P. vivax infection within the past 9 months) or seronegativity (no P. vivax infection in the same period). Only antibody levels were used as predictors. Serological classification algorithm outputs per individual were a binary classification of recently exposed (seropositive) or not (seronegative) based on majority voting across multiple decision trees. Adjusting the voting threshold refined sensitivity and specificity parameters.

The serological classification algorithm was previously trained using data on longitudinal cohort studies from Thailand, Brazil, and the Solomon Islands [10], utilizing a panel of 8 P. vivax proteins identified as robust markers of recent exposure (code: github.com/dionnecargy/PvSeroApp). These settings were considered low transmission, and while it is difficult to compare malaria transmission across countries and studies, we consider these cohorts to have lower transmission than the PRIMA study settings.

A modified version of the serological classification algorithm, tailored to this dataset, was also implemented (Supplementary Text 1). Various serological classification algorithms utilized in this study are outlined in Supplementary Table 1. The classification performance of all algorithms were assessed through a confusion matrix (Supplementary Table 2) with sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value (NPV) with maximized threshold (maximum sensitivity and specificity) calculated as detailed in Supplementary Text 1. Performance was evaluated using all available data and at a country-specific level. When comparing algorithms, the area under the curve (AUC) of receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curves was also considered.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (version 2024.04.0) and GraphPad PRISM (version 10.3.0). Relative antibody units differences by site and age were tested using the Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn's post hoc, and by gender using the Mann–Whitney U test. Kaplan–Meier curves assessed time-to-relapse, with group differences evaluated statistically. A P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

High Sensitivity, but Limited Specificity, of P. vivax Serological Exposure Markers When Applied to P. falciparum Samples

The primary objective was to determine whether P. vivax SEMs could reliably identify patients with P. falciparum malaria at greatest risk of P. vivax relapse, to facilitate targeted universal radical cure. IgG antibody levels were measured against a panel of 8 P. vivax antigens, and a previously trained random forest serological classification algorithm applied to the dataset to define each individual as recently exposed to P. vivax and at risk of relapse, or not (Figure 1A). Of the 244 individuals, 181 were classified as seropositive and 63 as seronegative (Figure 1B). At the maximized random forest votes threshold, the serological classification algorithm had high sensitivity, correctly classifying 81.8% (18/22) P. falciparum individuals who presented with microscopy-detectable P. vivax parasitemia during follow up, but poor specificity at 26.6% (59/222). There was a high rate of false positives, with many individuals classified as seropositive who did not have a subsequent P. vivax microscopy-detectable episode within 63 days (Figure 1B). Kaplan-Meier (time-to-event) analysis demonstrated a high episode-free probability of P. vivax for both groups throughout the study (Figure 1C, P = .49). By day 60, the probability of patients being P. vivax-free was 91.8% for seronegative and 89.7% for seropositive groups. We hypothesized that poor specificity could be due to antibody cross-reactivity, in which P. falciparum parasitemia boosted the antibody response to P. vivax proteins, although this effect was not observed in the initial validation study of the P. vivax SEMs [10]. Importantly, the NPV was high (93.7% [59/63], Figure 1B), with the majority of seronegative individuals having no microscopy-detected vivax episodes during follow-up.

Limited Evidence of Cross-reactivity Contributing to Poor Classification Performance

All 8 P. vivax proteins have low levels of sequence identity with their P. falciparum orthologs (<50%, Supplementary Table 3), and we previously demonstrated significant (>10-fold) cross-reactivity between P. vivax and P. knowlesi only when sequence identity was >65% [18]. To address the potential impact of the P. falciparum infection boosting IgG to the P. vivax SEMs, we used a modified serological classification algorithm that excluded P. vivax proteins with known temporary increases in IgG levels 7-days postclinical P. falciparum: RAMA, RBP2b, PTEX-150, and PvEBP (manuscript in preparation). These proteins were excluded in the “Longitudinal Low Pf” algorithm, trained on existing data from the published longitudinal cohort studies [10]. A second version, “Longitudinal Low Pf with RBP2b,” was also tested, given the importance of RBP2b in the classification [10]. Excluding potentially cross-reactive proteins improved sensitivity from 81.8% (18/22) to 90.9% (20/22), but reduced specificity from 26.6% (59/222) to 14.0% (31/222). Including RBP2b increased specificity slightly to 31.1% (61/222) suggesting that excluding potential cross-reactive proteins did not significantly improve classification performance for the PRIMA dataset (Table 1).

Impact of Past Exposure and Transmission Intensity on Classification Performance

The P. vivax SEMs were designed for use in low-transmission settings [19]. We hypothesized that the degree of past exposure present in areas with higher transmission intensity [15, 19], may have contributed to the poor specificity of the P. vivax SEMs in the PRIMA patients. This was explored through surrogate measures of location, age, and gender.

Study samples were collected from 3 sites [6], Bangladesh (annual parasite index [API] 21.06), Indonesia (API in 3 study regions varying from 6.07 to 36.98) [6], and Ethiopia (API unknown) [6]. The risk of P. vivax microscopy-detectable parasitemia during follow-up varied, ranging from 12.7% in Ethiopia, 16.9% in Bangladesh, and 2.2% in Indonesia [6]. Although this variance may have been due to differences in local transmission intensity, the different schizonticidals may have also played a role. In Indonesia, patients received dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine, a slowly eliminated drug, whereas in Ethiopia and Bangladesh patients received artemether–lumefantrine, which has shorter elimination and post-treatment prophylaxis. Few microscopy-detected vivax episodes were observed in Indonesia (1 out of 50 patients) and Bangladesh (3 out of 24 patients); thus, results are driven by Ethiopian data. However, sensitivity and specificity were consistent across countries (Supplementary Figure 1). IgG levels were compared across sites for the 8 P. vivax SEMs (Figure 2A); levels for Pvs16, MSP8, and RBP2b did not differ significantly. For the other antigens, IgG levels were higher in Bangladesh, with the exception of MSP1-19 which was significantly elevated in Ethiopia.

Age is a surrogate marker of exposure, with a trend of higher antibody levels in older individuals signifying greater lifetime exposure. We categorized participants into 4 age groups (<5, 6–18, 19–60, and >60 years) based on societal roles and life stage, noting that no children were enrolled in Ethiopia. Comparisons between age groups demonstrated significant variations in antibody level for several proteins (6/8) (Figure 2B), with adults exhibiting higher levels than children, particularly evident for PvEBP, MSP5 and RBP2b.

We observed no significant differences in IgG level between men and women for any of the 8 P. vivax SEMs (Supplementary Figure 2).

Improving Classification Accuracy in P. falciparum Patients

To overcome the limitations identified, we developed a PRIMA study-specific serological classification algorithm tailored to the local epidemiology, using serology data from the trial itself. Proteins with potential P. falciparum cross-reactivity were excluded, as their removal did not reduce sensitivity in earlier analyses. The PRIMA-specific algorithm showed an improved balance between sensitivity and specificity, with a specificity of up to 68.2% (Table 2). However, this was still lower than reported in the original longitudinal cohort studies (80%) [10]. Importantly, these prior studies utilized PCR for detection of sub-microscopic P. vivax infections. PCR was not performed during PRIMA follow-up, and due to the higher sensitivity of PCR compared with microscopy, may have resulted in missed asymptomatic sub-microscopic P. vivax infections which could contribute to the lower specificity.

Performance Summary of Each Serological Classification Algorithm With Different Specificities Target

To improve specificity within the current study, random forest voting thresholds were adjusted to target specificities of 85%, 90%, and 95% (Table 2). As the specificity target increased, sensitivity decreased for all algorithms. The modified specificity targets were also tested on the original and low Pf adjusted algorithms trained on the longitudinal cohort data, reaching a more balanced sensitivity and specificity at either the 90% or 95% specificity target. The best-balanced result overall remained with the PRIMA specific low Pf algorithm at 68% sensitivity and 68% specificity (NPV was high with all options tested). These tradeoffs between sensitivity and specificity are visualized in ROC curves (Figure 3), with AUC to quantify overall performance; the original model on the PRIMA dataset had an AUC of 0.583, whilst both dataset-specific models improved to 0.716.

DISCUSSION

Plasmodium vivax continues to pose a challenge for malaria elimination due to difficulties in treatment [20], control [21] and surveillance [19]. Novel approaches are needed to eliminate the undetected hypnozoite reservoir that maintains transmission within communities. Universal radical cure opportunistically eradicates P. vivax liver-stage hypnozoites that may be present in P. falciparum patients in co-endemic areas. PRIMA demonstrated that high-dose short-course primaquine reduced the risk of subsequent P. vivax microscopy-detected episodes in P. falciparum patients within 63 days by 5-fold, with the greatest effect in Ethiopia, where incidence of both species was greatest [6]. Universal radical cure has potential to reduce the hypnozoite reservoir, however the public health benefit will likely vary between different endemic settings [9] and is not yet recommended in WHO malaria treatment guidelines. Endemic countries may be reluctant due to concerns over the risk-benefit ratio, exposing patients who might not have hypnozoites to 8-aminoquinoline treatment. G6PD testing can mitigate this risk but is associated with logistical and financial challenges. A more targeted approach to universal radical cure using P. vivax SEMs as an additional screening tool could alleviate some concerns. Whilst we found limited specificity of P. vivax SEMs to predict risk of subsequent P. vivax microscopy-detectable episodes in patients with P. falciparum malaria, the NPV was high. Thus, P. vivax SEMs could be considered to limit exposing people to 8-aminoquinolines unnecessarily, and to reduce the numbers of G6PD-tests and treatment doses required. The high sensitivity (82%) suggests that most individuals with a subsequent microscopy-detectable episode of P. vivax were identified. However, whether this is sufficient to reduce transmission, and whether the costs of the P. vivax SEM assay are balanced by reduced numbers of G6PD-tests and 8-aminolquinoline treatment doses, warrants further study.

We explored various reasons for the low specificity, resulting from the high level of the P. falciparum patients classified as seropositive who did not have subsequent P. vivax microscopy-detected episodes. We modified the algorithm to remove P. vivax antigens potentially cross-reactive in P. falciparum (based on unpublished work). This provided no improvement in specificity, suggesting another cause for the high seropositivity rates. However, potential cross-reactive antigens were based on data from low-transmission settings; antibody cross-reactivity may differ in areas of moderate or high transmission and could be further assessed. This is broadly important for using P. vivax SEMs in surveillance and guiding interventions in co-endemic areas, such as P. vivax-endemic regions of Africa.

The high levels of seropositivity and low specificity of the serological classification algorithm likely reflect the higher transmission settings in PRIMA (particularly Ethiopia and Bangladesh) compared with previous evaluations. P. vivax SEMs were designed for use in low-transmission, near-elimination settings, but in higher transmission areas, antibody responses to the same P. vivax antigens can be longer-lasting [22], thus reducing P. vivax SEM performance [15]. PRIMA sites were selected because of higher case burden (in the context of their country-specific epidemiology) to facilitate recruitment of clinical malaria patients.

We developed a serological classification algorithm trained on the PRIMA serological data itself where specificity improved to 68% (with reduced sensitivity to 68%), though this needs validation on an independent cohort to test its generalizability in similar transmission settings. Other factors may have contributed to the poor performance of the P. vivax SEMs in P. falciparum patients in these settings. Patient follow-up was curtailed at 63 days and whilst this may have been sufficient to detect most relapses [23], they can occur at least up to 9-months following primary infection [11], thus some individuals identified as being seropositive may have had an episode of P. vivax after the end of follow-up. Importantly, screening for P. vivax parasitemia was done by microscopy and not by PCR, and low-density sub-microscopic infections may have been missed [24]. It is also possible that some patients received radical cure for earlier P. vivax infections in line with national guidelines, removing their hypnozoite reservoir, while they would still appear SEM positive. However, in practice, radical cure is often not prescribed, not fully adhered to [25] and current regimens have sub-optimal efficacy [26].

Conclusions

Our data provides limited evidence for using P. vivax SEMs to guide radical cure in P. falciparum patients, when microscopy-detectable vivax episodes is the outcome clinicians are trying to prevent. High sensitivity and NPV of the testing offer potential benefits in reducing numbers of individuals requiring G6PD testing and exposed to radical cure compared with universal treatment of all patients presenting with P. falciparum. However, this needs to be balanced against the increased cost and logistical considerations. The usefulness of P. vivax SEMs likely depends on how countries and program managers view the cost-benefit ratio of these options.

Performance and implementation are also likely to vary by transmission setting. In high-transmission settings, elevated baseline antibody responses can reduce SEM specificity unless models are trained and calibrated locally using multi-antigen panels. This constraint is likely less pronounced in lower-transmission settings, where the SEMs have been trained and are suitable for guiding treatment decisions for individuals at risk of relapse; in higher-transmission contexts, however, they are likely to be more useful for identifying community-level risk and guiding other targeted interventions, ideally alongside sensitive diagnostics for acute infections, such as PCR where available.

As our study was conducted in relatively high transmission settings, with most data from Ethiopia, it is possible that the P. vivax SEMs may be more accurate in guiding radical cure in P. falciparum patients in lower transmission settings. However, the risk of P. vivax following P. falciparum infection decreases with reduced overall disease burden [27], likely limiting the impact of universal radical cure on transmission. In these settings, community-wide approaches like P. vivax serological testing and treatment [19] may be more beneficial. Importantly, the SEM assay described requires a laboratory and trained staff, however, it is feasible to develop a point-of-contact test to measure these antibody biomarkers.
