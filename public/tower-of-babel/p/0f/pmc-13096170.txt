Evaluating a digital decision aid for atrial fibrillation rhythm control in a hybrid implementation-effectiveness trial
Authors: Meghan Reading Turchioe, Afra Shamnath, David Slotwiner, Yihong Zhao, Deepak Saluja, Seth Goldbarg, JoonHyuk Kim, Paul Varosy, Angelo Biviano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDigital decision aids significantly improve shared decision-making outcomes, but barriers to implementation in clinical settings remain. We conducted a Hybrid Type 2 implementation-effectiveness trial of an atrial fibrillation rhythm control decision aid (clinicaltrials.gov NCT04993807; registered 08/06/2021) among 75 older adults across two sites. Guided by the RE-AIM framework, we assessed decision quality and implementation outcomes. While the decision aid was highly acceptable and broadly adopted, changes in decisional conflict and self-efficacy varied widely, with no significant average improvement across the cohort. Subgroup and qualitative analyses revealed that the decision aid was most effective when delivered to the right patient, at the right time, and in the right clinical context. Barriers included variability in health literacy, digital access, and timing of delivery relative to the clinical decision-making process. Findings underscore the challenges of deploying digital interventions within real-world workflows and highlight the importance of targeting decision support tools based on patient readiness, literacy, and care context.Subject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Shared decision-making (SDM) is often preached but challenging to promise in clinical care. SDM is encouraged when patients and their care teams are deciding upon two or more treatments that have clinical equipoise, and thus should be guided equally by clinical considerations and the patients’ values and goals of care1. SDM is strongly encouraged for a wide range of clinical decisions because it improves patient knowledge, treatment adherence, satisfaction, and reduces decisional conflict2. Moreover, decision aids, which present evidence about treatment options and help patients clarify their preferences, can be useful tools to increase patients’ understanding, engagement, and satisfaction in SDM3–5. Because the definition of quality of life can vary widely for each person, without decision aids, many patients struggle to navigate complex decisions to identify treatments that will help them optimize their quality of life. In cardiovascular medicine, for example, decision aids have been developed for coronary artery disease, heart failure, and stroke prevention, yielding improvements in decision quality such as greater patient knowledge and reduced decisional conflict6.

Despite their benefits, the real-world implementation of decision aids beyond trial contexts faces persistent challenges, including meaningful integration into busy clinical workflows7. Clinicians report wanting to have thorough SDM conversations but facing competing demands on their time and limited resources to guide conversations8. Acknowledging these challenges, the International Patient Decision Aid Standards (IPDAS) Collaboration published an updated roadmap for implementing decision aids in routine clinical settings that provides a foundation for tackling these challenges9. Key recommendations include co-production or local adaptation of decision aids, training for the entire care team, proactive preparation and prompting of patients, securing leadership support, and establishing feedback mechanisms to drive continuous improvement.

This roadmap serves as a valuable starting point for implementing decision aids in clinical settings; by applying it in diverse contexts, we can advance the framework to uncover and respond to the unique, often overlooked barriers that affect digital tool uptake. For example, digital inclusion remains a challenge as decision aids increasingly transition to web-based or mobile formats10. Older adults, individuals with limited digital literacy, and marginalized populations often face substantial barriers to using digital decision aids, as many modern decision aids are web-based. These include lack of access to reliable internet or devices, difficulty navigating complex interfaces, low trust in digital tools, and sensory or cognitive impairments that affect engagement11. While the IPDAS review acknowledges the importance of human-centered design with patients, more work remains to address the nuanced support systems required for digitally excluded users.

To address this gap, we conducted a Hybrid Type 2 implementation-effectiveness design, combining a clinical feasibility trial (clinicaltrials.gov NCT04993807; registered 08/06/2021) with a structured implementation analysis to evaluate a decision aid tailored to older adults. This study was guided by the RE-AIM framework, a widely used implementation science model that evaluates health interventions across five dimensions: Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance12. The RE-AIM framework helps researchers and practitioners assess not only whether an intervention works, but also for whom, in what settings, and how it can be sustained over time.

We developed a decision aid for atrial fibrillation (AF), which is freely available at www.af-decisionaid.org. AF is the most common cardiac arrhythmia affecting 10% of older adults age 65 and older13. In the context of AF, extensive work has supported SDM for selecting stroke prevention strategies such as oral anticoagulants14. Another important decision that has received comparatively less attention is rate and rhythm management, particularly whether to take antiarrhythmic medications versus undergo a catheter ablation, a minimally invasive procedure aimed at restoring normal sinus rhythm and reducing symptom burden15. SDM for rate and rhythm control is a Class 1 recommendation in the most recent AF guidelines15, but few resources exist to support this decision14. For example, in a large survey study of adults with AF, only 22% reported participating in SDM when choosing a rhythm control approach, and more than half of those who did not engage in SDM felt they did not understand the different treatment options available16. Our prior research has confirmed that patients and clinicians agree there is a need to provide more information among adults with AF presenting for rhythm control17.

Results

Reach

A total of 75 adults with AF were recruited into this trial. Participant characteristics are described in Table 1. Participants had a mean age of 66 (standard deviation [SD] 12) years, 33% were female and 66% were male, 83% were White, 9% were Black or African American, 3% Asian, and 3% Hispanic/Latino. Half (47%) had inadequate health literacy. The mean subjective numeracy score was 14 (SD 4) out of 18, indicating moderate levels of numeracy. Many participants (38%) preferred to have equal or less agency in decision-making than their doctor.

We describe the exclusion cascade for participants who were screened, contacted, and recruited into the study in Supplementary Fig. 1. We recruited two cohorts. First, manual screening was conducted among patients referred to the electrophysiology (EP) clinics. Second, partway through the study, we began utilizing portal-based screening, which included any patients with an AF diagnosis and no history of ablation across the medical center.

To assess responder bias, we further compared the recruited patients to all potentially eligible patients at our health system who were screened for this study using both manual and patient portal recruitment methods in Supplementary Table 1. The recruited participants were similar demographically to all eligible patients in the cohort that was screened manually (i.e., EP practice patients), but compared to those screened via the portal, they were slightly older (mean 67 vs. 64 years), more were male (67% vs. 58%), and fewer were white (83% vs. 88%).

Effectiveness

The primary endpoints evaluating the effectiveness of the decision aid were decisional conflict and decision self-efficacy at baseline versus two weeks post-intervention. Fifty-eight (77%) completed the two-week follow-up surveys, and 45 (60%) completed the three-month follow-up surveys. A comparison of the characteristics of participants who completed the follow-up surveys compared to all recruited participants reveals no meaningful differences in sociodemographic characteristics, suggesting limited effects of responder bias (Supplementary Table 2).

Among participants who completed the two-week follow-up surveys, the total mean decisional conflict score at baseline was 25 out of 100 on average, with 100 representing higher decisional conflict (Table 2). The mean score did not significantly change at two weeks (p = 0.175). There were no differences from baseline to two weeks when we examined mean scores for each subscale of the Decisional Conflict Scale (Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2).

As shown in Table 2, when the sample was stratified by their baseline decisional conflict score, those with low baseline scores (<25) had significantly higher decisional conflict at two weeks (p = 0.005), while those with high baseline scores (≥25) had significantly lower decisional conflict at two weeks (p = 0.02).

In stratified analyses by baseline decisional conflict (Supplementary Table 4), participants with higher conflict were more likely to have adequate general health literacy but lower digital health literacy. Specifically, 63% of participants with high decisional conflict had adequate health literacy compared with 50% among those with low conflict, whereas only 16% of participants with high conflict demonstrated high digital health literacy versus 47% of those with low conflict.

To explore this finding further, we classified the sample into sub-groups based on their changes in decisional conflict from baseline to two weeks, using the same definitions of high versus low decisional conflict, resulting in four groups: (1) high baseline, high post-intervention (n = 6, 10%), (2) high baseline, low post-intervention (n = 7, 12%), (3) low baseline, high post-intervention (n = 11, 19%), and (4) low baseline, low post-intervention (n = 34, 59%). Group 2 represents the ideal outcome, as these participants had high decisional conflict, which was reportedly lower after viewing the decision aid. When descriptively examining differences in the proportion of participants in each of the four groups by their sociodemographic and clinical characteristics (Supplementary Table 4), more participants in group 2 were recruited via the patient portal (i.e., across the entire medical center, as opposed to only in the EP clinic) compared to the other groups. Interestingly, only one participant in group 2 said the decision aid made their decision easier, whereas more people in group 4 said it was easier, suggesting a poor correlation between decisional conflict scores and the self-reported effects of the decision aid.

The overall decision self-efficacy score at baseline was 41 out of 100 on average, with 100 representing higher self-efficacy (Table 2). The score did not significantly change on average at two weeks (p = 0.236). When the sample was stratified by their baseline decision self-efficacy score, those with high baseline scores had significantly lower self-efficacy at two weeks (p < 0.001). Those with low baseline scores did not have significantly higher self-efficacy at two weeks (p = 0.094).

Finally, we measured Decision Regret Scale scores at three months (Fig. 1). Among participants who completed three-month surveys, most participants reported they had opted for medication management (n = 26, 58%), 13 opted for ablation (29%), and six (13%) chose another treatment option. The overall median Decision Regret Scale score was 45 on average (interquartile range [IQR] 30) on a scale of 0 to 100, with 100 representing higher regret. When stratified by treatment type, the median Decision Regret Scale for was lowest for patients who opted for ablation (25 [IQR 15]), and highest for those who opted for medications (50 [IQR 24]).

Adoption

Adoption of the decision aid was evaluated through both an adoption survey completed at two weeks (reported fully in Supplementary Table 5) and qualitative interviews. Among participants completing the adoption survey (n = 58), all reported viewing the decision aid (100%). Most viewed it for 5–10 min (40%) or 10–20 min (28%), and most viewed it only once (69%). Nearly half viewed it on a computer (48%), but many viewed it on a smartphone (36%). Half of the participants planned to discuss the decision aid with their healthcare team (50%), and 21% reported having already discussed it. Approximately one-third (29%) viewed it with a family member or friend.

Three qualitative sub-themes related to adoption emerged from the interviews: the current process, facilitators, and barriers. Physicians described the current process for supporting patients through this treatment decision as relying primarily on verbal explanations, occasionally supplemented by existing educational materials. None reported using standardized decision aids in routine practice.

Facilitators to adoption included patients’ perceptions that the decision aid was timely, personally relevant, and provided valuable informational support during periods of uncertainty or pressure. Most patients were open to using the tool, although they were at varying stages of the decision-making process, ranging from being unfamiliar with catheter ablation to having already decided to pursue the procedure. Several participants noted that the decision aid was also useful for helping caregivers understand the treatment pathway. Among those who were undecided, the tool offered reassurance and helped confirm that they had considered all necessary information.

Reported barriers included patients’ pre-existing treatment preferences, cultural norms regarding deference to physician authority, and the inability to use the decision aid among patients with non-English-speaking patients, whom clinicians reported may stand to benefit most from the decision aid.

Implementation

Implementation of the decision aid was quantified through the Acceptability of Decision Aid Scale completed at two weeks (reported in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 6) and described further in qualitative interviews. The majority of participants rated the content on each page as “good” (53–71%) or “excellent” (12–28%). Most felt the decision aid length and amount of information were “just right” (81 and 71%, respectively). Most felt it presented a balanced view of treatment options (65%), though many felt it slanted towards ablation (35%). Two-thirds (67%) reported that the decision aid contained enough information to make their decision, while one-third (33%) felt it did not contain enough information. Approximately one-quarter (26%) reported it made their decision easier, a few (7%) that it made their decision harder, while the majority reported that it had no effect on decision-making (67%).

Interview data revealed six key sub-themes related to implementation of the decision aid: facilitators to use, timing of access, duration of engagement, use with others, feedback on design, and perceived barriers.

Regarding facilitators, patients described the decision aid as a valuable tool for reinforcing their understanding, enhancing visual learning, and increasing confidence in making or affirming treatment decisions. Many noted that it was helpful to have additional time to review information and consider their options, particularly given the limited time and heightened stress often experienced during clinical consultations.

The timing of decision aid access varied across participants. Some patients reviewed it immediately after receiving the link, while others waited until after their consultation. Both patients and clinicians suggested offering the decision aid prior to the clinical encounter to support preparation, though some acknowledged that certain content might be more meaningful after speaking with the physician.

There was substantial variability in how long patients engaged with the decision aid. For some, it was a brief, one-time review. Others returned to it multiple times, shared it with a spouse or family member, and continued to revisit the information over several weeks or months as they processed their treatment options.

Patients described viewing the decision aid either alone or with a partner; while some expressed an intention to discuss it with their clinician, few physicians reported patients bringing up the tool during the visit, suggesting that its use remained independent of the clinical conversation.

Feedback on the design was generally positive. Visual elements such as graphs and color schemes were highlighted as particularly helpful in conveying complex information. Some patients offered suggestions for improvement, including clearer labeling, more distinct color contrast, and enhancements to navigation features, which they felt were not prominent enough.

No participants reported major technical or usability barriers with the tool. However, some expressed a desire for more detailed information, particularly concerning procedural risks and medication side effects. Others noted that the range of treatment options was limited, with minimal discussion of therapies beyond medication.

Overall, both patients and clinicians expressed satisfaction with the decision aid. It was described as clear, non-disruptive, and consistent with the information provided during the consultation. In some cases, it helped to reinforce patients’ preferred treatment choices. No participants reported confusion about their decision resulting from its use.

Maintenance

Two key sub-themes emerged regarding future use and sustainability of the decision aid: facilitators to broader adoption and remaining barriers to implementation.

Many patients indicated they would recommend the decision aid to others facing similar treatment decisions, describing it as a helpful resource for navigating complex information. Several suggested that future versions could be improved by tailoring content to specific AF subtypes and incorporating personalized risk estimates to increase relevance and precision. Clinicians expressed cautious optimism about the potential for artificial intelligence (AI) to support individualized decision-making, particularly through predictive modeling. However, they noted that existing models currently lack the accuracy and clinical applicability required to inform high-stakes treatment decisions.

Barriers to broader implementation centered around timing, workflow integration, and infrastructure. Participants emphasized the importance of introducing the decision aid earlier in the care continuum and ensuring it aligns more directly with key clinical encounters. Clinicians also described structural challenges in workflows, including time constraints and lack of reimbursement for patient education. Limited digital literacy among some patient populations was also cited as a barrier. To address this, clinicians proposed alternative outreach strategies, such as text messaging, to increase accessibility and uptake.

Discussion

In this hybrid feasibility-implementation trial of 75 older adults with AF, we found high acceptability of a digital decision aid for rhythm control but limited impact on decisional conflict or self-efficacy across the entire sample. However, exploratory subgroup analyses revealed heterogeneity in response, with some patients experiencing reduced conflict and others experiencing an increase. Qualitative findings suggested this variation was driven by differences in baseline readiness, timing of the intervention, and clinical context. These results underscore the importance of tailoring decision aid delivery to the right patient, at the right time, and in the right care setting. We undertook extensive co-design activities to develop the decision aid in an effort to promote digital inclusion of older adult populations; this study highlights additional contextual factors related to clinical workflow that also influence digital inclusion.

A key takeaway from our trial is the need to identify the right patient populations for digital decision aids, particularly when there is substantial variability in health literacy, digital literacy, information needs, and decisional readiness. Nearly half of our cohort had inadequate health literacy, a known barrier to engagement with digital health tools. Without careful design that prioritizes accessibility and inclusivity, digital interventions risk excluding older adults, minoritized populations, and those with lower digital confidence, potentially exacerbating disparities in care and leading to intervention-generated inequality18. Studies show that decision aids tailored for patients with low literacy improve outcomes, especially when visuals and plain language are used effectively19. However, this may cause lower engagement among some higher literacy patients; in our study, some participants found the tool empowering and informative, while others hoped for more detailed and nuanced information, indicating that even well-designed tools may not be universally useful without adaptive delivery strategies.

We observed divergent patterns for general health literacy and digital health literacy, which provide important context for interpreting the effects of the decision aid intervention. While participants with higher decisional conflict demonstrated adequate capacity to comprehend health information, lower digital health literacy may have limited their ability to access or engage with decision-relevant content prior to intervention. This distinction highlights the role of decision aids in reducing decisional conflict by not only presenting evidence-based information but also by lowering barriers to information access. Decision aids that are intentionally designed for usability across varying levels of digital literacy, which we confirmed during our co-design work20, may therefore help mitigate decisional conflict even among patients who are otherwise health literate.

Additionally, prior work suggests that general AF knowledge alone does not reliably translate into patients’ ability to estimate personal risk in treatment decision-making21. This highlights a gap between disease literacy and meaningful risk interpretation. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of decision aids for AF anticoagulation demonstrated that tools incorporating explicit, comparative risk and benefit information reduce decisional conflict and modestly increase uptake of guideline-concordant therapy across diverse patient populations, independent of baseline health literacy22. Consistent with this, limited health literacy is frequently under-recognized in AF care and is associated with impaired shared decision-making and treatment adherence, underscoring the importance of decision aids that translate clinical evidence into patient-relevant outcomes while scaffolding both understanding and confidence23.

Timing also emerged as a critical determinant of decision aid effectiveness. Although we only recruited participants before a scheduled consultation, in reality, they were at varying stages of readiness for an ablation, from pre-contemplative to already committed to ablation even before their consultation. When introduced too late, after a decision had effectively been made, the tool added little value. In some cases, it increased decisional conflict by introducing new considerations after preferences had already solidified. Our baseline data showing that many participants had low control preferences in decision-making suggests it is also possible that some participants had deferred the decision to their care team without adequately understanding the decision, causing increased conflict and lower self-efficacy after receiving more information about the options. These findings mirror prior work on decision aids in cancer care, which shows that late delivery can increase confusion and distress rather than support decision-making19. Our study highlights the challenge of integrating decision support tools precisely in pragmatic settings where care pathways vary.

Finally, the context in which decision aids are delivered also influences their impact. Our dual-recruitment strategy allowed us to compare experiences between patients referred to a subspecialty clinic (i.e., the EP clinic) and those identified broadly across the medical center. Patients seen in EP clinics represent a select group, often having already been deemed procedural candidates by referring physicians. In contrast, those recruited via the portal included a more diverse cohort in terms of clinical severity, health access, and openness to various treatment options. Multiple studies have shown that disparities in referral to EP and in catheter ablation rates exist for women and minoritized populations24–26. Implementation strategies should prioritize earlier points of care such as primary care or emergency departments. Moreover, integration into clinical workflows remains a major barrier. Lack of time during visits and poor EHR integration are commonly cited challenges9. Our qualitative data echo these concerns. To be impactful at scale, decision aids must be embedded seamlessly within clinical systems and workflows, not layered on top of them.

Ideal workflows will look different for each health system, but could include sending educational materials about treatment options to patients when scheduling an initial consult for AF. Digital outreach via the patient portal helped expand reach across clinical services, suggesting promise for broader dissemination, but a just-in-time delivery mechanism, perhaps tied to referral or diagnosis, may better optimize timing. Capturing subgroups of patients who are systematically under-referred for consults will also be important at this stage, but aided by technical solutions like portals. Engaging clinicians who are frequently referring patients for consults in the process may also assist with delivering materials at the correct time. During the encounter, patients could then engage in shared decision-making during the encounter about appropriate treatments, and receive personalized decision aids afterwards based on the conversation points. Though challenging from a feasibility perspective, large language models (LLMs) and other AI-based tools could advance this model of care. Future work should emphasize adaptability to individual patient needs, which is now more possible than ever with the availability of LLMs. Personalizing content based on AF subtype, language, and digital fluency could increase relevance. LLMs may also help incorporate newer evidence reflecting advances in clinical care. Although wearable-derived AF predictors and objective rhythm measures are increasingly used in clinical practice to guide therapy and minimize medication-related adverse effects, such approaches were not incorporated into the decision aid evaluated here due to the requirement that decision aids rely on high quality evidence, for example from randomized trials and meta-analyses27,28. This highlights a broader challenge in decision aid development, whereby rapidly evolving technologies often outpace the accumulation of evidence required for inclusion, limiting the ability of decision aids to fully reflect current clinical practice. Additionally, leveraging predictive models to estimate procedural success rates may enhance decision confidence, though clinical stakeholders in our study expressed skepticism about the current readiness of AI tools to deliver this information accurately. Emerging AI models have shown early success in predicting post-ablation outcomes29,30, but more clinical validation work is needed to ensure readiness for point-of-care use. Finally, our findings align with calls from the IPDAS Collaboration to proactively prepare and prompt patients before visits, ensure tools are accessible across languages and literacy levels, and involve full care teams in implementation9.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at two sites within a single health system, which may limit generalizability. Second, the absence of a control group and the reliance on pre-post comparisons introduce potential for bias in estimating intervention effects. Third, attrition at follow-up may have influenced findings, though characteristics of responders and non-responders were similar. Finally, our power analysis assumed a large effect size, and thus we were underpowered to detect smaller effect sizes. Similarly, while we explored subgroup variation in outcome trajectories, this study was not powered to detect statistically significant differences across these subgroups, and such analyses remain exploratory.

Methods

Study Design and participants

This study followed a Hybrid Type 2 design, assessing both effectiveness through a feasibility trial (NCT04993807, registered 8/4/2021) and implementation outcomes using the RE-AIM framework. This single-arm, pre-post study design, shown in Fig. 3, involved delivering a web-based decision aid to patients around the time of their ablation consultation with changes in decision quality assessed over time. The trial was conducted across two sites in the NewYork-Presbyterian Health System, Columbia University Irving Medical Center (CUIMC) and NYP-Queens Hospital (NYP-Q). This trial was approved by the Columbia University Irving Medical Center Institutional Review Board (Protocol #AAAU2028) with a reliance agreement from the NewYork-Presbyterian-Queens Institutional Review Board (Protocol #AAAU2028).

Design of the single-arm hybrid trial.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) Diagnosis of symptomatic AF confirmed by the treating cardiologist and/or in the EHR, (2) Scheduled consultation at CUIMC or NYP-Q to discuss ablation, (3) Age 18 years and older, (4) Able to read and speak English, and (5) Willing and able to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) Prior history of ablation to treat AF, (2) Concomitant terminal illness, psychiatric illness, or cognitive impairment that would prevent the ability to provide informed consent or participate in the study, and (3) Lack of a functioning email address.

Intervention

The intervention consists of a web-based decision aid developed by our team, which consists of physicians and nurses with cardiology and informatics expertise, in partnership with a web development company (Fig. 4). This intervention was guided by the Adapted Model of Shared Decision-Making31. This model outlines the high-level constructs that are part of the decision-making context (antecedents), processes, and outcomes. Specifically, to address decision antecedents, we integrated information about the clinical context in which the decision was happening (i.e., existing resources, clinical workflows), perceived gaps in care, and preferences for decision support by conducting a formative qualitative needs assessment with patients and clinicians17. The decision aid was intended to be used in the decision-making process prior to or after the consultation as complementary guidance and education for patients, based on the findings of the needs assessment. We thus designed and iterated on a prototype that would be useful at this point in the care delivery process20. Decisional outcomes were measured as part of the evaluation plan.

Example screens of the atrial fibrillation rhythm control decision aid website.

The decision aid explains what AF is, two common treatment options (including risks and benefits of each), personal values to consider, and evidence on the outcomes after each treatment option. To ensure usability among older adults, the decision aid was co-designed with patients and clinicians using best practices established by IPDAS32 and gerontechnological design principals33, as described in our prior work17,20. We designed the decision aid in a four-week design sprint with a professional web development company.

During the design sprint, we conducted heuristic testing of the near-final prototype. Five usability experts rated the system along Nielsen’s 10 Heuristics: Visibility of system status, match between system and the real world, user control and freedom, consistency and standards, error prevention, recognition rather than recall, flexibility and efficiency, aesthetic and minimalist design, recognition and recovery from errors, and help and documentation. The experts ranked each heuristic from 0 to 4, with 0 being no usability problems and 4 being catastrophic usability problems; they also clearly documented any identified problems (even minor) and suggested resolutions. We summarized the feedback from the heuristic evaluation and relayed usability concerns and suggestions to the web developers, who incorporated them into the final design. The decision aid was designed to be viewed on a computer, tablet, or mobile phone.

Evaluation and Measurement

The evaluation process was guided by “RE-AIM,”34 a widely used implementation science framework. We used validated patient-reported outcomes (PROs) surveys, electronic health records (EHRs), and qualitative interview data to conduct the evaluation (Table 4). Reach was measured by examining the sociodemographic characteristics of enrolled participants compared to all potentially eligible patients across CUIMC and NYP-Q. Effectiveness was primarily measured by comparing decisional conflict at baseline and two weeks; secondarily, we stratified the sample by baseline decisional conflict score and examined changes in decisional conflict among those with low versus high baseline decisional conflict. We also measured changes in decision self-efficacy, AF symptom burden, and AF-related quality of life. Adoption was measured using a decision aid adoption survey, and implementation was measured using a validated decision aid acceptability questionnaire. Additionally, post-intervention qualitative interviews with participants and clinicians who guide patients through the treatment decision explored concepts related to adoption, implementation, and maintenance.

Power calculation

We aimed to recruit 75 participants. We determined that a sample of 37 patients would provide 80% power with two-sided α = 0.05 to detect a mean difference of 35 points in the primary outcome, decisional conflict. This effect size was based on a pilot study of a decision aid for anticoagulation in atrial fibrillation, which demonstrated a 35-point reduction in Decisional Conflict Scale scores after use of the aid41. However, we increased the target sample to 75 patients to adjust for attrition. In prior studies collecting longitudinal PROs with older adult cardiac populations, attrition after 3 months has been close to 50%42,43.

Screening and recruitment

Two parallel recruitment processes were employed: manual chart review and patient portal-based recruitment.

The manual chart review process involved the research coordinator (RC) screening for scheduled outpatient appointments in cardiac EP practices at CUIMC and NYP-Q. The RC identified patients with a confirmed diagnosis of AF and/or a planned AF ablation discussion, reviewed their medical records to confirm eligibility, and obtained permission to contact the patient from the treating physician. The RC contacted each patient by phone to describe the study and invite participation.

The portal-based recruitment strategy was only available for CUIMC patients during the trial period. We partnered with information technology services to specify eligibility criteria within the EHR that would identify potentially eligible patients, and a list of these patients was automatically generated for the RC to review. This process enabled direct outreach via the patient portal without requiring prior physician approval. Patients received a brief summary of the study via an alert in their patient portal and could either opt to learn more or decline further contact. For those who expressed interest, the RC reviewed their charts to confirm eligibility and then followed up by phone to describe the study and invite participation. Participants who agreed to participate were given the option to immediately enroll or schedule a separate phone call to undergo the enrollment process, depending on their availability.

All survey data were collected using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap). At the time of enrollment, the RC emailed a REDCap link to the participant so they could view and sign the informed consent document while the RC verbally explained the consent form via phone. The participant then proceeded to complete the baseline series of surveys (Table 4). Participants received $50 upon enrollment and $50 upon completing the three-month follow-up surveys.

Intervention delivery

After completing the initial baseline survey, participants automatically received the hyperlink to the decision aid. They were asked to navigate the decision aid independently and visit the decision aid as many times as they desired.

Follow-up data collection

Two weeks after the baseline intervention, participants received an automated email with a link to the first set of follow-up surveys (Table 4). Participants were asked if they had viewed the decision aid and were allowed to proceed only if they answered yes. If they answered no, they were asked to view the decision aid, provided with the link again, and received a follow-up invitation to complete the surveys two days later.

Three months after the intervention, participants received an automated email with a link to the second set of follow-up surveys (Table 4). They also indicated whether they would like to be contacted for a semi-structured interview.

At two weeks and three months, reminder emails were sent to non-responders two and four days after initial contact. In addition, the RC followed up with phone calls to encourage survey completion and help troubleshoot technical difficulties.

Semi-structured interviews

The RC contacted a subset of participants and invited them to participate in a brief phone-based interview. Additionally, the principal investigator contacted cardiac EP physicians whose patients were enrolled in the trial and invited them to participate in a Zoom- or phone-based interview. The interviews explored general experiences with the decision aid and perspectives on predisposing, enabling, and reinforcing factors to the adoption, implementation, and maintenance of the decision aid in clinical practice. All interviews were guided by semi-structured interview guides.

All interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim by a secure transcription company using human transcribers. Transcripts were spot-checked against the original audio to confirm accuracy. Interviews were conducted until thematic saturation was reached.

Data analysis

Quantitative analyses: Characteristics of the cohort were described using frequencies and percentages or means and standard deviations for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. The primary outcome was the change in decisional conflict from baseline to two weeks post-intervention, assessed using the Decisional Conflict Scale (DCS)38. This primary outcome and timeframe were selected based on the protocol for a similar decision aid trial44. After confirming assumptions of normality, we conducted pairwise comparisons using paired t-tests to evaluate the primary outcome, decisional conflict, as well as decision-self-efficacy. Other PROs (decision aid adoption, acceptability, and decision regret) were evaluated using descriptive statistics as they were collected at a single time point. The randomness of missing data was evaluated to determine the risk of bias by comparing the characteristics of participants with complete and incomplete data. All quantitative data were analyzed using R statistical software.

Qualitative coding: Qualitative data were analyzed using directed content analysis in which the RE-AIM framework formed the initial coding scheme45. The RC and principal investigator served as coders. They reviewed and analyzed four patient transcripts (two concurrently, two independently with post-coding agreement checks), followed by consensus discussions, as well as one physician transcript, to finalize the coding scheme. The RC coded the remaining transcripts, which were subsequently reviewed by the PI. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved collaboratively. To establish trustworthiness and credibility, we member checked during the interviews, shared an audit trail, cross-checked emerging concepts with the participants’ original statements, and recorded detailed notes outlining the analysis46.
