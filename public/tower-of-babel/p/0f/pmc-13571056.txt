Frequency and characteristics of multi‐domain autonomic dysfunction in patients with spontaneous intracranial hypotension
Authors: Woo‐Seok Ha, Da Eun Jung, Sojung Yoon, Joonho Kim, Min Kyung Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe aimed to evaluate multi‐domain autonomic function in patients with spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH) and investigate its association with clinical and radiological features.BackgroundSIH often presents with orthostatic symptoms that overlap with autonomic disorders; however, the prevalence and distribution of autonomic dysfunction in SIH remain poorly characterized.MethodsIn this cross‐sectional study conducted at a tertiary care center in Seoul, Republic of Korea, 34 patients with imaging‐confirmed SIH underwent a comprehensive autonomic function testing between October 2024 and February 2025. The autonomic function testing battery included heart rate response to deep breathing, Valsalva maneuver, head‐up tilt test, and quantitative sudomotor axon reflex test. Hemodynamic responses during head‐up tilt were monitored using both intermittent brachial and continuous beat‐to‐beat blood pressure measurements. Autonomic abnormalities were determined using age‐ and sex‐matched Korean normative data.ResultsAll enrolled patients presented with extradural fluid collection on spinal magnetic resonance imaging, indicating SIH due to dural tears. Autonomic dysfunction was identified in 18 of 34 (52.9%) of patients in the orthostatic domain, nine of 34 (26.5%) in the cardiovagal domain, and 10 of 34 (29.4%) in the sudomotor domain. Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome was the most frequent orthostatic abnormality, observed in 12 of 34 (35.3%) patients, followed by classical orthostatic hypotension in four of 34 (11.8%), syncope in one of 34 (2.9%), and delayed orthostatic hypotension in one of 34 (2.9%). No significant associations were identified between domains of autonomic dysfunction and the clinical or imaging features of SIH in false discovery rate‐corrected analyses.ConclusionIn patients with SIH due to dural tears, orthostatic hemodynamic abnormalities were common, with postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome being the most frequent phenotype. Keywords: autonomic nervous system, cerebrospinal fluid leak, intracranial hypotension, orthostatic intolerance, postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, standing position

INTRODUCTION

Spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH) is a condition caused by leakage of spinal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and typically manifests as a severe orthostatic headache that worsens in the upright position and improves when supine. 1 Furthermore, orthostatic intolerance symptoms, including dizziness, nausea, and muffled hearing, are frequently reported in SIH. 2

Interestingly, orthostatic intolerance is also a hallmark feature of postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), a disorder of autonomic regulation characterized by excessive heart rate increase upon standing. 3 Given that POTS can present with orthostatic headache, its co‐occurrence in a subset of patients with SIH complicates the clinical landscape. 4 This symptomatic overlap suggests that orthostatic symptoms in SIH may arise not only from CSF hypovolemia itself but also from concomitant autonomic dysregulation. 5

From an anatomical perspective, the brainstem harbors critical autonomic control centers, including the nucleus of the solitary tract, the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus, and the nucleus ambiguus, which are essential for cardiovagal and baroreflex regulation. 6 Previous studies evaluating autonomic function in SIH have been limited by small sample sizes. 4 , 7 Given the clinical overlap between orthostatic symptoms in SIH and autonomic disorders such as POTS, the prevalence, clinical relevance, and specific domains of autonomic dysfunction in SIH remain poorly characterized.

In this study, we performed a comprehensive autonomic function test (AFT) battery in patients with imaging‐confirmed SIH. We hypothesized that autonomic dysfunction would be prevalent in patients with SIH and might be associated with clinical and radiological features of SIH. Our objectives were to determine the prevalence and distribution of multi‐domain autonomic dysfunction and to evaluate its associations with the clinical and radiological features of the disease.

METHODS

Participants

This was a cross‐sectional study conducted at a tertiary care center in Seoul, Republic of Korea. Adult patients 19–64 years old diagnosed with SIH between October 2024 and February 2025 were screened for eligibility. SIH was diagnosed according to the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD‐3) criteria, 8 supported by neuroimaging findings consistent with CSF leak. Patients with spinal CSF leaks attributable to post‐dural puncture headache, iatrogenic causes, or trauma were excluded. Patients with known preexisting autonomic disorders were excluded, although no screened patients met this exclusion criterion. Only patients who underwent comprehensive AFT were included in the analysis. No formal medication washout period was required before AFT; however, concomitant medications taken on the day of testing, including autonomic‐active medications, were reviewed and recorded.

This is the primary analysis of the AFT data, which were collected as part of a pre‐planned clinical protocol. During the initial retrospective phase (October 2024), AFT was not systematically performed in all patients with SIH because the autonomic evaluation protocol had not yet been fully implemented. During the prospective phase from November 2024 through February 2025, comprehensive AFT was prospectively and systematically performed in consecutive patients with SIH. Demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, sex, height, weight, onset of symptoms, and the number of epidural blood patches (EBPs) received before the initial visit.

No a priori statistical power calculation was conducted. The final sample size was based on the available data from consecutive eligible patients with SIH who underwent comprehensive AFT during the study period.

This study was approved by the institution's institutional review board (IRB 4‐2024‐1323 and 4‐2024‐1324), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The requirement for informed consent was waived for patients enrolled retrospectively, whereas written informed consent was obtained from prospectively enrolled patients.

Clinical assessment

At the initial visit, all patients underwent standardized clinical assessments, including evaluation of headache characteristics and psychological status. Headache intensity was measured using an 11‐point numeric rating scale, with higher scores indicating greater pain intensity. Headache‐related disability was assessed using the Headache Impact Test‐6 (HIT‐6). 9 , 10 Symptom duration was defined as the time from symptom onset to the initial visit.

Psychological symptoms were evaluated using validated Korean versions of the Generalized Anxiety Disorder‐7 (GAD‐7) and the Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9), which assess anxiety and depressive symptoms, respectively. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 All assessments were performed at baseline before further diagnostic procedures.

Autonomic function testing

All patients underwent an AFT using a commercially available system (WR Medical Electronics Co., Stillwater, MN, USA). The standardized AFT battery, consisting of heart rate response to deep breathing (HRDB), the Valsalva maneuver, head‐up tilt (HUT) test, and quantitative sudomotor axon reflex test (QSART), was performed in accordance with clinical guidelines. 15 , 16 HRDB, Valsalva maneuver, and QSART were conducted in the supine position. HRDB and Valsalva testing were performed using a cardiac monitoring system (Cardiac Lab, HRV Acquire; WR Medical Electronics). Continuous beat‐to‐beat blood pressure (BP) and heart rate (HR) were monitored using a Finometer PRO device (Finapres Medical Systems, Enschede, the Netherlands). All AFTs and brain and spinal imaging studies were performed during the initial inpatient evaluation before treatment at our institution, including EBP or surgical repair.

The HRDB test measured heart rate variability during controlled deep breathing cycles. Participants performed eight cycles of deep breathing, with each cycle consisting of 5 s of inhalation and 5 s of exhalation. After a 2‐min resting period, the protocol was repeated. The mean of the five largest differences between the maximal and minimal heart rates during these cycles was defined as the E–I difference. The E:I ratio was calculated as the ratio of the longest RR interval during expiration to the shortest RR interval during inspiration.

Valsalva testing was performed with simultaneous beat‐to‐beat BP and HR monitoring. Disposable mouthpieces (Vyaire Medical, Inc., Mettawa, IL, USA) were used, and patients were instructed to maintain an expiratory pressure of at least 40 mm Hg for 15 s. The Valsalva ratio was assessed as the ratio between the highest heart rate during late phase II and the lowest heart rate during phase IV of the maneuver.

The HUT test was performed at an angle of approximately 60° for a total duration of 10 min after at least 5 min of resting in a supine position. Intermittent brachial BP measurements were obtained using an automated sphygmomanometer (OMRON HEM‐907, Omron Healthcare Co., Kyoto, Japan) at baseline, at 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 min during tilt, and on completion. Additionally, continuous beat‐to‐beat hemodynamic data were recorded via Finometer. Subjective symptoms of orthostatic intolerance and the timing of their occurrence were documented.

Sudomotor function was assessed using the QSART with a Q‐Sweat system (WR Medical Electronics). Sweat output was measured at four standard sites (forearm, proximal leg, distal leg, and foot) as total volume (μL) following iontophoresis using a constant‐current stimulator (Iontophor PM/DX; WR Medical Electronics).

Outcome assessment

All data were acquired and analyzed using dedicated software (WR TestWorks Software, version 2.8.3.0; WR Medical Electronics). Autonomic function was categorized into four domains: cardiovagal function, adrenergic function (Valsalva‐based), orthostatic hemodynamic response, and sudomotor function.

Cardiovagal dysfunction was defined as an abnormal E:I ratio, average E–I difference, or Valsalva ratio. Adrenergic dysfunction was identified by an impaired BP response during late phase II (failure of BP recovery to baseline) or phase IV (absence of BP overshoot) of the Valsalva maneuver. Orthostatic hemodynamic abnormality was defined as the presence of any of the following during the HUT test: classical orthostatic hypotension (OH), delayed OH, POTS, or syncope, based on the 2018 European Society of Cardiology guidelines. 17 To ensure diagnostic specificity, we primarily used intermittent brachial BP and HR measurements for assessment; in cases where results were within normal limits, beat‐to‐beat data from the Finometer were further reviewed for a secondary confirmatory analysis, because diagnostic criteria for OH and POTS are conventionally based on intermittent brachial measurements. Sudomotor dysfunction was defined as a sweat volume below the normal range at any of the four standard sites (forearm, proximal leg, distal leg, and foot). Interpretation of AFT findings, including beat‐to‐beat Finometer recordings, was performed by a neurologist blinded to the brain imaging findings.

Abnormalities in each domain were determined using age‐ and sex‐adjusted normative reference values derived from a healthy Korean population evaluated with the same AFT instrumentation used in this study. 18 Reference ranges for all parameters were stratified by age (10‐year intervals) and sex (Table S1). Abnormality was defined as a value below the 5th percentile of the corresponding reference distribution. 19 The Composite Autonomic Severity Score (CASS) was calculated to quantify overall severity of autonomic dysfunction by integrating scores from three subdomains: cardiovagal (0–3 points), adrenergic (0–4 points), and sudomotor function (0–3 points), resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 10. 20 , 21 The total CASS was used to categorize the degree of autonomic impairment as follows: mild (1–3 points), moderate (4–6 points), and severe (7–10 points). 20

Neuroimaging and clinical management

All patients underwent standard neuroimaging evaluation, including brain magnetic resonance imaging with contrast and heavily T2‐weighted MR myelography at the initial presentation. 22 The Bern score was calculated based on brain magnetic resonance imaging findings to assess imaging features suggestive of intracranial hypotension, and the investigator was blinded to the AFT results. Heavily T2‐weighted MR myelography was evaluated for the presence of spinal longitudinal extradural fluid collection (SLEC) and whether the SLEC was organized. 23 Notably, all included patients exhibited SLEC on heavily T2‐weighted MR myelography, ensuring a homogeneous study population with confirmed CSF leaks.

Detailed treatment protocols have been reported previously. 24 Before dynamic myelography, patients received at least two sessions of semi‐targeted EBPs. The target level for EBP was selected according to the location of the bony spur. Patients who remained symptomatic after at least two sessions of EBPs were considered refractory and underwent further evaluation with dynamic myelography, including digital subtraction myelography and dynamic CT myelography. CSF opening pressure was measured during dynamic myelography in patients who underwent this procedure.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviation if normally distributed (verified by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test) or as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) if non‐normally distributed. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

The primary outcomes were the prevalence of autonomic dysfunction across four domains, including cardiovagal, adrenergic (Valsalva‐based), orthostatic (HUT‐based), and sudomotor dysfunction, defined as dichotomous variables based on the presence of abnormal findings. The secondary outcomes were the detailed dysfunction in each domain, and CASS.

To evaluate associations between autonomic dysfunction and clinical variables, univariable logistic regression analyses were performed. Because adrenergic dysfunction was observed in only three patients, regression analyses for this domain were not performed because of the instability and limited interpretability of the resulting estimates. Dependent variables were dichotomized to indicate the presence or absence of dysfunction in each of the three domains. Independent variables included age, body mass index, headache intensity (assessed by numeric rating scale), symptom duration (by month), Bern score, 25 headache‐related impact (HIT‐6), anxiety (GAD‐7), and depressive symptoms (PHQ‐9). Because of the limited sample size and the risk of model overfitting, multivariable regression models were not constructed. All regression analyses were therefore conducted as univariable models, and the results were interpreted as exploratory. The assumption of linearity in the log‐odds for continuous variables was verified using the Box‐Tidwell test, which confirmed no significant deviations for the included clinical and imaging parameters. To account for multiple comparisons, p values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Furthermore, associations between CASS and clinical variables were evaluated using Spearman rank correlation analyses.

Additional analyses were performed to evaluate associations between autonomic dysfunction and individual components of the Bern score, including engorgement of the venous sinus, pachymeningeal enhancement, subdural fluid collection, suprasellar cistern, prepontine cistern, and mammillopontine distance. 25 Binary variables were coded as 1 (presence) or 0 (absence), and continuous variables were analyzed as numerical values recorded to one decimal place (mm).

Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p value <0.05. All analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). There were no missing data for demographic variables, headache‐related scales, psychological scores, or AFT parameters among the included patients. CSF opening pressure measurements were available only in patients who underwent dynamic myelography.

RESULTS

Study population and demographics

A total of 64 patients were referred for the evaluation of suspected intracranial hypotension during the study period (Figure 1). Eleven patients were excluded because an AFT could not be performed due to retrospective timing or scheduling issues. An additional 19 patients were excluded after failing to meet the diagnostic criteria for SIH or showing resolved imaging features. Notably, although digital subtraction myelography or decubitus CT myelography were performed in several SLEC‐negative patients suspected of having CSF‐venous fistulas, no cases were identified during this period.

A total of 34 patients with SIH were included in the analysis (Table 1). The mean age was 38.2 ± 8.6 years (range, 23–55 years), and 23 patients (67.6%) were female. The median symptom duration was 3 (IQR, 1–9) months, and the mean body mass index was 23.5 ± 2.8 kg/m2. The median headache intensity was 6 (IQR, 3.5–7) on the NRS, and the mean HIT‐6 score was 62.9 ± 9.3. The median Bern score was 3 (IQR, 1–4). The median number of EBPs before AFT was 2 (IQR, 1–3), and only four patients (11.8%) were treatment‐naive.

All patients showed SLEC on heavily T2‐weighted MR myelography, indicating SIH due to dural tears (type 1 or 2 leaks), and organized SLEC was observed in 17 patients (50.0%). Prevalence of autonomic dysfunction

Autonomic dysfunction was observed across multiple domains in patients with SIH (Table 1). Cardiovagal dysfunction was present in nine patients (26.5%), adrenergic dysfunction in three patients (8.8%), orthostatic hemodynamic abnormality in 18 patients (52.9%), and sudomotor dysfunction in 10 patients (29.4%). The median CASS was 1 (IQR, 1–3), indicating overall mild autonomic impairment, with the maximum score being 7.

Detailed phenotypes of autonomic dysfunction are summarized in Table S2. POTS accounted for two‐thirds of orthostatic abnormalities (12 patients, 35.3% of all patients). Classical OH was observed in four patients (11.8%), and delayed OH and syncope in one patient (2.9%). Among sudomotor dysfunctions, the forearm was the most frequently affected site (eight patients, 23.5%).

Among the 18 patients classified as having orthostatic hemodynamic abnormalities, 13 were identified based on intermittent brachial BP and HR measurements alone, whereas five additional cases were identified only after secondary review of beat‐to‐beat Finometer recordings. Of these five patients, four fulfilled criteria for POTS and one fulfilled criteria for delayed OH.

Association of autonomic dysfunction and clinical variables

Associations between autonomic dysfunction and clinical variables were evaluated using univariable logistic regression analyses (Figure 2A). The clinical variables included age, body mass index, symptom duration, headache intensity, and HIT‐6, as well as psychological status assessed by GAD‐7 and PHQ‐9. In unadjusted analyses, orthostatic dysfunction was associated with age (odds ratio, 0.87; 95% confidence interval, 0.78–0.97; p = 0.011); however, this association was no longer significant after FDR correction. Overall, the associations between autonomic dysfunction and clinical characteristics were modest (Table S3). Furthermore, no significant associations were observed between the CASS and clinical variables (Table S4). Additionally, there was no statistically significant association between the use of any concomitant pharmacotherapy capable of affecting hemodynamic status and the presence of orthostatic hemodynamic abnormalities (p = 0.717, χ2 test).

Association of autonomic dysfunction brain imaging findings

Associations between autonomic dysfunction and brain imaging features, including the Bern score and its individual components, were evaluated using univariable logistic regression analyses (Figure 2B). No significant associations were identified between autonomic dysfunction and imaging features of intracranial hypotension in either unadjusted or FDR‐corrected analyses (Table S5).

DISCUSSION

The major findings of this study are as follows: (1) autonomic dysfunction is highly prevalent in patients with SIH, particularly within the orthostatic hemodynamic domain, affecting approximately 53% of the study population; (2) POTS was the most frequent orthostatic abnormality, observed in nearly one‐third of the patients, followed by various forms of OH; and (3) no consistent associations were identified between autonomic dysfunction and the clinical or imaging features of SIH. These findings partially support our hypothesis that autonomic dysfunction is common in patients with SIH, but do not support a clear association between autonomic dysfunction and the clinical or radiological severity of the disease.

Previous studies evaluating autonomic function in SIH have been limited. 4 , 7 In a prior cohort study, nine patients with SIH underwent AFT, in which cardiovagal and adrenergic functions were largely preserved, whereas all patients demonstrated a POTS–like pattern on HUT. 4 Similarly, a case series of seven teenagers with SIH reported that all participants exhibited orthostatic intolerance, such as POTS or OH, during HUT testing. 7 Our study expands on these observations by using a larger, more homogeneous cohort of SLEC‐positive patients and employing high‐resolution hemodynamic measurements. In our cohort, 53% of patients demonstrated orthostatic hemodynamic abnormalities, confirming a high prevalence. These findings suggest that orthostatic symptoms in SIH may not be solely attributable to CSF hypovolemia itself; rather, intrinsic autonomic dysfunction may significantly contribute to the symptom burden in a subset of patients. Additionally, cardiovagal and sudomotor dysfunctions were observed in approximately 26–29% of patients. Given that our criteria for abnormality were based on the 5th percentile of age‐ and sex‐matched Korean normative data, these results suggest that multi‐domain autonomic dysfunction is a prevalent feature in the SIH population.

Although autonomic dysfunction was frequently observed in our cohort, we did not identify significant associations between autonomic dysfunction domains and clinical or radiological markers of SIH severity. This lack of correlation suggests that autonomic dysfunction in SIH is a pervasive clinical feature that may occur independently of conventional markers of disease severity. Therefore, our data highlight the importance of comprehensive autonomic assessment in the clinical management of SIH, as symptomatic severity alone may not reflect the extent of underlying physiological dysregulation.

The mechanisms underlying autonomic dysfunction in SIH remain uncertain and are likely multifactorial. The high prevalence of POTS‐like patterns may reflect compensatory sympathetic activation secondary to CSF hypovolemia. 26 , 27 Reduced intracranial pressure may increase sympathetic outflow to preserve cerebral perfusion, thereby producing a hemodynamic profile similar to that of primary POTS. In addition, prolonged bed rest and reduced physical activity related to orthostatic headache may lead to cardiovascular deconditioning, which can further aggravate orthostatic intolerance and tachycardic responses on standing. 28 , 29 Taken together, these considerations suggest a multifactorial basis for autonomic dysfunction in SIH.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may have limited the statistical power to identify subtle associations. Because of the exploratory nature of the study involving multiple comparisons, no associations remained significant after stringent Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction. Second, potential confounding factors could not be fully controlled; some patients were receiving medications for hypertension or diabetes, and some took analgesics for headache, which could potentially influence AFT results; however, a χ2 test showed no statistically significant association between the use of these medications and orthostatic hemodynamic abnormalities. Third, most patients had undergone at least one EBP before AFT, whereas only four patients were treatment‐naïve at the time of assessment. Because EBP may partially correct CSF hypovolemia and improve orthostatic symptoms, the prevalence and severity of autonomic dysfunction observed in this study may have been underestimated. Fourth, because this study was conducted in a tertiary referral center, selection bias may have been present. In particular, patients with the most severe orthostatic intolerance, especially during the hyperacute symptomatic phase, may have been disproportionately unable to attend or tolerate comprehensive AFT, potentially leading to underestimation of autonomic dysfunction prevalence. Fifth, the single‐center, single‐country design may limit the generalizability of our findings beyond Korean patients with SIH. In addition, as all enrolled patients were SLEC‐positive, our cohort predominantly consisted of type 1 or 2 leaks, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other types, such as type 3 (CSF‐venous fistulas). 30 , 31 Finally, the cross‐sectional design of this study precludes any definitive causal inferences regarding the relationship between CSF leakage and autonomic dysfunction.

CONCLUSIONS

In this cross‐sectional study of patients with SIH due to dural tears, multi‐domain autonomic dysfunction was common, particularly orthostatic hemodynamic abnormalities. No clinical or imaging features were significantly associated with autonomic dysfunction after FDR correction. These findings support consideration of autonomic assessment in patients with SIH, while the underlying mechanisms require further investigation.
