The prenatal anxiety trajectory and its correlation with stress and psychological flexibility: a longitudinal study
Authors: Jingwen Sun, Yan Gao, Yiping Nan, Yue Jiang, Duolao Wang, Atif Rahman, Xiaomei Li, Lei Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSeveral investigations have delved into the heterogeneity of anxiety symptom trajectories throughout pregnancy. However, the causal relationships between prenatal anxiety, stress, and psychological flexibility remain unclear. This study endeavored to identify distinct trajectory groups and influencing factors for anxiety symptoms in pregnant women, and explore the relationship between stress, psychological flexibility, and anxiety during this period.MethodsPregnant women were recruited from March to May 2022 at a hospital in Xi’an City, Shaanxi Province, China. Structured questionnaire surveys were conducted through a WeChat applet at five time points (12, 20, 26, 32, and 37 weeks), collecting information on basic demographics, stress, psychological flexibility, and anxiety throughout their pregnancies. The latent class growth model was employed to identify potential trajectory groups of prenatal anxiety, logistic regression was utilized to analyze the factors associated with these groups, and a linear mixed model was implemented to investigate the relationship between stress, psychological flexibility, and anxiety.ResultsThree distinct anxiety trajectory groups were identified. Unplanned pregnancy (OR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.08–2.87, P = 0.024) and a history of spontaneous abortion (OR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.10–2.79, P = 0.018) were significantly associated with a heightened risk of developing an anxiety trajectory group. Higher stress at 12 (β = 0.229, 95% CI: 0.187–0.271), 20 (β = 0.249, 95% CI: 0.211–0.287), 26 (β = 0.255, 95% CI: 0.213–0.296), and 32 weeks(β = 0.278, 95% CI: 0.241–0.315) significantly predicted elevated anxiety in late pregnancy (all P < 0.001). While higher psychological flexibility at the same time points predicted lower anxiety (βs from − 0.074 to − 0.099; all P < 0.001).ConclusionsThe trajectories of prenatal anxiety symptoms are dynamic and heterogeneous, with stress and psychological flexibility emerging as significant predictive factors. This study offers critical insights into the characteristics of pregnant women in high-risk trajectory groups, highlighting the potential for early intervention to mitigate worsening symptoms.Trial registrationThe study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Health Science Center, Xi’an Jiaotong University in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (Registration number: 2022 − 1436, date:2022.7.4).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03477-3.

Introduction

Prenatal anxiety is a common and serious mood disorder during pregnancy, imposing a substantial burden on global health [1]. Pregnant women not only undergo corresponding hormonal and reproductive system changes, but also experience role transitions that contribute to elevated anxiety levels [2]. A considerable percentage of women report persistent anxiety symptoms throughout their pregnancy [3]. Studies have shown that the prevalence of clinically diagnosed prenatal anxiety is 15.2% [4] and 8.1% [5] in developed and developing countries, respectively. A systematic review summarized the prevalence of prenatal anxiety among women in mainland China was 17.4% [6]. Long-term and excessive anxiety not only affects the physical and mental health of pregnant women, but also has adverse effects on the development of fetuses [7–9].

As pregnancy progresses, expectant mothers may experience varying degrees of anxiety across different gestational weeks. A study from Hong Kong revealed that the prevalence of prenatal anxiety follows a U-shaped curve, with higher levels in the first and third trimesters and lower levels in the second trimester [10]. However, studies originating from the Netherlands and mainland China demonstrated that the prevalence of anxiety in pregnant women tends to decrease as gestational age advances [11, 12]. While due to the large time interval between surveys in these studies, which only selected three time points during pregnancy to investigate anxiety levels, constructing a smooth trajectory was challenging, and it was difficult to accurately reflect the anxiety throughout the entire pregnancy. Therefore, further research on the prenatal anxiety trajectories and its influencing factors remains necessary to provide a scientific basis for developing targeted intervention measures to reduce prenatal anxiety and promote maternal and infant health.

Stress is a prevalent negative emotion among pregnant women. A study showed that 78% of pregnant women experienced low to moderate levels of stress, while 6% reported severe stress [13]. It is worth noting that high levels of stress during pregnancy have been linked to various complications such as low birth weight infants, preterm delivery, and negative health and behavioral outcomes [14]. In addition, maternal stress levels may be positively correlated with anxiety symptoms [15].

Psychological flexibility is the core concept and primary improvement goal of Acceptance Commitment Therapy (ACT), which is increasingly recognized as a crucial determinant of mental health Psychological flexibility is all about staying connected to the present moment and taking meaningful actions, even when confronted with tough emotions or thoughts [16, 17]. ACT emphasizes the impact of context on behavior and the connection between personal perspectives and emotions [18], and aims to assist individuals in enhancing psychological flexibility and better managing unavoidable life challenges. Recent studies have demonstrated that higher levels of psychological flexibility are associated with lower anxiety and depression during the perinatal period [19]. Furthermore, interventions aimed at increasing psychological flexibility, such as ACT, have shown promise in alleviating perinatal anxiety symptoms in pregnant women [20]. Moreover, psychological flexibility has been shown to positively influence patients’ adaptation to diseases, reduce emotional stress [21], and improve healthy behavior, so it is closely related to negative emotions such as depression and anxiety [22, 23].

Limited research indicates heterogeneity in the trajectories of prenatal anxiety, which may be associated with women’s stress and psychological flexibility [24–27]. Recognizing these variations is essential for developing prevention and intervention strategies that focus on the unique features of prenatal anxiety. However, most existing research on this topic has been conducted in Western populations and is predominantly cross-sectional in nature, posing challenges to comprehending how anxiety symptoms evolve over time and how early psychological characteristics predict later anxiety profiles. In particular, there is a paucity of longitudinal studies in China that have identified trajectory groups of prenatal anxiety and examined the predictive roles of psychological flexibility and stress within this framework.

Therefore, the purposes of this study were to (a) clarify the prenatal anxiety level and trajectory changes, further identifying the trajectory groups and pinpointing the influencing factors specific to each group, and (b) explore the relationship of stress, psychological flexibility and anxiety.

Materials and methods

Participants

A convenience sampling method was used to recruit participants from the gynecological clinic of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Medical College, Shaanxi Province. Eligible participants included women in the first trimester over 20 years of age who were able to cooperate with follow-up. Women were excluded if they had communication barriers, including severe cognitive impairment, significant language difficulties, or hearing loss that would interfere with independent questionnaire completion. In addition, those with fetal abnormalities requiring induction of labor, or with a current clinical diagnosis of mental illness, such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or major depressive disorder, were excluded based on medical records or self-reported history confirmed by a healthcare professional.

The sample size was calculated using the formula for estimating the prevalence of a binary outcome in a cross-sectional study. Based on a meta-analysis, the prevalence of prenatal anxiety among pregnant women was estimated at 17.4% [6]. With a margin of error (δ) set at 5% and a 95% confidence level (U = 1.96), the required sample size was calculated as 220.

Considering an expected attrition rate of 20%, the target sample size was adjusted to 264 participants. Ultimately, a total of 299 eligible women participated and obtained their informed consent from March 2022 to May 2022.

Procedures

A prospective longitudinal design was employed, involving five online questionnaires administered through a WeChat applet. Participants completed their baseline assessment at the obstetrical clinic during the first trimester. Four follow-up surveys were conducted during the second trimester (20 to 20+ 6 weeks, 26 to 26+ 6 weeks) and the third trimester (32 to 32+ 6 weeks, 37 to 37+ 6 weeks). This study was approved by the Biological and Medical Ethics Committee of Xi ‘an Jiaotong University (2022 − 1436), and informed consent was obtained from all participants before the assessment began.

Measurements

Sociodemographic characteristics

Sociodemographic information was collected at baseline survey using a self-designed questionnaire consisting of three aspects: demographic characteristics (including age, education level, and etc.), social-related characteristics (including relationship satisfaction with family members, family history of depression, and exercise frequency) and maternal-related characteristics (including gestational age, mode of conception, and etc.).

Stress

The Chinese Perceived Stress Scale (CPSS) was used to assess the stress levels of participants. The original scale was developed by Cohen et al. [28], and the Chinese version was adapted and popularized by Yang et al. [29]. The CPSS consists of 14 items, with a scoring range from 0 (never) to 4 (always), resulting in a total score of 56 points. A higher score indicates a greater level of stress. The CPSS has been widely validated and utilized in Chinese populations, including pregnant women [30], with a Cronbach’s α of 0.823–0.840. Although the CPSS is a general measure of stress, it was selected in this study to capture a broad range of stressors experienced during pregnancy, such as family dynamics, financial difficulties, work responsibilities, and social expectations.

Psychological flexibility

The Chinese version of Comprehensive Assessment of Acceptance and Commitment Therapy processes (CompACT) was utilized to assess the psychological flexibility of participants. It was originally developed by Francis [31], comprises a total of 23 items. The Chinese version of CompACT was adapted by Wang et al. which consists of 15 items and adopts a 7-point scoring system ranging from 0 (completely inconsistent) to 6 (completely consistent) [32]. A higher overall score signifies a higher level of psychological flexibility of participant. It has demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties, including internal consistency and construct validity, which is suitable for measuring psychological flexibility in Chinese cultural contexts [33]. The Cronbach ‘s α of Chinese version of CompACT was 0.87.

Anxiety

The anxiety symptom was measured by the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7). The GAD-7 was compiled by Spitzer et al. [34] and consists of 7 items. Each item scored on a scale from 0 (never) to 3 (almost daily), with a maximum total score of 21 points. 0–4 points for no anxiety, 5–9 points for mild anxiety, 10–14 points for moderate anxiety, more than 15 points for severe anxiety. The Chinese version of the GAD-7 has been validated for use among pregnant women in China, with a good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.89) and unidimensional factor structure [35]. The GAD-7 was selected for its robust psychometric properties and its frequent application in perinatal mental health research. While not pregnancy-specific, this scale effectively captures generalized anxiety symptoms that may arise from both gestational and non-gestational sources.

Statistical analyses

The statistical analyses were conducted in five steps. Initially, descriptive statistics were employed to evaluate the frequencies and proportions of population characteristics. Secondly, a linear mixed model was used to analyze the overall anxiety trajectory among all participants. Thirdly, the trajectory groups for anxiety were examined using the latent class growth model (LCGM), a classification model designed to accommodate longitudinal data with heterogeneity, to investigate the number of subgroups exhibiting distinct trajectories within the population and to analyze the developmental trends of each subgroup [36]. We adapted the model to accommodate one to four classes and set the criteria for model selection. The first set of criteria included Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and Sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC). The lower the value of these indicators, the better the LCGM fits [37]. The second classification criteria encompassed Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood Ratio Test test (LMR-LRT) and bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT). The significant likelihood ratio test for class k with a p-value less than 0.05 suggested that the specified class k model was superior to the model with class k − 1 [38]. The third parameter for evaluating classification accuracy was entropy, with a threshold of 0.8 being considered that the grouping effect is satisfactory [39]. At last, we also considered the population index, which typically entails having a minimum of 50 individuals or at least 5% of the total population in each subgroup [40]. Fourthly, the Chi-square test and logistic regression were used to analyze the influence of sociodemographic characterisrtics on the anxiety trajectory group. Finally, linear mixed models were employed to elucidate the causal relationship between stress, psychological flexibility, and anxiety.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 21.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) except for the latent class growth model (LCGM), which was run with Mplus 8.3 software (Linda Muthén & Bengt Muthén, Sweden).

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

Table 1 shows the sociodemographic characteristics of participants. The average age of the 299 participants was 29.51 ± 4.19 years old, and the average gestational age was 12.03 ± 1.27 weeks at enrollment. Most of the participants had high school or below (36.1%) or junior college (35.8%) education, the per capita monthly household income was 3001 ∼ 6000 yuan, exercised more than five times a week (35.1%). More than half of the participants paid for medical care out of their own pocket (62.2%), lived in cities (69.2%), had good relationships with parents (65.5%), husband (61.2%), and mother-in-law (61.2%), planned pregnancy (64.5%), had no expectations for the baby gender (69.6%), were primiparas (51.2%), and had no history of miscarriage (50.8%). Almost all participants had no direct relatives with a history of depression (94.3%), conceived naturally (94.3%), and had no pregnancy complications in the first trimester (89.3%).

Several obstetric risk factors were recorded among participants: 5.7% were aged over 35 years, 49.2% had a history of spontaneous abortion, 10.7% reported pregnancy complications at enrollment, and 1.3% conceived through assisted reproduction. These variables were included in subsequent analyses to assess their association with anxiety trajectory group membership.

A total of 69 participants were lost during the follow-up period. The reasons for the loss were the inability to contact participants, the refusal to continue the survey, the occurrence of abortion and the transfer of participants to hospitals. Identifying trajectory groups of prenatal anxiety

According to the established criteria, three anxiety trajectory groups in linear estimation (AIC = 6369.014; BIC = 6417.120; aBIC = 6375.890; entropy = 0.842) were identified as the most suitable model (see Supplementary Table S2).

The three anxiety trajectory groups had 16, 145 and 138 participants, accounting for 5.4%, 48.5%, and 46.1% of the total participants. The average intercepts of the three groups were 11.561, 5.606, and 2.057, with slopes of 0.140, 0.018, and − 0.062, respectively (see Supplementary Table S3). The results showed that the variation in the amplitude of prenatal anxiety across all three groups was small, and fell within the spectrum of moderate anxiety (10 ∼ 14 points), mild anxiety (5 ∼ 9 points) and no anxiety (0 ∼ 4), so they were named as the “Moderate stable” group, “Mild stable” group and “No anxiety stable” group, respectively (Fig. 3).

Predictors of trajectory subgroup membership

According to the results of the optimal LCGM model, there were only 16 participants in group 1(“Moderate stable” group), which precluded further univariate and multivariate analyses. Consequently, the group 1(“Moderate stable” group) and the group 2(“Mild stable” group) were merged to form a new group named the “Mild to moderate stable” group, encompassing a total of 161 participants. Univariate analyses were performed based on the sociodemographic data (Table S4), and variables with P < 0.05 were further incorporated into the stepwise logistic regression model. As shows in Table 3, unplanned pregnancies (OR = 1.757, 95%CI: 1.076 to 2.869) and history of spontaneous abortion (OR = 1.754, 95%CI: 1.103 to 2.790) were significantly associated with the " Mild to moderate stable " group.

Discussion

Currently, the majority of studies on prenatal anxiety are cross-sectional designs that tend to provide a general overview while overlooking the individual variations in development. Therefore, it is crucial to use a longitudinal study design and a trajectory analysis model to categorize the anxiety trajectories in pregnant women. This longitudinal study categorized distinct trajectory groups of anxiety symptoms during pregnancy among women in Northwest China and investigated the factors that potentially influenced these trajectories. Additionally, we also clarified the causal relationship of stress, psychological flexibility and anxiety of pregnant women.

Our findings revealed no significant fluctuations in anxiety scores over time, indicating that the anxiety levels of women remained relatively consistent throughout their pregnancies. Rothenberger et al. [41] also demonstrated that the anxiety levels of pregnant women did not undergo significant changes as time progressed. While studies conducted in Western countries [42, 43] found that pregnant women in the second trimester exhibited a significantly lower level of anxiety compared to those in the first and third trimesters. Additionally, findings from Buss et al. [25] in America indicated a decline in the anxiety levels of pregnant women as gestational age progressed. These differences may be influenced by variations in healthcare systems, social support structures, and cultural attitudes toward pregnancy and mental health.

We identified three distinct anxiety trajectory groups, which aligns with the findings of Ahmed et al. [44]. However, Kuo et al. [45] assessed anxiety symptoms in Taiwanese women during late pregnancy and at 1 day, 1 week, and 6 months post-delivery, which ultimately identified four distinct anxiety trajectory groups. Additionally, Bayrampour et al. [46] assessed the anxiety symptoms in Canadian women during the second trimester, third trimester, and at 4 months and 12 months postpartum, identifying five distinct anxiety trajectory groups. These findings suggest that maternal anxiety is heterogeneous and may be related to cultural factors. Therefore, it is crucial to identify the subgroups of prenatal anxiety trajectories and implement early intervention measures for high-risk pregnant women to reduce their prenatal anxiety levels.

These differences in anxiety trends and trajectory groups may reflect varying cultural expectations, healthcare access, and mental health literacy across populations. For instance, in collectivist societies such as China, emotional expression during pregnancy may be more restrained due to perceived stigma, while Western settings like Canada and America tend to normalize psychological vulnerability and encourage emotional disclosure. Understanding these cultural influences can help contextualize our findings and inform culturally appropriate interventions. Another potential explanation lie in the measurement tools employed. While we used validated general instruments to assess anxiety, some other studies may have utilized pregnancy-specific tools or alternative versions of the same scales. Such variations in measurement instruments can influence the sensitivity and contextual relevance of psychological assessments, potentially contributing to differences in observed results across studies.

Influencing factors for trajectory subgroups analyses showed that unplanned pregnancies and a history of abortion were associated with the subgroup of higher levels of anxiety. The results suggest that women exhibiting these traits are at higher risk of developing and maintaining mild to moderate anxiety symptoms throughout their pregnancy.

Unplanned pregnancy has been identified as a significant contributor to antenatal anxiety, women who became unexpectedly pregnant were more likely to experience persistent and escalating anxiety symptoms throughout their pregnancy. Previous studies also revealed that unplanned pregnancy significantly increases the risk of prenatal anxiety [47, 48]. Pregnant women with unplanned pregnancies often lack the necessary knowledge about pregnancy, childbirth, and parenting. Their psychological and physical preparation is inadequate. Therefore, medical professionals should enhance their health education efforts for pregnant women experiencing unplanned pregnancies.

Moreover, women with a history of spontaneous abortion were also at higher risk of experiencing persistent anxiety symptoms throughout their pregnancies compared to those without such experiences. This is consistent with the findings of a large prospective cohort study [49], which demonstrated that a history of miscarriage was significantly associated with elevated risks of common mental disorders, including anxiety, among pregnant women. The presence of a previous adverse pregnancy outcome causes the pregnant women to worry or fear about their current pregnancies, leading to elevated levels of anxiety. In light of this, healthcare professionals should provide more psychological support to pregnant women with a history of adverse pregnancies.

This study revealed that a high level of stress can significantly predict elevated anxiety levels in later stages. Rallis et al. [26] also demonstrated that a higher stress score during the second trimester is associated with an increase in anxiety during the third trimester. Besides, Ahmed et al. [44] found that pregnant women with high stress levels were more likely to be in the moderate-stable group, and stress was the only significant predictor of high anxiety levels. The above findings suggest a strong correlation between the stress and anxiety of women during pregnancy. Furthermore, high stress levels in the first trimester are likely to predict anxiety in the second or third trimester.

In addition, we also found that a high level of psychological flexibility in early pregnancy can significantly predict lower levels of anxiety in subsequent periods. Several intervention studies revealed that perinatal women who received the ACT intervention experienced a significant increase in psychological flexibility and a significant reduction in anxiety symptoms in all participants [27, 50]. These findings indicate that healthcare professionals can leverage psychotherapeutic interventions like ACT to address pregnant women’s concerns, thereby enhancing their psychological flexibility and mitigating anxiety levels. Specific ACT components such as acceptance, values clarification and committed action may play a crucial role in alleviating prenatal anxiety by helping pregnant women cope with uncertainty, regulate emotions, and reframe pregnancy-related stress. For practical implementation, ACT techniques can be integrated into digital platforms, like WeChat mini-programs, to offer scalable and accessible support. Tailoring ACT content with pregnancy-specific metaphors and exercises could further enhance relevance and effectiveness in addressing prenatal anxiety.

Limitations of this study need to be recognized. First, the participants were drawn from one hospital, and data was collected through an online self-reported questionnaire, which could potentially introduce selection and recall bias. Additionally, we excluded pregnant women with a current diagnosis of mental illness and the number of lost follow-up was relatively high. As a result, the findings of this study may not be generalizable to all pregnant women in mainland China. Therefore, interpretation and extrapolation of study results should consider these limitations. What’s more, this study employed general rather than pregnancy-specific instruments to assess anxiety and stress. Although these instruments have demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties in Chinese pregnant populations [30, 35], we acknowledge that the absence of pregnancy-specific scales may have affected the sensitivity to context-specific stressors and emotional states.

Based on the heterogeneity in prenatal anxiety, this study conducted longitudinal dynamic monitoring and evaluation from the perspective of subgroup, and identified the trajectory groups of prenatal anxiety and their influencing factors. Additionally, this study explored the causal relationship between stress, psychological flexibility, and prenatal anxiety.

The findings of this study have important clinical implications for prenatal care providers. First, identifying distinct anxiety trajectories allows for early risk stratification and monitoring of pregnant women who may be vulnerable to persistent or worsening anxiety. Clinicians should pay particular attention to women with unplanned pregnancies and a history of spontaneous abortion, as these groups were more likely to experience higher and sustained anxiety throughout pregnancy. Second, the strong predictive role of stress and psychological flexibility suggests that early screening and psychological interventions, such as ACT, may be effective in reducing anxiety. Surkan et al. [51] found that treating prenatal anxiety can prevent later depression. Therefore, future studies should investigate the impact of ACT on prenatal anxiety and determine whether this effect is associated with subsequent depressive symptoms in the study participants.

Conclusion

Based on the heterogeneity in prenatal anxiety, this study conducted longitudinal dynamic monitoring and evaluation from the perspective of subgroup, and identified three distinct trajectory groups of maternal anxiety symptoms, with unplanned pregnancy and abortion experience being the key influencing factors. Moreover, stress and psychological flexibility were strong predictors of anxiety. Therefore, regular psychological assessments of high-risk women are crucial, and early detection of mental health issues is essential to aid healthcare providers in identifying women who may experience adverse pregnancy outcomes. Furthermore, future studies should prioritize the application of psychological interventions such as ACT to alleviate negative emotions in pregnant women at high-risk for anxiety.
