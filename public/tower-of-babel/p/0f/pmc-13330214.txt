Mechanistic insights into the co-adsorption of crystal violet and allura red on a sustainable biomass-based nanocomposite
Authors: Neha Jarodhia, Kuldeep Joshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The simultaneous presence of various types of dyes in industrial effluents poses a significant challenge to conventional wastewater treatment due to cooperative and competitive adsorption phenomena. This study elucidates the single- and binary-component adsorption behaviour of crystal violet (CV) and allura red (AR) on a coconut-coir-derived CC@NiO–CuO nanocomposite, demonstrating its potential as a sustainable adsorbent. The nanocomposite was characterized by XRD, FTIR, and XPS to probe structural and interfacial transformations before and after dye adsorption. The adsorbent demonstrated an equilibrium adsorption capacity of 27.47 mg g−1 for AR and 406.50 mg g−1 for CV in a single-dye system. For AR and CV indicating the monolayer adsorption process, the Langmuir adsorption isotherm fits the data the best. Both dyes in this system adhere to a pseudo-second-order kinetic model, which implies that the availability of surface sites determines the adsorption rate. In binary systems, adsorption evolved into a cooperative–competitive network in which preferential AR anchoring under acidic conditions induced surface charge heterogeneity. The adsorption of AR in a binary mixture was well described by the Extended Freundlich isotherm, whereas for CV, the Extended Langmuir–Freundlich model fitted the best. Overall, the combined spectroscopic and adsorption analyses suggest that adsorption proceeds through a synergistic interplay of electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and possible coordination effects, operating collectively rather than independently. The findings demonstrate that multicomponent adsorption cannot be reliably predicted from single-solute systems and highlight the importance of inter-adsorbate interactions in governing adsorption behaviour. This work provides new mechanistic insight into dye-assisted adsorption and supports the development of biomass-based adsorbents for complex wastewater treatment.

Introduction

Since the Industrial Revolution, synthetic dyes have become widespread environmental contaminants, penetrating aquatic systems, disturbing ecological balance, and posing considerable risks to both the environment and human health.1 Their intricate aromatic structures, robust bonds, and low disintegrability make them resilient to natural degradation. Even at concentrations as small as 1 mg L−1, dyes can obstruct sunlight, impede photosynthesis, and show toxicity to aquatic organisms. Additionally, numerous dyes have genotoxic and carcinogenic capabilities, emphasizing the immediate need for efficacious wastewater remediation.2

Conventional dye-removal processes, including coagulation–flocculation, membrane filtration, and advanced oxidation, are often high-end, energy-intensive, and incompetent for complete elimination, particularly in complex effluents.3 Adsorption has appeared as a promising alternative due to its straightforwardness, cost-effectiveness, and high efficiency even at low pollutant concentrations.4 Recently, focus has shifted toward sustainable adsorbents derived from low-cost, renewable biomass, which can be developed into functional materials with upgraded surface properties.5

Agricultural residues (e.g., rice husks, peanut shells, banana peels), microbial biomass, and algal biomass have shown exceptional performance for removing dyes, antibiotics, heavy metals, and pesticides.6,7 Despite these advances, most studies aim on single-dye removal in basic, simulated wastewater systems.8 Such models neglect the chemical heterogeneity and competitive dynamics of real effluents, which often comprise multiple dyes of variable charges and structures, along with metals, organics, and suspended solids.9–11 This heterogeneity can lead to competitive or cooperative adsorption effects that single-solute studies cannot capture. Consequently, while biomass-derived adsorbents show high efficacy for individual dyes, their behaviour in realistic multicomponent wastewater continues to be inadequately understood. Addressing this gap is crucial for converting laboratory-scale findings into practical, real-world wastewater remediation strategies.

Our previous study displayed the efficient removal of crystal violet (CV) from aqueous solutions using a coconut-coir-derived NiO–CuO nanocomposite, highlighting its potential as a low-cost and sustainable adsorbent.12 While its achievement in single-dye systems was promising, its efficacy in multicomponent matrices remains unmapped. In the present work, we extend this investigation by evaluating the adsorption of allura red (AR) onto the same nanocomposite and assessing its behaviour in binary-dye systems comprising CV and AR. Despite the fact that multicomponent adsorption systems have been reported earlier, there is a limited spotlight on the mechanistic grasp of inter-adsorbate interactions. In particular, the role of one dye in transforming the adsorbent surface to influence the adsorption behaviour of another, remains meagrely explored.

In this frame of reference, the present investigation progresses the field by surpassing the conventional supposition of simple site competition to substantiate a cooperative–competitive adsorption framework in a binary dye system. It highlights intricate intermolecular interactions at the adsorbent interface by illuminating the AR and producing negative microdomains that improve CV adsorption. Spectroscopic analyses (XPS, FTIR, and XRD) are employed to explore structural and surface transformations before and after adsorption, providing molecular-level insight into the interaction mechanisms. These findings are further sustained by multicomponent adsorption isotherm models, which quantitatively capture the observed co-adsorption behaviour. Collectively, this integrated approach proposes a refined mechanistic understanding of adsorption in chemically complex systems, while meticulously evaluating the influence of molecular charge, steric hindrance, and functional group interactions under competitive conditions.

The binary dye system serves as a representative model of real wastewater matrices, providing insight into surface interactions, site selectivity, and the dynamic interplay between coexisting dye species.

Experimental

Materials

The study exclusively used chemicals and reagents of analytical-reagent quality, procured from LOBA Chemie, and used without any additional purification.

Synthesis of CC@NiO–CuO nanocomposite

The CC@NiO–CuO nanocomposite was synthesized following our previously reported work without modification.12 In brief, coconut coir powder was extracted from husks, while NiO–CuO nanoparticles were prepared via thermal decomposition of a mixed nickel–copper precursor, followed by calcination. The two components were subsequently integrated through an ex situ synthesis approach involving sonication and stirring, and the resulting material was dried to obtain the final composite. A detailed description of the synthesis of all materials is provided in Section 1 of the SI.

Characterization

The synthesized materials were characterized using multiple analytical methods to understand their structural, morphological, and surface properties thoroughly. To identify the functional groups, a Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR, PerkinElmer SP 65) spectrophotometer was used. Powder X-ray diffraction (P-XRD, Bruker AXS D8 Focus, Cu Kα, λ = 1.540589 Å) was performed over a 2θ range of 10°–80° at a scan rate of 0.5° s−1 to determine the crystalline structure and phase composition of the samples. The surface morphology and microstructural features were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Carl Zeiss EVO 18). Additionally, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed using a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (1486.6 eV) to investigate the surface chemical composition and electronic states. To investigate the adsorbate–adsorbent interactions and structural changes after adsorption, the nanocomposite was characterized using FTIR, XRD, and XPS after adsorption of CV, AR and their binary mixture.

Evaluation of the pHPZC

The point of zero charge (pHPZC) of the adsorbent CC@NiO–CuO was determined using the pH drift method.13 A set of 0.01 M NaCl solutions was prepared, and their initial pH was tuned to 2–12 using either 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH solutions. To the individual solution, 50 mg of the adsorbent was added, and the mixtures were stirred for 24 h at room temperature. After that, the suspensions were allowed to settle, and the final pH was measured directly in the suspension using a calibrated digital pH meter without filtration. The pHPZC was measured by plotting the graph of ΔpH (pHfinal − pHinitial) versus pHinitial. The pH at which ΔpH equals 0 was identified as the point of zero charge (pHPZC) of the adsorbent.

Batch adsorption

The adsorption of CV and AR in single systems was investigated using the One Factor at a Time (OFAT) approach. Detailed experimental procedures, including batch adsorption, isotherm, kinetic, and thermodynamic studies, are provided in the SI (Sections 1.3 and 1.5–1.7).

In binary system, the effect of pH (2–10), adsorbent dose (5–30 mg), contact time (10–70 min), and initial dye concentration was evaluated. Experiments used 25 mg of adsorbent in 20 mL dye solutions (15 mg L−1 each) for 50 min. Maximum removal for both dyes occurred at pH 2, which was used for subsequent studies. All adsorption experiments were performed at ambient laboratory condition at 34 ± 1 °C. The residual dye concentrations in both single and binary systems were quantified using the absorbance at their respective λmax values (Fig. S1–S3).

Further optimization of binary system was carried out by the statistical central composite design (CCD) under response surface methodology (RSM). The range and center point values of independent variables were determined from the single-factor study, and a total of 46 trials were performed (Section 1.4, Table S1 of the SI). Statistical software (Design-Expert® 13, StatEase Inc., Minneapolis, USA) was used for the experimental design and to generate a regression model for predicting the influence of these factors on CV and AR removal.

Co-adsorption behaviour in the binary dye system

Co-adsorption experiments were conducted to assess the mutual influence of CV and AR and to distinguish cooperative versus competitive effects. Binary systems were studied in an A/B format, where dye A's concentration was varied (10–60 mg L−1), and dye B's concentration was fixed (10, 30, or 50 mg L−1).14

CV/AR and AR/CV systems were investigated using 25 mg of adsorbent in 20 mL of solution at pH 2 and ∼34 °C for 50 min. Residual dye concentrations were measured by UV-vis spectroscopy. The adsorption capacity ratio (Rq) quantified binary versus single-system uptake.141Rq = qb,i/qm,iwhere qb,i and qm,i are the equilibrium adsorption capacities of component i in binary and single systems, respectively, at identical conditions.

The equilibrium data obtained from the binary adsorption system were analysed using established multicomponent isotherm models as represented by eqn [2]–[5], to describe competitive adsorption behaviour between the dye molecules.15 In a two-component system, the adsorption capacity of each component is influenced by the presence of the other due to competition for active sites. To elucidate the adsorption mechanism between the adsorbent surface and the adsorbate, the kinetics of binary dye adsorption were examined. Reusability of the adsorbent

The regeneration efficiency and practical applicability of the CC@NiO–CuO nanocomposite for binary dye removal were evaluated by conducting desorption and reusability experiments. After adsorption under optimised conditions (initial concentration: 15 mg L−1 each for CV and AR, pH = 2, adsorbent dose = 25 mg, contact time = 50 min), the dye-loaded adsorbent was separated by filtration and gently washed with deionised water to remove loosely bound dye molecules. Desorption was carried out using 0.1 M NaOH as the desorbing agent. The spent adsorbent was immersed in 20 mL of desorbing solution and agitated for 60 min at room temperature to facilitate dye removal from the surface. The material was then washed repeatedly with deionised water until a neutral pH was achieved, and dried at 100 °C for 1 h. The regenerated adsorbent was reused in subsequent adsorption cycles under identical conditions. This adsorption–desorption process was repeated for up to five cycles, and the removal efficiency (%) of CV and AR was determined by measuring absorbance using UV-vis spectrophotometry after each cycle.

Interference study

The effect of co-existing species on the adsorption performance of the CC@NiO–CuO nanocomposite was evaluated to assess its selectivity in complex aqueous environments. The adsorption behaviour of CV and AR in a binary system was investigated at different initial concentration combinations in both the absence and presence of interfering species. Three concentration sets were selected to evaluate competitive adsorption: (AR: 30 mg L−1, CV: 60 mg L−1), (AR: 60 mg L−1, CV: 30 mg L−1), and (AR: 30 mg L−1, CV: 30 mg L−1). All experiments were carried out under previously optimized conditions.

To simulate realistic wastewater conditions, a mixed interference system was prepared using common inorganic ions, including Na+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Cl−, SO42−, and Fe3+, as well as organic dyes, as potential interferents due to their common presence in real wastewater systems, each added at a concentration of 50 mg L−1 to create a representative complex matrix. Adsorption experiments were then performed under identical conditions for systems with and without interfering species, and the residual dye concentrations were determined using UV-vis spectroscopy.

Real wastewater assessment

The practical applicability of the CC@NiO–CuO nanocomposite was evaluated using real wastewater samples collected from industrial areas near Ahmedabad, Gujarat. The samples were filtered through Whatman filter paper to remove suspended particulates and then used without further treatment. As the wastewater did not contain the target dyes, the binary dye solution of CV and AR was spiked into the collected wastewater samples at initial concentrations of 15 mg L−1 each were labelled as S1–S5. A laboratory-simulated binary dye solution containing CV and AR at concentrations of 15 mg L−1 each prepared in distilled water was used as the control system and labelled as S6. Adsorption experiments for all samples (S1–S6) were carried out under the optimized conditions. After adsorption, the solutions were filtered and analyzed using UV-vis spectroscopy to determine residual dye concentrations, and removal efficiencies were calculated.

P-XRD analysis

The P-XRD patterns of the CC@NiO–CuO nanocomposite before and after the adsorption of CV, AR and their binary mixture are indicated in Fig. 1.

The CC@NiO–CuO nanocomposite (Fig. 1(a)) retains the characteristic peaks of both, confirming successful deposition of NiO–CuO on the biomass without altering their crystalline structure. Slight peak broadening and reduced intensity indicate nanoscale crystallites, good dispersion on the coir matrix, and attenuation from the organic component.

After adsorption of CV, AR, and the binary dye mixture (Fig. 1(b–d)), the oxide peak positions remain unchanged, showing no phase transformation. However, decreased intensity, mild broadening, and subtle 2θ shifts suggest surface coverage by dye molecules and weak dye–oxide interactions, leading to minor surface strain. The broad carbon hump (20–25°) also varies: slightly increased after CV, slightly reduced after AR, and significantly enhanced for the binary mixture, indicating differential interaction with the lignocellulosic matrix.

XPS analysis

The XPS was employed to elucidate the surface chemical composition and oxidation states of elements present in the nanocomposite before and after the adsorption of dyes CV, AR, and their binary mixture.

The survey spectrum of the CC@NiO–CuO nanocomposite, Fig. 3(a), revealed the existence of carbon (C), oxygen (O), nickel (Ni), and copper (Cu), indicating the successful synthesis of the nanocomposite. The high-resolution C 1s (Fig. 3(b)) spectrum of the nanocomposite displayed multiple peaks corresponding to various carbon bonding environments. Peak at binding energy 284.46 eV is attributed to sp2-hybridized carbon (CC), indicative of aromatic carbon structures,19 286.05 eV assigned to C–O/C–OH functionalities, suggesting the presence of hydroxyl and ether groups, and 287.83 eV corresponds to O–CO groups, such as carboxylic acids or esters.20 The O 1s spectrum (Fig. 3(c)) exhibited two main peaks: 532.83 eV attributable to oxygen in C–O, and 531.41 eV assigned to CO.21 Distinct peaks were observed in the Ni 2p spectra (Fig. 3(d)), 856.51 eV assigned to Ni 2p3/2 and 874.40 eV representing Ni 2p1/2 of Ni2+, indicating the presence of NiO, while the 861.52 eV and 865.25 eV and at 878.70 eV and 882.40 eV represent their corresponding shake-up satellite peaks typical of Ni2+ species, further confirming the oxidation state.22 As shown in Fig. 3(e), the Cu 2p spectrum exhibits four main peaks. The peaks at 932.90 eV (Cu 2p3/2) and 952.50 eV (Cu 2p1/2) are attributed to Cu0/Cu+ species (Cu or Cu2O), while the peaks at 934.80 eV (Cu 2p3/2) and 954.30 eV (Cu 2p1/2) correspond to Cu2+ species present in CuO. In addition, the peaks at 940.20 eV and 943.80 eV represent the characteristic shake-up satellite peaks of Cu2+, which are absent in Cu0 and Cu+, further confirming the coexistence of multiple copper oxidation states in the sample.23–25

The XPS spectrum of the ex situ CC@NiO–CuO nanocomposite after CV adsorption (Fig. 4) provides compelling evidence of dye–surface interaction. The wide-scan spectrum (Fig. 4(a)) indicates the continued detection of C, O, N, Cu, and Ni. The increased nitrogen content supports the successful adsorption of CV, which contains quaternary ammonium groups. In the high-resolution C 1s spectrum (Fig. 4(b)), a noticeable shift in the binding energy and an increase in the intensity of carbon functionalities indicate their active participation in the interaction with the dye molecules.26 Additionally, the O 1s spectrum (Fig. 4(c)) is consistent with the nanocomposite before adsorption, indicating no significant changes. The N 1s signal at 399.85 eV (Fig. 4(f)) corresponds to amine or quaternary amine nitrogen groups (–NH2/–N+(CH3)2) derived from the dye. Binary dye adsorption

Batch adsorption

The influence of pH on the simultaneous adsorption of CV and AR from a binary mixture is shown in Fig. 7(a). The results indicate that both CV (99.03%) and AR (75.27%) achieve their maximum removal at pH 2. Since the solution pH is below the pHPZC, the nanocomposite surface bears a positive charge, which strongly favours the adsorption of AR through electrostatic attraction between the sulfonate groups of AR and protonated surface sites. Although CV is cationic and electrostatically repelled, it still achieves nearly complete removal (∼99%), indicating a cooperative adsorption mechanism rather than simple competition for sites.28 As pH increases from 2 to 10, protonation of the adsorbent surface decreases, reducing the number of electropositive sites available for AR adsorption. A distinct pH-dependent surface charge transition from +18 mV (pH 2) to +12 mV (pH 4) and a reversal trend from −16 mV (pH 6) to −27 mV (pH 10) is shown in the nanocomposite's zeta potential profile. This behaviour controls the electrostatic interactions with dye molecules and represents the protonation/deprotonation balance of surface functional groups. Adsorption tendencies for specific dyes are therefore in line with electrostatic predictions.

Due to growing electrostatic affinity with the negatively charged surface, the cationic dye CV shows an increase in removal effectiveness from 77.21% at pH 2 to 92.69% at pH 6, staying consistently high at basic environments. In contrast, the anionic dye AR shows maximum elimination at acidic pH (∼76% at pH 2 and ∼67% at pH 4) and a fast fall to ∼30–32% at pH ≥ 6, indicating the change from favourable electrostatic attraction to repulsion. While the continuous high CV removal throughout the pH range indicates other contributions from non-electrostatic interactions such π–π interactions and pore diffusion, these observations corroborate that electrostatic interactions are the primary driving force for adsorption.

The presence of cooperative effects is shown by the adsorption behaviour in the binary system deviating from ideal competitive trends (Fig. 7(d)). Compared to the pure nanocomposite, the mixture's zeta potential values (+13 mV at pH 2 and +6 mV at pH 4) are significantly lower, indicating partial surface charge neutralization upon dye adsorption. As would be expected given the positively charged surface, the anionic dye AR is preferentially adsorbed in acidic environments (75.26% at pH 2 and 65.66% at pH 4). It is assumed that this first adsorption stage introduces negatively charged functional groups at the solid–liquid interface, causing localized charge heterogeneity as opposed to total surface charge reversal. The remarkably high CV removal seen in the mixture (∼99% at pH 2 and ∼96% at pH 4) in comparison to the single-component system (77.21% at pH 2) is consistent with the plausible facilitation of subsequent adsorption of cationic CV molecules via secondary electrostatic attraction by such localized negatively charged domains.

The adsorption process is more controlled by direct electrostatic interactions when the pH rises, and the surface becomes inherently negative. This results in preferential CV removal and inhibited AR uptake, which are signs of competitive adsorption for active sites. Overall, the results highlight the importance of cooperative mechanisms by showing that, in multicomponent adsorption systems, adsorbate-induced surface modification and localized charge redistribution greatly enhance CV uptake in the binary system at low pH, even though electrostatic interactions primarily determine adsorption behaviour. The influence of adsorbent dose on the simultaneous removal of CV and AR from a binary mixture was investigated by varying the adsorbent dose from 5 to 30 mg and is presented in Fig. 7(b). The results revealed a significant increase in removal efficiency for both dyes with increasing adsorbent dose, which can be assigned to the increased accessibility of active centres for adsorption. At a low dose of 5 mg, both dyes exhibited low removal efficiencies, indicating limited adsorption sites, as compared to single dye system. As the dose increased beyond 5 mg, a steep rise in removal efficiency was observed, with CV consistently exhibiting higher removal than AR. At the maximum tested dose of 30 mg, CV achieved a removal efficiency of ∼97%, while AR reached about 61%.

The time-dependent adsorption behaviour of CV and AR in a binary dye system was examined over a range of contact times (10–70 min), and the corresponding removal efficiencies are presented in Fig. 7(c). The findings indicate that the percentage removal of both dyes increases with contact time, although the rate and extent of removal vary significantly between CV and AR. Initially, both dyes exhibited low removal efficiency at 10 min, suggesting similar adsorption kinetics at the early stage. However, as time progressed, CV showed a much steeper increase in removal efficiency compared to AR. Between 10 and 40 min, CV removal efficiency rose sharply, reaching approximately 89%, and eventually plateaued around 93% after 60 min. In contrast, AR exhibited a more gradual and fluctuating trend. While its removal efficiency increased to around 64% at 30 min, a noticeable drop occurred at 40 min, possibly due to desorption or competition effects. Beyond this point, only a marginal improvement was observed, with the removal efficiency stabilizing near 59% at 70 min.

Adsorption mechanism

Adsorption in a binary dye system

In the binary dye system, the adsorption behaviour of CV and AR is governed by a combination of electrostatic interactions, surface heterogeneity, and adsorbate–adsorbate interactions, rather than a purely competitive mechanism. At pH 2—below the point of zero charge—the adsorbent surface is positively charged, creating a strong electrostatic driving force for AR adsorption due to its sulfonate groups. This preferential anchoring of AR establishes localized negatively charged microdomains that act as secondary high-affinity sites for CV. Electrostatic bridging, π–π interaction, and dye-assisted charge restructuring allow removal of CV by >99% despite electrostatic repulsion.

Additionally, as pH increases, surface deprotonation decreases AR anchoring, which lessens the simultaneous removal of both dyes and attenuates these cooperative mechanisms.1,28The competitive–cooperative ecosystem is further revealed by adsorbent-dosage effects. At low doses, limited active sites intensify competition, particularly suppressing AR uptake compared to its single-dye behaviour. For both dyes, increasing the dose reduces site scarcity and improves removal efficiency; CV constantly predominates because of its greater affinity for high-energy sites. In this case, AR plays a cooperative role by adjusting local surface charge to attract CV, whereas CV acts antagonistically against AR by occupying favoured spots. These dynamics are supported by time-resolved adsorption profiles, which show a sequential cooperative–competitive transition: AR peaks earlier (∼64%) before dropping due to competitive displacement by CV, while CV uptake accelerates quickly after 30 min and plateaus at ∼93%. These impacts are quantitatively captured by the Rq analysis. AR-mediated collaboration is confirmed by CV's continuous synergistic enhancement (Rq > 1), particularly at intermediate doses (30–40 mg L−1). AR exhibits a concentration-dependent transition between synergy (Rq > 1) and competition (Rq < 1), with lower synergy at high CV levels due to increased site competition and the maximum cooperative enhancement (Rq = 1.6) at moderate CV concentrations.

The interaction mechanisms were substantiated by the spectroscopic analysis. The increased C 1 s intensity after binary adsorption indicates π–π interaction due to dye–dye pairing, suggesting cooperative interaction between the dyes and the interaction between aromatic rings of dye/s with lignin-derived aromatic domains of the coconut coir matrix. This behaviour correlates well with the pronounced increase in the intensity of the amorphous carbon hump in the XRD patterns. The shifts in the S 2p peaks reflect MO–S linkages (NiO–SO3, CuO–SO3).26 The corresponding shifts in the Ni 2p and electron redistribution in the Cu 2p components suggest electron donation from the sulfonate oxygen of AR. Additionally, the shifts in the N 1 s spectra indicate hydrogen bonding interaction between the carbonyl/carboxyl groups with the –NH2 groups in CV, and the azo nitrogen atoms in AR. The XRD patterns and the FTIR spectra further support this dual interaction, displaying merged and broadened features of both dyes. The shifts in the CO and CC stretching regions and maintained peaks arising from CV's C–N modes and AR's sulfonate-associated vibrations. The more pronounced broadening in the M–O stretching region (700–400 cm−1) as compared to single dyes indicates the involvement of both dyes in altering the metal–oxygen environment.

Collectively, these interfacial signatures confirm distinct yet interconnected adsorption behaviours. CV follows monolayer adsorption on uniform secondary sites enabled by AR anchoring, consistent with Langmuir-type chemisorption; AR undergoes multilayer, heterogeneous adsorption via electrostatic attraction, π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, metal–ligand coordination, and dye–dye pairing, aligning with the Freundlich model.14,31

The emergent mechanism is a dynamic cooperative–competitive adsorption network, where each dye simultaneously facilitates and restrains the other depending on pH, concentration, and site availability.

The observed decline in removal efficiency can be attributed to the presence of co-existing ions and natural organic matter in the wastewater matrix, which compete with dye molecules for active adsorption sites. Multivalent ions may induce electrostatic shielding and partial site occupation, while organic constituents can contribute to site blocking and π–π competition, thereby reducing dye accessibility to the adsorbent surface.

Notably, the reduction in removal efficiency is more pronounced for AR (∼5.5%) compared to CV (∼4.5%), indicating that AR adsorption is more susceptible to matrix effects. These results confirm the potential applicability of the CC@NiO–CuO nanocomposite for treating complex wastewater systems containing mixed dye pollutants.

However, in the present study, the collected wastewater did not naturally contain CV and AR and therefore was artificially spiked for preliminary assessment of the adsorbent performance in a realistic aqueous matrix, further investigations using the using actual dye-contaminated industrial effluents are required to fully evaluate the applicability of the adsorbent under complex field conditions.

Conclusion

This study elucidates the adsorption behaviour of crystal violet (CV) and allura red (AR) on a coconut-coir-derived CC@NiO–CuO nanocomposite, demonstrating its effectiveness as a sustainable adsorbent for complex wastewater systems. In single-dye adsorption, removal is governed by synergistic electrostatic attraction, π–π interaction, hydrogen bonding, electron transfer, and metal–ligand coordination, as supported by spectroscopic, kinetic, isotherm, and thermodynamic analyses. CV exhibits faster uptake and higher adsorption capacity due to its strong interaction with metal-oxide and lignocellulosic domains, whereas AR adsorption is driven primarily by sulfonate- and azo-mediated interactions. In binary systems, adsorption evolves into a dynamic cooperative–competitive network. Preferential AR anchoring under acidic conditions creates localized charge heterogeneity that facilitates CV uptake via electrostatic bridging and π–π interactions, enabling near-quantitative CV removal. Increasing dye concentration and site competition suppress AR adsorption through competitive displacement, as confirmed by Rq analysis and spectroscopic evidence.

Overall, this work suggests that multicomponent adsorption behaviour cannot be extrapolated from single-solute models. The environmental implication of this study lies in the valorization of agricultural waste into multifunctional metal–biomass composites, providing a scalable, low-cost, and environmentally benign solution for the efficient treatment of chemically complex industrial effluents.
