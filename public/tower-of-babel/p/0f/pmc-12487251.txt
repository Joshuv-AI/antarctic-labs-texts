Hypertension in West Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prevalence and associated risk factors
Authors: Monday Nwankwo, Wusa Makena, Aisha Idris, Chikezie Jude Okamkpa, Anyanwu G Emeka, Elna Owembabazi, Elizabeth Bessy Umoren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension remains a critical modifiable risk factor contributing to the global burden of cardiovascular disease. Despite its escalating prevalence in West Africa, comprehensive regional data on its epidemiological patterns and associated determinants remain sparse. This study addresses this gap through a systematic review and meta-analysis, synthesizing existing evidence on hypertension prevalence and its risk factors across West African populations.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, CINAHL, and other sources to identify observational studies reporting hypertension prevalence or associated risk factors in adults aged ≥ 18 years in West Africa. Studies were included if they employed standardized diagnostic criteria (SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg or current antihypertensive medication use). Pooled prevalence estimates and odds ratios (ORs) for key risk factors were derived using random-effects meta-analysis. Heterogeneity was rigorously evaluated through prediction interval (PI), I² statistics, Q-tests, tau-squared, tau, and meta-regression analyses.ResultsThe analysis included 30 studies comprising 70,212 participants from 10 West African nations. The pooled prevalence of hypertension was estimated at 33.8% (95% CI: 29.3, 38.7%), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 99.4%; PI = 13 to 63%). Several factors were significantly associated with hypertension risk: overweight status (OR = 1.61; 95% CI: 1.40–1.85; I² = 88.4%; PI: 0.93–2.78), obesity (OR = 2.26; 95% CI: 2.02–2.53; I² = 75.2%; PI: 1.50–3.41), alcohol use (OR = 1.23; 95% CI: 1.05–1.44; I² = 79%; PI: 0.67–2.25), type 2 diabetes mellitus (OR = 1.98; 95% CI: 1.52–2.59; I² = 82.2%; PI: 0.81–4.84), reduced physical activity (OR = 1.46; 95% CI: 1.09–1.94; I² = 78.1%; PI: 0.57–3.70), and a family history of hypertension (OR = 1.74; 95% CI: 1.31–2.32; I² = 52.6%; PI: 0.59–5.18). Smoking showed a marginal association (OR = 1.03; 95% CI: 0.80–1.32; I² = 80.9%; PI: 0.42–2.51). Age demonstrated a strong positive association (β = 0.0537, p < 0.001), explaining 67% of the variance in the meta-regression model. Heterogeneity remained high across all risk factor analyses (I² range: 52.6–88.4%), underscoring variability in regional epidemiological patterns.ConclusionCombating West Africa’s high hypertension burden requires integrated strategies focusing on lifestyle modification, early detection, and strengthened primary healthcare systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24647-3.Keywords: Hypertension, West africa, Risk factors, Systematic review, Meta-analysis, Epidemiology, Cardiovascular disease

Background

Hypertension remains a critical global health challenge, serving as the primary modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular mortality and morbidity, including stroke, heart failure, and kidney disease. Current WHO estimates indicate approximately 1.28 billion affected adults worldwide, with low- and middle-income countries bearing 70% of this burden [1]. Sub-Saharan Africa experiences particularly high prevalence rates (16–50%), reflecting regional disparities in healthcare access and prevention strategies used [2, 3].

The West African subregion exemplifies the public health impact of this silent epidemic. For instance, a study conducted in Burkina Faso reported a hypertension prevalence of 18.5% nationally, with much higher rates observed among urban residents [3]. In Guinea, recent estimates indicated that 28.8% of older adults were hypertensive, with low awareness, treatment, and control rates [4]. Similarly, Nigeria has shown community-specific prevalence rates ranging between 20% and 46%, depending on urbanization, age, and comorbidity profiles [5]. These high and rising prevalence rates are reflective of broader transitions in the region’s demographic and epidemiological landscape.

Several modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors have been associated with hypertension in the West African context. These include advancing age, male sex, general and abdominal obesity, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, smoking, and comorbid conditions such as type 2 diabetes mellitus [5, 6]. The ongoing urbanization and lifestyle shifts–including dietary transitions and reduced physical activity–are believed to be contributing significantly to the increase in these risk factors in both rural and urban settings [7, 8].

Despite increasing awareness of hypertension, gaps remain in its detection, treatment, and control across West Africa. For example, studies have reported that over 60% of hypertensive individuals are unaware of their condition, and fewer than half receive any form of treatment [4, 6]. Factors contributing to this treatment gap include low health literacy, poor access to care, under-resourced health systems, and sociocultural barriers to seeking medical attention [8]. These realities amplify the risk of untreated hypertension progressing to complications such as stroke, chronic kidney disease, and ischemic heart disease.

Although individual studies across West African countries have contributed valuable epidemiological insights, their findings are often difficult to generalize due to heterogeneity in study designs, sampling strategies, population characteristics, and diagnostic criteria. For example, some studies rely on hospital-based samples, while others use community-based or nationally representative designs; the criteria for defining hypertension and associated risk factors also vary [7]. This variability creates challenges for synthesizing a coherent picture of the hypertension burden in the subregion and impairs the ability of policymakers to develop effective, evidence-based interventions.

Therefore, a systematic review and meta-analysis of hypertension and its risk factors in West Africa is both timely and essential. By using standardized data extraction methods, quality assessment tools such as the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (Supplementary File 1), and meta-analytic techniques, this review aims to: (i) quantify the prevalence of hypertension in West Africa; (ii) identify and quantify key risk factors associated with hypertension in the region; (iii) evaluate the methodological quality of existing studies; and (iv) provide actionable insights for clinical practice, public health programming, and policy development.

Despite growing awareness of hypertension as a critical public health issue in sub-Saharan Africa, there remains a striking gap in region-specific syntheses that capture the unique epidemiological profile of West Africa. This study addresses that gap by conducting the first targeted systematic review and meta-analysis to quantify the prevalence of hypertension and its major risk factors exclusively within West African populations. In contrast to previous broader reviews that often overlook subregional heterogeneity, our analysis focuses on intra-regional variations, stratifies findings by urban–rural settings, and evaluates the impact of key sociodemographic and behavioral factors such as obesity, age, alcohol use, and salt intake. Furthermore, this study applies subgroup and meta-regression techniques to explore underlying drivers of variability and identify high-risk subpopulations, offering actionable insights for regionally tailored prevention and screening strategies. By anchoring our synthesis in both epidemiological data and contextual realities, this work provides a timely and policy-relevant evidence base for addressing the rising burden of hypertension in West Africa.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [9], employing a PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome) framework and the recommendations outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews [10].

Data source and search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive search of seven electronic databases (PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL, Google Scholar, and Google) for studies published between January 2004 and December 2024. The literature search was developed and performed by WM. We used EndNote version 21 for automatic deduplication of records and screened using Rayyan [11]. Titles and abstracts blind screening were performed by WM and NM. Where disagreements occurred, an agreement was reached via discussion. Eligible studies underwent full-text screening by both reviewers, with additional articles identified through reference list examination. We included publications in English and French. Study selection

Our systematic search identified 2,297 records from databases and grey literature sources (Fig. 1). After removing 1,263 duplicates, we screened 1,034 unique records by title/abstract, excluding 679 irrelevant studies. Of 355 potentially eligible reports, 236 were unavailable, leaving 119 full-text articles for assessment. We excluded 89 studies due to: inaccessible full texts (n = 28), non-West African populations (n = 16), ineligible study designs (n = 24), or insufficient outcome data (n = 21). Ultimately, 30 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in our meta-analysis (Table 1).

Data extraction

We performed dual independent extraction from included studies using a standardized form. Data on prevalence of hypertension, association of age, alcohol consumption, tobacco smoking, physical inactivity, family history of hypertension, salt intake, and type-2-diabetes with hypertension were extracted. The odds ratios of each of these risk factors to hypertension were extracted. Where the odds ratios are not explicitly reported but results of the crosstabulations were reported, the results are used to compute the odds ratio. Discrepancies were resolved through iterative consensus discussions involving a third investigator when required, and corresponding authors were contacted to obtain any missing critical data - studies with irretrievable information were subsequently excluded from relevant quantitative syntheses to maintain analytic rigor.

Outcomes of interest

We systematically extracted study characteristics (including authorship, publication year, study design, country, setting, sample size, and participant demographics), hypertension prevalence rates with their measurement methodologies, and all reported associations between hypertension and prespecified risk factors - comprising demographic (age), behavioral (alcohol consumption, tobacco use, physical inactivity, salt intake), metabolic (type 2 diabetes), and genetic (family history) determinants. For each risk factor, we recorded either the reported odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals or sufficient raw data to calculate these effect measures when not explicitly provided in the original studies, with all extractions undergoing rigorous cross-verification during the consensus process to ensure data accuracy (Table 2).

Participant characteristics

Details on study participants comprised gender distribution, family history of hypertension, consumption of antihypertensive medication, age range, and sample size.

Variables and measurements

Particular attention was given to the criteria used in the included studies to define key variables such as hypertension, overweight, obese, and fasting blood glucose levels [12]. Hypertension was defined per WHO criteria as systolic BP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg. We applied standard WHO BMI classifications (overweight: 25.0–29.9 kg/m²; obesity: ≥30.0 kg/m²). Diabetes mellitus diagnosis required meeting established glycemic thresholds: (1) prediabetes/impaired glucose tolerance (fasting plasma glucose 5.6–6.9 mmol/L, 2-hour OGTT 7.8–11.0 mmol/L, or HbA1c 5.7–6.4%); or (2) diabetes (FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, OGTT ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%) [13, 14], with all diagnostic criteria extracted as reported in primary studies.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software, version 3 (Biostat Inc., Englewood, NJ, USA). The primary effect size metric for estimating the prevalence of hypertension was the event rate, while odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were employed to quantify the associations between hypertension and potential risk factors. Pooled effect sizes across studies were computed using the generic inverse-variance method. A random-effects model, based on the DerSimonian and Laird method, was utilized to synthesize overall prevalence estimates and pooled ORs for the associations between hypertension and various risk factors, including overweight/obesity (BMI categories), age, alcohol consumption, tobacco use, physical inactivity, family history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and salt intake. Between-study heterogeneity was evaluated using multiple statistical indices: prediction intervals (PIs), I² statistic, Cochran’s Q test, tau-squared (τ²), and tau (τ). Assuming a normal distribution of true effect sizes, the prediction interval was used to assess the dispersion of the true effects. The I² statistic quantified the proportion of observed variance attributable to true heterogeneity rather than sampling error. Cochran’s Q test was applied to test the null hypothesis of homogeneity across studies, i.e., that all studies share a common effect size. Between-study variance and its standard deviation were estimated using τ² and τ, respectively. To explore the potential moderating effect of age on hypertension, a random-effects meta-regression analysis was performed using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation method. The mean age of study populations was included as a continuous moderator. The regression coefficient (β), along with its 95% CI and associated p-value, was used to quantify the change in hypertension risk per one-year increase in mean age. Residual heterogeneity in the meta-regression model was estimated using τ², and the R² analog was computed to determine the proportion of between-study variance explained by age, representing the relative reduction in τ² when the moderator is included versus the null model.

To evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimates, sensitivity analyses were conducted using an iterative leave-one-out approach to assess the influence of individual studies. Publication bias and small-study effects were examined through both visual inspection of funnel plot asymmetry (when ≥ 10 studies were available) and formal statistical tests, including Egger’s linear regression test and Begg’s rank correlation test. In addition, the Duval and Tweedie Trim and Fill method was applied to adjust for potential publication bias. This method iteratively removes studies contributing to funnel plot asymmetry from the right side, estimates an unbiased central effect, and imputes their mirror images to the left side to restore symmetry. Although the meta-analysis employed a random-effects model for synthesis, the Trim and Fill imputation of potentially missing studies was performed using a fixed-effect model, limited to the left side of the distribution. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses, except for heterogeneity tests (Q statistic) and meta-regression, where a significance threshold of p < 0.10 was applied.

Results

The analysis utilized a DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model, accounting for within and between-study heterogeneity, by treating the included studies as a representative sample, drawn from a broader population of potential studies, thereby allowing generalization of our findings to the broader population of effect estimates within the West African subregion.

Burden of hypertension

The analysis is based on 30 studies. The effect size index is event rate and the findings of this analysis will be generalized to West African populations. The prevalence of hypertension for the 30 included studies is 33.8% with a 95% confidence interval of 29.3 to 38.7% (Fig. 3). The Z-value is 6.235 (p < 0.0001), which enables us to reject the null hypothesis of zero prevalence. The studies are sorted from the least reported prevalence to the highest prevalence. Equally noteworthy is the considerable heterogeneity in prevalence, with prevalence ranging from 18% [3] to 61.4% [4]. Although some of this variability is partly due to within-study error, some of it reflect variation in true effects. The Q-statistic was used to test the null hypothesis of homogeneity in effect sizes across all 30 studies. Cochran’s Q-value is 4,768.295, with df = 29 and p < 0.0001. Using a criterion alpha of 0.10, the statistically significant result indicates substantial heterogeneity. Therefore, we reject the assumption that the true effect size is the same in all the 30 studies. If the prevalence of hypertension is normally distributed in log units, the 95% prediction interval for the prevalence of hypertension in the next study in West Africa could fall in the range of 13–63%. The I-squared statistic of 99.4% suggests some 99.4% of the variance in observed effects reflects variance in true effect rather than sampling error. The variance of true effect sizes, quantified by tau-squared is 34.2%, corresponds to a standard deviation (tau) of 5.9%.

Association between overweight/obesity and hypertension

Figure 4 presents the pooled ORs for the association between overweight and obesity with hypertension, with some studies contributing to both subgroups. The overweight and obesity analyses included 20 and 24 studies, respectively. The pooled ORs were 1.61 (95% CI: 1.40, 1.85) for overweight and 2.26 (95% CI: 2.02, 2.53) for obesity, while the combined overweight/obese estimate was 1.97 (95% CI: 1.81, 2.15). For overweight individuals, ORs ranged from 0.42 (95% CI: 0.32, 0.56) [25], indicating a potential protective effect, to 3.25 (95% CI: 1.78, 5.95) [15], suggesting a more than threefold increase in hypertension odds. Only two studies [5, 25] reported ORs < 1, with one statistically significant (p = 0.048) and the other not (p = 0.800). The Q-value was 164.296 (df = 19, p < 0.0001), and the I² was 88.4%, indicating substantial heterogeneity. The estimated between-study variance was 0.063 with a standard deviation (τ) of 0.252 (log units). Assuming a normal distribution of true effects, the 95% prediction interval ranged from 0.93 to 2.78, suggesting that in 95% of West African populations, the odds of an overweight individual being hypertensive fall within this range. In the obesity subgroup, ORs ranged from 0.67 (95% CI: 0.45, 1.00) [25] to 4.78 (95% CI: 2.86, 8.00) [3]. Most studies reported ORs > 1 with significant p-values, reinforcing obesity as a risk factor for hypertension. Only one study [33] reported a non-significant or inverse association. The pooled effect yielded a Z-value of 12.997 (p < 0.001), confirming a significant overall effect. Heterogeneity was also present (Q = 92.699, df = 23, p < 0.0001; I² = 75.2%), with τ² = 0.036 and τ = 0.191 (log units). The 95% prediction interval was 1.50 to 3.41, indicating that in most West African populations, obesity is associated with increased odds of hypertension within this range.

Forest plot showing odds ratios for hypertension risk in overweight and obese individuals across multiple studies

Alcohol consumption and hypertension risk

Figure 5 displays the forest plot of 18 studies examining the association between alcohol consumption and hypertension. The pooled OR was 1.23 (95% CI: 1.05, 1.44), indicating a 5–44% increased risk among alcohol consumers. This association was statistically significant (Z = 2.568, p < 0.001), rejecting the null hypothesis of no effect. Individual study ORs ranged from 0.580 [35] to 6.148 [20], highlighting substantial heterogeneity. Three studies [28, 30, 35] suggested protective effects (OR < 1), while others [24, 32, 37] reported non-significant associations (OR ≈ 1, CI crossing unity). The majority, however, found elevated hypertension risk (OR > 1). Heterogeneity was significant (Q = 80.97, df = 17, p < 0.0001; I² = 79%), with between-study variance of 0.074 and standard deviation of 0.272 in log units. Assuming normally distributed true effects, the 95% prediction interval ranged from 0.67 to 2.25, suggesting future studies in similar settings may report ORs within this range.

Forest plot showing the association between alcohol consumption and risk of hypertension

Association between smoking and risk of hypertension

The forest plot in Fig. 6 illustrates the pooled association between smoking and the risk of hypertension across 15 studies. The mean ORs for the 18 studies is 1.04 with a confidence interval of 0.78 to 1.38. The Z-value is 0.240 (p = 0.810), suggesting a statistically non-significant association between smoking and hypertension. The Q-statistic is 78.188 with df = 14 and p < 0.0001. Tau-squared is 0.074 while the value of I-squared statistic is 82.1%, which implies that 82.1% of the observed variance comes from actual differences between studies, and can be explained by study-level covariates. This result implies no consistent effect direction across studies, as the confidence interval of the mean effect size crosses the null value of 1. The bidirectional findings may reflect smoking’s acute vasoconstrictive versus chronic weight-modifying effect, population differences in smoking intensity/duration or residual confounding (such as concurrent alcohol use). Nevertheless, the heterogeneity calls for cautious interpretation.

Forest plot of the association between smoking and hypertension in West African populations

Type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension risk

Figure 7 presents the meta-analysis of 11 studies on the association between type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and hypertension. The pooled OR was 1.98 (95% CI: 1.52–2.59), indicating nearly twice the risk of hypertension in individuals with T2DM (Z = 4.993, p < 0.0001). Most studies [15, 18, 30, 33, 35] reported significant associations, while a few [26, 28] found non-significant results with wide CIs, likely due to small samples or variability. Camara et al. [4] reported an unusually strong and precise effect (OR = 2.770; 95% CI: 2.696–2.846). Heterogeneity was substantial (Q = 56.235, df = 10, p < 0.0001; I² = 82.2%), with between-study variance τ² = 0.125 and τ = 0.353 (log units). The 95% prediction interval ranged from 0.84 to 4.67, suggesting the true effect in future studies could vary widely. These findings confirm T2DM as a strong risk factor for hypertension, likely reflecting shared pathophysiological pathways such as insulin resistance and vascular inflammation.

Low physical activity and hypertension risk

Figure 8 displays the meta-analysis of seven studies examining the association between low physical activity and hypertension. The pooled OR was 1.46 (95% CI: 1.09–1.94), indicating a significant association (Z = 2.563, p < 0.010). Four studies [20, 23, 26, 27] provided precise estimates with narrow CIs and low p-values, while [5, 24] reported protective and neutral associations, respectively.

Forest plot of the association between low physical activity and hypertension risk (random-effects model)

Heterogeneity was considerable (Q = 27.394, df = 6, p < 0.0001; I² = 78.1%), with τ² = 0.110 and τ = 0.331 (log units). The 95% prediction interval ranged from 0.57 to 3.70, indicating possible variability in future study findings. These results support a significant association between physical inactivity and elevated hypertension risk, underscoring its role in cardiovascular health.

Salt intake and hypertension risk

Figure 9 summarizes findings from six studies assessing the link between high salt intake and hypertension. The pooled OR was 1.44 (95% CI: 1.17, 1.77), confirming a significant association (Z = 3.449, p < 0.001). Three studies [23, 26, 37] reported strong positive associations, with Chukwu et al. [23] noting a particularly large effect (OR = 3.144). Between-study heterogeneity was moderate (Q = 9.806, df = 5, p = 0.081; I² = 49%), with τ² = 0.029 and τ = 0.170 (log units). These findings provide robust evidence linking high dietary salt consumption to increased hypertension risk.

Family history and risk of hypertension

Figure 10 summarizes the findings from four studies evaluating the association between positive family history of hypertension and the individual risk of developing hypertension. The mean effect size is 1.74 (95% CI: 1.31, 2.32). The Z-value is 3.813 (p < 0.0001), which allows us to reject the null hypothesis that family history is not a risk factor for hypertension. The Q-statistic is 6.329 with df = 3 and p = 0.097. The criterion α = 0.100, allows us to reject the null hypothesis that the true effect size is the same in the four studies.

Subgroup analysis and meta-regression

Heterogeneity in the findings were assessed using subgroup analysis and meta-regression. Table 3 presents summary of findings from the subgroup analysis conducted to evaluate the plausible sources of the high I-squared values. Meta-regression was applied to assess the relationship between participant age and the log OR of hypertension (Fig. 11). Table 4 presents summary results for the meta-regression using age to predict the log odds ratio of hypertension. The goal is to determine whether increasing age is associated with a greater likelihood of developing hypertension across included studies. The regression equation is Y = − 1.3676 + 0.0537 x Age (years). The slope (β = 0.0537) is positive, indicating that for each additional year of age, the log odds of hypertension increase by approximately 0.0537. The intercept is − 1.3676, suggesting that the baseline (predicted) log odds of hypertension is negative at the youngest end of the observed age spectrum. The R2 analog was 0.67, indicating that some 67% of the between-study heterogeneity in the log odds of hypertension was accounted for by participants’ age. This suggests that age is a substantial source of variation in hypertension risk across studies and underscores its significant role as a moderator in hypertension epidemiology.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was conducted by sequentially removing each study one at a time to assess the robustness of the pooled effect estimate, and the results showed that no single study significantly altered the overall association or analysis, indicating the stability and reliability of the meta-analysis findings. Publication bias was also performed as part of sensitivity analysis. The funnel plot in Fig. 12 appears symmetrical, with studies evenly distributed around the pooled effect estimate, suggesting minimal evidence of publication bias. The concentration of points near the apex and within the funnel boundaries further supports the absence of significant small-study effects, indicating a relatively low likelihood of systematic bias affecting the meta-analysis findings. Visual inspection of the funnel plot suggested approximate symmetry, although some mild asymmetry was noted. Egger’s test for small-study effects yielded a P-value of 0.292, indicating no statistically significant evidence of publication bias. Similarly, Begg’s rank correlation test produced a P-value of 0.682. Using the trim-and-fill procedure, no imbalance was observed between observed studies and hypothetical missing (such as small or unpublished) studies, suggesting minimal influence of publication bias on the overall results.

Discussion

Hypertension in West Africa is not merely a public health concern – it is a silent epidemic fueled by a collision of modernization and metabolic vulnerability. This systematic review and meta-analysis of 30 studies (n = 70,212) reveals a staggering 33.8% hypertension prevalence. Also, nearly 1 in 3 obese individuals facing 2.3-fold higher odds of hypertension and salt consumers facing 44% greater risk, painting a dire portrait of preventable cardiovascular catastrophe. These findings expose a healthcare time bomb: a region grappling with dual burdens of infectious diseases and skyrocketing cardiometabolic disorders, where urbanization and nutritional transitions are rewriting disease trajectories faster than interventions can respond. This study systematically examines the crisis through interconnected biological pathways, population-level epidemiological patterns, and evidence-based policy implications - providing critical insights to inform urgent public health interventions across West Africa.

What emerges clearly from the synthesis is that hypertension in West Africa is not the result of a single dominant cause but rather a constellation of interdependent risk factors, each with its own pathophysiological signature. Among the strongest and most consistent predictors is adiposity –general adiposity. Biologically, the hypertensive effects of obesity are mediated through an array of mechanisms that extend beyond simple hemodynamic load. Visceral adipose tissue operates as a potent endocrine organ, releasing leptin and resistin, which stimulate sympathetic nervous system activity, while simultaneously secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 that induce endothelial dysfunction and reduce nitric oxide bioavailability. These changes lead to increased vascular resistance, sodium retention, and impaired natriuresis, creating a state of chronic pressor load [41]. The degree to which obesity independently and synergistically elevates hypertension risk in this population underlines the importance of targeted interventions aimed at modulating adipose-induced vascular and metabolic dysregulation.

Equally central is the association between hypertension and T2DM, a relationship that speaks to the shared metabolic derangements underlying both conditions. Insulin resistance – hallmark of T2DM – has direct effects on vascular tone and renal sodium handling. Hyperinsulinemia enhances tubular sodium reabsorption and promotes sympathetic activation, while chronic hyperglycemia generates oxidative stress and impairs endothelial function. These pathways collectively foster arterial stiffness and vascular inflammation [42]. The strong and consistent association between diabetes and hypertension observed across studies in this review is therefore not incidental but mechanistically inevitable in the context of metabolic syndrome. It also reveals a missed opportunity for integrative care, as many health systems in West Africa still compartmentalize these conditions, failing to exploit the efficiency of co-management.

The aging process, an inevitable biological trajectory, remains an immutable yet powerful risk factor. The stiffening of central arteries, loss of elastin, increased collagen cross-linking, and diminished baroreceptor responsiveness result in a steady rise in systolic pressure with advancing age. These structural and functional changes in the vasculature, compounded by age-related decline in renal function and hormonal regulation, underpin the linear increase in hypertension prevalence observed in older cohorts [43]. In West Africa, where life expectancy is rising but geriatric care remains underdeveloped, the consequences of this shift are profound, heralding an imminent surge in age-related vascular complications unless addressed through anticipatory public health planning.

Dietary behaviors also featured prominently in the review, with salt intake demonstrating a clear and biologically plausible association with elevated blood pressure. The strength of the association between salt intake and hypertension might have been underpowered by sample size as only six studies reported association between salt consumption and risk of hypertension. Therefore, the association between salt consumption and hypertension observed in this review should be interpreted with caution. Also, most of the included studies assessed salt intake through self-reported dietary questionnaires, which are prone to recall and social desirability bias. Furthermore, there was no uniform method for quantifying salt intake across studies – some used frequency-based dietary questions, while others assessed discretionary salt use. This heterogeneity in measurement tools likely introduced variability and may have weakened the reliability of the pooled estimate. Future studies would benefit from standardized, objective methods for measuring salt intake (e.g., 24-hour urinary sodium excretion) to more accurately assess its contribution to hypertension risk in West African populations. The mechanisms of salt-induced hypertension are particularly potent in populations of African descent, who exhibit higher prevalence of salt sensitivity. High sodium intake leads to volume expansion, suppression of nitric oxide synthesis, and increased vascular resistance due to heightened reactivity to catecholamines and angiotensin II. Moreover, the widespread use of sodium-rich flavor enhancers such as bouillon cubes in West African cooking amplifies this risk [44]. The fact that most national health policies in the region lack coherent salt-reduction strategies is a public health oversight that undermines blood pressure control efforts.

The association between physical inactivity and hypertension is equally rooted in robust biological evidence. Physical activity modulates blood pressure by enhancing endothelial function, increasing vascular compliance, improving insulin sensitivity, and reducing systemic inflammation. Conversely, sedentary behavior reduces shear stress on arterial walls, downregulates endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), and accelerates arterial stiffness. In urbanizing West Africa, where traditional manual labor is being replaced by sedentary occupations, this behavioral shift becomes a key contributor to the hypertension epidemic [45]. Yet, promotion of physical activity remains largely absent from regional health promotion agendas, and where present, is rarely tailored to local cultural and socioeconomic realities.

Alcohol consumption, though variably reported across studies, was consistently linked to hypertension, especially in male cohorts. Ethanol influences blood pressure through complex neurohormonal pathways, including stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system, elevation of cortisol levels, and attenuation of baroreflex sensitivity. Chronic alcohol use also contributes to arterial remodeling, oxidative stress, and myocardial hypertrophy—changes that compound blood pressure elevation and cardiac workload. The social normalization of alcohol consumption in many West African communities, particularly among men, elevates the challenge of effective risk communication and behavior change.

Although smoking is a well-established cardiovascular risk factor globally, our meta-analysis found no significant association between smoking and hypertension among West African adults (OR = 1.03). This finding should be interpreted with caution due to several possible methodological limitations. First, only 15 of the included studies assessed smoking as a risk factor, which may have resulted in insufficient statistical power to detect a meaningful effect. Second, residual confounding is likely, particularly from behaviors that often co-occur with smoking, such as alcohol consumption, stress, and poor diet, which may not have been adequately adjusted for in the primary studies. Additionally, the lack of standardization in how smoking was defined and measured across studies – ranging from current smoking status to ever-use – may have diluted any true association. Given these limitations, the null finding does not rule out a potential relationship but underscores the need for better-quality data. Future research should aim to capture detailed smoking histories (e.g., intensity, duration, and type of tobacco) and adjust for key behavioral and sociodemographic covariates to clarify the role of smoking in hypertension risk in this population. Nonetheless, nicotine’s acute pressor effects via sympathetic activation, vasoconstriction, and catecholamine surge are well documented [46]. Moreover, smoking interacts synergistically with other risk factors such as alcohol use and dyslipidemia, and its exclusion from hypertension prevention programs would be shortsighted.

The significance of family history, consistently associated with a higher risk of hypertension in this review, underscores the genetic and epigenetic dimensions of blood pressure regulation. Polymorphisms in genes encoding RAAS components, ion channels, and renal transporters contribute to familial clustering of hypertension, and these genetic predispositions are often unmasked or accelerated by environmental triggers such as dietary sodium and psychosocial stress [47]. In the West African context, where genomic research is still emerging, family history remains a pragmatic surrogate marker for genetic susceptibility and should be systematically integrated into risk stratification algorithms.

Crucially, the biological coherence of these risk factors – adiposity, insulin resistance, vascular aging, endothelial dysfunction, neurohormonal activation – offers a unified pathophysiological narrative for hypertension in West Africa. Yet, the implications extend beyond the clinic. These mechanisms are not just biological – they are social, economic, and political. They are shaped by food systems that make ultra-processed foods cheaper than vegetables, by cities built for cars not people, and by health systems that screen more for malaria than for blood pressure. The intersectionality of these determinants demands responses that are as multifaceted as the disease itself.

The findings of this study have important clinical implications for hypertension screening and management in West Africa. The consistent association between hypertension and both increasing age and obesity underscores the need for targeted screening strategies. Specifically, prioritizing adults over the age of 40 and individuals with elevated body mass index (BMI) could improve the early identification of hypertension in resource-limited settings. Given the constrained healthcare infrastructure in many parts of the region, a focused approach that emphasizes routine blood pressure checks in outpatient clinics, community health outreaches, and workplace wellness programs for high-risk groups may be both cost-effective and impactful. Furthermore, integrating BMI assessment into standard clinical encounters could serve as a simple and practical risk stratification tool to guide preventive efforts and health education. These strategies align with existing WHO recommendations and could contribute to more efficient allocation of limited resources toward populations most at risk.

Efforts to reduce hypertension in West Africa, such as salt-reduction initiatives, are often hindered by infrastructural limitations including weak regulatory systems, limited food industry oversight, and low public awareness. Overcoming these barriers requires pragmatic, low-cost strategies such as public health education through community health workers and radio messaging, voluntary engagement with local food vendors to reduce salt content, and integration of dietary counseling into existing primary healthcare services. These approaches can help advance prevention goals despite limited resources.

What this review brings into sharp relief is that hypertension in West Africa is not merely rising—it is evolving. It is shaped not only by biology but by a rapidly changing environment, an underprepared health infrastructure, and a lack of region-specific strategies grounded in both evidence and context. The opportunity now lies in translating this evidence into action—by investing in population-wide prevention strategies, integrating chronic disease care into primary health systems, and deploying culturally resonant messaging that aligns with the lived realities of West African communities. Anything less would be a failure of evidence-based public health.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. There was substantial heterogeneity among the included studies in terms of methods and populations, which may have influenced the pooled estimates. The data were skewed toward urban settings. Such rural-urban imbalance may limit generalizability particularly in rural and underserved areas where health system access and hypertension awareness may differ substantially. Studies spanned different years, introducing temporal variability due to changing health trends (such as changes in health policies, lifestyle patterns, and awareness) over time. Although efforts were made to include gray literature, the majority of included studies were published in peer-reviewed journals. As a result, studies with significant findings may be overrepresented, potentially introducing publication bias. Although the review included studies published in English and French to reflect the dominant languages in West Africa, studies published in other languages or from underrepresented regions within West Africa may have been missed, which could slightly bias regional representation. Furthermore, 236 articles were excluded due to unavailability of full-texts despite multiple retrieval efforts. While this may have limited the breadth of included evidence, the remaining studies were sufficient to ensure robust and representative findings across West Africa. Finally, the predominance of cross-sectional studies limits causal interpretation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this review shows that hypertension in West Africa is a prevalent, multifactorial condition shaped by interrelated metabolic, behavioral, and demographic determinants. The consistent associations with modifiable factors such as obesity, physical inactivity, high salt intake, alcohol use, and type 2 diabetes underscore the urgency of context-specific interventions targeting both individual risk and system-level gaps. These findings support an integrated approach to non-communicable disease prevention – linking public health policy, culturally relevant education, and stronger primary care systems. Without timely action, the region’s demographic and nutritional transitions will accelerate the cardiovascular burden and strain already limited health resources. This synthesis provides a strong empirical and biological basis for renewed efforts in hypertension control across West Africa.
