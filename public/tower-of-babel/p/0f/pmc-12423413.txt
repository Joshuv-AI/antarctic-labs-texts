Influences of inter-bubble interactions on ultrasonic cavitation dynamics and subharmonic emissions in viscoelastic media with nonlinear elasticity☆
Authors: Dui Qin, Qianru Yang, Bingyu Zhang, Wei Wang, Xiaojuan Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUltrasonic cavitation in viscoelastic media is a physical process that features in a variety of chemical reactions, food processing, and biomedical applications due to a range of cavitation-associated physical, chemical, and biological effects. The cavitation effects are essentially caused by the dynamic behaviors of oscillating bubbles, which are strongly influenced by the complex and various inter-bubble interactions. However, how cavitation bubbles interact with each other and these interactions subsequently influence the ultrasonic cavitation dynamics and acoustic emissions in viscoelastic materials still remain poorly understood. This research, by incorporating both the interactions between bubbles and the nonlinear elasticity of viscoelastic medium, first modeled and numerically investigated the dynamics of interacting cavitation bubbles in viscoelastic media with nonlinear elasticity. Then, how the inter-bubble interactions affect the bubble dynamic behaviors and subharmonic cavitation emissions was quantitatively examined. Results revealed that the oscillation amplitudes are influenced differently for the case of two-bubble systems, i.e., either the enhancement or suppression effects, which affect the cavitation bubbles of different sizes to various degrees. It was found that, in most cases, the cavitation bubble of larger sizes can exert more pronounced influences on the smaller one, potentially determining whether the smaller bubble can produce subharmonic emissions. Moreover, the subharmonic emission from each cavitation bubble can be newly generated or eliminated, as respectively compared to the non-interacting cases where the single bubble is unable or able to generate the subharmonics. These phenomena primarily result from the shifts and dampening effects observed in the subharmonic resonance peak of cavitation bubbles. Additionally, it demonstrated that an increase in the amplitude of ultrasound and a decrease in the distance between bubbles would intensify the interaction effects on the subharmonic resonance characteristics. Such enhanced interactions can alter the resonance behaviors as well as amplify the strength of the 1/2 order subharmonics manifested in the power spectra. These findings might provide new insights into the interaction effects on the ultrasonic cavitation dynamics and resultant subharmonic emissions.Keywords: Ultrasonic cavitation, Cavitation dynamics, Subharmonic emissions, Inter-bubble interactions, Viscoelastic medium

Introduction

Ultrasonic cavitation holds substantial potential to enhance ultrasound utilization in various fields due to the cavitation-induced physical, chemical, and biological effects, such as the ultrasound-assisted drug/gene delivery [1,2], sonothrombolysis [3], food processing [4,5], ultrasonic extraction [6,7], etc. As a complex and microscopic physical process, ultrasonic cavitation includes the formation, growth, oscillation, and collapse of cavitation bubbles in liquids, exhibiting linear and non-linear responses to the ultrasonic waves [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Considerable evidence has accumulated to show that the cavitation effects are mainly determined by the dynamic behaviors of bubbles, and appropriately controlling the cavitation activity can produce highly efficient outcomes under specific conditions.

Several methods exist for measuring the cavitation activity, but acoustic methods are usually the most practical and feasible since they can be achieved non-invasively using external ultrasound probes for real-time cavitation monitoring, which can be more readily translated into actual applications [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. These methods exploit the secondary sound fields radiated by the cavitation bubbles because the nonlinear oscillations and collapse of the bubbles lead to energy being re-radiated over a wide range of frequencies. In particular, the frequency spectrum of the emitted acoustic signals is usually utilized to characterize the cavitation activity in water [[16], [17], [18]]. It has been demonstrated that at low and moderate acoustic pressures, one bubble undergoes stable cavitation and the frequency component of the cavitation emission contains multiples of the driving frequency (i.e., harmonics) and fractional harmonics (i.e., subharmonics and ultraharmonics). As the acoustic pressure increases to the inertial cavitation threshold, broadband noise becomes prominent. In addition to the acoustic pressure, several other parameters (e.g., the frequency of applied ultrasound, the radii of cavitation bubbles, and the properties of the surrounding environment etc.) can also notably affect the dynamics of cavitation bubbles and the characteristics of resultant cavitation emissions [[16], [17], [18]].

Recently, more research has been dedicated to investigating the ultrasonic cavitation in non-Newtonian fluids, namely, in viscoelastic media, which is crucial for a variety of chemical reactions [19], food processing [20], biomedical applications [21,22], and so on. Besides the experimental observation, numerical modelling and simulation have also been widely used to complement the experimental studies of cavitation dynamics in viscoelastic media. To explore the cavitation dynamics of an individual bubble within viscoelastic media, the early models were primarily developed based on the Rayleigh-Plesset cavitation model, coupled with the Maxwell constitutive equations [23,24]. Subsequent advancements have incorporated more sophisticated cavitation models, such as the Keller-Miksis and Gilmore-Akulichev models, alongside a range of viscoelastic constitutive equations, including the Kelvin-Voigt, Zener, and Neo-Hookean equations, and so on. [[25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. However, in most practical ultrasound applications, there generally exist multiple cavitation bubbles rather than a single bubble. During the acoustic cavitation, the strong acoustic emission generated by each oscillating cavitation bubble can act on the nearby bubbles as an additional driving acoustic pressure, which is termed the bubble − bubble interaction. Consequently, accounting for inter-bubble interactions has led to the development of single bubble cavitation models, and the developed models incorporated the effects of acoustic waves produced by other oscillating bubbles [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37]]. It has been demonstrated that the interactions between cavitation bubbles can remarkably influence the dynamic behaviors and the resultant cavitation emission of each bubble in water [30,31] or in viscoelastic media [32,33]. Regarding how mutual interactions between cavitation bubbles influences the bubble oscillation amplitudes, some previous studies showed a suppression effect [32,34], whereas others found an enhancement effect [35] or both could be observed [31,36,37]. With respect to the acoustic emissions of the bubbles, it has also been exemplified that a larger bubble undergoing subharmonic oscillations can enhance the 1/2 order subharmonics and 3/2 order ultraharmonics generated by a bubble cluster since it can force smaller bubbles to alter their original oscillation modes [31]. In contrast, some studies have pointed out that the 1/2 order subharmonic emission of one small bubble can also be suppressed due to the interaction effect with a nearby large bubble [30,32]. The observed discrepancies attest to the richness and complexity of interactions between bubbles, which exert different effects on the cavitation dynamic behaviors and the resultant emissions, but it is still not yet fully understood. Furthermore, the underlying physical mechanism responsible for the observed interaction effects between cavitation bubbles also remains to be further elucidated.

In this study, the Gilmore-Akulichev cavitation model coupled with the Kelvin-Voigt constitutive equation involving the neo-Hookean elasticity was employed to numerically explore the dynamics of cavitation bubbles and the acoustic emissions produced by oscillating bubbles within viscoelastic media. The present study simultaneously accounted for the interactions between cavitation bubbles and the nonlinear elasticity of the surrounding viscoelastic medium. Subsequently, qualitative and quantitative assessments were conducted on how and to what degree the interactions between bubbles affect the amplitudes of bubble oscillations, the oscillation patterns, and the acoustic emissions of cavitation in a two-bubble system. Finally, the complicated variations in the bubble oscillation amplitude, the subharmonic resonant radius, and the strengths of the 1/2 order subharmonics in the power spectra of the cavitation emissions were further analyzed within different simulation parameters, such as the initial bubble radii, ultrasound amplitude, and the distance between bubbles.

Results

Cavitation dynamics and emissions of a single bubble with different initial radii

The cavitation dynamics exhibits a great sensitivity to the initial bubble radius as other conditions are fixed [9,31,34,37]. Fig. 1(a) and (b) exemplify the cavitation dynamic behaviors of a single bubble and the corresponding frequency spectra of the cavitation emissions, respectively. The results of three differently sized cavitation bubbles (e.g., R0 = 2.9 μm, R0 = 5 μm, R0 = 6.5 μm) are compared. Fig. 1(a) illustrates that for the cavitation bubbles which are under the same acoustic conditions, remarkable variations in their oscillation amplitudes can be observed, generally demonstrating an initial decrease followed by an increase as the initial radius of the bubbles increases. Moreover, it is apparent that two cavitation bubbles having different initial radii (i.e., R0 = 2.9 μm and 5 μm) exhibit period 1 (P1) oscillations (i.e., there is one value for R/R0 at the end of each driving ultrasound period), whereas one cavitation bubble with an initial radius of R0 = 6.5 μm can undergo P2 oscillations. The corresponding frequency spectra of the cavitation emissions in Fig. 1(b) show the fundamental and harmonic components for the two cavitation bubbles undergoing P1 oscillations. In contrast, the frequency spectrum exhibits notable 1/2 order subharmonic components along with 3/2 and 5/2 orders ultraharmonics, indicating that P2 oscillation is concomitant with the generation of subharmonic and ultraharmonic cavitation emissions.

(a) Evolution of the cavitation dynamics for three bubbles with different initial radii (R0 = 2.9 μm, R0 = 5 μm, R0 = 6.5 μm) under the same ultrasonic excitation. (b) The corresponding frequency spectra of the cavitation emissions. (c) Bubble oscillation amplitudes (Rmax′=Rmax/R0) and (d) frequency spectra of the cavitation emissions as a function of R0.

To elucidate how the initial bubble size affects the radial oscillations of cavitation bubbles and the resulting cavitation emissions, Fig. 1(c) and (d) present the bubble oscillation amplitude, defined as Rmax′=Rmax/R0, and the frequency spectra of the emitted acoustic signals, in relation to the initial bubble radius R0. The radius response curve illustrated in Fig. 1(c) reveals three distinct peaks, specifically, the rightmost peak (i.e., R0 = 6.2 ∼ 8.0 μm, marked by the red arrow) represents the subharmonic resonance, while the other two peaks at R0 = 1.7 μm and R0 = 2.9 μm respectively correspond to the harmonic resonances (marked by the black arrows). This result can be verified by the frequency spectra of the cavitation emissions since the subharmonics and ultraharmonics are only generated within the range of R0 = 6.2 ∼ 8.0 μm, as displayed in Fig. 1(d). Taken together, these results shown in Fig. 1 demonstrate that under certain conditions, the cavitation bubble can undergo subharmonic oscillations and generate subharmonic and ultraharmonic emissions.

Bubble–bubble interactions: An enhancement effect

To explore the effects of interactions between bubbles, the dynamics of cavitation and the resulting acoustic emissions are illustrated and thoroughly analyzed in systems containing two bubbles with different sizes. Firstly, the initial radii of two cavitation bubbles are respectively fixed to be R01 = 5 μm and R02 = 6.3 μm to ensure that the smaller cavitation bubble maintains the P1 oscillations and the larger one manifests the P2 oscillations, as indicated in Fig. 1. For this case, it is easy to illustrate how the inter-bubble interactions between two cavitation bubbles that undergo different oscillatory patterns influence the cavitation dynamics as well as the acoustic emissions. As shown in Fig. 2(a) and (b), there is a clear enhancement in the cavitation dynamics of two bubbles (labeled as R01-w and R02-w) due to the inter-bubble interactions, when compared to the cases of single cavitation bubbles (R01-w/o and R02-w/o), respectively. Moreover, when considering the interactions, there is a transition from P1 to P2 for the oscillation mode of the smaller bubble. Furthermore, the power spectrum analysis of the cavitation emission from each bubble is performed and presented in Fig. 2(c). For the smaller cavitation bubble with taking the inter-bubble interactions into consideration (R01-w), its cavitation emissions contain distinct subharmonic and ultraharmonic components, while there appear to be no subharmonics and ultraharmonics for the single cavitation bubble case (R01-w/o). In contrast, the frequency spectra of the acoustic emissions for the larger cavitation bubble appear to remain unchanged when comparing the interacting (R02-w) and non-interacting (R02-w/o) cases. These results strongly indicate that one larger cavitation bubble undergoing P2 oscillations can evidently affect the dynamics of an adjacent smaller cavitation bubble, and specifically, one smaller cavitation bubble can be driven to transfer its vibration mode from P1 to P2 oscillations.

Furthermore, it is somewhat surprising that the oscillatory patterns of both cavitation bubbles and the characteristics of the cavitation emissions can be remarkably changed by the inter-bubble interactions for a two-bubble system (R01 = 5 μm and R02 = 6 μm). As illustrated in Fig. 2(d) and (e), the interactions between cavitation bubbles show various effects on the bubbles that have different initial radii. Specifically, the radial oscillations of the smaller cavitation bubble (R01-w) appear to be slightly enhanced (i.e., an increase in the maximum of R1/R01 from 1.491 to 1.536), whereas the oscillations of the larger cavitation bubble (R02-w) are noticeably enhanced (i.e., an increase in the maximum of R2/R02 from 1.270 to 1.378) when compared to those obtained in the single cavitation bubble cases (R01-w/o and R02-w/o), respectively. More importantly, taking the interactions between the two cavitation bubbles into consideration, each bubble changes its vibration mode from P1 to P2 oscillations. Therefore, within the frequency spectrum of the cavitation emissions produced by each bubble, the presence of subharmonic and ultraharmonic components emerges due to these inter-bubble interactions. In contrast, the single bubble scenarios do not exhibit any subharmonics or ultraharmonics, as demonstrated in Fig. 2(f). Considering the results presented in Fig. 2, it can be noted that interactions between cavitation bubbles demonstrate a clear enhancement effect on both the amplitude of bubble oscillation and the emissions of subharmonic and ultraharmonic acoustic waves from cavitation bubbles.

To explain the observed different interaction effects between the two-bubble systems that have distinct initial bubble radii, the radius response curves (R01 = 0.1 ∼ 10 μm) are plotted without and with taking into account the interactions with a nearby cavitation bubble (R02 = 6.3 μm), as displayed in Fig. 3(a). It is evident that the subharmonic resonance peak (as marked by the red arrow) in the radius response curve (i.e., R1max′=R1max/R01) presents a slight left shift and a new peak is formed in the curve of R2max′=R2max/R02 as a function of R01, when accounting for inter-bubble interactions. These results strongly indicate that the inter-bubble interactions significantly enhance the oscillation amplitude of nearby cavitation bubbles (R02) at the subharmonic resonance state of the observed cavitation bubble (R01). Moreover, the power spectra for the acoustic emissions produced by the first cavitation bubble (R01) and the second cavitation bubble (R02) are manifested in Fig. 3(b) and (c), respectively. It clearly shows that both bubbles can generate subharmonic and ultraharmonic cavitation emissions within a range of R01 = 0.1 ∼ 7.9 μm. In comparison to a single cavitation bubble case, it may be concluded that the cavitation bubble undergoing the subharmonic resonance (R02 = 6.3 μm) plays a predominant role in the two-bubble systems under the simulation conditions examined here, forcing a smaller cavitation bubble (R01 = 0.1 ∼ 6.1 μm) to shift its oscillatory pattern from P1 into P2 oscillations and thereby producing subharmonic and ultraharmonic cavitation emissions. On the contrary, the existing subharmonic and ultraharmonic cavitation emissions can also disappear owing to the interaction effects between one cavitation bubble (R02 = 6.3 μm) that undergoes the subharmonic resonant oscillations and another larger cavitation bubble whose radius within a range of R01 = 8 ∼ 10 μm, as manifested in Fig. 3(c).

In addition, the interactions between the two-bubble systems (R01 = 0.1 ∼ 10 μm and R02 = 6 μm) are further investigated. The radius response curves and the power spectra of the cavitation emissions of individual bubbles are respectively displayed in Fig. 3(d)-(f). Fig. 3(d) illustrates that there is an obvious left shift for the subharmonic resonance peak in the radius response curve, and there appears a new peak in the curve of R2max′=R2max/R02 as a function of R01. This might be the possible reason for the interaction effects between bubbles influencing the cavitation emissions as shown in Fig. 2(f). Furthermore, the power spectra depicted in Fig. 3(e) and (f) can also verify the interaction effects on the cavitation emissions. More specifically, Fig. 3(e) shows that the subharmonics and ultraharmonics can be generated from the first cavitation bubble within a larger range of R01 = 4.5 ∼ 7.9 μm when compared to the single cavitation bubble case (R0 = 6.2 ∼ 8.0 μm shown in Fig. 1(d)), whereas the cavitation emissions from the second cavitation bubble can maintain the generation of subharmonics and ultraharmonics within the same range of R01 = 4.5 ∼ 7.9 μm as illustrated in Fig. 3(f). It is noted that the second cavitation bubble (R02 = 6 μm) cannot generate the subharmonic emissions without accounting for the influences exerted by the interactions between bubbles. Therefore, these results suggest that an enhancement effect can be exerted on the generation of subharmonic and ultraharmonic cavitation emissions owing to the interactions between cavitation bubbles, especially for the cavitation bubbles of smaller sizes that cannot generate subharmonics and ultraharmonics in the single bubble case.

Bubble–bubble interactions: A suppression effect

In addition to the enhancement effects, the inter-bubble interactions may also induce a suppression effect on the cavitation behaviors in two-bubble systems, as demonstrated in Fig. 4. When the inter-bubble interaction is considered, it is apparent in Fig. 4(a) and (b) that the oscillation amplitudes of both cavitation bubbles (R01 = 7.5 μm and R02 = 7.8 μm) are reduced dramatically, and the oscillatory patterns of bubbles can be changed from P2 to P1 oscillations. Accordingly, in the power spectra of the cavitation emissions, the subharmonic and ultraharmonic components disappear due to the bubble − bubble interactions, as manifested in Fig. 4(c). Furthermore, an additional instance of the suppression effect for a two-bubble system (R01 = 7.5 μm and R02 = 9 μm), on their cavitation dynamics and the emissions is also presented, as depicted in Fig. 4(d)−(f). It can be clearly observed in Fig. 4(d) that the radical oscillations of the smaller cavitation bubble within the two-bubble system experience a prominent reduction with a decrease in the maximum of R1/R01 from 1.421 to 1.113). In comparison, for the larger cavitation bubble, the interactions between bubbles only exert a slight suppression effect on its oscillation amplitude (i.e., a decrease in the maximum of R2/R02 from 1.084 to 1.070 as displayed in Fig. 4(e)). Meanwhile, the oscillatory pattern of the smaller cavitation bubble is changed from P2 to P1 oscillations, and the larger cavitation bubble still remains the P1 oscillation. Consequently, it is evident in Fig. 4 (f) that the power spectra of cavitation emissions from the smaller bubble show a complete disappearance of both subharmonic and ultraharmonic components, which is attributed to the influences exerted by the inter-bubble interaction.

Furthermore, to explain the observed interaction effects on the oscillation amplitudes of cavitation bubbles (i.e., R1max′ and R2max′) and the characteristics of cavitation emissions, the radius response curves (R01 = 0.1 ∼ 10 μm) and the resultant power spectra of the cavitation emissions as a function of R01 are also presented in Fig. 5. Two cases in which the nearby cavitation bubble that is on or off the subharmonic resonance state are further compared. It is clear in Fig. 5(a) that the subharmonic resonance peak (marked by the red arrow) in the radius response curve can be noticeably suppressed by the inter-bubble interactions. This finding indicates that the resonance characteristics can be distinctly affected by the interactions between cavitation bubbles. Correspondingly, for the cavitation emissions from the two interacting bubbles, Fig. 5(b) shows that one larger cavitation bubble that is at the subharmonic resonance state (R02 = 7.8 μm) can force the smaller cavitation bubble (R01 = 0.1 ∼ 6.1 μm) in the two interacting bubble systems to generate the subharmonic and ultraharmonic emissions, which cannot be generated from a single cavitation bubble of size within this range (as compared to Fig. 1(d)). In contrast, Fig. 5(c) shows that the smaller cavitation bubble of two-bubble systems exhibits negligible effects on the cavitation emission of the larger bubble (R02 = 7.8 μm) that is at the subharmonic resonance state as R01 < 7.3 μm, but the generation of subharmonic and ultraharmonic emissions from the cavitation bubble (R02) can also be eliminated due to the interaction effects as R01 = 7.3 ∼ 10 μm. Moreover, when the larger cavitation bubble (R02 = 9 μm) is not at the subharmonic resonance state, as manifested in Fig. 5(d)−(f), the interactions between bubbles similarly affect the radius response curves and the characteristics of the cavitation emissions. Specifically, a noticeable suppression effect can be observed in the cavitation dynamics of the smaller bubbles, leading to the disappearance of the subharmonic resonance peak. Consequently, no subharmonic and ultraharmonic emissions can be generated for each cavitation bubble within the whole range of the initial bubble radius (R01 = 0.1 ∼ 10 μm) examined here.

In summary, the findings illustrated in Fig. 3, Fig. 5 clearly suggest the presence of distinct effects (i.e., enhancement or suppression effects) resulting from the inter-bubble interactions on the ultrasonic cavitation dynamics and the corresponding subharmonic emissions. Moreover, the extent of these influences varies greatly, depending on the initial sizes of the bubbles. Therefore, the oscillation amplitude of a cavitation bubble (R01 = 0.1 ∼ 10 μm) in the two-bubble systems and the power spectra of the resultant cavitation emissions are further investigated at various initial radii of the nearby cavitation bubble (R02 = 0.1 ∼ 10 μm) in the following sections, for an improved comprehension of the interactions between differently sized cavitation bubbles.

Effects of the initial radii of the nearby cavitation bubble

The oscillation amplitude (R1max′) and the strength of the 1/2 order subharmonic component in the power spectra (S1,1/2) of one cavitation bubble with various initial radii (R01 = 0.1 ∼ 10 μm) are presented in Fig. 6, while varying the initial radius of the other cavitation bubble (R02 = 0.1 ∼ 10 μm) in the two-bubble systems. It is evident that the bubble oscillation amplitude (R1max′) varies with its initial sizes (R01), and the initial radius of the nearby cavitation bubble (R02) would also remarkably influence the R1max′, as displayed in Fig. 6(a). There seems to be a considerably complex influence, exhibiting either an enhancement effect or a suppression effect with various degrees for different combinations of R01 and R02. Additionally, the differences in the R1max′ between the two cases with (w) and without (w/o) considering the inter-bubble interactions are also calculated (specifically, ΔR1max′=R1max′-w -R1max′-w/o) and plotted in Fig. 6(b). The results indicate that interaction effects can significantly reduce bubble oscillation amplitudes in the majority of instances. In particular, it appears that as the size of the nearby cavitation bubble increases, the suppression effect intensifies. However, in certain situations where both R01 and R02 are close to the subharmonic resonance peak, it should be noticed that the interactions between bubbles can prominently amplify the oscillation amplitudes of the cavitation bubbles.

When compared to those obtained in the case without considering the interaction effects (S1,1/2-w/o), as shown in Fig. 6(d), there are various influences on the S1,1/2 when two cavitation bubbles interact with each other. In the cases of two interacting bubbles having different initial radii examined here, one cavitation bubble (R02 = 6.2 ∼ 8.0 μm) which is on the subharmonic resonance state can force a smaller cavitation bubble (R01 = 0.1 ∼ 6.1 μm) that is off the subharmonic resonance state to generate the 1/2 order subharmonic cavitation emission, in turn, a larger cavitation bubble (approximately R02 = 8.1 ∼ 10 μm) can also eliminate the generation of subharmonic cavitation emission from a cavitation bubble whose initial radius approaches the subharmonic resonance peak. Furthermore, the maximum value of the subharmonic resonance peak in the radius response curve of R01 is defined as the bubble oscillation amplitude at the subharmonic resonance peak R1max-1/2′, which is calculated as a function of the initial radius of the other bubble R02, as depicted in Fig. 6(e). Correspondingly, the value of the R01 where the maximum value of the subharmonic resonance peak can be obtained is defined as the subharmonic resonant radius R1res-1/2 and it is plotted as a function of R02 (Fig. 6(f)). A schematic diagram of the calculation of R1max-1/2' and R1res-1/2 is presented in the supplementary materials (Fig. S1). It is apparent that with an increase in R02, the R1max-1/2′ first increases slightly, then decreases, and increases again, finally followed by a notable decrease or even a disappearance of the subharmonic resonance peak. Moreover, compared to the single bubble case, it is evident in Fig. 6(f) that the R1res-1/2 of one cavitation bubble can be either decreased or increased as a function of the initial radius of the other bubble R02 when the inter-bubble interactions are considered, indicating that the subharmonic resonance peak undergoes a left shift (i.e., a decrease in R1res-1/2) or a right shift (i.e., an increase in R1res-1/2) due to the interaction effects between cavitation bubbles. More importantly, the subharmonic resonance of one cavitation bubble can also be eliminated as the initial radius of the nearby bubble is sufficiently large (i.e., no values of R1res-1/2 can be obtained as R02 > 7.4 μm).

Effects of ultrasound amplitude

As illustrated in Fig. 7, how the inter-bubble interactions affect the characteristics of cavitation dynamics as well as the subharmonic emissions of one cavitation bubble (R01 = 0.1 ∼ 10 μm) with a nearby cavitation bubble of different sizes (R02 = 0.1 ∼ 10 μm) at various ultrasound amplitudes pA are further examined, including three evaluation indicators (a) R1max-1/2′, (b) R1res-1/2 and (c) S1,1/2 at the subharmonic resonance conditions. For the single bubble cases (denoted by the symbol “w/o”), it is evident that with an increase in the pA, the radial oscillation of the cavitation bubble becomes more pronounced, i.e., the higher the pA is, the larger the R1max-1/2′ becomes. Additionally, as demonstrated in Fig. 7(b), the R1res-1/2 decreases with increasing pA, which suggests a left shift of the subharmonic resonance peak when the pA increases. For the interacting cases (denoted by the symbol “w”), the findings distinctly reveal that the subharmonic resonance peak can be slightly enhanced or remarkably reduced (Fig. 7(a)), accompanied by a left or right shift of the subharmonic resonance peak (Fig. 7(b)). Noticeably, at lower ultrasonic pressures, no values of R1max-1/2′ (Fig. 7(a)) and R1res-1/2 (Fig. 7(b)) can be obtained as the R02 is sufficiently large (e.g., R02 > 7.1 μm as pA = 200 kPa and R02 > 7.4 μm as pA = 250 kPa), suggesting that the subharmonic resonance of one cavitation bubble can be eliminated due to the interactions with a larger nearby cavitation bubble. Correspondingly, the power spectra of the cavitation emissions do not exhibit any 1/2 order subharmonic components when the subharmonic resonance peak disappears, as depicted in Fig. 7(c). Overall, the findings illustrated in Fig. 7 indicate that the fundamental traits of subharmonic resonance in a single cavitation bubble and the resultant subharmonic emissions as well as the effects of interactions between cavitation bubbles, are prominently influenced by the amplitude of the applied ultrasound.

Effects of inter-bubble distance

The interactions between cavitation bubbles are severely influenced by the distance between them (d12), as indicated in Eq. (1). Fig. 8(a)−(c) displays the variation trends of R1max-1/2′,R1res-1/2 and S1,1/2 for a cavitation bubble (R01 = 0.1 ∼ 10 μm) as a function of R02 at various d12. It can be clearly observed that the differences in the R1max-1/2′, R1res-1/2 and S1,1/2 between the non-interacting case (indicated by the black dashed lines) and the interacting cases (indicated by the color lines) are prominently reduced as the d12 increases. This indicates that a larger inter-bubble distance leads to a weaker inter-bubble interaction effect. Furthermore, it is important to highlight that interactions between cavitation bubbles can eliminate the subharmonic emissions when the interacting cavitation bubbles are sufficiently proximate (e.g., the case of d12 = 100 μm, R02 > 7.8 μm as displayed in Fig. 8(c)). This highlights the need to take into account inter-bubble interactions when examining the dynamic characteristics and the acoustic emissions of ultrasonic cavitation in practical applications, particularly in situations where a high concentration of cavitation bubbles exists.

Discussion

Prior studies have noted the importance of interactions between bubbles on their dynamic behaviors and the resultant cavitation emissions, exhibiting evident discrepancies in the interaction effects among different studies [31,32,[34], [35], [36], [37]]. Furthermore, how and why the inter-bubble interactions affect the ultrasonic cavitation dynamics and the subharmonic emissions in viscoelastic media with nonlinear elasticity are still poorly understood. The present study was designed to numerically investigate the complex influences of inter-bubble interactions on the cavitation dynamics and subharmonic cavitation emissions via modelling the cavitation dynamics of interacting bubbles with considering the inter-bubble interactions and the nonlinear elasticity of the surrounding medium. The Gilmore-Akulichev cavitation model with taking into account the bubble-to-bubble interactions, was integrated with the Kelvin–Voigt constitutive equation featuring neo-Hookean elasticity, which accounts for the reference configuration of the viscoelastic medium [25,29]. This approach effectively addresses the nonlinear elasticity of the medium with a hyperelastic term to adequately represent the large medium deformation, making it particularly appropriate for scenarios involving large-amplitude oscillations of cavitation bubbles in viscoelastic environments.

This study demonstrates that under certain conditions, the cavitation bubble can undergo subharmonic oscillations and generate subharmonic and ultraharmonic emissions. As exemplified in Fig. 1, it should be noted that the cavitation dynamics of one bubble and its acoustic emission are considerably complex due to various acoustic responses of cavitation bubbles with different initial radii. Furthermore, in most situations, multiple cavitation bubbles are commonly generated rather than a single bubble in the ultrasound applications involving bubble cavitation, and accordingly, the mutual interactions between cavitation bubbles of different sizes should be considered. The interactions between bubbles will introduce additional complexity to the behaviors of cavitation bubbles as well as the nature of the resulting acoustic emissions. For two-bubble systems with various initial bubble sizes, the results demonstrate that the amplitudes of the bubble oscillations, the oscillatory patterns, and the cavitation emissions of bubbles are highly sensitive to the inter-bubble interactions. More specifically, the bubble oscillation amplitude and the subharmonic cavitation emission can be either enhanced (Fig. 2, Fig. 3) or suppressed (Fig. 4, Fig. 5), affecting the cavitation bubbles of different sizes to various degrees.

For the enhancement effect, it is somewhat surprising that one larger cavitation bubble undergoing P2 oscillations can evidently affect the dynamics of an adjacent smaller cavitation bubble, and specifically, smaller cavitation bubbles can be driven to transfer their vibration modes from P1 to P2 oscillations. This finding is consistent with the previous observation of the inter-bubble interactions influencing the oscillatory patterns and the acoustic emissions of the interacting bubbles that are oscillating in water [31]. Moreover, another unanticipated result is that each of the two cavitation bubbles can undergo a transition from P1 to P2 oscillations owing to the inter-bubble interactions, and correspondingly, the subharmonic and ultraharmonic cavitation emissions appear. Taken together, the interactions between cavitation bubbles demonstrate a clear enhancement effect on both the amplitudes of bubble oscillations as well as the subharmonic and ultraharmonic emissions from cavitation bubbles. This enhancement is very beneficial for utilizing the specific cavitation emissions as an indicator to monitor the acoustic cavitation during its applications. In contrast, our findings also show that the oscillation amplitude of one cavitation bubble undergoing P2 oscillations can be remarkably suppressed, and its oscillatory pattern can be changed into P1 oscillations by the inter-bubble interactions with a nearby cavitation bubble, whether the nearby bubble is undergoing the P2 oscillations or not. Moreover, the oscillatory pattern of the nearby cavitation bubble undergoing P2 oscillations can also be changed from P2 to P1 oscillations in certain cases. Consequently, as compared to the corresponding non-interacting cases respectively, there exist significant differences in the subharmonic and ultraharmonic emissions from the two-bubble systems, which completely disappear while considering the interactions between cavitation bubbles. This suppression effect on the bubble oscillation amplitudes accords well with our earlier observations [32,34]. Combining the results shown in Fig. 2 − 5, it can be suggested that the interactions between neighboring cavitation bubbles show strong correlations with the cavitation behavior of each bubble as well as the initial sizes of cavitation bubbles, and these findings also strongly highlight the complexity of cavitation bubble dynamics and emphasize the significance of inter-bubble interactions in investigating ultrasonic cavitation dynamics and subharmonic emissions.

Furthermore, the amplitude of bubble oscillation at the subharmonic resonance peak, along with the subharmonic resonant radius and the intensity of the 1/2 order subharmonics in the power spectra of cavitation emissions, are quantitatively analyzed under different conditions. The results for the two-bubble systems with varying the initial radii of both bubbles (Fig. 6) further support the fact that the enhancement and suppression effects on the oscillation amplitudes of bubbles and the resultant subharmonic cavitation emissions highly depend on the initial radii of the two-interacting bubbles, potentially indicating that these complex influences can be explained by the shift and damping of the subharmonic resonance peak owing to the inter-bubble interactions. A similar observation has been reported in the previous study, indicating that the bubble oscillation amplitude can be increased or decreased when using a suitable frequency, since the frequency response curve decreases and shifts to the left due to the bubble–bubble interactions [36]. For the effects of the ultrasound parameters, the findings illustrated in Fig. 7 indicate that the fundamental traits of subharmonic resonance, along with the resulting subharmonic emissions and the effects of interactions among multiple cavitation bubbles, are significantly influenced by the amplitude of the applied ultrasound. Meanwhile, the resonance response of bubbles subjected to ultrasound is also frequency-dependent, and the resonance frequency varies inversely with the bubble radius [34,39,40]. It can be expected that as the ultrasound frequency increases, the subharmonic resonant radius can be decreased from micron to sub-micron scales, as observed in our previous study [30]. Finally, the results in Fig. 8 show how the inter-bubble distance affects the inter-bubble interactions, and one can anticipate that these influences may become negligible when the inter-bubble distance is sufficiently large, as a single bubble can be viewed as a special case of two interacting cavitation bubbles with an infinite separation. These findings align well with the earlier experimental observation [16] and the numerical studies on the inter-bubble interactions within two- or multi-bubble systems [16,31,34,36].

The subharmonics and ultraharmonics are exclusive to the oscillating cavitation bubbles rather than the surrounding media. Thus, the distinctive subharmonic signal has been utilized in various ultrasound applications related to microbubbles, which encompass, but are not restricted to, the enhancement of contrast in ultrasound imagery [[41], [42], [43]], the observation of stable cavitation as an indicator [31,44,45], non-invasive testing of blood pressure [[46], [47], [48]], as well as other applications. This study provides important insights into how the inter-bubble interactions affect the ultrasonic cavitation dynamics and subharmonic emissions in viscoelastic media with nonlinear elasticity; however, it also has some notable limitations. First, the effect of nonlinear wave propagation has been neglected, which is essential for more accurate simulations of cavitation bubble dynamics, particularly for some cases, such as at sufficiently high acoustic pressures. Second, it is assumed that cavitation bubbles remain spherical during the whole acoustic cavitation, but non-spherical oscillations and fragmentation of bubbles may occur at the bubble collapse stage in certain scenarios. Third, the current analysis utilizes a two-bubble system to examine the bubble − bubble interactions, while microbubbles actually exist in the form of bubble cluster. Finally, the current model assumes an infinite viscoelastic medium, but the medium surrounding the cavitation bubbles may have boundaries in some laboratory scenarios (e.g., hydrogels, tissues and phantom models, and so on) or real biomedical applications. There is some evidence that the medium boundaries can affect the bubble dynamics (e.g., the bubble displacement [49,50], jet direction [51,52], and so on). Moreover, the resonance frequency of the bubble oscillation can also be notably altered [11,53,54]. It is therefore noteworthy that the medium boundaries potentially influence the cavitation bubble dynamics and the resultant subharmonic emissions. These aforementioned issues need to be reasonably addressed in further research.

Conclusion

The current study was conducted to numerically investigate the cavitation dynamics of interacting bubbles in viscoelastic media and their subharmonic emissions under ultrasound excitation. For two-bubble systems examined here, it is observed that one cavitation bubble, with a radius close to or exactly at the peak of subharmonic resonance, can amplify the oscillation amplitude of another smaller cavitation bubble and facilitate a shift in its oscillatory behavior from P1 to P2 oscillations, resulting in the emergence of new subharmonic cavitation emissions. By contrast, the subharmonic emission of one cavitation bubble can also be suppressed notably or even disappear completely by the inter-bubble interaction with a nearby cavitation bubble that is able or unable to generate subharmonic emissions. Nevertheless, the findings generally provide a clear evidence that in a two-bubble system, one cavitation bubble of a larger size has a significantly more pronounced effect on a smaller bubble. In addition, the results show that the oscillation amplitude and the strengths of the 1/2 order subharmonic emissions of the cavitation bubbles at the subharmonic resonance state can be enhanced, but the subharmonic resonant radius is decreased as the ultrasound amplitude increases. Additionally, it shows that the farther away the two cavitation bubbles are, the weaker the influence of the inter-bubble interaction becomes, and when the distance is far enough, the interaction effect can even be negligible. This study has the potential to deepen the understanding of the intricate interaction effects on the cavitation dynamics and subharmonic emissions, thereby facilitating the optimal utilization of subharmonic cavitation emissions to monitor the ultrasound applications associated with acoustic cavitation.
