A dataset encompassing length-at-age data of early and adult stages of geoduck clam Panopea globosa
Authors: Marlene A Luquin-Covarrubias, Sergio S González-Peláez, Viridiana Y Zepeda-Benítez, Enrique Morales-Bojórquez, Mariana Díaz Santana Iturrios, Juan Carlos Hernández-Padilla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndividual growth is an important demographic component in population dynamics that indicates the contribution of biomass to the population, such as an increase in length and weight over time. In addition, the length-at-age provides information concerning mortality, reproduction rates, survival, and biological factors, which are essential for stock assessment and fishery management. Age and growth studies have focused on estimating average growth trajectories based on length-at-age data from only one developmental stage, assuming a constant growth throughout the life cycle. However, the individuals within a cohort grow at different rates and speeds. This causes uncertainty in the mathematical models fitted to the data, both in the growth parameters and in the mean length-at-age within a cohort.The datasets included in this article were collected from two sources: 1) Fishery-independent data of shell length frequency and age of individuals in early growth stages (0–68 days) obtained under rearing experimental conditions. 2) Fishery-dependent data of shell length-at-age of adult individuals aged 3–47 years collected from commercial landings. The straight-line distance of live specimens was measured between the anterior and posterior margin of the shell. Specimens were then dissected to extract the shells. Age was determined as the number of internal growth lines.The length and age (days) datasets comprised 797 records for larvae and juveniles, and 357 records for adult individuals. These data provide valuable information for researchers, decision makers, the government, and private and academic institutions. The dataset enables comparative analyses and further investigations. Its reuse potential extends to broader topics, such as stock assessment, population dynamics, and fishery management.Keywords: Individual growth, Shell length, Growth rings, Development stages

Background

Age and growth studies are typically constrained to the datasets available for the adult population, as the biological data are primarily collected from fishery-dependent sources. However, different datasets could describe different segments of the growth curve [1]. The addition of auxiliary data for modelling individual growth is limited in literature. The main reasons are associated to the following issues: a) the limited availability of different sources of data (age-length data); b) length frequency distribution datasets are commonly available; however, their incorporation require a complex statistical treatment [2]; c) mark-recapture data are excellent for studying individual growth; however, these data are expensive, and field implementation depends on recapture success, which is often limited [3]; d) the use of several sources of data or combinations between them (e.g. length frequency distribution, mark-recapture data, and age) require complex statistical solutions based on the application of joint likelihood functions [4]. Thus, our rationale was to increase the sample size to complete the whole life cycle of Panopea globosa. The data collected come from two sources: rearing experimental conditions and the fishery, which are features that make the study unique. The paper published by Luquin-Covarrubias et al. [1] used the two sources of data for modeling individual growth for Panopea globosa using a joint likelihood function, which is the first published paper for the genus Panopea applying this mathematical approach. Data collection relied entirely on direct biological measurement (shell length via digital imaging/caliper) and hard-part age determination (internal growth-line counting from shell thin sections). No survey instrument, questionnaire, or structured interview was employed.

The age at length data are a critical input in stock assessment models providing the foundation for estimating the individual growth. This parameter is essential for quantifying the weight gain of each individual and, consequently, its contribution to overall population biomass. For this reason, the modeling of the growth pattern allows to identify whether the individuals have a monotonic growth with a constant growth rate, which diminishes when the individuals reach their maximum asymptotic length; or sigmoid growth, exhibiting inflexion points in early stages, which is indicating a phase of rapid growth that progressively diminishes when the individuals reach their maximum asymptotic length. A substantial body of literature documents diverse growth trajectories across marine taxa and highlights how each pattern distinctly influences biomass accumulation within a population.

Integrated stock assessment models such as Age Structured Assessment Program, Stock Synthesis Model, Woods Hole Assessmment Model have a specific submodel where individual growth can be estimated from joint likelihood functions. This framework allows the incorporation of different variables and data to the analysis, such as mark-recapture data, length frequency distribution data, and length at age data, which are simultaneously used for estimating the individual growth. The quantity and quality of the data used in the joint likelihood functions can increase the precision in the parameterization process, diminishing bias and increasing the theoretical description of the observed data. In this context, the early stages data are valuable as they can fix the theoretical model in the origin with parameter values according to the observed data. The lack of data in the origin of growth models is the main problem, as without data, parameters are highly biased and the theoretical growth model can be non-informative on the individual contribution in biomass to the population. Assuming a scenario where a joint likelihood function is used with several data sources, the outputs provided for the stock assessment models (i.e. spawning biomass, vulnerable biomass, total biomass) can be used for defining biological reference points to identify the status of the population, which allows to estimate harvest control rules for defining the annual harvest in the fishery (i.e. Acceptable Biological Catch), and implement harvest tactics (i.e. minimum size at which a fish can be legally harvested). Similar studies have been applied in aquaculture, identifying the best theoretical growth model, indicating the age (days) where the optimal growth in biomass has been reached. This is a frontier topic of academic research where new approaches, findings and innovative solutions are being published and used in marine biology.

The geoduck clam fishery in the Mexican Pacific is a high-value commercial resource; however, the data collection is limited. The best-sampled region in terms of biological and statistical data is Bahía Magdalena, located in the southwest coast of the Baja California Peninsula. In contrast, the fishing grounds located in the Gulf of California (Puerto Peñasco, Santa Clara, Guaymas, Sinaloa) are the least-sampled regions, and there is no dataset availability. Notably, with the data reported in our study, research from Bahía Magdalena has generated biological measurements spanning the full ontogenetic cycle of the species, from larval and juvenile stages through recruits to adults. This comprehensive coverage is particularly significant, as it represents the only dataset of its kind in the northwestern Mexican Pacific. Additionally, the measurements of early stages reported in this paper could be used and extrapolated in age and growth studies for the species, improving the parameter estimation in the candidate growth models analyzed for each region, where only measurements for the adult stages are available.

Data Description

The dataset provides information on the geoduck Panopea globosa from Bahía Magdalena, on the west coast of the Baja California Peninsula, Mexico. The dataset is available in CVS format and includes two spreadsheets. The first contains length (µm) and age (days) data for 797 individuals in the early stages; the second comprises length (mm) and age (years) data for 357 adult individuals. Each spreadsheet is structured in two ​​columns, which correspond to each biological variable. Fishery-independent data were obtained under rearing conditions; details of shell length and age structure from early stages of geoduck clam Panopea globosa provide valuable information on its growth and development (Fig. 1). Fishery-dependent data were collected from commercial catches and provide the shell length and age structure from adult individuals (Fig. 2). This information describes the life history traits of geoduck, and its use in mathematical models can contribute to stock assessment and fishery management.

Measurements of age and length for early stages in geoduck clam are not common as these must be collected under rearing experimental conditions. For this reason, the dataset are valuable, however, the mathematical approach is also a proof-of-concept for modeling individual growth using different datasets and building joint maximum likelihood functions [1]. The dataset can be used for modeling individual growth in stock assessment models, which commonly require an estimation of average weight. In this context, modeling the length‑at‑age dataset allows for the estimation of mean weight at each age, thereby improving the realism of biomass projections by explicitly accounting for variability in individual weight gain across the population. The dataset can be utilized in aquaculture for identifying the cessation of rapid growth, so that the species can be harvested at an optimal length, or for identifying the length at first maturity for the maintenance of spawners. Experimental Design, Materials, and Methods

Biological data collection

In April 2013, adult and mature individuals of Panopea globosa were obtained from a commercial fishery in Bahía Magdalena, with shell lengths ranging 122 to 182 mm. The samples collected according to simple random sampling. The procedure involved the following steps: a) technician personnel from the Mexican Agency for Fisheries Research (IMIPAS, https://www.gob.mx/imipas), who were onboard the fishing boats locally known as pangas (small boats of fiberglass reinforced plastic with outboard motor); b) the sampling sites were located in the authorized fishing beds where the geoduck clam is available; c) the geoduck clam was harvested by commercial fishers (hookah diving) using a transect of 25 m, sampling 1 m in both sides, such that approximately 25 m2 were sampled; d) once onboard, the organisms were counted and stored in plastic coolers with wet sponges; e) in land, the length (mm) and weight (g) of each individual was measured, including biological information (e.g. tissue, shell, gonads), and geographic position of each site sampled. The data are deposited in the scientific logbooks of IMIPAS.

Geoducks tolerate transportation under low water levels; therefore, the individuals were transported in plastic coolers with wet sponges at the bottom to reduce vibration-induced stress between individuals, and seawater was added to raise the water level sufficiently to cover them. To maintain the temperature at nearly 17 °C, ziplock bags with ice were placed at the top of the cooler, preventing freshwater runoff. The temperature was monitored throughout transportation from the field to the laboratory (approximately 3 h). Temperature was measured using a scientific laboratory thermometer (liquid-in-glass thermometer). During transportation from the field to laboratory, temperature ranged 16.5 °C to 18 °C. In the laboratory temperature ranged 16 °C to 17.4 °C. Before relocating the individuals, the geoduck clams were manually cleaned of epibionts. Fiberglass tanks of 400 L, containing UV-treated and filtered seawater at a salinity of 35 0/000 were used. The spawners were maintained at a density of 10 geoduck clams/0.4 m3 using 2 fiberglass tanks. The individuals were fed with ratio 1:1 of microalgae, mainly Isochrysis galbana and Chaetoceros sp. For two months, the system was maintained with daily seawater recirculation exchanges of 100%. This procedure avoided the mortality of geoduck clams and favored their growth and healthy condition. The gametes were obtained according to criteria proposed by Helm et al. [5] and Bustos and Olave [6], where the main criteria of quality were based in the sperm motility and oocyte sphericity. Later, in vitro fertilization was implemented using containers of 10 L with seawater (19 °C, salinity of 35 0/000, pH of 8.4). The ratio used for a successful fertilization was based in a mix of one million oocytes and 100 million sperm.

Subsequently, the larvae were cultured in densities of 1 ind/mL and fed with Isochrysis galbana (approximately between 30,000 and 45,000 cells/mL). For this early stage, different samples were selected during 21 days assuming simple random sampling, whereas from days 34 to 68, 72 individuals were measured weekly using the same sample design. The main variable was shell length, which was measured by taking digital images and processing them with Sigma Scan Pro, ver. 5.0 (Systat Software, Richmond, CA, USA). Comparatively, age (days) was a controllable variable as it was always known from the time at which the samples were taken. Juveniles were isolated individually and fed a 1:1 mixture of the microalgae Isochrysis galbana and Chaetoceros sp. at densities similar to those used for feeding the larvae stage. Shell length frequency and age data of 797 individuals in the early growth stages were collected. Specific details concerning embryonic and larval development of Panopea globosa can be seen in González-Peláez et al. [7], these authors documented the ontogenic development of early stages for the species, including morphometric relationships of the individuals.

From November 2006 to October 2007 fishery-dependent data were monthly collected in Bahía Magdalena, Mexico. The biological samples were provided by fishers in the study area, where the specimens were fished by hookah diving at depths of 11–17 m using small boats equipped with an outboard motor and a low-pressure compressor connected to a hose. For the ageing procedure, the shells were collected removing the internal tissues and muscle (mainly the siphon). The shell length (mm) of the geoduck clams was measured using a digital caliper (±0.1 mm). Throughout the study period, shell length-at-age data of 357 adult individuals aged 3–47 years were collected. The procedure for ageing is described in the following sections, denoting: a) sampling preparation, b) reading and accuracy verification, and c) age validation.

The datasets documented in this paper includes two sources of data, the first is length frequency distribution of early stages, which provides information to determine the parameters associated to the theoretical age at time zero, avoiding bias and ambiguity in the initial point of the growth curve. The early stages also made it possible to determine the slope of the growth curve, which typically represents the growth rate. In fact, reared individuals have been commonly used for estimating the individual growth in benthic mollusks (e.g. pearl oysters), improving the trajectories computed from theoretical growth models [8,9]. In contrast, the recruits (i.e. individuals reaching a specific size or age becoming part of the fishery and suitable for harvesting) along with older individuals harvested from the fishery, provided valuable information on the asymptotic growth of the species and the inflection point of the growth curve, indicating that the individual has reached its asymptotic length. Include different sources of data (juveniles and adults) provides information on the theoretical age at time zero and the asymptotic growth, avoiding bias initial point and the inflection point of the growth curve.

Sampling preparation

Literature indicates several methods for age reading in shells, the most common are: 1) opercula reading, 2) external growth ring counting, and 3) shell sectioning. In our paper, the ageing data were obtained by examining concentric growth bands deposited within the hard structure. This procedure allowed for the identification of structural growth marks that indicate growth, as the external growth rings can be affected by environmental stressors, which may cause damage in the growth marks or result in the formation of false growth marks that could be misinterpreted as true age indicators. Such scenarios must be avoided, as they influence the datasets causing serious biases in the readings. Therefore, shell sectioning was our method of choice. Based on this criterion, geoduck shells were used to determine the age according to Shaul and Goodwin [10], which has been widely used for ageing different species of genus Panopea. The procedure consists on counting the number of internal growth lines in the hinge plates, where each growth line exhibits an annual deposition, indicating an annual growth pattern. The sampling preparation was supported in three steps, briefly described as follows: a) the right valve of each individual was cut with a low-speed diamond saw (Isomet Buehler) at the center of the hinge plate region; b) the cut sections were polished with fine grain (1200-grit) sandpaper and etched with 2% hydrochloric acid solution (HCl) for 6–8 sec, rinsed with distilled water, and dried; and c) thin sections were covered with a small layer of graphite powder to highlight and increase the contrast of the bands and growth lines. More extensive details can be read in Shaul and Goodwin [10]. The cut sections of each valve were photographed and electronically coded using a Nikon SMZ25 stereomicroscope. A database on JPG format was generated to facilitate the visualization and identification of the growth lines to the readers. Each photograph was analyzed with NIS-Elements imaging software.

Reading and accuracy verification

Once the samples were ready for reading, the age of each individual was determined by counting the growth bands in each thin section using a dissecting microscope. Each section was analyzed by two experienced ageing readers. However, as discrepancies between readers are commonly reported, differences between readings require a statistical analysis to indicate the precision of the growth line counts. In this sense, literature indicates the estimation of the coefficient of variation (CV) for the datasets provided by the readers, as the CV computes a standardized precision within a comparative scale. Two results are clearly distinguishable under the scale: a) if CV ≤ 5%, then an acceptable precision is assumed, indicating that the discrepancies between the number of growth marks provided by the readers are negligible, hence, the age datasets are reliable for modeling in statistical growth models; and b) if CV > 5%, there is a low precision between the number of growth marks provided by the readers. There are different criteria to treat the samples when the CV > 5%, for example, the ageing must be repeated, or the shells with higher discrepancies and less reliability must be eliminated from the biological sample. The standard equation of CV and its variables are widely reported in the literature. In brief, the equation requires the age of the individual, and the number of times that each individual was aged. Details on the mathematical structure of the CV and its application can be read in [11,12].

The combined data of early and adult stages have improved the trajectory of the theoretical growth model for Panopea globosa. The parameter “t0” (the hypothetical age in years that the individual would have had at a length of zero) for the genus Panopea has been previously reported as overestimated. The literature shows t0 values computed with 3-year-old specimens and older (5 years old) and with variable shell length depending on the species (Panopea globosa, P. zelandica, P. abbreviata). The effect of this size variation with individuals older than 2 years for modeling individual growth negatively influences the estimation of t0. Luquin-Covarrubias et al. [1] discussed the overestimation of t0 values, which varied from 28 to 131 years for this parameter, these values are not biologically acceptable, and the fitted models did not describe the observed data exhibiting wide residuals. In contrast, the use of the early stages allows to improve the estimation of t0, with a value of 0.069 years. The accuracy in t0 parameter is important, such that the dataset documented in this paper could be used for improving the estimates of individual growth for geoduck clam populations in different regions. These data will enable to reach a better understanding of the expected length at age, the contribution in individual weight, the biomass of each individual to the population, and finally, the expected yield expressed through the harvest.

Age validation

Once that a reliable CV was estimated from biological samples, the age validation is the last step. Age validation defines the temporal periodicity of growth line formation and can be based on different protocols (e.g. observing a strong year-class through the fishery, tetracycline ink, measuring the timing of ring formation). Age validation must be applied to any hard structure used for ageing. The literature recognizes that the most common statistical method for validation is supported in the marginal increment ratio (MIR). For the datasets provided in our paper, the MIR was used. Conceptually, MIR defines the quantity of shell material deposited in the last increment during a period of time, which constitutes the relative proportion of the penultimate increment observed [13]. The temporal periodicity of growth line formation is not conditioned to be annual; it depends on the growth biology of the individual, consequently, there are populations with growth line formation of less than one year (e.g. six months). For Panopea globosa harvested in the study area, a wide range of age groups were sampled (3–47 years). Thus, the seasonal effect in type of shell edge was assumed to be clear and marked for all individuals. Accordingly, changes in the shell edge were informative of the period during which the material was deposited and indicated a clear growth increment [14]. Specific details on age validation can be seen in Campana [15]. For geoduck clam, the basic equation for measuring MIR computes a proportion defined between the distance between the hinge plate margin and the last growth line, and the penultimate increment, defined as the distance between the last band pair or thickness of the last complete band. The result is typically expressed as a percentage for an easier interpretation [16].

Statistical analysis

The dataset can be analyzed using different statistical computing software (e.g., R, Python, MATLAB). The statistical models for estimating theoretical age‑and‑growth curves are usually nonlinear, and their parameterization requires skills related to numerical methods, likelihood function optimization, and the interpretation of mathematical response surfaces.

Quality-control flags

The quality-control flags associated to the datasets were provided through coefficient of variation and marginal increment ratio, both described in the sections of reading and accuracy verification, and age validation. The Figs. 1 and 2 showing the length frequency distributions for early and adult stages are a description for visualizing the data, the interpretative value is limited for potential conclusions. For increasing the accuracy concerning the datasets reported in this paper the term “validated data” was used. The procedures used for age-reading reconciliation are not reported here as the procedure is widely described and reported in Luquin-Covarrubias et al. [1], the publication is open access, and it is available from CalCOFI webpage (https://calcofi.org).

Ethics Statement

Biological data were acquired from commercial landings, which were endorsed by fishing licenses that establish basic conditions for harvest activities. Hence, the biological samples were legally obtained, and the individuals were collected in the fishing ground clearly defined in the fishing licenses authorized for harvesting geoduck clam in Bahía Magdalena, Mexico. Scientific collection and the use of biological data were endorsed by the Mexican Federal Government (CONAPESCA) through these commercial fishery licenses. All samples were collected according to the scientific protocol established for Instituto Mexicano de Investigación en Pesca y Acuacultura Sustentables (Mexican Agency for Fisheries Science and Management). Therefore, these datasets comply with the ethical requirements for publication in Data in Brief. The authors corroborate that the current study does not involve human subjects, animal experiments, or any data collected from social media platforms.
