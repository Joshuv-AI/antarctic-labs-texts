Multi-omics analysis of arginine metabolism in ovarian cancer: A prognostic signature and GTF2F2-driven stromal remodeling
Authors: Dongyao Zhang, Jing Sheng, Yue Hu, Jinyang Wang, Yixuan Sun, Yang Zhou, Xiaolu Zhu, Yincheng Teng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•AMRS stratifies ovarian cancer prognosis and immune states.•RSF and SHAP identify GTF2F2 as a key arginine-metabolic driver.•High AMRS tumors are stromal activated and immune-cold.•GTF2F2 regulates arginine enzymes and cancer malignant phenotypes.•Targeting arginine metabolism reveals therapeutic vulnerabilities in ovarian cancer.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OV) is regarded as one of the most malignant gynecological malignancies and imposes a substantial global burden. In 2022, OV accounted for about 0.32 million prevalent cases and approximately 0.21 million deaths worldwide [1,2]. Although the integration of surgery, platinum-taxane chemotherapy, bevacizumab, and PARP inhibitors has improved results for some patients, enduring control is infrequent, and relapse rates continue to be high, resulting in an overall 5-year survival rate of between 30% and 50%. This underscores the imperative for biology-based categorization and the investigation of novel treatment approaches [[3], [4], [5]]. Moreover, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized treatment in various solid tumors, yet single-agent ICIs yield modest responses in OV, likely reflecting an immune-cold, stromal-rich tumor microenvironment (TME) [6,7].

Recently, amino acids have gained prominence as key regulators of cancer metabolism, shaping tumor development and progression through metabolic reprogramming, redox homeostasis, and epigenetic modulation. Amino acid metabolism has grown into a hallmark of cancer-immunity crosstalk [8,9]. Among these pathways, arginine metabolism is uniquely positioned at the interface of tumor proliferation, stromal remodeling, and immune suppression. Arginine fuels biomass production via the urea–polyamine–proline axis, regulates nitric oxide (NO) synthesis, and shapes T-cell function through the activities of arginases (ARG1 or ARG2) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (NOS2) in myeloid cells [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Tumor cells frequently rewire arginine metabolism by silencing argininosuccinate synthase-1 (ASS1), creating arginine auxotrophy that can be therapeutically exploited with arginine-depleting enzymes, such as pegargiminase (ADI-PEG20) [14]. Although ASS1 loss and arginine auxotrophy are well documented across cancers and have prompted early-phase trials, their prevalence, microenvironmental correlates, and therapeutic implications in OV remain incompletely defined [15,16].

Research in other cancers has shown that how arginine is processed can lead to immune-suppressing myeloid states, often through the production of polyamines [17,18]. However, OV-specific evidence remains sparse, and the links between arginine programs, stromal activation, and immune escape are undefined. Three gaps persist: (i) a single-cell atlas of arginine-associated programs spanning malignant, immune, and stromal compartments in OV; (ii) cohort-robust bulk molecular subtypes anchored in Arg metabolism that stratify prognosis and therapy response; and (iii) definition of upstream transcriptional regulators that coordinate key arginine-pathway enzymes (ASS1, ASL, ARG2, and NOS2) and remodel the TME.

Here, we integrate tumor-only single-cell RNA-seq (TISCH), bulk transcriptomes (TCGA and GEO), and spatial transcriptomics to systemically chart arginine-metabolism features in OV. We (i) delineate cell-type–specific arginine programs and intercellular communication; (ii) derive and externally validate arginine-based molecular subtypes and a machine-learning prognostic signature (AMRS); and (iii) nominate and minimally validate a transcriptional hub, GTF2F2, that links Arg metabolic control to stromal–immune remodeling. By coupling pathway-level scoring, cell–cell communication, and multi-dataset survival modeling, our study seeks to deliver actionable biomarkers and therapeutic hypotheses for OV, including patient selection for arginine-targeted strategies (e.g., ADI-PEG20, ARG inhibitors) and rational combinations with PARP inhibitors, anti-angiogenic agents, and immunotherapy. We have generated experimental validation for these findings; nevertheless, broader mechanistic studies and clinical confirmation are required before translation to practice. Collectively, our results provide a reference framework for investigating arginine metabolism in OV and for developing arginine-based clinical applications.

Materials and methods

Sources of data and curation

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) profiles included 67,364 cells and 27,311 genes from 10 OV samples (accession numbers OV_EMTAB8107 and OV_GSE154600) retrieved from the Tumor Immune Single-cell Hub 2 (TISCH2) (http://tisch.comp-genomics.org/). Public bulk RNA-sequencing expression matrices and clinical annotations for OV were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA-OV, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/projects/) and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) (accession numbers GGSE13876 and GSE140082). After harmonization and de-duplication, the merged clinical dataset comprised 1149 cases, of which 1148 had complete survival information and were included in prognostic analyses. The intersection of gene features shared across all bulk cohorts yielded 17,103 common genes. The spatial transcriptomics data (accession number VISDS000756) for OV in this study was obtained from the Comprehensive Repository of Spatial Transcriptomics database (CROST, https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/crost/home). Arginine-metabolism-related gene sets (AMRGs) were curated from GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) (http://www.gseamsigdb.org/), resulting in 77 candidates, 68 of which were detected across the bulk cohorts (Supplementary Tables S1–S2).

scRNA-seq processing

scRNA-seq raw matrices were processed in R using the “Seurat” package. Cells were filtered by unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts, gene counts, and mitochondrial content (default: nFeature_RNA 200–6000; nCount_RNA >500; percent.mt <15%). Doublets were removed with the R package “DoubletFinder.” Batches were aligned using the R package “harmony.” Normalization used the “NormalizeData” (log-normalize) function, variable features by the “FindVariableFeatures” (2000 genes) function, dimensionality by principal component analysis (PCA), and embedding by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Clusters were called with a shared nearest neighbor graph via “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” functions. Cell types were annotated with markers matching TISCH references and cross-validated with the "SingleR" package. Cluster markers were identified with FindAllMarkers (|log2FC|>0.25, min.pct>0.25, Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P < 0.05). Cell-type-specific markers were also computed with the R package “COSG.” The AMRGs were scored per cell using the Seurat function “AddModuleScore.” A sensitivity analysis was performed using the single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) from the "GSVA" package. Following on each sample's median score, the samples were classified into arginine-high and arginine-low categories. The "clusterProfiler" package was applied to enrich pathways at the cluster level for Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Reactome. p-values were Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted.

Gene enrichment analysis and pathway activity analysis

We implemented Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) with the R package “clusterProfiler” for functional enrichment in bulk transcriptome data, incorporating gene sets from MSigDB Hallmark, KEGG, GO, and conducting analyses with clusterProfiler/reactomePA. Visualization utilized the R packages “ggplot2,” “enrichplot” (including dotplot, gseaplot2, and cnetplot), and “ggcircos” for dot, balloon, and circos representations.

Analysis of cell-to-cell communication

We constructed communication networks with the R package “CellChat” on normalized single-cell data, using all the ligand-receptor pairs (secreted signaling, ECM-receptor, and cell-cell contact). For arginine-high vs. arginine-low groups (median split on the arginine score), we built per-group CellChat objects and inferred communication probabilities by the “computeCommunProb” and “computeCommunProbPathway” functions. We then quantified incoming/outgoing signaling strength and information flow per pathway and computed centrality metrics using the functions “netAnalysis_computeCentrality” and “netAnalysis_signalingRole” in the “CellChat” package. Differential LR pairs were extracted with permutation tests (default 1000). Visualizations included circle plots, centrality scatter, heatmaps of efferent/afferent patterns, and dot plots for significantly altered LR pairs.

Consensus clustering analysis

Raw/normalized matrices from TCGA and GEO were merged by common genes and batch-corrected with ComBat in the “sva” package, then univariate Cox regression (“survival”) on AMRGs to derive prognosis-associated genes (Cox P < 0.05). Unsupervised subtyping was then performed with the R package “ConsensusClusterPlus” (PAM, Pearson distance; reps = 1000; 80% item resampling). The optimal K was chosen by cumulative distribution function (CDF) and delta area. PCA visualized separation.

Analysis of the tumor immune microenvironment

Initially, we calculated enrichment scores with ssGSEA (“GSVA” package) to demonstrate the infiltration of 28 immune cell types in the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) to investigate the impact of different arginine-metabolic subtypes and risk groups. Further analysis of immune infiltration was estimated using the “IOBR” package wrappers for MCPcounter, EPIC, CIBERSORT, quanTIseq, TIMER, and ESTIMATE (ImmuneScore, StromalScore, ESTIMATEScore, and TumorPurity). Immunophenoscore (IPS) subscores—MHC, effector cells (EC), immune checkpoints/co-inhibitors (CP), immunomodulators/other (AZ), and suppressor cells (SC)—were also computed. Between-group comparisons used the Wilcoxon rank-sum with BH correction.

Arginine metabolism-related risk prognostic gene analysis

To identify genes related to arginine metabolism that influence prognosis, we performed differential analysis between arginine metabolism subtypes using the “limma” R package. We used |LogFC| > 0.5 and adjusted p 〈 0.05 to screen for differentially expressed genes pairwise. The identified differentially expressed genes were further analyzed using Cox regression analysis. Genes with a p-value < 0.05 and HR 〉 1 were considered “undesirable” genes, while those with a p-value < 0.05 and HR < 1 were considered “advantageous” genes. Finally, we identified the genes associated with risk prognosis.

Machine learning-based construction of arginine metabolism-related signature

We integrated ten survival-learning algorithms—random survival forest (RSF; “randomForestSRC”), LASSO and elastic net (“glmnet”), CoxBoost (“CoxBoost”), gradient boosting machine (“gbm”), supervised PCA (“superpc”), survival SVM (“survivalsvm”), ridge, stepwise Cox, and partial least squares for Cox (“plsRcox”)—and evaluated 115 model combinations by 5-fold cross-validation. The top model by average concordance index (C-index) across validation datasets was RSF; thus, an RSF-based AMRS was trained on TCGA and validated in GEO cohorts. The time-dependent area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUC) used the “timeROC” package. We separated the data into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the median risk score of the selected model.

Model interpretation and DepMap analysis

Model explanation used SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). SHAP values for RSF were approximated with the R package “fastshap,” generating feature importance ranks, dependence plots, and distribution ridgelines. DepMap (The Cancer Dependency Map) analysis uses data from CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) to perform large-scale loss-of-function screening to define gene essentiality, which can be used to study the impact of genes on cell survival and function.

Nomogram development and assessment

An AMRS's viability as an independent predictor was examined using univariate and multivariate Cox regression ("survival," "survminer") after adjusting for age, stage, grade, etc. A nomogram integrating AMRS and clinicopathologic parameters was developed using the R package “rms”, which contains calibration curves, C-index, and decision curve analysis (DCA) conducted with the “rmda” package to assess clinical value.

Somatic mutation and heterogeneity analysis

Mutation annotation format (MAF) files were analyzed with “maftools” to depict top mutated genes, oncoplots, and co-occurrence/mutual exclusivity (Fisher tests). Mutations per megabase (Mb) was used to compute the tumor mutation burden (TMB). Mutant-Allele Tumor Heterogeneity (MATH) scores were calculated with the function “inferHeterogeneity” in the R package “maftools.”

Prediction of immunotherapy and molecular therapy response

Potential immunotherapy response was deduced utilizing IPS and immune-checkpoint expression profiles, when applicable, and the TIDE online site (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/) was used to compute tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) scores. Drug sensitivity (half-maximal inhibitory concentration, IC50) was forecasted utilizing “oncoPredict” with training data from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC, https://www.cancerrxgene.org/). Differences in projected IC50 between groups were evaluated using Wilcoxon tests with Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Wilcoxon tests were used to compare high-risk and low-risk groups and are shown with violin plots.

Spatial data functional information estimation

Spot-level cell-type proportions were estimated by deconvolution with the R package “selma” using scRNA-derived references. We mapped spatial distributions of endothelial cells and fibroblasts, visualized GTF2F2 expression, and computed Spearman correlations between GTF2F2 expression and cell-type proportions across spots.

Cell culture and siRNA transfection

The human ovarian cell lines A2780 and HEY were sourced from the Shanghai Sixth People's Hospital, were validated through short tandem repeat profiling and cultured in Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM, GIBCO, #11,995,065) supplemented with 1% P/S (GIBCO, #15,070,063) and 10% fetal bovine serum (VivaCell, #C04001–500) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere.

Following the manufacturer's instructions, siRNAs (GenePharma) were transfected with Lipofectamine™ 2000 (Invitrogen, #11,668,019). The sequences for si-negative control (NC) and siGTF2F2 were the following: si-NC sense, 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′, and antisense, 5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′; si-GTF2F2–1 sense, 5′-GACGGAAAGCGAGCUCGAGCU-3′, and antisense, 5′-CUCGAGCUCGCUUUCCGUCUU-3′; si-GTF2F2–2 sense, 5′-GUGACAGUACUUUGAUUUCUC-3′, and antisense, 5′-GAAAUCAAAGUACUGUCACUU-3′.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR

OV cell lines were harvested for total RNA using the SteadyPure Quick RNA Extraction Kit (Accurate Biology, #AG21023). Reverse transcription was performed utilizing the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit (Accurate Biology, #AG11728). Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted using the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Accurate Biology, #AG11701) in accordance with the manufacturer's specified thermal cycling conditions. This document is a compilation of primer sequences employed in this research: GTF2F2 forward, 5′-CAATGGGCTAAAGCCTCTGGA-3′, and reverse 5′-GGATGTTCTCTAGGAGCACTGA-3′; RPLP0 (housekeeping) forward, 5′-GCGTCCTCGTGGAAGTGAC-3′, and reverse 5′-GTAGTTGGACTTCCAGGTCGC-3′. All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Relative mRNA levels can be calculated by the 2^−ΔΔCt method with RPLP0 as the internal control.

Western blot assay

Protease and phosphatase inhibitors were used to lyse OV cells in RIPA buffer (Cell Signaling Technology, #5872); nuclear extracts (for GTF2F2) were prepared with a standard NE-PER-type kit or hypotonic lysis + high-salt extraction. Protein was quantified by the BCA method (Thermo Fisher, #23,227), resolved on SDS-PAGE, and transferred to nitrocellulose (NC) membranes (AmershamTM, #10,600,001). Primary antibodies included GTF2F2 (1:1000, Abcam #ab128945), ASS1 (1:2000, Abcam #ab170952), ASL (1:2000, Abcam #ab201026), ARG2 (1:1000, Abcam #ab264066), and NOS2 (1:1000, Abcam #ab178945); β-Tubulin (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, #2148) and GAPDH (1:2000; Abcam, #ab8245) were employed as loading controls, and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Abways, #AB0102) and ECL (whole cell lysate) were used for detection. The band intensity was normalized with the corresponding control. Detailed experimental procedures were performed as previously described [19,20].

CCK8 assays for cell proliferation

Cell viability was assayed by the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (Abbkine, #BMU106). OV cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 3 × 103 cells/well, with six technical replicates. For arginine deprivation experiments, cells transfected with si-NC or si-GTF2F2 were cultured in complete medium or arginine-free medium (Gibco, #A14431) for indicated times. Then, CCK-8 reagent (10 μL/well) was added and cells incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Cell viability was measured by absorbance at 450 nm.

Colony formation assay

For colony formation analysis, eight hundred per well were seeded in 6-well plates and cultivated for 14 days. Colonies were immobilized (4% paraformaldehyde, 15 min), stained (0.1% crystal violet, 20 min), photographed, enumerated (≥50 cells/colony), and quantified by quantifying the clone area by image analysis.

Transwell assay

In the transwell assay, OV cells (3 × 10⁴) in medium without serum were introduced into the upper chamber (8-µm pore; Matrigel-free for migration, Corning). The lower chamber housed a medium comprising 10% FBS. Following a duration of 24–48 hours, migrating cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, subsequently stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min, and enumerated in five randomly chosen microscopic areas [21].

Apoptosis assay

Forty-eight hours after transfection, the cells were planted in a 12-well plate and grown in DMEM without FBS for 24 hours. They were then treated according to the instructions for Annexin V and PI (ShareBio, #SB-Y6026), avoiding light for 20 min. After treatment, the cells were detected by flow cytometry, analyzed with FlowJo software (version 10.6.2), and compared among si-NC, si-GTF2F2–1, and si-GTF2F2–2. For arginine-deprivation assays, si-NC- or si-GTF2F2-transfected cells were cultured in complete medium or arginine-free medium before CCK-8 or Annexin V/PI analysis

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

The promoter regions of ASS1 and ARG2 were cloned into the pGL4.10 firefly luciferase reporter vector to generate pGL4.10-ASS1 promoter-WT and pGL4.10-ARG2 promoter-WT for the assessment of the effect of GTF2F2 on ASS1 and ARG2 promoter activity (GenePharma). The pGL4.10 vector was used as a negative control.

A2780 and HEY cells were co-transfected with reporter plasmids and the Renilla luciferase plasmid pRL-TK with either si-NC or si-GTF2F2 using jetPRIME reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Polyplus-Transfection SA, #114–15). Luciferase activities were then measured using a dual-luciferase reporter gene assay kit (Abbkine, # KTA8010) after 36–48 hours. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity and relative promoter activity was calculated as the firefly/Renilla ratio. All experiments were done in triplicate and repeated independently three times.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.2) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). For continuous variables, Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for two-group comparisons, while one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test was applied for comparisons among multiple groups according to data distribution. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Overall survival was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank tests, and Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to identify prognostic factors. Associations between variables were evaluated by Spearman correlation analysis. All statistical tests were two-sided and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To investigate how arginine metabolism shapes the tumor microenvironment, we calculated an arginine metabolism score using the “AddModuleScore” function of Seurat based on 77 arginine-metabolism–related genes and visualized scores across cell types (Fig. 1C). Subsequently, the median score was used to classify cells into Arg-High and Arg-Low groups; the resulting distribution on the UMAP embedding is shown in Fig. 1D, and the number and proportion of each cell subtype within the two groups are summarized in Fig. 1E. Among all annotated populations, monocytes/macrophages, epithelial cells, and malignant cells exhibited the highest arginine metabolism scores, whereas CD8⁺ T cells showed the lowest (Fig. 1F). Consistently, the comparison of cell-type composition across the two groups demonstrated significant alterations in CD8⁺ T cells, monocytes/macrophages, and tumor cells; the Arg-Low group contained a higher fraction of CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 1G).

Arginine metabolism shapes intercellular communication and TME states

We analyzed the strength of intercellular interactions and the specific contributions of each cell at the incoming and outgoing ends (Fig. S2A and 2C). The results suggest that Tprolif exhibited higher incoming strength in the low-arginine metabolite group, followed by CD8+ T cells. Furthermore, a comparison of the total number and intensity of cell communication interactions in the two groups reported that although the low-arginine metabolite group showed a lower total number of interactions, it exhibited higher interaction intensity (Fig. S2E-F). To further analyze the intercellular communication mechanisms, we used a heatmap to illustrate the efferent (left) and afferent signaling patterns (right) of different cell types in different arginine metabolism groups. The results showed in the arginine-high group, malignant cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells acted as dominant senders, with strong outgoing signals across ECM-remodeling and pro-angiogenic pathways (e.g., COLLAGEN, LAMININ, THBS, FN, and VEGF) as well as immunosuppressive axes (e.g., MIF, SPP1, and TIGIT). Concordantly, these stromal and tumor compartments displayed elevated incoming strengths, indicating reinforced autocrine and paracrine loops. By contrast, the arginine-low group exhibited relatively enhanced incoming chemokine and co-stimulatory inputs to CD8⁺ T cells, consistent with its higher CD8⁺ T-cell fraction (Fig. S2B and 2D). Differential ligand–receptor analysis revealed that, relative to the arginine-high group, the arginine-low group displayed enhanced MHC-II–CD4 signaling across tumor and myeloid compartments, indicating more active CD4⁺ T-cell antigen presentation and helper priming. In contrast, multiple ECM-adhesion pathways and the immunosuppressive MIF-CD74 axis were down-regulated in the low-score group, consistent with pronounced matrix remodeling, angiogenic cues, and immune suppression in the arginine-high microenvironment. Similar patterns were seen for the interactions of PTN and MDK with SDC1 and NCL, further supporting stromal and vascular activation in the high-score group (Fig. S2G). Information flow analysis revealed a clear separation between the two arginine-score groups. The high-score group demonstrated significantly improved extracellular matrix remodeling, angiogenic signaling, cell-adhesion programs, and immunosuppressive pathways, such as MIF and TIGIT–NECTIN signaling, consistent with a matrix-remodeling and immune-cold microenvironment. Instead, the low-score group exhibited stronger antigen-presentation and immune-activating signals such as MHC-II, CD86 and BAFF, and CXCL and CCL chemokine pathways, which might indicate a more immune-inflamed state (Fig. S2H).

TIME of different arginine metabolism subtypes

Our single-cell analysis demonstrated that increased arginine metabolism is associated with TIME, prompting us to subsequently assess the bulk cohorts. PCA distinguished the three arginine-based subtypes (Fig. S4A). Using the “ssGSEA” function via the “GSVA” package, we quantified immune infiltration (Fig. S4B). Subtype A exhibited higher levels of various effector and antigen-presenting populations, which include activated, central, and effector-memory CD8⁺ T cells, activated CD4⁺ T cells, dendritic cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and T-helper subsets. In contrast, Subtype C was enriched for myeloid and suppressive compartments, such as macrophages, monocytes, neutrophils, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), while Subtype B exhibited intermediate characteristics. Most contrasts remained significant after multiple-testing correction (p < 0.001), supporting the classification of Subtype A as immune-inflamed and Subtype C as immune-cold or suppressive. We further visualized the distribution of Immunophenoscore (IPS) components—Stromal Cell (SC), Checkpoint (CP), Effector Cell (EC), MHC Class (MHC), and Activation Zone (AZ)—as well as total IPS scores across molecular subtypes using violin plots (Fig. S4C). Subtype A had the highest total IPS because it had stronger MHC, antigen presentation, and effector programs. Subtype C had the strongest suppressive signature (SC_IPS) and less antigen presentation. Subtype B had higher checkpoint and co-regulatory modules (CP_IPS, AZ_IPS) but the lowest total IPS, which means it could be activated without effective effector activity (p < 0.05). Consistently, checkpoint profiling revealed a checkpoint-high pattern in Subtype B (TIGIT, TIM-3, LAG3 with CD27, 4–1BB, OX40, ICOS, and CD40 co-stimulation), a suppressive bias in Subtype C (IDO1, VTCN1), and overall lower checkpoint expression in Subtype A (Fig. S4D). Finally, immune infiltration estimated by the “IOBR” R package corroborated these patterns, as summarized in heatmaps linking subtype identity to immune-cell landscapes (Fig. S4E).

Differential analysis and enrichment analysis of arginine metabolic subtypes

We compared the three arginine-metabolic subtypes, namely subtypes A, B, and C. Pairwise differential expression analysis identified 806 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using thresholds of |log₂FC| > 0.5 and adjusted p < 0.05 (Fig. 3A). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses showed that these DEGs were mainly involved in autophagy, lysosomal function, endocytosis, vesicle trafficking, endoplasmic reticulum processing, ubiquitin–proteasome regulation, RNA processing, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, AMPK signaling, mitophagy, cell-cycle regulation, spindle and kinetochore organization, glycosylation, and extracellular matrix remodeling. These enriched biological processes suggest that the arginine-high state is associated with nutrient recycling, secretory trafficking, mitochondrial metabolism, and stromal–vascular remodeling, thereby contributing to an immune-suppressive tumor microenvironment. Constructing a consensus prognostic AMRS through integrated machine learning

Based on the 93 genes identified by Cox regression, we benchmarked 10 machine-learning algorithms and 115 model combinations to derive an arginine-metabolism–related signature (AMRS). The training set was the TCGA-OV cohort, and three independent cohorts were used for validation. Fig. 4A shows that the top three models by average Harrell’s C-index all used the Random Survival Forest (RSF) algorithm, which also obtained the best C-index in the test sets. We therefore selected RSF to construct the final prognostic model. The resulting AMRS comprises 93 genes, with GTF2F2, PFDN6, LUZP1, DIABLO, and NUAK1 ranking highest in feature importance (Fig. S6). The median score of AMRS was employed to classify individuals into high-risk and low-risk categories. The Kaplan-Meier analysis indicated that the survival rate in the high-risk group (AMRS-high) was significantly lower than that in the low-risk group (AMRS-low) within the training cohort, and the validation cohort corroborated these findings. (Figs. 4B and 4E-G). Discriminative performance was excellent: time-specific ROC AUCs for 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival were 0.908, 0.985, and 0.991, respectively (Fig. 4C). Evaluate the AMRS's prognostic value

We utilized data from the TCGA-OV cohort to assess the viability of the AMRS risk score as an autonomous prognostic indicator for OV through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses (Fig. S7). Univariate analysis revealed that the risk score served as an essential prognostic predictor for OV (HR > 1, p < 0.05). Multivariate analysis additionally validated the risk score's independence as a prognostic factor (HR > 1, p < 0.05). To improve the clinical applicability of this model, we developed a nomogram that incorporates the risk score alongside clinical parameters (Fig. 5A). The nomogram results demonstrated consistent and strong predictive efficacy, especially in predicting 1- to 10-year overall survival (OS), with a significantly higher concordance index compared to clinical indicators such as age, stage, and grade (Fig. 5B). It surpassed other clinical features in accuracy, achieving AUC values of 0.908, 0.973, and 0.982 for one-year, three-year, and five-year survival forecasts, respectively (Fig. 5C). Strong correlation between expected and observed values was demonstrated by the calibration curve analysis (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, the model produced a better net clinical benefit than other clinical measures, according to decision curve analysis (DCA). (Fig. 5E), hence reinforcing its predictive accuracy. These findings confirm the nomogram, which includes risk scores and clinical features, as a dependable instrument for individualized prognostic forecasting in OV patients. Analysis the biological functional of the AMRS

To interrogate the biological implications of the AMRS, we first correlated the AMRS score to genome-wide gene expression and displayed the top 50 positively and negatively associated genes (Fig. S8A-B). Correlation-ranked gene lists were then used for Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) with the clusterProfiler program. KEGG-GSEA showed positive enrichment of extracellular matrix remodeling, cell adhesion, cytoskeletal regulation, endocytosis, autophagy, mitophagy, TGF-β signaling, Hedgehog signaling, cellular senescence, and miRNA- or Polycomb-associated regulatory processes. These pathways are compatible with a matrix-remodeling, motility-enhanced, and stress-adaptive phenotype, notably in AMRS-high tumors and suggest an immune-suppressive tumor microenvironment. Moreover, immune trafficking pathways including leukocyte transendothelial migration and Fcγ receptor-mediated phagocytosis were enriched although at lower ranks, suggesting altered immune cell trafficking rather than effective antitumor activation. In line with this, GO-GSEA identified considerable enrichment of extracellular matrix and adhesion related terms including cell–substrate junction architecture, collagen containing extracellular matrix, collagen trimer production and extracellular matrix structural constituents. In contrast, words associated to mitochondrial energy metabolism such as proton motive force-driven ATP generation and respiratory chain complex activity were negatively enriched, indicating a switch from oxidative phosphorylation to stress-adaptive metabolic states. Olfactory receptor-related words were similarly reduced (Fig. S8C-E). Reactome-GSEA provided similar results (Fig. S8F). Overall, these results suggest that the AMRS-high phenotype is linked to stromal activation, enhanced invasive potential, metabolic adaptability, and an immune-cold microenvironment.

Mutation landscape and tumor heterogeneity in AMRS

Tumor stemness was quantified using ssGSEA-derived enrichment scores. AMRS-high tumors exhibited higher enrichment of multiple stemness-associated programs, including embryonic stem cell-, mesenchymal stem cell-, neural stem cell-, and hematopoietic stem cell-related signatures, as well as stemness gene sets derived from KEGG and GeneCards (Fig. S9A). In contrast, AMRS-high tumors showed lower tumor mutational burden (TMB), and the AMRS risk score was inversely correlated with the mRNA expression-based stemness index (mRNAsi) (Fig. S9B-E). Mutation profiling further showed that the low-risk cohort contained a slightly higher proportion of mutated samples and generally higher TMB than the high-risk cohort (90.6% versus 87.0%). TP53 and TTN were the predominant mutated genes in both groups, whereas group-specific differences were mainly observed in long-tail genes associated with adhesion and cytoskeletal organization (Fig. S9F-G). These findings suggest that the unfavorable biology of AMRS-high tumors is less likely to be driven by neoantigen burden, but instead may be associated with extracellular matrix remodeling, angiogenesis, and immune-suppressive networks. Co-mutation analysis showed predominant gene co-occurrence with limited mutual exclusivity. In the low risk group, LRP2-MUC17 and LRP2-USH2A were the pairs of co-occurrence, while in the high risk group, MACF1-MUC16 and RYR2-LRP2 were the pairs of co-occurrence, which was in line with the polygenic and cooperative evolutionary pattern (Fig. S10A-B). No significant difference in MATH scores was observed between the two risk groups (Fig. S10C-D).

The correlation between AMRS and the TIME and prediction of immunotherapy and molecular therapy response

To further investigate the difference of immune microenvironment between AMRS risk groups, we assessed the tumor-infiltrating immune cells (TIICs) and immune-related functional states. The ssGSEA analysis revealed that AMRS-low tumors were marked by the infiltration of effector and antigen-presenting immune populations including activated, central memory, and effector memory CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, natural killer cells, and dendritic cells (Fig. 6A). In contrast, AMRS-high tumors showed relatively reduced effector immune infiltration. Consistently, multiple deconvolution algorithms implemented in IOBR revealed that AMRS-high tumors were enriched in endothelial cells, fibroblasts, cancer-associated fibroblasts, and macrophages, accompanied by increased SC_IPS and AZ_IPS scores and decreased MHC_IPS scores, suggesting a stromal- and myeloid-dominant immune-cold phenotype (Fig. S11). ESTIMATE analysis further demonstrated that AMRS-high tumors had higher stromal scores and lower immune scores, whereas no significant differences were observed in ESTIMATE scores or tumor purity between the two risk groups (Fig. 6B). Thus, the prognostic differences captured by AMRS are mainly attributed to different compositions of the tumor microenvironment rather than differences in tumor purity. We then evaluated the possible association of AMRS risk stratification with immunotherapy response. TIDE prediction showed that AMRS-high tumors had higher TIDE scores than AMRS-low tumors, suggesting a higher possibility of immune evasion and a lower potential benefit from immune checkpoint blockade (Fig. 6C-D). Consistently, 70% of patients in the AMRS-high group were predicted to be non-responders to immunotherapy, compared with 54% in the AMRS-low group (Fig. 6E). These results suggest that AMRS-low patients may be more likely to benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors, whereas AMRS-high tumors may require additional strategies to overcome stromal- and myeloid-associated immune suppression.

We further examined the expression patterns of immune checkpoint and co-stimulatory genes between the two AMRS risk groups. A total of 22 immune regulatory genes were differentially expressed. Overall, AMRS-low tumors exhibited higher expression of multiple inhibitory checkpoint molecules, including CTLA4, HAVCR2 (TIM-3), LAG3, TIGIT, PDCD1LG2 (PD-L2), and IDO1, together with comparable or increased expression of co-stimulatory molecules such as CD27, ICOS, CD40LG, CD80, and CD86. Alternative immune checkpoints, including LAIR1 and HHLA2, showed similar trends. These results show that AMRS-low tumors are immune-inflamed with high checkpoint and co-stimulation, suggesting active immune engagement, whereas AMRS-high tumors display a globally lower checkpoint and costimulatory profile, consistent with an immune-cold and suppressed microenvironmental setting (Fig. S12A).

Drug sensitivity analysis

Chemotherapy and targeted therapy remain essential components of OV management. To identify potential therapeutic vulnerabilities associated with AMRS stratification, we estimated sample-level half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values for 198 compounds from the GDSC database using the “oncoPredict” package. A total of 73 compounds showed significant differences in predicted IC₅₀ values between the AMRS-high and AMRS-low groups (p < 0.05; Fig. S12B, top 50 compounds ranked by sensitivity). The AMRS-high group showed lower predicted IC50 values of IGF1R_3801_1738, staurosporine_1034, taselisib_1561, and docetaxel_1819, suggesting sensitivity to inhibition of IGF-1R and PI3K pathway and microtubule-targeting chemotherapy. These results are consistent with the proliferative, adhesive, and stromal-activated programs observed in AMRS-high tumors (Fig. 6F–I). In contrast, AMRS-low tumors exhibited lower predicted IC₅₀ values for dihydrorotenone_1827, a mitochondrial complex I inhibitor; PF-4,708,671_1129, a p70-S6K inhibitor; TAF1_5496_1732, a transcriptional and epigenetic regulator; and sepantronium bromide_1941, also known as YM155, a survivin inhibitor (Fig. 6J–M). These observations imply that AMRS-low tumors may possess therapeutically exploitable vulnerabilities in mitochondrial metabolism, mTOR–S6K signaling, survivin-mediated apoptosis regulation and TAF1-dependent transcription and further propose that AMRS could be useful in stratifying different therapeutic modalities with AMRS-high tumors predicted to be sensitive to IGF-1R and PI3K-directed agents and taxanes while AMRS-low tumors may be more susceptible to complex I inhibition, S6K inhibition and survivin- or TAF1-targeted approaches, potentially in combination with immunotherapy.

Transcriptional hub GTF2F2 connects the AMRS to pro-malignant programs

A SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) is a mechanism for clarifying predictions given by machine learning algorithms. According to the Shapley value theory in game theory, it seeks to assign a “importance” score to each feature, representing the relevance of that feature's impact on the model's predictions. We performed SHAP analysis on the RSF model. A SHAP bee swarm plot summarizes the contribution of the top 10 features (genes) to the AMRS risk score. Along the x-axis, higher SHAP values indicate stronger positive contributions to risk, whereas negative values denote protective effects (Fig. 7A). Among them, GTF2F2 displays the largest right-shift and dense high-value tails, highlighting GTF2F2 as a top global driver in the model. Furthermore, SHAP dependence demonstrates a sigmoid, monotonic increase: low levels of GTF2F2 yield negative SHAP values (indicating protective effects), and a sharp risk inflection occurs near the median. A high-expression plateau indicates that the additional impact is decreasing, highlighting that GTF2F2-high tumors are significant contributors to the AMRS (Fig. 7B). To investigate the impact of these key AMRS genes on cell survival and function, we performed DepMap (The Cancer Dependency Map) analysis based on gene essentiality data defined by large-scale loss-of-function screening using CRISPRi. The ridge plot shows a consistent right-shift of GTF2F2, indicating a stable, positive marginal effect on risk, whereas PFDN6 and SBDS are left-shifted (protective) and display stronger essentiality in DepMap, raising toxicity concerns if directly targeted (Fig. 7C). Since arginine-metabolism enzymes were downregulated upon GTF2F2 depletion, we next tested if GTF2F2 modulates the transcriptional activity of key arginine-metabolism genes. Dual-luciferase reporter assays showed that GTF2F2 silencing significantly attenuated the promoter activities of ASS1 and ARG2 in both A2780 and HEY cells (p < 0.01), whereas the promoterless pGL4.10 empty vector remained at background activity and was not significantly affected by GTF2F2 knockdown (Fig. 8E-H). Together, these results suggest that GTF2F2 regulates the transcriptional activation of ASS1 and ARG2, but whether GTF2F2 directly binds to the promoters of these genes remains to be confirmed.

Finally, spatial transcriptomic deconvolution was performed to determine whether GTF2F2 expression was associated with specific microenvironmental niches in OV tissues. Deconvolution of the spatial section resolved major malignant, immune, and stromal compartments (Fig. 8I-J). GTF2F2 expression formed spatial hotspots that were preferentially located in fibroblast- and endothelial-enriched regions (Fig. 8K). Quantitative correlation analysis further confirmed a significant positive correlation between GTF2F2 expression and fibroblast proportion and a weaker but statistically significant correlation with endothelial cell abundance (Fig. 8L-M). Together, these results indicate that GTF2F2 may connect arginine-metabolism regulation with stromal and vascular remodeling, thereby contributing to a pro-survival and immune-suppressive OV microenvironment.

Discussion

In this study, we integrated multi-layered datasets, including single-cell, bulk, and spatial transcriptomic profiles together with clinical cohorts, to delineate an arginine-metabolism program that stratifies OV into biologically and clinically distinct states. The arginine-high state was characterized by stromal activation, including cancer-associated fibroblast and endothelial enrichment, extracellular matrix remodeling, cell-adhesion signaling, pro-angiogenic and immunosuppressive intercellular crosstalk, increased transcriptional stemness, lower tumor mutational burden, and poorer survival. In contrast, the arginine-low state was associated with T- and NK-cell–inflamed features and more favorable clinical outcomes. We further developed and externally validated a random survival forest-based AMRS comprising 93 genes, which achieved robust and time-stable prognostic performance across independent cohorts. SHAP analysis identified GTF2F2 as a major positive contributor to AMRS-defined risk. At the tissue level, GTF2F2 expression co-localized with fibroblast- and endothelial-enriched spatial niches, while functional validation showed that GTF2F2 knockdown reduced ASS1, ASL, ARG2, and NOS2 protein abundance, promoted apoptosis, and suppressed proliferation, clonogenic growth, and migration of OV cells.

Arginine deprivation further reduced the OV cell survival and increased the apoptosis consistent with these results. The effects were further enhanced by the reduction of GTF2F2. Results demonstrate that the loss of GTF2F2 increases the susceptibility of OV cells to arginine deprivation stress. Moreover, dual-luciferase reporter assays indicated that knockdown of GTF2F2 dramatically decreased the promoter activity of ASS1 and ARG2, giving functional evidence for the participation of GTF2F2 in the transcriptional regulation of critical arginine metabolism genes. Drug response modeling revealed treatment vulnerabilities between AMRS subgroups AMRS-high tumors were predicted to be more vulnerable to inhibition of IGF-1R and PI3K pathways and taxane-based therapies, while AMRS-low tumors were more vulnerable to inhibition of mitochondrial complex I, p70-S6K, survivin, or TAF1-dependent transcriptional mechanisms. Together, these data support a working model in which GTF2F2-driven transcriptional control of arginine metabolism sustains stromal-vascular remodeling and immune suppression, thereby promoting disease progression and treatment resistance.

Arginine, a semi-essential amino acid, is a critical metabolic node in cancer biology [22]. Arginine activates many, occasionally contradictory pathways: ARG2 directs arginine towards ornithine or polyamines (promoting proliferation and M2-like myeloid polarization), whereas NOS2 facilitates NO synthesis with context-dependent impacts on vascular and immune responses [23,24]. Across cancers, arginine scarcity and arginase activity blunt T-cell proliferation and effector function, while polyamine metabolism programs myeloid suppressors and tumor tolerance [[25], [26], [27]]. In our research, the arginine-high state was co-enriched with cancer-associated fibroblast and endothelial signatures, extracellular matrix remodeling, cell adhesion, and TGF-β and Hedgehog signaling, suggesting the formation of a matrix-remodeling, pro-angiogenic, and stress-adaptive niche. Consistently, single-cell CellChat analysis revealed enhanced outgoing and incoming communication through collagen, laminin, and growth factor-related signaling axes, particularly between stromal compartments and immune or malignant cells in arginine-high tumors. Bulk transcriptomic enrichment analysis further showed that arginine-associated subtype differences were mainly related to autophagy, lysosomal function, endocytosis, vesicle trafficking, endoplasmic reticulum processing, ubiquitin–proteasome regulation, RNA processing, cell-cycle control, spindle and kinetochore organization, glycosylation, and extracellular matrix remodeling. These biological programs are consistent with immune exclusion and myeloid-skewed microenvironmental remodeling. Notably, despite their worse clinical outcomes, arginine-high tumors exhibited lower tumor mutational burden, supporting a model in which metabolic fitness, proteostasis, autophagy, and polyamine-supported genome maintenance may reduce neoantigenic pressure, while stromal networks sustain tumor progression through a neoantigen-independent mechanism [[28], [29], [30]].

To investigate the influence of arginine metabolism on VO growth, we used unsupervised consensus clustering based on arginine metabolism levels, categorizing the disease into subtypes A, B, and C. Scoring the AMRS revealed that subtypes B and C exhibited high levels of arginine metabolism, and survival prognosis analysis indicated that subtype BC had a poorer outcome compared to subtype A (p < 0.001). Wang et al. [31] developed a scoring system related to arginine–proline metabolism in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), revealing that increased pathway activity correlates with diminished overall survival. Clinical immunohistochemistry (IHC) data in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) indicated that elevated ARG2 expression stratifies patients into progressively worse survival outcomes (median OS 32 months for ARG2⁻, 18 months for ARG2⁺, and 15 months for ARG2⁺⁺; p < 0.01), suggesting that enhanced arginine catabolism is associated with a negative prognosis [32]. We identified 93 differentially expressed genes across three categories with significant prognostic implications. Utilizing these genes, we evaluated 10 machine learning techniques to develop an AMRS. Following the assessment of the AMRS using the optimal model, RSF, we categorized patients into high- and low-risk categories depending on the median risk score. Clinical Kaplan-Meier survival analysis indicated a poorer outcome for high-risk patients. Both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses demonstrated that the AMRS risk score is independent. A nomogram was developed utilizing clinical markers associated with grade, stage, and age. The outstanding concordance index and DCA results demonstrated that this nomogram provided the greatest net therapeutic benefit. The results from the mulberry chart show that people in the high-risk category, as determined by the AMGS score, are likely to have higher levels of arginine metabolism. Methodologically, RSF effectively handles censored data, captures nonlinear effects and interactions among multiple genes and clinical variables, and demonstrates significant resilience to multicollinearity, making it highly beneficial for AMRS based on multigene signals. Recent assessments indicate that Survival Forest excels in diverse survival analysis contexts and high-dimensional data, validating our choice of RSF as the "optimal" learner for AMRS [33,34]. AMRS can facilitate clinical risk stratification and individualized treatment; for instance, it can prioritize arginine-targeted therapy or improve follow-up for high-risk patients. Additionally, the score may be employed to pre-screen individuals most susceptible to relapse or those likely to benefit from arginine-modulated combination therapy [35].

Further analysis of tumor mutations and heterogeneity revealed that AMRS-high tumors were enriched for multiple lineage-associated stemness programs, including embryonic stem cell-, mesenchymal stem cell-, neural stem cell-, and hematopoietic stem cell-related signatures. This pattern was consistent with epithelial–mesenchymal transition, extracellular matrix remodeling, immune exclusion, and a stroma-rich, pro-angiogenic progression phenotype. Importantly, AMRS-high tumors had lower tumor mutational burden, and were inversely correlated with mRNA expression-based stemness index (mRNAsi), suggesting that disease progression in this subgroup may have more to do with stromal activation, angiogenesis and immunosuppressive signaling than with neoantigen load. This finding is biologically plausible, because ESC-like stemness may be reduced in stroma-rich tumors, while invasion- and mesenchymal-like stemness programs may be more active. Such a distinction may explain the inverse association of AMRS risk with mRNAsi in our study [36]. Moreover, aggressive and immune-resistant tumors may arise through chromosomal instability, copy-number alterations, and epigenetic remodeling rather than accumulation of point mutations, potentially explaining the elevated risk of AMRS-high tumors despite their lower TMB [37]. Additionally, polyamine or arginine metabolism facilitates proteostasis, redox regulation, and chromatin modulation that promote stress adaptation and invasion without necessitating high mutational loads [38]. The cooperative co-mutation landscape of adhesion and cytoskeletal “long-tail” genes corresponds with a polygenic, collaborative evolutionary model instead of a single-pathway dominance, which is consistent with immune-cold, stroma-led environments [39,40].

OV typically presents a stroma-dense, myeloid-dominant, T-cell–excluded TME, a pattern repeatedly shown by recent single-cell and spatial atlases and reviews [41]. Clinically, this biology explains the limited efficacy of PD-1 and PD-L1 monotherapy and the negative results of phase III combination trials such as IMagyn050 and JAVELIN Ovarian 200, highlighting the challenge of turning OV into a disease that responds to immune checkpoint inhibitors [42,43]. In line with an immune-inflamed phenotype, AMRS-low tumors in our study showed increased infiltration of effector and antigen-presenting immune cells, and immune checkpoint and co-stimulatory molecule expression. In contrast, tumors with high AMRS showed enrichment of endothelial cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts, and macrophages and had higher stromal but lower immune scores, consistent with a stromal- and myeloid-dominant immune-cold state. In line with this, TIDE and IPS analyses showed that the AMRS-high group had reduced sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade. Inferences on drug responses also indicated the presence of specific therapeutic vulnerabilities, with AMRS-high tumors predicted to be sensitive to inhibition of the IGF-1R and PI3K pathways and to taxanes, while AMRS-low tumors were more sensitive to blockade of mitochondrial complex I, S6K inhibition and survivin or TAF1-directed strategies. These patterns are coherent with recent evidence that CAF and TGF-β programs orchestrate immune exclusion in OC and that arginine/arginase circuitry in CAF–myeloid niches suppress T-cell fitness and reinforces immune coldness [44]. In this line of research, arginine-axis modulation (e.g. arginine deprivation, arginase inhibition) may represent a metabolic sensitization approach that can be combined with anti-angiogenic, anti-fibrotic, TGF-β-targeted, FAK-targeted or immune checkpoint-based approaches in rationally designed trials. In contrast, AMRS-low tumors with high checkpoint and co-stimulatory signaling might be more responsive to immune checkpoint inhibitor-based regimens. Methodologically, the multi-tool immune deconvolution and drug-response inference we used are well-validated frameworks for translational biomarker work in immuno-oncology. Prospectively, incorporating AMRS risk, TIIC composition, and TIDE into the trial design could refine patient selection and illuminate how arginine metabolism rewires the OC immune microenvironment to shape immunotherapy responses.

SHAP attributes a model’s prediction to each feature via game-theoretic Shapley values, yielding directional, magnitude-aware importance at both global and per-sample levels. In oncology ML, recent studies show that SHAP improves interpretability, helps detect non-linear cut-points, and can influence clinical decision behavior—addressing the “black-box” barrier that often limits model adoption [46,47]. In our RSF, a SHAP beeswarm and dependence analysis highlighted sigmoid, monotonic effects and sample-level heterogeneity, which are difficult to capture with linear coefficients alone. These properties make SHAP a suitable tool to prioritize actionable features (genes) for bench validation and trial design. Across our 93 prognosis-related genes, RSF-SHAP ranked GTF2F2 as a top global driver with a right-shifted SHAP distribution and an inflection near the median expression, flagging a biologically plausible risk threshold. GTF2F2 encodes the small subunit of TFIIF, a core Pol II factor controlling initiation, elongation and promoter escape. Recent structural studies reveal a central role for TFIIF in controlling Pol II interaction with DNA and pausing transitions, suggesting a link between GTF2F2 and the efficiency of transcription in stress-adapted cells [48]. A recent single-cell investigation on OV corroborates our model findings, indicating that elevated GTF2F2 levels are linked to stemness and invasiveness, whereas its knockdown inhibits migration and invasion, aligning with a pro-malignant function [49]. In A2780 and HEY cells, siGTF2F2 reduced proliferation, clonogenicity, and migration, while increasing early and late apoptosis, confirming on-target dependency. Mechanistically, we observed coordinated reductions of ASS1, ASL, ARG2, and NOS2 at the protein level after GTF2F2 silencing, indicating down-regulation of the arginine-metabolism axis, a pathway that supports polyamine and NO biosynthesis, redox homeostasis, and stress tolerance. This aligns with contemporary evidence that amino acid metabolism, including arginine handling, underwrites tumor fitness and immune escape and is a viable therapeutic handle in solid tumors [50].

To examine the translational relevance of the GTF2F2-arginine axis, we also tested whether GTF2F2 depletion affects the cellular response to arginine deprivation. Arginine-free culture inhibited OV cell viability and induced apoptosis, and these effects were further increased by GTF2F2 knockdown in both A2780 and HEY cells. These data imply that decreased GTF2F2 expression may influence OV cell metabolic plasticity under arginine restriction conditions and to increase their vulnerability to arginine deprivation stress. This observation is especially pertinent in the context of arginine auxotrophy, where disrupted arginine biosynthesis, often linked to decreased ASS1 activity, compels cancer cells to rely on exogenous arginine. Therefore, therapeutic arginine depletion, such as ADI-PEG20-mediated arginine deprivation, has been proposed as a rational strategy for tumors with defective arginine recycling [51]. Our results extend this concept by proposing that GTF2F2 may influence arginine deprivation sensitivity by regulating arginine metabolism enzymes such as ASS1, ASL, ARG2, and NOS2. Thus, AMRS-high or GTF2F2-high tumors may constitute a biologically relevant subgroup where arginine-axis targeting can be investigated either alone or in combination with anti-stromal, anti-angiogenic or immune checkpoint-based strategies.

Mechanistically, we also investigated whether GTF2F2 has an impact on the transcriptional regulation of key arginine-metabolism genes. Dual-luciferase reporter assays showed that knockdown of GTF2F2 significantly decreased the promoter activities of ASS1 and ARG2 in both OV cell lines, while the promoterless pGL4.10 empty vector was at background levels and was not significantly affected by GTF2F2 depletion. These results provide functional evidence at the promoter level that GTF2F2 contributes to transcriptional activation of ASS1 and ARG2. Although GTF2F2 is a general transcription factor and not a classical sequence-specific transcription factor, the reduction in promoter activity seen with the reduction in arginine-metabolism enzymes after knockdown of GTF2F2 supports a regulatory link between GTF2F2 and the arginine-metabolism program. However, these data do not support direct physical binding of GTF2F2 to the promoters of ASS1 or ARG2. Future studies using ChIP-qPCR will be required to determine if GTF2F2 is directly recruited to these promoter regions and to identify potential co-factors mediating its transcriptional effects.

Spatial deconvolution revealed GTF2F2 hotspots co-localizing with fibroblast- and endothelial-rich regions; spot-level GTF2F2 correlated with CAF and endothelial fractions, suggesting cross-talk between transcriptional throughput and stromal activation. Recent ovarian-cancer work shows that CAF arginase activity (DDR2-regulated) drives collagen production and omental colonization, positioning the arginine enzymatic node at the heart of stroma-mediated progression [45]. Based on these findings, we propose a working model where GTF2F2 promotes an arginine-metabolism enzyme program that drives stromal activation, angiogenic remodeling and creation of an immune-cold tumor microenvironment. Conversely, GTF2F2 depletion may lead to reduced arginine metabolism activity, reduced stroma support and increased apoptosis in tumor cells. This model offers two possible directions for translation. Targeting the arginine axis, either by depleting arginine or modulating arginase-dependent metabolism, may serve as a microenvironmental sensitization strategy in AMRS-high tumors [52]. With the stromal-rich and immune-suppressive phenotype in this subgroup, arginine-directed approaches may be of particular value in combination with anti-angiogenic, anti-fibrotic or immune checkpoint-based strategies. Second, AMRS-low tumors, which are more immune inflamed, may be more appropriate for immune checkpoint inhibitor–centered regimens. Our drug-response analysis also indicates that metabolic and signaling vulnerabilities, including mitochondrial complex I, p70-S6K, survivin and TAF1-dependent transcriptional programs, may provide further combination opportunities in this subgroup. Future studies should prospectively stratify patients by AMRS and GTF2F2 expression, incorporate spatially resolved microenvironmental readouts and evaluate clinical utility using decision curve analysis and treatment-response endpoints.

There are some limitations to the present study. First, it is retrospective, based on public cohorts. Small sample sizes, residual batch effects and in silico drug‐response inference may bias estimates, requiring multicenter prospective validation and wet‐lab confirmation. Second, GTF2F2 knockdown reduced ASS1, ASL, ARG2 and NOS2, and compromised tumor phenotypes in vitro. In vivo efficacy and biomarker-guided clinical testing are necessary to demonstrate translational utility.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we propose that GTF2F2-enabled transcriptional licensing of arginine metabolism underlies a stromal-activated, immune-suppressed state of OV that escapes neoantigen dependence yet portends poor outcome. The AMRS translates this biology into a robust risk tool and actionable roadmap for metabolism-aligned therapy. By integrating multi-omics with perturbation evidence, our study provides a mechanistic and translational framework to exploit the arginine–stroma–immunity axis in OV.
