Cardiovascular responses during blood flow-restricted resistance exercise: a systematic review with meta-analysis
Authors: Yashveer Singh, Leslie A Avila, Marty Spranger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextBlood flow-restricted resistance exercise (BFR-RE) is used to elicit hypertrophy and strength adaptations with low external loads, making it attractive for clinical and rehabilitative populations. However, concerns regarding exaggerated cardiovascular responses highlight the need to clarify haemodynamic effects before broader clinical adoption.ObjectivesTo compare acute cardiovascular responses—systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and heart rate (HR)—during low-intensity BFR-RE (LI-BFR), low-intensity resistance exercise (LI-RE) and high-intensity resistance exercise (HI-RE).Data sourcesMEDLINE, EMBASE, ResearchGate, Web of Science and Cochrane Library were searched through February 2025. Randomised controlled trials comparing SBP, DBP and HR during LI-BFR versus LI-RE and/or HI-RE in healthy adults were included.Data synthesisFour trials for LI-BFR versus LI-RE (n=86) and three for LI-BFR versus HI-RE (n=46) were identified, with six reports eligible for random-effects meta-analyses adjusted for muscle and age. SBP (MD=14.06 mm Hg; 95% CI (0.24 to 27.90) and DBP (MD=11.18 mm Hg; 95% CI (2.8 to 19.57)) were significantly increased during LI-BFR versus LI-RE, while HR was not significant (MD=6.00 bpm; 95% CI (–5.47 to 17.5)). Data on real-time cardiovascular responses during BFR-RE are limited; therefore, future studies should be designed to measure haemodynamic parameters during exercise accurately.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42025633694Keywords: Cardiovascular, Exercise physiology, Exercise rehabilitation

Introduction

Blood flow restriction training (BFRT), initially developed as Kaatsu training,1 has consistently been shown to increase muscle hypertrophy and strength in healthy adults under controlled experimental conditions.2,7 BFRT consists of blood flow restriction during resistance exercise across a wide range of external loads, most commonly 20%–40% one-repetition maximum (1RM), using a pressure device, such as an inflated pneumatic cuff, proximally on the desired upper and/or lower limb.8 The imposed ischaemia during blood flow-restricted resistance exercise (BFR-RE) induces metabolic stress, characterised by the accumulation of metabolites (eg, proton, lactate, ATP) which promote muscle growth.9

BFR-RE appears to offer a rehabilitative alternative for individuals with musculoskeletal disorders not able to engage in high-intensity, free-flow resistance exercise (HI-RE).10,13 Moreover, BFR-RE may be beneficial in cardiac rehabilitation as well.14 15 However, concerns regarding the safety of BFR-RE across populations, especially those with existing cardiovascular disease, point to potential increased risks of adverse cardiovascular events brought about by activation of the exercise pressor reflex (EPR) secondary to BFR.16,18

The cardiovascular responses to exercise are regulated by feed-forward control through central command and autonomic reflexes such as the arterial baroreflex and the EPR (muscle metaboreflex and muscle mechanoreflex).19,21 Germane to BFRT, the muscle metaboreflex is elicited due to muscle metabolites that accumulate in the muscle interstitium during exercise.19,21 When engaged during exercise, the EPR reflexively increases sympathetic nerve activity which, in turn, augments cardiovascular responses (eg, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and heart rate (HR)).19,21

As the restriction of blood flow during BFR-RE results in metabolite accumulation, the muscle metaboreflex would be engaged during BFRT leading to augmented cardiovascular responses during exercise.16 17 What is more, autonomic reflex function is significantly altered in cardiovascular disease states.16 17 20 In particular, muscle metaboreflex responses are exaggerated in hypertension and heart failure.16 17 20 22 This is potentially concerning for individuals with hypertension and heart failure who perform BFR-RE.16,18 Indeed, some studies demonstrate significant increases in blood pressure and HR compared with moderate and high-intensity resistance exercise in hypertensive subjects.23 24

The majority of the select BFR-RE reports that have measured haemodynamic parameters only assessed cardiovascular responses pre-exercise and postexercise, or during rest periods between exercise sets (with the cuff deflated).25 On the cessation of exercise, there is a rapid reduction in sympathetic nerve activity with a concomitant increase in parasympathetic nerve activity, which collectively results in an abrupt decline in cardiovascular responses generated during exercise.26 Thus, cardiovascular responses measured during rest and recovery from BFR exercise are not representative of the cardiovascular responses during BFR exercise.

Previous meta-analyses attempting to unify the cardiovascular responses to BFR-RE are largely composed of reports that measured cardiovascular responses during rest and the recovery from BFR.27,30 Moreover, even in some reports where haemodynamic data were measured during BFR exercise, the meta-analyses still select and analyse data during rest and recovery from exercise modalities.27,29 Considering that the muscle metaboreflex is not actively elicited during rest nor during recovery from exercise, it is especially important to determine the cardiovascular responses that occur during BFR-RE. Therefore, the purpose of the present systematic review with meta-analysis is to compare and quantify the real-time cardiovascular responses (SBP, DBP and HR) during low-intensity resistance exercise with blood flow restriction (LI-BFR) to low-intensity, free-flow resistance exercise (LI-RE) and HI-RE.

Methods

This systematic review with meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the guidelines indicated in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses. Additionally, the review and protocol were registered through PROSPERO under the registration ID CRD42025633694 (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42025633694), with no amendments to the registered protocol.

Eligibility criteria

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) involving BFR-RE and measuring cardiovascular outcomes were included throughout the review. More specifically, inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) participants were healthy adults, including elderly populations, without previous disease; (2) the study measured differences during the same exercise between LI-BFR versus LI-RE and/or HI-RE; (3) cardiovascular outcomes measured contained at least SBP, DBP and HR; (4) the protocol included outcome measurements during exercise modalities (a. The BFR cuff must have been inflated during the entire measurement period in LI-BFR groups; b. the measurement period must have been during actual exercise, rather than pre-exercise and/or postexercise sessions or during rests between sets). Grey literature, including dissertations, trial registries and conference abstracts, was also considered. Exclusion criteria barred reports with participants with histories of cardiovascular, respiratory and/or musculoskeletal diseases, and inconsistencies or insufficient data. Reports were grouped based on their included modalities of LI-BFR versus LI-RE and/or HI-RE.

Information sources

Reports were independently screened and selected by at least two reviewers (YS and LAA) from the electronic databases MEDLINE, EMBASE, ResearchGate, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library searched through February 2025. Additionally, reference lists of relevant reviews from the search process were utilised to find relevant reports.

Search strategy

Five electronic databases (MEDLINE, EMBASE, ResearchGate, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library) were systematically searched ranging from the oldest to most recent records (February 2025). Disagreements on inclusion were handled through deliberation or calling on a third reviewer (MS). The primary search strategy utilised relevant keywords entered into each database, specifically: ((blood flow restriction training) OR (Kaatsu) OR (occlusion training) OR (blood flow restriction) OR (ischemic training)) AND ((hemodynamic) OR (cardiovascular)) AND (healthy adults). A secondary strategy of ‘snowballing’ using reference lists of relevant reports and reviews was also utilised.31 No automated approaches were used for screening records, and duplicate reports were removed using EndNote Web.

Selection process

Reports were independently screened and selected by two reviewers (YS and LAA). Eligibility assessment during both title-abstract and full-text screening was conducted by two reviewers (YS and LAA), and disagreements on inclusion were handled through deliberation or calling on a third reviewer (MS). We did not contact authors of primary studies to clarify eligibility.

Data collection process and data items

Two independent reviewers extracted appropriate data from selected reports (YS and LAA). Similarly to study selection, disagreements on data were handled through deliberation or calling on a third reviewer (MS). Participant and methodological characteristics were extracted from included reports from figures, including participant age, sex and health status as well as BFR protocol, exercise modalities, outcomes measured, moments of haemodynamic analysis and outcome results. Data on each outcome were extracted from figures, including all mean values, SD, SEs of the mean (when applicable) and sample sizes. If only individual participant values were given, the mean and SD of each subgroup were quantified by two independent reviewers (YS and LAA). For inclusions lacking exact values of outcome data within figures, the corresponding authors of each study were contacted via email; reports containing these values were noted as such.

Study risk of bias and reporting bias assessment

We assessed risk of bias using the RoB V.2.0 tool and made single study-level judgments for each report because (1) the included outcomes were measured and reported using the same methods and time points across each report and (2) our review focuses only on acute haemodynamic measures taken during exercise. Two authors (YS and LAA) assessed the risk of bias and reporting bias for the following domains: (1) bias arising from the randomisation process, (2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions, (3) bias due to missing outcome data, (4) bias in measurement of the outcome and (5) bias in selection of the reported result.32 Reports were classified according to the RoB2 guidance.32 Reports were deemed low risk of bias if all domains were rated as ‘low risk’ for the assessed outcome. Reports were judged to have some concerns if at least one domain was rated as ‘some concerns’ but no domains were rated as ‘high risk’. Reports were considered at high risk of bias if at least one domain was rated ‘high risk’, or if multiple domains were rated as ‘some concerns’ in a way that substantially lowers confidence in the result. Two reviewers (YS and LAA) performed assessments and resolved disagreements by consensus. Cochrane Revman was utilised in producing the traffic light risk-of-bias plots for included reports. Reporting bias could not be formally assessed using funnel plots or statistical tests given the limited number of included reports per outcome.

Effect measures and synthesis methods

Selected reports were summarised based on participant, outcome, BFR, exercise and measurement characteristics. Effect sizes were calculated as mean differences with corresponding sampling variances using the escalc function from the metafor package in RStudio (V.4.4.0). Two reports reported SEM and were converted to SD using the formula: SD=SEM*√n for each study.33 34 Meta-analyses were conducted using multilevel random-effects models to account for within-study variance and clustering of multiple outcomes within reports. All included reports employed crossover designs. However, paired outcome data (eg, SD of within-participant differences or correlation coefficients) were not consistently reported. Therefore, crossover trials were conservatively analysed as parallel-group comparisons. When trials contributed multiple comparisons sharing a common comparator (eg, Brandner et al), the comparator group sample size was evenly divided across comparisons to avoid unit-of-analysis errors, while group means and SD were retained. An unadjusted model was employed that estimated the pooled mean difference in haemodynamic outcomes between LI-BFR and LI-RE (≤40% 1RM), or HI-RE (≥60% 1RM), across reports. The adjusted model incorporated study-level moderators for muscle group (upper vs lower limb) and age group (young (average of <60 years) versus elderly (average of ≥60 years)) to explore potential sources of heterogeneity only for LI-BFR and LI-RE due to a lack of robust covariate data. As a multivariate meta-analytic framework was employed, the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman adjustment,35 which is designed for univariate random-effects analyses, was not utilised as it did not align with this modelling approach. Random-effects multilevel models were used to account for within-study clustering. Forest plots were generated for both unadjusted and adjusted models, with pooled estimates displayed alongside p values. Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using Tau2 and assessed with the Q-test for residual heterogeneity. Tests of moderators were conducted via the QM (Q-model) statistic. All analyses were performed in RStudio (V.4.4.0) using the metafor package.

Certainty assessment

Assessment of the certainty of evidence across outcomes between LI-BFR versus LI-RE and LI-BFR versus HI-RE was conducted using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE).36 GRADE considers five domains that may lower the certainty of evidence: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. Evidence can be rated with high, moderate, low or very low certainty, with RCTs starting at high certainty and downgraded depending on concerns within these domains. Two authors (YS and LAA) independently evaluated the certainty of evidence for each outcome, with consensus reached through deliberation.

Results

Study selection

The initial search strategy (figure 1) across databases yielded 2376 records, reduced to 1920 records after removing duplicates. Records were screened by titles, abstracts and then full text in accordance with the selection criteria. To avoid using multiple reports from the same study, we cross-checked all included records for multiple publications arising from the same trial. Several reports initially appeared to meet criteria, but methodological issues such as measurement period or unhealthy participants barred inclusion37,40 (see online supplemental table 1). Seven reports were eligible for inclusion and were selected for systematic review, with six reports providing sufficient data for meta-analysis, providing a total sample of 96 participants. Four authors41,44 were contacted for specific data values, and three responded with results,41 42 44 where Vieira et al provided arterial blood pressure values, but not HR.

Risk of bias and reporting bias in studies

Risk of bias was illustrated in a traffic plot, indicating some concerns of bias across all reports, primarily due to the lack of blinding of participants and assessors (see online supplemental table 2). Furthermore, only one study33 exhibited an additional risk of bias due to missing outcome data. Reporting bias was not formally assessed, but the concentration of reports from a small number of research groups may increase the risk of publication and outcome reporting bias.

Certainty of evidence

Certainty of evidence across cardiovascular outcomes was assessed for each comparison, where the results were illustrated in a GRADE summary of findings table (see online supplemental table 3). When comparing LI-BFR to LI-RE, only the domain of indirectness to clinical importance was deemed serious, due to the use of healthy participants in controlled, laboratory settings that may not reflect realistic environments of cardiovascular risk. Comparisons of LI-BFR versus HI-RE presented similar indirectness but were additionally imprecise due to the large CI and smaller sample size. LI-BFR versus LI-RE presented moderate certainty of evidence, while LI-BFR versus HI-RE presented low certainty of evidence.

Results of individual studies and syntheses

Low-intensity resistance exercise with and without BFR

The effects on SBP

In the unadjusted model, we observed that LI-BFR significantly increased SBP compared with LI-RE across seven eligible comparisons from four reports, including 86 participants (MD=16.67 mm Hg; 95% CI (10.07 to 23.27); p<0.0001) (see figure 2a). Between-study heterogeneity was moderate but not statistically significant (Q(6)=9.3, p=0.16). In the adjusted model, which included muscle group and age group as moderators, the overall effect of LI-BFR compared with LI-RE remained significant (MD=14.06 mmHg; 95% CI (0.24 to 27.90); p=0.0462) (see figure 3a). Tests of moderators indicated that neither muscle group (upper vs lower body) nor age group (young vs elderly) explained significant heterogeneity across reports (QM(2)=0.72, p=0.70). Residual heterogeneity remained non-significant (QE(4)=6.2, p=0.19).

The effects on DBP

The unadjusted model described that LI-BFR significantly increased DBP compared with LI-RE across seven eligible comparisons from four reports, including 86 participants (MD=8.03 mm Hg; 95% CI (3.87 to 12.19); p=0.0002) (see figure 2b). Between-study heterogeneity was low and not statistically significant (Q(6)=3.9, p=0.69). In the adjusted model, which included muscle group and age group as moderators, the overall effect of LI-BFR compared with LI-RE remained significant (MD=11.18 mm Hg; 95% CI (2.8 to 19.57); p=0.0090) (see figure 3b). Neither muscle group nor age group moderated the effect (QM(2)=0.80, p=0.67), and residual heterogeneity remained non-significant (QE(4)=3.07, p=0.55).

The effects on HR

In the unadjusted model, we observed that LI-BFR significantly increased HR compared with LI-RE across five eligible comparisons from three reports, including 58 participants (MD=9.17 bpm; 95% CI (3.56 to 14.77); p=0.001) (see figure 2c). Between-study heterogeneity was low and not statistically significant (Q(4)=1.9, p=0.75). In the adjusted model, which included muscle group and age group as moderators, the effect of LI-BFR compared with LI-RE was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (MD=6.00 bpm; 95% CI (–5.47 to 17.5]) p=0.305) (see figure 3c). Neither muscle group nor age group moderated the effect (QM(2)=0.43, p=0.81), and residual heterogeneity remained non-significant (QE(2)=1.47, p=0.48).

High-intensity resistance exercise and low-intensity resistance exercise with BFR

The effects on SBP

The unadjusted model featured four eligible comparisons from three reports including 46 participants and presented that LI-BFR did not significantly differ from HI-RE in the effects of SBP (MD=0.53 mm Hg; 95% CI (–15.21 to 16.28); p=0.95). Between-study heterogeneity was significant (Q(3)=10.40, p=0.02) (see figure 4a).

The effects on DBP

Similarly, in the unadjusted model based on four comparisons across three reports including 46 participants, we observed that HI-RE and LI-BFR did not significantly differ in effects on DBP (MD=2.54 mm Hg; 95% CI (–5.59 to 10.67); p=0.54). Between-study heterogeneity was low and non-significant (Q(3)=3.85, p=0.28) (see figure 4b).

The effects on HR

Finally, across the same four comparisons and participants, LI-BFR and HI-RE did not significantly differ across HR (MD=–5.90 bpm; 95% CI (–12.55 to 0.74); p=0.0817). Between-study heterogeneity was negligible (Q(3)=2.6, p=0.45) (see figure 4c).

Discussion

The aim of the present systematic review with meta-analysis was to analyse and quantify the real-time cardiovascular responses during LI-BFR in comparison to LI-RE and/or HI-RE in healthy individuals. Our findings show that the cardiovascular responses during LI-BFR are exaggerated when compared with LI-RE across multiple protocols while controlling for participant age and exercising muscle group. Conversely, the cardiovascular responses during LI-BFR were not any different from those during HI-RE.

When blood flow is reduced to exercising muscle, metabolic byproducts of exercise (eg, proton, lactate, ATP) accumulate in the muscle interstitium and stimulate group IV afferents of the muscle.19 In a feedforward response, the muscle metaboreflex detects metabolic stress and triggers an increase in sympathetic nerve activity to the heart and vasculature.19 This results in marked increases in cardiovascular parameters, including cardiac output (CO), HR and arterial blood pressure, thereby increasing perfusion pressure to help sustain blood flow to the active muscle.19

The reported cardiovascular responses to BFR-RE are limited and equivocal. The majority of studies assessing the cardiovascular responses to BFR-RE fail to measure the haemodynamic changes during BFR-RE, capturing only pre-exercise and postexercise data.25 Moreover, the cardiovascular responses reported vary widely due to protocol differences (eg, exercise intensity, arm vs leg, AOP, intermittent vs continuous occlusion).8 25 While the present review only includes reports that measured cardiovascular responses during exercise, there nonetheless remain inconsistencies of haemodynamic outcomes across reports due to variances in exercise modality and BFR methodology.

Arterial blood pressure

The present analysis shows that SBP and DBP responses are significantly higher during LI-BFR when compared with LI-RE. The premise of BFRT is to artificially restrict blood flow to exercising muscle under low-intensity conditions (ie, LI-BFR), thereby creating a metabolic environment similar to that of high-intensity exercise in the absence of BFR (ie, HI-RE).8 Indeed, multiple studies of LI-BFR report metabolic environments similar to that of HI-RE.46,48 The goal of BFRT is to accumulate lactate and other muscle metabolites, creating metabolic stress that stimulates anabolic signalling pathways promoting muscle hypertrophy and enhanced tissue healing.9 49 50 However, several of these muscle metabolites are also known to activate group IV muscle afferents,17 19 and therefore the exaggerated increases in SBP and DBP observed during LI-BFR are likely due to elicitation of the muscle metaboreflex. Very recent studies of BFRT in dynamically exercising canines51 and humans undergoing BFR-RE52 reported metaboreflex-induced increases in cardiovascular responses, although reported increases were small in humans. The latter study was not included in the present review as the augmented cardiovascular responses were determined during postexercise circulatory occlusion (PECO) rather than during exercise per se. With that said, PECO is a valid protocol used to sustain muscle metaboreflex activation immediately following exercise and thus closely captures the cardiovascular responses during exercise.53,55

Importantly, this elevation was not moderated by the active muscle group or age group, as neither factor reached statistical significance. These findings suggest that the exercise pressor response to LI-BFR is robust across both upper and lower limb exercise and independent of participant age. However, previous literature indicates that smaller muscle groups may evoke greater arterial blood pressure responses than larger muscle groups due to an increase in total peripheral resistance (TPR), resulting from compression of the muscle vasculature due to elevated intramuscular pressures56,60 and a potential reduction in functional sympatholysis caused by diminished metabolic hyperaemia.60,63 Both factors enhance activation of the muscle metaboreflex, leading to increased sympathetic outflow and further elevations in arterial blood pressure.56 64

The functional benefits of LI-BFR are similar to those realised from HI-RE. In line with this, SBP and DBP responses during LI-BFR are not different from during HI-RE, with one exception. Hori et al reported that SBP and DBP were higher during LI-BFR than during HI-RE. However, these higher pressures were likely due to an inordinately high AOP applied during BFR-RE,8 which likely led to greater metabolite accumulation, enhancing muscle metaboreflex activation and sympathetic outflow.65

As activation of the muscle metaboreflex increases sympathetic outflow, subsequent increases in CO and/or TPR would be responsible for the increased SBP and DBP responses observed during BFR-RE. Four reports34 41 43 45 measured CO and TPR during BFR-RE and reported that these parameters were not different between LI-BFR and LI-RE. In two of these reports,41 43 CO was elevated during HI-RE when compared with LI-BFR and LI-RE, while TPR was elevated during HI-RE when compared with LI-RE in one study,43 but decreased when compared with LI-BFR and LI-RE in the other.41 A study performing BFR-RE until failure found significant elevations in CO and TPR in LI-RE compared with LI-BFR and HI-RE only at the start of exercise.66 While other BFR-RE studies have reported similarly inconclusive CO and TPR values, the measurements were not taken during exercise.67 Variances in exercise methodology likely contributed to these conflicting findings regarding CO and TPR in the present study, though proper analysis suffers from a small sample size as well.

Heart rate

Similarly to arterial blood pressure, HR responses during LI-BFR were significantly increased in comparison to LI-RE. In contrast, HR responses during LI-BFR were on average lower than during HI-RE, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. These HR responses may reflect the reduced systemic oxygen demand of low-load exercise, relative to heavy resistance training, such that LI-BFR increases vascular resistance and arterial pressure without eliciting the same chronotropic drive. While increases in HR and/or stroke volume (SV) can drive flow-mediated increases in CO, and therefore arterial blood pressure, the present data on CO and SV are again limited and equivocal. One included study found that SV decreased and HR increased during LI-BFR compared with LI-RE,45 likely responsible for a nonsignificant difference in CO between the two settings. Conversely, other studies found no difference in SV between LI-RE and LI-BFR.34 41 43 Therefore, the exact mechanism responsible for the exaggerated increase in arterial blood pressure during BFR-RE remains unclear. Further experimentation is required to elucidate the distinction between, and driving forces for, the arterial blood pressure responses during BFR-RE, LI-RE and HI-RE.

Advancing the understanding of cardiovascular responses to BFR-RE

Recent meta-analyses have attempted to unify the acute cardiovascular responses to BFR-RE and have concluded that LI-BFR does not have a significant impact on the cardiovascular system when compared with LI-RE27 29 30 and/or HI-RE.27,29 Importantly, however, these meta-analyses, and the majority of reports they analysed, did not assess the cardiovascular responses during BFR-RE, but rather pre-exercise and post-exercise. To understand the true cardiovascular implications of BFR-RE, it is imperative that the cardiovascular responses are measured in real-time.25 The present systematic review and meta-analysis is unique in that it (1) deliberately includes reports that only measured cardiovascular responses during BFR-RE, (2) controls for some extent of heterogeneity through meta-regression models incorporating age group and active muscle group as moderators and (3) contains reports with a generally good quality of evidence.

Age and safety considerations

Although similar cardiovascular responses to LI-BFR were observed between elderly and younger adults,34 44 it is important to recognise that baseline haemodynamics ultimately determine the peak response achieved. As older populations often present with hypertension,68 BFR protocols should be prescribed with caution, as further research is required to elucidate the acute responses during BFR-RE. Furthermore, given the results of the present review, consideration of previous cardiovascular history is recommended when considering BFR-RE to avoid complications associated with an exaggerated sympathoexcitatory response.16 17 25 69

Limitations

Our review process has its limitations. First, the exclusion of grey literature may have increased the risk of publication bias. Second, moderator analyses were limited by insufficient reporting of study-level covariates across reports, restricting exploration to only age and muscle group. Finally, while the risk of bias was assessed with RoB V.2.0, a formal analysis of reporting bias was unable to be performed due to the low number of included reports. Limitations of the evidence surround the low participant count, reflecting the limited number of studies that have measured cardiovascular responses during BFR-RE. Moreover, our participant pool only contained one female participant, preventing moderator analysis based on sex. While the cardiovascular responses to traditional exercise do not significantly differ between sexes,70 such analyses have yet to be done during BFR exercise. Additionally, the included evidence featured some concerns of risk of bias according to the RoB V.2.0 tool and ranged from a moderate to low certainty of evidence through GRADE. Finally, the variability in the included study protocols introduced substantial heterogeneity, making it challenging to draw unified conclusions about the physiological effects of resistance exercise with and without BFR. Therefore, future research should consider measuring the physiological responses to BFR-RE with diverse methodological approaches to better understand how different exercise and BFR prescriptions affect a broader population with and without BFR.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis determined that, despite methodological differences and participant age variations, arterial blood pressure is significantly elevated during LI-BFR when compared with LI-RE. Conversely, the haemodynamic responses during LI-BFR were not any different from HI-RE, suggesting that both BFR-RE and HI-RE create a similar metabolic and mechanical environment. The various components of BFR-RE methodology must be considered, working alongside clinical recommendations and patient histories of not only musculoskeletal disorders but also cardiovascular history, given the findings of this review. Further experimentation to elucidate the real-time cardiovascular responses during BFR-RE in healthy subjects and subjects with cardiovascular disease is crucial to assess its safety in the clinic and beyond.
