Transthyretin Cardiac Amyloidosis in an Older Athlete
Authors: Daniel O'Brien, Robert A Reiss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiac amyloidosis is a clinical condition characterized by deposition of amyloid fibrils in the myocardium, leading to increased left ventricular wall thickness and ultimately heart failure. We present a case of a former athlete who was found to have severe left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) on echocardiogram and was ultimately diagnosed with wild-type transthyretin (ATTR) cardiac amyloidosis, for which he was started on tafamidis. The recognition and diagnosis of cardiac amyloidosis are important given advances in the ability to diagnose and treat this condition, ideally before a patient develops heart failure symptoms. This report also emphasizes the differences between athletic cardiac remodeling of the heart and pathologic changes suggestive of cardiac disease.

Introduction

Amyloidosis is a disease characterized by abnormal deposition of amyloid fibrils within various organs. These fibrils consist of abnormally folded proteins that deposit within the extracellular matrix of cells. The accumulation of these fibrils can subsequently lead to problems with the structure and function of the affected organ. Cardiac amyloidosis occurs when the amyloid fibrils deposit in the myocardium, which leads to increased wall thickness, stiffening of the left ventricle, and clinical heart failure. The recognition of this condition, especially transthyretin (ATTR) amyloidosis, has increased significantly in recent years due to both increased awareness of the condition and improved cardiac imaging [1]. ATTR cardiac amyloidosis accounts for 6-16% of older patients presenting with left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) and heart failure [1]. This condition leads to significant cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, with a median survival of three to five years without treatment [2]. We present a case of a highly functional patient who was found to have severe LVH on imaging and ultimately diagnosed with ATTR cardiac amyloidosis. We review the different forms of cardiac amyloidosis, diagnostic testing, and treatment options for this condition. We also discuss the differences between pathologic left ventricular changes and normal structural changes seen in athletes.

Case presentation

A 75-year-old male presented to establish care and complete his annual physical exam. He was not taking any medications and did not report any symptoms. He did state that he had a history of both smoking and alcohol use, but had not used either for approximately 12 years. The patient was very active, swimming 4000 yards three to four days per week, with a long history of swimming both recreationally and competitively in the past. He denied any cough, chest pain, or shortness of breath. He was up to date on all of his routine vaccines. Vital signs were notable for hypertension, with a blood pressure of 154/79 mmHg. Heart rate was 66 bpm, and he was afebrile, with a normal respiratory rate and a normal BMI. The physical exam was notable for normal jugular venous pressure, clear lungs bilaterally on auscultation, no peripheral edema, and normal pulses bilaterally. Cardiac exam was notable for regular rate and rhythm, along with a 2/6 systolic ejection murmur heard across the precordium and faintly in the neck.

Lab testing was notable for a normal red blood cell count, a low platelet count of 103,000/µL (reference range 143,000-398,000/µL), and a normal chemistry panel except for a low sodium of 133 mmol/L (135-146 mmol/L) and chloride of 95 mmol/L (96-106 mmol/L). Thyroid function testing was normal. Additional lab testing was notable for an IgM monoclonal gammopathy as well as elevated serum free light chains.

Because of the patient's systolic hypertension and heart murmur, an electrocardiogram (EKG) and echocardiogram were obtained. The EKG showed normal sinus rhythm with inferior T wave inversions and a heart rate of 60 beats per minute (Figure 1). Transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) showed severe concentric LVH with hyperdynamic left ventricular systolic function (Figure 2). The ejection fraction was estimated at 70-75%. Strain imaging showed reduced global longitudinal strain with relative apical sparing. There was grade II diastolic dysfunction. The right ventricle was normal in size and had normal systolic function. The aortic valve was calcified without aortic stenosis; the calculated aortic valve area was 2.13 cm². Pulmonary artery systolic pressure was normal.

Discussion

Cardiac amyloidosis is characterized by the deposition of amyloid fibrils in the myocardium, which leads to increased wall thickness, stiffening of the left ventricle, and reduced contractility. The recognition of cardiac amyloidosis can be difficult since, in the early stages of disease, clinical manifestations are often nonspecific. However, early diagnosis is paramount to allow prompt initiation of therapy aimed at slowing disease progression [1,2]. Cardiac amyloidosis should be suspected in patients with unexplained LVH on TTE, especially if they have associated cardiac symptoms, including heart failure, syncope, or angina. Through this case, we aim to educate providers about the often subtle findings seen in cardiac amyloidosis and increase the likelihood that this diagnosis is considered when evaluating patients in the early stages of this condition.

There are various forms of amyloid that can deposit in the myocardium. The most common forms to deposit in the heart are light chain amyloid (referred to as AL amyloid) and ATTR amyloid. AL amyloid is of particular clinical importance because this condition portends significantly higher morbidity and mortality compared with ATTR amyloidosis, with a median survival of approximately six months after diagnosis [2]. In AL amyloid, the fibrils consist of fragments of monoclonal light chains. This can occur in the setting of another plasma cell dyscrasia or alone.

ATTR amyloidosis occurs due to misfolding of transthyretin, a tetrameric protein synthesized in the liver. This misfolding can occur through two mechanisms. The first is an inherited mutation in the TTR gene, referred to as hereditary ATTR. The second is an acquired age-related process referred to as wild-type ATTR (wtATTR). The prevalence of wtATTR is likely underestimated, but studies have shown the presence of cardiac deposits in up to 10-15% of autopsies, although the exact prevalence of pathologic wtATTR amyloid is less clear [1]. ATTR cardiac amyloidosis has a slower rate of disease progression compared to AL amyloidosis, with a median survival of three to five years without treatment [2]. The awareness of cardiac amyloidosis, especially ATTR amyloidosis, has grown dramatically in recent years due to improved diagnostic capabilities and new treatment options for amyloidosis [3].

Several cardiac tests are useful in the diagnosis of cardiac amyloidosis. The initial imaging modality of choice for amyloidosis is TTE. TTE will typically show ventricular hypertrophy, often in a biventricular pattern [3]. The degree of hypertrophy is often out of proportion to a patient’s blood pressure history or EKG findings, as in our patient. There is often dilation of both atria and evidence of diastolic dysfunction, sometimes with restrictive filling patterns [3]. On strain imaging, the classic finding for cardiac amyloidosis is a reduction in global longitudinal strain with relative apical sparing. This strain pattern has high specificity and sensitivity for cardiac amyloidosis [3]. The TTE of our patient was notable for severe concentric LVH (1.7-cm wall thickness), diastolic dysfunction, and reduced global longitudinal strain with relative apical sparing, all of which raised suspicion for cardiac amyloidosis.

When further evaluating LVH, besides amyloidosis, the differential should include long-standing hypertension, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and other infiltrative cardiomyopathies such as hemochromatosis and sarcoidosis. In hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and long-standing hypertension, concentric hypertrophy is often seen on TTE. However, both conditions are differentiated from cardiac amyloidosis by the presence of LVH on EKG, and strain imaging should not show the apical sparing pattern seen in amyloidosis. Other infiltrative cardiomyopathies, such as hemochromatosis and sarcoidosis, will not have the strain pattern seen in amyloidosis and can be further differentiated with cardiac MRI. Hemochromatosis should be considered in patients with other features of the disease, including skin changes, liver dysfunction, and unexplained fatigue. Patients with hilar adenopathy, pulmonary symptoms, or cardiac arrhythmias should be further evaluated for cardiac sarcoidosis. In our patient, there was high suspicion for cardiac amyloidosis because of the LVH seen on TTE, the apical sparing pattern on strain imaging, and the lack of LVH on EKG.

It is also important to consider whether our patient’s structural heart changes may be benign cardiac remodeling due to his long history of intense physical activity, often referred to as athlete’s heart. He was a master's-level swimmer who had competed in the past and swam 4000 yards three to four times per week. In athletes, there is well-studied and expected cardiac remodeling as a result of recurrent high-intensity activity. The common changes to heart structure and function in athletes include left ventricular and right ventricular dilation, atrial dilation, low-normal ejection fraction, and increased left ventricular wall thickness [4]. However, the degree of increased wall thickness is often mild and rarely greater than 1.3 cm [4]. Additionally, athletes’ hearts have normal or even supernormal diastolic function [4]. This patient’s severe hypertrophy of 1.7 cm and abnormal diastolic function strongly suggested pathology rather than athletic remodeling.

When amyloidosis is suspected based on clinical history and echocardiography, additional workup must be done to further confirm the diagnosis. In order to evaluate for AL amyloidosis, lab testing should be performed to assess for the presence of a monoclonal protein. This testing includes serum and urine protein electrophoresis with immunofixation, as well as serum free light chains. If these tests are abnormal, the patient should be evaluated by a specialist to determine whether they meet criteria for AL amyloidosis. The patient in our case did have a monoclonal gammopathy, prompting a referral to hematology, where he was not found to have any evidence of AL amyloidosis. If AL amyloidosis is ruled out, then additional testing for ATTR amyloidosis should be pursued.

The most commonly used test to further assess for ATTR amyloid is bone tracer scintigraphy, referred to as pyrophosphate scanning (PYP) [2]. This nuclear imaging modality assesses the uptake of tracer into the myocardium compared to the degree of uptake in bone. This uptake is graded as 0 (no uptake) or grades 1-3 based on the degree of uptake. The presence of grade 2 or 3 uptake is highly specific for cardiac amyloidosis [5]. Importantly, AL amyloidosis can also lead to a positive PYP test and therefore must be ruled out with laboratory testing. Our patient had grade 3 uptake, strongly suggestive of cardiac amyloidosis.

If there is any uncertainty regarding the diagnosis of cardiac amyloidosis, tissue biopsy is a valuable option [1]. Tissue biopsy is usually first performed on a fat pad, and if amyloid is found in the fat pad, then cardiac involvement is assumed. If the fat pad biopsy is non-diagnostic, direct biopsy of the heart can be pursued for definitive diagnosis. In our patient, given his abnormal serum studies showing a monoclonal gammopathy with elevated serum free light chains, a cardiac biopsy was pursued to confirm a diagnosis of ATTR amyloidosis as opposed to AL amyloidosis.

Once the diagnosis of cardiac amyloidosis is confirmed, treatment should be initiated. For AL amyloidosis with cardiac involvement, consultation with a hematology-oncology specialist is needed, as the treatment typically involves chemotherapy. For ATTR amyloidosis, there are now several therapies approved for treatment. Tafamidis was the first approved medication for cardiac ATTR amyloidosis and functions by preventing misfolding of transthyretin, which reduces new amyloid deposition. Tafamidis was shown to reduce mortality compared with placebo (29.5% vs. 42.9%), along with slower rates of decline in the six-minute walk test and the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire score. Importantly, this benefit was not seen in patients with significant heart failure symptoms at baseline (New York Heart Association Class III) [6].

Acoramidis is a similar agent that stabilizes transthyretin but has greater affinity for the protein compared to tafamidis. Acoramidis was shown to reduce rates of hospitalizations in patients with ATTR amyloidosis compared to placebo [7]. Vutisiran is an agent that alters hepatic transthyretin synthesis and reduces new amyloid deposition. Vutisiran was also shown to lead to a lower rate of all-cause death at 36 months (16% vs. 21%) compared to placebo [8]. After shared decision-making, our patient elected to proceed with tafamidis due to its simpler dosing regimen. He has been doing well with no new cardiac symptoms since starting therapy.

Conclusions

This case report illustrates a diagnostic approach to wtATTR cardiac amyloidosis and highlights several high-yield steps in the diagnostic process. First is the importance of a thorough physical exam, which identified abnormalities that prompted further evaluation with echocardiography. Being seen at a center with expertise in amyloidosis and athletic cardiac remodeling was essential to discern findings of cardiac amyloidosis vs. athlete’s heart. Having an expert team of cardiomyopathy specialists allowed this patient to have a thorough diagnostic workup to determine his exact type of amyloidosis and promptly start treatment. The interactions between multiple healthcare providers and teams were necessary to establish this often under-recognized yet clinically important diagnosis.
