Observed and projected climate extremes in northwest highlands of Ethiopia and their implications in potato-based farming systems
Authors: Asrat Ayalew, Mezegebu Getnet, Dereje Ademe, Achenafi Teklay, Aleminew Tagele, Shegaw Getu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Potato, a vital staple crop widely grown in the Ethiopian highlands, is sensitive to climate extremes. In this study, extreme rainfall and temperature trends were analyzed in northwest Ethiopia lower highland (Lower Dega) and upper highland (Upper Dega) agroecosystems (AESs) using Enhancing National Climate Services (ENACTS) dataset for 1989–2018 and MIROC6 projections for near-term (2019–2048) and mid-century (2049–2078) period under moderate (SSP2-4.5) and high (SSP5-8.5) emission scenarios to inform adaptation strategies. The extreme indices were computed using RClimDex2.0, while the significance of observed trends was evaluated using the Modified Mann–Kendall test and Sen’s slope estimator in R. Relatively higher extreme rainfall events were observed in Upper Dega. Observed annual total wet day rainfall (PRCPTOT) increased by 168 and 67 mm per decade, while the highest maximum monthly consecutive five-day precipitation (RX5day) increased by 13.8 and 2.5 mm per decade in Lower Dega and Upper Dega AESs, respectively. The projected PRCPTOT, extremely wet days (R99P), and number of very heavy precipitation days (R20) showed an increase in the range of 13.11–16.29%, 20.25–35.17%, and 44.83–57.95% across AESs, with the highest values anticipated in Upper Dega under SSP5-8.5 in mid-century. The highest rise in temperature extremes was projected for the warmest days (TXx) and warmest nights (TNx) with values of 1.74 and 1.77 °C and 1.65 and 1.95 °C in Lower Dega and Upper Dega AESs, respectively, under SSP5-8.5 in mid-century. Future agronomic practices and variety improvement activities should consider these results for climate-resilient potato production in the northwest Ethiopia potato-growing areas.

Introduction

Climate change is exerting a profound influence on climate extremes worldwide. Anthropogenic climate change has intensified extreme events and resulted in widespread impacts on both people and ecosystems1. It is anticipated to pose significant obstacles for agricultural production in the future, owing to its inherent sensitivity to and reliance on meteorological variables2–5. Climate variability and extremes significantly contribute to rising global hunger, with the greatest impact on the most vulnerable populations6. Extreme weather, climate, and water-related events accounted for 11,778 reported disasters worldwide from 1970 to 2021, leading to over 2 million deaths and economic losses estimated at US$4.3 trillion7.

Across Africa, South America, and Western Europe, an increase in temperature is causing heatwaves and aridity8–10. Over the past forty years, global temperatures have risen to their highest levels since 1850, with 2001–2020 warming by 0.99 °C relative to 1850–1900, and projections for 2081–2100 indicating further increases ranging from about 1.0 °C under low emissions to as much as 5.7 °C under the high-emission scenarios11. Global temperatures, which have risen unusually over the past decade, continued to climb in 2024, marking the highest levels on record, with the global average surface temperature reaching 1.55 °C above the 1850–1900 baseline12.

The rapid progression of climate change is increasingly threatening agricultural production and global food security, and by mid-century, higher emission scenarios are expected to reduce the productivity of key food crops13,14. Across all zones in Europe, higher growing season temperatures increased heat hazard risks, accelerated crop development, and reduced yields, with the Mediterranean south facing up to a 31% decline under high-emission scenarios by the 2080s15. Hultgren et al16. found that under a high-emissions scenario, end-of-century global crop yields could drop sharply: maize − 40% in major regions (U.S., Eastern China, Southern Africa), with smaller losses in South America and Central Africa, while Europe sees mixed effects (+ 10% in north, − 40% in Mediterranean). Habib-ur-rahman et al17. utilized the crop models, Decision Support System for Agrotechnology Transfer (DSSAT) and Agricultural Production Systems Simulator (APSIM), and predicted substantial yield reductions for rice (15.2%–17.2%) and wheat (12%–14.1%), noting that rice is more vulnerable because rising minimum temperatures during the summer season further exacerbate heat stress compared to winter-grown wheat in Asia. Using linear mixed-effects models, Tran et al18. found that global warming consistently degrades crop yields, most severely in arid regions, with wheat and rice losses accelerating from 6.1% to 8.2% and 1.1% to 7.1% per 1 °C once warming exceeds 2.38 °C and 3.13 °C thresholds, respectively. Applying the Safe Climatic Space (SCS) mapping concept, Heikonen et al19. reported that climate change is projected to shift cropland suitability and reduce crop diversity in low-latitude regions while increasing it in mid- to high-latitudes, with 10–31% of current production falling outside suitable climates under 2 °C warming and 20–48% under 3 °C warming.

In Africa, temperatures are projected to increase, with the most intense warming expected in the Sahara Desert, the Kalahari Desert, and parts of the Mediterranean region20–22. Climate change-induced weather extremes are expected to increase hazards linked to extreme rainfall events, flooding, intense rainstorms, heatwaves, and droughts11,23–25. In 2024, Africa faced several extreme weather events with far-reaching impacts, largely influenced by the positive El Niño and Indian Ocean Dipole phases that started in 2023 and continued into early 2024, resulting in severe flooding in East Africa and drought conditions in Southern Africa26. Using the WOFOST crop model, Dlamini et al27. projected that rising temperatures and shifting rainfall patterns will reduce cereal yields by 6–32% by mid-century in East Africa, with tuber crops like potato facing even greater losses.

Agriculture in Ethiopia, which supports the livelihoods of more than 70 million people and accounts for about 32.4% of the national gross domestic product (GDP)28, is highly dependent on rainfall patterns29,30. Thus, climate-related disruptions to Ethiopia’s agricultural sector are likely to strongly affect the overall economy. Yalew et al31. estimated that during severe climate-stress years, the country’s GDP could decline by as much as 8%.

In Ethiopia, climate change is likely to enhance variability in precipitation and temperature, augmenting the vulnerability of agricultural stakeholders to climate-induced risks and food insecurity32–38. Climate change is profoundly influencing the weather patterns in Ethiopia, resulting in considerable alterations in rainfall and temperature extremes25,39–42. The shifts in extreme precipitation and temperature are expected to create substantial consequences on agriculture and other climate-exposed businesses43. The rainfall patterns have become a decisive factor that adversely impacts the production of oilseeds, pulses, and cereal crops44. Using the CERES-Sorghum model, Gardi et al45. reported that in Ethiopia’s semi-arid regions, a 1.5 °C temperature rise combined with a 20% rainfall reduction could cut sorghum yields by up to 44%. Using a panel data regression model, Ginbo46 projected that under a medium-emissions scenario, climate change is projected to decrease barley, maize, and wheat yields at high altitudes by 22.7%, 48%, and 10%, respectively, for 2041–2060 compared with 1988–2018 in Ethiopia.

Potato, identified as the fourth most valuable agricultural product internationally after maize, rice, and wheat47, plays a fundamental role as a staple crop in the Ethiopian highlands. According to CSA48, Ethiopia harvested roughly 1.31 million tons of potato tubers, of which about 34.6% came from the Amhara National Regional State. In the Ethiopian highlands, future climate projections indicate rising temperatures and increased rainfall, which could improve the productivity of potatoes, provided that appropriate adaptive management strategies are adopted49.

While higher CO₂ levels and modest increases in rainfall may offer minor advantages, they are not enough to offset the negative impacts of rising temperatures, which continue to be the main limiting factor for potato productivity5,50,51. Potato growth and yield are highly vulnerable to climate-driven warming, with heat and drought stress most damaging during tuber bulking52–55. When temperatures rise beyond 28 °C, potato growth, yield, and quality are strongly suppressed56,57. Rising temperatures projected under future climate conditions may limit potato yields in tropical areas, yet could benefit production in temperate areas5,58,59. Elevated temperatures reduce tuberization and bulking rate in potatoes60,61. On the other hand, frost severely damages potato crops62. Long dry spells at the start of planting and frost events in later growth stages are significant climate-related challenges63. Heavy rainfall led to a substantial decline in potato tuber yield64,65. Under the high-emission scenario (RCP 8.5), potato yields are projected to decline by an average of 18% by 205066. Without adaptation, simulation outcomes revealed that fall potato yields face a wider potential reduction range (19–36%) compared to spring potatoes (23–29%) under semi-arid conditions67.

Investigating long-term variability and trends in climate extremes at a local scale is essential for designing site-specific adaptation strategies. While previous research has addressed broader regional yield patterns or specific impacts on Ethiopian cereals, a critical gap remains regarding the climatic thresholds governing potato-based systems in the northwest Ethiopian highlands. In this region, characterized by strong topographic contrasts, the magnitude and trajectory of extreme climate indices remain insufficiently understood at the AES level and seasonal time scales. This study addresses this gap by analyzing observed and projected changes in rainfall and temperature extremes across upper and lower highland AESs at annual and seasonal time scales. Unlike generalized regional studies, our analysis explicitly considers the unique differences between these two AES at the seasonal level, interpreting results based on their specific implications for local agricultural activities. To ensure the findings are practically relevant, we focused on extreme climate indices most critical for potato production to align with the crop’s unique physiological requirements. Ultimately, the study provides a localized evidence base to support the tailored adaptation strategies in sustainable potato-based farming in the northwest highlands of Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

The study site

The study was conducted in the Debark, Dabat, and Wogera districts of Northwest Amhara, Ethiopia, situated at 110, 75, and 40 km north of Gondar city, respectively, and extending between 37°30′E and 38°05′E longitude and 12°40′N and 13°15′N latitude (Fig. 1). The three districts were selected because they represent major potato-growing areas of the northwest Ethiopian highlands. The study sites are situated at an elevation range of 2685–2948 m above sea level, receiving between 1217 and 1394 mm of mean annual rainfall, with mean daily temperatures ranging from a minimum temperature (Tmin) of 8.70–11.04 °C to a maximum temperature (Tmax) of 20.26–23.92 °C68 (Fig. 2). The major crops grown in the districts are barley, wheat, peas, beans, and potatoes. Crop production in these districts is mainly rainfed. The main rainy season (Kiremt) begins in June and ends in September, although it can occasionally extend into October69.

Data Source

Observed daily data for precipitation, maximum temperature, and minimum temperature for the period 1989–2018 were obtained from the Enhancing National Climate Services (ENACTS) supplied by the National Meteorology Institute (NMI) of Ethiopia. ENACTS provides spatially complete rainfall and temperature data at a 4 km grid over a 30-year time series by blending data from the national observation network with satellite and climate reanalysis and elevation maps70. ENACTS data is selected as the station data in the study areas have many missing values.

Projections of future climates were grouped into near-term (NT) (2019–2048) and mid-century (MC) (2049–2078) simulations under moderate emissions of Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP245) and high emissions (SSP585) climate scenarios. Climate change data under the SSP245 and SSP585 scenarios were extracted from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) archive. CMIP6 is the latest phase of CMIP, which aims to enhance the representation of emission scenarios and increase horizontal resolution, thereby making future projections more realistic71,72. To incorporate bias-corrected and high-resolution (0.25°) projections, the study relied on Model for Interdisciplinary Research on Climate, version 6 (MIROC6) data processed through NASA’s Earth Exchange Global Daily Downscaled Projections (NEX-GDDP) system73. MIROC6 was selected based on a Compromise Programming Lp-metric ranking74, as it performed best among 10 CMIP6 models in simulating precipitation (r > 0.82) and temperature (r > 0.64), while also effectively capturing teleconnections in the Ethiopian highlands75. Climate data were averaged across all stations within each AES for the AES-level analyses.

Data reparation and quality control

For the analysis of climate extremes, daily data were converted to monthly, seasonal, and annual values as appropriate. The penalized maximal F test76,77 was employed to evaluate data homogeneity and detect change points. The temperature data were examined with RHtestsV3, while precipitation data were assessed using the RHtests_dlyPrcp tools in R to identify and correct artificial breaks in the records. For additional data quality assurance, the procedures implemented in RClimDex2.0 were applied78.

Observed climate extreme indices analysis

Temperature and precipitation extreme indices (Table 2) proposed by the Expert Team on Climate Change Detection and Indices (ETCCDI) were analyzed using RClimDex2.0 in R78. The indices were calculated on a seasonal basis, corresponding to bega (dry season; October–January), belg (short rainy season; February–May), and kiremt (main rainy season; June–September).

Observed trend in climate extremes analysis

Trend assessment was done employing the modified Mann–Kendall and Spearman’s Rho tests79–81 in R with the “modifiedmk” package (version 1.6) for determining trend significance, and Sen’s estimator82 to compute the trend magnitude. If the serial autocorrelation test was insignificant, trends were analyzed using the standard “mkktest” package; otherwise, the bias-corrected pre-whitening technique from the “bcpw” package was employed.

Projected changes in extreme rainfall and temperatures

Since NEX-GDDP is bias-corrected based on a global reference dataset, a second bias correction was applied to better align with ENACTS observational data. This adjustment used an additive (delta change correction) method for temperature variables and a multiplicative (ratio-based correction) for rainfall variables83 using Climate Model data for hydrologic modeling (CMhyd), a specialized tool designed for processing and bias-correcting climate model outputs from global and regional climate models84.

In the additive approach, temperature projections were adjusted by adding the difference between modeled future and historical baseline means to the observed historical records. Results and Discussion

Observed climate extreme indices analysis

Extreme precipitation

The results of the extreme precipitation indices analysis are presented in Table 3. Overall, extreme rainfall intensity indices have shown significant increases in the Upper Dega compared to the Lower Dega AES. In Upper Dega AES, extreme precipitation indices such as PRCPTOT, R95P, R20, R10, and Rx5day during both the annual and kiremt seasons, and Rx1day during the kiremt season, exhibited significant increases (P < 0.05). Annual PRCPTOT, R95P, and monthly RX5day increased by 168.00, 66.80, and 13.80 mm per decade, respectively in Upper Dega AES. In contrast, in the Lower Dega AES, significant (P < 0.05) increases were observed only in PRCPTOT (66.6 mm per decade), R10 (3.3 days per decade), and monthly Rx5day during belg season (6.60 mm per decade) and Rx1day during belg (2.90 mm per decade).

The more pronounced increase in extreme rainfall indices in Upper Dega AES suggests greater climate variability at higher elevations. The significant rise in R95P, R20, and Rx5day indicates extreme precipitation events are becoming more intense, which could exacerbate flood risks and soil erosion in Upper Dega. In contrast, the insignificant trends in most extreme precipitation indices in the Lower Dega AES suggest a relatively stable rainfall regime. However, the increases observed in RX5day and Rx1day during the belg season still indicate a move toward shorter but more violent rainfall. This spatial variability in trends between the two AESs shows the need for locally focused adaptation measures, particularly in Upper Dega, where extreme rainfall events may increasingly threaten potato production. A pronounced rising trend in the number of heavy precipitation days in the Upper Dega AES may affect potato growth and development by creating water-logging conditions in the root zone, which can impair normal root functioning and reduce overall plant performance. Heavy rainfall may also reduce nutrient use efficiency by leaching and washing away applied nutrients. According to Ahrari et al.64, a single day of intense rainfall resulted in greater yield losses in potato than those caused by temperature extremes.

Regarding the CDD and CWD, both the Upper Dega and Lower Dega AESs exhibited similar trends. Notably, CWD showed a significant increase (P < 0.05) in both AES, indicating that wet spells are becoming longer or more persistent over time. This trend may indicate the increasing durations of soil moisture availability, which could be particularly relevant for long-maturing potato cultivars. On the contrary, CDD showed an insignificant increase (P > 0.05), meaning that the duration of dry spells has not changed significantly in either AES. Similarly, the SDII exhibited an insignificant rising trend (P > 0.05) in both AESs.

This observation is in agreement with the report of Wubye et al.43, who reported an increasing trend in annual PRCPTOT, CWD, R10, Rx5day, and SDII in potato-growing highland areas of Amhara. In another study, Mohammed et al.39 reported an increasing trend in PRCPTOT and Rx1day in most stations in Ethiopia. Likinaw42 also detected a rising trend in PRCPTOT, CWD, and R10 in potato-growing areas of South Gondar highlands. In contrast to this result, a decreasing trend in rainfall intensity-related extreme indices was reported in Chokie Mountain watershed, northwest Amhara85.

Extreme temperatures

The extreme temperature analysis results are depicted in Table 4. Across the Lower and Upper Dega AESs, SU25 exhibited significant increases of 30 and 16 days per decade, while chill days (FD10) showed significant decreases of approximately 39 and 19 days per decade (P < 0.05), respectively. Significant (P < 0.05) increasing trends in TXx, TXn, and TNx were detected in both AES at seasonal and annual time scales, with Lower Dega AES exhibiting a higher rate of increase than Upper Dega AES. For annual TNn, an insignificant decreasing trend was observed in Lower Dega AES, whereas Upper Dega AES exhibited a significant decreasing trend. More variability was detected in this index across seasons.

In both AESs, TX10P declined significantly (P < 0.05) across all seasons. A significant (P < 0.05) decreasing trend in TN10P was detected in Lower Dega AES during all seasons except kiremt, whereas in Upper Dega it increased insignificantly (P > 0.05). Both TX90P and TN90P increased significantly (P < 0.05) at all seasons in both AESs except the kiremt season in Upper Dega AES. The annual and kiremt season DTR increased significantly (P < 0.05) in both AESs, while the corresponding trends during belg and bega seasons increased insignificantly (P < 0.05) in both AESs. The DTR increased by 0.90 and 0.4 °C per decade in the kiremt season in Lower Dega and Upper Dega AES, respectively.

A rising trend in SU25, TXx, TXn, TX90P, and TN90P, alongside a decreasing trend in FD10, indicated that the daily temperatures are increasing (heating) at a greater rate of increase in Lower Dega. Heating stress may reduce tuber yield. Similarly, based on the findings of IPCC11, there has been a notable decrease in the number of cold days and nights, while warm days and nights have become more frequent since the early 20th century. Elevated temperatures markedly inhibit tuber formation in potatoes61. Kim and Lee86 confirmed that exposing potato plants to high temperatures during the tuber-initiation stage led to a decline in the tuber size, early harvest index, photosynthetic activity, and the greatest yield reduction of 30.3%. High temperature reduces dry matter partitioning to tubers87. Hot and dry weather during the potato growing period can shorten the natural dormancy of tubers, causing them to sprout earlier during storage88. This result aligns with the report of Birhan et al.85, who reported an increase in SU25, TXx, TXn, TNx, and TNn in lower-elevation areas compared to higher-elevation areas in Chokie Mountain watershed, northwest Amhara.

The rate of change in DTR was significantly higher during the kiremtseason and annual timescales in both AES. This has happened due to an increase in TXx and TNn in both AESs. This result aligns with the report of Perez et al89., who reported higher DTR in highland areas due to a reduction in cloud cover, which may raise the daily maximum temperatures more than the daily minimum temperatures. This DTR change may harm potato productivity because late blight of potato is favored by cool night and moderately warm day temperatures in moist environments90, which is a likely case during the kiremt season in Ethiopian highland areas.

Projected changes in extreme climates

Extreme rainfall

The projected changes in precipitation extreme indices under the medium (SSP245) and high (SSP585) emission scenarios in the NT (2019–2048) and NC (2049–2078) with respect to the baseline period (1989–2018) are presented in Fig. 3.

Almost all precipitation extreme indices have shown an increasing trend under all emission scenarios and time periods across AESs, with greater increases under the SSP585. The annual PRCPTOT in the Lower Dega AES is projected to increase by 5.20% in the NT under the SSP245 and by 13.11% in the MC under the SSP585 compared to the baseline period. In the Upper Dega AES, projected increases are higher, reaching 8.70% in the NT period under the SSP245, and 16.29% in the MC period under the SSP585 relative to the baseline period. The higher precipitation intensity indices like R99P, R20, and Rx5days will be increased by 20.24, 44.82, and 24.29% under the SSP585 in the MC period in Lower Dega AES, respectively, while the corresponding values in Upper Dega AES in the same scenario and timeframe will be 35.17, 57.95, and 21.72%. Similarly, SDII exhibits trends comparable to those of PRCPTOT across AESs and emission scenarios.

The Upper Dega AES generally showed larger increases than Lower Dega AES for most extreme precipitation indices, particularly for R99P, R20, and Rx1day under the high-emission scenario. Such extremely intense rainfall events can increase flood risk, lead to excessive soil saturation, and promote tuber and root rot in potato crops. Waterlogging can also disrupt farming activities and schedules. Similarly, Kassahun et al.91 reported projected increases in PRCPTOT, R20, R10, and R95P during the MC period under the high-emission scenario at Debark station in the northwest Ethiopian highlands, where potato is a key food crop. The results of this analysis are also in agreement with the report of Getachew and Manjunatha92, who detected an increasing trend in rainfall at Bahir Dar and Gondar meteorological stations in northwest Ethiopia. In the Upper Blue Nile Basin of northwest Ethiopia, the upper subbasin will experience a higher annual frequency of R10 under the SSP1-2.6, and R20 in the far future40. Generally, increasing trends in R10 and R20 indices were projected in large parts of Ethiopia93. Another study in southwest Ethiopia confirmed that across the mid-century and end-century periods, rainfall was projected to increase slightly under the medium and high emission scenarios and decrease under the lower emission pathways94.

Regarding wet and dry spells, CWD is projected to decline slightly under both emission pathways and time periods in the Lower Dega AES, except during the MC period under the SSP585 scenario. In contrast, CWD is projected to increase slightly across all emission scenarios and time periods in the Upper Dega AES. In contrast, CDD is projected to rise substantially across both AESs under all emission scenarios and time periods, except during the MC period under SSP585 in the Upper Dega AES. CDD is projected to increase by up to about 40% during the MC period under SSP585 in the Lower Dega AES, and by approximately 30% during the NT period under the same scenario in the Upper Dega AES. This finding is consistent with Alemu and Wubneh40, who reported an increase in CDD at Debre Tabor in northwest Ethiopia during the mid-future period under the high-emission (SSP585) scenario.

The Lower Dega AES showed stronger warming signals in dry spells than the Upper Dega AES. Potatoes require optimum moisture, particularly during tuber initiation and bulking periods. Prolonged CDD may reduce potato growth and yield. Potato yield is highly sensitive to drought stress, particularly during the critical early tuber initiation phase95.

Extreme temperatures

Projected changes in extreme temperature indices relative to the baseline (1989–2018) period under the medium (SSP245) and high (SSP585) emission scenarios in the NT (2019–2048) and MC (2049–2078) periods are presented in Figs. 4 and 5.

In both AESs, SU25 was projected to rise steadily across all emission scenarios and time periods, whereas FD10 was expected to decline under the same scenarios and time periods, with a slight increase under SSP245 during the NT period across AESs (Fig. 4). Under the SSP585 scenario in the MC period, SU25 was projected to increase by approximately 62 and 24 days, whereas FD10 was anticipated to decrease by approximately 111 and 101 days in Lower Dega and Upper Dega AESs, respectively.

The magnitudes of temperatures for TXx, TNx, TXn, and TNn were expected to rise under the high and medium emission scenarios at all future time periods across AES, except for a slight decrease in TNn under the SSP245 scenario in the NT period (Fig. 5). The largest temperature increases are projected for TXx and TNx during the MC period under the SSP585 scenario, reaching 1.74 °C and 1.77 °C in the Lower Dega AES, and 1.65 °C and 1.95 °C in the Upper Dega AES, respectively. DTR was projected to decrease in both scenarios and timeframes, except under SSP245 in the NT period across AESs.

This study aligns with the reports of many previous investigators who revealed that an increasing tendency in extreme temperatures will be recorded under the SSP585 or RCP8.5 scenario in Ethiopia40,92,93,96.

The projected increment in extreme high temperatures indicates that the future temperatures will be harsh for potato cultivation unless appropriate adaptive measures are implemented. High temperatures inflict the greatest damage on potato crops, specifically during the tuber initiation stage56,86,97. A large increase in temperature can also reduce tuber bulking during late growth51,60. The rising temperatures remain the primary constraint to sustaining potato production5.

Limitations of the study

Despite providing valuable insights into observed and projected climate extremes in northwest Ethiopia, this study has some limitations. Due to a lack of complete station climate data, the analysis relied on the ENACTS gridded dataset, which, although quality-controlled, may not fully capture localized microclimatic variability across complex highland topography. Future climate projections inherently contain uncertainties associated with climate model structure, parameterization, and emission scenario assumptions, which may influence the magnitude and timing of projected changes in temperature and rainfall extremes.

Conclusion

The baseline period analysis revealed that intensity and frequency of heavy precipitation were increased across AESs with significantly higher rises in Upper Dega AES. Similarly, the projected heavy precipitation indices showed an increasing tendency at all emission scenarios and time periods with the highest increment being anticipated in the mid-century period under the high emission scenario (SSP585) across AESs.

The projected SU25 and FD10 revealed that Lower Dega AES will be warmer than Upper Dega AES. The observed maximum and minimum temperatures extreme indices like TXx, TXn, TNx showed a significantly increasing trend across AESs, with the highest rate of change in TNx in Upper Dega AES. The projected changes in these extremes compared to the observed values also exhibited an increasing trend with the highest values under the high-emission scenario (SSP585) across AES. The rising trend in TN90P and TX90P and a decreasing trend in TN10P and TX10P were detected across AES.

The observed climate extremes, such as historical heatwaves, frost events, and extreme rainfall episodes, provide a reference pattern for future projections. The projected extremes under SSP245 and SSP585 follow similar spatial and seasonal trends as historically observed events but with amplified intensity. For instance, extreme rainfall indices (R95p, R20, Rx5day) and temperature extremes (TXx, TXn, SU25, TX90P, and TN90P) increase relative to the 1989–2018 baseline, suggesting that past patterns of rainfall and temperature can analogically inform how extreme events will manifest under warming scenarios. This link allows for anticipating risk areas and timing for agricultural interventions. Current measures may mitigate historical risks, but the intensified extremes under SSP585 highlight the need for proactive, climate-informed interventions to sustain potato production in the northwest Ethiopian highlands.

To enhance the resilience of potato production under projected climate extremes, several strategies are essential. Developing heat- and drought-tolerant potato varieties is crucial, given the anticipated rise in maximum temperatures. Complementing this, improved soil and water management, such as enhanced drainage systems, can help mitigate crop losses from extreme rainfall events. Adopting flexible planting calendars that adjust sowing dates to avoid the most severe periods of heatwaves and heavy rainfall, and irrigation water management can further safeguard yields and optimize crop performance.
