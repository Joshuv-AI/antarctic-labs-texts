CApEsid biOfilm: a suggested pipeline for clinical phage microbiology for biofilm infections based on comparative method study
Authors: Amit Rimon, Ron Braunstein, Ortal Yerushalmy, Noa Katz, Lidor Yosef, Yitzchak Gvili, Shunit Coppenhagen-Glazer, Ronen Hazan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rise of antibiotic-resistant infections, particularly those involving biofilms, presents a significant global health threat. Phage therapy, the use of bacteriophages as antimicrobial agents, offers promising solutions to this crisis. A critical component of phage therapy is the assessment of phage efficacy, in both the presence and absence of antibiotics, prior to clinical application. While considerable progress has been made using planktonic bacterial cultures, there remains an urgent need for standardized methods to evaluate phage efficacy against biofilms. In this study, we address this gap by systematically comparing ten different methods for quantifying phage activity in biofilm settings. Each method was evaluated using a panel of five anti-Pseudomonas aeruginosa phages, which were tested against both planktonic and biofilm cultures. Based on these comparisons, we propose a robust pipeline for detecting phage activity in biofilms. This pipeline, termed CApEsid biOfilm, integrates modified colony-forming unit (CFU) assays using stainless steel washers, crystal violet staining, extracellular DNA quantification using a dye, and extracellular ATP measurements. The pipeline was further validated with additional bacterial species and their respective phages. We also demonstrate its utility in detecting interactions between phages and antibiotics. Overall, this work presents a foundational pipeline that may enhance the clinical matching of phages for treating biofilm-associated infections, thereby improving the outcomes of phage therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52935-4.

Introduction

Antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections are currently ranked as the second leading cause of mortality and are anticipated to become the primary cause of death by 20501,2. One significant contributor to these persistent infections is the formation of bacterial biofilms, which are structures composed of microorganisms embedded in an extracellular matrix3,4. Unlike individual bacterial cells, these biofilms exhibit remarkable resilience to conventional antibiotics due to antibiotic-limited penetration capabilities5,6, making these infections particularly challenging to treat7. Some examples of such clinical conditions are osteomyelitis8,9, diabetic foot ulcers10, medical device-related infections, including catheter-associated UTIs9,11,12, and dental-associated diseases4,13. Moreover, biofilms pose a considerable threat in many fields, including the food industry14, agriculture15, water systems16, and many more.

A promising approach in the battle against antibiotic-resistant pathogens, including in biofilms, is the utilization of bacteriophages (phages), viruses that invade bacterial cells, exploit their systems to propagate, and ultimately kill the bacteria by lysis, followed by the spreading of hundreds of new phage particles, infecting, in a chain reaction, more cells. Phage therapy demonstrated efficacy in compassionate treatments16–19 and in vivo studies20,21 and offers several advantages over antibiotics. Phages are highly strain-specific, minimally affect the host microbiome, can evolve to overcome bacterial resistance, have ‘auto-dosing’ kinetics linked to their target22,23, and are easy to isolate and modify genetically24. Additionally, certain phages carry depolymerases, enzymes capable of degrading the extracellular matrix, enhancing their ability to penetrate and dismantle biofilm layers20,21,25,26. Despite these advantages, the clinical efficacy of phage therapy is frequently challenged by the rapid emergence of phage-resistant bacterial populations. Bacteria employ diverse defense mechanisms to evade viral infection, including the modification of surface receptors to prevent phage adsorption and the use of intracellular systems like CRISPR-Cas or Restriction-Modification27,28.

A significant hurdle in phage therapy lies in the need for precise matching of suitable phages to the patient’s bacterium. To this end, various phages, alone or in combination with antibiotics, are evaluated through Clinical Phage Microbiology (CPM) methods19,29. However, while these matching methods suit planktonic bacteria, validated and reliable methods for assessing the impact of different phages on biofilms are currently lacking20,30,31. Consequently, the phage-matching process for biofilm-associated infections relies on testing phages’ efficacy against bacteria in their planktonic state or utilizing plaque plate assays32.

In this study, we addressed this critical gap by comparing ten methods that have demonstrated potential for quantifying bacterial growth within biofilms33,34 to assess phage activity in these complex communities.

To this end, we used a panel of five Pseudomonas aeruginosa phages35 grown in planktonic and biofilm cultures. The methods compared are from various approaches, including microbiological36, microscopy37,38, metabolic39,40, lysis indicators41, and extracellular matrix assessment. Based on this comparison, we propose a pipeline, which was further evaluated with combinations of phages and antibiotics on various bacterial species, including an additional P. aeruginosa strain, Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Enterococcus faecalis.

Results

Approach, experimental design, and biofilm model establishment

Our approach was to evaluate methods that can quantify the growth and lysis of planktonic bacteria (Fig. 1a-f) as described42, and use them to assess growth and lysis in biofilm settings (Fig. 1g-n). We prioritized kinetic methods that detect real-time changes in growth over endpoint detection and used the “area under the curve (AUC)” method43 as a quantitative measurement of phage efficacy.

For biofilms, P. aeruginosa bacteria were cultivated in 96-well plates as described (See methods section ”Bacterial strain and cultural conditions”)44 followed by removal of loosely attached bacteria by gently washing the wells with fresh media. To confirm that the bacterial growth represented true biofilm formation rather than merely adhered planktonic cells, freshly grown overnight planktonic bacterial cultures were added to separate wells in parallel to the biofilm cultivation wells. Enumerating the colony-forming units (CFU count) showed that the planktonic control had an approximate three orders of magnitude lower CFU than that of the pre-grown biofilm (Fig. 1m), demonstrating a “true”, mature biofilm, which was also confirmed by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) imaging (Fig. 1n).

To evaluate the phage activity in the biofilm, we utilized a panel of five anti-P. aeruginosa-targeting phages (Table 1)38 from the Israeli Phage Bank48. Among them, phage PASA16, in particular, is notable for its lytic activity reflected in its involvement in numerous phage therapy compassionate cases worldwide18,46,47.

Since we did not know a priori which phages would be more effective against biofilms or which detection method better reflects phage activity, we made three assumptions and characterized the methods accordingly: First, as an inclusive criterion, we sought methods that distinguished between the ten phage-treated groups to the untreated control group (Criteria 1: Difference from Control). Second, it is generally accepted that a phage at a higher titer is more effective than the same phage at a lower titer36 Thus, we screened for methods that can differentiate between two titers of the five phages added to the biofilm (Criteria 2: Titer Difference ). Third, given that phage PASA16 has established relatively high efficacy in planktonic culture (Fig. 1a), we considered it a potential high-efficacy reference and examined whether methods could reflect this in biofilms (Criteria 3: PASA16 Potential Superiority). However, as this criterion was less robust and more context-specific, it was not used in the final selection of the preferred protocols, but only for validation. The maximum amount of differences in this category is eight.

The problem: optical density growth curve analysis is not suitable for evaluating phage efficacy within biofilms

The most frequently employed method for evaluating planktonic bacterial growth inhibition by phages is through growth curve analysis, detected by optical density at OD 600 nm19,48,49. Thus, initially, we compared our panel of phages at two concentrations on planktonic bacteria to evaluate their effectiveness. The AUC varies significantly between the phages’ MOI of 0.1 and 10 (Fig. 1a-f). Additionally, in four out of the five phages, a higher MOI exhibited increased activity (Fig. 1a-f). Phage PADD was an exception to this observation.

However, in agreement with the literature50, optical density proved largely ineffective in assessing phage activity in biofilms due to the visibility obstruction caused by the bacterial layers (Fig. 1g,h). Accordingly, we excluded the optical density method as a valid method for phage matching in biofilm and screened other techniques.

Microbiological methods

We examined two methods that are commonly used for assessing bacterial growth in biofilms: CFU count36, and live/dead stain51.

CFU count

The commonly used CFU count was conducted by sonicating the biofilm, followed by plating and colony count as a measure of bacterial cell viability43,52,53. One inherent drawback of the CFU count is that it is performed in time intervals and not real-time. We tested the CFU assay according to the three criteria described above: Criteria 1: Difference from Control: A significant reduction in comparison to the untreated biofilm control was observed in 100% of the phages tested (Fig. 2a). Criteria 2: Titer Difference: the CFU assay differentiated between the phage concentrations of 40% of the panel, and in Criteria 3: PASA16 Superiority, was observed in 0% of the phages (Fig. 2a).

Remarkably, an improvement in all the later two criteria were achieved when CFU counts were performed on bacteria grown on a stainless steel washer rather than directly from the 96-well plate. It is important to note that the change in surface material (from plastic to stainless steel) likely influences biofilm architecture and extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) composition, which may independently affect phage penetration and bacterial recovery (Fig. 2b)43,52,53.

Live/dead stain

This assay visualizes bacterial viability using two dyes: SYTO9, which is a green-fluorescent, cell-permeant nucleic acid stain that binds to DNA and RNA in both live and dead bacteria. The second is propidium iodide (PI), which also stains DNA, but only enters dead cells with a non-intact membrane. Thus, live cells appear green, while membrane-compromised cells appear red43,54,55. First, we validated that these dyes are non-toxic to bacteria and the phage panel (Supplemental Fig. S1a-b). While SYTO9 did not show toxicity to either bacteria or any of the phages (Supplemental Fig S1a-b), PI reduced bacterial viability by approximately one log, but was not phage toxic (Supplemental Fig S1a-b). Consequently, the Live/Dead stain should be performed at the endpoint and not real-time. The results of this analysis, according to the above criteria, were as follows: A Difference from Control was observed in 50% of phages (Fig. 2d, e). Remarkably, Titer Difference was observed in only 40% of the phage panel, and 25% showed PASA16 Superiority (Fig. 2d, e).

In summary, the modified CFU count method using washers emerges as a sensitive and effective technique for detecting phage efficacy in biofilms. On the other hand, Live/Dead stain though widely used lacks sufficient sensitivity for distinguishing between phages, is more difficult to implement, and requires specialized microscopy equipment (Table 2). A common limitation of both methods is that they can only be performed at discrete time points or at the experimental endpoint, rather than in real time.

Metabolic assessments

In contrast to optical methods, metabolic assays such as determination of calorimetry and extracellular acidification rate are usually insensitive to the transparency of the well or aggregated bacteria. Moreover, given the right equipment, they can be performed at real-time.

Calorimetry assessment

Calorimeters quantify heat produced by the bacteria, which is associated with metabolic changes and correlates with the quantity of living bacterial cells39,40 In this study, we utilized the CalScreener® (https://symcel.com/) calorimeter, which operates within a closed 32-well plate system, to measure heat reduction as a measure of phage activity.

80% of the phages showed Difference from Control, and the AUC for 60% of phages fulfilled the Titer Difference criteria. On the other hand, 50% of phages showed PASA16 Superiority (Fig. 3a–f). A significant difference between the control and phage-treated groups was most evident during the first 10 h of measurement (Fig. 3a–e). A general decline in metabolic activity was observed across all CalScreener wells, likely due to anaerobic conditions caused by limited oxygen availability. Notably, signs of bacterial regrowth and adaptation were detected in certain cases (Fig. 3a, b, d). Heat measurements commenced one hour after inoculation, in accordance with the CalScreener protocol, which required transferring the treated samples into sealed titanium wells prior to measurement.

Extracellular acidification rate

This approach detects the amount of oxygen that cells consume over time by measuring the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) reflected in changes in pH of the culture medium over time56 To this end, we used the Seahorse XFe96 technology, which measures and reports ECAR in a designated microplate56. The AUC of 9 0% of phage-treated groups exhibited a positive Difference from Control (Supplemental Fig S2a-f). Additionally, in 60% of the set, differences in Titer Difference were observed, and none have shown PASA16 Superiority (Supplemental Fig S2f).

In summary, we found significant differences in the performance of the two metabolic-based real-time methods tested here in evaluating phage efficacy in biofilms.

The calorimetry approach, while highly accurate, is limited due to the requirement for anaerobic conditions during the measurements and the substantial cost of the instrument.

ECAR assessment had insufficient sensitivity in detecting lysis in biofilm (Fig. 4; Table 2).

Lysis indicators

Another approach to assess lysis is by detecting the levels of intracellular substances such as ATP and DNA that are released from lysed cells41. The advantages of these kinds of methods are that they can be highly rapid (detection of lysis in a few hours), very sensitive, and they are not costly.

Extracellular ATP

ATP, an essential part of bacterial metabolism, is an intracellular molecule57. Therefore, the levels of extracellular ATP (eATP), reflect the levels of lysed bacteria and hence phage efficacy58 (Fig. 4). The ATP levels are assessed using two parameters: “time to maximum”, which is the time to maximal luminescence, and “maximal luminescence”, which reports the value of this maximum point.

To validate the method, we used the bacteriolytic antibiotic Carbenicillin to lyse P. aeruginosa bacteria in a dose-response and measured the eATP levels (Supplemental Fig S3a).

To better characterize the assay’s dynamics, we first tested varying concentrations of lysed planktonic bacteria. A clear dose-dependent response was observed, with a detection threshold of < 10⁵ CFU/mL (Supplemental Fig. S3a). We then monitored planktonic bacteria over extended periods. According to the manufacturer’s datasheet (promega.com), the reagent is supposed to be non-toxic to both bacteria and phages; however, we noticed a reduction in the OD₆₀₀ values (Supplemental Fig. S1a, S3b), Which did not appear to affect the trend in all three categories. The ATP release was detected rapidly upon exposure to phages in comparison to the untreated culture (Supplemental Fig. 3c–h) and assessed by the two described parameters. When measuring “time to maximum”, 90% of the panel phages demonstrated a significant Difference from Control, with PASA16 and PAKlein3 showing the most pronounced effect. 40% of the phages exhibited significant differences in Titer Difference criteria, and 37.5% showed PASA16 Superiority (Fig. 4a-f).

When measuring the “maximal luminescence”, 30% were Different from the Control, 80% of phages showed Titer Difference, and 62.5% of them showed PASA16 Superiority (Fig. 4a-g).

Extracellular DNA

The appearance of extracellular DNA (eDNA) can be a strong marker for cell lysis. Here we used two approaches to detect its levels: qPCR and staining.

qPCR

The advantages of Quantitative PCR (qPCR) in assessing bacterial lysis are that it is a highly sensitive and relatively rapid method using standard lab instruments. Its disadvantage is that it is not a real-time method, and it requires sampling.

We compared the eDNA in the supernatant (SN) of phage-exposed and unexposed biofilm cultures over 48 h. For the PCR, we used primers against the housekeeping gene rpoD of PA14 grown on biofilms (Supplemental Fig S4a), but basically any primers against any sequence of the target bacteria genome can be used, including the 16 S universal primers59.

In these experiments, the most pronounced phage effect occurred at 24 h post-treatment (Supplemental Fig S4a). Subsequent evaluation of the phage panel showed significant differences in the eDNA reflected by the quantification cycle (Cq) values. Difference from Control was observed in 90% of the phage-treated groups (Supplemental Fig S4b). 40% treated groups had positive phage Titer Difference (Supplemental Fig S4b). and PASA16 Superiority was observed only in 37.5% of phages.

To assess the validity of this method, we also performed it using 16 S rRNA primers. Consistent with our rpoD findings, the kinetics of lysis using 16 S primers also showed maximal effect at 24 h (Supplemental Fig S4c). Using a calibration curve, we found that DNA released through lysis was detectable at concentrations as low as 10–9 ng/µL. A strong linear correlation between DNA content and qPCR detection was observed, with an average Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.79 and an R² value of 0.624 (Supplemental Fig S4d).

DNA stain

The advantage of using non-toxic, non-penetrating DNA dyes over the qPCR method is the ability to perform the assay in real-time in a “fire-and-forget” manner. To this end, we used TOTO-160,61, a fluorescent dye that selectively binds extracellular DNA, to quantitatively assess changes in extracellular nucleic acid accumulation60,61. Consistent with previous reports on eukaryotic cells62, TOTO-1 was confirmed to be non-bactericidal at the concentrations used (Supplemental Fig S1), and optimal staining was achieved at a dilution of 1:2500 (Supplemental Fig S5a-d).

Our findings revealed increased fluorescence of TOTO1-stained cells following phage treatment, reflecting the corresponding elevation of extracellular DNA in biofilm assays (Supplemental Fig S5e-f). Regarding Difference from Control, 90% of the phage panel was positive, 80% of phages were positive for the Titer Difference criteria, and 37.5% of phages showed PASA16 Superiority (Fig. 5a-f).

In summary, the three methods provide robust sensitive and reproducible measurements of phage-mediated bacterial lysis without the need for costly equipment or special conditions. TOTO1 and eATP offer the advantage of real-time data collection. qPCR is a more sensitive method but less suitable for high-throughput analysis compared to the real-time monitoring methods (Table 2). Therefore, we included the eDNA detection by stain in our pipeline (see below “suggested pipeline” section). qPCR at the endpoint or some time intervals can be optionally added in cases of inconsistency or discrepancy between the other methods.

Biofilm extracellular matrix assessment

A common approach of biofilm disassociation detection is by measuring the biomass or the integrity of the extracellular matrix of the biofilm, which is one of its hallmarks3.

Crystal violet (CV) assay

This assay assesses the biomass of the biofilm rather than living cells63 and, like CFU count, is performed at time intervals or the growth endpoint, and it is not a real-time method43,51,64 With our anti-P. aeruginosa phage panel, CV demonstrated a Difference from control of 80% of phages. Titer Difference was observed for 80% of phages (Fig. 6), with only one phage (PAB1) not showing a significant difference between the two titers. PASA16 Superiority was observed in 25% of phages (Fig. 6).

EbbaBiolight 680

EbbaBiolight 680 is a fluorescent optotracer dye designed for the selective labeling of extracellular matrix (ECM) components within bacterial biofilms65,66. The method is based on the dye’s ability to bind specifically to β-glucans and other amyloid-like structures present in the ECM of various microorganisms (https://www.ebbabiotech.com/products/ebbabiolight-680), including P. aeruginosa65–67. The application of this dye followed the manufacturer’s instructions, with confirmation of its non-cytotoxic and non-phage-toxic nature (Supplemental Fig S1a-b). Briefly, P. aeruginosa biofilm cultures treated with each of the five phages of the panel at two concentrations underwent fluorescence measurements using a plate reader (Excitation at 540 nm and absorbance at 680 nm).

There was a 60% significant reduction in Difference from Control (Supplemental Fig S6a-f); only 60% of the five tested phages demonstrated a Titer Difference (Supplemental Fig S6a-f). Notably, PAB2 exhibited an increase in absorbed fluorescence and is the only phage to show PASA16 Superiority (1/8, Supplemental Fig S6a-f).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Although SEM is qualitative rather than quantitative, it can still provide useful insights into biofilm integrity. For visualization of both biofilm viability and biomass, SEM imaging was performed at magnifications of X5k and X20k (Supplemental Fig. S7a), while phage particles were observed at X100k magnification. Biofilm formed on a plastic surface was treated with the previously described phage panel at two different concentrations. Visual assessment only allows qualitative visualization of phage effect.

In summary, all three methods have potential for evaluating biofilm degradation. Crystal violet (CV) staining provides a practical and quantitative measure of biofilm biomass. It effectively distinguishes phage-treated samples from untreated controls and highlights differences in phage titers; however, its ability to detect variability between different phages is somewhat limited. EbbaBiolight offers a more accessible and user-friendly approach, but in our assay, it demonstrated limited sensitivity, which may restrict its applicability in certain contexts (Table 2). While SEM provided high-resolution visualization of biofilm architecture and confirmed direct phage-bacterium interactions, the technique proved resource-intensive and lacked the scalability required for routine analysis. Furthermore, SEM remained a qualitative tool, as it did not provide the quantitative precision or numerical throughput68.

A suggested pipeline

Since none of the tested methods yielded perfect results in the three assessed criteria, we decided to develop a pipeline of four complementary methods that differ in approach, showing promising results with the tested criteria, suite large numbers of bacteria and phages in biofilm settings, and requiring less expensive devices, materials, or labor. The pipeline was named CApEsid biOfilm according to the selected methods: Crystal violet, extracellular ATP, extracellular DNA (stain), and CFU of biofilm grown on stainless steel washers (Fig. 7).

The pipeline begins with overnight bacterial cultures that are diluted and dispensed into 96-well plates containing stainless steel washers, followed by incubation for 24 h in 37 °C. Phages are then added at various multiplicities of infection, along with untreated controls. After additional incubation of 24 h, planktonic bacteria are gently removed, and the wells are washed to retain biofilm-associated cells (Fig. 7).

The biofilm biomass is quantified using 0.1% crystal violet staining, followed by solubilization and absorbance measurement. Metabolic activity is assessed through extracellular ATP quantification using a luciferase-based assay, with luminescence serving as a readout for cell viability. In parallel, extracellular DNA is assessed using TOTO-1 staining to reflect biofilm disruption and phage-induced lysis.

When the full CApEsid biOfilm pipeline was applied to a biofilm model, it averages 70% in Difference from Control, 42.5% in Titer Difference and 87.5 in difference from PASA16. In all four of the selected methods, PASA16 showed the highest activity at the higher concentration of 10⁸ PFU/ml, the titer typically used for clinical treatments. This finding is consistent with its previously observed superiority in planktonic bacteria and its documented successful clinical use. Nevertheless, as the current study was not designed to establish definitive superiority among phages, this result should be interpreted with caution.

The combination of these methods enabled reliable differentiation between untreated controls, varying phage titers, and underperforming phages (Fig. 8a–e). The protocol requires a 96-well plate reader with fluorescence and luminescence detection capabilities, optical density (OD) reading, temperature control, stainless steel washers, and reagents such as TOTO-1 and crystal violet (CV).

Cross-species pipeline validation

To evaluate the versatility of the integrated pipeline, we validated the protocol across a diverse panel of clinically relevant pathogens, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PAO1), Escherichia coli, Enterococcus faecalis, and Staphylococcus aureus. Initial screening confirmed synergistic interactions between selected phage-antibiotic pairings against planktonic cultures (Fig. 8a, Supplemental Table S1). Following biofilm maturation, we applied the CApEsid protocol to quantify the individual and combined effects of phages and antibiotics.

Analysis of eATP revealed significant phage efficacy across all species, with synergistic biofilm eradication observed in P. aeruginosa, E. coli, and S. aureus (Fig. 8b). Regarding eDNA levels, both phage efficacy and phage-antibiotic synergism were consistently demonstrated across all four bacterial species (Fig. 8c). In modified CFU assays, phage effects were prominent in P. aeruginosa and S. aureus, while synergistic reductions occurred in P. aeruginosa and E. faecalis (Fig. 8d). Crystal violet (CV) staining further confirmed phage efficacy and synergy for all tested strains (Fig. 8e). Collectively, these results demonstrate the high sensitivity of this pipeline in detecting phage-antibiotic synergism within complex biofilm architectures.

Discussion

Antibiotic-resistant infections—especially those involving biofilms—remain a critical challenge in clinical practice. While phage therapy has emerged as a promising alternative, its success hinges on robust and standardized methods to evaluate phage efficacy against biofilms, which differ substantially from planktonic cultures.

In this study, we systematically assessed a broad array of methods to quantify phage activity in biofilm models and established three primary criteria to evaluate their performance: (1) ability to distinguish phage-treated samples from untreated controls, (2) dose-dependency with phage titer, and (3) capacity to differentiate between phages, particularly highlighting the superior activity of PASA16. PASA16 efficacy is well established in planktonic bacteria and clinical cases with several published positive outcomes69–73 and just one negative case published74. While this third criterion was not exclusive, it reinforced our final selection of methods, as those chosen consistently addressed all three aspects. These criteria provided a framework for comparing assays in a biofilm context, where conventional planktonic metrics, such as optical density growth curves, proved inadequate due to matrix interference and signal limitations.

Conventional metrics such as optical density (OD) growth curves, though widely used in planktonic studies42,48,49, proved inadequate in biofilms due to signal obstruction from biofilm matrices50. In contrast, isothermal calorimetry emerged as a powerful real-time metabolic tool39,40, showing strong sensitivity and titer responsiveness. However, its need for anaerobic conditions and specialized equipment may limit routine clinical use. Seahorse ECAR analysis, though relevant in eukaryotic cell metabolism56, lacked the resolution and duration required for biofilm monitoring in our study—this is the first reported application of this method for phage-biofilm assessment.

Lysis markers offered a particularly effective approach for evaluating phage efficacy. These included extracellular DNA (eDNA) quantification by qPCR or real-time fluorescence (TOTO-1) and extracellular ATP (eATP) detection58. To distinguish phage-induced lysis from constitutive matrix-associated eDNA, we utilized untreated biofilm controls and established baseline levels at t = 0. This allowed us to specifically quantify the increase in eDNA resulting from phage activity rather than pre-existing matrix components. While qPCR provided high sensitivity, its need for filtration and endpoint sampling limits scalability. TOTO-1 and eATP, in contrast, are rapid, real-time-compatible, and cost-effective methods. Both performed strongly across our defined criteria, with TOTO-1 especially showing consistency in phage discrimination60,61. Given the widespread accessibility of qPCR platforms post-COVID-1975, this method may still serve as a confirmatory tool in critical cases.

The conventional Colony Forming Unit (CFU) method, typically employed post-sonication, proved insufficient in this study of phage-infected P. aeruginosa biofilm, potentially due to the biofilm’s high viscosity and inadequate mechanical separation76,77. CFU, known for inaccurate representation of the fraction of detached live cells76,77. To address mechanical separation challenges, we utilized a biofilm model grown on stainless-steel washers. While this setup demonstrated superior sensitivity and reproducibility in our tests, these results reflect improved assay performance under these specific conditions rather than a general methodological superiority over traditional CFU methods. Additionally, substrate material may independently influence biofilm formation and phage interaction. This may also be achieved using a 96-peg lid plate, which can next be moved to a different medium78. Additionally, in cases involving prosthetic treatment, the same material as the patient’s clinical infection site could be utilized for more targeted analysis. Crystal violet (CV) staining also proved robust, offering endpoint biomass quantification that was sensitive, low-cost, and reproducible, despite its inability to distinguish between live and dead cells43,51,64.

Some techniques showed more limited applicability. Live/Dead staining combined with Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM) is a long-standing method for generating 3D images of biofilms, typically utilizing the membrane-permeable green stain SYTO9 for all cells and the membrane-impermeable red propidium iodide (PI) for those with compromised membranes76,79–81. However, this approach faces significant documented constraints in biofilm systems, including differential dye penetration, competitive binding between the two fluorophores, and potential overestimation of either viable or non-viable fractions depending on biofilm density82. A major limitation is its reliance on membrane integrity alone, which can overlook the metabolic state of the bacteria and lead to false results in the presence of extracellular DNA (eDNA)—a common component of the biofilm matrix that intercalates with these nucleic acid-binding dyes. Studies have shown that SYTO9/PI can significantly underestimate viability in certain species, such as Klebsiella pneumoniae, or fail to accurately reflect changes in phage-infected biofilms where induced lysis releases high levels of eDNA. Consequently, there is a growing shift toward metabolic-based probes, such as the calcein acetoxymethyl (CAM) and TMA-DPH combination, which offers a more precise assessment of functional vitality by targeting intracellular esterase activity rather than simple membrane exclusion83,84. Despite its high-resolution visual appeal, SEM is limited by its high operational costs, low scalability, and its inability to provide objective quantitative measurements76,85,86.

Based on our comparative evaluation, we propose the CApEsid biOfilm pipeline as a clinically relevant, cost-efficient protocol for assessing phage activity in biofilms. This pipeline integrates four complementary methods-crystal violet staining, eATP detection, TOTO-1 eDNA staining, and CFU enumeration on stainless steel washers-each selected for high performance across our three criteria and suitability for routine lab use. This protocol reliably distinguishes between untreated controls, phage titers, and functionally distinct phages (e.g., PASA16).

Importantly, we validated this pipeline across multiple bacterial species (P. aeruginosa, E. coli, S. aureus, E. faecalis) and demonstrated its sensitivity in detecting phage–antibiotic synergy45 The minimal equipment requirement (plate reader, sonicator, basic microbiology tools) makes the protocol accessible to many clinical and research settings.

While the CApEsid biOfilm pipeline offers a robust framework for Clinical Phage Microbiology, several limitations remain. First, our experiments utilized static in vitro models, which lack the dynamic flow and host immune factors present in clinical infection sites. Second, while we validated the pipeline across four key pathogens, these were monomicrobial biofilms; the complexity of polymicrobial clinical environments may alter phage penetration and matrix degradation. Third, the current 24–48 h window may not fully capture the long-term emergence of phage-resistant subpopulations. Finally, although the selected assays effectively measure biomass and lysis, they may still overlook viable but non-culturable (VBNC) cells often found in mature biofilms. Future studies should evaluate the pipeline’s performance under flow conditions and within complex multi-species communities87,88.

In conclusion, the development of effective methods for matching phages to biofilm infections is crucial for advancing phage therapy. Future research should focus on refining and validating promising techniques to overcome current limitations. By leveraging these advancements, we can enhance our ability to tailor phage treatments to specific biofilm infections, ultimately improving therapeutic outcomes.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

P. aeruginosa strain PA14 was used as the primary model microorganism in this study, alongside P. aeruginosa PAO1, Enterococcus faecalis V583, Staphylococcus aureus R1, and Escherichia coli DSM 13,127, all obtained from lab stock. These bacteria were routinely cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB, BD Franklin Lakes, New Jersey) broth or on LB agar plates (1.5% agar) at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for liquid cultures, or Brain Heart Infusion (BHI, BD) for Enterococcus faecalis. For biofilm formation, 180 µL of overnight-grown bacteria were inoculated into each well of a 96-well plate, unless otherwise specified, and then incubated statically at 37 °C for 24 h to allow biofilm development44. Bacterial stocks were maintained at -80 °C in LB broth containing 25% glycerol for long-term storage, while working cultures on LB agar plates were stored at 4 °C for up to one week.

Determination of bacterial concentration

The determination of bacterial concentrations followed the standard Colony Forming Unit (CFU) assay method. Initially, the biofilm underwent washing with a saline solution and subsequent mechanical removal using a sterile tip. The contents of each well were then transferred into a 1.5 ml tube, following a previously established protocol89 The tubes were placed in a SONOREX sonication water bath (Bandelin, Berlin, Germany) for 5 min to effectively disrupt the biofilm. Subsequently, 5 µl of 10-fold serial dilutions were plated on LB agar plates. After 24 h of incubation at 37℃, colonies were counted, and the bacterial concentration was calculated in terms of CFU/ml.

For the modified washers CFU method, biofilm growth occurred on a Standard Flat stainless steel Washer (Fajoeda, Brazil) with these properties, M3 /0.118”; Outer Dia: 7 mm/0.276”; Thickness: 0.5 mm/0.02”, for 24 hours at 37℃ in LB medium. Prior to phage application, the washer was transferred to a well with fresh LB medium. Preceding sonication, the washer underwent a saline wash and was then moved to a 15-ml tube. Subsequent dilutions were carried out from the saline solution, following the same procedure as described above.

Phage used and determination of phage concentration

P. aeruginosa phages (Table 1) isolation was described in different publications35,47. Mostly, strictly lytic phages from our previously published phage panel were selected, as lysogenic phages are unsuitable for phage therapy35,90. We did implement one phage with lysogenic potential to increase variability. Potential for temperate life cycle determined by PhaBOX91. Other phages used were E. faecalis targeting phage EFDG192, and S. aureus targeting phage OMS193 and E. coli targeting phage T794–96. Phages were stored in liquid LB at 4 °C. The phage concentration was determined according to the PFU assay method. Phage suspensions underwent 10-fold serial dilutions. 200 µl of overnight-grown bacteria were put in 4 ml of 0.5% agarose on LB agar plates to make bacterial lawns. 5 µl of each phage dilution was spotted twice on the bacterial lawns. The plates were dried and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. The number of plaques was counted, and the initial concentration of PFU/ml was calculated as described previously97. If not specified otherwise, phages were grown to an initial concentration of 108 PFU/ml.

Antibiotics used

Each antibiotic was used in a final concentration of 1 MIC. For P. aeruginosa, ceftazidime was used at 8 µg/mL, for Enterococcus faecalis, ampicillin at 2 µg/mL, for Staphylococcus aureus, vancomycin at 2 µg/mL, and for Escherichia coli, amikacin at 16 µg/mL. These concentrations align with current CLSI interpretive MIC breakpoints for susceptibility98.

Growth curves of Phage activity on planktonic bacteria or biofilm were evaluated by inoculating logarithmic PA14 (at 107 CFU/ml) or pre-grown biofilm with each unique phage sample. Turbidity, indicative of bacterial growth, was measured using a plate reader (Synergy; BioTek, Winooski, VT) at OD600 nm. The measurements were conducted at 37 °C with 5-second shaking intervals every 20 min over a period of 24 h.

Calorimetry

For this purpose, calScreener® (Symcel, Solna, Sweden) was used, and biofilm was grown in designated activated Symcel-manufactured plates, as instructed by the manufacturer. 20 µl of phages were added, and disposable plastic wells were placed in titanium chambers and sealed using a screwdriver. The heat measurement started after 1 h due to the calScreener® initiation protocol. Data was analyzed using the supplied software.

Extracellular ATP measurement

Biofilm cultures were established using standardized protocols. To each culture, 10 µl of either bacteriophage suspension or 0.9% NaCl (Saline) was added, bringing the final volume to 75 µl. The RealTime-Glo™ Extracellular ATP Assay (Promega, Madison, WI) was then applied according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with 25 µl of the assay reagent added to each sample99 Luminescence measurements were recorded at 20-minute intervals using a Synergy plate reader. This assay allows for real-time monitoring of eATP levels, which can serve as an indicator of bacterial lysis and phage activity58,99 For calibration, eATP amounts, and bacterial lysis was achieved with carbenicillin at 50 µg/ml. Subsequently, the lysate was serially diluted and assessed as described above.

TOTO-1 extracellular DNA measurement

Biofilm was cultured following established procedures, and 20 µl of either phage or 0.9% NaCl was added. The TOTO-1 nucleic acid stain (ThermoFisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was then applied at the concentration described in the text, 1:2500 unless stated otherwise. Fluorescence measurements were recorded at 20-minute intervals using a Synergy plate reader, using a 514 nm excitation and 533 nm emission settings. Carbenicillin was used as described above.

Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR)

For this purpose, the Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) was used with the company-supplied plates. Overnight culture of P. aeruginosa PA14 was grown in the Cell Plate (Agilent) for 24 h. The utility plate was put in a calibrant medium as instructed by the manufacturer. Before treating the biofilm with phages, the biofilm was washed in 0.9% NaCl, and 180 µl of Running DMEM medium (Agilent) was put in the wells according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 20 µl of either treatment was added to the different wells, and the plate was read by the machine every 12 min for 10 h, the maximal period allowed by the manufacturer. Data was exported using Wave (Agilent) to PRISM (GraphPad, San Diego, CA).

qPCR extracellular DNA measurement

Biofilm was cultured following established procedures, and 20 µl of either phage or 0.9% NaCl was added. Quantification of relative extracellular DNA (eDNA) was performed on cell-free supernatants, filtered through a 0.22-µm pore size filter (Merck Millipore Ltd., Ireland). Real-time PCR was employed, utilizing specific primers for the rpoD (ACCGTCGTGGCTACAAATTC, GGCGATCTTCAGTACCTTGC100) and 16 S (CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG, GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC101) genes, as previously described100 The real-time PCR was conducted in a final volume of 25 µl, using the iQ™ SYBR® Green Supermix (Biorad, Hercules, CA) and the CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Biorad). Bacterial lysis was achieved with carbenicillin at 50 µg/ml. Subsequently, eDNA was filtered and quantified using a nanodrop (Thermo Fisher).

Biolight 680

Biofilm was rinsed, and 20 µl of phage treatment was applied as described, together with 20 µl of 10X Biolight 680 dye stock solution (Ebba Biotech AB, Solna, Sweden), diluted according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence was measured by a Synergy plate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT): 540 nm excitation and 680 nm emission for the Biolight 680 stain.

Crystal violet

Biofilm was grown on a 96-well plate and treated as described above. CV (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was used as previously described43, 96-well plates were washed with 0.9% NaCl, then stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (125 µl per well), incubated in ambient conditions for 15 min, and then emptied. Excess dye was rinsed twice in a water bath. The plate was dried at 37 °C for 1 h and extracted with 30% acetic acid (100 µl per well), and the extracted crystal violet was transferred into a new 96-well plate, and its intensity was measured at OD550 nm.

Live/dead stain

Biofilm was grown on a 96-well plate and treated as described above. The 96-well plate was washed and then stained using Live/Dead cell viability kits (Life Technologies, Waltham, MA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The fluorescence emissions of the samples were detected by using a Zeiss LSM 410 confocal laser microscope (Carl Zeiss). To ensure all images were captured within the linear detection range, acquisition parameters including laser power and detector gain were optimized using the brightest control samples and remained constant across all experimental conditions. Red fluorescence was measured at 630 nm, and green fluorescence was measured at 520 nm. Horizontal plane optical sections were made at 5-µm intervals from the surface outward, with the pinhole set to 0.95 Airy Units, and the images were displayed individually. The microscopy slices were combined into a 3D image using Bio-formats and UC± plugins (ImageJ 1.49G). Biofilms were primarily screened at 10× magnification to capture the representative heterogeneity of the community. The stained biofilms were examined using a confocal microscope and analyzed using ImageJ 1.49G software (http://imagej.nih.gov/ij/). %live of total bacteria was calculated as previously described by Robertson et al.54.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Biofilm samples were prepared for SEM using Karnovsky’s fixative (2% PFA, 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, pH = 7.4). Samples in fixative were put for 4 h at room temperature, followed by 1:2 diluted Karnovsky’s fixative overnight at 4◦C. Then, the biofilm samples were post-fixed in 1% OSO4 in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer for 2 h and dehydrated in a graded series of alcohols, followed by Critical Point Drying (CPD). After sputter coating with Pd/Au, biofilm samples were observed by a SEM-trained technician (FEI, Quanta 200; CPD—Quorum Technologies, K850 Critical Point Drier; Sputtering—Quorum Technologies, SC7620 Sputter coater). Each well was treated and visualized in duplicate.

Assessing dye’s bacterial toxicity and phage toxicity

In this assay, the bacterium used was P. aeruginosa PA14, and the phage used was PASA16. Phage or bacteria were incubated overnight with the tested dyes or 0.9% NaCl solution, followed by CFU assay for bacteria and PFU assay for phages.

Statistical analysis

Prism (GraphPad, version 8.0.2 (263), San Diego, CA) was used for the graph formation, and design. The results were analyzed as the mean ± standard deviation of a technical triplicate in each experimental group and were repeated in two independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed in Python (v3.12) using the SciPy library. For time-series datasets (OD600, fluorescence RFU, luminescence RLU, calorimetric heat flow, and ECAR), the area under the curve (AUC) was computed per replicate using the trapezoidal rule. Each treatment vs. untreated control was tested using one-way ANOVA + Dunnett’s test as post hoc analysis, untreated as reference. Titer effect, 10⁶ vs. 10⁸ within each phage, was calculated using unpaired t-test. PASA16 comparison, each phage vs. PASA16 at the same titer was calculated one-way ANOVA + Dunnett’s test as post hoc analysis, PASA16 as reference, run per titer. Summary statistics count only results that are both statistically significant (p < 0.05).
