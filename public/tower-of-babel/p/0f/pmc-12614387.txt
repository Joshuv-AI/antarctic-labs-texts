Longitudinal Synthetic Data Generation by Artificial Intelligence to Accelerate Clinical and Translational Research in Breast Cancer

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEReal-world data (RWD) are critical for breast cancer (BC) research but are limited by privacy concerns, missing information, and data fragmentation. This study explores synthetic data (SD) generated through advanced generative models to address these challenges and create harmonized longitudinal data sets.METHODSA data set of 1052 patients with human epidermal growth factor receptor 2–positive and triple-negative BC from the Informatics for Integrating Biology and the Bedside (i2b2) platform was used. Advanced generative models, including generative adversarial networks (GANs), variational autoencoders (VAEs), and language models (LMs), were applied to generate synthetic longitudinal data sets replicating disease progression, treatment patterns, and clinical outcomes. The Synthethic Validation Framework (SAFE) powered by Train was used to evaluate the fidelity, utility, and privacy. SD were tested across three settings: (1) integration with i2b2 for privacy-preserving data sets; (2) multistate disease modeling to predict clinical outcomes; and (3) generation of synthetic control groups for clinical trials.RESULTSThe synthetic data sets exhibited high fidelity (score 0.94) and ensured privacy, with temporal patterns validated through time-series analyses and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection embeddings. In setting A, SD accurately mirrored RWD on the i2b2 platform while maintaining privacy. In setting B, incorporating SD improved the predictive performance of a multistate disease progression model, increasing the C-index by up to 10%. In setting C, SD replicated the end points of the APT trial, demonstrating its feasibility for generating synthetic control arms with preserved statistical properties of the real data set.CONCLUSIONAI-generated longitudinal SD effectively address key challenges in RWD use in BC. This approach can improve translational research and clinical trial design while ensuring robust privacy protection. Integration with platforms such as i2b2 highlights their scalability and potential for broader applications in oncology.

INTRODUCTION

Breast cancer (BC), the most common malignancy among women worldwide, accounts for approximately 2.3 million new cases each year1 and is the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality.2 With a rapidly evolving therapeutic landscape,3 personalized treatments have become crucial to improving outcomes while reducing side effects.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has been gaining importance in BC, facilitating early diagnosis, disease progression prediction, and the development of personalized treatment strategies.4,5 Real-world data (RWD)6,7 from electronic health records (EHRs) is essential for advancing AI-driven strategies, but challenges such as privacy issues, regulatory constraints, data fragmentation, and missing information restrict its effective use, slowing the development of innovative AI-based health care solutions.8

Synthetic data (SD) offer a promising solution to overcome obstacles in using RWD. Synthetic EHR data, generated by statistically modeling original data sets, replicate the complexity and statistical properties of real data while preserving patient privacy, as they do not directly correlate with individual records. This allows secure data sharing without violating privacy laws.9,10 SD also support privacy preservation, facilitate data sharing, and enhance the development of machine learning (ML) models. Additionally, they augment existing data sets, improving AI model performance and accelerating progress in oncology research,11 supporting hypothesis testing and trial simulations, especially in cases where patient recruitment is difficult. SD present challenges and opportunities across various domains, as explored by Jacobs et al11 in oncology. In this work, we aim to propose and validate technological approaches to address these limitations and challenges and enhance their applicability in the oncologic domain.

This project investigated the use of deep generative models such as generative adversarial networks (GANs), variational autoencoder (VAEs), and transformer-based language models (LMs) to generate longitudinal synthetic BC data as a reliable source for accelerating translational and clinical research. To ensure the robustness of the generated data, the Synthetic Validation Framework powered by Train12 (SAFE) was developed to evaluate longitudinal data across three key perspectives: fidelity, utility, and privacy preservation. The primary objective was to create a valid data set suitable for integration with the Informatics for Integrating Biology and the Bedside (i2b2) platform, to obtain augmented, reliable, and anonymous data. A secondary objective focused on testing the ability of SD to accelerate research by applying a multistate model to predict state transition probabilities over time and replicating a clinical study to further assess its utility.

METHODS

The research proposal received approval from the local Ethics Committee (Independent Ethics Committee IRCCS Istituto Clinico Humanitas, protocol number ONC/OSS-08/2022). All the study procedures were compliant with the 2021 WHO guidance on ethics and governance of AI for health.13

i2b2 Platform and Identification of the Study Cohort

The Papa Giovanni XXIII Hospital (HPG23) in Bergamo, together with the University of Milano-Bicocca in Milan, have started an information technology initiative to support clinical research in oncology, grounds on the software developed by the i2b2 National Institutes of Health project.14-17 More information on the i2b2 platform is provided in the Data Supplement.

Study Population

The study included 1,052 female patients age 18 years or older, diagnosed with malignant neoplasm of the female breast (ICD-9 code 17418), who were admitted between 2008 and 2019. Patients were selected from the i2b2 platform at ASST-PG23, integrating structured data and unstructured clinical reports processed via natural language processing. Inclusion criteria were age >18 years, a confirmed diagnosis of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)–positive or triple-negative BC (TNBC), and at least two clinical visits. Patients with incomplete or conflicting receptor status, fewer visits, or diagnostic codes not corresponding to invasive BC were excluded. As previously reported, this population was selected to focus on two aggressive clinically and biologically distinct subgroups, HER2-positive and TNBC, which allow for more accurate longitudinal reconstruction of treatment and disease trajectories. Demographic characteristics, tumor biology, procedures, treatments, and outcome events were extracted with exact time stamps. The median age at enrollment was 56 years, with 58.8% of patients being HER2-positive and 41.2% having TNBC. The median follow-up was 6.2 years within a 10-year observation window. Generative Models for Longitudinal SD

We selected three prominent families of generative models: GANs, VAEs, and transformer-based LMs, widely adopted in recent literature for synthetic longitudinal EHR generation, because of their ability to capture complex dependencies and high-dimensional structures in health care data. These models have shown promising results in comparative studies and surveys on synthetic EHR generation.20-23

We applied Multi-label Time-series GAN24 and Dual Adversarial Autoencoder25 (DAAE), the EHR variational autoencoder25 model, LMs such as Hierarchical Autoregressive Language model27 and the generative pretrained transformer28 model, trained on our data set from scratch without pretrained weights, and the pretrained open-weight large language models (LLMs) Llama-3.1-8B29 and Mistral-7B.30

Models were used to generate EHRs with discrete variables. Subsequently, the temporal component was incorporated into the top-performing models, LLMs, and DAAE. Mistral emerged as the best overall model and was used for further analyses. Details on model selection criteria, training, and implementation are provided in the Data Supplement.

Development of SAFE, a Synthetic Validation Framework for Longitudinal Data

SAFE was developed to assess the fidelity, correlation, privacy, and utility in generated longitudinal synthetic data sets.23,27,31,32

SAFE evaluates fidelity using metrics that compare distributions of discrete and continuous variables overall and per visit. Overall metrics include root mean squared error (RMSE), correlation coefficient (CC), coefficient of determination (R2) for discrete temporal event probabilities, and maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) for continuous variables. Per-visit fidelity uses total variation distance (TVD) for discrete variables and Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test for continuous variables. Temporal coherence of the longitudinal time series is assessed via symmetric mean absolute percentage error (SMAPE), Granger causality, and correlation across visits. Visualization through Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embeddings compares synthetic and real data.

A correlation metric captures consistency between correlations in real and synthetic time series data by combining the mean absolute difference of Pearson correlation matrices (μP) and correlation accuracy (CorAcc).32

SAFE ensures privacy using a membership inference attack, where real records are classified as in training or not in training. If accuracy, precision, and recall are close to 0.5 (random guessing), it indicates strong privacy protection.

To ensure that the SD are clinically useful and reliable for research analyses, the framework compares the Kaplan-Meier survival curves using the log-rank test. In addition, a clinical validation score is calculated on the basis of the percentages of patients who do not adhere to the constraints identified in collaboration with the clinicians. These rules are based on the compatibility between tumor characteristics, procedures, and treatments.

A final total score given by the average of fidelity, correlation, privacy, and utility is used to select the best generated data set. Details are provided in the Data Supplement.

Multistate Disease Progression Model

A multistate deep learning model was developed to predict transition probabilities between disease states over time in patients with BC. Four clinically defined disease states were modeled: early-stage disease (TNM staging at diagnosis with no previous events), recurrence (locoregional event after initial disease-free interval), metastasis (ICD-9 codes for secondary neoplasms, metastatic lesions annotated in oncologic reports or advanced-line treatments), and death (confirmed from registry or discharge records).

The model used to predict transition probabilities from an initial disease state was the DeepHit33 model. DeepHit uses a deep neural network to learn directly the estimated joint distribution of survival time and event, capturing complex, nonlinear covariate-risk relationships over time without strict parametric assumptions. Time is discretized on a fixed grid, assuming at most one event per transition, in line with competing risk settings. DeepHit adapts to time-varying risks and manages competing events effectively.

The performance of the model was evaluated using time-dependent concordance index34 Ctd. Covariates included age, therapies, procedures, and disease characteristics.

Experimental Setup

This study evaluated the utility of the SD across three different settings (Fig 1). The real data set used for model training was extracted from the i2b2 platform.

Setting A: Integration With i2b2

This setting aimed to demonstrate that generative AI models can produce a reliable, privacy-preserving longitudinal synthetic data set suitable for integration with clinical research systems on the basis of structured longitudinal data, such as the i2b2 platform. This integration would enable the use of augmented, anonymized data in clinical research, facilitating the analysis and utilization of clinical data for research purposes.

Setting B: Enhancing Multistate Disease Progression Model

The second setting tested SD's effectiveness in improving the performance and robustness of a multistate disease progression model. The DeepHit model was used to predict the probability of transitions to disease states from an initial state. The model was trained using the real data set, the synthetic data set, the augmented synthetic data set, and then a combination of both the real and augmented synthetic data sets. Performances are compared to measure the improvement with the inclusion of SD.

Setting C: Accelerating Clinical Research by Generating Synthetic Control Arms

To explore SD's potential in developing longitudinal artificial control arms for clinical trials, the phase II, single-arm APT trial35 was replicated and extended with a synthetic control arm.

Patients with HER2-positive, node-negative early BC were selected from a real-world i2b2 cohort of 1,052 patients to generate two synthetic cohorts (n = 406 each) treated with taxane/trastuzumab regimens, with or without anthracyclines.

The first cohort (without anthracyclines) replicated APT trial end points using Kaplan-Meier analysis for disease-free survival (DFS), while the second served as a synthetic control arm, strengthening the original trial's conclusions.

Setting A: Creating Reliable and Privacy-Preserving Longitudinal Synthetic Data Set for i2b2 Integration

Data Preparation

After preprocessing, the data set included 1,046 patients, excluding those with fewer than two visits. The data set was split into training (80%) and testing (20%), with an additional split within the training set for validation (90%-10%). SD was generated with a size equal to the training data set (752 patients), while the test data set was reserved for evaluating privacy and utility metrics.

Fidelity

The fidelity of SD was assessed by SAFE. Probability tests of unigrams and bigrams conducted across visits, as shown in scatterplots (Figs 2A and 2B), demonstrated a close alignment between the real and synthetic data sets, indicating strong fidelity in modeling intervisit and intravisit dependencies. For continuous data, the MMD score for the time variable (time since diagnosis) was 0.983, confirming good agreement.

Correlation of Information Between Real and Synthetic Data

The framework calculates correlations between variables and visits using the Pearson pairwise correlation matrix for real and synthetic data sets, along with metrics such as mean absolute difference μP and CorAcc. The synthetic data set achieved a CorAcc of 0.823, a μP of 0.978, and an overall metric of 0.9, demonstrating effective capture of temporal dynamics and correlations. Figure 2E shows the Pearson correlation matrices across six visits, demonstrating that the synthetic data set effectively mirrors the real data set's correlation pattern among variables.

Privacy

Privacy of SD was assessed through a data set attack, which demonstrated that the training data could not be recovered or identified from the SD, preserving patient anonymity (Fig 3B).

Clinical Validation

Clinical validation was assessed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves. The log-rank test comparing the survival curves yielded a P value of .773, showing no statistically significant difference in survival (Fig 3C). A clinical validation score, on the basis of adherence to clinician-defined rules for tumor characteristics, procedures, and treatments, was 0.932, demonstrating that SD closely mirrored real patients effectively while adhering to clinical constraints. Graphical comparison of treatment sequences showed realistic privacy-preserving synthetic patients, who were not direct copies of real patients (Fig 3D).

The Mistral model, with only 7B parameters, successfully generated complete, reliable, and privacy-friendly synthetic patient data. This approach is easily extensible in different clinical contexts for the generation of longitudinal data. This implies easy integration into a system such as i2b2 to produce scalable, anonymized data sets for analysis and research.

Multistate Model

The DeepHit model was used to predict transition probabilities between disease states. The concordance index (C-index) was calculated for transitions between early-stage, recurrent, and metastatic states at key time intervals. The model was first trained on real data, then on SD of equal size, followed by a combined data set three times larger (one-third real, two-thirds synthetic), and finally on SD only, expanded to three times the original size.

The same real test data, reserved during both the DeepHit training and SD generation process, was used for evaluation in these scenarios. The generated data were evaluated using SAFE, resulting in a score of 0.926.

Performance Results

The C-indexes obtained in the various settings are shown in Figure 4B, with the respective numbers of real training data. Results showed an improved performance, especially in cases where real training data were limited. SD effectively enhanced the predictive capabilities of the DeepHit model, leading to an improvement of 10.28% in the C-index in the recurrent case. Transition probability curves for disease states are shown in Figure 4C and illustrate the results for an initial configuration of tumor characteristics, therapies, and procedures. The curves obtained from the model trained with SD, augmented to three times the size of the real data set, are comparable to or more detailed than those derived from real data.

Setting C: Accelerating Clinical Research and Conduction of Clinical Trials by Using SD

From the i2b2 real world platform data set, 81 patients with pT1, HER2-positive, node-negative early BC were identified. Among these, 49 patients received adjuvant treatment with taxane-/trastuzumab-based therapy, while 32 received a combination of taxane/trastuzumab and anthracyclines. On the basis of these cases, two cohorts of 406 synthetic patients were generated, to match the number used in the original phase II, single-arm, APT trial study. SAFE was applied to evaluate the generated data set, resulting in a total score of 0.929, which reflects strong fidelity, correlation, privacy, utility, and clinical relevance. DFS for the two synthetic cohorts was estimated using Kaplan-Meier survival curves.

Figure 5A shows the DFS curve derived from the synthetic data set of the first cohort, treated with taxanes and trastuzumab, compared with that of the APT trial. The curve created with the SD reflects the one of the original study, demonstrating their potential for clinical applicability. Furthermore, the synthetic data set offered more reliable estimates than the limited real data (49 patients), reducing confidence intervals by an average of 72%. Figure 5B highlights the synthetic data set's replication of clinical end points from the original study.

In a second step, DFS curves were generated using the second synthetic cohort (taxane/trastuzumab and anthracyclines), resulting in a reduction of confidence intervals widths by an average of 77% compared with those from RWD. A DFS curve was then constructed by combining data from the 32 real patients and 374 synthetic patients from the second synthetic cohort, yielding a total sample size of 406 patients. The resulting curve was compared with that of the original APT study in Figure 5C. This approach provided, for the first time, a synthetic control arm to the phase 2, single-arm APT trial. The comparability of the curves in the figure further validates the study's findings on the efficacy of the anthracycline-free regimen for HER2-positive, node-negative, early BC.

DISCUSSION

This study shows AI-generated longitudinal SD can replicate key statistical properties and tackle challenges in oncology research, including missing data, class imbalance, harmonization, and privacy in RWD. Using GANs, VAEs, and LMs, SD reproduced complex clinical patterns, survival outcomes, and treatment effects while preserving privacy.

We introduce the SAFE framework developed to evaluate synthetic longitudinal data. SAFE integrates and extends methods from existing frameworks for SD evaluation,36-38 aiming to address key limitations in their application to longitudinal and domain-specific clinical contexts. In addition to standard statistical indicators, SAFE includes a clinical validation approach, making it suitable for health care contexts. It also leverages state-of-the-art statistical and ML libraries,39-43 ensuring a comprehensive evaluation pipeline.

The SAFE framework effectively assessed fidelity, utility, and privacy. SD accurately reproduced temporal patterns and clinical correlations, ensuring suitability for advanced modeling. Privacy metrics confirmed strong protection, while Kaplan-Meier demonstrated the SD's ability to predict survival outcomes, highlighting clinical utility when RWD are limited.

The integration of SD with the i2b2 platform validated its potential to support real-world research applications. i2b2 has proven useful in reconstructing care patterns for patients with BC, identifying treatment trajectories for HER2-positive and TNBC cohorts, and calculating survival and time-to-treatment-change metrics with minimal manual input.19 Building on this foundation, our study showed that SD could integrate into the i2b2 system, while ensuring compatibility with other clinical research platforms that use structured longitudinal data, providing augmented, privacy-preserving data sets for longitudinal research.

A notable example illustrating the utility of SD was its replication of the APT trial by Tolaney et al,35 which established adjuvant paclitaxel with trastuzumab as a treatment recommended by international guidelines.44-46 Using synthetic cohorts generated from RWD, we replicated end points such as DFS and treatment efficacy, and simulated a missing control arm for comparative analysis. This illustrates SD's potential to support early-phase trials and, in phase III, reduce reliance on real control arms, offering a cost-effective, time-efficient, ethical alternative that eases recruitment.

Beyond clinical trials, SD also enhanced translational research, by augmenting real data sets to improve predictive model performance. The addition of SD in this study improved accuracy of a multistate model forecasting disease progression, particularly for rare events such as disease recurrence, resulting in a C-index increase of over 10%.

However, challenges remain. Current generative models struggle to replicate rare events, such as long-term treatment responses or late-onset toxicities, because of their scarcity in RWD. Addressing this limitation requires refining model architectures to better capture low-frequency occurrences. Recent advances, such as conditional matrices in generative models and fine-tuning of pretrained models, have partially improved rare clinical event modeling. However, data needs still depend on task complexity, making rare event prediction difficult in detailed, time-sensitive settings. A major concern remains bias from training data, which can worsen disparities and affect fairness and generalizability. These challenges underscore the need for careful, context-aware use and ongoing method refinement.

Temporal fidelity, especially for long-term disease progression and treatment outcomes, is another hurdle. Addressing these challenges involves choosing and refining generative models that capture long-range temporal dependencies, alongside strong evaluation methods focused on temporal accuracy and clinical relevance. Incorporating diverse external data sets can improve training by providing more representative longitudinal records. External validation cohorts are key to testing generalizability across populations and time, while prospective studies help confirm real-world predictive value. Hybrid generative approaches and integration of external genomic or molecular data can enhance SD reliability and expand their application in precision oncology.47

SD hold significant potential for real-world impact, particularly in regulatory evaluation, clinical trial design, and the development of clinical guidelines. They can support health technology assessments, enable privacy-preserving in silico trials, and enrich data sets for underrepresented populations or rare conditions.

SD have been widely implemented in the clinical context of rare hematologic malignancies, addressing unmet needs through tailored generative models and validation frameworks.10 Recently, this approach has been extended to other oncologic areas (eg, breast, lung, and colorectal) and nononcologic domains (eg, neurology, rheumatology, and metabolic diseases), highlighting the scalability and adaptability of the technology.

However, strengths and limitations of SD vary across domains. In rare and complex diseases, they can mitigate data scarcity but require complex modeling. In common tumors, they may enhance robustness but face harmonization issues because of heterogeneous data sources, coding systems, and time scales.11 Neurologic and rheumatologic diseases may benefit from trajectory simulation, although irregular and sparse follow-up data pose challenges.48 In metabolic diseases, standardized numerical measures support generation, but high interpatient variability and underrepresented subgroups can introduce bias. These observations underscore the need for disease-specific modeling and validation strategies.

An important signal of the growing relevance of this technology is the emergence of dedicated initiatives such as the European SYNTHIA49 project under the Innovative Health Initiative,50 which actively investigates these applications, aiming to demonstrate how SD can accelerate evidence generation and improve clinical practice.

In conclusion, this study highlights the transformative potential of AI-generated SD in oncology research. By addressing key limitations of RWD, such as privacy concerns and data scarcity, SD can accelerate precision medicine and improve the efficiency of clinical trials. Integration with platforms such as i2b2 offers a scalable solution for leveraging real-world evidence while preserving patient privacy, promoting innovative research, and improving patient outcomes.
