Selective preservation of shear-dependent platelet adhesion during microvolume storage in a compact agitation system
Authors: Tiana Pelaia, Yiyao C. Chen, Alexander Dupuy, Ivy Guan, Jianfang Ren, Laura Moldovan, Lautaro Pons, Andreas Ludwig, Xuyu Liu, Mike C.L. Wu, Lining A. Ju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlatelet-based applications relying on fresh samples create logistical constraints in research workflows. While the platelet storage lesion (PSL) is well described, its impact on biomechanical platelet function remains unclear, and existing systems are poorly suited to lab-based assays. The digital storage device (DSD) is a compact device designed for room temperature (20-24 °C) microvolume (600 μL) platelet storage under gentle, oscillatory agitation.ObjectivesTo assess the capacity for the DSD to preserve platelet biomechanical (shear-dependent) and biochemical functions.MethodsPlatelet-rich plasma (PRP) and washed platelets (WP) were stored for up to 7 days in the DSD or statically at 2 to 6 °C. Shear-dependent platelet adhesion to von Willebrand factor was assessed using a microfluidic model. Biochemical responses were evaluated by TRAP-6–induced PAC-1 activation and ADP-induced aggregation. PSL markers were also measured.ResultsShear-driven adhesion was maintained for 3 days in the DSD (WP: 93% of baseline at day 2, 97% day 3; PRP: 83% day 2, and 74% day 3) and remained GPIb-dependent. Biochemical responsiveness declined, particularly in PRP, where TRAP-6 responses decreased from 52% to 12% after 2 days and ADP aggregation from 60% to 20%. WP retained 84% to 89% TRAP-6 responsiveness over 7 days under microscopy. PSL markers increased alongside progressive glucose consumption.ConclusionThe DSD supports shear-dependent platelet adhesion for 3 days of storage in both PRP and WP. In contrast, biochemical responsiveness is not maintained. This divergence highlights that distinct platelet functions are differentially affected during storage, enabling targeted interrogation of biomechanical functions.

Introduction

Platelets play a central role in hemostasis and thrombosis through both biochemical signaling and biomechanical interactions under flow [1]. Advances in platelet-based assays, including flow cytometry, aggregometry, and microfluidics, now enable interrogation of platelet function using minimal sample volumes [2,3]. In particular, microfluidic systems have highlighted shear-dependent platelet adhesion mediated by the von Willebrand factor (VWF)–GPIb axis as a critical early biomechanical step in thrombus formation [4] and enabled screening of shear-sensitive antithrombotic strategies under defined flow conditions [2].

However, these approaches rely almost exclusively on fresh samples, necessitating collection and processing within restricted timeframes that are not always feasible [5]. In preclinical settings, maintaining an accessible donor pool for the consistent availability of fresh samples poses significant logistical challenges [6]. Platelets kept at physiological temperatures (37 °C) rapidly lose function within 24 hours [7], underscoring the need for storage strategies that preserve platelet functionality for in vitro experimentation.

Transfusion research has identified the platelet storage lesion (PSL) as a fundamental limitation [8]. PSL encompasses metabolic and structural changes during storage, including receptor shedding, microparticle formation, oxidative stress, and morphologic alterations, leading to impaired responsiveness to biochemical agonists [8,9]. Storage conditions influence these outcomes [10], with current transfusion standards relying on room temperature (RT) storage (22 ± 2 °C) with agitation for 5 to 7 days [11]. Cold storage (2-6 °C) without agitation remains of strong clinical interest [12] but is associated with functional alterations and accelerated clearance linked to a procoagulant phenotype [13].

Despite these advances, the application of platelet storage principles into experimental workflows remains limited. This reflects unresolved PSL challenges and the incompatibility of conventional platelet storage systems with laboratory-scale, microvolume assays [14]. Accordingly, accessible laboratory conditions such as refrigeration (2-6 °C) provide pragmatic comparators to examine storage-associated functional changes [15].

Critically, the effect of storage on biomechanical platelet behavior remains largely unexplored, despite increasing reliance on microfluidic models [16]. Given that shear-driven platelet adhesion represents a distinct functional axis that may not parallel biochemical agonist responses [2,4], these components may be differentially affected during storage. Laboratory-scale storage solutions that enable interrogation of defined platelet functions within a controlled time window are therefore needed.

The digital storage device (DSD) (Pplus Medical Limited) is a compact electromechanical device designed to store microvolume platelet suspensions under gentle agitation at RT. Rather than replacing fresh platelets, we assess whether specific platelet functions can be preserved over a defined short-term storage window to support experimental workflows when immediate processing is not feasible.

Methods

Blood collection and platelet-rich plasma preparation

Human blood collection was approved by the University of Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC, 2023/HE000582), with written informed consent from all donors. Platelet samples were obtained from healthy volunteer donors using a convenience sampling approach. Whole blood for platelet-rich plasma (PRP) was collected via venipuncture into 3.8% sodium citrate, and blood for platelet washing was collected into acid citrate dextrose (ACD, 85 mM sodium citrate, 72.9 mM citric acid anhydrous, 110 mM d-glucose, and 70 mM theophylline, pH 4.6). After resting for 25 min at 37 °C, citrate-anticoagulated whole blood was centrifuged at 200 g for 10 min without brake and PRP supernatant was collected.

Platelet washing and isolation

ACD-anticoagulated whole blood was supplemented with 20 U/mL clexane and 0.005 U/mL apyrase and rested for 20 min at 37 °C, followed by centrifuging at 200 g for 10 min without brake. PRP was extracted, rested for 10 min at 37 °C, and centrifuged at 1700 g for 5 min. The platelet pellet was washed in platelet washing buffer (4.3 mM K2HPO4, 4.3 mM Na2HPO4, 24.3 mM NaH2PO4, 113 mM NaCl, 5.5 mM D-glucose, 10 mM theophylline and 1% BSA, 0.01 U/mL apyrase, pH 6.5), at 1500 g for 5 min, then resuspended in Tyrode’s buffer (134 mM NaCl, 12 mM NaHCO3, 2.9 mM KCl, 0.34 mM NaH2PO4, 5 mM HEPES, and 5 mM glucose, 0.02 U/mL apyrase, pH 7.4) [17].

Platelet storage

PRP and WP were divided into fresh (day 0), cold-stored platelets (CSP), and DSD-stored conditions. Fresh samples were stored at 37 °C for 30 min before experiments and used within 4 hours post isolation. To assess next-day viability under standard physiological incubation conditions, a subset of fresh samples was held statically at 37 °C overnight and assayed the following day (day 1). CSP (600 μL aliquots) were held at 2 to 6 °C without agitation. DSD and CSP-stored platelets were assayed on days 2, 3, 5, and 7. Before experimentation, samples were recalcified with 1 mM CaCl2.

DSD storage

The DSD (Pplus Medical Limited) is a portable, benchtop electromechanical system operating at 20 to 24 °C. Samples (600 μL) were stored in sealed, sterile polypropylene ampoules with injectable caps. Ampoules were placed in a circular holder accommodating up to 14 positions and subjected to continuous oscillatory agitation (90 oscillations per minute, ∼5 mm amplitude), ensuring comparable motion and mixing across all positions. The device operates at ambient temperature and includes an internal temperature sensor with automatic shutdown if outside the specified range.

Routine markers of platelet quality

PRP and WP were assessed visually for swirl by pooling in Falcon 15 mL high-clarity polypropylene tubes, held at a 45° angle against a uniform light source and gently agitated using a wide-bore pipette. Swirling was graded on a modified 0 to 2 scale (0 [no swirl] = homogeneous turbidity, 1 [poor] = faint swirl, 2 [clear] = homogeneous swirling with strong shimmer) [18]. Platelet counts were measured using a Sysmex XN-450 analyzer. pH was measured in WP at 22 °C using a compact pH meter (Horiba LAQUAtwin), and glucose concentration was measured in PRP using a glucose monitor (Cardiocheck PA Analyser). For flow cytometry, P-selectin (CD62P-AF647, BioLegend 304918) and Annexin-V (Annexin-V-AF647, BD Biosciences, 567536) were added (1:100) to PRP and WP diluted to 1 × 107 platelets/mL (100 μL total volume) and incubated for 10 min. Reactions were stopped by adding 300 μL Tyrode’s buffer, and samples were analyzed immediately on the BD Accuri C6.

Platelet activity

PRP and WP were diluted to 1 × 107/mL at 100 μL for flow cytometric (PRP and WP) and fluorescence microscopy (WP) analyses. For flow cytometry, PAC-1 FITC (1:100, BD Biosciences, 340507) was added to PRP and WP before stimulation with 50 μM TRAP-6 (Abcam, ab120801) for 10 min. For microscopy experiments, WP were activated with 10 μM TRAP-6 for 10 min in the presence of PAC-1-Alexa647 (1:200, BioLegend, 362806). Reactions were stopped by adding 300 μL Tyrode’s buffer, after which samples were analyzed immediately on the BD Accuri C6. Platelet activation was quantified as the percentage of PAC-1–positive platelets within the gated platelet population (flow cytometry) [19], or as the percentage of PAC-1–positive platelets visualized within the field of view using differential image contrast to define total platelet counts on the Olympus IX83, 40 ×. Platelet aggregation

Platelet aggregation was performed on 300 μL PRP stimulated with 10 μM ADP as previously described [17]. The extent of platelet aggregation was monitored using an automated AggRAM analyzer (Helena Laboratories).

Platelet adhesion under flow conditions

Microfluidic assays were performed using PDMS channels containing cylindrical posts (D = 80 μm; post height = 20 μm; channel width 200 μm; height 70 μm) as previously described and validated [[19], [20], [21]]. Devices were coated with the same lot recombinant VWF (rVWF, Sino Biological #10973-H08C, 50 μg mL-1) for 1 hour at 37 °C and perfused with PRP or WP at 1000 s-1 for 5 min. Platelet adhesion on the first post was imaged by DIC microscopy (Olympus IX81, 40 × oil). Platelet coverage on the post over the 5-minute perfusion was quantified using the ImageJ freehand selection tool to measure the area (μm2) of adhered platelets. For GPIbα blocking, blood was pre-incubated with ALMA12 (25 μg mL−1) for 30 min at 37 °C.

GPIbα shedding ELISA

Soluble GPIbα levels in platelet-free supernatants of WP samples were quantified using a commercially available human GPIbα sandwich ELISA kit (Invitrogen, EH91RB) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a PHERAstar microplate reader (BMG LABTECH), and concentrations were calculated from a four-parameter logistic standard curve.

Statistical analysis

All experiments used samples from at least 3 independent donors. Analyses were performed in a paired manner within donors across storage conditions (DSD, CSP, and 37 °C incubation), with each donor including a baseline (day 0) measurement and at least one additional storage time point. The number of time points and assays per donor varied depending on platelet yield and assay-specific volume requirements. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. Levene’s test was applied to assess variance across storage days and to avoid interpreting nonsignificant differences as stability in the presence of increased variability. Comparisons between storage conditions and storage duration were evaluated using repeated measures two-way ANOVA, with a mixed-effects model applied when datasets contained missing values. Paired t-tests were used for comparisons between fresh and overnight (37 °C) samples. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

Under perfusion at 1000 s-1, PRP and WP stored in the DSD retained >70% of baseline rVWF adhesion at days 2 and 3 (Figure 1B–D, F–G) [day 3 WP: 97% (Figure 1C), PRP: 74% (Figure 1G)]. CSP also showed no significant difference from baseline at day 2 but exhibited increased adhesion relative to DSD, accompanied by spherical morphology in both WP (Figure 1B–C, E, J) and PRP (Figure 1F–G, I, K). By day 3, adhesion was significantly increased in cold-stored WP compared to fresh samples (Figure 1B–E). Enhanced adhesion with CSP has also been reported in shear-induced platelet aggregation, whereas ristocetin-induced platelet aggregation (RIPA) is reduced [22], underscoring the contribution of shear-dependent mechanisms. However, this reflects a less controlled phenotype relative to baseline, suggesting that the DSD provides a more stable platform to interrogate shear-dependent adhesion within this early window. By day 5, adhesion declined in both DSD and CSP conditions (Figure 1B, F), indicating a subsequent loss of shear-dependent function. PRP kept at 37 °C overnight displayed markedly reduced adhesion the following day (Figure 1F) whereas WP are partially protected (Figure 1B). This aligns with heightened basal activation and storage-associated priming during conventional plasma-based platelet storage, whereas the absence of plasma components in WP may delay these effects at 37 °C [23]. At later time points, WP showed a more pronounced decline in adhesion (Figure 1B) than PRP (Figure 1F), potentially due to the presence of plasma components that support platelet metabolism and receptor function during later storage [24]. This late loss of adhesion by day 5 is more consistent with platelet-intrinsic receptor dysfunction rather than depletion of plasma constituents alone [25].

Having defined this early window, we examined whether adhesion remained mediated by the VWF–GPIbα axis (Figure 2). GPIbα inhibition with ALMA12 reduced adhesion by 79–96% across all conditions and time points, with no loss of inhibitory efficacy over 3 days in WP (Figure 2B–D) or PRP (Figure 2E–G). This confirms that preserved adhesion remains GPIbα-dependent throughout this window and enables targeted interrogation of this pathway during storage. Soluble GPIbα ELISA demonstrated progressive receptor shedding during storage (Figure 2A). Notably, GPIbα release remained low during days 2 and 3 in DSD-stored platelets, corresponding to the period in which shear-dependent adhesion was largely preserved. By day 5, soluble GPIbα levels increased approximately 11-fold relative to fresh controls, coinciding with the reduction in adhesive function, although this increase did not reach statistical significance. Together, these findings suggest that progressive GPIbα shedding may contribute to the eventual loss of adhesion observed during prolonged DSD storage. Consistent with prior studies from conventional platelet storage, where progressive reductions in RIPA are associated with GPIbα loss [25], our microfluidic model provides a complementary shear-based assessment of VWF–GPIbα interactions. While biochemical readouts such as RIPA decline with PSL progression [22,25], initial shear-driven tethering to VWF remains detectable over a shorter early-storage window. Together, these findings indicate that the DSD holds potential to expedite preliminary screening of VWF–GPIbα–targeted thrombotic strategies under flow during an early storage window.

Interestingly, CSP exhibited significantly greater GPIbα shedding than DSD at all storage timepoints (Figure 2A) despite demonstrating enhanced adhesion during early storage. This apparent divergence may reflect cold-induced GPIbα clustering and desialylation, which increase receptor avidity for VWF under shear conditions [22]. While not directly measured, this interpretation is supported by complete abolition of the CSP hyper-adhesive phenotype following ALMA12 inhibition (Figure 2D, G–I). Additional evidence of a broader cold-induced platelet priming phenotype is presented below. Collectively, these findings suggest that early CSP hyper-adhesion reflects enhanced GPIbα-dependent adhesion within a broader activated platelet state, whereas progressive receptor shedding may contribute to the subsequent decline in adhesive function.

To assess biochemical responsiveness of the DSD-stored platelets with single-platelet resolution, stored WP was activated with TRAP-6, and activated platelets were observed using PAC-1-Alexa647 under fluorescence microscopy (Figure 3A). Unstimulated WP showed minimal PAC-1 abundance across storage days (Figure 3B, D), consistent with the transient and reversible nature of αIIbβ3 activation [26]. Following 10 μM TRAP-6 stimulation, WP maintained robust activation (80%-95%) over 7 days (Figure 3C, E). Flow cytometry showed lower and more variable absolute PAC-1 levels without clear storage-dependent decline (Figure 3G–H), likely reflecting the transient nature of αIIbβ3 activation in suspension vs its stabilization in static, surface-based imaging [27]. In PRP, biochemical responsiveness showed a distinct profile. Following 50 μM TRAP-6 stimulation, responses declined after 2 days in both storage conditions (Figure 3J–K). This result likely reflects a multifactorial process, where the divergence from WP suggests contributions from a combination of plasma-mediated conditioning and platelet-intrinsic dysfunction [23]. Reduced PAC-1 is consistent with oxidative stress–mediated impairment of αIIbβ3 signaling, including β3 cleavage described during blood transfusion storage [26].

ADP-induced aggregation followed similar trends in PRP, showing reduced aggregation on day 2 under both storage conditions (Figure 3L–M), typically characterized by the loss of the secondary wave (Figure 3N), consistent with storage-induced ADP release and subsequent P2Y1/P2Y12 desensitization under clinical storage conditions [28]. Despite this decline, PRP stored in the DSD retained a measurable ADP-induced aggregation response at day 2 across all samples, whereas PRP held statically overnight at 37 °C showed near-complete loss (Figure 3L). While biochemical responsiveness was better maintained in WP, the early deterioration in PRP mirrors canonical features of the PSL.

Together, these findings demonstrate a divergence between preserved biomechanical adhesion and declining biochemical responsiveness during storage. Given the sustained inhibitory efficacy of ALMA12 and the minimal GPIbα shedding observed during the early storage window, the preserved adhesive phenotype appears largely attributable to retention of the GPIbα–VWF tethering axis rather than broad preservation of platelet function. Notably, the biochemical readouts assessed reflect later-stage, activation-dependent stabilization processes, which appear more sensitive to storage-associated dysfunction than initial shear-driven tethering. This suggests that platelet storage suitability is assay-dependent, particularly in PRP, with mechanically driven responses persisting longer.

Finally, routine quality control parameters were monitored throughout DSD and CSP storage as global indicators of platelet viability (Figure 4A). Platelet counts remained stable in DSD-stored PRP through day 2, contrasting with the substantial reductions seen overnight at 37 °C (Figure 4E–F). By days 5 and 7, counts in DSD PRP decreased but remained significantly higher than CSP-stored PRP, which declined sharply by day 2 (Figure 4E–F). Platelet counts in WP stored in Tyrode’s buffer remained stable through day 2 but exhibited variability across days 5 and 7 (Figure 4C–D), likely reflecting progressive instability in the absence of metabolic support in Tyrode’s buffer that may drive microparticle formation [29,30]. Swirling behavior mirrored these trends. PRP and WP stored in the DSD retained strong swirling at day 2, with progressive decline thereafter (Figure 4B). CSP samples lost swirling by day 2, consistent with a morphologic transition to spherical platelets [31].

To further characterize storage-associated changes, pH remained within 6.9 to 7.8 over 5 days of DSD storage in WP, while CSP showed a significant increase (>7.8) at later time points (Figure 4K). Glucose levels progressively declined during PRP storage, with greater glucose consumption observed in DSD samples, resulting in near-complete depletion by day 5 (Figure 4L). Irrespective of storage condition, P-selectin expression significantly increased after day 2 in both WP (Figure 4G) and PRP (Figure 4I), reflecting rapid α-granule release. Annexin-V binding increased in PRP (Figure 4J), while remaining relatively stable in DSD-stored WP after 2 days (Figure 4H), indicating greater early biochemical dysfunction in PRP that parallels with diminished PAC-1 responsiveness. Together, these findings show that PSL development remains inevitable and limits the use of stored platelets for studies requiring fully resting behavior. However, within an early storage window, platelets may remain suitable for defined applications, including microfluidic interrogation of shear-dependent behavior and GPIb–VWF–mediated interactions, even in the presence of progressive biochemical deterioration.

This study has several limitations. Sample sizes were small and variable across endpoints. A more comprehensive PSL panel (eg, lactate, microparticles, or direct assessment of GPIbα clustering and surface expression) would provide additional mechanistic insight and assist future optimization of storage conditions. Benchmarking against standard blood-bank RT storage was not performed, limiting comparison to transfusion systems. Importantly, biochemical responsiveness in PRP declined early with progressive PSL accumulation, highlighting the sensitivity of activation-dependent pathways compared to shear-dependent adhesion. Collectively, these limitations emphasize that the DSD is not intended to replace fresh platelets but rather to define controlled experimental windows in which specific platelet functions, particularly biomechanical responses, may be interrogated. Future work will focus on optimizing storage conditions, including additive solutions and modulation of oxidative stress (eg, promising ROS scavengers such as N-acetylcysteine [26,29]).

In conclusion shear-dependent platelet adhesion is preserved for up to 3 days of DSD storage and remains GPIbα-dependent, despite progressive PSL. In contrast, biochemical responsiveness declines earlier, particularly in PRP, highlighting a divergence between biomechanical and activation-dependent function. These findings emphasize the assay-dependent nature of platelet storage suitability and position the DSD as a laboratory-scale tool to support experimental workflows, particularly microfluidic assays, enabling interrogation of shear-based platelet functions within a controlled experimental window.
