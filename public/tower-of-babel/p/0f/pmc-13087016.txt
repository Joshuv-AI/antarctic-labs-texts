Unveiling cryptic diversity: integrative taxonomy discovers eight new species of moths and exposes biodiversity shortfalls in a Neotropical region
Authors: Simeão de Souza Moraes, Patricia Avelino Machado, Mariana Alves Stanton, Georgette Paola Ancajima, Lydia Fumiko Yamaguchi, Luiza de Moraes Magaldi, Massuo Jorge Kato, André Victor Lucci Freitas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The genus Eois Hübner (Geometridae: Larentiinae) is a highly diverse Neotropical lineage comprising 257 valid species, yet it has never been comprehensively revised. Previous studies indicate that wing polymorphism in several nominal taxa hides potential cryptic species, a pattern supported by COI data, host plant preferences, and female genitalia morphology. As a result, the true diversity of Eois is likely underestimated in the Neotropical region. Among some cryptic species complexes, Eois russearia stands out by the characteristic wing pattern with yellow background and reddish bands, and by representing the type species of the genus, however the type specimen is reported to be lost. In this study, we investigate the E. russearia complex and describe eight new species: Eois iemanja sp. nov., Eois nanan sp. nov., Eois ibeji sp. nov., Eois oxumare sp. nov., Eois logunede sp. nov., Eois orumila sp. nov., Eois iroco sp. nov., and Eois stantonae sp. nov., using an integrative taxonomy framework that combines molecular species delimitation, morphology, host plant associations, and geographical distribution. We also discuss how these findings relate to biodiversity shortfalls in hyperdiverse groups and highlight the relevance of cryptic diversity and the taxonomic impediment in documenting Neotropical biodiversity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41222-x.

Introduction

Shortfalls in biodiversity knowledge occur when there is a gap between the knowledge we actually acquire and the knowledge that would be possible at a given moment in a biological system1. The scarcity of morphological data for taxonomic studies (the Linnean shortfall), the limited understanding of species’ geographic limits (the Wallacean shortfall) and the lack of information on species interactions (the Eltonian shortfall) represent major obstacles not only for describing species, but also for understanding their ecology and distribution, especially within cryptic species complexes. These limitations are intrinsically linked to the scarcity of specialists working on particular taxa, and to methodological approaches that address only one aspect of this multidimensional problem: while modern molecular tools are highly effective at discovering putative evolutionary lineages (the “discovery component” of the Linnean shortfall) they alone do not fulfill the requirements for formal species descriptions, diagnosis, or for elucidating the ecological and distribution questions (Eltonian and Wallacean shortfalls), which are essential for applied biodiversity studies. This situation is particularly evident in hyperdiverse groups2,3, where the accumulated diversity from evolutionary history far surpasses our current capacity to describe new taxa and their associated morphological or ecological traits. Such shortfalls are further intensified in small organisms with complex geographic distributions across topographically diverse landscapes and low phenotypic conspicuousness, as commonly seen in cryptic species complexes4.

Within Lepidoptera, Geometridae exemplifies this challenge. With over 24,000 species, the family stands as one of the most diverse lineages of Lepidoptera, currently ranking as the second most species-rich among Macrolepidoptera5–7. With several genera having a large number of species, Geometridae represents a challenge for researchers dealing with the description of the diversity circumscribed within the family8–10, especially because some of the most diverse genera fits on the biodiversity shortfall context.

One particularly species-rich genus is Eois Hübner5,11, with 257 described species2,3, 210 of which occur in the Neotropics. These moths are small, with wingspans of up to 2 cm, and show remarkable wing pattern variation2. Background colors range from yellow to brown or light green and are typically accompanied by dots or symmetrical reddish or ferruginous bands3. Although Eois is an important component of Neotropical moth diversity, substantial knowledge gaps persist2.

Single-locus species delimitation approaches have been useful in resolving diversity in montane Eois8. Studies using the COI barcode region (658 bp) in Brazilian montane and lowland biomes have also expanded knowledge of the genus3,10. These methods accelerate species discovery when used as an initial step in diversity estimation3,8,10,12. To establish robust species hypotheses that lead to formal diagnoses and species descriptions, it is insufficient to rely solely on genetic data for accurate taxonomic identification. Therefore, integrative approaches, which test molecular clusters against independent evidence from morphology, ecology, and distribution are essential 13. This does not undermine the value of genomic data; rather, it emphasizes that different lines of evidence answer different questions. Molecular data is particularly useful for uncovering putative lineages, while information on morphology, ecology, and natural history are critical for diagnosing species, creating identification tools, and linking new findings to historical collections and field biology 13–15.

Eois russearia Hübner, 1818 is particularly notable because it exhibits a distinctive yellow background with reddish bands and is the type species of the genus, although the type specimen is reportedly lost. This color pattern is shared with E. contractata (Walker, 1861), E. binaria (Guenée, 1858), the Colombian junior synonym E. hyriata (Snellen, 1874), and E. crocina (Schaus, 1912) from Costa Rica3. Subtle differences in the wing pattern make these species difficult to distinguish.

COI analyses in Moraes et al.3 revealed E. russearia as a polyphyletic assemblage composed of three clusters: two containing GenBank sequences and a third including Brazilian specimens, additional GenBank sequences, and the type specimen of E. contractata. Within this Brazilian cluster, branch lengths exceeded 3%, a threshold commonly associated with independent evolutionary lineages or operational taxonomic units (OTUs)16. Preliminary results indicated at least three molecular OTUs (MOTUs) among Brazilian specimens reared from larvae, corroborated by differences in wing pattern and larval coloration. However, broader sampling, additional morphological traits, and expanded host plant data were needed to better assess species boundaries within the E. russearia complex.

In this study, we expand the geographical sampling and apply the Assemble Species by Automatic Partitioning (ASAP) method17 to evaluate species limits within the E. russearia complex. We complement molecular results with host plant preferences and genital morphology to corroborate species delimitation in the genetic distance tree (Neighbor Joining). We describe eight new species and discuss their implications for understanding biodiversity shortfalls, particularly the Eltonian shortfall, in hyperdiverse Neotropical taxa. Our integrative approach advances the knowledge of Eois diversity and underscores the importance of studying cryptic species complexes in the Neotropics using integrative approaches.

Results

Species delimitation

Based on the present taxonomic sampling, the ASAP tree shows between nine and twenty MOTUs (Fig. 1a), depending on the p-value and the threshold genetic distance. All ten hypotheses for clustering specimens in MOTUs, reveals cryptic diversity within the E. russearia complex. The host plant preferences and the genital morphology corroborate eight MOTUs in our data set, with distribution in Brazil (Fig. 1a,c). We cautiously interpreted some hypotheses in which samples from GenBank were merged with Brazilian samples, ensuring that the specimens linked to the GenBank sequences were not considered part of the type series. Although the retrieved sequence JX150849 shows only 0.6% of genetic distance from remaining Brazilian samples, it represents sampling from Ecuadorian Amazon Forest, while the Brazilian samples in the same MOTU were from Atlantic Forest. Similarity the MOTU including the sequence JQ179881 has internal branches with up to 1.6% of genetic distances, being the Brazilian samples from Pantanal wetlands and the sequence retrieved from GenBank belonging to Costa Rica. Discussion

Lepidoptera typically follow the global pattern of the latitudinal biodiversity gradient (LBG), with high species diversity and richness near the tropics declining with increasing latitude18–20. In tropical mountains, similar patterns occur along elevational gradients, with diversity and richness generally decreasing as altitude increases. Nevertheless, exceptions exist in certain Lepidoptera groups. Larentiine moths, to which Eois belongs, are a well-documented example in the Andean Lepidoptera fauna21–25, with several genera showing peak diversity between 1000 and 2000 m.a.s.l.8,23. These findings, however, remain geographically restricted to the Northern Andes.

Recent studies have expanded our understanding of Larentiinae diversity in mid-altitude regions outside the Andes, such as the Southeastern Brazilian plateau3,10, where sampling in mountainous Atlantic Forest has revealed new species. Although some Eois (e.g., E. ewa Moraes & Stanton 2021; E. oxum Moraes & Freitas 2021) occur at 800—1500 m.a.s.l., several species described in the present study were collected at low altitudes in the Amazon basin and Brazilian wetlands (Pantanal). Within the E. russearia complex, our results demonstrate that lowland regions also harbor significant species diversity, likely driven by highly specialized larval host-plant interactions that create heterogeneous selective pressures over short geographic distances. Multiple species co-occur in sympatry in different lowland areas: in the Amazon Basin (e.g. E. oxumare sp. nov., E. orumila sp. nov., E. iroco sp. nov., E. stantonae sp. nov.), in the Atlantic Forest (E. iemanja sp. nov. and E. ibeji sp. nov.), and in the Pantanal wetlands (e.g. E. nanan sp. nov., E. logunede sp. nov.). In the E. russearia complex male genitalia remain only subtly divergent among geographically proximate lineages. Conversely, female genitalia morphology, host-plant associations, and haplotype structure unveil hidden species-level diversity, reinforcing the importance of species-interaction data among other complementary evidence for delimiting MOTUs.

In the present study, two MOTUs deserve further discussion. The Eois ibeji sp. nov. cluster comprises a specimen from the Ecuadorian Amazon (GenBank) and specimens from the Atlantic Forest. Although these biomes are currently isolated by the South American diagonal of open formations 26,27, evidence suggests past connections between Amazon and Atlantic Forests before the last Pleistocene glacial maximum, explaining the presence of shared biotic elements28–30. This historical scenario may account for the clustering of geographically distant specimens within E. ibeji sp. nov., a hypothesis requiring further investigation. Similarly, Eois logunede sp. nov. clusters Brazilian Pantanal specimens with a GenBank sequence from Costa Rica. The Pantanal constitutes a heterogeneous mosaic influenced by Amazon Forest, Atlantic Forest, Cerrado, and Chaco biomes. This mosaic contributes to forming a rich mixture of floodplains, marshes, gallery forests, and open savannas (Cerrado) that could explain this clustering. However, previous Lepidoptera studies27 indicate that the northern Andes act as a major barrier between Central and South America. Broader sampling and lineage-dating analyses will be necessary to clarify these biogeographic relationships.

The hyperdiverse genus Eois exemplifies challenges imposed by the global biodiversity crisis and the taxonomic impediment31. Although more than 250 species are currently recognized, diversity is underestimated, with projections suggesting a potential 176% increase in actual species number3,8,9. These estimates are based mainly on wing polymorphism, which can hide distinct species within nominal taxa, as previously shown in the E. pallidicosta complex10. Our findings on the E. russearia complex corroborate this trend, highlighting the need of integrative approaches using genetics, morphology, and ecology to detect and describe cryptic lineages.

Adding to the above scenario, academic metrics driven by productivity have generated a dialectic between the rapid production of molecular data and the time investment required for morphological and natural-history research32. This imbalance disproportionately affects the Global South33, where limited investment in taxonomy has led to a shortage of trained specialists3,34. While high-throughput molecular tools are often presented as a universal solution, their application in the Global South, home to much of the world’s tropical biodiversity, faces unique barriers that include volatile costs of imported reagents, infrastructure maintenance, and training gaps33. Moreover, a focus on generating molecular data does not address the crisis in morphological taxonomy expertise35. Such expertise is essential for interpreting molecular results within a biological context, including accurate species identification, meaningful interpretation of genetic patterns, and insights into evolution10,15. When morphology is overlooked in biodiversity studies, the taxonomic impediment persists, constraining conservation policies and the sustainable management of biodiversity31,35. Therefore, “sustainable” progress in taxonomy and biodiversity requires a balanced investment in both molecular and morphological/ecological approaches. Entomological collections further enrich biodiversity research by providing phenotypic, geographic, temporal, and ecological metadata36–38, contributing to a multidimensional understanding of biodiversity across time and space, highlighting the pivotal role of these institutions in biodiversity studies.

In this context, Eois represents a model illustrating both the opportunities offered by integrative taxonomy and the challenges faced by biodiversity research under productivity-driven systems.

The herein integrative approach demonstrates that overcoming biodiversity shortfalls requires a hierarchical strategy. Single-locus molecular species delimitation methods, such as ASAP, are powerful first-step tools for biodiversity prospection, directly tackling the Linnean shortfall by identifying candidate species. However, fully closing the Linnean shortfall requires formal descriptions and diagnoses based on consistent morphological/ecological traits, which allow for species identification and classification beyond the genetic data. Furthermore, the use of host plant records in this study provides critical data to start addressing the Eltonian shortfall for the Eois species, while data on species distribution help to mitigate the Wallacean shortfall. Therefore, taxonomy is not a one-step process but a synthesis where molecular data raise the hypotheses of existence of new taxa, while morphological, ecological, and distribution data test, solidify, and provide biological meaning to those hypotheses, transforming genetic clusters into named, diagnosed species with known interactions and ranges2,3,10.

Ultimately, while genetic data are indispensable for estimating biodiversity, it is traditional disciplines such as comparative morphology and taxonomy that turn the estimates into reality, "giving life” to species, formally recognizing them and making the biodiversity accessible to scientific and non-scientific communities.

Materials and methods

Sampling

Immature stages of Eois were obtained from three localities: i) Biological Reserve of Mogi-Guaçu (28° 18’ S, 47° 11’ W), Mogi-Guaçu, São Paulo, Brazil; ii) Adolpho Ducke Forest Reserve (2° 57’ S, 59° 55’ W), Manaus, Amazonas, Brazil; iii) vicinities of Aquidauana, Mato Grosso do Sul, Brazil (20° 28’ S, 55° 47’ W).

Larvae were collected by searching plants of the genus Piper L. (Piperaceae), which are known to be the host plants for the larvae of several Eois species. The larvae were reared in the laboratory. Samples of the host plant, including leaves, inflorescence and fruits, were also collected for further identification.

Rearing methods

We reared the immatures at the Laboratório de Química de Produtos Naturais (LQPN) da Universidade de São Paulo following the procedures described in Moraes et al.10,39: larvae were reared in individual 300 mL clear plastic pots with lids and provided with leaves of the same Piper plants on which they were collected. Pupae were transferred to individual 50 mL clear plastic pots with lids and moist cotton wool until emergence. Larvae and pupae were maintained under constant temperature (25 oC) and 12 h light: 12 h dark cycle. After emergence, adults were sacrificed for DNA extraction and genitalia dissection. Samples of the host plants were collected and compared to previously identified samples from the same locality at the LQPN, at the University of São Paulo, and compared to the species description in the Brazilian Flora 2020 project40.

DNA extraction and PCR conditions

After the emergence of the adults, three legs were removed from each specimen before spreading. Sampled legs were preserved dry and stored in 1.5 ml tubes at—20˚C. Total genomic DNA was extracted with DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Netherlands), according to the manufacturer’s protocol with final elution in 60 µL elution buffer. The 5’ end (barcode region, COI, 650 bp) was amplified using the primers HCO and LCO41. Polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) were performed with 13 µl total volume containing 1–2 µl of extracted DNA, 3,2–4,2 µl of H2O milli-Q, 6.5 µl of 2 × MyTaq HS red mix (Bioline Co., UK), and 0.65 µl of each primer (10 mM). PCR products were amplified as follows: 96˚C for 7 min, followed by 40 cycles of 96˚C for 30 s, 50˚C for 30 s and 72˚C for 90 s, and a final extension period of 72˚C for 10 min.

Amplicons were purified by adding a mix of 1.3 µL of FastAP Thermosensitive Alkaline Phosphatase (Thermofisher Scientific, USA) and 0.7 µl of Exonuclease 1 (Thermofisher Scientific, USA) to 10 µL of PCR products. Purified products were sent for Sanger sequencing on an ABI 3500 automated sequencer (Life Technologies).

Alignment, tree inference and species delimitation

The genetic dataset consisted of 73 sequenced specimens from Brazilian fauna combined with 17 sequences obtained from GenBank, including two type specimens (Table S1). We primarily relied on the ASAP analysis of a curated dataset rather than Barcode Index Numbers (BINs). This decision was taken based on a previous similar study that also unveiled the overall misidentification in public sequences for Eois3, which can lead to erroneous BIN assignments and obscure the true species diversity. The present approach prioritizes a dataset in which every sequence is linked to a morphologically examined voucher specimen with precise collection and host-plant data, ensuring the taxonomic reliability of the proposed species hypotheses.

Sequences were aligned using MAFFT42 implemented in Geneious v.11.0.243. The alignments were carefully checked by eye, taking into consideration the reading frame relative to the reference sequence.

In order to study the species boundaries within our dataset, we used a species delimitation method focused on single-locus gene analysis: The Assemble Species by Automatic Partitioning method (ASAP)17. The ASAP is a hierarchical clustering algorithm that uses pairwise genetic distances and proposes species partitions ranked by a scoring system output on an tree17. Differently from the ABGD method12 used in our previous studies3,10, the ASAP uses no biological prior insight of intraspecific diversity. The analyses were performed using the graphic web version and the results were compared using Jukes-Cantor (JC69) corrected distances http://wwwabi.snv.jussieu.fr/public/abgd/abgdweb.html.

Haplotype study

We explored the genealogical relationships among COI barcode region haplotypes by generating haplotype networks in PopArt44 using the parsimony method45,46 as implemented in TCS. The number of haplotypes was also estimated in PopArt and were colored according to the groups inferred by ASAP.

Morphological study

The external morphology and color pattern were analyzed following usual protocols47. Wing color patterns were recorded and genitalia of females and males were dissected. For interpretation and descriptions of genital structures we followed the procedures outlined in Moraes & Duarte48, based on classical studies on Lepidoptera morphology (male genitalia49–53; female genitalia54–56).

A total of 56 specimens belonging to the eight new species described were examined and deposited at the Zoological Collection, Museu de Diversidade Biológica da UNICAMP (ZUEC). Wing color patterns and male and female genitalia were analyzed. The habitus of adults and genitalia were photographed using a ZEISS AxioCam MRc5 camera coupled to ZEISS SteREO Discovery V20 stereomicroscope. Photos in multiple focal planes were extracted with the AxioVision Rel. 4.8 software and saved in TIFF format; subsequently combined using the software CombineZP. The photos were edited and organized using Adobe Photoshop 2024 and Adobe Illustrator 2024, respectively.
