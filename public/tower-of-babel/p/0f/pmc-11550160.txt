How to treat patients with bleeding disorder of unknown cause?
Authors: Caroline Mussert, Amaury Monard, Floor Heubel-Moenen
Published: 2024

Sixty to seventy percent of persons with a clinically relevant bleeding tendency who are referred to hemostasis experts are classified as having a bleeding disorder of unknown cause (BDUC) when a diagnosis cannot be established after extensive laboratory testing [1]. A frequent (presenting) symptom in persons with BDUC is periprocedural bleeding during surgical and dental procedures as well as postpartum hemorrhage, next to less severe bleeding events such as easy bruising and mucocutaneous bleeding, eg, epistaxis and heavy menstrual bleeding [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Most often, a step-wise treatment plan is deployed when confronted with risk of bleeding during procedures, which is based on the severity of bleeding tendency and prior bleeding complications [6]. Treatment regularly consists of tranexamic acid and desmopressin [3,6,7], with more sporadic administration of platelets, fresh frozen plasma, and/or recombinant activated factor (F)VII when bleeding is severe or refractory [[6], [7], [8]]. Although the recently published International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) Scientific and Standardization Committee (SSC) communication provides recommendations on periprocedural and peripartum management in persons with BDUC, clear guidelines on targeted treatment strategies are still lacking, and clinical management in case of medical procedures and child delivery remains challenging [1]. Only a few studies have investigated bleeding complications with or without prophylactic treatment when confronted with hemostatic challenges in persons with BDUC, therefore there is only limited evidence regarding efficacy and safety. Moreover, knowledge of the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms resulting in bleeding is lacking, prompting the need for in-depth research in BDUC as stated in the list of key outstanding questions published by the ISTH SSC [1].

In this edition of Research and Practice in Thrombosis and Haemostasis, Berkowitz et al. [9] publish their data on periprocedural hemostatic prophylaxis and bleeding outcomes in persons with BDUC. This retrospective study included 127 persons with BDUC of which the majority were female (91.3%), diagnosed according to predefined criteria. Data were analyzed from 48 major procedures, 70 minor procedures, and 19 deliveries including the occurrence of major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding according to the ISTH criteria. Moreover, earlier observed bleeding phenotype was reported for all patients [9].

Most frequently applied prophylactic treatment was antifibrinolytic agents, most often prescribed as monotherapy [9]. These were often also used in combination with desmopressin, platelet transfusions, or both. Major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding was observed in 10.4% of major procedures, 1.4% of minor procedures, and 26% of deliveries. When procedures were performed under prophylactic treatment bleeding occurred in 4.1% compared with 10% of procedures without hemostatic prophylaxis. In 2 of a total of 19 deliveries, no prophylactic agents were administered, one of these was complicated by bleeding. Contrastingly, bleeding also occurred in 23.5% of the other 17 deliveries in which hemostatic prophylaxis was prescribed. Molecular and clinical markers for the diagnosis and management of type 1 VWD bleeding scores were not associated with the occurrence of bleeding events.

A review of the literature includes a few retrospective chart review case series [3,7,10,11]. One study by MacDonald et al. [3] reported bleeding complications after surgery in 85 patients prior to their BDUC diagnosis who did not receive hemostatic prophylaxis. Although 55% had undergone at least one surgery without bleeding complications, 64 of 85 (75%) did experience abnormal postoperative bleeding. In this study, 69 patients were investigated undergoing minor or major surgery while being treated with tranexamic acid and/or desmopressin of which only one patient was re-admitted with bleeding requiring additional hemostatic treatment.

Another study by Castle et al. [10] investigated outcomes and clinical management of pregnancies and childbirth in women with BDUC, reporting data on 60 deliveries in 36 patients in a retrospective cohort review. Eleven percent did not receive prophylactic treatment, 41% received tranexamic acid, 30% received tranexamic acid and desmopressin, and 13% received tranexamic acid and platelets. The incidence of postpartum hemorrhage was 77%, 32%, 38%, and 43% in each group, respectively. The odds ratio for postpartum hemorrhage was 6.3 for no primary hemostatic prophylaxis versus prophylaxis.

Obaji et al. [7] published results from a retrospective chart review of 33 BDUC patients undergoing 78 major and minor medical procedures. Prophylactic treatment consisted of tranexamic acid in 28 procedures, desmopressin in 2, and a combination of both in 45 procedures. The majority (70/78) was performed successfully and without bleeding complications. In 1 patient, a platelet transfusion was needed to stop postoperative bleeding.

Lastly, Veen et al. [11] reported on retrospective outcomes of 53 BDUC patients undergoing surgery and 26 pregnant women with BDUC. Fifty-five percent of BDUC patients undergoing surgery received prophylactic treatment. Despite treatment, 14% experienced major bleeding. In BDUC patients who did not receive prophylactic treatment, 25% experienced periprocedural bleeding. In pregnant BDUC patients, only 8% received prophylactic treatment before delivery. Postpartum hemorrhage occurred in one patient who received prophylactic treatment and in 11 patients who did not.

When comparing bleeding outcomes between the different studies, results are clearly heterogeneous as depicted in table 4 in the article by Berkowitz et al. [9]. Possible explanations for this diversity are differences in methodology; BDUC definition applied to include patients as well as definitions for outcome measures and procedures. Berkowitz et al. [9] observed fewer periprocedural bleeding complications than Veen et al. [11], both in patients with and without prophylactic treatment. Also, similarities are present, most striking is the high proportion of postpartum hemorrhage in these patients, both with and without preventative measures.

The frequent use of antifibrinolytic agents is in line with previously published studies and the recommendations by the ISTH SSC [1,3,6,7]. However, perioperative platelet transfusions were used in 26% of major procedures, 9% of minor procedures, and 14% of deliveries which is more frequent than reported previously [6,7]. Due to the risk of alloimmunization, the use of platelet transfusions should be carefully considered in persons with BDUC to prevent long-term platelet refractoriness, leading to ineffective treatment [12]. Until better evidence is available, the ISTH SSC recommends a pragmatic approach regarding the use of hemostatic agents in persons with BDUC based on patient and procedure-related factors to establish a personalized treatment plan [1]. Interestingly, the authors report that in 22 procedures, including a hip arthroplasty that was complicated by bleeding, hemostatic prophylaxis was not given because a hematologist was either not consulted or their recommendations regarding treatment were not followed [9]. This highlights a general lack of knowledge on BDUC and the importance of increased awareness among healthcare professionals to regard BDUC as a diagnostic entity and act accordingly to avoid adverse health outcomes.

The authors acknowledge the limitations of their study, including the fact that its design is retrospective using medical chart review for data collection, with potential selection bias due to for example incomplete hemostatic data. Another limitation is the use of the MCMDC-1 bleeding score instead of the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis Bleeding Assessment Tool (ISTH-BAT), which is ISTH-recommended and the most widely used tool, and the fact that almost solely females were included.

Overall, the data presented by Berkowitz et al. [9] are a valuable addition to the sparse literature on periprocedural treatment in BDUC patients. This study underlines that current periprocedural and peripartum management in persons with BDUC is insufficient, as bleeding complications still occur despite hemostatic prophylaxis. It therefore emphasizes the urgent need for prospective therapeutic studies, ideally through randomized controlled trials, to obtain more evidence on effective treatment strategies to develop more informed guidelines. Furthermore, it highlights the importance of fundamental and diagnostic research to identify the pathophysiological mechanisms causative for bleeding in BDUC to enable the development of targeted therapies. Both can only be achieved by increased awareness among healthcare professionals, scientists, payers, and policymakers that BDUC is currently not adequately recognized, understood and therefore insufficiently treated, especially around medical procedures.
