Drug resistance mechanisms and molecular epidemiology of carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae in pulmonary infections
Authors: Yinzhu Mo, Baojun Ren, Huifeng Zhang, Xingde Li, Xianxiong Cheng, Shijie Lv, Zhibang Ye, Cangsang Song, Huanzhi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs a highly drug-resistant nosocomial pathogen, Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) often causes pulmonary infections with high mortality rates, and deciphering its molecular characteristics and drug resistance mechanisms is crucial for rational clinical drug use.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the drug resistance mechanisms and molecular epidemiological characteristics of pulmonary infections caused by CRKP. The findings intend to provide a scientific basis for optimizing antibiotic use and developing effective clinical prevention and control measures.MethodsNon-repetitive CRKP strains isolated from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and qualified sputum samples of hospitalized patients at a Class A tertiary hospital in Yunnan Province from April 2020 to June 2024 were collected. The VITEK 2 Compact automatic bacterial identification system, along with the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method and broth microdilution method, were employed to conduct antimicrobial susceptibility testing and assess resistance phenotypes. Additionally, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) technology was employed to analyze resistance genes, virulence genes, and multi-locus sequence typing (MLST), and a phylogenetic tree was constructed.ResultsA total of 70 non-repetitive CRKP isolates were collected. The strains exhibited high resistance to most antimicrobial agents but remained sensitive to tigecycline, polymyxin B, and ceftazidime/avibactam. Various antibiotic resistance genes were identified, with blaKPC−2 (62.9%) being the predominant carbapenemase gene, followed by blaOXA−232 (22.9%). High detection rates were observed for virulence genes, including type I pili (fimCDFGHI), type III pili (mrkABCDFHJ), and iron-uptake system genes (iroN, iutA, fep, ent, fes). Six sequence types were identified, with ST11 (61.4%) being the most prevalent.ConclusionCRKP strains isolated from pulmonary infections displayed extensive multidrug resistance, primarily attributed to the ST11 lineage and blaKPC−2 as the main carbapenemase gene. The frequent detection of multiple key virulence factors underscores the importance of precise antibacterial treatment and vigilant molecular epidemiological surveillance.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-12954-y.

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) is a common Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen frequently associated with hospital-acquired infections, including pneumonia, urinary tract infections, liver abscesses, and bloodstream infections [1]. Although carbapenems have been widely used as first-line antibiotics against K. pneumoniae, their extensive clinical application has resulted in the global emergence of carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP), now considered a serious public health issue. Published data indicate that CRKP has become a predominant pathogen in numerous healthcare facilities, representing 20–69% of all K. pneumoniae infections [2]. Importantly, the elevated antibiotic resistance of CRKP is strongly linked to poor clinical outcomes. Previous studies reported mortality rates of bloodstream infections caused by CRKP as high as 50%–60% [3]. For pulmonary infections specifically, the reported 14-day mortality rate is approximately 23.2%, with an in-hospital mortality rate reaching up to 34.8% [4].

The treatment challenges posed by CRKP infections stem not only from extensive antimicrobial resistance, but also from the diversity and complexity of underlying resistance mechanisms [3]. Studies have demonstrated that CRKP exhibits antibiotic resistance through various pathways, including carbapenemase enzyme production, outer membrane porin loss or conformational change, efflux pump genes overexpression, and structural changes in penicillin-binding proteins [5]. Of these mechanisms, carbapenemase enzyme production remains the predominant mode of resistance. Commonly identified carbapenemase genes include blaKPC, blaNDM, blaOXA, and blaIMP [6]. In China, approximately 60% of CRKP strains carry the blaKPC gene, primarily the KPC-2 subtype [7]. Ceftazidime/avibactam, an effective antibiotic combination against KPC-type and OXA-48-type carbapenemases, is often employed clinically to manage non-metalloenzyme-producing CRKP infections [8]. Therefore, detailed epidemiological and resistance profile data on CRKP are essential to guide clinicians in selecting appropriate therapeutic strategies.

Pulmonary infection is the predominant type of CRKP infection, significantly influencing patient outcomes and survival [9]. The current study investigates the mechanisms underlying antibiotic resistance and the molecular epidemiology of pulmonary infections caused by CRKP, aiming to provide a scientific basis for optimizing antibiotic selection and developing effective prevention and control measures.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strain sources

Non-duplicate CRKP isolates were collected from qualified sputum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid samples obtained from patients hospitalized in a Class A tertiary hospital in Yunnan Province from April 2020 to June 2024. Strain identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were performed using the Vitek-2 Compact automated microbiology system. Quality control strains included K. pneumoniae ATCC BAA-1705 and ATCC BAA-1706. All collected isolates were stored at − 80 ℃ for subsequent analysis. Inclusion criteria: (1) Diagnosis of pulmonary infection based on established diagnostic criteria [10], requiring at least one of the following clinical features: ① at least one clinical feature from the following: (a) new-onset cough, increased sputum or respiratory secretions (e.g., purulent sputum), with or without chest pain; (b) temperature > 38 ℃, and dry or moist rales heard on lung auscultation; (c) peripheral blood leukocyte count > 10 × 109/L or < 4 × 109/L; and ② chest imaging (X-ray or CT) showing new or progressive infiltrates, consolidations, or ground-glass opacities. All diagnoses were confirmed after excluding non-infectious pulmonary lesions such as atelectasis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and pulmonary edema. (2) If multiple pathogen-positive cultures were obtained from the same patient, only the first positive culture was included. Exclusion criteria: Concurrent infection at other anatomical sites. This study received approval from the institutional ethics committee (Approval No.: YLS2024-002).

Bacterial recovery and culture

Frozen bacterial strains stored at − 80 ℃ were retrieved, and an inoculation loop was used under aseptic conditions in a biosafety cabinet to transfer bacterial solution onto Columbia blood agar plates using the three-zone streaking method. Plates were appropriately labeled and incubated at 37 ℃ in a CO2 incubator for 18–24 h.

Bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Bacterial isolation and cultivation were performed in strict accordance with the National Clinical Laboratory Operating Procedures [11]. Identification and antibiotic susceptibility testing were conducted using the VITEK 2 Compact automatic identification analyzer (bioMérieux, France). The paper disc diffusion method (K-B method, discs from Oxoid, UK) and microdilution broth method (for polymyxin B) were employed for supplementary antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Results of antimicrobial susceptibility testing were interpreted in accordance with CLSI guidelines (M100-S34) [12]. Quality control strains were K. pneumoniae ATCC BAA-1705 and ATCC BAA-1706.

Genomic DNA extraction and whole genome sequencing

Genomic DNA extraction was conducted using the E.Z-N.A® Bacterial DNA Kit, following the manufacturer’s protocol. After ensuring DNA concentration and purity met quality requirements, sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq™ Nano DNA Sample Preparation Kit. Whole-genome sequencing was subsequently performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform, generating paired-end reads of 2 × 150 bp.

Genome assembly and annotation

Raw sequencing data underwent quality filtering using Trimmomatic v0.39, followed by genome assembly using ABySS v2.0.2. Subsequently, GapCloser v1.12 was applied to refine genome assembly results by gap filling and base correction. Coding genes within assembled genomes were predicted using GeneMarkS software. Predicted protein sequences were annotated functionally by aligning them against databases including NR, Swiss-Prot, KEGG, CARD, and VFDB. Resistance genes and virulence factors were further identified by matching sequences to the CARD (https://card.mcmaster.ca/) and VFDB databases, respectively.

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) and phylogenetic tree construction

MLST was conducted using MLST 2.23.0 software (https://cge.cbs.dtu.dk/services/MLST), based on sequence alignment of seven housekeeping genes from the CRKP genomes to determine sequence types (STs). Phylogenetic relationships were evaluated by constructing a phylogenetic tree utilizing PhyML v3.0 software (http://www.atgc-montpellier.fr/phyml/) with the maximum-likelihood method, Bayesian correction, and 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using WHONET 5.6 and Excel 2021 software. Count data were expressed as absolute numbers (n) or percentages (%).

Results

Clinical characteristics of isolated strains

Seventy CRKP isolates were obtained from respiratory samples collected from 70 hospitalized patients meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria at a Class A tertiary hospital. Among the patients, 53 (75.7%) were male and 17 (24.3%) were female, predominantly older than 65 years (55.7%). The highest frequency of K. pneumoniae isolates was recorded in the neurosurgery department (22 cases, 31.4%), followed by the intensive care unit (ICU; 18 cases, 25.7%) and the respiratory department (18 cases, 25.7%), of which nine cases (12.9%) originated from the respiratory ICU. Most patients had underlying comorbidities and had undergone invasive medical procedures. All patients received antibiotic therapy during hospitalization, and the overall mortality rate was 31.4% (Table 1).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing findings

Susceptibility testing results of CRKP strains to commonly used antibiotics

The results demonstrated that the CRKP strains exhibited high resistance rates to penicillins, cephalosporins, and β-lactamase inhibitor combinations. Among these antibiotics, the resistance rate to ceftazidime was 98.6%, whereas the resistance rates to all other tested antibiotics were 100.0%. For carbapenems, resistance rates of isolated strains to meropenem, imipenem, and ertapenem were 95.7%, 94.5%, and 100.0%, respectively. In addition, resistance to quinolones and aminoglycosides was relatively high, with only amikacin having a resistance rate below 90.0%. The resistance rates to aztreonam and sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim were 98.6% and 50.0%, respectively. However, these strains displayed significantly lower resistance rates to polymyxin B (1.9%) and ceftazidime/avibactam (10.0%). Notably, no tigecycline-resistant strains were detected (Fig. 1).

Detection results of drug resistance genes

Detection of resistance genes in 70 isolated CRKP strains indicated that 62 strains carried carbapenemase genes, while the remaining 8 strains lacked these genes. Among carbapenemase genes, blaKPC−2 was predominant (62.9%), followed by blaOXA−232 (22.9%), blaNDM−25 (4.3%), blaOXA−10 (2.9%), blaVIM−1 (1.4%), and blaOXA−1 (1.4%). Additionally, several strains carried multiple extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBLs) genes, primarily of the CTX-M, SHV, OXY, or TEM types. Common ESBLs subtypes included blaCTX−M−65 (37.1%), blaCTX−M−163 (27.1%), blaSHV−14 (31.4%), and blaSHV−64 (25.7%). Only one strain carried the AmpC-related gene blaDHA−1. Moreover, additional resistance-associated genes were identified, including aminoglycoside resistance genes [AAC(6’)-Ib8 (11.4%), aadA2 (12.9%), aadA3 (15.7%), rmtB (57.1%), rmtF (27.1%)], quinolone resistance genes [QnrS1 (50.0%), oqxA (45.7%), oqxB (45.7%)], macrolide resistance genes [mPhA (32.9%), mPhE (1.4%)], sulfonamide resistance genes [sul1 (77.1%), dfrA12 (27.1%), dfrA14 (15.7)], tetracycline resistance genes [tet (A) (8.6%)], fosfomycin resistance genes [FosA3 (31.4%), FosA5 (4.3%), FosA6 (92.9%)], and chloramphenicol resistance gene [catB2 (1.4%)] (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Virulence genes detection findings

CRKP strains carried a broad array of virulence genes, including fimCDFGHI, mrkABCDFHJ, iroN, iutA, entABCEFS, fepABCDG, and fes, all of which exhibited 100% prevalence. In contrast, the prevalence rates of genes such as fimT, fimU, fimV, iroB, iroC, iroD, fyuA, rmpA, rmpA2, and allS were relatively low (Supplementary Fig. 2).

MLST of CRKP strains

MLST of the 70 CRKP strains identified a total of six STs. Four strains could not be classified due to incomplete allele sequences. Among the identified STs, ST11 (61.4%, 43 strains) and ST231 (27.1%, 19 strains) were the predominant subtypes, while the other types (ST23, ST268, ST309, and ST525) each accounted for only one strain. Strains harboring the blaKPC−2 gene mainly belonged to ST11, while those harboring blaOXA−232 were mainly classified as ST231. The phylogenetic relationships among these strains (Fig. 2) were generally consistent with their MLST profiles, with strains of the same ST type clustering in the same branch, suggesting common evolutionary origins and close genetic relatedness.

Discussion

At present, CRKP pulmonary infections are widely regarded as one of the most pressing global public health challenges, posing a significant threat to human health due to their high incidence and mortality rates [13]. However, research concerning the molecular epidemiological characteristics of CRKP-related pulmonary infections remains limited, as existing studies predominantly focus on bacteremia and other types of hospital-acquired infections. Therefore, there is an urgent need to further explore the drug resistance mechanisms and molecular epidemiological characteristics of CRKP pulmonary infections. This study utilized whole-genome sequencing (WGS) technology to analyze the genomic features of CRKP strains associated with pulmonary infections isolated from a Class A tertiary hospital, thus providing crucial data to support infection prevention and control efforts.

The 70 CRKP strains collected in this study were predominantly isolated from patients in the neurosurgery department and ICU, consistent with the findings of Shao et al. [14]. This distribution may be attributed to the severity of underlying diseases and higher frequency of invasive procedures among patients in these departments [9]. Additionally, CRKP pulmonary infections in this study mainly affected elderly patients, who commonly had multiple underlying diseases, history of invasive procedures, and repeated antibiotic treatments. These observations align with the results reported by Xu et al. [15]. The prognosis for CRKP-infected patients was generally poor, with an in-hospital mortality rate reaching 31.4%, similar to previous reports [4]. Therefore, particular attention should be paid to high-risk patients with multiple comorbidities undergoing invasive procedures. Optimizing antibiotic usage and standardizing invasive procedures could effectively reduce the incidence and associated mortality of CRKP infections.

Therapeutic options against CRKP infections are limited. The present study revealed that most strains exhibited high resistance rates to conventional antibiotics, including cephalosporins, carbapenems, β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, and quinolones, with resistance rates approaching 100.0% for certain antibiotics. This finding is consistent with previously reported studies on highly drug-resistant K. pneumoniae strains both domestically and internationally [16, 17]. Consequently, these antibiotics are often unsuitable as first-line treatment options. The resistance rates of CRKP strains to gentamicin and amikacin were 90.0% and 87.1%, respectively, higher than those reported in other studies [18]. This discrepancy might be related to variations in individual patient characteristics, antibiotic prescribing practices, and local infection control measures, indicating significant regional differences in CRKP resistance patterns. Accordingly, physicians should comprehensively evaluate regional epidemiological data and local antibiotic susceptibility results, make prudent antibiotic selections, and implement personalized treatment plans in clinical practice. Currently, tigecycline, polymyxin B, and ceftazidime/avibactam are the primary treatments for CRKP infections in China [19], however, resistance to these drugs has already emerged in some strains. Therefore, hospitals must strengthen antibiotic stewardship programs, optimize antibiotic usage, and prevent further exacerbation of antibiotic resistance. Regular monitoring and evaluation of antibiotic resistance patterns can facilitate a better understanding of evolving resistance dynamics, thus enabling the development of more effective countermeasures.

In China, blaKPC−2 is the predominant subtype of carbapenemase genes found in CRKP isolates [14]. Similarly, the vast majority (62.9%) of CRKP isolates in this study carried the blaKPC−2 gene, a finding consistent with the general epidemiological trend of CRKP in China. This further indicates that the drug resistance mechanisms in pulmonary infections caused by CRKP are similar to those of other infection routes. Since the initial detection of blaOXA−232-carrying CRKP in five neonates in China in 2017, multiple small-scale outbreaks have occurred in hospitals, primarily affecting children and elderly populations [20]. In this study, isolates harboring the blaOXA−232 gene represented a considerable proportion (22.9%), with no other carbapenemase genes detected in these isolates, consistent with findings reported by Das et al. [21]. Previous studies indicated that blaNDM−1 and blaNDM−5 are the primary NDM subtypes found among CRKP strains in China [22]. However, the current study identified blaNDM−25 as the dominant subtype, which has not been documented in other studies. Reports of the blaNDM−25 subtype in K. pneumoniae remain scarce, indicating limited attention regarding its dissemination. Future research should investigate the transmission characteristics of this subtype and its impact on antibiotic resistance, thus providing novel insights into resistance mechanisms and clinical management strategies of K. pneumoniae. Moreover, this study observed the coexistence of blaNDM−25 with blaKPC−2 and blaOXA−10 potentially exacerbating CRKP resistance and further limiting effective antibiotic options.

In addition to carbapenemase genes, most isolates carried ESBLs and/or AmpC enzyme resistance genes, predominantly blaCTX−M−65 and blaSHV−14. Another important resistance mechanism in CRKP strains lacking carbapenemase genes involves the loss of porin genes associated with high-level ESBLs and/or AmpC enzymes [23]. In the current study, eight CRKP strains lacking carbapenemase genes carried ESBLs and/or AmpC resistance genes along with deletions in ompK35 and ompK36, suggesting that carbapenem resistance in these strains may result from impaired porin function associated with high-level ESBLs and/or AmpC enzyme production. Furthermore, these isolates also harbored resistance genes against aminoglycosides, quinolones, sulfonamides, macrolides, tetracyclines, fosfomycin, and chloramphenicol antibiotics, demonstrating extensive multidrug resistance. Such multidrug resistance considerably complicates infection management and poses a severe threat to public health. Therefore, accurate identification of CRKP resistance genotypes and continuous surveillance of molecular epidemiological characteristics can facilitate optimized antibiotic strategies, reduce unnecessary antibiotic use, and subsequently decrease the risk of resistance transmission [6].

The virulence of CRKP is closely associated with the virulence genes it harbors. The results of this study revealed that all strains carried fimCDFGHI genes encoding type I pili, mrkABCDFHJ genes encoding type III pili, iroN gene encoding salmonellin, iutA gene encoding aerobactin, and entABCEFS, fepABCDG, and fes genes encoding enterobactin. These findings align with those of Liao et al. [23], who reported that K. pneumoniae commonly possesses type I and type III pili, which facilitate bacterial pathogenesis by attaching to biological surfaces (e.g., urogenital epithelial cells) and non-biological surfaces (e.g., catheters), thereby initiating colonization, biofilm formation, and invasion processes [24]. Iron uptake systems (such as salmonellin, aerobactin, and yersiniabactin) help bacteria acquire iron under iron-deficient conditions, supporting their metabolic and proliferative capabilities [25]. Iron acquisition not only underpins bacterial survival but also significantly contributes to pathogenicity and drug resistance, enhancing bacterial adaptability to host immune defenses and antibiotic therapies [24].

This study conducted MLST on 70 CRKP isolates and identified six distinct STs, with ST11 being the predominant subtype (61.4%), consistent with previous findings [6, 26]. While ST258 is the predominant CRKP subtype in Europe and the United States, ST11 prevails in China and South America [27]. The ST11 subtype results from a single tonB gene mutation in ST258, and both originate from the clonal complex CC258 [7]. According to drug resistance gene analysis, ST11-type CRKP primarily carried the blaKPC−2 gene, aligning with the observation that ST11-blaKPC−2 is the dominant genotype in China [28]. ST231, second only to ST11 in prevalence, was mainly isolated from the neurosurgery ward in this study. Although the ST231 isolates were isolated over an extended period without evident temporal clustering, this departmental distribution suggests potential colonization of specific hospital environments or hidden transmission chains. Molecular characterization indicated that ST231 isolates predominantly carried the blaOXA−232 carbapenemase gene, significantly different from the currently reported main epidemic OXA-232-producing K. pneumoniae type (ST15) in China [20]. This clear difference highlights regional specificity in the distribution of ST types carrying blaOXA−232, further highlighting the regional and clinical-environment-specific epidemiological patterns of CRKP. Therefore, infection control strategies must incorporate regional surveillance data to implement precise, targeted interventions.

Conclusion

In summary, this study indicates that CRKP strains isolated from patients with pulmonary infections exhibited high resistance rates to β-lactam antibiotics. Regarding molecular resistance mechanisms, blaKPC−2 was identified as the predominant carbapenemase resistance gene, alongside the coexistence of multiple other resistance genes. MLST analysis confirmed ST11 as the predominant subtype, with isolates carrying numerous virulence-associated genes. Although this study provides valuable insights to inform drug resistance surveillance and preventive strategies in comparable clinical environments, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center, retrospective study with limited sample size, the generalizability of findings may be constrained. Second, the absence of integrated clinical treatment information restricted the analysis of correlations between antimicrobial resistance characteristics and clinical outcomes (e.g., 31.4% hospital mortality rate). Furthermore, some critically ill patients discharged against medical advice due to ineffective treatment or financial constraints, potentially introducing bias into outcome evaluations. Future research should include multicenter, prospective studies integrating comprehensive clinical and follow-up data to achieve a more robust evaluation of CRKP prognosis and associated factors. In addition, further exploration of resistance mechanisms, pathogenic characteristics, and transmission pathways is necessary to support the development of targeted prevention and control measures.
