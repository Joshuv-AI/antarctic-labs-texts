Clinical applications of 4D flow magnetic resonance imaging in hepatic vascular disorders: practical biomarkers, protocol design, and translational implementation
Authors: Hanxiang Liu, Lingyun Xu, Jing Yang, Liang Chen, Yuwei Chen, Yueyan Zhao, Shuguang Han, Jiali Wang, Tong Wang, Tongyu Wang, Rongkai Yan, Huixian Zhou, Jiling Zeng, Qingqiao Zhang, Siyuan Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatic vascular disorders include portal hypertension in cirrhosis, Budd-Chiari syndrome (BCS), hepatic vascular malformations, and vascular complications after liver transplantation. These diseases depend on blood-flow direction, flow redistribution, and pressure transmission, and not only on gross vessel shape. Doppler ultrasound, computed tomography angiography (CTA), and digital subtraction angiography (DSA) are widely used in current clinical practice, but each has distinct limits for full hemodynamic assessment, and repeated follow-up can be difficult to implement effectively in daily clinical work. Four-dimensional flow magnetic resonance imaging (4D flow MRI) is a time-resolved three-dimensional (3D) phase-contrast MRI method with three-directional velocity encoding (VENC). It provides volumetric flow and multidirectional velocity data across the cardiac cycle, and it allows flexible retrospective plane placement and comprehensive network-level analysis. It can accurately measure flow volume, peak and mean velocity, retrograde fraction, fractional flow change, and shear-related metrics. It also supports pressure-gradient estimation when combined with physics-based modeling in selected clinical settings. Hepatic imaging still faces multiple major constraints, including VENC tradeoffs, respiratory motion, limited small-vessel resolution, and the need for unified standardized acquisition and analysis. Workflow limits from segmentation and plane placement can be greatly reduced by modern automated analysis. This review systematically summarizes technical foundations, processing steps, disease-focused applications, and practical barriers for clinical translation.

Introduction

Hepatic vascular disorders feature altered vascular resistance, aberrant flow redistribution, and impaired venous drainage, which cause liver injury and clinical complications. In cirrhosis, elevated intrahepatic resistance increases portal pressure and remodels portomesenteric hemodynamics, promoting collateral formation and a dynamic balance among portal inflow, collateral shunting, and hepatic arterial compensation (1). In Budd-Chiari syndrome (BCS), hepatic venous outflow obstruction causes sinusoidal congestion and secondary portal hypertension, and it reshapes intrahepatic drainage and collateral pathways. Hepatic vascular malformations create direct shunts between arterial, portal, and systemic venous compartments, and their clinical impact depends on shunt size and direction (2). After liver transplantation, graft outcome depends on stable hepatic arterial inflow, portal inflow, and venous outflow, and functional impairment can appear before clear laboratory deterioration (3).

In these disorders, anatomy alone often does not explain symptoms, complications, or treatment response. Stenoses become clinically significant when inducing hemodynamic disturbances, while collaterals alter the risks of decompression, bleeding, and encephalopathy via excessive shunting. A shunt or a stent can look acceptable on morphology but may remain functionally defective due to focal flow acceleration, local stasis, or altered perfusion routing (4,5). Accordingly, most clinical inquiries focus on hemodynamic function. Clinicians require quantitative data on vascular perfusion volume, flow destination, flow reversal, and network redistribution following disease progression or interventional therapy (6).

Conventional imaging modalities only partially address these clinical needs. Doppler ultrasound is widely used and provides real-time velocity estimates, but it samples limited sites and is sensitive to acoustic windows and operator technique. Computed tomography (CT) angiography (CTA) shows vascular anatomy well, but it does not provide direct volumetric flow quantification and it has contrast and radiation constraints in many chronic patients. Catheter-based angiography and pressure measurements can resolve specific questions, but they are invasive and not suited to repeated follow-up (7). These gaps are most visible in portal hypertension and post-intervention care, where management depends on hemodynamic severity, collateral burden, and shunt function over time.

Four-dimensional flow magnetic resonance imaging (4D flow MRI) offers a different approach. It acquires time-resolved, three-dimensional flow data and enables retrospective analysis at any volumetric position (8,9). This advantage is well suited for hepatic vasculature featuring tortuous vessels, intricate branching and heterogeneous collateral pathways. It further eliminates reliance on predefined scan planes, a critical drawback of conventional 2D phase-contrast imaging for portomesenteric evaluation. Quantitatively, 4D flow MRI yields volumetric flow, velocity profiles and directional indices including retrograde flow fraction. Optimized post-processing additionally enables calculation of shear and flow complexity metrics to characterize hemodynamic impairment within stenoses, vascular shunts and post-surgical vasculature (10).

At the same time, hepatic 4D flow MRI has practical barriers. The coexistence of slow portal flow and rapid arterial or stenotic jet flow creates inherent velocity encoding (VENC) matching challenges. Respiratory motion can degrade phase-based measurements (11,12). Post-processing has traditionally been time-consuming and sensitive to segmentation quality and plane placement. Recent improvements in accelerated acquisition and automated analysis have reduced these barriers and have made broader clinical workflows more realistic. This creates a need for a focused review that links technical choices to measurable biomarkers, and links those biomarkers to specific hepatic vascular diseases and clinical decisions.

In this review, we summarize key acquisition and processing considerations for hepatic 4D flow MRI, then we discuss disease-specific applications in portal hypertension, hepatic venous outflow obstruction, hepatic vascular malformations, and post-transplant vascular complications. We emphasize quantitative biomarkers that can be measured with current methods, and we describe how these outputs can support diagnosis, risk stratification, intervention planning, and longitudinal surveillance (13).

Technical foundations of hepatic 4D flow MRI

Physical basis

4D flow MRI is a phase-contrast MRI method. Moving blood causes a phase shift when bipolar gradients are applied, and the phase shift scales with velocity along the encoding direction. Encoding along three orthogonal axes gives three velocity components, so each voxel has a velocity vector at each cardiac phase. The dataset includes magnitude images for lumen definition and phase images for VENC. The phase signal also contains non-flow contributions from eddy currents, gradient nonlinearity, and concomitant fields, so background phase correction is required (14). A common approach estimates slowly varying phase offsets from static tissue, subtracts them from the velocity maps, and then applies additional steps for phase-wrap handling and noise suppression (15).

Volumetric acquisition stabilizes flow quantification for tortuous, branching hepatic vasculature. Conventional two-dimensional (2D) phase-contrast needs predefined slice positioning pre-scan, with accuracy reliant on perpendicularity between imaging plane and vascular long axis. Oblique angulation underestimates through-plane flow and yields artificially low readings. By contrast, 4D flow MRI enables retrospective centerline alignment post-scan. Measurements can be acquired away from bifurcations, signal dropout, or strong curvature, enabling more consistent repeated measurements (16).

Technical constraints in hepatic circulation

Hepatic 4D flow MRI must cover a wide velocity range within one field of view. Portal venous flow is slow (~10–20 cm/s), while hepatic arterial flow is faster (~30–50 cm/s), and focal jets at anastomoses, stenoses, and shunts can exceed 100 cm/s (8). This creates a VENC tradeoff that affects both slow-flow sensitivity and aliasing risk, with protocol strategies detailed in later in this review.

Respiratory motion is a major constraint. The liver translates and deforms during breathing, changing vessel position and local flow direction and triggering misregistration between magnitude and phase images at vessel edges, which damages segmentation and near-wall velocity sampling (17). Such artifacts become prominent in patients with ascites or poor breath control, further destabilizing cardiac-phase-related hemodynamic measurements.

Spatial resolution limitations also affect quantification accuracy. Fine portomesenteric branches and small collaterals often approximate clinical voxel size, causing partial-volume averaging between blood and static tissue and underestimating flow velocity. This notably impairs wall shear stress (WSS) calculation, which depends on precise near-wall velocity gradients. Low SNR in slow portal flow amplifies random velocity noise and biases shear- and energy-related parameters. Currently, WSS derived from hepatic 4D flow MRI has been reported only in single-center feasibility studies (n=15–30) without multi-center validation or established normal ranges. Its clinical utility for predicting variceal bleeding or shunt dysfunction remains unproven (18). Plane placement and segmentation quality are critical for metric reliability. Multidirectional flow, flow reversal, and high-speed jets within shunts/stents make peak velocity and retrograde fraction highly sensitive to slight plane offset, leading to inconsistent quantification results (10).

Collectively, VENC compromise, respiratory motion, limited spatial resolution and complex in-vivo flow patterns constitute the core technical barriers of hepatic 4D flow MRI, with detailed protocol configuration and post-processing solutions elaborated separately in the following sections.

In addition, computational fluid dynamics (CFD)-based intravascular pressure quantification and blood flow energy loss calculation remain purely experimental techniques without standardized measuring protocols; their outcomes cannot be reliably extrapolated to patients complicated with severe ascites or complicated vascular malformations owing to limitations in spatial resolution and segmentation precision. The combination of 4D-flow MRI and CFD has been regarded as an innovative strategy for hemodynamic assessment in hepatobiliary and pancreatic disorders (19).

Acquisition and motion control

ECG gating is commonly used to resolve cardiac-phase variation. Respiratory control is implemented using navigator gating, self-navigation, or free-breathing acquisition with motion compensation. Breath-hold approaches can reduce respiratory artifacts in selected patients, yet breath-hold duration limits coverage and resolution, and failed breath-holds can cause severe artifact. Free-breathing approaches can provide stable coverage in patients who cannot cooperate with long breath-holds, yet they require strategies that reduce motion blur and maintain phase consistency (20).

Reported abdominal protocols illustrate practical operating points (10). Whole-volume abdominal 4D flow acquisitions have been performed with imaging volumes on the order of 32 cm × 32 cm × 24–32 cm, isotropic reconstructed voxel size around 1.25 mm, and scan times around ~12 minutes under respiratory bellows control with about 50% acceptance. Temporal resolution has been reported around ~67 ms, with cardiac time frames commonly in the ~10 range for some abdominal implementations. These numbers define what the dataset can and cannot resolve. Such temporal and spatial resolution limitations impair hemodynamic quantification as detailed in earlier in this review. Acceleration methods reduce scan time and shift practical tradeoffs. Parallel imaging uses coil sensitivity information to undersample k-space. Compressed sensing uses sparse reconstruction to recover images from stronger undersampling. k-t approaches use correlations in space and time. These methods reduce scan time, yet stronger acceleration tends to lower effective SNR and can introduce spatial or temporal smoothing. Smoothing can reduce noise, yet it can also reduce true peak velocities in narrow jets and can blunt steep gradients used for WSS-related measures (21).

VENC, aliasing control, and protocol logic

VENC sets the measurable velocity dynamic range. Phase wraps occur when true velocity exceeds VENC. Unwrapping is feasible in many cases, yet it is less reliable in low magnitude regions, near vessel edges, and in jet regions where intravoxel dephasing reduces signal. When VENC is set too high, slow portal flow produces small phase shifts, and the velocity map becomes dominated by noise and residual background phase (10).

Reported hepatic protocols often use VENC values such as ~60 cm/s for portal-focused measurements and ~100 cm/s when higher velocities are expected or when contrast support is used. In venous outflow obstruction and jet-dominant settings, higher VENC values such as ~120–150 cm/s have been used for focused acquisitions around a stenotic channel. These values show the practical compromise: a lower VENC supports portal sensitivity, while a higher VENC reduces aliasing in jets (22).

Post-processing pipeline and hemodynamic metrics

Post-processing determines whether the dataset supports quantitative reporting. Background phase correction removes spatially smooth non-flow phase offsets. Anti-aliasing is applied where VENC is exceeded. Noise filtering reduces random phase noise while preserving true gradients in narrow jets (10). Vessel segmentation defines the lumen used for measurement, and segmentation quality affects nearly all downstream metrics (23). Centerline extraction supports consistent plane placement.

Net volumetric flow is calculated by integrating through-plane velocity over the segmented lumen for each cardiac phase and then averaging across the cycle. Peak velocity is often defined as the maximum velocity magnitude within the lumen, yet this value is sensitive to noise spikes and single-voxel artifacts. Robust peak definitions often use percentile peaks or spatial constraints to avoid isolated outliers. Mean velocity depends on segmentation because near-wall voxels often include partial-volume signal that reduces measured velocity (8). Retrograde flow fraction is derived from negative through-plane flow relative to absolute flow, so it is sensitive to plane orientation and residual background phase. Fractional flow change between connected segments requires stable plane placement across the vessel path and stable segmentation at both planes (10).

Streamlines and pathlines provide qualitative flow tracing, yet they are also sensitive to vector noise, so visualization should be paired with quantitative checks such as flow conservation at junctions and stability of measurements across nearby planes (18).

Automated analysis methods reduce manual variability in segmentation and plane placement. Automated portal venous segmentation has been reported with Dice values around 0.81 for the portal venous system and 0.87 for the main portal vein, with flow-rate agreement of ρ ≥0.96, and segmentation time reduced from about 35 minutes to under 10 seconds. These results support automated pipelines, yet quality control is still required for abnormal anatomy, heavy motion, and low-signal segments (20). Automated plane placement based on centerlines also needs constraints to avoid bifurcations and artifact-prone segments. Standard landmarks and standard reporting fields remain necessary for comparability across patients and across follow-up exams. At present, automated analysis should be considered a supportive tool requiring manual quality control, not a replacement for expert review. Multi-center validation data are insufficient for widespread routine clinical use.

Hemodynamic interpretation as measurable clinical signals

Clinical interpretation starts from the velocity field and converts it into reproducible measures linked to decisions. Volumetric flow is used when the key question is inflow adequacy, diversion magnitude, or shunt dominance. Retrograde flow fraction is used when flow reversal indicates abnormal routing or decompression pathways. Peak velocity and localized acceleration are used when focal narrowing or anastomotic dysfunction is suspected. Fractional flow change across connected segments is used when redistribution within a network is the main signal (24).

Internal validity checks are part of routine interpretation. Mass conservation across junctions provides a check for segmentation and plane placement. Agreement between repeated planes in the same straight segment provides a check for local artifacts. Stable flow direction across cardiac phases provides a check against residual phase offsets that can flip direction in low-flow regions. These checks reduce the risk of treating reconstruction artifacts as disease signals and support use of 4D flow MRI as a quantitative exam (25) (Figure 1).

Based on published clinical findings, all hemodynamic biomarkers involved in this review are summarized into three grades: (I) clinically available: volumetric flow, mean velocity and retrograde fraction; these parameters have mature clinical data and can be used for routine clinical assessment. (II) Preliminary evidence: fractional flow change; existing cohort studies prove its diagnostic value, yet uniform clinical application standards are lacking. (III) Experimental only: WSS, energy loss and CFD-derived non-invasive pressure estimation; relevant evidence is limited to small-scale researches and cannot be applied in daily clinical work at present.

Portal hypertension in cirrhosis

Pathophysiologic rationale for 4D flow MRI

Portal hypertension in cirrhosis reflects increased intrahepatic resistance plus changes in splanchnic inflow and flow routing. The portal system becomes a coupled network in which inflow from the splenic vein and superior mesenteric vein is redistributed at the confluence, then partitioned into intrahepatic portal branches and extrahepatic collateral pathways. In this scenario, single-site velocity sampling is insufficient, as hemodynamic alterations are driven primarily by flow redistribution rather than simple flow deceleration. A volumetric velocity dataset allows measurement at fixed anatomic landmarks, and it supports internal consistency checks such as conservation of mass at the spleno-mesenteric confluence and at the portal bifurcation, which is important for quality control in low-flow vessels (10,24,26).

Hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) is pressure-based, but it is invasive and it represents a limited compartment. Endoscopy describes variceal status, but it does not quantify portal inflow, collateral diversion, or post-intervention routing. Doppler ultrasound enables portal velocity measurement but relies on sparse sampling and is highly susceptible to insonation angle and acoustic window quality. In contrast, 4D flow MRI can quantify flow in the main portal vein, splenic vein, and superior mesenteric vein in the same exam, and it can add direction-based measures that represent flow reversal or bidirectional flow. These direction-based measures become relevant when hepatofugal components appear in segments near collaterals or near shunts (27,28).

Noninvasive assessment of clinically significant portal hypertension

Valid noninvasive hemodynamic markers should reflect the functional state of clinically significant portal hypertension, conventionally defined by an HVPG threshold of ≥10 mmHg. In a prospective cohort that included 71 patients with cirrhosis and 11 healthy controls, portal venous 4D flow MRI parameters were extracted from the portal vein and then tested against this pressure threshold (29). The study used a normalized flow volume across one cardiac cycle [Qnormal, reported as ml·m2/(cycle·kg)] and a portal-vein regurgitant fraction (R%). In that cohort, Qnormal was higher in the clinically significant portal hypertension group [0.66±0.19 ml·m2/(cycle·kg)] and R% was also higher (median 1.98 with an interquartile range of 2.05). Using receiver-operating curves, Qnormal showed an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.93 and R% showed an AUC of 0.91 for diagnosing clinically significant portal hypertension, and a multivariable model combining these features reached an AUC of 0.95.

These measures are not simple “faster or slower flow” markers. Qnormal minimizes the confounding effects of body size and weight on raw flow values, serving as a standardized index of cyclic portal perfusion. R% is a direction-based marker that becomes non-zero when a measurable fraction of the cardiac-cycle flow is reversed across the chosen analysis plane. In portal hypertension, R% can increase when local flow becomes unstable near collateral outflow regions or when shunt-related routing produces mixed forward and backward components (30).

Protocol details matter because they shape the dynamic range and the noise floor. In the same cohort above, the reported 4D flow acquisition covered a field of view of 25 cm × 25 cm × 15 cm centered on the portal vein, with a 3-mm acquired slice thickness (30). Separate studies in abdominal 4D flow MRI have used portal-focused VENC values such as 60 cm/s, which improves sensitivity to portal velocities in the 10–30 cm/s range but increases aliasing risk in faster arterial or stenotic segments (31).

Prediction of high-risk gastroesophageal varices

Variceal bleeding risk is determined not only by portal pressure but also by patterned flow redistribution within collateral networks. A flow-network measurement can therefore add value by capturing redistribution patterns in the porto-mesenteric system (32). In a prospective cohort of 38 patients with esophageal or gastric varices, porto-mesenteric 4D flow MRI quantified peak flow, average flow, and peak velocity at standardized sites that included the portal vein confluence (PV1), portal hilum (PV2), splenic vein hilum (SV1), splenic confluence (SV2), and the superior mesenteric vein. The analysis also measured portal and splenic fractional flow changes between paired sites.

In that cohort, ROC analysis showed that PV2 average flow and portal fractional average flow change each reached 100% sensitivity for high-risk varices. The portal fractional peak flow change had an AUC of 0.936 with a cut-off of <−0.348, and it achieved 83.3% sensitivity and 92.3% specificity (accuracy 87.1%). The study also reported that SV1 average flow, SV2 average flow, SV2 peak flow, and SV2 peak velocity were higher in patients with esophageal compared with gastric varices, with P values reported as 0.022, <0.001, <0.001, and 0.001, respectively (32).

These findings validate comparative metrics along identical vascular segments. Fractional flow change quantifies inter-segmental flow loss, reflecting collateral shunting undetectable via single-plane sampling. This method reduces reliance on absolute flow values, which are susceptible to variations in fasting status, cardiac output, and pharmacotherapy. Still, these measures require strict standardization of site definitions (for example PV1 vs. PV2) and consistent plane placement away from bifurcations because branch inflow can change measured peak flow and can bias fractional change (33).

Collateral circulation and network mapping

Collateral vessels are clinically important when they carry substantial flow and when they alter portal delivery or bleeding risk. Conventional anatomical imaging can identify collateral vessels but fails to quantify their hemodynamic dominance. 4D flow MRI can measure direction and net flow through a collateral when the collateral is within the acquisition volume and has adequate spatial resolution. This is especially relevant for large spontaneous shunts such as gastrorenal and splenorenal shunts, because they can divert a large fraction of portal inflow and they can change interpretation of portal trunk measurements. In patients with dominant shunts, substantial splanchnic inflow may coexist with reduced portal trunk perfusion; thus, isolated portal trunk flow cannot reliably reflect disease severity. A network dataset allows paired measurement of splenic inflow, mesenteric inflow, portal trunk delivery, and measured shunt flow, so routing can be interpreted directly (20).

A second routing effect is hepatic arterial compensation. Portal hypertension can be associated with changes in hepatic arterial inflow that partially compensate for reduced portal delivery. A portal-only assessment can therefore miss clinically relevant inflow redistribution (34). In a practical workflow, portal-system metrics are interpreted together with arterial inflow when the acquisition includes the hepatic artery and when VENC settings and resolution are adequate for that compartment. This paired interpretation is important because portal redistribution and arterial compensation can produce similar portal trunk measurements in different physiologic states (35).

Transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) planning and post-TIPS surveillance

Post-TIPS clinical management targets adequate portal decompression and sustained shunt patency. Anatomical patency alone is unreliable, as functionally significant focal stenosis and segmental pressure loss may be invisible on routine morphological imaging (36). Velocity mapping inside the stent and at its inflow and outflow regions can identify focal acceleration patterns that suggest functional stenosis (37). Volumetric flow through the stent can be compared with portal inflow and with hepatic venous outflow routing to describe whether decompression is being achieved through the intended pathway.

A physics-based extension couples measured 4D flow velocity fields with CFD to estimate the post-TIPS portosystemic pressure gradient (PSPG). In a pilot cohort of 20 participants with cirrhosis and TIPS, noninvasive PSPG estimates correlated with invasive measurements (r=0.77; P<0.001). The method correctly classified whether PSPG was greater than 12 mmHg in 16 of 20 participants (80%), with two false negatives and two false positives. Among five participants with venographic stenosis and invasively measured PSPG >12 mmHg, two were misclassified as ≤12 mmHg by the noninvasive method (38).

These findings highlight key technical limitations affecting the clinical translation of noninvasive CFD-based pressure estimation. Misclassification occurred in a subset that included stenosis cases, which indicates that segmentation fidelity, boundary conditions, and spatial resolution at the stenotic region can directly affect pressure estimates. This also indicates that velocity noise, aliasing, and intravoxel dephasing in stent jets can propagate into the pressure field when CFD is applied. For clinical use, this places emphasis on quality control of velocity maps inside the shunt, confirmation of stable inflow and outflow plane placement, and explicit reporting of regions with low magnitude signal or severe phase wraps. BCS

Hemodynamic features relevant to 4D flow MRI

BCS is defined by hepatic venous outflow obstruction at the hepatic veins, the hepatic inferior vena cava, or both. Cross-sectional imaging can show thrombosis, membranous webs, and focal narrowing, and it can show indirect signs such as caudate lobe enlargement and heterogeneous enhancement (39). Yet the clinical state depends on functional drainage. A narrow channel can still pass flow, and a similar-looking lesion can behave differently if the dominant return uses collateral veins (40). This makes a velocity-field exam useful because it can show jet flow through a small aperture, and it can show stagnation and recirculation upstream, and it can show whether net flow becomes antegrade or remains hepatofugal in key segments (41). Respiration is a strong modifier of abdominal venous return, and it can change both the magnitude and direction of inferior vena cava transport. A respiratory-resolved 4D flow approach has shown clear phase-dependent differences in outflow routing. In one documented Budd-Chiari case with membranous IVC stenosis, expiratory-phase 4D flow MRI showed complex venous patterns with vortex flow in a dilated suprarenal IVC, and inspiratory-phase imaging showed more continuous transport from infrarenal IVC toward the right atrium through the narrowed channel (41). These patterns provide a functional description of “how drainage happens”, not only “where the stenosis is”.

Lesion localization and functional grading

Functional grading can be anchored to measurable changes across a lesion. Hemodynamically significant membranous or short-segment stenosis generates focal high-velocity jets, accompanied by diminished distal antegrade flow and elevated proximal recirculation. These features can be detected by placing analysis planes in laminar segments on each side of the lesion, and by using streamline or pathline visualization to confirm dominant routing (42). In the reported respiratory-gated Budd-Chiari case, the stenotic site produced a visually evident jet stream at the membranous IVC channel, and the direction of infrarenal IVC flow differed from the suprahepatic IVC flow, which was consistent with collateral escape at one level and antegrade passage through the aperture at another level (43).

Catheter pressure data provide a concrete frame for interpreting these flow findings. In that same case, invasive measurement reported IVC pressure of 10 mmHg and right atrial pressure of 5 mmHg, which indicated a 5 mmHg gradient across the lesion. After balloon dilation using a 12 mm × 40 mm balloon, the reported gradient remained 4 mmHg. This combination illustrates a common scenario in Budd-Chiari intervention: visible anatomic narrowing can persist and the gradient can fall only modestly, while net transport and routing can still improve (44). A flow-based exam can therefore be used to describe treatment effect in terms of drainage efficiency, not only lumen change.

Quantitative metrics and concrete measurement targets

The most practical metrics in BCS are flow direction, flow rate in major venous conduits, and jet velocity at the restrictive segment. Flow rate is best measured in a proximal segment where flow is closer to laminar (45), as intravoxel dephasing within turbulent jets degrades signal amplitude and biases peak velocity quantification (46). In the same Budd-Chiari case, the infrarenal IVC flow was measured proximal to the stenosis at a plane selected for laminar flow conditions. The reported IVC flow rate increased to 30.4 mL/s after intervention, and the infrarenal IVC flow direction changed to antegrade. These are concrete functional endpoints that align with the clinical goal of restoring effective venous return (41). Jet velocity is a second useful endpoint because it tracks the hemodynamic penalty of a restrictive channel (47). Doppler ultrasound in that same case reported a decrease in maximum jet velocity from 150–200 cm/s to 60–100 cm/s after treatment. These values define the practical dynamic range that a Budd-Chiari membranous lesion can produce, and they explain why a venous-optimized VENC can fail if it is set too low.

Respiratory-driven flow fluctuation can be quantified as a useful biomarker instead of being regarded as imaging artifact. When infrarenal IVC transport is intermittent or reverses during expiration, and becomes more antegrade during inspiration, the patient’s effective drainage becomes sensitive to breathing mechanics (41). A respiratory-phase 4D flow dataset can document this dependence explicitly, and it can support follow-up by checking whether post-treatment flow becomes less phase-dependent (48).

Acquisition and parameter examples used for Budd-Chiari evaluation

Budd-Chiari-dedicated protocols require dual optimization: adequate sensitivity for sluggish venous flow in dilated veins and sufficient dynamic range to capture high-velocity stenotic jets (49). One published workflow used whole-abdomen 4D flow MRI with a coronal volume and reported acquired voxel size of 2.0 mm × 1.6 mm × 3.0 mm with reconstructed voxel size of about 1.4 mm × 1.4 mm × 1.5 mm, and 10 cardiac time frames. The same report used VENC settings of 50 and 100 cm/s for whole-volume imaging, and it noted aliasing at the lower VENC. A focused acquisition around the stenosis used an axial orientation with higher VENC values of 120 and 150 cm/s, and the reported scan times for the focused scan were 5:32 and 4:19 minutes (10). These concrete settings show the protocol logic: low VENC supports slow-flow quantification, and higher VENC supports jet characterization.

These parameters also explain how analysis choices are made. Flow integration is placed in laminar segments to reduce partial-volume and turbulence bias (50), while jet segments are used mainly for localization and qualitative grading unless spatial resolution and magnitude signal are strong enough for stable quantitative sampling (51,52).

Post-intervention follow-up signals that can be reported

Post-procedural assessment can be described in a standardized way using direction, magnitude, and routing. Direction can be reported as correction of infrarenal IVC flow to antegrade. Magnitude can be reported as change in flow rate in a proximal plane that remains in a laminar segment across studies. Routing can be reported as reduction of collateral dominance and reduction of recirculation zones in dilated segments. In the documented Budd-Chiari case, post-intervention 4D flow MRI showed antegrade correction of flow direction in the infrarenal IVC and a rise in proximal IVC flow rate to 30.4 mL/s, while streamline visualization still showed high-velocity flow at a segment that remained somewhat narrow. This pattern enables clear differentiation between anatomical residual stenosis and functional hemodynamic improvement (41).

Independent Budd-Chiari case reports also support similar endpoints at the hepatic-vein to right-atrium route. One report described membranous stenosis with jet flow between the middle hepatic vein and the right atrium on preoperative 4D flow MRI, and it described postoperative restoration of flow from the IVC and middle hepatic vein to the right atrium (43). Hepatic vascular malformations

Flow characterization and shunt physiology

Hepatic vascular malformations are defined by abnormal vascular connections that allow blood to bypass the normal sinusoidal pathway (53). The key lesion is a direct communication between vessel compartments, so the clinical phenotype depends on shunt type, shunt direction, and shunt magnitude (54). In normal physiology, most hepatic blood flow comes from the portal vein, and typical estimates place portal contribution at about 75–80% of total hepatic inflow (55). A shunt that increases arterial inflow into venous channels changes this balance and can shift the liver toward arterial-dominant perfusion or toward portal overloading, depending on the connectivity (55). In practical imaging terms, the relevant outputs are not only vessel size or early enhancement timing. The outputs that change management are the amount of flow entering the shunt pathway, the amount of flow leaving through the draining pathway, and the fraction of total hepatic inflow that is diverted away from parenchymal perfusion.

Time-resolved 3D velocity mapping quantifies net volumetric flow at user-defined cross-sections and enables retrospective plane positioning along tortuous vasculature. In abdominal venous applications, internal consistency checks are commonly used to verify flow measurements. For example, measuring the splenic vein and superior mesenteric vein inflow and comparing their sum with main portal vein flow provides a conservation check, and published abdominal 4D flow work reports small mean errors on the order of a few percent in such junction checks when planes are placed carefully away from non-laminar regions (23). This type of conservation logic is important in malformation imaging because shunts create new junctions and new outflow routes, and a flow exam should report both the shunt pathway and the residual physiologic pathways in the same volume (56).

Hepatic arteriovenous malformations and diffuse shunting patterns

Hepatic arteriovenous malformations can occur as focal lesions (57), and they can also occur as diffuse vascular malformation patterns in hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (58). In diffuse hepatic involvement, abnormal communications often involve the hepatic artery as the driving inflow source, and the dominant shunt pattern can be hepatic artery to hepatic vein (arteriosystemic) or hepatic artery to portal vein (arterioportal). The physiologic consequences differ by shunt type. Arteriosystemic shunting routes arterial blood into the systemic venous system, and it can increase cardiac preload and contribute to high-output cardiac failure. Arterioportal shunting delivers arterial pressure and arterial flow into the portal vein, and it can produce presinusoidal portal hypertension with ascites, varices, and splenomegaly (59).

Several concrete imaging thresholds used in clinical screening highlight the hemodynamic nature of these lesions. Key Doppler markers include extrahepatic proper hepatic arterial dilation, with an early cutoff diameter exceeding 4–5 mm. Increased arterial velocity is another early signal, and peak proper hepatic artery velocity greater than 100 cm/s has been suggested as an indicator of hepatic vascular malformations in this setting. Doppler waveform changes also reflect shunt direction, including “arterialized” venous waveforms in hepatic veins for arteriosystemic shunts, and reversed pulsatile flow in portal branches for arterioportal shunts (59).

4D flow MRI can support a quantitative shunt model by measuring inflow in the hepatic artery or proximal feeding artery segments, and by measuring outflow in the draining hepatic veins or portal branches, when those vessels are included in the acquisition volume and have adequate lumen-to-voxel size (56). It can also measure net portal trunk flow at standard locations and compare it with mesenteric and splenic inflow, which helps separate true portal inflow reduction from diversion through a dominant shunt pathway (24). In diffuse malformations, the goal of quantification is often not a single “shunt fraction” number, but a compartment-level description that reports arterial inflow load, portal exposure, and systemic venous runoff in the same exam, so follow-up can track whether the dominant pathway is stable or changing (60).

Intervention decisions in hepatic arteriovenous malformations are tightly linked to hemodynamic burden, yet some endovascular strategies carry high risk in diffuse hepatic vascular malformations. Reported outcomes in symptomatic hepatic malformation management describe liver transplantation as the curative option with reported survival in the range of 82–92% (61). In contrast, hepatic artery embolization has been associated with substantial risk, with reported mortality around 10% and complication rates around 20% requiring re-intervention in this context (62). These clinical outcomes highlight the value of noninvasive 4D flow MRI for individualized treatment planning and avoiding high-risk unnecessary interventions.

Portal venous malformations and cavernous transformation

Portal venous malformations and chronic portal vein obstruction with cavernous transformation create an alternative venous network that replaces or bypasses the normal portal trunk. Conventional cross-sectional imaging delineates cavernous transformation as tangled collateral channels within the native portal venous bed (63). core hemodynamic concerns center on the adequacy of hepatopetal perfusion and the risk of abnormal flow diversion or reversal. Descriptive radiology resources commonly note that cavernous transformation is often associated with continuous hepatopetal flow within the collateral network, although the pattern can vary by patient and by the balance of upstream and downstream resistance.

A whole-volume velocity dataset can quantify how much flow traverses the collateral network, how much enters the liver through intrahepatic portal branches, and how much is diverted into portosystemic routes (20). Practical analysis relies on standardized plane placement and on internal consistency checks at junctions. In portal-system 4D flow methods, the direction of flow, maximum and mean velocity, and flow volume are treated as core parameters, and flow volume is commonly reported in L/min because portal flow is relatively steady across the cardiac cycle. In this framework, cavernous transformation can be described as a set of measured parallel channels, each contributing a portion of net hepatopetal delivery. This is clinically relevant when considering recanalization, surgical reconstruction, or surveillance, because a cavernoma with robust hepatopetal flow is a different functional state than a cavernoma with marginal delivery and strong diversion (64).

Differentiation from hypervascular tumors in selected cases

Hypervascular tumors and vascular malformations can overlap on arterial-phase imaging because both can show strong enhancement and prominent vessels. Conventional MRI evaluation of hypervascular liver lesions depends heavily on enhancement pattern, lesion margins, and background liver status, and radiology reviews emphasize structured approaches to differentiate benign and malignant lesions using multiphasic imaging features. In difficult cases, a flow-based exam can add a different type of signal that is not based on enhancement alone. A true arteriovenous shunt shows direct coupling between an arterial inflow route and a venous outflow route with early venous loading, and it can produce a narrow high-throughput pathway rather than diffuse microvascular perfusion heterogeneity. In contrast, tumor vascularity often reflects complex microvascular networks that do not behave like a single dominant circuit, even when the lesion is hypervascular (65).

In practice, a supportive 4D flow MRI pattern for a malformation would include a measurable high-throughput arterial-to-venous or arterial-to-portal pathway, with a consistent direction of net flow and a coherent streamline pattern along a defined connection (66), while the residual portal and hepatic venous flows remain explainable by conservation across junctions. Portal-system 4D flow methodology also emphasizes avoiding non-laminar regions when placing planes for quantitative reporting, which matters because both malformations and tumors can create local vortices and signal loss that bias peak velocities and flow integration (67).

Liver transplantation and post-transplant vascular complications

The graft as a hemodynamic system

Hepatic arterial complications may induce rapid early graft injury within days post-transplant (68), whereas impaired portal or venous drainage triggers progressive allograft dysfunction. Hepatic artery thrombosis is reported across series in a broad range, and a commonly cited range is about 4% to 15% after orthotopic liver transplantation. Portal vein stenosis and portal vein thrombosis can reduce portal delivery, raise upstream portal pressure, and trigger collateral pathways, so the graft can show impaired perfusion even when cross-sectional anatomy looks only mildly abnormal (69). Hepatic venous or caval outflow problems can cause graft congestion and impaired sinusoidal drainage, and this can present as graft swelling and abnormal liver tests before a clear focal lesion is seen (70).

In this setting, a flow-based exam targets three linked questions. It measures whether arterial inflow is preserved at and beyond the anastomosis, whether portal inflow is adequate and correctly routed into intrahepatic branches, and whether venous drainage is unobstructed (71). A key point is that early dysfunction can start as a functional change, such as a focal velocity step-up with downstream damping, or a flow redistribution pattern that precedes frank thrombosis. This is the physiologic niche for volumetric velocity mapping, because it can report both local lesion behavior and whole-network consequences (72).

Hepatic artery assessment

The hepatic artery is the critical conduit for biliary viability, so surveillance focuses on detecting hemodynamically significant stenosis and early thrombosis. Doppler ultrasound uses a small set of quantitative thresholds that reflect stenosis physiology. Post-transplant reference ranges commonly cited include hepatic artery resistive index (RI) greater than 0.5, systolic acceleration time (SAT) less than 0.08 seconds, and peak systolic velocity (PSV) at the anastomosis below 200 cm/s. Findings used to support hepatic artery stenosis include PSV above about 200 to 250 cm/s at the anastomosis, plus a downstream “tardus-parvus” pattern with acceleration time above 0.08 seconds and RI below 0.5. These Doppler thresholds guide 4D flow protocol design by defining the target velocity range for aliasing-free measurement and the distal flow-dampening pattern indicative of downstream stenosis (73).

A 4D flow MRI dataset can support a stenosis assessment by sampling velocity magnitude and net flow at standardized sites. A practical scheme places one plane in the extrahepatic hepatic artery proximal to the anastomosis, a second plane at the anastomosis or the tightest visible segment, and one or more planes in intrahepatic branches that are distal and away from bifurcations. This allows measurement of a focal PSV step-up and also allows measurement of distal flow reduction (74). The dataset can also display jet shape and post-stenotic disturbance, because a narrow segment can create a concentrated high-velocity core that expands downstream, which is a pattern that aligns with the Doppler criteria above (74).

Hepatic artery thrombosis is defined clinically by absence of arterial flow, and Doppler uses absent color and spectral signal as the primary sign. A velocity-field exam can support this by showing lack of measurable arterial velocity in the expected arterial path and by documenting whether collateral arterialization is present. This distinction is relevant because delayed collateralization can sometimes maintain partial perfusion, while early complete loss of arterial inflow can cause rapid biliary ischemia (13).

Portal vein complications

Portal vein complications affect graft inflow and also alter upstream portal pressures. Doppler ultrasound criteria for portal vein stenosis commonly use morphologic narrowing and a velocity ratio between the anastomotic site and the pre-stenotic segment. One published approach defined a stenotic ratio above 50% as clinically significant stenosis, and it used a velocity ratio threshold above 3:1 between the anastomosis and the pre-stenotic segment (75). These thresholds are useful anchors for 4D flow MRI because they provide a numeric target for “step-up” behavior and help define what to report across the anastomosis.

A volumetric 4D flow MRI exam can quantify net portal flow and also show how flow is distributed into left and right portal branches, which is often difficult to assess reliably with a single Doppler sample gate in complex postoperative geometry. Portal velocities after transplantation can also differ from native-liver portal velocities. In one Doppler-based dataset, the velocity in the largest portal vein (main or left depending on transplant type) averaged about 65 cm/s (75). This value is not a universal cutoff but confirms higher portal flow velocities in transplant recipients versus cirrhotic patients, necessitating transplant-customized VENC instead of cirrhosis-derived scan parameters.

Partial portal thrombosis can produce a mixed effect in which a portion of the lumen carries flow and another portion carries no flow, so cross-sectional anatomy alone may underestimate functional impact. A 4D flow dataset can measure effective lumen flow by segmenting the flow-carrying region and integrating velocity over that area. It can also show whether portal inflow is being diverted into collaterals, which becomes relevant in chronic or late complications where alternative pathways can develop (8).

Global graft perfusion as measurable inflow-outflow balance

A transplanted liver depends on balanced portal inflow, arterial inflow, and venous drainage. A useful systems view reports total hepatic inflow as the sum of portal inflow and hepatic arterial inflow, and it reports the arterial-to-portal inflow ratio as a compact measure of inflow balance. In normal physiology, portal inflow is the dominant contributor to total hepatic inflow, often described as about 75–80% in standard physiology references (76). After transplantation, this balance can shift in a compensatory way when portal inflow is reduced, and it can also shift in a pathologic way when arterial stenosis or thrombosis reduces arterial inflow.

A volumetric 4D flow MRI dataset can also report intrahepatic distribution symmetry by comparing left and right portal branch flows and comparing left and right hepatic venous drainage patterns when those vessels are included. This distribution information can be clinically relevant in segmental perfusion problems, in size mismatch, and in anastomotic geometry that preferentially directs flow to one branch. It can also support follow-up because the same planes can be placed retrospectively at the same landmarks across serial studies, which improves comparability when anatomy is complex (13).

Practical role in surveillance and escalation pathways

Post-transplant surveillance often starts with Doppler ultrasound, and escalation to CTA, MRA, or angiography is used when Doppler is equivocal or when a high-risk pattern is present. Common Doppler cutoffs (e.g., PSV >200 cm/s for hepatic arterial stenosis with high specificity) are prone to false-negative results, such that normal PSV cannot reliably rule out underlying stenosis (77). A volumetric 4D flow MRI exam can add value in these equivocal settings because it can sample multiple segments in a single dataset and can report both focal acceleration and distal flow penalty. It can also support a structured report that links measured velocities and flow rates to the same hemodynamic concepts used in Doppler interpretation, such as focal step-up, distal damping, and flow redistribution (8) (Figure 4).

Quantitative biomarkers and their clinical meaning

Flow volume

Net volumetric flow is computed by integrating through-plane velocity over the segmented lumen area for each cardiac phase, then averaging across the cardiac cycle. In portal applications, reporting flow in L/min is common because portal venous pulsatility is modest compared with arterial flow, and the main variability comes from respiration and from redistribution across branches. In healthy fasting adults studied with abdominal 4D flow MRI at standardized planes, average flow in the main portal vein was reported around 0.9±0.2 L/min at the plane near the spleno-mesenteric confluence (PV1), and 1.0±0.2 L/min at mid-portal and pre-bifurcation planes (PV2 and PV3). The reported reference ranges for these planes were 0.5–1.2 L/min (PV1) and 0.7–1.4 L/min (PV2) and 0.7–1.3 L/min (PV3). These values provide a concrete baseline for interpreting reduced portal delivery, diversion into collaterals, or post-intervention redistribution (20). Flow consistency checks at junctions are also used as quality control. In the same reference dataset, conservation-of-mass error at the spleno-mesenteric confluence had a reported relative error of 1.1%±4.6% and at the portal bifurcation −1.4%±4.1%.

Standardization limits transferability of absolute flow values. Even with the same disease stage, portal flow changes with fasting state, splanchnic vasodilation, and cardiac output, and large spontaneous shunts can divert flow away from the main portal trunk. For this reason, relative flow partitions within the same exam can be more stable than a single absolute number. Branch partitioning in healthy adults is also asymmetric. Right portal vein flow was reported as 0.7±0.1 L/min and left portal vein flow as 0.3±0.1 L/min in standardized planes distal to the bifurcation. This asymmetry provides a concrete expectation for transplant and portal-reconstruction follow-up, where disproportionate branch delivery can signal anastomotic geometry effects or segmental inflow restriction (23).

Peak and mean velocity

Velocity metrics are directly derived from the velocity field, yet they depend on segmentation, plane placement, and acquisition settings. In healthy fasting adults, average portal vein velocity in the main portal vein was reported as 7.9±1.3 cm/s at PV1, and 8.4±1.3 cm/s at PV2 and 8.4±1.2 cm/s at PV3, with reference ranges of 5.2–10.6 cm/s (PV1), 5.8–10.9 cm/s (PV2), and 6.0–10.9 cm/s (PV3). These values are lower than many Doppler ultrasound “typical” portal velocities because 4D flow MRI averages velocity over the full cross-section, including slower near-wall regions (22). This systematic difference is explicitly discussed in the same reference study, which notes that MRI tends to report lower velocities and larger vessel areas than Doppler, and it cites ultrasound inter-observer variability around 33% and intra-observer variability around 20% in portal flow measurement.

Peak velocity is useful for focal stenoses and shunts, but it is the most sensitive to temporal resolution and to noise spikes. In the healthy portal reference dataset, temporal resolution was reported as 66.8±15.1 ms, and the authors note that lower temporal resolution can undersample true peak systolic flow compared with 2D phase-contrast approaches that often use ~20–40 ms temporal resolution. This has a direct clinical implication: peak velocity thresholds and peak flow thresholds should not be transferred between protocols without accounting for temporal resolution and reconstruction smoothing. Peak velocity interpretation is also tied to VENC, because aliasing at stenotic jets can create false maxima if phase unwrapping fails (23).

Regurgitant fraction and retrograde flow

Retrograde flow fraction is derived by separating forward and backward through-plane flow components over the cardiac cycle, then expressing the backward component as a fraction of total absolute flow (8). In portal disease, a non-zero retrograde fraction can appear when flow becomes bidirectional near collateral outflow, near shunts, or in tributaries that reverse direction. The clinical value is that retrograde fraction is a direction marker, so it can capture routing inefficiency even when net flow is preserved (13). This metric is sensitive to background phase offset correction because portal velocities are low, so small residual offsets can flip low-magnitude velocity voxels from slightly forward to slightly backward. It is also sensitive to plane orientation because oblique planes can introduce projection errors that create artificial negative components in curved segments (10).

Concrete implementation details show how protocols affect stability. In the healthy portal reference dataset, VENC was reported as 60 cm/s for non-contrast acquisitions and 100 cm/s for contrast-supported acquisitions, and spatial resolution was reported as 1.25 mm × 1.25 mm × 1.25 mm with an imaging volume of 32 cm × 32 cm × 24–32 cm and an approximate scan time of 12 minutes, using respiratory bellows with a 50% acceptance rate. These parameters define the noise floor and the motion tolerance of direction-based measures. Retrograde fraction values should be interpreted alongside these acquisition constraints, and reported methods should specify phase correction and unwrapping rules (23).

Fractional flow change

Fractional flow change compares flow measured at two connected sites and expresses the change as a fraction of a reference flow (8). It can be computed using peak flow, mean flow, or integrated flow over the cardiac cycle, as long as the same definition is used at both planes. This metric is useful in portal hypertension because redistribution into collaterals often occurs between standard planes. It is also useful in post-surgical anatomy because local branch takeoffs can cause large local changes that are not captured by a single trunk measurement (13). A practical constraint is that planes must be placed away from bifurcations and away from recirculation zones, because junction flow violates the assumption that a single plane represents a stable conduit segment (10).

In a porto-mesenteric 4D flow MRI variceal-risk study, portal fractional peak flow change showed strong discrimination for high-risk varices, with an AUC reported as 0.936, and a cut-off reported as <−0.348, yielding 83.3% sensitivity and 92.3% specificity in that cohort. This example illustrates how fractional metrics produce actionable numeric thresholds, and it also shows that thresholds depend on how planes are defined (for example PV1 versus PV2) and how peak flow is defined (true maximum versus robust percentile peak) (78).

Shear-related metrics and energy loss

WSS and oscillatory shear index are derived from near-wall velocity gradients and temporal variation of the shear vector. These metrics depend strongly on spatial resolution, segmentation accuracy, and smoothing. In portal venous 4D flow MRI, segmentation decisions at the wall change the effective radius and the near-wall gradient, so WSS is more sensitive to boundary definition than net flow (8). Energy-loss surrogates and turbulence-related measures also depend on noise handling and intravoxel dephasing, especially in jets or in recirculation zones (10). These metrics are mainly used to describe vessel–flow interaction and flow inefficiency, so they are commonly discussed for chronic stenosis remodeling, stent surveillance, and post-intervention monitoring where abnormal gradients persist despite adequate net flow (13).

Automated workflows are starting to treat these parameters as standard outputs. A portal venous 4D flow MRI automation study reported comparison of main portal vein flow rate, mean velocity, WSS, oscillatory shear index, and energy loss between automated and manual segmentations, and it reported strong agreement for flow rate with ρ ≥0.96 and segmentation completed in under 10 seconds versus a manual median of 35 minutes. This matters clinically because shear and energy metrics are not usable at scale if segmentation is slow and variable, and automation addresses that barrier before large outcome datasets exist for these advanced measures (8).

Pressure-field reconstruction

Pressure is a central clinical variable in portal hypertension and in shunt management, yet direct pressure measurement is invasive. Pressure-field reconstruction uses measured velocity fields as boundary information, then applies physics-based modeling to infer pressure differences. A clinically focused example is PSPG estimation using 4D flow MRI coupled with CFD. In a pilot cohort of 20 participants with cirrhosis and TIPS, noninvasive pressure estimates correlated with invasive measures with r=0.77 (P<0.001). The method classified whether post-TIPS pressure gradient exceeded 12 mmHg correctly in 16 of 20 participants (80%), with two false negatives and two false positives, and two of five participants with venographic stenosis and invasive gradient >12 mmHg were misclassified as ≤12 mmHg. These numbers define the current performance envelope and the key error modes. They also show that reconstruction accuracy depends on segmentation fidelity, spatial resolution at the stenotic segment, and velocity reliability in the stent jet region, because modeling error increases when the measured velocity field is uncertain (38). CFD-derived non-invasive pressure estimation only has preliminary evidence from small pilot cohorts and cannot be applied for routine clinical diagnosis currently. Comparison with existing modalities

Although comparisons between 4D flow MRI and Doppler ultrasound, CTA and catheter angiography have been discussed above, further refined comparative analysis is supplemented herein. Instead of acting as an alternative to routine imaging modalities, 4D flow MRI functions as a valuable complementary tool. Its unique strengths lie in quantifying global vascular hemodynamics for complicated hepatic vasculopathy, long-term post-procedural follow-up and comprehensive assessment of whole-network blood redistribution. Meanwhile, conventional imaging retains irreplaceable clinical merits in routine preliminary screening and anatomical localization. Specific superiorities of each conventional technique are supplemented below for more objective comparison.

Doppler ultrasound

Doppler ultrasound provides real-time velocity sampling and remains the first examination in most portal and transplant workflows. It has irreplaceable strengths including bedside accessibility, zero ionizing radiation and low cost for initial clinical screening. It supports stenosis screening using numeric waveform targets. After liver transplantation, commonly cited reference targets include hepatic artery RI above 0.5, SAT below 0.08 s, and PSV at the anastomosis below 200 cm/s. Hepatic artery stenosis is often suspected when PSV rises above 200–250 cm/s at the anastomosis and the intrahepatic waveform shows a tardus-parvus pattern with acceleration time above 0.08 s and RI below 0.5 (79).

Portal Doppler is also used for portal hypertension assessment, yet quantitative portal measurements show substantial observer variability and strong dependence on insonation angle and sampling location. In abdominal 4D flow MRI reference work, Doppler portal measurements are cited as having inter-observer variability around 33% and intra-observer variability around 20%, and MRI tends to report lower cross-section-averaged velocities compared with Doppler due to different sampling and area definitions (23). These limitations are most evident in obesity, bowel gas, ascites, deep hilar anatomy, and complex post-operative geometry (13). They are also evident when the dominant question is network routing, because Doppler typically samples a small number of points rather than the full porto-mesenteric and collateral system (10).

CT and CTA

CT angiography provides high spatial resolution anatomy and fast acquisition, so it is effective for showing thrombosis, focal narrowing, aneurysm, and major collateral channels, making it the first choice for emergency anatomical evaluation. It remains limited for hemodynamic quantification because it does not provide time-resolved 3D velocity fields and it does not directly measure volumetric blood flow over the cardiac cycle (10). Iodinated contrast exposure is also a practical issue in patients needing repeated imaging and in patients with chronic kidney disease. Clinical guidance documents note that the overall risk of contrast-associated acute kidney injury is low in many patients, yet risk increases in advanced CKD, diabetes, dehydration, and severe illness. For chronic liver disease populations that frequently undergo repeated imaging for complications and surveillance, these constraints can affect modality selection and follow-up frequency (79).

Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) and catheter-based hemodynamics

DSA and catheter-based pressure measurements remain the procedural reference when intervention is planned or when noninvasive results conflict, with unique advantages for intraoperative lesion verification and immediate interventional treatment. These tests provide direct lesion interrogation and immediate treatment options, yet they are invasive and they carry access-site and contrast-related risks (79). Reviews of DSA complications describe events such as access-site hematoma, infection, and contrast allergy, with complication frequency depending on indication and patient risk profile. For portal hypertension interventions such as TIPS, complication profiles include hemorrhage, and reported rates of clinically significant hemorrhage in the TIPS literature range from 0.7% to 6%, while broader procedural morbidity rates can be higher when all technical and post-procedural events are included (38). These risks make repeated invasive assessment undesirable for routine surveillance, especially when the clinical question is functional follow-up rather than immediate therapy (13).

A practical comparison point is post-TIPS evaluation, where pressure gradient is a key clinical variable. A 4D flow MRI plus computational modeling approach has reported correlation of noninvasive portosystemic pressure gradient estimates with invasive measurements (r=0.77, P<0.001) in a pilot cohort, and correct classification of a clinically used threshold (>12 mmHg) in 16 of 20 participants (80%) (38). This type of result supports using noninvasive hemodynamic mapping to reduce unnecessary invasive studies in follow-up pathways when anatomy appears acceptable but functional status is uncertain (38).

Future directions

Pressure estimation constitutes a computational modeling workflow deriving clinically relevant pressure gradients from acquired 3D velocity datasets. A practical near-term target is post-shunt pressure assessment, where invasive gradients such as 12 mmHg are already used in care pathways. Current pilot results show that noninvasive estimates can correlate with invasive measures (r=0.77) and can classify the >12 mmHg threshold correctly in 16/20 cases, yet misclassification still occurs in a subset that includes stenosis cases (38). This makes the technical priorities clear: tighter control of segmentation around jets, stable boundary condition definition, and explicit uncertainty flags when velocity signal is weak inside a stent or near a tight narrowing (8).

Hemodynamic endotyping can be implemented using a small set of network metrics that are already measurable in routine 4D flow workflows. One feasible classification stratifies patients into three hemodynamic subtypes: impaired portal perfusion, high-volume spontaneous shunting, and compensatory arterial hyperperfusion (23). These patterns can be encoded using flow partition ratios at the spleno-mesenteric confluence, branch distribution in left versus right portal flow, and the arterial-to-portal inflow ratio when hepatic arterial measurement is reliable (80). The key technical requirement is standardized plane landmarks and standardized reporting fields so the same phenotype definition remains stable across sites and across follow-up exams (56).

Automation and abbreviated protocols are likely to move adoption from expert centers toward broader use. Automated portal segmentation has already shown near-expert agreement with Dice around 0.81–0.87, flow agreement ρ ≥0.96, and reduction of segmentation time from about 35 minutes to <10 seconds (81). Future development will tie automated pipelines to protocol-tailored readouts: predefined portal/collateral metrics for portal hypertension, shunt flow and computed pressure for post-TIPS surveillance, and paired inflow plus branch distribution data with embedded QC for liver transplant evaluation (82).

Conclusions

4D flow MRI yields dynamic three-dimensional blood flow information to comprehensively evaluate hepatic inlet, outlet and collateral vessel circulation. In cirrhosis, this technique quantifies portal hemodynamic redistribution, collateral shunting and postoperative shunt patency via multiple flow parameters. In hepatic venous outflow obstruction, flow features including jet signal and respiratory-related flow variation help grade the severity of vascular stenosis. For hepatic vascular malformations, the modality accurately localizes shunt location and quantifies shunt volume in one single scan. After liver transplantation, it jointly evaluates hepatic artery, portal vein and venous drainage, especially valuable when conventional ultrasound yields ambiguous results or the vascular anatomy is abnormally complicated.

Widespread clinical application of 4D flow MRI relies on unified scanning protocols, standardized measuring methods and unified diagnostic criteria to guide risk grading, interventional scheme formulation and long-term patient follow-up. Key practical indicators include blood flow volume, flow velocity and flow distribution ratio. Non-invasive pressure estimation can be achieved for clinical use with high-quality image datasets, alongside rigorous quality checks at stenotic and high-flow jet regions. Automatic post-processing techniques simplify operational steps, improve measurement consistency and facilitate multi-center clinical popularization.
