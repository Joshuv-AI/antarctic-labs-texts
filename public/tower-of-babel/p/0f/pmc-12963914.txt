Men’s involvement in contraception: a qualitative study exploring the perceptions and practices of general practitioners in France
Authors: Sarah Zaalouni, Delphine Teigné, Rosalie Rousseau, Pauline Jeanmougin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore French general practitioners' (GPs) views and practices regarding men's involvement in contraception.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative study between March and November 2023 with 15 GPs from three primary care facilities in western France. Three focus groups (five GPs per site) were conducted using a semi-structured interview guide. Data were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed iteratively using a thematic approach inspired by grounded theory, following Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).ResultsThree conceptual categories emerged: (1) diverse perceptions and increasing involvement of men in contraception; (2) variation in professional practices and knowledge; (3) a normative and institutional environment that hinders male involvement. While some participants described evolving social expectations, contraceptive responsibility was still largely perceived as female. Gender-related norms and knowledge gaps influenced perceptions and options presented to patients, limiting fully informed choice. However, some participants challenged these norms and sought to encourage patients to reconsider their options.ConclusionThis study provides new insights into the perceptions and practices of GPs regarding men's involvement in contraception. It highlights the need to strengthen training on contraceptive methods and encourage reflection on gender issues, and suggests that public policies and pharmaceutical initiatives could support fairer practices.Keywords: Contraception, Unplanned pregnancy, Health worker preferences, Patient preferences, General practitioner, Qualitative research report

Introduction

Sexual and reproductive health encompasses the physical, emotional, mental, and social well-being of every individual throughout their life (World Health Organization, 2025). In particular, it concerns intimate relationships, access to safe care, family planning, and contraception. The World Health Organization promotes the use of contraception, and publishes guidelines focused on safety, accessibility, and respect for human rights, which provide a reference for national healthcare policies (World Health Organization, 2023).

Contraceptive practices are shaped by public policies, biomedical progress, gender relations, social norms, and feminist movements (Kimport, 2018; Littlejohn, 2021). Over time, contraceptive responsibility has become increasingly feminized. Contraceptive choices are often presented as ‘free’ and ‘empowering’, yet they remain socially constrained, illustrating the contraceptive paradox (Littlejohn, 2021). For some women, contraception represents a motor for empowerment: the ‘pill revolution’ has enabled women to gain better control over their fertility (Littlejohn, 2021). For others, however, contraception may be experienced as unfair or constraining, particularly when certain methods or interactions with the healthcare system limit autonomy (Gomez et al., 2014). Contraception has also led to male disengagement, and rendered their share of responsibility invisible (Thomé and Rouzaud-Cornabas, 2017).

The international literature highlights that the practices of healthcare professionals play a key role in the distribution of contraceptive responsibility, with gender being a major determinant of social inequalities in the domain of health, and influencing caregivers' perceptions and stereotypes (Heise et al., 2019). Several studies show that contraceptive counseling still largely relies on the assumption that pregnancy prevention primarily concerns women, while men's involvement remains limited (Kimport, 2018; Littlejohn, 2021). Ventola et al. (Ventola, 2016) demonstrated that gendered perceptions among general practitioners and gynecologists were similar in France and England. In the United Kingdom, the standardization of healthcare protocols moderated the influence of these perceptions on prescribing practices, whereas in France, greater variability in practices has allowed more room for individual professional perceptions. In this French context, prescribers tended to view women as naturally interested in their fertility, unlike men, who were seen as less involved, justifying the exclusion of male contraceptive methods from the options offered (Ventola, 2014).

However, in recent years, the concept of contraceptive burden has become popularized in civil society (Koechlin, 2022), and associated demands have led to the question being revisited (Littlejohn, 2021). International health policies present contraceptive sharing as a gender equality challenge (European Parliament, 2021). Changes in the use and the distribution of contraceptive methods, reflecting broader societal transformations, have been documented both at the international scale, and in France (Donval et al., 2022; Slaymaker et al., 2020). These developments have been accompanied by growing male interest in adopting contraceptive methods for their own use (Kaur et al., 2024). This observation is supported by Hupertan et al. who reported a tenfold increase in the number of vasectomies in France between 2010 and 2021 (1900 vs. 23,000 procedures) (Hupertan et al., 2023).

In a context where societal and political views of sharing contraceptive responsibility are changing, it is relevant to examine how this transformation is reflected in the views of general practitioners, who are the main prescribers of contraceptives in France (Donval et al., 2022), and who play a key role in helping male and female patients to find a contraceptive method that is suited to their needs. The aim of this study is, therefore, to explore general practitioners' perceptions of men's involvement in contraception, and their own professional practices.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a qualitative focus group study between January 2023 and June 2024 with general practitioners from three healthcare facilities located in western France (Loire-Atlantique and Vendée). It was conducted in two multidisciplinary primary care centers, one located in an urban setting, and the other in a semi-rural setting, along with a medical center located in an urban area (Table 1). All 26 general practitioners working in these facilities were invited to participate in the study by email. There were no other inclusion or exclusion criteria. In total, 15 general practitioners agreed to participate (five general practitioners per healthcare setting). The protocol followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (Tong et al., 2007).

Data collection and qualitative analysis

The interview guide was developed progressively, and began with clinical cases illustrating various requests for contraception. It explored differences in the management of unintended pregnancy prevention among women and men, and factors shaping the approach to men's reproductive health (Appendix A). It was pre-tested (2 interviews, 3 general practitioners) and refined during the study in response to emerging themes, in an iterative process (Appendix A).

A research journal was used to document the process, to encourage a reflexive approach and thereby strengthen external validity. This approach was essential to identify and deconstruct the possible preconceptions of the researcher who conducted the interviews (SZ).

Three focus groups, each composed of five participants from the same healthcare setting, were held at their workplace. The focus groups were conducted by SZ, a general practitioner, between March and November 2023. SZ did not know any of the participants.

Recruitment was iterative. As the analysis progressed, participants were selected to confirm or challenge the emerging interpretations from the field, using a maximum variation approach. Their characteristics (age, gender, professional status within the facility) and focus group session duration were summarized using descriptive statistics (counts for categorical variables, means for continuous variables) to provide an overview of the sample and contextualize the sessions (Table 1).

Data collection and analysis were performed simultaneously and iteratively, following a thematic approach inspired by grounded theory. Each focus group was recorded, anonymized, and transcribed by SZ. Transcripts were not returned to the participants. Data analysis was conducted in two stages: (1) open coding of transcripts to identify emerging properties and categories; and (2) axial coding to identify relationships between categories. The analysis was performed manually using Word® software (version 16.0, Microsoft Corp.). Theoretical purposive sampling continued until data saturation was reached.

Material from the first focus group was analyzed independently by another general practitioner (AL) who did not participate in the study. This allowed for triangulation of the open coding and strengthened the internal validity of the work. Data analysis was completed in June 2024.

Ethical considerations

The protocole was approved by the Ethics Committee for Non-Interventional Research of the University of Nantes (No. 20122022). Participants gave written informed consent and received no financial compensation.

Results

Characteristics of participants and focus groups

The participants comprised 13 women and two men, aged between 25 and 54 years. Four were aged between 25 and 34 years, eight were aged 35 to 44 years, and three were aged 45 to 54 years; three were locum general practitioners and 12 were established general practitioners. The mean duration of the focus groups was 1 h and 12 min (±11 min). Table 1 presents the main characteristics of participating general practitioners and the focus groups.

The analysis of the discussions among professionals revealed three conceptual categories. The first was a diversity of perceptions among participants, and increasing male involvement in contraception. Second, professional practices varied regarding the advice provided to patients, and participants' knowledge. Third, current social norms and the institutional framework were identified as major barriers to men's involvement in contraception. Variation in the views of professionals and men's involvement in contraception

Gendered personal perceptions among participants

Participants noted that discussions about contraceptive methods in clinical consultations were frequently gendered, “with women, we talk about contraception, but with men, yes, it doesn't occur to me so often” (M8). Those who did raise the question with men perceived it as an opportunity to raise awareness, and encourage them to think about the issue. For others, the idea seemed “inappropriate” (M1), or “even intrusive” (M8).

For some participants, sharing responsibility for contraception within the couple was not always seen as feasible, thereby limiting women's ability to control their reproductive capacity, “there may also be a lot of women who want to stay in control and who would be afraid that their partner might mess things up” (M7).

The assumed lack of contraceptive competence among men was explicitly discussed by the two male participants who, in order to ensure greater acceptability, proposed methods requiring no ongoing effort that could be used “just before intercourse or with long-lasting effects […] so you don't have to think about it too much” (M5), or even methods that improved sexual performance, such as “a pill combined with Viagra” (M1). For others, male non-adherence was not inevitable, and they did not see why male patients could not learn in the same way as female patients.

Practices also differed regarding consultations with young people. Several participants had strategies for talking to adolescent boys about the risk of unplanned pregnancies and encouraging them to be proactive: “I talk a little about the morning-after pill [...], or [...] ‘Are you making sure your partner is using contraception?’” (M10). For others, the question wasn't useful “at that age […] when you want to talk about contraception, there's a fifty-fifty chance that he […] won't understand […] what the point is” (M5).

Increasing involvement of men

Participants noted an increase in men's participation in contraceptive strategies, especially among younger generations, “I have the growing impression that men who are a little younger, 30 or under, are getting more involved” (M14). It was described as: (i) sharing the cost of contraception; (ii) sharing day-to-day management; and (iii) the unanimously observed increase in requests for vasectomy.

The concept of contraceptive burden

The two male participants were unfamiliar with the concept of contraceptive burden. The others endorsed the concept, and acknowledged the burden, “women will keep using contraception, even if there are side effects” (M6). Some explicitly raised this issue with their female patients: “I also often tend to add [...] that for once the contraceptive burden can be carried by the man” (M13). Some tried to encourage the partner's presence by explicitly suggesting that their patients come to the consultation together, or asking whether they had both thought about contraceptive methods. However, for some participants, the presence of a partner could prompt them to check for signs of spousal control.

Some participants occasionally asked their patients (especially adolescents and young adults) gender-neutral questions about contraceptive use, seeing it as a way to raise awareness and encourage reflection among male patients.

Variability in professional practices and knowledge

Personalized advice

During consultations, participants tailored the information they provided according to what their patients asked, “in the end, I never say the same thing to everyone, I tailor it a lot” (M9), believing that a comprehensive explanation of all available methods was not necessary.

Information about vasectomy or tubal ligation was not systematically provided, and often limited to patients who were deemed ‘interested’ based on subjective criteria related to age or family situation, “it's unconscious and so deeply ingrained […] this somewhat ‘patriarchal’ view, having children […] I'm not going to raise the subject of definitive contraception with a woman who hasn't had a child” (M14).

Lack of knowledge and discomfort regarding male methods.

Condoms were often presented to patients as a way to prevent sexually transmitted diseases, rather than as a contraceptive method in their own right, “but saying, be careful, it's not a contraceptive method” (M1), which limited their promotion as a birth control method.

There was no knowledge of male hormonal methods, and thermal methods—such as rings or jockstraps—elicited amused reactions among some participants: “What else did we see? Rings? A whole bunch of weird stuff... (laughs)” (M5) or more cautious responses, “it's not very well known […] we don't really have much to suggest” (M9).

The normative and institutional environment as a barrier to male involvement

The weight of social norms

The female participants reported being strongly influenced by social conditioning. This gendered education assigned women, from adolescence onwards, responsibility for medical and gynecological monitoring, “I think that […] women have become accustomed from a very young age to talking about gynecology, monitoring, sexuality, and this hasn't happened with men, or at least, less so” (M8). As a result, contraception was described as being much easier to talk about with young girls than boys, “the issue of contraception […] I talk about it earlier, more easily, especially at 16, with a girl than a boy” (M3). Some participants justified these gendered practices by referring to the direct physical consequences of contraceptive failure for women, while at the same time underestimating men's willingness to be involved, “not many men will be that motivated, whereas women don't have the choice of whether to be motivated or not” (M14).

Some participants reported the need to move toward less gendered practices, by taking a more active role in promoting men's sexual health. Some explicitly acknowledged their role in perpetuating these perceptions, calling for a change in practices, “it's up to us first and foremost… to say, ‘a man has the right to come for a consultation to talk about sexual health’” (M8).

The discussions showed that sharing contraceptive burden was generally envisioned only within the context of a stable couple, thereby limiting the possibility of a broader and more flexible distribution of this responsibility, “from the moment they come to ask for a contraceptive method […] it's because they're already sharing responsibility for contraception within the couple” (M8).

Limitations related to health policies

Public policy responsibilities were also mentioned. Pro-natalist policies, the limited promotion of methods such as vasectomy, and the absence of “explicitly feminist policies” (M6) in sexual and reproductive health contributed to maintaining these inequalities. Nevertheless, the female participants welcomed recent legislative advances, such as the inclusion of adolescent boys in HPV vaccination programs, which opened up new opportunities to discuss sexual health with them. The National Education System was also seen as a key player, as school-based interventions could encourage more egalitarian thinking about contraception from an early age, “at middle school, they really are provided with a lot of information” (M1).

Discussion

This study explored general practitioners' perceptions and professional practices regarding men's involvement in contraception. While some participants described a shift in social expectations, particularly among younger generations, contraception was still largely thought of as a female responsibility. This study highlighted different perceptions and practices regarding the subject. These perceptions, or general practitioners' lack of knowledge, may limit the information provided to patients about available options, thereby hindering fully informed choice.

The influence of social and gender norms remains evident in general practitioners' representations of contraception. Our study confirmed that these views on contraception lie at the intersection of several socio-cultural norms that have been described in the literature. Social norms refer to often implicit rules shared by a society or group of individuals that define normal and acceptable behavior within that group (Cislaghi and Heise, 2020). Among these, gender norms describe gendered expectations, roles, or behaviors, which largely favor men, and reinforce inequalities between men and women (Cislaghi and Heise, 2020). In our study, the analysis of the views and practices reported by general practitioners highlighted differences in the social and gender norms shared by these healthcare professionals, without these differences necessarily being identified as such. However, individual characteristics and the personal preferences of healthcare professional influence practices (Bullington et al., 2025), either by devaluing or marginalizing certain contraceptive methods (Kimport, 2018), or by prioritizing their own preferences (Mann, 2022).

We noted the prevalence of the procreative norm (‘the right time for motherhood’) and the contraceptive norm (an ‘ideal’ contraceptive pathway depending on age) (Bajos and Ferrand, 2011). Participants lamented the persistence of the gynecological norm (Koechlin, 2022), which fosters gender asymmetry, with regular gynecological monitoring for women versus a lack of institutionalized care for men. A conjugal norm (Neyrand, 2018) hindered men's involvement, as it was more easily envisaged in a stable couple, understood as the only setting conducive to contraceptive cooperation. Finally, the sexual norm [22] was still very much present: male sexuality was more carefree, unlike female sexuality, which was rational and anticipatory, thereby legitimizing the unseen contraceptive burden carried by women (Hay et al., 2019), from a very young age (Soin et al., 2022). These gendered norms and prejudices identified in our study foster unequal access to sexual healthcare or prevention between men and women, perpetuate the risks and burdens associated with reproductive health borne by women (Kidula et al., 2025), and have a major impact on health (Heise et al., 2019). The failure to recognize one's own biases perpetuates a contraceptive norm for women of reproductive age and hinders the provision of truly patient-centered care (Mann et al., 2025; Thornton et al., 2024).

Changes in the medical profession's stance are reflected in the ways general practitioners identify and adapt their perceptions and practices regarding contraception. Participants differed in their ability to recognize these norms, and to modify their practices despite their influence.

Some participants (the ‘passive’ group), had rarely or never questioned men's involvement. Others seemed to be more open to a shift toward shared contraceptive responsibility, but nothing had been implemented to encourage it, and their practices ultimately reinforced the female contraceptive norm. Nevertheless, they could be challenged by their patients, with increasing demand for male involvement, as reported by Mawson et al. (Mawson et al., 2024).

In contrast, others (the ‘willing’ group) sought to denaturalize contraceptive capabilities and recognized their own responsibilities in maintaining or changing their patients' perceptions and use of contraception. Many participants in this group had undertaken training on the subject, and reflecting on their work had enabled them to identify their own professional habitus and explore strategies to gradually change their practices. However, these strategies often remained centered on the medical-technical view of contraception, as also highlighted in the literature (Roux, 2023). These dynamics echo the findings of Ventola et al., who, ten years ago highlighted that the medical discourse naturalized reproductive roles, excluded men from contraception, or adopted a neutral stance on gender inequalities (Ventola, 2014).

A third (‘pro-active’) group focused on achievable goals to begin to empower and engage men, even beyond the use of male contraception. Seeking to promote gender equality, they sometimes initiated discussions around the burden of contraception and socio-cultural perceptions.

Limitations and strengths

The study has several limitations. The main limitation is recruitment: participants only came from three healthcare facilities, with an overrepresentation of young women (aged under 44) and only two men. This sample does not reflect the overall demographics of general practitioners, who are on average older. A self-selection bias is also likely, as respondents were probably more interested in the topic. The lack of triangulation with respect to the last focus groups may have reduced the depth of the analyses.

However, this is the first French study to address this topic using focus groups. It provides new insights and contributes to current societal and medical debates, at a time when reducing inequalities is a key priority of public policy. The study highlights the persistence of certain gender-related assumptions among general practitioners, who play a key role in sexual and reproductive healthcare in France. It may encourage critical reflection on these assumptions, with a view to changing them, and promoting more equitable access to care for all patients.

Conclusions

This study shows that general practitioners' views of contraception remain largely shaped by norms, particularly gender norms. While some general practitioners continue to perpetuate gender stereotypes, others are more actively engaged in challenging them in response to changing social expectations. To support this dynamic, it seems necessary to promote a more balanced approach that involves both partners, supported by gender-awareness training and a better understanding of the different contraceptive methods. Development of male contraceptive methods by the pharmaceutical industry is another important lever for expanding the range of available options and responding to the growing demand among men to take responsibility for contraception. However, a more equitable distribution of the contraceptive burden will also require a greater commitment to the topic of sexual health in public policy.
