How to measure the core functions of primary care in low-income and middle-income country settings
Authors: Robert Mash, Innocent Besigye, Kéfilath Bello, Anna Galle
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe core functions of primary care are first contact access, comprehensiveness, continuity, coordination and person-centredness. These five core functions are highlighted as essential aspects of quality by the new WHO’s measurement framework. In low-income and middle-income countries, the core functions are rarely measured and routinely collected data does not support their measurement. Existing international tools to measure the quality of primary care may evaluate some aspects of these core functions, but none of the reviewed tools focused on all of them. In sub-Saharan Africa, the Primary Care Assessment Tool has been developed over the last decade and a regional version of the tool has been validated to only measure the core functions as defined by the WHO. The tool uses exit interviews with users as recommended by the WHO. The tool has been piloted in South Africa, Uganda and Benin and will now be implemented in 11 African countries. The tool can enable low-income and middle-income countries to measure the core functions and plan interventions to improve the quality of primary care.Keywords: Delivery of Health Care, Global Health, Health services research, Health systems evaluation, Health Services Accessibility

Introduction

The core functions of primary care are first contact access, comprehensiveness, continuity, coordination and person-centredness.1 High-quality primary care is characterised by its ability to deliver a model of care that embodies these functions. These five core functions were included in the Primary Health Care Performance Initiative (PHCPI) framework and more recently in the WHO’s Measurement Framework.1 2 Despite their centrality in these frameworks, they are rarely measured or reported on by health information systems. Measuring these core functions is particularly difficult in countries that lack electronic medical records or ‘big data’, as is usually the case in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). Nevertheless, primary care researchers in SSA have developed the Primary Care Assessment Tool (PCAT), which in our viewpoint can be incorporated into health information systems to measure the core functions of primary care.3 This article presents the rationale for this analysis and elaborates on our experience of designing, developing and implementing the PCAT.

The core functions of primary care

To develop a measurement tool, it is important to define what is understood by the concepts it is intended to measure. In this section, we briefly define the essential elements of these concepts.4

Primary care should be easily accessible and provide the first contact with the health system for people with a health-related problem.5 The principle that all people should have access to a full range of essential and quality health services is a cornerstone of the global commitment to universal health coverage, which also emphasises that this should not result in financial hardship.6 Apart from financial barriers, access can also be limited by geography, such as the distance to the clinic, state of the roads or availability of public transport. Health services may limit access by opening times, appointment systems and how walk-in services are organised. Even when primary care services are accessible, utilisation may be hindered by negative perceptions, stigma, prior negative experiences or lack of awareness. Affiliation to a particular primary care facility may, therefore, vary and people may use a variety of formal, informal or traditional services for different needs.

The comprehensiveness of primary care services has different dimensions.5 first, services should be offered across the life course from preconception and pregnancy to newborn and children, adolescents, adults and older adults. A range of services should be offered from health promotion and disease prevention to treatment, rehabilitation and palliative care. These services should also cover all the common health problems and burden of disease in the community.

Continuity of care has also been defined in different ways.7 On the one hand, informational continuity looks at whether the medical record is available and up to date at the consultation. Even if seen by a different primary care provider, informational continuity will enable clinical decision-making and management to be coherent. Relational continuity refers to the development of a trusting relationship over time with a primary care provider who knows you more deeply as a person. Such relational continuity allows care to be tailored effectively and efficiently to the person.

Coordination of care has two different dimensions.7 Parallel coordination refers to activities that coordinate care between different teams of providers within the primary care context. For example, between community health workers in the community and nurse practitioners in the facility. Within primary care facilities, this might refer to coordination between those responsible for HIV treatment and antenatal care when these are offered by different teams. Sequential coordination refers to the coordination of care between different levels of care. For example, between the primary care team in the clinic and the clinical team at the district or referral hospital.

Person-centred care within the consultation refers to the nature of the interaction between the person and the provider and the extent to which the provider considers the whole person.8 9 Whole person medicine considers not only the problem or symptoms from a biomedical perspective, but also the person’s own perspective and their context. The person’s own perspective may include their expectations of the provider, their beliefs, concerns or fears regarding their illness, as well as their feelings and emotions. A person-centred approach also involves shared decision-making, where patients actively participate in setting goals and choosing treatment options in collaboration with the provider. Their family, work, household and community context are also considered in understanding the problem holistically.

Most measurement indicators focus on health system inputs and service delivery outputs, with relative neglect of the health system structures, service delivery processes and health system objectives.10 When measuring service delivery outputs, there is usually a focus on prioritised conditions such as HIV, tuberculosis (TB) or malaria. Vertical programmes that are supported by external donor funds may have developed more sophisticated health information.11 Cross-cutting issues such as the core functions are rarely measured or reported on and, therefore, not considered in routine performance management and improvement initiatives.

Routine data are therefore not suited to measure the core functions. For example, the PHCPI that attempted to use routine data to measure the core functions struggled to find suitable indicators.12 Person-centredness was measured with one indicator for the proportion of caregivers who were told their child’s diagnosis, continuity by the dropout rate for the third dose of diphtheria-pertussis-tetanus vaccination and the quality score did not include any measures of coordination. The district health information systems in most African countries do not include indicators to measure the core functions.

Researchers and health system specialists have developed several tools to measure health system and primary care performance as shown in table 1. These tools are used in research studies or could be used by health systems as ad hoc periodic measurements. None of the tools measured health system outcomes or impact. Although a few of the tools measured aspects of the core functions, none of them covered all functions or intentionally focused on the core functions. Measuring the core functions of primary care is, therefore, an important but neglected area in health system strengthening. In the WHO measurement framework, the WHO recommends measuring the core functions by periodic exit interviews with users.1

Developing the PCAT in SSA

The PCAT was originally developed in the 1990s by Barbara Starfield and her team at Johns Hopkins University to measure the core functions of primary care as they were understood at the time.13 The tool measured several core constructs: affiliation and access, continuity, coordination and comprehensiveness. In addition, it measured three additional constructs: family orientation, community orientation and cultural competence. The tool also intended to measure whether these functions were both available and utilised. The original PCAT was adapted for use in South America and Asia, before coming to South Africa.14

When the original PCAT was adapted for use in South Africa, a new construct of teamwork was added, as multidisciplinary teamwork was a key feature of primary care in this context.14 The PCAT was then used by researchers to measure primary care performance and to compare facilities with and without family physicians.15,17 Subsequently, the PCAT was adapted for Kenya and used to measure primary care performance in private sector clinics in Nairobi.18 It was also adapted for Uganda and used to measure public sector primary care performance in a rural district.19 Uganda also added items to measure person-centredness from a tool that was validated in Kenya.20 A study in Malawi used exploratory factor analysis to adapt the PCAT and created a new version with different constructs and fewer items.21

It was at this point that researchers saw the potential for a more streamlined tool that could measure the core functions of primary care. Constructs that were not part of the five core functions were excluded, such as teamwork and community orientation. At the same time, the tool was content validated for use in SSA so that the core functions could be measured consistently and compared both within and between countries.3

Piloting the PCAT in Benin, Uganda and South Africa

The newly validated regional version of the PCAT measures the core functions in exit interviews with users as envisaged by the WHO with a total of 82 items that take approximately 10 min to administer (table 2). The items to measure the core functions are given as statements that the user must respond to on a Likert scale (definitely (4), probably (3), probably not (2), definitely not (1)). There is also an option for ‘not sure or don’t remember’.

The items are scored from 1 to 4 on the Likert scale and are aggregated to give a median score for each of the core functions. An overall primary care score can also be calculated by combining the five core functions. The results can also be reported as the proportion of respondents scoring the core function as three or more. A score of three indicates a minimum level of acceptable performance. Table 3 illustrates how the PCAT can help policy and decision-makers compare performance across core functions, between districts and in different countries. Cross-country comparisons of primary care performance can also facilitate mutual learning by highlighting effective strategies, revealing gaps and enabling the adaptation of best practices across different health systems. One way of interpreting the findings is that a score of >3.5 suggests that this function is performing well, a score of 2.5–3.4 would suggest borderline to acceptable performance, a score of 1.5–2.4 poor performance and <1.5 very poor. The overall primary score for South Africa appeared significantly higher than that for Uganda or Benin, with Benin having the lowest score. All the primary care scores were in the borderline acceptable range.

Access scored poorly and was similar across all three country settings, although Uganda had the highest score, and utilisation was much higher in South Africa. Comprehensiveness was borderline acceptable in all settings, but much lower in Benin. Continuity was also borderline acceptable in South Africa and Benin, but poor in Uganda. Person-centredness appeared to be strong in all settings, but still lower in Benin. Co-ordination was relatively strong in South Africa and Uganda, although Uganda scored substantially lower and Benin had only borderline acceptable performance. The findings will be presented and discussed in more depth in individual country-level articles.22

Measuring performance is only of value if this can be incorporated into performance management at the level of districts or facilities.23 In Uganda, the PCAT results were used by the district management team to identify the strengths and weaknesses of district performance and to analyse the root causes.24 They were able to conceptualise a feasible intervention and implement this with available resources. In this example, they focused on community dialogues to improve access and utilisation of primary care. This illustrates the utility of the tool within a performance management cycle.

Conclusions

The SSA-PCAT is a useful addition to the researchers’ and health information system’s toolbox. For countries that do not have routinely collected electronic data that can measure the core functions, the SSA-PCAT provides a method for doing this that is aligned with the WHO measurement framework. It has been validated for SSA, and we are now using it across 11 countries to measure primary care performance. We are also investigating how to best visualise the findings and to embed the PCAT into health information systems. The tool can also be adapted for other low-income and middle-income country contexts and may even have utility in high-income countries—for example, researchers in Italy have shown interest in performing a national survey.
