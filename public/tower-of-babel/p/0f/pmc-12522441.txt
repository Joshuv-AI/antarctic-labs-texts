Multidrug resistance including borderline-oxacillin resistance and spa typing of Staphylococcus aureus from milk and milk products of Tigray region, Northern Ethiopia
Authors: Enquebaher K Tarekgne, Taran Skjerdal, Siv Skeie, Knut Rudi, Davide Porcellato, Judith A Narvhus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the greatest global public health threats, and Staphylococcus aureus is well known for its remarkable ability to acquire resistance. Livestock-associated methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) is an emerging pathogen epidemiologically linked to foods of animal origin. This study aimed to (i) assess the antimicrobial resistance profile of S. aureus isolates from dairy foods, (ii) determine the presence of MRSA, and (iii) investigate the genetic characteristics of resistant strains by spa typing.ResultsA total of 160 genetically confirmed S. aureus isolates were obtained from bulk milk (n = 100) and dairy products (n = 60). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method, and PCR was used to detect the mecA and mecC genes. Spa typing was applied to characterize resistant strains.Overall, 61 (45%) isolates were multidrug-resistant (MDR), showing 35 distinct resistance patterns. Among these, 61% were resistant to five or more antibiotic classes. MDR strains were more common in milk (69%) than in milk products (30%). Thirty-one isolates were classified as borderline oxacillin-resistant S. aureus (BORSA), characterized by cefoxitin resistance and susceptibility to amoxicillin–clavulanic acid, but lacking both mecA and mecC. The highest resistance rates were observed for penicillin G (69.4%), followed by streptomycin (53.1%) and erythromycin (41.9%). Mastitis in cows, antibiotic misuse, and poor hygiene during dairy processing were implicated as sources of MDR strains.Spa typing of MDR and BORSA isolates identified 20 spa types, including three novel sequences. The most prevalent were t314, t458, and t6218. No consistent correlation was observed between spa types and resistance profiles. Of the spa types detected, 12 were unique to milk, four to dairy products, and seven were shared, indicating multiple contamination routes.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated the widespread occurrence of MDR S. aureus and BORSA with diverse spa types in dairy foods. The detection of resistant S. aureus with heterogeneous genetic backgrounds in dairy products highlights a significant public health risk, as resistance genes may spread through the food chain. Strengthened monitoring and control measures are urgently needed to limit the dissemination of AMR.Keywords: S. aureus, Multidrug resistance (MDR), BORSA, Dairy products, Spa typing, Ethiopia

Background

Staphylococcus aureus is a ubiquitous organism and may be found in humans, animals and the environment. S. aureus causes many health problems in humans and animals due to its toxin-mediated virulence factors, invasiveness and propensity for antibiotic resistance. In addition to staphylococcal food poisoning (SFP) and toxic shock syndrome (TSS), which are mediated by exotoxins, the bacterium is responsible for various skin and superficial infections (furuncles, boils). Furthermore, the bacterium infects internal organs resulting in pneumonia, osteomyelitis and endocarditis [1, 2]. In animals, it causes subclinical mastitis in dairy cows that significantly reduces milk production and has also public health importance [3].

S. aureus is notorious for its ability to become resistant to antibiotics and the development of multidrug resistance is a global problem. S. aureus can develop drug resistance more readily because of its ability to produce an exopolysaccharide barrier and their location in the body in micro-abscesses that limit the penetration of the drug [4]. Of all the resistance characteristics S. aureus has, methicillin resistance S. aureus (MRSA) is clinically the most important, since a single genetic element confers resistance to β-lactam antibiotics, such as penicillins, cephalosporins and carbapenems which are the most commonly prescribed class of antimicrobials [5].

MRSA is currently a major health problem among hospital patients and health workers. It is an endemic problem globally except in Scandinavian countries and in The Netherlands, where it is controlled by a “search and destroy” strategy [6]. In addition to this, a livestock-associated MRSA is currently emerging which is epidemiologically linked to food animals. After, the first livestock-associated MRSA was isolated from the milk of a mastitis cow [7], the prevalence of livestock associated MRSA in food animals has steadily increased. There are many reports showing the transfer of antibiotic-resistant microorganisms from food animals to humans. Resistance genes and resistance patterns observed in food animals have been identified in humans, particularly those who have been in contact with the animals, such as veterinarians and farm employees. Direct contact with animals, environmental contamination, as well as eating or handling contaminated food are the main routes by which livestock-associated MRSA could be transferred to humans [8, 9].

The methicillin-resistance trait of S. aureus is attributed to the penicillin-binding proteins (PBP) 2a protein, which is encoded by chromosomal mecA gene. The mecA gene encodes a unique penicillin-binding protein known as PBP2a (or PBP2′), which enables bacterial cells to continue cell wall synthesis. Normally, β-lactam antibiotics (such as penicillin, methicillin, and cephalosporins) exert their effect by binding to native PBPs and inhibiting cell wall synthesis. However, PBP2a has a very low affinity for β-lactam antibiotics. As a result, even in the presence of high concentrations of these drugs, PBP2a remains functional and allows cell wall synthesis to proceed. This is the key mechanism by which bacteria carrying the mecA gene develop resistance to β-lactam antibiotics [10]. The presence of mecA has been the “golden standard” for detection of MRSA. However, recently a novel mecC previously known as mecA LGA251 gene, which has 70% nucleotide homology with the conventional mecA, has been investigated from MRSA of bovine and human isolates which typically have a livestock lineage; CC130, CC705 and ST 425 [11, 12].

Some S. aureus strains have neither mecA nor mecC gene nevertheless show resistance to methicillin and oxacillin. Researchers have reported the presence of such S. aureus strains, which exhibit total resistance to penicillin and partial resistance to oxacillin, methicillin and cephalosporins and yet do not have the mecA gene. They named them as borderline oxacillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (BORSA) [13, 14].

Molecular typing of S. aureus is important for many reasons. From a molecular epidemiological perspective, typing of S. aureus is important in order to trace the source and understand transmission during epidemiological disease outbreak investigations and surveillance studies. Besides, it is important to follow the phylogenetic evolution of the strains and understand their genetic relatedness or diversity [15, 16]. There are two main categories of molecular typing: “band-based” and “sequence-based”. Sequence-based method like spa typing is preferable, because data can be exchanged easier among researchers and institutions [10]. For spa typing, the polymorphic X-region of the protein A gene (spa) is used. It is composed of repeats approximately 24 base pairs in length. The region shows polymorphisms due to point mutation, deletions and duplications [17, 18].

Several molecular methods exist for typing pathogens, and the choice depends on the intended purpose [19]. Pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) has long been considered the gold standard for S. aureus typing. However, it is labor-intensive, technically demanding, and requires inter-laboratory standardization, which limits its use in long-term macro-epidemiological studies of MRSA [20]. In contrast, sequence-based spa typing is rapid, cost-effective, and easy to perform, while providing sensitivity comparable to PFGE [21].

The global magnitude of the antibiotic resistance problem remains poorly understood, highlighting the need for such information to guide timely public health action [22]. In light of this, there is no sufficient documented data on resistance profile of S. aureus and MRSA in dairy food value chain, in the Tigray region, Northern Ethiopia.

Hence, the objectives of this study were: (i) to assess the antimicrobial resistance profile of S. aureus isolated from milk and milk products to the commonly-used antibiotics in Veterinary and Human Medicine in the project area (ii) to verify the presence or absence of MRSA in the isolates. (iii) to assess the genetic background of multidrug resistant strains using spa typing.

Results

Using conventional microbiology and molecular techniques, 160 (29.1%) S. aureus were isolated from 549 milk and milk product samples: raw milk (n = 100), butter milk (n = 28), butter (n = 10), sour milk (n = 16), cheese (n = 1), Ethiopian cottage cheese (n = 3) and cake made from milk (n = 2).

Antimicrobial susceptibility test

The 160 S. aureus isolates were tested for 10 antibiotics and 137 (86%) strains showed resistance to one or more types of antimicrobial agents. Among these 137 resistant strains, 22 (16%) were resistant to one type/class of antibiotic, 54 (39%) to two types/classes of antibiotics and 61 (45%) to three or more types/classes of antibiotics. Among the antimicrobial agents tested, the resistance most frequently observed was for penicillin G (69.4%) followed by streptomycin (53.1%), and erythromycin (41.9%). Less frequent resistance was observed for ciprofloxacin (4.3%) and amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (4.4%). cefoxitin (For oxacillin resistance detection) resistance was observed in 32 (20%) S. aureus isolates, among which 21 (13.1%) were fully resistant and 11 (6.9%, 11) showed intermediate resistance (Table 1).

Phenotypic MRSA and detection of mecA and mecC genes

According to CLSI [24] guideline, S. aureus strains resistance to Cefoxitin 30 µg (for oxacillin test) by disk diffusion method are considered as MRSA as this test is surrogate test for MRSA. However, the golden standard is the detection of mecA or mecC genes in the isolates. Hence, the 32 S. aureus isolates intermediate/fully resistance to Cefoxitin (Table 1) were subjected to PCR for detection of either the mecA or the MecC gene. None of the strains were positive for these genes.

However, analysis of the phenotypic results of each isolate to each antibiotic revealed that of the 32 strains of intermediate/fully cefoxitin (oxacillin) resistant S. aureus, 31 (86.1%) were fully susceptible to amoxicllin/clavulanic acid. Such strains of S. aureus with this characteristic are named as borderline oxacillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (BORSA) [13, 14]. Spa typing

Spa typing of the 61 MDR S. aureus isolates identified 20 spa types and 3 novel spa sequences (not available in the Ridom data base). The distribution of the total 23 spa types and sequences in milk and milk product samples as well as in different localities of the studied area is presented in Table 3. The most common spa types identified were t314 (25%, n = 15), t458 (18%, n = 11) and t6218 (7%, n = 4). Out of the 23 spa types, only 7 spa types (t127, t1828, t2613, t314, t355, t458, and t6218) were common to both bulk milk and products. The remaining 16 spa types were found distributed either in bulk milk (n = 12) or milk products (n = 4) samples.

Genetic relatedness of the spa types using minimum spanning tree (MST)

A Minimum Spanning Tree (MST) is a graph-based method used to illustrate the genetic relationships among different genotypes such as spa types. In this analysis, each node represents a genotype (spa types, MLST type…etc) while the connecting edges indicate the genetic differences between them. The MST links all genotypes using the shortest possible total distance without forming loops, providing a clear view of their evolutionary or genetic relatedness. The Minimum Spanning Tree (Fig. 2) illustrates the genetic relatedness among the identified spa types and the distribution of the 35 antibiotic resistance patterns within each spa type. The distance between circles reflects their genetic relatedness: the closer the circles, the more closely related the spa types are. For instance, the most common spa type, t314, is genetically more related to t2613 than to t355 or t186, which are positioned farther away. The colors inside the circles represent different antibiotic resistance profiles, showing that there is no clear association between spa types and resistance patterns. The size of each circle corresponds to the number of isolates within that spa type; for example, spa type t314 includes more isolates than t605, as indicated by its larger circle.

Discussion

MDR in dairy food and public health

The present study showed that 86% of S. aureus isolates from dairy foods were resistant to various antibiotics, of which 45% were multidrug-resistant (MDR), displaying 35 distinct resistance patterns with diverse genetic backgrounds. This poses a potential public health risk, as antimicrobial resistance can be transferred to humans via the food chain. Such transfer may occur through antibiotic residues in food, the transmission of resistance genes to foodborne pathogens, or the ingestion of resistant strains from the original food microflora [25–28]. MDR S. aureus present in dairy foods may colonize the intestinal tract of consumers, particularly immunocompromised individuals [29]. Resistance genes harbored by MDR S. aureus can subsequently be transferred to commensal gut microflora via horizontal gene transfer, as most antibiotic resistance genes are carried on mobile genetic elements (MGE) [30, 31]. This situation constitutes a significant risk, since commensal intestinal bacteria can act as reservoirs of resistance genes for pathogenic bacteria. Furthermore, when resistant bacteria proliferate in the intestine, they may be excreted through feces and contribute to environmental contamination [32, 33]. U Stiefel and CJ Donskey [34] reported strong evidence that colonization with resistant bacteria, such as S. aureus, plays an important role in the dissemination of resistance to other bacterial populations.

It is well documented that Staphylococcus aureus causes a wide range of serious diseases in humans, including sepsis, abscess formation, endocarditis, and toxic shock syndrome [35–38]. With this risk in mind, the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) S. aureus may result in increased morbidity and mortality due to therapeutic failure.

From a veterinary medicine perspective, the emergence of MDR S. aureus also has significant therapeutic implications, as the bacterium is a major cause of animal diseases, particularly clinical and subclinical mastitis. Mastitis remains one of the leading health problems in dairy farms across the country, including in the study area [39–41]. Treatment failure in mastitis not only results in substantial economic losses for farmers but also poses important public health risks.

Prevalences of MDR

Literature on the resistance profiles of Staphylococcus aureus from dairy foods is limited both in Ethiopia in general and in the study area in particular. However, a recent study on antibiotic resistance in S. aureus isolated from milk and milk products in Central Ethiopia reported that 57% of the isolates were resistant to more than one class of antibiotics, with the highest resistance observed against penicillin (83%) and the lowest against amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (3%) [42]. Similarly, another study on the antimicrobial susceptibility of S. aureus from cow milk samples reported that 45% of the isolates were multidrug resistant (MDR). In this study, the highest resistance was documented for penicillin G (92.2%), followed by tetracycline (66.7%) [43] which is comparable with the current study.

Likewise, studies from other countries have also reported the emergence of antibiotic-resistant S. aureus from milk and dairy products. For example, in Italy, 68.8% of S. aureus isolates from dairy and other food products were resistant to at least one class of antibiotics [44]. In South Africa 59% of S. aureus isolates from bulk milk were MDR [45]. A study conducted in Iran [46], reported also that 5.8% and 2.1% of the S. aureus isolates from milk and dairy products were resistant to gentamicin and streptomycin, respectively. Differences in the prevalence of antibiotic resistance reported in the above studies may be explained by several factors, including the frequency and manner of antibiotic use, as well as the prevailing sanitation conditions in the study areas, all of which can influence the development of resistant strains in a given geographical region [30].

BORSA strains

In the present study, 31 phenotypically cefoxitin-resistant S. aureus strains carried neither the mecA nor the mecC gene. This finding implies that their oxacillin resistance is not mediated by the wild-type PBP2a, and therefore they cannot be classified as MRSA. However, all oxacillin-resistant S. aureus strains were susceptible to amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, a β-lactamase inhibitor. Based on these observations, these strains can be considered as BORSA. According to LM McDougal and C Thornsberry [47] BORSA exhibit resistance to β-lactam antibiotics such as oxacillin and methicillin through an extrinsic, non-mecA-mediated mechanism. They develop intermediate or full resistance to oxacillin and other β-lactams, including cephalothin and methicillin, due to the overproduction of β-lactamase, which can partially hydrolyze β-lactam compounds [47, 48]. In addition to the hyperproduction of β-lactamase, other mechanisms underlying the phenotypic oxacillin resistance of BORSA have also been identified. These include plasmid-mediated methicillinase production [49] and alterations in PBPs, where amino acid substitutions in the transpeptidase domain reduce oxacillin binding efficiency [50, 51].Similar to how MRSA acquired the mecA gene to withstand antibiotic pressure, BORSA appear to have developed these alternative mechanisms to counteract antibiotic exposure [52].

Similar to our study, other researchers abroad and in Ethiopia, have reported the presence of phenotypically oxacillin-resistant S. aureus without mecA gene carriage in dairy foods. In China [53] 28 out of 34 strains initially classified as MRSA based on phenotype were found to be mecA-negative. In South Africa, among 92 phenotypically oxacillin-resistant S. aureus isolates from milk, 80% tested negative for PBP2a using the latex agglutination assay [45]. Likewise, in central Ethiopia, 58 phenotypically cefoxitin-resistant S. aureus isolates from milk were also negative for the mecA gene [42].

Source of MDR

The antibiotic resistance of S. aureus in dairy foods may originate from two main sources: mastitic cows or/and human carriers involved in dairy production, transportation, or processing. S. aureus is a well-known causative agent of both clinical and subclinical mastitis, and prolonged or inappropriate use of antibiotics for mastitis treatment can contribute to the development of multidrug resistance. Researchers have reported that mastitis is a major problem in dairy cows within the project area. The overall prevalence ranged from 45.5% to 53.9%, with S. aureus identified as the predominant causative agent. Notably, the isolates showed 100% resistance to the commonly used antibiotics in the regon [54, 55].

In addition, it is well-documented that humans can carry MDR S. aureus and serve as a major source of food contamination [56, 57]. Among school children and other community members from Southwest Ethiopia, 23% (39/169) S. aureus from nasal isolates were MDR [58]. Other researchers [43] also reported 33% MDR S. aureus isolates from nasal swab of farm workers in Addis Abeba, Ethiopia. In Botswana, out of the 200 food handlers examined, 57% were carriers of MDR S. aureus showing resistance to 9 types of antimicrobial agents at different frequencies, including vancomycin [59]. Likewise, contamination of animals by human MRSA carriers has been also documented in a veterinary teaching hospital in USA [60].

Prolonged and misuse of antibiotics are the major problem in developing resistance strains in a given geographical location [30]. In the study area, several forms of antibiotic misuse were observed. These include the adminstration of antibiotics by unauthorized perosnnel, without prescription, administration of incorrect dosages (sub-dose), inappropriate treatment durations, and failure to maintain the proper milk withdrawal period after treatment (antibiotic residues). Moreover, the availability of illegal veterinary drugs on the open market encourages farmers to treat their sick animals on their own, particularly in the Shireendaselasse area, where the prevalence of MDR is relatively higher (Dr Abrha, Gebremedhin, personal communication, 2016). Such practices, may result in the presence of antibiotic residues in milk and contribute to the emergence of multidrug-resistant bacteria [61].

Spa typing

Spa typing of MDR S. aureus isolates from milk and milk products revealed 20 spa types and 3 novel spa sequences, demonstrating the genetic diversity of the isolates. The 3 spa sequences are novel in that they were not found in the Ridom SpaServer data base during the search. Knowledge of the distribution of common spa types—t314 in Shireendaselase, t458 in Mekelle, and t6218 in Wukro—is important for advancing molecular understanding of the sources, transmission pathways, and other epidemiologically significant factors of MDR S. aureus strains. Such insights are instrumental in proposing effective control interventions. Furthermore, from a macro-epidemiological perspective, documenting such data is important for understanding the phylogenetic lineages of MDR S. aureus isolates from milk and milk products in these geographical areas. For example, in Ghana, similar to our findings, spa types t314, t311, and t355 were identified among MDR S. aureus clinical isolates, with spa type t314 being the most common [62]. The spa types t314, t127 and t325 were also reported from five African countries, Cameroon, Niger, Senegal, Madagascar and Morocco but in methicillin susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) isolates [63]. The spa type t127 which is the 5th common spa in this study, was registered as one of the top ten-registered clones in the global frequency of S. aureus on the Radom spa data base server [64].

The minimum spanning tree analysis of the spa types versus the resistance profile of the MDR isolates indicated that there is no specific pattern or relationship between spa types and resistance pattern. This showed that risk of specific MDR S. aureus attributed by specific spa type is not apparent.

Limitation of the study

In contrast to the wide application of spa tying for epidemiological investigations, some limitation of the method has been reported such as least discriminatory power [65] and some strains may be designated as “non-typeable” by the method [66]. Moreover, some suggested also that as spa typing depends on a single locus, it is preferable to use the method in combination with other typing methods such as SCCmec or se genes [67].

Conclusions

To conclude, MDR and BORSA S. aureus isolates with diversified spa types (genetic background) are widely distributed in cow’s milk and milk products at different locality of the studied areas. The sources MDR/BORSA strains could be mastitis cows, misuse of antibiotics by unauthorized personnel (antibiotics residues) and poor personal hygiene/practices at dairy farms and during the milk process. The presence of MDR S. aureus with diversified genetic background has significant public health implications, as resistance genes could be transferred to human via food chain. Therefore, measures should be implemented to control the current scenario through community education and regulatory action of the government. The education may include proper use of antibiotics in dairy farms, as well as handling and processing of dairy food with food safety principles along the milk value chain.

Furthermore, a regular antibiotic resistance-monitoring and surveillance program integrating to the national policy framework to know the magnitude of the problem and hence to act on time is of paramount important. Moreover, whole-genome sequencing of MDR/BORSA S. aureus should be a future research priority to better understand its molecular epidemiology and phylogenetic lineage.

Methods

Bulk milk and dairy product samples

A total of 549 bulk raw cow’s milk (n = 297) and milk products (n = 254) were collected from the Tigray region, Northern Ethiopia following the milk value chain. Before collection, permission/consent was obtained from milk and milk product owners after explaining the purpose of the study (informed consent). The samples comprised raw bulk milk (n = 297), buttermilk (n = 64), butter (n = 58), sour milk (spontaneously fermented milk, n = 97), Ethiopian cottage cheese (n = 14), cheese (n = 15) and cake made from milk (n = 4). They were collected from eight purposely-selected cities/towns of the region, namely Mekelle (n = 147), Shireendaselase (n = 139), Wurko (n = 60), Adigudome (n = 52), Hagreselame (n = 21), Maichew (n = 53), Adigrate (n = 43) and Abi-adi (n = 34) taking into consideration their accessibility to transport as well as having known milk shade areas. The sampling points, along the milk value chain, included dairy farms, milk collection centers, milk and milk product vendors, restaurants and cafeterias.

Isolation and molecular identification of S. aureus

The primary isolation of S. aureus by culture was performed at College of Veterinary Sciences, Mekelle University, Ethiopia, while the molecular identification to species level, the antimicrobial susceptibility test, and spa typing were performed at NMBU, Department of Chemistry, Biotechnology and Food Sciences, Norway.

Conventional microbiology

From each sampling unit, 100 ml of mixed raw bulk milk or 100 g of milk product was collected in a sterile container, maintained at 4 °C and transported to the laboratory. From the raw milk, tenfold serial dilutions were made. While for the milk products, 11 g of the product was mixed with 99 ml peptone water (Sigma-Aldrich, Switzerland) and blended for 2 min in a stomacher (Stomac0her® 400, UK) before further dilutions. The isolation and enumeration of S. aureus were performed as per [68] with some modifications. Briefly, 100 µl of each tenfold dilution of the samples was spread with a sterile bent glass rod into duplicate Baird-Parker agar (Oxoid, England) supplemented with egg yolk tellurite (Merck, Germany). Typical and non-typical colonies were differentially counted after incubation of the plates at 37 °C for 48 h. Circular colonies of 2 to 3 mm diameter, jet-black to gray-black in color and surrounded by an opaque halo and clear zone were considered as a typical S. aureus colony while gray colonies without halos or clear zones were considered as non-typical. Both typical and non-typical colonies were gram-stained and tested for catalase activity (3% H2O2, VWR/International). Moreover, to ensure the purity of the colony before further biochemical tests, 3 to 4 colonies from each type of colony were sub-cultured into Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth (Oxoid, England) and incubated overnight at 37 °C. From the overnight culture, the following tests were performed as per [69] recommendations: tube coagulase test on rabbit plasma with EDTA (Remel, Lenexa, KS, USA); DNase test on agar with toluidine blue (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and anaerobic fermentation of 1% mannitol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Pure typical cultures were preserved with final concentration of 25% glycerol in cryovials (Sarstedt, Nümbrecht, Germany) and stored at – 20 °C until they were transported to NMBU, Norway for further molecular work. At NMBU, they were stored at − 80 until analysis.

In NMBU the isolates were culture in Baird-Parker agar (Oxoid, England) supplemented with egg yolk tellurite (Merck, Germany) and the pure colonies were further subculture in BHI broth (Oxoid, England).

Molecular identification

DNA extraction: From the over-night BHI culture, DNA was extracted by GenElute TM Bacterial Genomic DNA Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) as per the manufacturer’s instructions with some minor modifications. In summary, 1 ml of the broth was centrifuged at 16 363 x g for 2 min. The pellet was washed twice with 0.9% aqueous NaCI solution, homogenized with 200 µl of lysosome solution and incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. The suspension was treated with 20 µl each of RNase and proteinase K, followed by 200 µl of lysis solution C, and incubated at 55 °C for 60 min. The lysate was treated with 200 µl of ethanol and transferred into the new binding column and centrifuged at 3968 x g for 1 min. The content was washed twice with washing solution 1 and concentrated wash solution and the eluate was discarded. The column was put into a new 2 ml tube and the DNA was collected with elution buffer solution. The concentration (ng/µl) and quality (260/280 and 260/230 ratio) of the collected DNA was determined by NanoDrop® − 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fischer Scientific Inc. Waltham MA, USA). The genomic DNA was stored at −20 °C until use.

16 S rRNA gene sequencing

According to the recommendation by [70], the final identification of the isolates to species level was performed by sequencing the 16S RNA gene. The oligonucleotide sequences: Forward: 5’TCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG3’ Reverse: 5’ CGGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT 3’ primers described by [71] were used to amplify the 16 S rRNA gene with expected 1200 bp product. The amplification was conducted with a final PCR reaction volume of 50 µl. It contained, 5 µl of 10x ThermopolTm reaction buffer (BioLabs, New England, USA), 0.25 µl Taq Polymerase of 5,000 µ/ml (BioLabs), 1 µl of each primer of 10 pMol con, 1 µl of 10 Mm deoxynucletide triphosphate mixture (Sigma- Aldrich, USA) 2 µl of genomic DNA and finally adjusted to final volume of 50 µl with milliQwater. The amplification was carried out in C1000 TMThermal cycler (BIO RAD laboratories) programmed to initial denaturation of 95 °C for 1 min, 30 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s., 55 °C for 30 Sect. 68 °C for 80 s. and a final extension period at 68 °C for 5 min. After amplification, the expected PCR product (1200 bp) was verified by gel-electrophoresis and purified by QIAquick R PCR purification kit (QIAGEN®, Oslo, Norway) as per the company’s procedures. The purified PCR products were mixed with the same primer and sent to GATC Biotech AG (European Genome and Diagnostic Centre, Konstanz, Germany) for sequencing as per the company´s instructions. The sequence results were bio edited (Bio Edit version 7.0.0) and compared in GenBank using the nucleotide BLAST algorithm (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/blast). Maximum identification at species level was considered with result scores of 99 to 100% that appear in the first row.

Antimicrobial susceptibility test

Antimicrobial susceptibility test was performed on S. aureus isolates from milk and milk products as per the recommended procedure by clinical and laboratory standard institutes (CLSI). The test was performed by the disk diffusion method using Muller-Hinton agar (Oxoid, CM0405B, UK). The isolates were tested for 10 types of antibiotics which had been reported as being commonly in use in the project area, confirmed by a pharmaceutical wholesaler and veterinary team of the region. Accordingly, the following antibiotics, obtained from Oxoid and Remel (UK) were tested: Penicillin G (10 units), Cefoxitin (30 µg),, Amoxicillin/Clavulanic acid (30 µg), Gentamicin (10 µg), Streptomycin (10 µg), Tetracycline (30 µg), Erythromycin (15 µg), Ciprofloxacin (30 µg), Norfloxacin (5 µg), and. Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (25 µg).

According to CLSI (2020), if one needs to determine the susceptibility of Staphylococcus aureus to oxacillin using the disk diffusion method, the isolate should be tested against cefoxitin (30 µg), a second-generation cephalosporin, as a surrogate agent. Testing with oxacillin disks is not recommended due to issues related to availability and performance.

The procedures recommended by CLSI [24] for antimicrobial susceptibility testing includes the following:.Each S. aureus isolate was grown on tryptic soya agar (Remel, UK) for 18 h. From the fresh growth of each isolate, 2–5 colonies were taken to prepare a suspension in a saline water and the turbidity was adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard using a spectrophotometer (HACH-Company, USA). A petri dish of Muller-Hinton agar was inoculated with the colony suspension using a fresh sterile cotton tipped swab. To each inoculated plate, 4 antibiotics discs were applied using a disc dispenser (Oxoid). The plates were incubated at 35 °C for 16–18 h and the zone of inhibition was measured and compared to the CLSI [24] break point (Table 4).

Control strain

A penicillin-susceptible (ATCC 25923), a penicillin-resistant but oxacillin-susceptible (ATCC 29213) and an oxacillin-resistant (ATCC 43300) S. aureus strains were used as controls in the antimicrobial susceptibility test.

Detection of mecA or mecC genes/Search for MRSA

For phenotypically cefoxitin-resistant S. aureus strains, a conventional PCR was performed to detect the presence of either mecA or mecC genes, as the presence of mecA or mecC has been the “golden standard” for detection of MRSA.

For mecA gene detection the utilized primers were: 5’-CTCAGGTACTGCTATCCACC-3’ (upstream) and 5’-CCTTGGTATATCTTCACC-3’ (Downstream) described by G Hedin and S Løfdahl [72] with expected 449 bp product and for mecC gene the primers 5’-GAAAAAAAGGCTTAGAACGCCTC-3’ (mecALGA251MultiFP) and 5’GAAGATCTTTTCCGTTTTCAGC-3’ (mecALG251 MultiRP) recommended by [73] with expected PCR product of 138 bp. The PCR was performed with final volume of 20 µl that contain 10 x PCR buffer, 25mM of dNTP, 0.4 µM of each primer, and Taq Polymerase and 1 µl of genomic DNA. The program was adjusted to run at 95 °C for 15 min, and 30 cycles of 95 °C for 25 s., 56 °C for 30 Sect. 72 °C for 30 s. and finally 72 °C for 5 min.

Control strains

The S. aureus strains 2012-50-2037 (Supplied from National Veterinary Institutes, Ullevảlsvein, 68, 0454, Oslo, Norway) and ATCC 4330 were use as positive controls for detection of mecC gene and mecA gene, respectively.

Spa typing of the MDR S. aureus isolates

Amplification of the region X of the spa (protein A) gene was performed using primers spa − 1113f (5′AAAGACGATCCTTCGGTGAGC-3′) and spa − 1514r (5′-CAGCAGTAGTGCCGTTTGCTT-3′) [64]. PCR was performed in 40 µL final volume containing 1X of buffer HF, 0.4 µM of each primer, 200 µM of dNTPs and 0.02 U/µL of iProof™ taq polymerase (Biorad, Hercules, CA, USA). The PCR reaction for amplification of the DNA was: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 30 s, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s and elongation at 72 °C for 20 s. Final elongation was performed at 72 °C for 10 min. The PCR was purified using 1X of Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA, USA) according to manufacturer’s instruction and submitted to GATC-biotech AG (GATC-biotech AG, Konstanz, Germany) for sequencing. Sequences were processed using Geneious V7 [74] and the spa types were determined using DNAgear [75]. Minimum spanning trees were prepared using BioNumerix software as described by [76].

Statistical analysis

Microsoft Excel (Windows version 8.1 version) was utilized for descriptive analysis of the data and to generate the box plot that show the prevalence of antibiotic resistance in the studied areas. Epi info™ version 7.1.3.10 (CDC, Atlanta, USA) was utilized to calculate rates with confidence intervals (CI) which were required for the box plot.
