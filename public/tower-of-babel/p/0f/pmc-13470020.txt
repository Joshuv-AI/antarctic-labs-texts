Spatial assessment of heat risk from deregulation of greenbelt in Seoul, South Korea
Authors: Yijune Choi, Jiheui Yoon, Seungmin Jeon, Yeonjae Cho, Heeseong Min, Yongmin Kim, NaYeon Kim, Sang Jun Lee, Chaeyoung Lee, Soonjae Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urban space is a dynamic domain, and areas once designated as greenbelts are often released to secure land for development. Deregulation of greenbelts may alter the urban thermal environment and increase spatial heat risk. This study examined changes in the urban thermal environment following greenbelt deregulation using a Heat Risk Index (HRI). The analysis was conducted in Seoul, South Korea, including a southern district where a greenbelt had been removed. Heat risk was assessed spatially using GIS based on repeated HRI calculations with publicly available data. The HRI was calculated by integrating hazards, exposure, and vulnerability. A historical before-and-after comparison was conducted to evaluate changes in heat risk following greenbelt deregulation, and a high-resolution HRI map was produced for the entire Seoul. The spatial distribution of heat risk in Seoul was influenced not only by average temperature and population density, but also by social vulnerability and inequality in response infrastructure. The analysis of the southern district demonstrated a significant increase in HRI following greenbelt deregulation, driven by reduced green space and increased population density. These findings underscore the need for multidimensional, spatially integrated assessments when formulating urban adaptation strategies in response to future environmental change.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55030-w.

Introduction

Urban space is defined as areas within the urban domain where people reside and carry out their daily activities. It encompasses the total influence of physical and functional infrastructure that enables stable and continuous activities1. The structure and utilization of urban space are shaped by urban planning, a statutory framework to enhance the quality of life and to guide the sustainable long-term development of cities2. Recognizing that modern cities transcend mere physical infrastructure, urban planning has evolved to prioritize public interest and locality3. As a result, the composition of urban space has continuously changed in response to demographic and social structural shifts4.

As a key regulatory tool within this planning framework, the greenbelt is an area designated to restrict urban development to curb the disorderly urban expansion5. The purpose of the greenbelt is to prevent the disorderly expansion of cities into surrounding areas and to provide the urban residents with green spaces necessary for health. Simultaneously, the release of greenbelts is often pursued to mitigate the urban decline associated with the high population density and land scarcity of compact metropolitan regions6. Consequently, greenbelt deregulation is a common response to urban expansion in growing cities, regardless of location7. Reflecting this global shift, a growing number of nations have opted to downsize or deregulate their greenbelt zones to accommodate urban growth8. South Korea exemplifies this trend, as its designated area has remained largely unchanged while the area released from designation has gradually increased, with significant spikes in 2010 and 20219. For example, Seoul implemented decentralization policies from the 1960s to 1980s, evolving from initial dispersal efforts to the urbanization of Gangnam and later the promotion of secondary urban centers10.

The release of greenbelt induces structural changes in the urban environment. Structural changes in land cover and land use are recognized as primary drivers of environmental issues11. During urban development, impermeable surfaces such as roads and buildings increase, while green space decreases. This leads to disruptions in the water cycle, including urban flooding, groundwater depletion, reduced streamflow for ecological maintenance, and water pollution, ultimately contributing to biodiversity loss. Additionally, changes in land cover, such as reductions in tree canopy due to green space loss, alter land surface energy processes, which in turn affect urban temperatures12.

The most prominent manifestation of these structural and thermal changes is the Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect. This phenomenon occurs when urban air temperatures significantly exceed those of surrounding areas, primarily driven by anthropogenic heat release and the expansion of impermeable surfaces, which enhance heat storage. Beyond its environmental impact, the UHI induces various societal problems, including increased electricity demand, higher greenhouse gas emissions, and elevated rates of heat-related illnesses during summer13. UHI is a critical factor in formulating urban climate adaptation policies, highlighting the growing need for early-stage response in urban planning. The intensity of UHI varies according to the types of urban elements present. For instance, previous research in Seoul has demonstrated a correlation between urban temperatures and neighborhood environment characteristics such as land use, floor area ratio, green space, and rivers14.

Given the urgency of these climate-driven challenges, it is imperative to move beyond the mere observation of temperature anomalies and instead quantify the multifaceted impacts of heat on urban environments and their inhabitants. As a robust framework for this purpose, the Heat Risk Index (HRI) is a valuable tool for quantifying the impacts of heat events induced by climate change and identifying urban areas vulnerable to heat. GIS-based HRI analysis integrates spatial datasets such as average land surface temperature, vegetation cover, and population density to visualize and measure heat risk across a city in a quantifiable manner15. This method supports the identification of high-risk areas and the prioritization of mitigation efforts, including urban greening and cooling infrastructure. HRI has been assessed the heat risk in various sites such as Birmingham, UK16, Manchester, UK17, China18 d o Paulo, Brazil19. Previous studies have shown that HRI captures heat risk through the combined dimension of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability rather than relying on temperature alone. Accordingly, it is useful to understand not only citywide heat-risk patterns but also localized heat-risk changes associated with urban development.

Seoul, one of the world’s major megacities, is currently undergoing deregulation to secure development land, making heat-risk assessment increasingly important in the context of ongoing urban expansion. Previous research in Seoul has demonstrated the applicability of GIS-based approaches to urban thermal vulnerability20 and heat-risk assessment21. However, these studies primarily characterized the spatial distribution of vulnerability or heat risk within the existing urban structure, rather than examining how heat-risk conditions change in relation to specific urban development interventions such as greenbelt deregulation. Therefore, this study not only identifies the citywide spatial pattern of heat risk across Seoul using a 500-m grid but also examines the spatiotemporal changes in Segok-dong as a greenbelt deregulation case at a finer 100-m resolution. By incorporating an undeveloped internal area within Segok-dong as a spatial reference, this study further aims to distinguish development-associated heat-risk changes from broader temporal trends, providing a spatially explicit interpretation of heat-risk change in relation to greenbelt deregulation.

Materials and methods

Research area

This study selects Seoul, the capital of South Korea, as its research area (Fig. 1a). Seoul is a representative megacity with a high risk of heatwaves due to its high-density urban structure, uneven distribution of green spaces, and rising average temperatures. Accordingly, this research was conducted to address the need for a systematic heat risk assessment across the entire city. The scope of these studies varies widely, from analyzing the UHI effect22 to calculating an HRI21.

To evaluate the impact of greenbelt deregulation, this study focuses on a southern district of Seoul, Segok-dong (Fig. 1b). This area was designated as a site for national rental housing development and removed from the greenbelt zone in 2005. The previous greenbelt was converted for urban infrastructure, and the development zone has gradually expanded from the southeastern part of the administrative district toward the southwest23. This rapid urbanization led to a demographic explosion, with the population value was approximately 5348 at the start of construction in late 200924, and it surged to 45,531 by late 202325, representing nearly a nine-fold increase. Within Segok-dong, the undeveloped area of the district was designated as a spatial reference zone26 to enable comparison with the developed area.

Heat risk assessment

An HRI was utilized to quantitatively assess heat-related risk and to examine the spatial distribution of heat risk across Seoul. Specifically, three core indicators of heat risk - high temperature hazard, population exposure, and social vulnerability - were assessed independently (Fig. 2). This definition follows the general framework of risk assessment27. Each indicator was independently constructed using spatially relevant indicators. The HRI was calculated by multiplying the values of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability. The calculation of HRI proceeded in two steps: (1) assessing the individual values for hazard, vulnerability, and exposure, and (2) multiplying these indicators to derive the heat risk index.

Assessment of hazard

Hazard refers to the degree to which the external environment threatens human thermoregulation and residential stability40. It is closely associated with urban environmental characteristics such as extreme temperatures and the presence of green infrastructure41. In this study, average temperature and lack of green coverage were applied for the assessment of hazard. Average temperature captures the baseline level of heat stress. Elevated temperatures are associated with increased risks of heat-related illnesses, such as heatstroke and heat exhaustion42. In terms of lack of green coverage, green space scarcity is strongly linked to intensified urban UHI effects.

Assessment of exposure

Exposure indicates the extent to which the population is subjected to heat-related hazards. It was quantified using population density. Strong positive correlation exists between population size and urban temperature variation in large cities43. Densely populated areas are more susceptible to collective heat impacts; therefore, higher exposure values were assigned to regions with greater population density.

Assessment of vulnerability

Vulnerability reflects the relative susceptibility of a population to harm under the same heat exposure conditions44. To account for social and physical sensitivity to heat stress, seven indicators were selected: elderly population ratio, proportion of single-person households, basic livelihood recipients, people with disabilities, ratio of aging buildings, shelter density, and medical accessibility. Notably, both shelter density and medical accessibility were considered as adaptation factors, functioning to reduce the overall vulnerability within the HRI framework45.

Heat risk calculation and mapping

Heat risk maps were produced by repeatedly calculating the HRI at multiple points to identify the spatial distribution of heat risk using ArcGIS Pro. Heat risk maps were produced at two distinct spatial resolutions, each tailored to specific analytical objectives. The mapping framework involved spatially integrating the three dimensions: hazard, exposure, and vulnerability into these respective structures. Heterogeneous spatial datasets were unified into 500-m and 100-m grid structures through rigorous preprocessing, including resampling and spatial allocation, to ensure spatial consistency and comparability. Each indicator was then normalized and aggregated according to the weighting scheme, with the final HRI values iteratively computed for all grid cells using raster functions. This approach was designed to overcome the limitations of conventional aggregate evaluations at the district (Si/Gun/Gu) scale and to ensure the precision of spatial analysis. For the entire Seoul metropolitan area, a 500-m grid was employed to identify the macroscopic spatial patterns of heat risk. In contrast, for the southern district where greenbelt deregulation was conducted, a finer 100-m grid was utilized to enable detailed assessment of localized thermal environmental changes.

For the entire Seoul area, all spatial datasets were resampled to a consistent 500-m grid. The average temperature for the hazard component was derived from Landsat Level 2 imagery50, specifically selecting scenes from the summer period (June–August) with less than 10% cloud cover. While Landsat-derived LST is widely applied in urban heat risk studies18,40, it represents radiative surface temperature rather than ambient air temperature experienced by residents and may not fully capture human thermal stress under varying built-environment conditions. This limitation is acknowledged in Section “Methodological considerations and study limitations”. Urban green coverage was quantified using the European Space Agency (ESA) WorldCover 2021 dataset51). Among the available vegetation classes, areas classified as ‘Tree Cover’ were extracted to calculate the proportional area of tree canopy within each 500-m grid cell, given the dominant role of tree canopy in reducing urban surface temperatures through shading and evapotranspiration12. The lack of green coverage was subsequently calculated by subtracting the tree coverage ratio from 1. Population density for 2023 exposure was spatially allocated from administrative gu-level census data to a 500 m grid, with each cell assigned the population density value of the gu containing its centroid. Vulnerability—comprising elderly residents, single-person households, basic livelihood security recipients, people with disabilities, and aged buildings—was mapped from district-level statistics onto the grid. Finally, the density of cooling shelters and pre-processed 500-m grid data for medical accessibility52 was mapped for representation of adaptive capacity. Each map across Seoul was classified into 10 equal-interval categories for visualization. The resulting HRI map, however, was classified into five classes using natural breaks (Jenks optimization), given the skewed distribution of composite index values.

For the southern district case study, a 100-m grid was employed to evaluate temporal changes between 2009 and 2023. Three dynamic variables, average temperature, urban green coverage, and population density, were calculated for both years to capture the impact of greenbelt deregulation. Average temperature was derived from Landsat Level 2 imagery using the same methodology applied to the Seoul-wide 500-m analysis. Green coverage at the 100-m scale for the southern district case study was calculated by applying a biotope area ratio to each land cover type using the Land Cover Map53, with differentiated weights assigned per cover type, including diverse vegetation classes (Table 2). This approach, rather than simple area ratios, accounts for the spatial distribution of green areas within each land cover type, thereby reducing the potential underestimation of green coverage at the site level. Lack of green coverage was similarly calculated with a Seoul-wide 500-m map as the complement of the urban green coverage. Population density for exposure was derived from census tract-level data, one of the finest available statistical units in South Korea. Due to the limited availability of 2009 census tract-level data, the 2010 population dataset was used to represent the pre-deregulation condition, while the 2023 dataset was used for the post-deregulation condition. The administrative population of Segok-dong was 5,348 in late 2009, whereas the 2010 census tract-level dataset used for the 100-m grid allocation contained 4,389 residents. Although this decrease of 959 residents may reflect early-stage relocation or population redistribution after construction began, the 2010 dataset was selected because it was the closest available population dataset at the spatial resolution required for the 100-m grid analysis. Each year of population density data was allocated to a 100 m grid by assigning each cell the value of its enclosing census tract. This dual-year approach was adopted to capture the temporal demographic shifts driven by urban development. For visualization, population density was classified into ten Natural Breaks classes based on the 2023 distribution, and this classification scheme was applied consistently to both periods. Crucially, to isolate the effects of hazard and exposure, vulnerability was held constant using 2023 values, downscaled from the 500-m Seoul dataset to 100-m resolution using nearest-neighbor assignment for outlying areas. Hazard and vulnerability values were each classified into five equal intervals based on the range observed across both years. Exposure values were classified using a quartile-based scheme in which the upper three classes follow equal intervals of 0.50 derived from the theoretical exposure range [0.25, 2.00] (Q1: 0.75, Q2: 1.25, Q3: 1.75); the lowest range (0.25–0.75) was further subdivided at 0.40 to differentiate near-baseline exposure from areas with marginally elevated values. A bivariate map was additionally constructed to visualize the spatial co-occurrence of changes in hazard and exposure between 2009 and 2023. For this map, Δhazard and Δexposure were each classified into three classes using the equal-interval method, in which the data range of each class is held constant. The two classified variables were then combined into a 3 × 3 bivariate matrix. The class breaks were 0.50 and 1.45 for Δhazard, and 0.55 and 1.75 for Δexposure. To assess temporal change in heat risk, ΔHRI was computed as the cell-wise difference between the 2023 and 2009 HRI values (ΔHRI = HRI₂₀₂₃ − HRI₂₀₀₉). To enable direct temporal comparison, the 2009 and 2023 HRI maps for the southern district were classified into five classes using standard deviation-based thresholds derived from the 2023 HRI distribution, anchoring the classification to the post-deregulation period. The resulting ΔHRI map was similarly classified into five classes using standard deviation-based thresholds derived from the observed ΔHRI distribution.

To distinguish development-associated heat-risk changes from broader temporal trends, the undeveloped area within Segok-dong was incorporated as a spatial reference zone. HRI changes observed in the developed zone were interpreted in relation to concurrent changes in this reference area over the same period. To explicitly delineate the intra-district comparison zones, polygon layers representing the deregulated development area and the undeveloped reference area in 2023 were utilized. The development-area polygon was defined based on the spatial extent of the former greenbelt area that was released for housing and urban infrastructure development, while the reference-area polygon was delineated as the contiguous undeveloped area within Segok-dong that remained outside the development footprint throughout the study period. These polygons were overlaid on the Segok-dong administrative boundary and are shown in Fig. 1b. Paired t-tests were conducted at the grid-cell level to assess (1) temporal changes in HRI sub-components (hazard, exposure, and vulnerability) between 2009 and 2023 within the deregulated development area, and (2) differences in ΔHRI between the deregulated development area and the undeveloped reference area, with statistical significance assessed at α = 0.05.

Results

Heat risk assessment for Seoul

Figure 3 presents the spatial distribution of the ten input indicators used to calculate the HRI for Seoul, each visualized using ten equal-interval classes. Figure 3a displays average land surface temperature during the summer season, with high-temperature zones concentrated in the southern and central urban areas. These zones largely coincide with areas of high lack of green coverage (Fig. 3b), suggesting a spatially reinforcing relationship between thermal exposure and lack of vegetation.

Figure 3c presents population density based on administrative gu-level census data. Because the same gu-level population density value was assigned to grid cells within each gu, this map represents inter-gu differences in registered residential population density rather than fine-scale intra-gu population variation. Higher population density values are observed in the northeastern and southwestern parts of Seoul, where residential districts with large registered populations are located, rather than in the central business district. This pattern reflects the residential-address basis of the administrative population records and should therefore be interpreted as a district-level exposure gradient rather than as fine-scale population variation within each gu.

Figure 3d and h visualize five vulnerability indicators with positive weights: the proportions of elderly population, single-person households, basic livelihood recipients, people with disabilities, and aging buildings, respectively. Specifically, the proportion of elderly, basic livelihood recipients, and disabled residents (Fig. 3d, f and g) tends to increase toward the urban peripheries, especially in the northern and western parts of Seoul.

Figure 3i and j represent negatively weighted indicators: cooling shelter density and medical accessibility. Cooling shelter density (Fig. 3i) reflects the existing provision of designated cooling resources, which may already be shaped by government placement policies and siting guidelines that consider vulnerable populations and thermal conditions55. Medical accessibility (Fig. 3j) is notably higher in densely developed urban and commercial zones, where emergency medical facilities are more concentrated, while peripheral areas dominated by natural terrain, such as mountainous regions, exhibit comparatively lower accessibility due to greater distances to the nearest facilities. These ten indicators were subsequently aggregated into three components (hazard, exposure, and vulnerability) to compute the HRI.

High heat risk is primarily concentrated in peripheral residential zones and high-density vulnerable clusters. Peripheral residential zones in the western, southern, and northeastern parts of Seoul exhibit elevated HRI values despite relatively lower population density, with the elevated risk primarily driven by higher hazard and vulnerability levels in these areas. High-density urban residential areas in the eastern part of Seoul also exhibited elevated heat risk, driven by the combination of high population density and high vulnerability. In contrast, areas exhibiting comparatively low HRI values fall into two broad categories. The first comprises areas where low social vulnerability is the primary suppressing factor, particularly in the central-western and southern parts of Seoul. The second includes areas where abundant green coverage and lower surface temperatures reduce hazard levels, with some of these areas also characterized by low population density, resulting in a simultaneous minimization of all three risk components.

Heat risk assessment for greenbelt deregulation domain

Figure 5 presents the spatiotemporal distribution of raw input variables—land cover, land surface temperature, and population density—for 2009 and 2023 across Segok-dong. With respect to land cover in 2009 and 2023 (Fig. 5a and b), urban land use expanded substantially across the study area by 2023, with an increase observed in transportation infrastructure and commercial facilities. The undeveloped reference area in the western portion of the district retained its green coverage throughout the study period, providing a spatial baseline against which development-associated changes can be distinguished from broader temporal trends.

Comparing summer average temperatures in 2009 and 2023 (Fig. 5c and d), the spatial patterns of cooler zones remain broadly consistent between both periods, with low-temperature areas concentrated along the northeast stream and adjacent to forests and parks in the northwest and southwest portions of the district, reflecting the influence of stable underlying topography. However, land surface temperatures increased substantially across the study area in 2023 relative to 2009, with the most pronounced rise observed in the central and eastern urbanized zone, which formerly consisted of green space within the greenbelt. In contrast, the reference area exhibited comparatively limited thermal change over the same period.

The spatial pattern of population density distribution (Fig. 5e and f) exhibits strong spatial alignment with the physical land surface transformation. Population density increased explosively by 2023, showing a concentrated pattern in the northeastern, western, and west-central parts of the study area alongside the urban development. The reference area, being uninhabited throughout both periods, remained at near-zero population density.

Discussion

Interpreting the spatial distribution of heat risk across Seoul

The spatial distribution of HRI across Seoul shows that elevated heat risk is not uniformly distributed but is concentrated in areas where multiple risk components overlap. In several western and northeastern areas, HRI remains high despite relatively low exposure, suggesting that elevated hazard and vulnerability can sustain high heat risk even where population density is not particularly high. In contrast, some southwestern areas exhibit comparatively low HRI values despite relatively high exposure, indicating that exposure alone does not determine the spatial pattern of heat risk. Rather, exposure appears to amplify heat risk when it coincides with elevated hazard or vulnerability.

This pattern suggests that the Seoul-wide HRI is shaped by the spatial interaction among hazard, exposure, and vulnerability, rather than by a single component. Population density remains an important element of heat risk, especially in high-density residential districts where exposure and vulnerability coincide. However, the results also indicate that areas with lower population density can still experience elevated heat risk when thermal hazard and social vulnerability are high. This is consistent with the observation that residential population density does not necessarily correspond directly to the level of urbanization or thermal stress, because residential distribution is also shaped by social and economic factors56.

Specifically, the spatial pattern of vulnerability further helps explain why HRI differs across districts. Vulnerability-related indicators show heterogeneous distribution across Seoul. In some peripheral areas, elevated social vulnerability appears to reinforce the effect of heat hazard, which may be related to broader patterns of housing affordability and residential inequality in Seoul57. Additionally, the spatial distribution of vulnerabilities is not uniform across all indicators. For example, single-person households tend to be concentrated near major employment and educational centers, reflecting residential preferences of younger working-age and student populations58, whereas aging buildings are more prevalent in central, southwestern, and northern parts of the city where older urban buildings remain32,59. These patterns suggest that vulnerability in Seoul is shaped by multiple social and built-environment factors rather than by a single demographic variable.

In contrast, districts where hazard and vulnerability are both comparatively low tend to exhibit reduced HRI values. Environmentally buffered zones or areas with relatively low vulnerability show low HRI values. These findings are consistent with previous studies that explored the correlation between green-space distribution and heat risk, as well as vulnerability and heat risk18,60,61. The consistency of these patterns with findings from other urban contexts suggests that the analytical framework applied here may be transferable to cities undergoing similar processes of greenbelt deregulation or peri-urban densification. Overall, the Seoul-wide analysis establishes a citywide heat-risk distribution against which localized changes in the southern district can be interpreted. The citywide HRI map thus serves as a broader spatial context for understanding whether the changes observed in the former greenbelt deregulation area represent an isolated local pattern or reflect broader spatial relationships among hazard, exposure, and vulnerability in Seoul.

Spatially associated heat-risk changes in a deregulated greenbelt area

Although Segok-dong is located in the southern part of Seoul, where the citywide HRI map generally indicates relatively low heat-risk conditions compared with major high-risk clusters, the 100 m analysis revealed a localized increase in HRI within the deregulated development area. This contrast highlights the value of linking the Seoul-wide 500 m assessment with the finer-scale Segok-dong analysis: the citywide map provides the broader heat-risk context, whereas the high-resolution case analysis reveals intra-district heterogeneity that would be difficult to identify from the citywide assessment alone. The undeveloped area within Segok-dong further serves as a spatial reference zone that shares the same temporal background as the development area while remaining largely unaffected by land-use conversion. Therefore, the divergence between the development area and the reference zone supports a cautious interpretation of the observed HRI increase as a development-associated spatial change following greenbelt deregulation, rather than as a simple extension of Seoul’s pre-existing heat-risk pattern.

Within the development area, the increase in HRI was closely associated with the spatial cooccurrence of land-cover transformation and population concentration. The replacement of former vegetated surfaces with residential, commercial, and transportation infrastructure likely reduced green coverage and increased impervious surfaces, contributing to higher hazard values. At the same time, residential development was accompanied by a substantial increase in population exposure in areas where hazards had also increased. Notably, while hazard increased broadly across the development area, exposure exhibited a more spatially concentrated pattern aligned with newly constructed residential infrastructure, suggesting the locations of greatest HRI change were shaped by the coincidence of population influx and thermal deterioration. The observed rise in HRI therefore, cannot be explained by a single factor but reflects the simultaneous increase in hazard and exposure within the same grid cells. In contrast, the reference area showed a much smaller and less spatially extensive increase in exposure, indicating that this compound risk dynamic was more pronounced within the deregulated development zone. This pattern is consistent with previous evidence from public residential development areas in Seoul, where land-cover conversion and increased impervious surfaces were associated with higher land surface temperatures62. This pattern is consistent with previous studies showing that reduced green coverage, urban densification, and increased human exposure can amplify heat risk18,61. Also, this observed change aligns with large-scale empirical evidence indicating a strong positive correlation between urban area size and heat island intensity, suggesting that the mere expansion of urban footprints significantly drives surface temperature increases63. In this sense, Segok-dong provides a spatial example of how greenbelt redevelopment may create conditions in which thermal and demographic changes spatially reinforce each other, with implications for heat-risk governance in rapidly urbanizing contexts.

The zone-wise comparison further clarifies the difference between the development area and the undeveloped reference area. Mean hazard increased in both zones between 2009 and 2023, suggesting that a broader temporal background trend documented across South Korea may have affected the study area64. At the same time, the slightly greater increase in hazard in the development area may partly reflect land-cover-driven changes in surface energy conditions following the conversion of vegetated surfaces into built-up land. However, the magnitude of HRI change was substantially greater in the development area than in the reference area. This difference was associated with both a larger increase in exposure and hazard differential within the development area. Exposure showed the stronger zone-wise divergence, increasing approximately 2.4 times more in the development area than in the reference area. As discussed above, HRI increases were most pronounced where hazard and exposure increased simultaneously. In addition, cells with large HRI increases were spatially concentrated within the development area, indicating that heat-risk change was spatially uneven and intensified particularly in areas affected by land-use conversion. These results suggest that, although background temporal changes influenced the entire study area, the development area showed an additional development-associated increase in heat risk beyond that observed in the undeveloped reference area.

The cell-level comparison further illustrates the spatial heterogeneity of heat-risk change within the study area. Cell ID 147, located within the deregulated development area and identified as the grid cell with the maximum ΔHRI, showed a sharp increase in HRI as both hazard and exposure increased simultaneously. This indicates that the most extreme heat-risk increase occurred where thermal deterioration and population concentration overlapped with the same grid cell. In contrast, Cell ID 584, located within the undeveloped reference area and identified as the grid cell with the minimum ΔHRI, maintained the baseline exposure value of 0.25 across both periods and showed a slight decrease in hazard, resulting in a small decline in HRI. The contrasting trajectories of these two cells demonstrate that heat-risk change was not spatially uniform even within the same administrative district. This finding highlights the need for fine-scale spatial assessment when evaluating greenbelt deregulation or large-scale residential development, as district-level averages may obscure localized accumulations of heat risk65. In this regard, the dual-resolution framework adopted in this study helps link the broader citywide heat-risk context with localized intra-district changes.

Methodological considerations and study limitations

One methodological consideration concerns the treatment of vulnerability in the before-and-after comparison of greenbelt deregulation in Segok-dong. In this study, the vulnerability component was held constant using the 2023 vulnerability layer for both the 2009 and 2023 HRI calculations. This was an analytical choice intended to focus on the temporal comparison of changes in hazard and exposure, particularly land-surface transformation, green-cover change, and population redistribution following greenbelt deregulation. We also reviewed the feasibility of reconstructing a 2009 vulnerability layer; however, only three (proportions of single-person households, basic livelihood recipients, and disabilities) of the seven vulnerability-related indicators could be consistently obtained for 2009, while four indicators (elderly population ratio, proportion of aging buildings, shelter density, and medical accessibility) were unavailable or not comparable with the 2023 data. Constructing a partial 2009 vulnerability layer from incomplete indicators could have introduced additional uncertainty and reduced comparability between the two periods. Therefore, a fixed 2023 vulnerability baseline was used to maintain consistency in the vulnerability component while examining changes in hazard and exposure. Consequently, the HRI difference reported in this study should be interpreted not as a complete reconstruction of total heat-risk change, but as a relative assessment of hazard- and exposure-related changes under a fixed vulnerability baseline. Further studies should incorporate time-varying vulnerability data when consistent historical indicators become available.

Although this study provides evidence of development-associated heat-risk changes, the present research design does not constitute a fully replicated BACI (Before-After-Control-Impact) framework66. Segok-dong is a geographically bounded case—study area with only a single internally defined reference zone. Therefore, it was not feasible to apply a fully replicated design in which changes in the impacted area are compared with concurrent changes across multiple control areas to more rigorously separate temporal background variation from impact-related change. Recognizing that such practical constraints often arise in environmental monitoring contexts, where the use of a single reference area may be necessary67, this study used the undeveloped area within Segok-dong as an internal spatial reference zone rather than as a fully replicated control. This reference-zone comparison helps contextualize the observed changes by showing that broader temporal background changes, including regional warming trends, may have affected the entire study area. However, because the development area exhibited a substantially larger additional increase in HRI than the reference area, the results may be interpreted as indicating development-associated spatial change beyond the background trend. Accordingly, the findings should be understood as spatially associated and comparative evidence, rather than as a definitive causal attribution of heat-risk increase to greenbelt deregulation.

Another methodological consideration concerns the structure of the HRI itself. The HRI used in this study is a composite index based on normalized indicators, assigned weights, and a multiplicative integration of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability. Although this structure is theoretically consistent with the premise that heat risk emerges from the co-occurrence of these three components, the resulting spatial patterns may still be influenced by the selected weighting scheme and index formulation. In this study, the sensitivity analysis was designed as a limited robustness check rather than a comprehensive uncertainty analysis of the weighting scheme. Specifically, it compared the baseline weighting scheme with one equal-weight scenario and evaluated spatial agreement using Pearson’s correlation coefficients and grid-level differences. Therefore, the reported correlations should be interpreted as evidence of spatial agreement between the two tested scenarios, not as uncertainty bounds for the HRI or as a systematic evaluation of the full weight-uncertainty space. The results suggest that the broad spatial patterns are reasonably stable under the tested equal-weight scenario, especially for the Seoul-wide analysis and the 2023 Segok-dong analysis. That said, because the analysis was limited to two deterministic scenarios, the stability of broad spatial patterns should not be interpreted as evidence that local HRI values and grid-level rankings are robust to all plausible weighting choices. Therefore, future studies should apply ensemble-based or Monte Carlo-based weight perturbation approaches to more systematically propagate weight uncertainty. In addition, because the HRI integrates hazard, exposure, and vulnerability multiplicatively, high values may emerge where all three components are simultaneously elevated. For example, the extreme HRI increase observed in Cell ID 147 reflects the compound amplification of simultaneously elevated hazard and exposure under the multiplicative structure. These values are therefore interpreted as relative composite index values that indicate spatial priority within the study area, rather than as direct estimates of absolute heat-risk magnitude. Furthermore, the max (0) truncation applied to negative HRI values should be regarded as an operational choice for maintaining a non-negative and interpretable risk scale, rather than as a universal property of heat-risk assessment.

Furthermore, several limitations are associated with the spatial datasets and proxy variables used in this study. In the Seoul-wide 500 m analysis, green coverage was represented by the Tree Cover class from ESA WorldCover, which may underestimate the cooling effects of grassland, shrubs, and other low-lying vegetation. This limitation was partly mitigated in the Segok-dong 100 m analysis by applying a biotope area ratio to land-cover classes, which allowed a broader range of vegetation and surface-cover conditions to be considered. Landsat-derived LST was used as a proxy for thermal hazard, while this approach is widely applied in urban heat risk studies18,40. LST represents radiative surface temperature rather than ambient air temperature or human-perceived thermal stress and therefore may not fully capture the heat conditions experienced by residents.

A further limitation concerns the spatial disaggregation of population data. For the Seoul-wide analysis, population density was allocated from gu-level census data to the 500-m grid. Therefore, the exposure component reflects gu-level average residential population density rather than fine-scale intra-gu variation. Future studies could further refine the exposure component through dasymetric mapping or other population-disaggregation methods using finer-resolution ancillary data. For the Segok-dong case study, this concern was partially mitigated through the use of census tract-level data, representing one of the finest publicly available statistical units in South Korea. However, because construction began in late 2009, the 2010 census tract-level population dataset may already reflect early-stage relocation or population redistribution. The dataset used for the 100-m grid allocation contained 4,389 residents in 2010, compared with the administrative population of 5,348 in late 2009. Thus, the 2010 population layer should be interpreted as an approximate baseline around the onset of development, and the estimated pre-post exposure contrast should be considered with this temporal mismatch in mind. These limitations highlight the need for more consistent, high-resolution spatiotemporal datasets in future heat-risk assessments.

Conclusion

This study applied a GIS-based HRI framework to assess citywide heat-risk distribution in Seoul and localized heat-risk changes in a greenbelt deregulation area, Segok-dong. By integrating hazard, exposure, and vulnerability, the HRI framework enabled heat risk to be evaluated beyond temperature alone and provided a spatially explicit basis for interpreting urban environmental change. The Seoul-wide analysis showed that elevated heat risk was concentrated where hazard and vulnerability spatially overlapped, thereby providing a citywide reference context for the Segok-dong case. In Segok-dong, the mean HRI increased from 0.36 in 2009 to 0.92 in 2023, with the development area showing a larger increase than the undeveloped reference area. This pattern suggests that broader temporal changes affected the study area, while the additional increase in the development area was spatially associated with land-cover transformation, reduced green coverage, and increased population exposure following greenbelt deregulation. These findings are particularly relevant to Seoul’s current planning context, in which partial greenbelt deregulation has recently been reconsidered as part of housing-supply policy. In this context, fine-scale heat-risk screening should be incorporated into the planning and review process for greenbelt deregulation and large-scale residential development, so that land-supply objectives can be evaluated together with potential changes in green coverage, population exposure, and heat-adaptation capacity. Such screening would be consistent with Seoul’s broader climate-adaptation and greening strategies, which emphasize reducing heat-island effects, expanding urban green spaces, and protecting climate-vulnerable populations. Further studies should incorporate time-varying vulnerability indicators, high-resolution population allocation, improved thermal comfort metrics, and climate-change scenarios such as SSPs. Despite its limitations, the dual-resolution HRI framework used in this study shows its potential as a useful approach for detecting localized heat-risk changes in former greenbelt deregulation areas and for supporting spatially informed urban adaptation planning.
