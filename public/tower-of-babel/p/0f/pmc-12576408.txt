Therapeutic Plasma Exchange in Severe Graves Disease With Hematologic and Hepatic Complications
Authors: Vivek Jha, Amal Shaharyar, Kavyanjali Kundu, Siddharth Madhad, Sanja Medenica, Pinaki Dutta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report 2 young women with severe Graves thyrotoxicosis in whom antithyroid drugs were contraindicated—one due to drug induced moderate neutropenia and the other due to Graves disease–associated hepatic dysfunction and cytopenia. Both patients presented with life-threatening manifestations of thyrotoxicosis and were unsuitable for immediate surgery or radioiodine therapy. Therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) was used as a bridging strategy to achieve rapid biochemical control. In the first case, a single session of TPE enabled prompt stabilization for total thyroidectomy. In the second, 3 sessions of TPE led to marked clinical improvement, reversal of hepatic dysfunction and cytopenia, and facilitated successful radioiodine ablation. Neither patient experienced TPE-related complications. These 2 cases highlight the utility of TPE as a safe and effective interim therapy in complicated thyrotoxicosis, allowing timely transition to definitive treatment in high-risk clinical settings where conventional antithyroid therapies are contraindicated.

Introduction

Thyrotoxicosis and thyroid storm, although rare, are life-threatening endocrine emergencies that require urgent treatment to reduce the risk of severe complications [1]. Graves disease is the most common cause and results from autoantibodies stimulating excessive thyroid hormone production. Standard first-line therapies include thionamides such as methimazole, carbimazole, or propylthiouracil, with surgery or radioactive iodine used for definitive management. However, serious adverse effects such as agranulocytosis or hepatic dysfunction—though rare (<1%)—can make antithyroid drug therapy unsafe [2].

In such scenarios, therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) offers a rapid, effective alternative. TPE eliminates circulating free and protein-bound thyroid hormones, thyrotropin receptor antibodies (TRAb), and inflammatory cytokines by filtering plasma and replacing it with albumin or fresh frozen plasma [1, 2]. Originally reserved for thyroid storm, TPE is now applied more broadly in cases where conventional therapies are either ineffective or contraindicated [3]. Preoperative control of thyroid function is critical to avoid intraoperative complications.

First reported by Saïe et al in 1970 [4], the role of TPE in endocrine emergencies has been reinforced by multiple case reports and institutional series [2, 3]. However, few reports illustrate TPE's role across divergent contraindications or as a bridge to both surgery and radioiodine, particularly with multi-organ dysfunction.

We describe 2 young women with Graves disease and severe thyrotoxicosis who were ineligible for standard antithyroid drugs—one due to moderate neutropenia, the other due to hepatic dysfunction and cytopenia, likely from uncontrolled disease. Both required urgent hormonal control but were unfit for immediate surgery or radioiodine. TPE was used successfully in both cases to achieve biochemical stabilization, enabling total thyroidectomy in one and radioiodine ablation in the other. These cases highlight the expanding role of TPE as a safe and effective bridge in complex hyperthyroidism when conventional therapies are contraindicated.

Case Presentation

Case 1

A 26-year-old woman with a 2.5-year history of anterior neck swelling presented with classic symptoms of thyrotoxicosis, including 20 kg weight loss, hyperphagia, palpitations, sweating, and frequent loose stools. She also reported menstrual irregularities—polymenorrhea and menorrhagia—for the past 7 to 8 months. Diagnosed with hyperthyroidism earlier, she had been inconsistently taking carbimazole (20-30 mg/day). There was no history of alcohol use, substance abuse, or hepatic, renal, or infectious comorbidities. She was admitted for planned thyroidectomy due to worsening compressive symptoms, including dysphagia and choking.

Case 2

A 22-year-old woman with Graves disease, diagnosed during early pregnancy 2 years earlier, presented with acute febrile illness, dyspnea, palpitations, diarrhea, and dry cough. Following medical termination of pregnancy, she had been started on carbimazole (30 mg/day), which she discontinued 2 months earlier due to family concerns about potential adverse effects, despite the physician’s recommendation to continue therapy. In this part of the country, such decisions are often influenced by prevailing social taboos and disbelief regarding long-term medication use. At a local hospital, she was found in supraventricular tachycardia (heart rate 196 bpm), treated with intravenous metoprolol and adenosine, and discharged on oral propranolol. Her symptoms progressed, and she was referred to our center.

On presentation, the patient was febrile (101.4 °F [∼38.6 °]), agitated, and tremulous, with a diffuse grade 2 goiter (Fig. 1). Her Burch-Wartofsky point scale score was 80, strongly suggesting thyroid storm, with contributions from thermoregulatory dysfunction, central nervous system effects, gastrointestinal symptoms, tachycardia, dyspnea, and recent antithyroid drug withdrawal. She denied chest pain, recent contrast exposure, or other chronic illnesses.

Diagnostic Assessment

Case 1

Thyroid function testing revealed suppressed thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) at 0.005 µIU/mL (SI: 0.005 mIU/L) (reference range, 0.5-4.5 µIU/mL [SI: 0.5-4.5 mIU/L]), with markedly elevated total triiodothyronine (T3: 4.11-5.86 ng/dL [SI: 6.3-9.0 nmol/L]; reference range, 0.8-2.0 ng/dL [SI: 1.2-3.1 nmol/L]) and total thyroxine (T4: 15.5-21.3 µg/dL [SI: 200-274 nmol/L]; reference range, 4.8-12.7 µg/dL [SI: 62-163 nmol/L]). Thyrotropin receptor antibody (TRAb) was positive at 8.9 IU/L (reference range, <1.75 IU/L). Neck ultrasonography showed a diffusely enlarged, hypervascular thyroid without nodules, with lobes measuring approximately 6 × 2.5 × 2.4 cm. Chest radiograph and electrocardiogram were unremarkable. Case 2

In the second patient, TSH was suppressed to less than 0.001 µIU/mL (SI: < 0.001 mIU/L), with free T3 14.8 pg/mL (SI: 22.8 pmol/L) (reference range, 2.6-4.2 pg/mL [SI: 4.0-6.5 pmol/L]) and free T4 2.73 ng/dL (SI: 35.2 pmol/L) (reference range, 0.8-1.8 ng/dL [SI: 10.3-24.5 pmol/L]). TRAb was elevated at 4.06 IU/L (reference range, <1.75 IU/L).

She exhibited significant cytopenia, with total leukocyte count decreasing from 2800 to 1600/mm³ (SI: 2.8-1.6 × 10⁹/L), ANC of 1120/mm³ (SI: 1.12 × 10⁹/L), hemoglobin declining from 11.2 to 9.4 g/dL [SI: 112-94 g/L] (reference range, 12-16 g/dL [SI: 120-160 g/L]), and persistently low platelet counts of 68 000 to 69 000/µL [SI: 68-69 × 10⁹/L] (reference range, 150 000-450 000/µL [SI: 150-450 × 10⁹/L]).

LFTs were grossly deranged, with peak aspartate transaminase (AST) 1999 IU/L (reference range, <40 IU/L) and alanine transaminase (ALT) 484 IU/L (reference range, <40 IU/L). Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) was 220 IU/L (reference range, 40-125 IU/L). Serum albumin was mildly reduced at 3.53 g/dL [SI: 35.3 g/L] (reference range, 3.5-5.0 g/dL [SI: 35-50 g/L]), and international normalized ratio was elevated at 1.4 (reference range, 0.9-1.2).

Treatment

Case 1

Antithyroid medications were withheld, and carbimazole was discontinued due to leukopenia, raising suspicion of drug-induced cytopenia. The patient was started on lithium carbonate (300 mg 3 times daily) and cholestyramine (4 g 3 times daily) for temporary control. A single session of TPE was performed via central venous access, exchanging approximately 2 L of plasma with 5% albumin, fresh frozen plasma (FFP), and normal saline as replacement fluids. Within 48 hours, total T4 fell to 11 µg/dL (SI: 142 nmol/L), enabling surgical planning. She tolerated the procedure without complications.

Case 2

Antithyroid drugs were avoided due to neutropenia and severe hepatic dysfunction. Given the severity of thyrotoxicosis, the patient underwent 3 alternate-day TPE sessions using the same replacement protocol. This resulted in marked clinical improvement, normalization of liver enzymes, resolution of ARDS, and hematologic recovery (see Table 2). No adverse effects or hemodynamic instability occurred.

Outcome and Follow-up

Case 1

Two days after TPE, the patient underwent total thyroidectomy; histology showed nodular hyperplasia without malignancy. Postoperatively, she developed hypocalcemia, managed with calcium, calcitriol, and vitamin D. Levothyroxine 100 mcg was started at 1 month, and by 3 months she remained well and biochemically euthyroid.

Case 2

One week after her final TPE, the patient received 15 mCi of I-131. Chest infiltrates resolved (Fig. 2B), hepatic and hematologic parameters normalized (see Table 2), and levothyroxine 75 mcg was started at 1 month. At 3 months, she remained clinically stable and euthyroid on follow-up.

Discussion

TPE is increasingly acknowledged as a vital bridging therapy in the acute management of thyrotoxicosis, particularly when conventional treatments like thionamides or radioactive iodine are contraindicated. It facilitates rapid systemic and biochemical stabilization, enabling timely transition to definitive interventions such as surgery or radioiodine ablation. The novelty of these cases lies in contrasting contraindication profiles, systemic recovery, and use of TPE to bridge two distinct definitive therapies.

The effectiveness of TPE lies in its capacity to remove circulating free and protein-bound thyroid hormones, TSH receptor antibodies, and proinflammatory cytokines that contribute to the hypermetabolic state. This is especially valuable in scenarios where pharmacologic suppression is not feasible due to adverse drug reactions, including neutropenia, agranulocytosis. or hepatotoxicity [1, 2]. By also targeting cytokine-mediated systemic effects—such as fever, tachycardia, and hepatic dysfunction—TPE addresses both endocrine and inflammatory drivers of thyroid storm [3, 4].

In both cases, TPE facilitated timely intervention when standard therapies were no longer viable. In the first patient, it enabled rapid hormonal control, reducing the risk of perioperative thyroid storm and allowing safe surgery. In the second, TPE permitted a gradual decline in thyroid hormone levels while awaiting recovery of hepatic and hematologic function. These cases highlight the evolving role of TPE—not merely as an emergency rescue therapy, but as a planned bridge to definitive treatment in complex thyrotoxicosis.

Recent evidence supports this evolving strategy. A comprehensive scoping review by Koziak et al (2024) [5], involving more than 234 patients, reported that TPE resulted in an average 50% to 66% reduction in free T4 and T3 levels after 3 to 6 sessions, leading to improved hemodynamic stability and facilitating timely definitive interventions such as surgery. However, this effect is short-lived due to redistribution of hormones from tissue stores, highlighting the need for early transition to definitive therapy to prevent rebound [6, 7]. This pharmacokinetic consideration reinforces the importance of closely timing the final TPE session with surgery or radioiodine ablation.

The choice of replacement fluids during TPE significantly influences both safety and therapeutic efficacy. A combination of 5% albumin and FFP was used during TPE: albumin for safety and FFP for coagulation factors and T4-binding globulin. In case 1, FFP supported perioperative hemostasis, while in case 2 it addressed hepatic dysfunction and elevated international normalized ratio, ensuring both efficacy and safety [5, 8].

While TPE is generally well tolerated, common risks—such as hypocalcemia, hypotension, and allergic reactions—can be effectively minimized through close monitoring and prophylactic calcium supplementation. Data from large registries and institutional cohorts indicate that serious complications occur in less than 0.5% of procedures when performed by trained teams, affirming the safety of TPE in experienced hands [9].

A previous reported case of propylthiouracil-induced neutropenia further supports the role of TPE in endocrine emergencies. In that instance, TPE facilitated hormonal and immune stabilization, enabling successful radioiodine therapy and recovery [10]. Alongside the current experience, this adds to emerging real-world evidence positioning TPE as a proactive strategy in managing complex thyrotoxicosis.

While the American Society for Apheresis currently classifies TPE as a category II indication—implying second-line use—it is increasingly adopted earlier in patients requiring urgent hormone control or in whom antithyroid drugs are contraindicated [1]. This evolving trend reflects growing clinical consensus on its utility in minimizing morbidity and expediting definitive therapy.

From a technical standpoint, the literature suggests that 3 to 6 TPE sessions are generally sufficient for effective biochemical stabilization, though the range can vary from 2 to 9 sessions depending on clinical context [4, 5]. In our experience, careful coordination of thyroidectomy soon after the final TPE session minimized rebound thyrotoxicosis and ensured surgical safety. Such timing should be individualized, based on hormonal trends, coagulation parameters, and overall clinical recovery.

Despite its proven efficacy, TPE carries considerable financial and logistical burdens, particularly in resource-limited settings. Therefore, careful consideration of cost, availability of expertise, and timely access to definitive therapy is crucial when deciding on its use.

In summary, our experience, consistent with a recent review [5], suggests TPE may facilitate thyroidectomy or radioiodine when antithyroid drugs are contraindicated, though larger studies are needed to establish definitive evidence.
