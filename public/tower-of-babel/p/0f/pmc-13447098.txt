Metabolically and epigenetically reprogrammed splenic TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells exacerbate liver fibrosis
Authors: Liyuan Zhang, Yahui Wang, Kai Wei, Wen Nie, Yayuan Feng, Zhiwen Shi, Hongmei Xiao, Weifen Xie, Yong Lin, Xin Zeng, Yongquan Shi, Wei Tang, Tuo Li, Fu Yang, Ye Zhou, Minjun Wang, Yanfang Liu, Shanrong Liu, Jin Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), especially its severe form, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), progresses to liver fibrosis, leading to cirrhosis and liver cancer. The cross-talk between spleen and liver in MASLD/MASH progression remains poorly understood. Here we found enlarged spleens in patients with MASLD and identified induced TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells in spleens of MASLD/MASH mouse models and patients. These cells exhibited pro-fibrotic properties through secretion of INSR-α. Mechanistically, demethylated DNA and H3K27me3, increased H3K27ac and bolstered enhancer-promoter contact synergistically reorganized chromatin topologically associating domains spatially to initiate the expression of transcription factor TRNP1 in splenic CD8+ T cells. TRNP1 then transcriptionally activated the expression of FURIN and CTSD, promoting the maturation and ectodomain shedding of INSR-α and facilitating its secretion to activate hepatic stellate cells. In vivo blockade of INSR-α using neutralizing antibodies alleviated MASLD/MASH-induced liver fibrosis. This study reveals splenic TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells with pro-fibrotic properties and suggests a potential anti-fibrotic strategy.

Main

The global prevalence of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is rising annually, with an incidence of approximately 30% in the adult population1,2. Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), the severe form of MASLD, is featured by not only steatosis but also hepatocyte damage and inflammation3,4. MASH progression leads to liver fibrosis, which is marked by excessive deposition of extracellular matrix produced by activated hepatic stellate cells (HSCs)5–7. Elucidating the mechanisms underlying MASLD and MASH and their progression to fibrosis, along with the discovery of effective intervention strategies, remains a critical priority in this field.

The aberrant activation of immune cells has been shown to promote MASLD/MASH development8–12. Given that the spleen is a secondary lymphoid organ harboring various immune cells, and that splenic outflow constitutes a substantial portion of intrahepatic blood flow, the potential spleen–liver axis may be important in the pathogenesis and progression of MASLD/MASH13–18. A variety of spleen-derived immune cells, cytokines and metabolic factors have been demonstrated to link the spleen and liver14–18. However, whether the cross-talk between the spleen and liver contributes to the progression from MASLD/MASH to fibrosis remains poorly understood.

Immune cells, particularly CD8+ T cells and their subsets, have garnered much attention in the pathogenesis of MASLD/MASH19–21. Liver-resident CXCR6+CD8+ T cells accumulate during MASH and exhibit auto-aggressive liver damage properties19. Hepatic CD8+ T cells with memory phenotype have been shown to suppress fibrosis by inducing the apoptosis of activated HSCs20. These reports indicate that various subsets of CD8+ T cells with distinct properties may participate in MASLD/MASH development, but it remains unclear whether splenic CD8+ T cells are reprogrammed during MASLD/MASH.

Here, we recruited 4,537 participants in a population-based cross-sectional study and found enlarged spleens in patients with MASLD, suggesting a potential spleen–liver axis in MASLD/MASH progression. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of splenocytes suggested that TMF1-regulated nuclear protein 1 (TRNP1), a transcription factor involved in cerebral cortex development22, was prominently induced in splenic CD8+ T cells and contributed to their pro-fibrotic properties. Therefore, in this study, we focused on the generation and function of splenic TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells induced during MASLD/MASH.

Results

MASLD/MASH progression is correlated with an enlarged spleen

To investigate the potential cross-talk between the spleen and liver during MASLD progression, we conducted a population-based cross-sectional study by recruiting two cohorts. Cohort 1 comprised 1,077 individuals with computed tomography (CT) images (Fig. 1a,b and Supplementary Table 1)23,24, and a negative correlation was observed between spleen size and liver/spleen Hounsfield unit (HU) ratio (Fig. 1c,d), indicating that CT hepatic attenuation manifesting MASLD progression is correlated with enlarged spleen size. Cohort 2 included 3,460 participants in our health survey (Fig. 1e,f and Supplementary Table 2)25,26, and a positive correlation was also found between MASLD severity and spleen size (Fig. 1g). Additionally, an increased spleen-to-body weight ratio was confirmed in MASLD/MASH mouse models using high-fat diet (HFD), Western diet (WD), choline-deficient, l-amino acid-defined HFD (CDAHFD) and methionine/choline-deficient HFD (MCDHFD) (Fig. 1h). Induction of TRNP1 in splenic CD8+ T cells was confirmed by RNAscope co-detection of Trnp1 mRNA and CD8 protein (Fig. 2g), and TRNP1 mRNA and protein expression were also validated in MASLD/MASH splenic CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2h–k and Extended Data Fig. 1f–h). However, Trnp1 expression was not markedly increased in liver CD8+ T cells during MASLD/MASH (Fig. 2l). In the peripheral blood of MASLD/MASH mice, these TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells were present but to a lesser extent compared to those in the spleen (Fig. 2m,n). Furthermore, the increase in TRNP1 in CD8+ peripheral blood T cells during MASLD/MASH was abolished by splenectomy (Fig. 2m,n), suggesting that these cells were mainly induced in the spleen. We conclude that TRNP1 is induced in splenic CD8+ T cells during MASLD/MASH.

Splenic TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells exacerbate liver fibrosis

To elucidate the function of the induced splenic TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells, we constructed CD8+ T-specific TRNP1 knockout (Trnp1cko) mice by crossing Trnp1f/f with Cd8Cre mice (Extended Data Fig. 2a–c). There were no differences in splenic CD4+ or CD8+ T populations between Trnp1f/f and Trnp1cko mice (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). In the MCDHFD-induced MASLD/MASH model (Fig. 3a), Trnp1cko mice showed suppressed MASH-induced liver fibrosis, as evidenced by reduced Sirius Red, Masson and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) staining in liver tissues (Fig. 3b), and lower serum alkaline phosphatase levels (Extended Data Fig. 2d). Expression of hepatic fibrotic genes, excluding transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), was decreased in Trnp1cko mice, suggesting repressed HSC activation independent of TGF-β (Fig. 3c). Notably, the pro-fibrotic effect of TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells was not attributable to changes in liver-to-body weight, hepatic lipid deposition, hepatocyte damage or liver inflammation (Extended Data Fig. 2d–h). Moreover, we adoptively transferred splenic TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells (Fig. 3a) and observed aggravated liver fibrosis in Trnp1f/f mice and rescued fibrosis in Trnp1cko mice (Fig. 3b,c). Additionally, consistent results were also observed in mice exposed to CDAHFD (Extended Data Fig. 2i–o). We further constructed Trnp1dtrCd8cre mice to deplete TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells specifically in vivo following diphtheria toxin (DT) injection (Extended Data Fig. 3a–c). There was no gross difference between CD4+ or CD8+ T cell populations in spleens of Trnp1dtr and Trnp1dtrCd8cre (Supplementary Fig. 4a,b) or in CD3+CD8− T cell populations after DT injection (Supplementary Fig. 4c,d). Similar to Trnp1cko mice, depletion of TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells suppressed liver fibrosis (Fig. 3d,e and Extended Data Fig. 3d) and reduced fibrotic gene expression, excluding TGF-β (Fig. 3f), whereas liver-to-body weight, hepatic lipid deposition, hepatocyte damage and liver inflammation were not influenced (Extended Data Fig. 3d–h). Moreover, after depletion of TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells, their adoptive transfer rescued MASLD/MASH-induced liver fibrosis (Fig. 3d–f). Similar results were also observed in the CDAHFD-induced MASLD/MASH model (Extended Data Fig. 3i–o). Additionally, we performed splenectomy and observed alleviated MASLD/MASH-induced liver fibrosis, which was rescued by the adoptive transfer of TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells (Fig. 3g–i and Extended Data Fig. 3p,q). Collectively, these findings suggest that induced splenic TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells promote the progression from MASLD/MASH to fibrosis, which is dependent on the transcription factor TRNP1.

Methyl insufficiency reorganizes chromatin to induce TRNP1

We first analyzed how TRNP1 is induced in splenic CD8+ T cells during MASLD/MASH. As TRNP1 in splenic CD8+ T cells was induced by exposure to MCDHFD and CDAHFD, but to a lesser extent by HFD or WD (Fig. 2g–k and Extended Data Fig. 1f–h), we hypothesized that an insufficient methyl supply resulting from deprivation of choline and/or methionine contributed to its induction, because MASLD and MASH are associated with unhealthy diets characterized by not only high caloric and fat intake but also insufficient methionine and choline, the essential raw materials for methyl27–31. RNA-seq analysis revealed the enrichment of one-carbon metabolism and methylation pathways in MASLD/MASH splenic CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4a, Extended Data Fig. 4a,b, Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Note 2). Insufficient methyl leads to demethylation of DNA and histones, thereby remodeling chromatin structures29,32. We constructed comprehensive epigenetic libraries using Hi-C (a derivative of the chromosome conformation capture technique), cleavage under targets and tagmentation sequencing (CUT&TAG-seq), assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing (ATAC-seq), whole-genome bisulfite sequencing and RNA-seq in splenic CD8+ T cells of MASLD/MASH both in vivo and in vitro (Fig. 4c, Extended Data Fig. 4c–j and Supplementary Notes 3–6).

Hi-C and CCCTC-binding factor CUT&TAG-seq, the insulator occupied the boundary of topologically associating domains (TADs) to halt extrusion of DNA loop32, revealed the reorganization of TADs and specific long-range chromatin interactions (henceforth referred to as loops) at Trnp1 chromatin loci in MASLD/MASH splenic CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4d). As histone 3 lysine 9 trimethylation (H3K9me3) and H3K27me3 are histone repressive marks associated with gene silencing, we performed CUT&TAG-seq of H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 (Extended Data Fig. 4c–e) and found global demethylation of H3K27me3, but not H3K9me3, in CD8+ T cells from MASLD/MASH spleens (Extended Data Fig. 4c–f and Supplementary Table 4). Demethylation of H3K27me3 was evident in the promotor regions of Trnp1 (Fig. 4e), as confirmed by cleavage under targets and release using nuclease (CUT&RUN)-qPCR (Fig. 4f). At the chromatin level, consistent with the higher mRNA expression of Trnp1, the increased chromatin accessibility was observed at its multiple loci in MASLD/MASH CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 4i,j). We also performed CUT&TAG-seq of H3K27 acetylation (H3K27ac), marking active enhancers and promotors, and found globally enhanced H3K27ac in MASLD/MASH splenic CD8+ T cells (Extended Data Fig. 4g,h), which may be attributed to elevated intracellular acetyl-CoA serving as the acetyl donor for histone acetylation (Extended Data Fig. 4k). Differential peak analysis revealed that MCDHFD exposure increased the chromatin opening of Trnp1 at its superenhancer (SE) regions (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6)33, as confirmed by CUT&RUN-qPCR (Fig. 4f). The precise contacts between a cluster of enhancers (E), marked by H3K27ac, and their target promoter (P) are critical for mammalian gene expression33,34. Hi-C revealed the bolstered E–P communications at Trnp1 loci in MASLD/MASH splenic CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4e). DNA methylation levels in CpG, CHG and CHH within the promoter region of Trnp1 were also reduced in CD8+ T cells from MASLD/MASH spleens (Fig. 4g). These data suggest the epigenetic induction of Trnp1 in splenic CD8+ T cells of MASLD/MASH.

Similarly, consistent results were obtained by the construction of these epigenetic libraries in the CD8+ T cells from CDAHFD-induced MASLD/MASH spleens, although to a lesser extent compared to MCDHFD (Fig. 4h and Extended Data Fig. 4l–n). Additionally, decreased H3K27me3 levels, increased chromatin accessibility and elevated expression of Trnp1 were also observed in splenic CD8+ T cells cultured using l-methionine-deficient medium in vitro (Fig. 4i,j and Extended Data Fig. 4o–q). Furthermore, EPZ6438, an inhibitor of H3K27 methyltransferase, decreased H3K27me3 modification at the Trnp1 promoter and increased its expression in splenic CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4k,l), whereas 5-aza-2′-deoxycytidine (5-Aza-CdR), an inhibitor of DNA methyltransferases that suppresses DNA methylation, also increased Trnp1 expression (Fig. 4m). Collectively, methyl insufficiency induces TRNP1 in splenic CD8+ T cells through DNA and H3K27me3 demethylation, increased H3K27ac modification, bolstered E–P communication and subsequent chromatin reorganization.

Splenic TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells secrete INSR-α to activate HSCs

HSCs were then treated with recombinant INSR-α in vitro, which induced their activation, as shown by increased fibrotic genes expression and decreased lipid storage (Fig. 5e,f). Furthermore, serum INSR-α during MASLD/MASH was reduced in Trnp1cko and TRNP1hiCD8+ T cell-depleted mice, or by splenectomy, and was rescued by adoptive transfer of TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells (Fig. 5g–i). Increased INSR-α in the culture supernatant of MASLD/MASH splenic CD8+ T cells was also abolished by Trnp1cko (Extended Data Fig. 5i). However, serum INSR-β was not influenced by MASLD/MASH (Extended Data Fig. 5j). Therefore, induced splenic TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells secrete INSR-α to exacerbate liver fibrosis by activating HSCs.

To investigate whether targeting secreted INSR-α blocks liver fibrosis, we used INSR neutralizing antibody and ersodetug (RZ358), a monoclonal antibody targeting INSR37 (Fig. 5j), and found that both alleviated liver fibrosis in vivo (Fig. 5k,l and Extended Data Fig. 6a–d). Furthermore, the anti-fibrotic potential of ersodetug was also demonstrated in MASLD/MASH-induced fibrosis aggravated by the adoptive transfer of TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells (Extended Data Fig. 6e–i), and the current treatment dose had little effect on blood glucose tolerance or serum insulin (Extended Data Fig. 6j,k). Therefore, targeting INSR-α using neutralizing antibody or ersodetug may hold considerable therapeutic potential in alleviating MASLD/MASH-induced fibrosis.

How INSR-α activates HSCs was then investigated. INSR comprises extracellular, transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains and exists as a tetramer formed by two αβ homodimers, with its ectodomain encompassing the entire α-subunit and part of the β-subunit38. The α-subunit serves as the ligand-binding site, transducing extracellular signals into intracellular MAPK signaling39, and downstream extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) may phosphorylate p90 ribosomal S6 kinase (p90RSK) to initiate HSC activation40. We queried the BioGRID protein-protein interaction database and found that INSR interacts with itself, and we confirmed this interaction (Fig. 5m), demonstrating that circulating INSR-α can bind to cell membrane INSR of HSCs. Furthermore, exposure to recombinant INSR-α resulted in the phosphorylation of ERK and downstream p90RSK, which translocated into the nucleus, thereby promoting HSC activation (Fig. 5n–p). Additionally, increased phosphorylation of ERK and p90RSK was observed in primary HSCs from MASLD/MASH livers, which was abolished by anti-INSR treatment in vivo (Extended Data Fig. 6l–o). Together, these findings suggest that splenic TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells secrete INSR-α to promote MASLD/MASH-induced liver fibrosis by activating HSCs, which can be targeted by INSR neutralizing antibody or ersodetug.

TRNP1 activates FURIN and CTSD to cleave and secrete INSR-α

Commonly upregulated genes were further intersected with transcription factor TRNP1-binding genes in the genome identified by CUT&TAG-seq (Supplementary Table 8), pinpointing 79 genes (Fig. 6a and Extended Data Fig. 7a,b). Increased mRNA and protein expression of Furin and Ctsd was confirmed in CD8+ T cells from MASLD/MASH spleens (Fig. 6b,c and Extended Data Fig. 7d) and was abolished by Trnp1cko (Fig. 6d and Extended Data Fig. 7e,f). In MASLD/MASH splenic CD8+ T cells, protein levels of both INSR-α and INSR-β were elevated (Fig. 6c and Extended Data Fig. 7d), and their mature forms were increased in the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus and cell membrane (Fig. 6e and Extended Data Fig. 7g), suggesting enhanced cleavage and maturation of pro-INSR and its trafficking to the cell membrane. Furthermore, the co-localization of FURIN and INSR in the Golgi apparatus of CD8+ T cells was observed through immunostaining (Fig. 6f), and overexpression of FURIN decreased pro-INSR while increased INSR-α and INSR-β (Fig. 6g). Thus, increased FURIN in TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells promotes the cleavage of pro-INSR into INSR-α and INSR-β, thereby enhancing their trafficking to the cell membrane.

Subsequently, to examine whether increased CTSD promotes the ectodomain shedding of mature INSR on cell membrane, we found the elevated CTSD on cell membrane of CD8+ T cells from MASLD/MASH spleens (Extended Data Fig. 7h,i), which co-localized with INSR (Fig. 6h). Overexpression of CTSD led to increased INSR-α in the culture supernatant (Fig. 6i,j), indicating the ectodomain shedding of INSR by CTSD. Collectively, the increased FURIN proteolyzes pro-INSR into mature INSR tetramer, thereafter, the upregulated CTSD promotes its ectodomain shedding in TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells, resulting in the elevated circulating INSR-α.

How TRNP1 transcriptionally activates Furin and Ctsd expression was then investigated. Hi-C and CCCTC-binding factor CUT&TAG-seq revealed the reorganization of TADs and specific loops at Furin and Ctsd in MASLD/MASH splenic CD8+ T cells (Extended Data Fig. 8a,b and Supplementary Note 3). CUT&TAG-seq of H3K27ac showed increased modification at Furin and (Fig. 6k,l, Extended Data Fig. 8c–e and Supplementary Notes 4 and 5), and Hi-C revealed increased chromatin accessibility and enhanced E–P communication (Fig. 6k,l). In addition to the reduced DNA methylation at Furin and Ctsd (Fig. 6m,n and Supplementary Note 6), epigenetic reprogramming potentially contributed to their elevated expression in CD8+ T cells from MASLD/MASH spleens. However, H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 modifications were not significantly decreased, likely due to their low baseline levels (Extended Data Fig. 8f–i). In vitro culture using l-methionine-deficient medium further demonstrated increased chromatin accessibility and upregulated expression of Furin and Ctsd in splenic CD8+ T cells (Extended Data Fig. 8j–m). In line with the decreased DNA methylation and unchanged H3K27me3 at Furin and Ctsd, their expression in splenic CD8+ T cells was increased by the DNA methylation inhibitor 5-Aza-CdR (Extended Data Fig. 8n) but was not influenced by histone methyltransferase inhibitor (Extended Data Fig. 8o).

TRNP1 was predominantly localized in the nucleus of CD8+ T cells from MASLD/MASH spleens (Fig. 6o and Extended Data Fig. 9a), and CUT&TAG-seq and CUT&RUN-qPCR revealed enhanced binding between TRNP1 with Furin and Ctsd (Fig. 6p,q and Extended Data Fig. 9b–d), with consensus DNA-binding motifs (Extended Data Fig. 9e,f). Additionally, promoter luciferase reporter assays showed that TRNP1 amplified Furin and Ctsd promoter activity (Extended Data Fig. 9g). Together, DNA demethylation, increased H3K27ac, bolstered E–P contacts and opened chromatin synergize with TRNP1 transcriptional activity to promote FURIN and CTSD expression and enhance the maturation and ectodomain shedding of INSR-α in splenic TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells, thereby promoting the secretion pro-fibrotic INSR-α during MASLD/MASH.

To examine the epigenetic status of CD8+ T cells in healthy individuals and patients with MASH, peripheral CD8+ T cells were isolated from three age- and sex-matched healthy individuals and three patients with MASH (Fig. 7e and Extended Data Fig. 10e–k). 3D reconstruction showed the larger spleens in patients with MASH (Fig. 7f and Extended Data Fig. 10e), and TRNP1, FURIN and CTSD expression in CD8+ T cells and serum INSR-α was also elevated (Fig. 7g–i and Extended Data Fig. 10e). Moreover, CUT&TAG-seq showed less H3K27me3 modification in TRNP1 (Fig. 7g), whereas ATAC-seq revealed higher chromatin accessibility in TRNP1, FURIN and CTSD of CD8+ T cells from patients with MASH (Fig. 7g–i and Supplementary Notes 4 and 5). These human data support the generation of TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells through demethylation and chromatin decompaction.

We also isolated peripheral CD8+ T cells from a healthy male and a healthy female and cultured them using l-methionine-deficient medium (Fig. 7j and Extended Data Fig. 10l,m). CUT&TAG-seq analysis identified reduced H3K27me3 in TRNP1 (Fig. 7k), whereas ATAC-seq revealed increased chromatin accessibility in TRNP1, FURIN and CTSD following methionine starvation in vitro (Fig. 7k–m and Supplementary Notes 4 and 5). Additionally, TRNP1, FURIN and CTSD expression was increased by methionine starvation, which was abolished by l-methionine supplementation (Fig. 7n). Collectively, methyl insufficiency promotes the generation of TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells through epigenetic reprogramming in human MASLD/MASH.

Discussion

Following the association between enlarged spleen and MASLD indicated by our population-based cross-sectional study of 4,537 individuals, we focused on the role of spleen–liver axis in MASLD/MASH progression. Demethylated DNA and H3K27me3, increased H3K27ac and E–P communication, synergistically induce the transcription factor TRNP1 in splenic CD8+ T cells. TRNP1 then transcriptionally activates FURIN and CTSD expression, promoting the maturation and ectodomain shedding of INSR-α, thereby facilitating its secretion to activate HSCs and aggravate fibrosis. Moreover, blockade of INSR-α using neutralizing antibody or ersodetug alleviates MASLD/MASH-induced liver fibrosis. This study shows that TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells have profibrotic properties and suggests a potential anti-fibrotic strategy. The outflow of spleen constitutes a substantial portion of intrahepatic blood flow, establishing the anatomical basis for spleen–liver axis46–48. Studies have shown that splenic CD4+ T cells, particularly Th2 and regulatory T cell subsets, contribute to liver fibrosis induced by CCl4 and schistosome15,16, and splenic CD11b+CD43hiLy6Clo monocytes and CX3CR1+ monocytes migrate from spleen into liver to activate HSCs14,17. Together with the finding that splenic TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells exhibit pro-fibrotic properties, these findings suggest that splenectomy may benefit patients with various types of chronic liver disease 48, which warrants further examination, especially in clinical settings.

The progression of chronic liver diseases culminates in liver fibrosis and cirrhosis, which are generally considered irreversible49, underscoring the block or delay of progressive fibrosis as a therapeutic strategy50. Here, anti-INSR is suggested to hold considerable therapeutic potential. Notably, ersodetug, a monoclonal antibody treating hypoglycemia37, demonstrates promising effects in blocking circulating INSR and attenuating fibrosis without causing significant hyperglycemia, likely due to the much lower dosage used. Therefore, ersodetug shows considerable potential as an anti-fibrotic strategy, particularly for liver fibrosis caused by MASLD/MASH.

In addition to the open questions asked here, this study has several limitations. First, although we have suggested that methyl insufficiency induces TRNP1 in splenic CD8+ T cells, the reason why TRNP1 is not increased in hepatic CD8+ T cells remains largely unexplored. Further investigations are required to determine whether the splenic microenvironment also contributes to the induction of TRNP1 in CD8+ T cells. Second, although the genome-wide binding profile of TRNP1 has been characterized, the transcription machinery responsible for initiating downstream gene expression, including transcription co-factors and RNA polymerases, remains to be elucidated. Clarifying these mechanisms will provide more definitive evidence and enhance our understanding of the identity and property of TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells.

Methods

This study complied with all relevant ethical regulations and was approved by the Ethics Committees of Changzheng Hospital (2018SL041 and 2016SL039) and Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital (EHBHKY2023G-H001-P001), Naval Medical University, Shanghai, China. All individuals provided written informed consent. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, with the approval (32400743) of the Scientific Investigation Board of Naval Medical University, Shanghai, China.

Study population and design

Cohort 1 participants were enrolled from March to May 2018 who underwent abdominal CT scan in outpatient or physical examination department. Eligibility criteria included age 20 to 80 y, normal limb functions and available abdominal CT or chest CT scans from which an adequate image at the lumbar 3 level can be obtained. Exclusion criteria included: no available CT image, history of chronic liver disease, liver cirrhosis or hepatic malignancy, or average daily alcohol consumption >30 g in men and 20 g in women. The collimating reconstruction thickness of CT scan was set as 1 mm, and the interval was 1 mm. The images were analyzed by two independent radiologists using SliceOmatic V5.0 software (Rev-8, Tomovision)23,24. Attenuation values of liver and spleen were measured by obtaining HU for regions of interest >100 mm2, and the liver/spleen (L/S) HU ratio was calculated. Hepatic steatosis was defined as mild (0.7 ≤ L/S < 1), moderate (0.5 ≤ L/S < 0.7) and severe (L/S < 0.5)51. Splenic size was measured on sections through splenic hilum52. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Changzheng Hospital, Naval Medical University, Shanghai, China (2018SL041).

Cohort 2 participants were enrolled from December 2016 to July 2017, which was supported by a Three-Year Action Plan for strengthening the construction of public health system in Shanghai (GWIV-27.7)25. This was a neighborhood-based cross-sectional health survey in Shanghai, and the inhabitants were enrolled from 242 community health service centers across 18 districts in Shanghai25. Inhabitants who lived in Shanghai for more than 6 months initially met the eligibility criteria of the survey; individuals with mental disorders or dementia, deafness, infectious diseases (hepatitis, active tuberculosis, AIDS or other infectious diseases) or who were bedridden or pregnant were excluded. The target population was selected among inhabitants according to a multistage, stratified, random sampling scheme25. A total of 4,120 subjects completed the cross-sectional health survey and underwent abdominal ultrasound examination. Hepatic steatosis was determined by the diffuse increasing of hepatic echogenicity in comparison to renal echogenicity and visualization of intrahepatic vessels, liver parenchyma and diaphragm. Hepatic steatosis severity was stratified as absent (normal hepatic echotexture), mild (increased hepatic echogenicity compared to renal cortex only), moderate (increased hepatic echogenicity greater than renal cortex and slightly impaired appearance of portal vein wall and diaphragm) or severe (markedly increased hepatic echogenicity compared to renal cortex and poor or no visualization of portal vein wall, posterior part of right liver lobe and diaphragm). In this study, exclusion criteria included: missing data, history of chronic liver diseases, age <18 y, liver cirrhosis or hepatic malignancy, or average daily alcohol consumption >30 g in men and 20 g in women. Spleen cross-sectional area was determined by S = 0.8 × length × width53. Hepatic steatosis index was calculated as follows: 8 × (alanine aminotransferase (ALT)/aspartate aminotransferase (AST)) + body mass index + (2, if female) + (2, if diabetes)54. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Changzheng Hospital, Naval Medical University, Shanghai, China (2016SL039).

Cohort 3 participants were enrolled from December 2023 to March 2025 at Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital. Healthy controls were enrolled during their annual physical examination, which excluded chronic diseases such as liver disease measured by abdominal CT scan. Patients with MASLD were enrolled based on a diagnosis using abdominal CT scan and met the diagnostic criteria in MASLD guidelines47. Peripheral blood was collected, and patients with MASLD were treatment naive. In these patients, hepatic fibrotic index, including FIB-4 and NFS, was calculated as follows: FIB-4 = [age (years) × AST (U liter−1)] / [platelet count (1,000 cells μl−1) × ALT (U liter−1)1/2]55; NFS = −1.675 + [0.37 × age (years)] + [0.094 × body mass index (kg/m2)] + [1.13 × impaired fasting glucose (or diabetes, yes = 1, no = 0)] + (0.99 × AST/ALT) − [0.013 × platelet count (×109 liter−1)] − [0.66 × albumin (g dl−1)]56. All human peripheral blood samples were obtained with written informed consent from each subject. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital, Naval Medical University, Shanghai, China (EHBHKY2023G-H001-P001).

Mice

C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Joint Ventures Sipper BK Experimental Animal. Trnp1f/f mice, Trnp1dtr mice, and Cd8cre transgenic mice were constructed by Cyagen Biosciences. For targeted deletion of TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells, Trnp1dtrCd8cre mice were intraperitoneally injected with DT (D381867, Aladdin) at 10 ng g−1 twice a week. The genotyping primers of mice are listed in Supplementary Table 10. All mice were C57BL/6 background and housed in ventilated cages in specific-pathogen-free conditions, with constant temperature (22 ± 1 °C), controlled humidity and a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with National Institute of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, with the approval (32400743) of the Scientific Investigation Board of Naval Medical University, Shanghai, China.

For MASLD/MASH mice models, 6-week-old mice were fed an MCDHFD (A06071301B, Research Diets), CDAHFD (A06071302, Research Diets) or WD (D12079B, Research Diets) for 7 weeks or fed an HFD (D12492, Research Diets) for 5 months. For adoptive transfer experiments, splenic TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells were isolated from mice fed an MCDHFD or CDAHFD for 7 weeks. Isolated splenic TRNP1hiCD8+ T cells (2 × 105 cells) were then adoptively transferred into recipient mice respectively once a week by tail vein injection.

Reagents

RPMI 1640 medium (11875093), RPMI 1640 l-methionine-deficient medium (A1451701), DMEM (11965092), penicillin-streptomycin (15140122), fetal bovine serum (FBS; 10099141 C) and trypsin-EDTA (0.05%) (25300054) were from Gibco. Information regarding antibodies used is summarized in Supplementary Table 11.

Cell culture

HEK293T cells (CRL-3216) were obtained from ATCC and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cells were cultured in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

CD8+ T cell isolation

For mouse CD8+ T cells, spleens were detached from euthanized mice and mashed through the 40 μm nylon cell strainer (BD Falcon) for splenocyte suspension. Splenic CD8+ T cells were positively selected using CD8+ T Cell MicroBeads (130-117-044, Miltenyi Biotec). For human CD8+ T cells, peripheral blood was loaded onto Lymphoprep (1858, Serumwerk Bemburg) to separate peripheral blood mononuclear cells by centrifugation (800 g for 20 min, room temperature), and primary CD8+ T cells were further purified by CD8 MicroBeads (130-045-201, Miltenyi Biotec).

Ex vivo CD8+ T cell culture

Purified primary CD8+ T cells were seeded into 96-well culture plates coated with anti-CD3 (5 μg ml−1, mouse: 553057, human: 555329, BD Biosciences) and anti-CD28 (2.5 μg ml−1, mouse: 553294, human: 555725, BD Biosciences) in RPMI 1640 complete medium supplemented with 100 ng ml−1 IL-2 (mouse: Ck24, human: C013, Novoprotein). RPMI 1640 l-methionine-deficient medium was administrated in CD8+ T cells, ATAC-seq and CUT&TAG-seq were performed 6 h later and mRNA or protein extraction was performed 8 h later. In specific experiments, primary CD8+ T cells were treated with DMSO (HY-Y0320), l-methionine (HY-N0326), chloride choline (HY-B1337), EPZ6438 (HY-13803) or 5-Aza-CdR (HY-A0004) for 8 h (all from MedChemExpress) and then harvested for analysis.

Injection of recombinant INSR or its neutralizing antibodies

For administration of INSR in vivo, 100 ng INSR recombinant protein (HY-P73873, MedChemExpress) was intraperitoneally injected twice per week. Similarly, INSR neutralizing antibody (100 μg, BE0338, BioXcell), ersodetug (100 μg, HY-P990712, MedChemExpress) or 100 μg control isotype was intraperitoneally injected twice per week.

Isolation of HSCs

Eight-week-old mice were used to isolated hepatic non-parenchymal cells following a published protocol57,58. Briefly, the mouse was anesthetized and the exposed liver was perfused in the inferior vena cava above kidney with perfusion and digestion buffer. Thereafter, the liver capsule was peeled to release cells. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 50 g for 2 min. The supernatant was collected for isolating HSCs using 12% OptiPrep (D1556, Sigma-Aldrich) at 1,400 g for 20 min at 4 °C. After centrifugation, HSCs were in the upper layer of OptiPrep solution. Isolated HSCs were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. For knockdown of INSR, isolated HSCs were transfected with shRNA lentivirus with polybrene (TR-1003-G, Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h and then selected with puromycin (1 μg ml−1) for 4 days. Before addition of recombinant INSR protein (600 ng ml−1, HY-P73873A, MedChemExpress), primary HSCs were serum starved overnight.

ELISA

Concentrations of acetyl-CoA (E-EL-0125, Elabscience), INSR-α (mouse: YBS-10199, human: YBS-20199, YOUBOSEN), INSR-β (EM1175, Fine Test) and serum insulin (PI602, Beyotime) were measured using ELISA kits.

Immunoprecipitation

The Flag-tagged INSR-α was transfected into HEK293T cells for 24 h and then harvested in 500 μl ice-cold IP buffer containing protease inhibitors. One-tenth of each lysate was pipetted and set aside as an input control. His-tagged INSR-α was added in the remaining lysates, incubated for 2 h at 4 °C and pulled down with anti-His (H9914, Sigma-Aldrich) or anti-Flag (M8823, Sigma-Aldrich) beads. Thereafter, the beads were washed for five times by IP buffer, and the proteins bounded to the beads were eluted by loading buffer.

CUT&RUN-qPCR assay

CUT&RUN-qPCR was performed by Hyperactive pG-MNase CUT&RUN Assay Kit for PCR/qPCR (HD101, Vazyme). In brief, 5 × 105 splenic CD8+ T cells were suspended and bound with ConA beads for 10 min and then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing, pG/MNase Enzyme was added into the sample and incubated for 1 h at 4 °C. Additionally, CaCl2 was added for DNA fragmentation lasting 90 min on ice and stopped by stop buffer at 37 °C for 15 min. To normalize the samples, Spike in was added into stop buffer as uniform correction. Subsequently, the DNA was extracted by FastPure gDNA Mini Columns for qPCR.

Immunofluorescence

Splenic CD8+ T cells were seeded into 24-well plate coated with poly-d-lysine solution (E607014, Sangon Biotech), and the plate was centrifuged at 500 g lasting 2 min for cell attachment. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with BD Perm/Wash Buffer (554723, BD Biosciences). The fixed and permeable cells were blocked in 5% BSA and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The cells were then washed and incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h. After washing, the slides were counterstained with DAPI (ab104139, Abcam) and mounted for confocal analysis.

RNAscope and immunofluorescence assay

The RNAscope probe targeting Trnp1 mRNA was designed and synthesized (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, CA). The co-staining of Trnp1 mRNA (851361, Advanced Cell Diagnostics) and CD8 protein were performed by RNA-Protein Co-Detection Ancillary Kit (323180, Advanced Cell Diagnostics)59.

Lipid droplet staining

Primary HSCs were stained with 10 μg ml−1 Nile Red reagents (HY-D0718, MedChemExpress) or 2 μM BODIPY (HY-D1614, MedChemExpress) at 4 °C overnight, and the lipids were visualized with confocal microscopy.

Liver function and lipid test

Liver function was measured by ALT, AST and alkaline phosphatase activity in serum diluted by four using FDC-7000i (FUJIFILM)60. Hepatic TC content was measured using the Tissue Cholesterol Content Assay Kit (BC1985, Solarbio), and hepatic TG content was measured using the Tissue Triglyceride Content Assay Kit (BC0625, Solarbio).

Histological analysis

H&E, oil red, Sirius red and Masson staining were performed using standard protocols61–63. Antibodies against α-SMA (GB111364, Servicebio), F4/80 (GB113373, Servicebio) and Ly6G (GB11229, Servicebio) were used for immunohistochemistry or immunofluorescence staining. Images were obtained using a light microscope (Pannoramic DESK, 3D HISTECH).

Viral transduction

For lentiviral transduction, splenic CD8+ T cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 5 × 105 cells per well precoated with anti-CD3/CD28 and cultured for 24 h. Lentiviral packaged vectors were infected at a multiplicity of infection of 20 in medium containing 100 ng ml−1 IL-2 and 10 μg ml−1 Vectofusin-1 (130-111-163, Miltenyi Biotec). Transfected cells were cultured in the medium containing IL-2 (100 ng/ml) for 2 days, and then GFP+ cells were sorted.

Flow cytometry

Cell surface staining with the antibodies was performed in FACS buffer. For TRNP1, staining was performed with 4% paraformaldehyde fixation and permeabilization with Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (eBioscience). Stained cells were analyzed by Fortessa flow cytometer and FlowJo Software (BD Bioscience).

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent and reverse transcribed to cDNA by HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR (R333, Vazyme). Real-time qPCR was performed by applying ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711, Vazyme) and LightCycler (Roche). The qPCR primers used are listed in Supplementary Table 10. Relative expression of individual genes was normalized to that of internal control using the 2−ΔΔCt method in each sample64.

Cellular fractionation

Cellular fractions of splenic CD8+ T cells were isolated using a plasma membrane protein isolation kit (SM-005, Minute), Golgi apparatus protein isolation kit (GO-037, Minute), endoplasmic reticulum protein isolation kit (ER-036, Minute) and nuclear protein isolation kit (P0027, Beyotime).

SomaScan proteomic analysis

Mouse blood was collected in tubes and centrifuged at 1,000 g for 10 min to obtain plasma. Plasma samples were sent to SomaLogic for SomaScan proteomic analysis using 7k SomaScan assay. Briefly, the 7k SomaScan panel encompassed 7,596 highly specific single-stranded modified slow off-rate modified DNA aptamers (SOMAmer) to capture and quantify plasma proteins by relative fluorescent units. SomaLogic output data were proceeded by quality control, calibration and normalization to obtain the level of each protein following the manufacturer’s protocol65. A two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t-test was used to evaluate differential proteins between groups. A P value cutoff of 0.05 was used as the significance threshold.

Endophenotype mendelian randomization

Causality inference was performed using the generalized summary-level data Mendelian randomization method66. We used TRNP1 mRNA expression quantitative trait loci summary data from GTEx and TwinsUK as the exposures67,68, protein expression quantitative trait loci summary data of seven proteins from deCODE (SomaScan, n = 35,374) and UKB (Olink, n = 46,215) as outcomes69,70 and 503 individuals of European ancestry from the 1000 Genomes Project as the linkage disequilibrium reference cohort. Generalized summary-level data Mendelian randomization was performed for all exposures for which we were able to identify two or more suitable single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Instrument SNPs were further selected with the following criteria71,72 at the threshold value of 0.05: SNP to exposure association P < 0.01, minor allele frequency > 0.005, linkage disequilibrium clumping lead SNPs only (±1 Mb, r2 < 0.05) and SNPs not removed by heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI)-outlier filtering (for the removal of SNPs with evidence of horizontal pleiotropy). We considered as significant those exposure-outcome pairs with P < 0.05.

Luciferase reporter assays

The promoter regions of mouse Furin and Ctsd (from −2,001 to −1, respectively) were cloned into pGL3 vectors as described elsewhere73,74. HEK293T cells were co-transfected with 60 ng luciferase reporter plasmid and 30 ng pRL-TK-Renilla-luciferase plasmid, together with the other plasmids indicated. After 24 h, Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) was applied to determine luciferase activity. Data were normalized by dividing firefly luciferase activity with that of Renilla luciferase.

CT image 3D reconstruction

The 3D reconstruction of CT images was performed using IQQA-portal System by EDDA Technology to evaluate spleen volume.

Western blot

Tissues and cells were lysed in cell lysis buffer (9803, Cell Signaling Technology), which is supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail. BCA Protein Assay Kit (23250, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to determine protein concentrations. Proteins were separated on SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (GE Healthcare) and incubated with antibodies in appropriate dilution. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using SuperSignal West Pico Kit (34580, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Statistics and reproducibility

Data in human cohorts were statistically analyzed by SPSS 22.0 (IBM SPSS). Continuous variables are shown as mean ± s.d., and categorical variables are described as numbers and percentages. Normally distributed continuous parameters were compared using two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t-test, and non-normally distributed continuous parameters were compared using Mann–Whitney U test. Comparison of categorical parameters was determined using two-tailed chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The relationship between two normally distributed continuous variables was evaluated by Pearson correlation coefficient assay, whereas the relationship between two non-normally distributed continuous parameters was evaluated by Spearman correlation coefficient assay. The ROC curve was generated to verify the values of genes and serum INSR-α for identifying MASLD. Two-tailed P < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Data in mouse experiments are shown as mean ± s.d. or photographs. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Comparison between two groups was analyzed using two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t-test. Comparison between more than two groups was analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s HSD test or Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. The number of animals or samples representing sample size of biological replicates in each group is indicated in the figure legends. All mouse experiments were performed independently at least three times unless specified otherwise, and similar results were obtained. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size, but the chosen sample sizes were consistent with those generally used in the field and sufficient to support the conclusions of this study. Samples were randomly assigned to experimental groups, and data collection was performed in randomized order. The investigators were blinded at the time of performing experiments (excluding western blot) and during data collection, but not during data analysis. No data were excluded from the analysis. Two-tailed P < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.
