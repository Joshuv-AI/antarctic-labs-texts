Carbon-sequestration gradient insulation composites
Authors: Arpita Sarkar, Long Zhu, Donald Petit, Abdullah Islam, Zipeng Guo, Chi Zhou, Jason N. Armstrong, Shenqiang Ren
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThe massive use of carbon-sequestration building materials promises a potential global carbon sink in decarbonizing the building industry. Renewable biogenic materials from abundant agriculture waste for building practice have been around over thousands of years. However, in addition to their flammability and moisture problems, addressing their low thermal and structural performance is also becoming indispensable and urgent when it comes to environmentally sustainable and energy-efficient buildings. Here, we report a nature-inspired biogenic gradient insulation composite with an optimized silica concentration of 30 wt %, a density of 0.246 g/cm3, and a porosity of 86%. The gradient hybrid composite exhibits a thermal conductivity of 28.2 mW m−1 K−1, which is the lowest achieved under optimal preparation conditions. It also shows a flexural modulus of 590 MPa for the aerogel-rich layer without surface modification, and it demonstrates superior fire retardancy and superhydrophobicity after surface treatment.

INTRODUCTION

Carbon-storing biogenic materials produced from abundant renewable biomass, such as annually harvested agriculture waste streams, have the promise to catalyze net-zero buildings (built by nature) and reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emission in building sectors.1–6 In this context, straw is one of the major carbon-storing agricultural crops grown around the world,7,8 and the traditional disposal of straw is by discarding it in the landfill or processing by burning, which causes an impact on the economic waste and environmental pollution.9–11 The construction practice with straw has been used for thousands of years. However, challenges associated with this wave of bio-based construction have been identified as the main materials science barrier to address, such as its low-performing thermal insulation and structural performance, flammability, and moisture/condensation issues.12–14 Moreover, the conflict between thermal insulation and mechanical strength makes it even harder in biogenic materials. In this regard, nature-inspired gradient structures show a promising way to enhance mechanical strength without hindering thermal insulation behavior due to their unique microstructure and composition gradient features.15–18 The functional gradient materials have immensely evolved in nature and are found in seashells, fish scales, bone, teeth, and plants (e.g., bamboo),19–21 which exhibit locally tailored multifunctional properties as a result of multi-axial stress states and a systematic gradual change in microstructure and composition gradience.22–24

Here we report a nature-inspired functional gradient hybrid composite material consisting of cross-linked straw-extracted cellulose fiber and silica gel networks followed by ambient pressure drying for the formation of carbon-sequestration building composites. The recycling and extraction of cellulose fiber networks from the straw is carried out through chemical pretreatment to remove lignin and hemicellulose. Silica gel has a three-dimensional (3D) skeleton structure that is cross-linked and percolated into the cellulose fiber networks. After ambient pressure drying, the cross-linked gradient composites, with low density (0.246 g/cm3) and high porosity (86%), exhibit high thermal insulation and mechanical strength, with the optimum thermal conductivity of 28.2 mW m−1 K−1, a flexural modulus of 590 MPa, and recyclability with a recovery of 92%. The as-prepared gradient composite shows promising flame retardancy (18% mass loss over a period of 360 s), while the life-cycle assessment (LCA) of the gradient biogenic composite shows a carbon footprint of 0.76 kg CO2eq/m2 @RSI-1. Along with the eco-friendly biogenic materials, a scalable manufacturing strategy of gradient materials is shown through 3D-printed additive manufacturing (AM) or roll-to-roll printing with a great potential to revolutionize carbon-storing buildings. Overall, this work represents a new scalable manufacturing method for next-generation biogenic carbon-sequestration building materials exhibiting carbon storing, flame and moisture resistance, and mechanical structural integrity.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Schematic diagram and building application

Wheat straw is composed of lignin, hemicellulose, and cellulose, and one of the major obstacles is the effective removal of lignin and hemicellulose from wheat straw while extracting and separating cellulose.25,26 The lignin holds the biomass matrix and fills the space between cellulose and hemicellulose. Moreover, the wax layer on the outer layer of wheat straw inhibits the bonding quality and makes it less accessible to cellulose.27 Therefore, chemical pretreatment is necessary to recycle and extract cellulose from wheat straw biomass. Figure 1A shows the chemical recycling pretreatment of wheat straw to extract cellulose fibers and a schematic manufacturing diagram of gradient cellulose-silica composites (G-composites) consisting of silica gel precursor-impregnated cellulose fiber networks. The cellulose and silica gel networks are cross-linked through hydrogen bonding between the silanol groups on the surface of silica gel and the hydroxyl end groups of cellulose. As a result of the density gradient and ambient pressure drying, the hybrid G-composites exhibit a systematic gradual change in the composition along its thickness direction. Figure 1B displays the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of the cross-linked composite consisting of nanoporous silica-impregnated cellulose fiber. Figure 1C summarizes the flexural modulus and thermal conductivity of G-composites as compared to other biogenic or mineral-based building materials.28–36 As observed, the G-composites prepared in our work exhibit lower thermal conductivity compared to natural materials, polymers, elastomers, and composites. Additionally, they demonstrate a superior flexural modulus when compared to flexible polymer composites. Figure 1D shows the low embodied carbon footprint of the G-composites, on which its carbon-storing nature offsets its recycling process and manufacturing carbon emissions.

Chemical pretreatment of wheat straw

Chemical pretreatment plays an important role in recycling and extracting wheat straw-derived cellulose fibers. Figure 2A shows the Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra of the hot-water-, acidified-glycerol-, sulfuric acid (H2SO4)-, and sodium hydroxide (NaOH)-treated straw fibers. The broad peak appears at 3,332 cm−1, which is assigned to the OH stretching vibrations. The intensity of these bands is noticeably higher in the case of the NaOH-pretreated straw fiber. This finding indicates that the NaOH pretreatment results in higher cellulose content than that of hot water, acidified glycerol, and H2SO4 pretreatment. The peak at 1,733 cm−1 is assigned to the hemicellulose acetyl groups, and uronic ester or ester linkage of the carboxylic group of the ferulic and p-coumaric acids of hemicellulose/lignin and a noticeable reduction in peak intensity at 1,733 cm−1 in the case of NaOH-pretreated fibers indicate the removal of lignin/hemicellulose from the wheat straw. The bands for the aromatic stretching vibration appeared in the region of 1,445–1,635 cm−1 and a series of small peaks has been observed between 1,300 and 1,000 cm−1, which signifies the dissolution of carbohydrates and fat followed by the exposure of lignin and cellulose. Moreover, the band at 892 cm−1 is assigned to the asymmetric out-of-phase ring stretch in the C1–O–C4 linkage.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD; Figure 2B) spectra provide the relative crystallinity index of different pretreated straw fibers. A set of two characteristic peaks of cellulose, located at 16.2° and 22.3°, correspond to the (101) and (002) crystalline planes of cellulose. A slightly increased peak intensity is observed in the case of NaOH-treated straw, which could be attributed to the reduction of the amorphous portion in the biomass after the pretreatment process. The crystallinity index (CI) was estimated by using the Segal equation from the XRD patterns (Figure 2C) and shows the changes in the crystallinity index using different pretreatment methods. The crystallinity indices for hot-water-, acetified-glycerol-, and H2SO4-treated fibers are 49%, 49%, and 45%, respectively, while reaching a maximum of 60% in the case of the NaOH-pretreated fibers. An increased CI indicates the removal of amorphous components such as lignin and hemicellulose, resulting in a higher proportion of cellulose fibers (Figure S3). The thermal conductivity of the pretreated straw fibers is also shown in Figure 2C. The thermal conductivity values for water-, acidified-glycerol-, and H2SO4-treated fibers are 39.6, 39.2, and 38.9 mW m−1 K−1, respectively. Notably, NaOH treated straw fibers exhibit an optimal thermal conductivity of 34.8 mW m−1 K−1. This lower thermal conductivity of NaOH-pretreated fibers suggests effective removal of hemicellulose and lignin by breaking ester bonds, leading to increased porosity and reduced thermal conductivity.

To further examine the structural changes in pretreated wheat straw, SEM is performed, and the morphological features are presented in Figures 2D–2F and S1. Figure 2D reveals that the surface of water-treated wheat straw remains intact with a plate-like structure. In contrast, in the case of acidified glycerol and acid treatment, the wheat straw surface is partially modified, showing a combination of partial porosity and plate-like structures due to the cell wall removal (Figures 2E and S1). Significantly, NaOH-treated straws (Figures 2F and S3) exhibit porous structures and high cellulose contents, indicating the breakage of the lignin-hemicellulose-cellulose network. Furthermore, in Figures S2, S3, and S4, it is evident that NaOH-treated fibers with the different processing concentrations and times display a smaller diameter when compared to that of the water, H2SO4, and acidified glycerol treatments, attributed to the dissolution of lignin and hemicellulose. Thermogravi-metric analyses of the pretreated straw fibers are shown in Figure S5, exhibiting three distinct stages. The first stage, between 100°C and 140°C, refers to the initial weight loss due to moisture evaporation, while the second stage (between 250°C and 400°C) relates to the mass weight loss caused by cellulose degradation. The onset degradation temperature of NaOH treated straw fibers (398°C) is higher than that of hot-water, H2SO4-, and acidified-glycerol-treated straw fibers due to the higher crystalline structure of NaOH-treated straw fibers. The alkaline chemical treatment plays an important role in the carbon footprint of straw-based building materials (Figure S6). In this regard, 0.5 M NaOH pretreatment of wheat straw is selected for the following studies due to the balance between carbon footprint, thermal performance, and flexural modulus (Note S1; Figures S6 and S7).

Thermal insulation and mechanical strength of G-composites

To further increase the thermal insulation and structural performance of extracted straw cellulose, we explore the cross-linked cellulose silica-gel networks for the formation of hybrid gradient G-composites after ambient pressure drying. Figure 3A shows the schematic diagram of the G-composites, showing the gradual concentration of nanoporous silica at the bottom of the composite due to gravitational forces. Moreover, SEM images (Figures 3B–3D and S8) of the cellulose-rich layer, intermediate layer, and aerogel-rich layer of gradient composites confirm the gradient distribution of nanoporous silica across the thickness direction. Furthermore, Figure S9 reveals the comparison between the thermogravimetry analysis (TGA) of different sliced layers of the gradient composites with 30 wt % silica concentration along the thickness direction. All the different layers of the composites show a major weight loss between 250°C and 380°C due to the decomposition of cellulose. Moreover, the weight percentage for the SiO2 residue is estimated to be 20%, 27%, and 40% for the cellulose-rich layer, intermediate layer, and aerogel-rich layer, respectively, which also indicates the variation of nanoporous silica concentration from top to bottom of the composites supporting the formation of gradient structure. Figure 3E shows the FTIR spectra of different sliced layers in the gradient composites along the thickness direction. Due to the increased nanoporous silica concentration in the bottom layer of the composite from the density gradience, the intensity of the −OH band at 3,339 cm−1 decreases and slightly shifts to the higher wave number, which indicates the combination of cellulose with SiO2 with the formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonding between carboxylic and silanol (asymmetric vibration of SiOH) groups. Additionally, a peak for bending vibration of the Si–OH group appeared at 960 cm−1, and a peak at 790 cm−1 for asymmetric SiO–Si vibration also indicates silica presence.

Figure 3F reveals the variation in porosity and density with respect to the wt % of nanoporous silica. The pure cellulose sample without aerogel has a porosity of 73% and density of 0.3 g/cm3. As the silica concentration increases from 10 to 30 wt %, the porosity increases from 80 to 86 wt %, while the density decreases from 0.255 to 0.246 g/cm3. Further increasing the silica wt % shows the opposite trend, with the porosity decreasing to 75 wt % and the density increasing to 0.266 g/cm3 when the silica is increased to 50 wt %. Figure 3G shows the change in thermal conductivity with increasing average silica concentration in the G-composites while exhibiting a decreasing trend from 30.2 to 28.2 mW m−1 K−1 with increasing the silica concentration from 10 to 30 wt %. However, increasing silica concentration by more than 30 wt % shows an increase in thermal conductivity due to the aggregation of particles in the composite. Figures S10 and S11 represent the SEM images of different wt % of gradient composites, indicating the particle aggregation with more than 30 wt % loading. Such aggregation can lead to inconsistent thermal insulation by creating thermal bridges and disrupting the uniformity of heat resistance across the composite, which compromises its overall insulating effectiveness. The optimum thermal conductivity of the as-prepared gradient composite was found to be 28.2 mW m−1 K−1, which is superior in comparison with many of the reported biogenic composite materials as shown in Table S1. Furthermore, thermal insulation exhibits a difference between aerogel-rich and cellulose-rich layers due to the gradient characteristics of the composite. Figure 3G represents its corresponding thermal conductivity of the G-composites, displaying a thermal conductivity difference from 1.8 to 2.5 mW m−1 K−1 between the aerogel-rich and cellulose-rich layers. The gradient characteristic of the G-composites is related to the accumulation of nanoporous silica particles in the bottom layer, and this porous silica layer exhibits lower thermal conductivity. On the other hand, the cellulose-rich top layer of G-composites shows higher thermal conductivity than that of the aerogel rich bottom layer, which signifies the gradient distribution of porous silica particles into the G-composites. The gradient structure plays an important role in mechanical stability for building applications. The flexural modulus of the pure cellulose sample is around 700 MPa. Figure 3H displays the change in flexural modulus with increasing the silica concentration in the composite. The flexural modulus shows a decreasing trend (from 660 to 590 MPa) with increasing the silica concentration from 10 to 30 wt % due to the increased porosity, and further increases to 680 MPa with the increase of silica concentration. Moreover, the aerogel-rich layer reveals a higher flexural modulus than the cellulose-rich layer of the composite (Figure 3H). A noticeable difference in flexural modulus between the aerogel-rich layer and cellulose-rich layer has been observed (80 to 150 MPa), further confirming its gradient characteristics of G-composites. We also summarize the compressive strength and flexural strength of the G-composites (Figure S12). The compressive strength of the composite for the cellulose-rich layer decreases from 8.5 to 7.5 MPa as the aerogel concentration increases from 10% to 50%. The flexural strength follows the same trend, decreasing from 20.5 to 13.4 MPa.

AM of G-composites

Extrusion-based AM is a fully automated, low-waste, and open-source technology with great potential for large-scale 3D fabrication, such as for producing building envelopes. The cross-linked straw-fiber-silica gel networks form a uniform, shear-thinning viscous fluid to facilitate the extrusion-based AM process. Figures 4A and 4B exhibit the shear-thinning rheology properties of the printable biogenic-based slurry. At a quasistatic state, it shows a high viscosity of 5.7 × 103 Pa s and decreases exponentially to 54.49 Pa s at a higher shear rate of 17.59 s−1. The shear-thinning properties enable a smooth and consistent extrusion printing process. The high storage modulus of 5.96 kPa at low shear stress supports the weight of the subsequent layer to provide reliable shape fidelity of the 3D-printed specimen. As shown in Figure 4C, various 3D structures are produced to demonstrate the 3D printing capabilities. Figure 4D shows the 3D printability for rapid-prototyping biogenic composite for building applications. The thermal conductivity of the 3D printed composite is approximately 32 mW m−1 K−1. A house model with different wall thicknesses of 5, 8, and 10 mm is printed and demonstrates its remarkable thermal insulation performance (Figure 4E). The results show that 3D-printed straw-fiber house models maintain a low temperature of 36°C when placed in a high-temperature environment of 110°C for 2 h (Figure 4E). This indicates that the biogenic hybrid composites can be effectively used for extrusion-based AM and can produce 3D building thermal insulation structures.

Building applications of G-composites

Automatic and scalable manufacturing is crucial for cost savings and large-scale production of G-composites in the construction practice. A roll-to-roll system (Figure 5A) is developed to achieve the automatic manufacturing of the G-composite panels. A controller is utilized to manage the platform’s movement in the x and y directions, while the z axis controls the extrusion. The speed can be adjusted for each axis to create G-composite samples. Figure 5B displays a picture of a prepared G-composite panel with a lateral dimension of 267 mm. When compared to traditional polystyrene insulation sheathing panel, the as-manufactured biogenic gradient G-composite panel exhibits remarkable structural integrity and high thermal insulation, as shown through both field performance measurements and thermal imaging (Figures 5C and 5D; Video S1), making it suitable for green building envelopes. Weatherability and recyclability of biogenic materials are critical for the carbon footprint of buildings. The water absorption capacity and wettability of the gradient composites are shown in the inset of Figure 5E by comparing surface hydrophobic treatment, on which the water absorption capacity of the wax-coated G-composites is seven times lower than that of the pristine composite. Furthermore, the water contact angle of the hydrophobic G-composites is 112°, indicating its hydrophobic nature (Figure 5E inset), while Figure S13 shows an SEM image of the hydrophobic gradient composite. Figure 5E displays the humidity-dependent thermal insulation using a programmable temperature and humidity chamber, while the hydrophobic and pristine gradient composites show a change in thermal conductivity of 0.5% and 6.45% under humidity level of 40%, respectively. The thermal conductivity of the wax-coated composite and boric acidtreated composite are 33.2 and 34 mW m−1 K−1, respectively. The flame retardancy of boric acid-treated G-composites is shown in Figures 5F and 5G; Video S2. The untreated composites are ignited quickly and engulfed in flames within a few seconds, while boric acid-treated gradient composites exhibit a distinctly low burning rate (Figure 5F inset). The concentration of boric acid and silica plays a significant role in controlling the flame retardancy performance of G-composites. With increasing boric acid concentration from 0 to 5 wt %, the mass losses for the 10, 20, and 30 wt % gradient composites are 56%, 38%, and 18% (Figures 5G and S14), respectively. The 30 wt % silica composites show a considerable flame-retarding effect with a mass loss of 18% over a fire exposure of 360 s (Figure 5G). Moreover, the surface morphology of the 30 wt % G-composites remains unchanged after boric acid coating, as shown in Figure S15.

Carbon footprint and reusability of G-composites

LCA is an effective tool for evaluating environmental impacts and the development of renewable and sustainable building materials. A framework of LCA is established to estimate the carbon footprint for the G-composites. Figures 6A and S16, S17, S18, S19, and S20 confirm the chemical treatment’s role on the overall carbon footprint. Figure 6A compares the carbon footprint for varying silica concentrations of the G-composites. The calculated carbon footprint values for 5, 10, 20, 30, and 40 wt % gradient G-composites are −0.32, −0.14, 0.23, 0.76, and 1.37 kg CO2eq/m2 @RSI-1, respectively (Figures S16, S17, S18, S19, S20, and S21), which are drastically lower and more eco-friendly than the industrial insulation materials (Table S3), providing a significant potential of G-composites for future sustainable building applications. In addition, the recyclability and reusability of the G-composites are shown in Figure 6B, demonstrating a recovery percentage of 92% when recycling the gradient composites for the closed-loop materials cycle construction.

In summary, we report scalable biogenic gradient structural insulation composites for green buildings. The carbon-sequestration gradient composite is manufactured by cross linking straw extracted cellulose fiber with silica gel networks, followed by ambient-pressure drying. The gradient characteristics of the hybrid composites, with the density of 0.246 g/cm3 and high porosity of 86%, lead to tailored and anisotropic thermal insulation and mechanical properties. The structural insulation gradient composites show thermal conductivity of 28.2 mW m−1 K−1, which is the lowest achieved under optimal preparation conditions, a flexural modulus of 590 MPa without surface modification, hydrophobicity and fire-retardance after surface treatment, and recyclability with a recovery of 92%. Moreover, the gradient composites show a carbon footprint of 0.76 kg CO2eq/m2 @RSI-1 according to the LCA. The carbon-storing biogenic gradient structural insulation materials hold promise for the development of next-generation green building materials.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

Materials

Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), mesitylene (1,3,5 trimethylbenzene; TMB), ammonium hydroxide, and tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Wheat straw was obtained from Dumor Straw. Glycerol, NaOH pellets, boric acid, and H2SO4 were purchased from Fischer Scientific. The hydrophobic coating was carried out using wax purchased from U.C. Coatings.

Alkaline, acid, and glycerol treatment of wheat straw fiber

Different pretreatments such as alkaline, acidified glycerol, and acid were used to pretreat wheat straw. For alkaline treatment, 20 g of wheat straw fibers were cut into small pieces and ground with 500 mL of deionized (DI) water using a blender. Then, the ground fibers were placed in a beaker and mixed with a solution of NaOH at various concentrations (0.1%5% w/w) and stirred at different temperatures (90°C, 110°C, 130°C) for a designated time (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8 h). Next, the residue was filtered, washed with distilled water until the pH became neutral, and dried at 60°C overnight. In the case of acidified-glycerol treatment, the ground wheat straw (20 g) was treated with 400 mL of acidified glycerol (78.8% glycerol, HCl 1.2% w/w) for 3 h. In a similar way, wheat straw was treated with dilute acid (H2SO4 1.84% w/w) at 100°C for 3 h. The cleaning and drying procedures were similar to the alkaline treatment.

Synthesis of nanoporous silica precursor

At first, 18.75 g of CTAB was dissolved in 1.5 L of water at 35°C. Then, 1.5 mL of ammonium hydroxide and 15 mL of mesitylene were added to it and the solution was stirred for 5h. Then, 15 mL of TEOS was added slowly and the solution was left for stirring overnight at 35°C. Next, the solution was kept in a 60°C oven for another 24 h for gelation. For the purification process, the silica gel precursor was first dialyzed in 2.5 L of DI water and homogenized using a mechanical agitator and then kept at ambient temperature for 24 h. After phase separation, the water part was drained out and this process was repeated five times by changing the solution over the course of 2 days.

Synthesis of gradient cellulose-silica composite panels

Different wt % of cellulose-silica gradient composites were synthesized by varying silica concentration into the precursor mixture. The dried NaOH treated straw fibers were first blended with 300 mL of DI water and then mixed with the required volume of silica gel using a mechanical agitator. After that, the slurry was poured into the paper-making setup, kept between metallic perforated sheets, and clamped using screws to maintain the thickness and avoid buckling. During this process, the nanoporous silica gradually concentrated toward the bottom of the composite, forming a gradient structure with nanoporous silica concentrated on the bottom surface due to gravitational forces. Finally, the as-prepared composites were kept in a preheated oven at 60°C at ambient pressure for drying. For the wax coating, a thin layer of Minwax Paste Finishing Wax (boiling point, 148°C201°C) was applied to the composite panel using an applicator pad to ensure even coverage. The coating was then dried overnight in an oven at 60°C. For the boric acid treatment, the samples were immersed in boric acid solutions of different concentrations for 5 min and then dried in an oven overnight at 60°C.

LCA

Table S2 shows the carbon footprints for the materials, chemicals, and processes used in the LCA, along with the LCA data source; a range of values were used in the calculation if the LCA data were not available. Only a portion of the total water use was included in the calculation, as the water for alkaline treatment and straw washing could be reused for many batches. Energy consumption was divided into two categories: (1) the energy use for general processing and materials handling, which was estimated from data provided by an industrial partner; and (2) the energy required for panel drying, as this is a major contribution above and beyond the typical industrial processes; the calculation uses natural gas as the fuel supply for drying. The LCA also assumes that panels do not end up in a landfill and are recycled; the potential LCA benefit of incorporating recycled material into the panels was not included in this assessment. The wax coating and boric acid treatment processes were not included in this assessment. The 3D-printed AM or roll-to-roll printing processes were also not included in this assessment.

Physical measurements

Finely ground composite samples were prepared to find the chemical bonding state and the interfacial bonding of composite synthesized at different concentrations and different layers and were examined using FTIR (FTIR-Agilent carry 560). Microstructures of the silica gel and composite structures were studied using TEM (Jeol JEM-2100 LaB6, 200 kV) and focused ion beam scanning electron microscope (FIB-SEM, Carl Zeiss AURIGA Crossbeam).

Thermtest’s heat flow meter 100 series (HFM-100), which complies with ASTM C518, was used to measure the thermal conductivity of the gradient, bi-layer, and pure fiber samples. Before each measurement, a calibration was performed on a standard sample of the NIST SRM 1450d with a thermal conductivity of 32.5 mW m−1 K−1. For measuring the thermal conductivity of the aerogel-rich layer of the composite, we placed the aerogel-rich layer close to the hot plate of the flow meter. For the thermal conductivity of the cellulose-rich layer, we placed the cellulose-rich layer of the composite close to the hot plate of the flow meter. For the samples tested in different humidity environments, each sample was first placed in each humidity environment for 5 h and then its thermal conductivity was measured immediately. All the thermal conductivity measurements were conducted at a room temperature of 20°C. The porosity of the gradient, bi-layer, and pure fiber samples is calculated from the bulk and skeletal density using Equation 1: (Equation 1) Porosity%=1-ρbρs where ρb is the bulk density, calculated from the weight-to-volume ratio of the sample, and ρs is the skeletal density, measured using a pycnometer system (Micromeritics Accu-Pyc II 1340 Gas Pycnometer). This system employs the gas displacement method to measure the volume and determine the density on a skeletal level. Flexural modulus was used as a property to study the mechanical behavior of the samples. Uniaxial flexural modulus tests of specimens with bulk dimensions of 130 × 25 × 6 mm were carried out using a universal test system (Model SSTM-20KN from United Testing Systems) for testing samples up to 500 N.

Printable ink preparation

The sodium hydroxide-treated fiber was mixed with silica gel at a ratio of 7:3 and mixed using a propeller mixer for 12 h. A dispersing agent (Darvan 811, Vanderbilt Minerals) was added to the mixture to facilitate uniform blending. The cellulose nanofiber (92% Crystallinity, Nanografi) was added to the ink to increase viscosity to facilitate extrusion-based 3D printing. The viscoelastic property of the cellulose-aerogel ink was evaluated using a MCR 72 modular compact rheometer (Anton Paar, Graz, Austria).

Extrusion-based AM process

A fused deposition modeling printer (Creality Ender-3 Pro) is reconfigured for the extrusion-based AM process. A motorized linear stage is mounted to the printer and replaced the original heating extruder. The prepared ink is loaded into a 10-cc syringe and attached to the liner stage. A helix-locked nozzle (1.0 mm, Nordson EFD) is attached to the syringe. The motion of the printing bed and extrusion is controlled by g-code path generated using open-source software (slic3r). The as-printed specimen is dried in a vacuum environment for 48 h with pressure maintained at 0.013–0.018 mBar.
