Effect of broiler litter stockpiling methods on ammonia and greenhouse gas emissions
Authors: Shruthi Koneti, Rishi Prasad, Debolina Chakraborty, Dexter B Watts, H Allen Torbert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBroiler litter (BL) has the potential to emit ammonia (NH3) and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions such as carbon dioxide (CO2), nitrous oxide (N2O), and methane (CH4), which have a greater impact on climate change. Understanding the impact of stockpiling methods on emissions is essential, as they influence the emission rates. This study focused on quantifying the emissions of N2O, CO2, CH4, and NH3 from the BL stockpiles during 126 days of storage under three stockpiling conditions a) Broiler litter stockpile covered with tarp (treatment-T), b) uncovered litter stockpile (treatment-U), c) Broiler litter stockpile covered with a 0.08 m layer of soil (treatment-S). Gas emissions were measured with an INNOVA-1512 photoacoustic gas analyzer. Daily emission rates were calculated by using the ideal gas equation, and cumulative emissions were calculated from interpolated daily emission rates. The results suggested that over the 126 days of storage period, treatment-T emitted significantly lower (P < 0.05) cumulative NH3-N (89.5 g m-2), N2O-N (47.85g m-2), CO2-C (9708.5 g m-2), and CH4-C (123.27 g m-2) emissions compared to treatments -U and -S. Covering the BL stockpiles with a tarp reduced the NH3-N, N2O-N, CO2-C, and CH4-C emissions by 38 %, 54 %, 28 %, and 19 %, respectively, compared to the control (treatment -U). In contrast, covering BL with soil (treatment-S) increased the NH3-N, N2O-N, CO2-C, and CH4-C emissions by 13 %, 17 %, 123 %, and 9 %, respectively, compared to the control (treatment-U). Overall global warming potential (GWP) expressed as CO2 equivalents (CO2-eq) was also low for treatment-T, indicating its effectiveness in mitigating emissions.Keywords: Ammonia, Greenhouse gas emissions, Stockpiling, Broiler litter, Mitigation strategy

Introduction

Climate change is of great concern across the world (Zhu et al., 2020). An increased concentration of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, such as nitrous oxide (N2O), carbon dioxide (CO2), and methane (CH4), results in global warming, a prime precursor for climate change (Houghton, 2001). Among these, N2O has a higher potential to trap heat with a global warming potential (GWP) of 298 times higher than CO2, while CH4 has 25 times CO2 ( IPCC, 2021). According to USEPA (2024), agricultural activities have accounted for 10 % of total GHG emissions in the US, with manure management contributing 14 % of the agricultural sector’s emissions. Agriculture heavily depends on synthetic fertilizers and organic manure to meet crop nutrient requirements (Cai et al., 2019). However, indiscriminate use of fertilizer and manure has led to increased GHG emissions (He et al., 2023). This rise in GHG emissions causes climate change, which in turn decreases crop productivity and poses a threat to food security. Therefore, it is important to mitigate GHG emissions from the agriculture sector, which ensures sustainability and food security (He et al., 2023). The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change has made the Paris Agreement, which was signed by 196 countries with an aim to reduce global GHG emissions (UNFCCC, 2015). As a part of this agreement, all nations need to develop plans to cut down corresponding GHG emissions (UNFCCC, 2015 ). Soil nutrient management, enteric fermentation, and manure management are the major sources of anthropogenic N2O and CH4 emissions from the agriculture sector (Xiong et al., 2022; S. Zhang et al., 2021). So, it is important to cut down the emissions from these sectors to reduce global emissions.

In the livestock sector, the broiler industry is the fastest-growing agro-based industry in the world. According to FAO (2023), broiler meat accounted for 34 % of global meat production by being the most consumed meat in 2021. The US is the leading producer and exporter of broiler products worldwide (USDA - ERS, 2023). In 2023, the US produced 9.16 billion broilers, which were valued at $42.6 billion (USDA-NASS, 2023). Within the US, the Southeastern region accounts for 41 % of the broiler industry and contributes 20 % of the broiler population in the US (USEPA, 2025). This tremendous growth in the US broiler industry has resulted in the production of 13-26 million metric tons of broiler litter (BL) annually (Amato et al., 2020). Broiler litter consists of feces, feathers, spilled feed, and bedding material (Chakraborty et al., 2021). Broiler houses in the US are cleaned out seasonally or annually based on management practices (Yao et al., 2011), and BL is usually stockpiled in the open air until applied to the farmlands prior to planting season. Even though stockpiling is widely used to store manure for later use, this practice is considered a potential source of NH3 and GHG emissions (Naylor et al., 2016) and it also releases approximately 200 chemical compounds into the atmosphere (Liu et al., 2023) posing serious environmental concerns. Broiler litter emits N2O by nitrification and denitrification processes (Zainudin et al., 2020). Methane is produced under anaerobic conditions by the action of methanogenic bacteria that use formate, acetate, carbon dioxide, and hydrogen as substrates (Liu et al., 2018; Montes et al., 2013). Besides GHG, BL also emits NH3 (Zhu et al., 2020) a potential air pollutant (Ritz et al., 2004). Uric acid present in the BL when acted upon by the microbes results in NH3 emissions (Zhao et al., 2016). Elevated levels of NH3 emissions from BL affect bird’s health by causing respiratory issues and reduces the productivity of chickens (Miles et al., 2006). In addition to having an impact on broiler production and the environment, NH3 emissions from BL reduce the N content and lower the manure quality (Sagoo et al., 2007).

Manure treatment, handling, and storage conditions can also influence the GHG and NH3 emissions (Petersen et al., 2013). Previous studies have documented the potential of GHG and NH3 emissions from different types of manure during open storage. Zhu et al. (2020) determined the emissions from different livestock manures during storage and found that broiler and layer litter storage accounted for 73 g kg-1 and 62 g kg-1 of CO2- equivalents. Kamp and Feilberg. (2024) showed that uncovered BL emitted 11.8-16.3 kg day-1 of CO2 -equivalents over 44 days. According to Shan et al. 2019, storage of pig manure as static piles for 60 days resulted in 5 g kg-1 of NH3 and 68 g kg-1 of CO2-equivalents, with N2O and CH4 being the major contributors. Thorman et al. (2007) showed that open storage of cattle and pig manure resulted in 0.22 and 0.21 kg N t-1 of cumulative N2O emissions, respectively. According to Oenema et al. (2007), storage of manure accounted for 30 % of N losses, with most emissions happening in the form of NH3 (19 %), followed by other N gases (7 %).

In order to reduce these emissions, various studies have been conducted to identify the best mitigation strategy. Naylor et al. (2016) demonstrated that covering the layer manure stockpiles has the potential to mitigate 74 % of GHG and 88 % of NH3 emissions. Hansen et al. (2006) reported that sheeting pig manure reduced the emissions of N2O (99 %), CH4 (88 %), and NH3 (12 %). Chadwick et al. (2005) showed that compacting and covering cattle manure can reduce N2O and NH3 emissions, but observed inconsistency in reduction during different storage periods. Covering the BL heaps during the storage period reduced the NH3 emissions by 45 – 90 % (Sagoo et al., 2007; Shah et al., 2014; Thorman et al., 2006) as covering reduces the contact area of manure with air and eventually decreases NH3 emissions (McCollough et al., 2022). Kamp and Feilberg. (2024) quantified both NH3 and GHG emissions from stockpiled BL by implementing covering as a mitigation strategy and reported that covering is effective in reducing NH3 (92-95 %), CH4 (25-40 %) and N2O (82-89 %) emissions. This study used micrometeorological methods to quantify the emissions over 44 days of storage. Semi-permeable membrane technology has also been found effective in reducing NH3 and GHG emissions from cattle manure (Fang et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2018). Semi-permeable membrane technology, when coupled with aeration, promotes aerobic decomposition and reduces CH4 emissions and N2O emissions by denitrification. Decomposition of organic matter releases water vapor, which in turn forms a water film under the membrane due to permeability limitation. This water film absorbs the NH3 released from the manure and reduce the emissions. Apart from sheeting, natural media such as straw, peat, and clay have also been found to be effective in reducing GHG and NH3 emissions (Knoknerienė et al., 2024; di Perta et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2018; Rodhe and Karlsson, 2002).

Although many studies have evaluated the mitigation potential of covering manure during the storage period, there is limited research focused on quantifying both NH3 and GHG emissions from BL under field storage conditions. There is a pressing need to explore efficient mitigation strategies to reduce gas emissions (Aneja et al., 2006) from manure storage. Therefore, the present study aimed at quantifying the NH3 and GHG emissions from the BL stockpiled under three different stockpiling conditions (broiler litter stockpile covered with tarp, uncovered broiler litter stockpile and broiler litter stockpile covered with 0.08 m layer of soil), in an attempt to identify the best storage technique that has the least emission potential. We hypothesize that BL stockpiling methods will alter NH3 and GHG emission potential, and covering of BL will have the greatest impact on reducing emissions and lowering the environmental risk. We tested the hypothesis by investigating three different methods of BL stockpiling.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and study design

The study was established at E.V. Smith Research Center located in Shorter, Alabama (32°26′40.6″N 85°54′43.4″ W). The BL stockpiles were constructed in September 2023 and removed in January 2024. Broiler litter was obtained from a commercial poultry house that went through a total cleanout process and was hauled to the research station the same day. The experimental field was divided into three blocks, and each block was further subdivided into three treatment plots, representing a randomized complete block design. Each treatment consisted of three replicates, resulting in nine experimental units. Broiler litter stockpiles of 1.8m in height and 3.6m in width were constructed. The three treatments consisted of (i) Broiler litter stockpile covered with a tarp (treatment-T) (Core tarps Inc., waterproof commercial polyethylene), (ii) uncovered broiler litter stockpile (treatment-U, control), (iii) Broiler litter stockpile covered with a 0.08-m layer of soil (Altavista silt loam soil) (treatment-S). The edges of the tarp were completely sealed with the help of concrete blocks and soil to avoid the exchange of air. A visual representation of the treatments is shown in Fig. 1.

The treatments in the study: a) Broiler litter stockpile covered with tarp (treatment -T), b) Uncovered broiler litter stockpile (treatment -U), c) Broiler litter stockpile covered with 0.08m layer of soil (treatment -S).

Broiler litter and soil characterization

The broiler litter used in this study was characterized by collecting samples at multiple locations of the stockpiles. Following collection, samples were thoroughly mixed in a plastic bucket to make a composite sample, packed in airtight plastic bags, and brought back to the laboratory for analysis. Samples were air-dried, ground, and passed through a 1mm sieve for further analysis. Samples were analyzed for pH using a 1:2 (litter to DI) ratio (Wolf, 2003). Moisture content was estimated by drying the BL in the oven at 110 °C for 6 hours (Hoskins et al., 2003). Total nitrogen (TN) and total carbon (TC) were analyzed by combusting the BL samples in a CNS analyzer (Vario Max, Elementar Americas). Mineral nitrogen (NH4-N and NO3-N) was determined by using a 1: 60 (litter to 2M KCl) ratio, the extract was passed through a Whatman No. 42 filter paper, and analyzed by a flow injection analyzer (FIA lab instruments Inc., Seattle, WA) (Peters et al., 2003). Total digestible elements (Al, Ca, Cu, Mg, Zn, K and P) were obtained by digesting the BL with 5ml of concentrated nitric acid and 3ml of 30 % peroxide, and the extract was analyzed using inductively coupled argon plasma spectrometry (ICP-OES; Agilent technologies, Santa Clara, CA) (Wolf et al., 2003).

The soil used to cover the BL stockpiles was procured from the nearby field and was Altavista silt loam (Fine-loamy, mixed, semiactive, thermic Aquic Hapludults). The soil was air-dried and passed through a 2mm sieve for homogenization. pH was measured by using a 1:1 ratio of soil to DI water. Mineral nitrogen was determined by extracting the soil with 2M KCl at a 1:5 (soil: 2M KCl) ratio (Keeney and Nelson, 1982) and was analyzed by an autoanalyzer as mentioned above. Total digestible elements in the soil were determined following Wolf et al. (2003).

Monitoring BL stockpile temperature, volumetric water content (VWC), rainfall, and air temperature during the study

Volumetric water content and temperature of the BL stockpiles were recorded throughout the experimental period by the SMEC 300 water scout sensors (Spectrum Technologies, Inc., accuracy: VWC 3 %, Temp ± 1°F (0.6 °C)). Two sensors were installed per stockpile at the top and middle positions and connected to the watchdog data loggers. A 3000 series watchdog wireless weather station (Spectrum Technologies, Inc.) was installed in the field for monitoring rainfall and ambient air temperature. Moisture and temperature sensors and a weather station were set to log the data at every 30-minute interval. All the recorded data was imported at biweekly intervals into specware software. The downloaded data was exported from the software into Excel for further analysis. The moisture and temperature data from sensors installed at two positions were used to calculate the daily means. The data points from each replication are used to calculate the daily mean moisture and temperature for each position, which were further averaged to represent the treatment.

Gas Measurements

Gas concentrations of NH3, N2O, CO2, and CH4 were measured using a stainless-steel dynamic flux chamber (Woodbury et al., 2006) of 0.17m in height and 0.48m in diameter connected to a photoacoustic gas analyzer (INNOVA 1512; LumaSense Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA). Gas measurements for tarp-covered BL stockpiles (Treatment-T) were done by creating a circular cutout based on the dimensions of the chamber, with zippers provided for convenient opening and closing access. For treatment-U and treatment-S, gas concentrations were monitored by placing the chamber on the surface of the BL stockpile and sealing the edges of the chamber with BL. Dynamic flux chambers were equipped with battery-operated fans that help to circulate the air inside the chamber. On each sampling date, gas concentrations were measured at 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 minutes. The concentration of NH3 and GHG was monitored simultaneously at the top and middle positions of each stockpile. Gas emissions were monitored every other day for the first 9 days following BL stockpile formation, then every two days until day 18, and subsequently at weekly intervals. Broiler litter temperature was also recorded at each gas sampling event by using a digital thermometer.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analysis was performed in the SAS (SAS Institute) 9.4 version. Before performing statistical analysis, the data set was tested for normality using PROC UNIVARIATE. Based on the residual distribution and homogeneity, the data were log-transformed to fit the normality assumptions. When reporting, the means were back-transformed. PROC GLIMMIX procedure was used to test the effect of treatments by fitting the model with a lower AIC value, followed by a comparison of means using the Tukey-Kramer test (alpha = 0.05). Treatment and days were considered as fixed effects, with replication being a random effect. The temperature and VWC sensor data were tested using a PROC GLIMMIX model. Day and treatment were considered as fixed effects, and replication as a random effect. Additionally, the day was also considered a random factor nested. The LSMESTIMATE statement was used to evaluate the specific contrast between the treatments across days. The fitted model was stored and run with PROC PLM to create interaction plots. A simple slicing was performed using the SLICE statement to evaluate the statistically significant temperature and VWC differences across the treatments on each day.

Results and discussion

Soil and BL properties

Table 1 shows the initial characteristics of the BL and soil (used as a cover for the BL stockpile) used in this study. Broiler litter had an initial pH of 7.6. At pH values >7, the equilibrium between ammonia (NH₃) and ammonium (NH₄⁺) shifts toward the un-ionized NH₃ form, thereby promoting NH₃ formation and consequently promoting volatilization losses. Additionally, BL had an initial moisture content of 27.7 %. The moisture content of BL plays a crucial role as it influences microbial activity, aeration, and temperature, which drive the emissions of gases from litter (Chen et al., 2019). The TC and TN content of BL was 192.1 g kg-1 and 25.3 g kg-1, respectively, with a major portion of N present in organic form (20.75 g kg-1). Among mineral N species, NH4-N was the dominant form. The total P in BL was 14 g kg-1. The soil used as a cover for the BL stockpiles had an acidic pH of 5.6 with a silt loam texture. The soil had a TN content of 1.3 g kg-1 and mineral N content of 0.3 g kg-1 of NH4-N and 2.1 mg kg-1 of NO3-N. Similar to BL, most of the mineral N in soil was in ammoniacal form. The soil has a very low phosphorus concentration of 0.76 g kg-1. Compared to BL, the soil had a low concentration of both macro and micronutrients (Table 1).

Broiler litter stockpile temperature, VWC, precipitation patterns, and ambient air temperatures during the study period

The temperature in the BL stockpiles of all the treatments showed a decreasing trend, with the highest temperature recorded immediately after the stockpile formation and ranged between 59 °C and 65 °C (Fig. 2a). This high temperature of the stockpile was a result of rapid decomposition following stockpile creation, which generated a large amount of heat. Initially, all treatments maintained comparable temperatures, but differences began to appear after day 20. The treatments -U and -S exhibited significantly higher (P < 0.05) temperatures than the treatment-T for most of the study duration, up to the experiment's conclusion. Lemes et al. (2023) and Sagoo et al. (2007) reported similar findings, with covered stockpiles exhibiting lower temperatures compared to uncovered stockpiles. By the end of the study, all treatments showed a decline in stockpile temperature, suggesting the slowdown of BL decomposition (Sun et al., 2018). Temperature inside the BL stockpiles influences the NH3 and GHG emissions. High temperature inside the BL stockpiles promotes ammonia volatilization (Swelum et al., 2021) and methanogenesis (Hu et al., 2018). Elsgaard et al. (2016) observed a 3.4 times increase in CH4 emissions with a 10 °C rise in temperature. Increased temperature in BL stockpiles is an indication of rapid decomposition, which in turn produces CO2 emissions.

Daily fluctuations in the a) BL stockpile temperature, b) BL volumetric water content (VWC), c) rainfall, and air temperature during 126 days of the study period for treatment-T (tarp-covered BL stockpile), treatment-U (uncovered BL stockpile), and treatment-S (soil-covered BL stockpile). * Represents significant difference across the treatments on a given day.

The mean daily ambient air temperature exhibited a downward trend from the beginning of the experiment in September to its conclusion in January, ranging between -1.9 °C and 25 °C (Fig. 2c). This change in air temperature followed a close pattern to the typical seasonal trend. Rainfall was distributed across 126 days during the study, with peak events observed on days 20 (84.3 mm), 58 (42.4 mm), 104 (93.1 mm), and 123 (52 mm) (Fig. 2c). Over the 126 days of the storage period, 447.3 mm of rainfall was recorded. Volumetric water content of the BL stockpiles showed contrasting trends across the treatments (Fig. 2b). The VWC of BL stockpiles in treatment-T remained stable within the 25–31 % range during the entire study, indicating that these stockpiles were protected from rainfall. Treatments -U and -S showed stable VWC levels immediately after stockpile formation. However, the heavy rainfall on day 20 (84.3 mm) triggered a rapid rise in VWC to 50–55 %. This pattern of increasing VWC in response to rainfall continued throughout the study, with final values reaching 70–78 % by the end. Increased VWC can directly affect GHG emissions by influencing the rate of oxygen diffusion (Zhu et al., 2013). Increased moisture content reduces the availability of oxygen and triggers the methanogenesis and denitrification process, resulting in CH4 and N2O emissions (Amon et al., 2001). Moisture content also influences the NH3 emissions by creating favorable conditions for the hydrolysis of urea (Liu et al., 2007). The decomposition of uric acid present in the BL by uricolytic bacteria (Bacillus pasteurii) in presence of water and oxygen is the primary source of NH3 production (Ritz et al ., 2004). This reaction occurs in two different steps facilitated by uricase and urease enzymes. In presence of uricase enzyme, uric acid present in BL is converted to allantoin which later transforms into glyoxylate and urea. Urea in presence of urease enzyme and water forms NH3 and CO2. However, NH3 emissions rates greatly depend on the optimal moisture range, with any increase above the optimal levels suppressing the emissions. Miles et al . (2011) reported an increase in NH3 emissions from BL within moisture range of 37.4 % -51.1 % and any increase in moisture above this limit reduced the emissions. Maliselo et al. (2016) evaluated the effect of moisture content on NH3 emissions with moisture content ranging from 20.6 % to 27 % and observed maintaining the moisture content on higher end which is 26-27 % resulted in higher emissions. Liu et al. (2007) also reported reduction in NH3 emissions at very high moisture content range.

Daily NH3 and GHG emissions from BL stockpiles

NH3 Emissions

Daily NH3-N emissions from all the treatments were significantly high initially and then showed a declining trend over 126 days period (Fig. 3a). On Day 1, emissions from the treatment -T, -U and -S were 12.3 g NH3-N m-2 day-1, 15.2 g NH3-N m-2 day-1and 8.6 g NH3-N m-2 day-1 respectively, which declined to 0.10 g NH3-N m-2day-1, 0.8 g NH3-N m-2day-1 and 0.55 g NH3-N m-2day-1 by the end of storage period. Emissions from the treatment -T tapered off from day 35, whereas the emissions from treatment -U and -S tapered late, which is after day 50 and 56. The decrease in NH3-N emissions after this period was due to frequent rainfall events, which increased the moisture content in BL above optimal levels and suppressed the emissions. There was a significant difference observed across the treatments in daily NH3-N emissions over 126 days, with treatment T emitting significantly lower (P < 0.05) emissions compared to treatments -U and -S.

Daily a) NH3-N, b) N2O-N, c) CO2-C, and d) CH4-C emissions during 126 days of the study period for treatment-T (tarp-covered BL stockpile), treatment-U (uncovered BL stockpile), and treatment-S (soil-covered BL stockpile). * Represents significant difference across the treatments on a given day.

Ammonia volatilization is influenced by several factors such as BL exposure area, temperature, and wind speed (Pardo et al., 2015; Sommer and Hutchings, 2001). The high NH3-N emissions from treatment -U and -S were due to BL exposure to the external environment and higher temperature of BL throughout the study period, which promoted NH3 volatilization. The lower emissions from the treatment-T were because of the permeability limitation of the tarp, which hindered the escape of NH3 from the system and also reduced the exposure area of the BL to the external environment(Kamp and Feilberg, 2024). Additionally, the decomposition of the BL resulted in evaporation, causing water vapor to accumulate and form a water film under the tarp that would have absorbed NH3 and reduced the emissions (Sun et al., 2018).

N2O Emissions

Daily N2O-N emission trends was evident across the three storage methods as shown in Fig. 3b. Nitrous oxide emissions from the treatment -T were significantly lower (P < 0.05) and showed a gradual decrease in emissions, with higher emissions on day 1 (5.49 g N2O-N m-2day-1) and emissions stabilized by day 31. The N2O-N emissions were significantly higher (P < 0.05) from the treatment -S and -U compared to treatment -T. Nitrous oxide emissions from the treatment -U and -S were 2.65 g N2O-N m-2day-1 and 9.19 g N2O-N m-2day-1 at the start and decreased to 1.12 g N2O-N m-2day-1 and 1.52 g N2O-N m-2day-1 by day 126 respectively. High N2O-N emissions from all treatments immediately after stockpile formation were due to ammonia oxidation with the availability of oxygen pockets in the stockpile (Zhu-Barker et al., 2017).

There was a peak in N2O-N emissions from treatment -U and -S on sampling days 22, 59, 105, and 126 coinciding with the highest rainfall events on days 20, 58, 104, and 123 (Fig. 3b). The positive correlation between N2O-N emissions and rainfall events during the storage period was also observed in other studies (Bai et al., 2020; Parker et al., 2017). There could be several reasons for this peak in N2O-N emissions with wetting of the BL. Nitrous oxide emissions occur in both aerobic and anaerobic conditions through nitrification and denitrification processes (Broucek, 2018). Under aerobic conditions ammonium present in the BL is converted to nitrite and then to nitrate. This process is called nitrification. Conversion of ammonium to nitrite is driven by ammonia-oxidizing bacteria such as Nitrosomonas, Nitrosococcus, and Nitrospira, while nitrite is converted to nitrate in the presence of nitrite-oxidizing bacteria such as Nitrobacter(Thakur and Medhi, 2019). Denitrification occurs in anerobic conditions where nitrate is converted to nitrogen in the presence of denitrifying bacteria species (Pseudomonas, Paracoccus, Agrobacterium, Acinetobacter, and Bacillus licheniformis)(Thakur and Medhi, 2019). In both processes, N2O is released as an intermediate product (VanderZaag et al., 2011). The sudden wetting after a dry period could cause rapid denitrification and result in a burst in N2O production (Prieme and Christensen, 2001). Denitrifying bacteria can survive in extreme temperatures and prolonged dry conditions and become active with the addition of moisture (Davidson, 1992). Rainfall events during the storage period replaced the air-filled pore spaces in the BL with water and triggered the anaerobic conditions in the stockpiles, which resulted in denitrification and higher N2O-N emissions(Shan et al., 2019; Aguilar et al., 2014). Significantly higher (P < 0.05) N2O-N emissions from the treatment-S compared to the treatment-U were due to the soil cover that can hold the water for a longer duration and maintain anaerobic conditions for an extended period. Watts et al. (2015) reported an increase in the N2O emissions from poultry litter amended soil with the increase in moisture from rainfall events. This relation was also found in previous studies that examined the N2O emissions from the soils amended with manure after rainfall events(H. Zhang et al., 2021; Parker et al., 2017; Krol et al., 2015; Watts et al., 2015). Rainfall events increase soil moisture and trigger organic matter decomposition, which could promote the denitrification process by providing the nitrogen and carbon as substrates(Chen et al., 2013). It was also noted that a sudden increase in N2O emissions under wet conditions could be the result of microbial action and release of soil adsorbed N2O (Prieme and Christensen, 2001; Parker et al., 2017).

CO2 emissions

Daily CO2-C emissions differed across the three storage methods as shown in Fig. 3c. The CO2-C emissions from all treatments showed an increasing trend at the start of the storage period with emissions peaked on day 28 with treatment -T emitting 173.07 g CO2-C m-2day-1, treatment -U with 276.70 g CO2-C m-2day-1 and treatment -S with 539.63 g CO2-C m-2day-1 indicating greater microbial activity. After this, CO2-C emissions from all treatments showed a decreasing trend, with emissions dropping to 15.01 g CO2-C m-2day-1, 68.25 g CO2-C m-2day-1, and 145.86 g CO2-C m-2day-1 for treatment -T, -U, and -S, respectively, by day 126. In all the treatments, high CO2-C emissions occurred in the first 30 days of storage. Emissions from the three storage methods were significantly different (P < 0.05), with treatment -S emitting significantly high (P < 0.05) CO2-C emissions, followed by treatments -U and -T. CO2 emissions were mainly influenced by microbial activity(Sánchez-García et al., 2015). Microbial activity is further influenced by the availability of organic carbon content, as it acts as a source and positively correlates with CO2 emissions (Ren et al., 2017). The addition of soil as a layer on top of BL stockpiles enhanced the favorable conditions for microbes, leading to higher CO2 emissions. Similar results were found in a previous study by Chen et al. (2018), with higher rates of clay addition to chicken litter enhancing CO2 emissions. Apart from this, there are many studies that reported an increase in CO2 emissions when amending the soils with manure. A study conducted by Ray et al. (2020) reported the highest CO2 emissions from the soils amended with higher rates of chicken litter, as the chicken litter addition promoted microbial activity due to high P and K content. According to Abbas et al. (2012), soil treated with chicken manure released twice the amount of CO2 compared to soil amended with compost.

CH4 emissions

The daily CH4-C emissions from all the treatments fluctuated over 126 days of storage time (Fig. 3d). There was a significant difference (P < 0.05) in the CH4-C emissions across all the treatments over storage time. Emissions from all the treatments were low initially and started to increase 22 days after the stockpile construction. This finding is in agreement with other studies such as Zhu-Barker et al., (2017) and Andersen et al., (2010). After day 22, CH4-C emissions from all treatments did not show a clear trend, and emissions tapered off at 0.41 g CH4-C m-2day-1, 1.58 g CH4-C m-2day-1, and 0.79 g CH4-C m-2day-1 by day 126 for treatments -T, -U, and -S, respectively. Methane is strictly produced under anaerobic conditions by the action of methanogens (Meslé et al., 2013). So, the delayed start in the CH4-C emission could be because of the availability of oxygen in the air pockets created during the construction of BL stockpiles, which provided oxygen and maintained the aerobic conditions. The sudden increase in CH4-C emissions from the treatment -U and -S from day 22 coincided with the rainfall event that occurred on day 20, which created the anaerobic conditions in BL stockpiles by replacing the air pores with water. Higher emissions of CH4-C from treatment -T after day 22 were due to buildup of anaerobic conditions under the tarp over the storage time (Chadwick et al., 2011).

Even though BL under treatment -T was in an airtight condition, emissions were low compared to treatment -U and -S. This decrease in CH4-C emissions in treatment -T compared to treatment -U and -S was due to the low internal stockpile temperature and rainfall events during the study period. Temperature plays a crucial role in the CH4-C emissions, with lower temperatures reducing emissions(Kamp and Feilberg, 2024; Hansen et al., 2006). Husted, (1994) found CH4 emissions were reduced by 2.7 to 10.3 times when the pile temperature dropped by 10 °C. Hansen et al. (2006) reported that covering the piles emits less CH4-C emissions compared to conventional storage. Additionally, treatment T was also protected from the rainfall events during the study period. The higher CH4-C emissions from treatment -U and -S treatments were due to rainfall events received during the study period, which promoted anaerobic conditions. Cardoso et al. (2019)reported CH4 emissions from the beef cattle excreta were higher during wet seasons compared to dry seasons. Similarly, Mazzetto et al. (2014) also noticed the peak in CH4 emissions correlating with rainfall events during the study period.

Cumulative NH3 and GHG emissions

Cumulative emissions from all the treatments differed from each other over 126 days of storage period (Fig. 4). Percent change in the NH3 and GHG emissions during the storage period compared to the control (treatment -U) were shown in Table 2. Cumulative NH3-N emissions across all three treatments showed a significant difference (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4a). The treatment -U and -S showed significantly higher (P < 0.05) cumulative NH3-N emissions compared to treatment -T. During 126 days, treatment -U produced 144.3 g NH3-N m-2, treatment -S was 163.3 g NH3-N m-2 and treatment -T emitted significantly lower (P < 0.05) emissions at 89.5 g NH3-N m-2. The treatment-T showed a higher percent reduction by 38 %, whereas treatment-S increased the emissions by 13 % compared to the control. Ammonia volatilization is susceptible to temperature and area of BL exposed, which promoted emissions in treatment -U and -S. The reduction in cumulative NH3-N emissions from the treatment-T was due to the permeability limitation of the tarp. Sun et al. (2018) reported permeability of a membrane impacts the NH3 emissions as it does not allow the water vapor released from the decomposition to escape the system and forms a water film. The NH3-N emissions, when they come in contact with water, form NH4+-N and reflux it back to the stockpile and reduce the emissions.

Cumulative N2O-N emissions from three treatments were shown in (Fig. 4b). There was a significant difference (P < 0.05) in cumulative N2O-N emission during the storage period with treatment -T showing significantly lower (P < 0.05) emissions at 47.8 g N2O-N m-2 compared to treatment -U and -S at 104.2 g N2O-N m-2 and 122.5 g N2O-N m-2. The treatment-T resulted in a 54 % reduction in N2O-N emissions, while treatment-S enhanced emissions by 17 % compared to the control. Regarding the N2O-N emissions, environmental factors such as rainfall played a major role in promoting emissions. The significantly higher cumulative emissions from the treatment -U and -S compared to treatment-T were due to the rainfall events received during the study period, which created alternate wet and dry periods and triggered the nitrification and denitrification processes and promoted N2O-N emissions.

Cumulative CO2-C emissions from all treatments differed significantly (P < 0.05) with treatment -T producing 9708.5 g CO2-C m-2, treatment -U producing 13245.6 g CO2-C m-2 and treatment -S producing 29511.6 g CO2-C m-2 (Fig. 4c). Among the three treatments, treatment -T showed the highest reduction potential by 28 % compared to control but treatment -S increased the emissions by 123 %. Higher CO2 emissions from treatment-S were due to the additional carbon source provided by the soil layer for microbes. Additionally, the soil layer also provides a cooling effect to create favorable conditions for diverse microbes to decompose the BL apart from thermophilic bacteria. There was a significant (P < 0.05) difference in the cumulative CH4-C emissions across the three treatments (Fig. 4d). Over 126 days of BL litter storage, treatment-T emitted 123.2 g CH4-C m-2, treatment-U emitted 152.3 g CH4-C m-2, and treatment-S emitted 166 g CH4-C m-2. Treatment -T reduced the emissions by 19 % compared to the control. In contrast, treatment S increased the cumulative CH4-C emissions by 9 % compared to the control. Similar to N2O-N emissions, anerobic conditions created by rainfall events greatly influenced the CH4-C emissions from treatment -U and -S. Rainfall events during the study period have created anerobic conditions and promoted the CH4-C emissions. Alongside, temperature also played a major role, with significantly higher (P < 0.05) temperature inside the BL stockpiles of treatment-U and -S increased the CH4-C emissions compared to treatment-T. This reduction in NH3 and GHG emissions by covering was evident from previous studies (Kamp and Feilberg, 2024; Lemes et al., 2023; Soto-Herranz et al., 2021; Fang et al., 2021; di Perta et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2018; Naylor et al., 2016; Guarino et al., 2006; Chadwick, 2005).

Global warming potential

Cumulative emissions of N2O and CH4 were converted to CO2-equivalents, and the total GWP of each treatment and the percentage contribution of individual gases to the total GWP were calculated, as shown in Table 3. Over 126 days of storage, treatment -S resulted in the highest total GWP (172829) followed by treatment -U (103466) and treatment -T (62703). The total GWP for the three treatments over 126 days of storage can be ranked as S > U > T. The percentage contribution of each gas to the total GWP across the three treatments showed variability. Under treatment -T, among the three gases (N2O, CO2 and CH4) CO2 made the highest percentage contribution (57 %) to the total GWP followed by N2O (36 %) and CH4 being the least with 7 %. For treatment -U, both N2O (47 %) and CO2 (47 %) have contributed equally to the total GWP, while CH4 contributed the least with 5 %. In treatment-S, the CO2 made highest contribution to the total GWP by 63 %, with N2O and CH4 at 34 % and 3 % respectively. Comparing the percentage contribution of each individual gas to the total GWP, methane was found to be the minor contributor to the total GWP across the three treatments during the storage of BL. Based on the total GWP and percent contribution of each gas to the total GWP, treatment-T has proven to be effective in mitigating emissions compared to treatment-U and treatment-S. The results suggested that to reduce the GWP and its associated environmental concerns, stockpiling of BL in uncovered conditions should be minimized. Even the addition of a natural medium, such as soil, was not the best mitigation strategy. With the ongoing global efforts to reduce GHG emissions and global warming from the animal agriculture sector, it is important to impose strict regulations and policies for better storage and management of manure. The results from this study suggests that further research should focus on mitigating the N2O and CO2 emissions during the BL storage as they have contributed majorly to the total GWP.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the NH3 and GHG emissions from the BL under different stockpiling methods. Covering BL with a tarp (treatment-T) reduced the NH3-N, N2O-N, CO2-C, and CH4-C emissions by 38 %, 54 %, 28 %, and 19 %, respectively, compared to uncovered BL (treatment-U). Covering the BL with soil (treatment-S) increased the NH3-N emissions by 13 %, N2O-N emissions by 17 %, CO2-C emissions by 123 %, and CH4-C emissions by 9 % compared to uncovered (treatment-U). During 126 days of BL storage, treatments -U and -S had significantly greater contributions to the GWP compared to treatment-T. The results from this study suggest that covering the BL stockpiles with a tarp during the storage period is an efficient strategy to mitigate NH3 and GHG emissions. Implementation of this practice by farmers has the potential to substantially reduce NH3 and GHG emissions in the environment.
