Management of Supraclavicular Nerve Transection With Regenerative Peripheral Nerve Interface During Neurogenic Thoracic Outlet Decompression
Authors: Samantha Cervantes Valadez, Alexa de la Fuente Hagopian, Anthony Echo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Chronic pain is a known complication following neurogenic thoracic outlet syndrome (nTOS) procedures, often resulting from supraclavicular nerve (SCN) injury and neuroma formation. Despite its clinical significance, there is limited literature on techniques to address the SCN in nTOS surgery. This study evaluates the use of regenerative peripheral nerve interface (RPNI) to mitigate neuropathic pain and reduce the risk of symptomatic neuroma formation when the SCN is divided during nTOS surgery.Methods:A retrospective review was conducted of 12 patients who underwent supraclavicular nTOS surgery with RPNI of the SCN between 2021 and 2024. The SCN and its branches were coapted to an autologous free muscle graft, secured within its epimysium, and folded to complete the RPNI. The omohyoid was used as the donor muscle in most cases.Results:A total of 12 patients (median age 32 y) underwent SCN RPNI at the time of nTOS decompression. The omohyoid was used in 7 cases, the scalene in 3, the trapezius in 1, and the sternocleidomastoid in 1. At follow-up (mean 11 mo), most patients reported improvement in nTOS-related symptoms. Five patients, all with prior nTOS surgery, reported persistent discomfort reflecting ongoing nTOS rather than new focal supraclavicular neuropathic pain. No clinical symptoms characteristic of supraclavicular neuromas were observed at short-term follow-up.Conclusions:RPNI of the SCN is a feasible adjunct during supraclavicular nTOS when the nerve must be divided, with no clinically evident neuromas observed at short-term follow-up. Larger controlled studies with standardized outcome measures are needed to clarify its independent impact.

Takeaways

Question: Can regenerative peripheral nerve interface (RPNI) of the supraclavicular nerve be feasibly incorporated as an adjunct during neurogenic thoracic outlet syndrome (nTOS) decompression when nerve branches require transection?

Findings: In a retrospective review of 12 nTOS patients who received RPNI during supraclavicular decompression, the technique was successfully performed using multiple donor muscles. No patients developed a clinically evident supraclavicular neuroma at short-term follow-up. Persistent discomfort was observed only in revision cases and was attributed to ongoing nTOS.

Meaning: Supraclavicular nerve RPNI is a technically feasible adjunct during nTOS decompression; however, efficacy cannot be established without larger controlled studies.

INTRODUCTION

Neurogenic thoracic outlet syndrome (nTOS) results from compression or irritation of the brachial plexus as it traverses the space between the clavicle and the first rib.1,2 The most common symptoms are pain, paresthesia, numbness, and weakness of the affected upper extremity. Surgical decompression, typically involving first rib resection and scalenectomy, is often indicated for refractory cases.3 Injury to the supraclavicular nerve (SCN), a purely sensory branch that innervates the clavicular and upper chest region, is a common complication during nTOS surgery. This can result in neuroma formation and chronic neuropathic pain.4,5

Targeted muscle reinnervation (TMR) has recently been explored as a technique to prevent neuroma formation and reduce chronic pain associated with SCN injury during nTOS procedures.5 This approach demonstrated high feasibility and was associated with reduced postoperative pain, proving to be a promising intervention for chronic pain and neuroma prevention in patients undergoing surgical treatment for nTOS. Given the impact of chronic pain on a patient’s quality of life, it is important to consider these possible outcomes during nTOS management, implementing surgical techniques that aid in their prevention.6,7

The regenerative peripheral nerve interface (RPNI) procedure has been used in postamputation patients to reduce the risk of symptomatic neuroma formation and aid with neuropathic pain by coapting the transected nerve end to a free muscle graft, which provides a physiological target for regenerating axons.8–10 Unlike TMR, RPNI does not rely on the presence of motor nerve branches, expanding its applicability across various clinical scenarios.11

To our knowledge, only 1 prior pilot study has described the use of RPNI in supraclavicular surgery, demonstrating technical feasibility by using the omohyoid muscle as the nerve implantation target.12 Building on this foundation, our study expands the application by using multiple donor muscles and evaluating postoperative clinical outcomes such as neuropathic pain reduction and neuroma prevention in patients undergoing nTOS surgery.

METHODOLOGY

A retrospective review was conducted of all patients who underwent surgical decompression for nTOS by a single surgeon between 2016 and 2024. Among these, patients who underwent a supraclavicular approach with concomitant SCN RPNI were included in the study (n = 13). One patient was excluded due to loss of postoperative follow-up. This study was approved by the institutional review board at Houston Methodist. Data were extracted from the electronic medical records in accordance with institutional protocols. Collected variables included patient demographics, relevant medical history, and surgical details. Preoperative evaluation data encompassed clinical history, diagnostic studies, and clinical examination findings. The last follow-up appointment, surgical outcomes, and complications were also recorded. The diagnosis of nTOS was established based on clinical history and physical examination findings consistent with nTOS, with supportive diagnostic studies such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (with or without contrast), MRI neurography, and/or electromyography.

Refractory nTOS was defined as persistent symptoms despite prior nonoperative management, most commonly including physical therapy and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medications. The duration of conservative treatment varied, as many patients underwent initial management before referral.

With the patient in the supine position, a supraclavicular incision was made along the natural skin crease, extending from the lateral border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle toward the trapezius (Fig. 1). Dissection proceeded through the skin and subcutaneous tissue until the platysma muscle was exposed and divided to reveal the supraclavicular fat pad.

Surgical positioning and planned supraclavicular incision.

Dissection continued through the fat pad to identify the SCN and its branches. Branches outside the central operative field were preserved with retraction, whereas those overlying the middle portion were divided to optimize exposure. RPNI of the SCN was performed only in cases requiring division of SCN branches for adequate brachial plexus exposure. The external jugular vein was preserved or ligated as needed. A nerve stimulator was used to assist in identifying nerve branches and confirming signal integrity throughout the procedure.

The omohyoid muscle was identified within the supraclavicular fat pad and divided at its tendinous portion to facilitate mobilization of the muscle belly (Fig. 2). Neurolysis and decompression of the brachial plexus, including the upper trunk and suprascapular nerve, were performed (Fig. 3). Neurolysis was extended along the brachial plexus, including careful dissection of the suprascapular, phrenic, and thoracodorsal nerves. The anterior and middle scalene muscles were divided as needed to improve visualization and access. In cases where indicated, a segment of the first rib overlying the brachial plexus was resected to complete the thoracic outlet decompression.

Identification of the omohyoid muscle and lateral branch of the SCN.

SCN branches after dissection and division.

A muscle graft was then harvested from the omohyoid, scalene, trapezius, or sternocleidomastoid muscles, depending on exposure and availability. The divided branches of the SCN underwent RPNI (Fig. 4). Each nerve end was coapted to the epimysium of a free muscle graft using 6.0 Prolene epineural sutures. The muscle grafts were folded to envelop the nerve ends and positioned tension-free in the supraclavicular fat pad in a deep plane away from the surface to allow denervation and minimize neuroma formation.

Creation of the RPNI.

No alternative nerve management techniques, such as split-nerve approaches or end-to-end nerve anastomosis, were used in this cohort. A 10-French Blake drain was placed in the wound. The supraclavicular fat pad and platysma were reapproximated with 3-0 Vicryl sutures, and skin closure was performed with 4-0 Monocryl.

Selection of the target muscle for RPNI was based on intraoperative anatomy, muscle availability, and tissue quality. When present and of adequate size, the omohyoid muscle was preferentially used given its proximity to the SCN and minimal donor morbidity. If the omohyoid was not suitable or unavailable, residual scalene muscle was used, provided it was of sufficient quality and not compromised by prior resection or thermal injury. In revision cases where the omohyoid and scalene muscles had been previously divided or were unsuitable, alternative donor muscles such as the trapezius or sternocleidomastoid were selected. These decisions were made intraoperatively to allow tension-free coaptation to healthy muscle tissue.

Patients were evaluated at routine follow-up visits using focused history and physical examination. Assessment included examination of the supraclavicular region for localized pain, dysesthesia, hypersensitivity, focal tenderness, or Tinel sign along the SCN distribution, which may suggest symptomatic neuroma following sensory nerve transection, and is distinct from the radiating upper extremity pain and paresthesias characteristic of nTOS. Imaging was not routinely obtained if no symptoms were present.

RESULTS

A total of 12 patients (11 female, 1 male) underwent supraclavicular nTOS surgery with concomitant RPNI of the SCN by a single surgeon between 2021 and 2024. Patient age at the time of surgery ranged from 14 to 62 years, with a mean age of 31.6 years. Six patients presented for revision surgery after failure of prior brachial plexus decompression; 3 had a traumatic etiology, 2 were idiopathic without a clear precipitating cause, and 1 had radiation-induced bilateral brachial plexopathy (Table 1).

All patients presented with subjective symptoms consistent with nTOS, most commonly including pain, paresthesia, or burning sensation in the supraclavicular or upper extremity distribution. The duration of symptoms before surgery ranged from 7 months to 12 years, with a mean of 4.5 years. All patients had at least 1 positive provocative test on physical examination (eg, Tinel sign or Roos test) and underwent further diagnostic evaluation with at least 1 modality such as electromyography, MRI, or magnetic resonance neurography.

The mean follow-up period was 11 months, with a median of 6 months. During routine postoperative follow-up, no patients developed new focal supraclavicular neuropathic symptoms suggestive of SCN injury or neuroma formation. In addition, all patients reported subjective improvement in pain and paresthesia, along with gains in upper extremity function and strength following nTOS decompression. Four patients reported some degree of mild or intermittent pain or discomfort; however, all 4 had previously undergone TOS surgery without significant relief before intervention with RPNI. In each of these cases, patients described a reduction in symptom burden compared with their preoperative baseline. No clinically evident neuroma formation was observed in any patient at short-term follow-up.

The omohyoid muscle was used as the donor site in 7 cases, the anterior scalene in 3, the trapezius in 1, and the sternocleidomastoid in 1. There were no complications related to the RPNI technique.

DISCUSSION

Neuroma formation and chronic pain are among the most frequent complications following nTOS surgery and have been reported in up to 5% to 10% of patients in some studies.13–15 One of the proposed causes is injury to the SCN. The nerve originates from C3 and C4 nerve roots and divides into medial, intermediate, and lateral branches at the thoracic outlet triangle.16,17 These branches course superficially to innervate the skin over the clavicle, upper chest, and shoulder region.18 When they run laterally, they can typically be preserved by gentle retraction. However, when they are positioned medially, they may obstruct surgical anatomy exposure and must be transected to be able to successfully decompress the brachial plexus. Because these are sensory branches, their injury can lead to painful neuroma formation and chronic pain.4,5,19

Performing a regenerative procedure at the time of nerve transection may help decrease the risk. TMR has shown benefit in preventing neuroma-related pain by redirecting axonal growth into a motor target.20 However, TMR requires the presence of an accessible and suitable motor nerve branch, which is not always possible.20,21 In revision cases, or when the anatomy is altered or scarred, a motor branch may be damaged or too small to allow for safe coaptation.

RPNI involves coapting the transected nerve to a free muscle graft, giving the regenerating axons a physiological target and helping to prevent disorganized growth and neuroma formation.22 Because it does not rely on a motor branch, RPNI expands the range of patients who may benefit from regenerative strategies. Although SCN-specific data remain limited, the rationale for this approach is supported by a broader body of literature demonstrating the effectiveness of regenerative peripheral nerve strategies—including RPNI and TMR—in reducing symptomatic neuroma-related pain following sensory nerve transection in both amputation and nonamputation settings.

Management of nTOS remains complex. There is wide variability in diagnostic criteria, treatment approaches, and expected outcomes across institutions. Surgery itself can be technically demanding, and even when decompression is successful, some patients may continue to experience pain.23 For this reason, exploring strategies to address sensory nerve injury at the time of surgery becomes increasingly important.

Adding RPNI to the procedure did not meaningfully increase operative time or perioperative risk, and the technique was feasible and safe. At short-term follow-up, no patients demonstrated clinically evident supraclavicular neuropathic pain or findings suggestive of SCN neuroma following nerve branch division. Although some patients—primarily revision cases—reported persistent postoperative discomfort, this reflected ongoing nTOS-related symptoms rather than new focal supraclavicular sensory morbidity. Given the descriptive nature of this study, no comparative conclusions can be drawn.

This study is limited by its retrospective design, small sample size, and lack of a control group. The inclusion of both primary and revision cases, variable symptom duration, and heterogeneous etiologies reflects real-world nTOS practice and is presented descriptively rather than for comparative outcome analysis. Future studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up will be helpful to better understand the long-term impact of RPNI in the treatment of nTOS and to identify which patients are most likely to benefit.

CONCLUSIONS

nTOS remains a surgically complex condition with variable outcomes and a meaningful risk of sensory morbidity when SCN branches must be divided. RPNI of the SCN is a technically feasible adjunct that can be incorporated into the decompression procedure without meaningful added operative time or perioperative risk. In this small descriptive series, no patients developed clinically evident supraclavicular neuroma at short-term follow-up. Larger prospective studies with longer follow-up and comparative designs are needed to determine whether RPNI meaningfully reduces neuroma-related morbidity in this setting.
