DNA Methylation–Based Epigenetic Clocks and Cardiovascular Disease
Authors: Yi Li, Jiantao Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: biomarkers, cancer survivorship, epidemiology, epigenetics, prevention, treatment

Growing advances in high-throughput technologies are driving a paradigm shift in biomedical research. DNA methylation (DNAm), an epigenetic modification entailing the addition of a methyl group to the genome, plays a crucial role in regulating gene expression.1 Genome-wide DNAm assessment is enhancing our understanding of the mechanisms underlying aging and diseases.2,3 Multiple algorithms have been developed to derive DNAm-based epigenetic clocks on the basis of a set of selected CpGs (ie, DNAm sites). Most clocks are highly correlated with chronological age. Epigenetic age acceleration (EAA), the difference between DNAm clocks and chronological age, is often used to reflect an individual’s biological age. Prior studies have demonstrated the association between EAA and cardiovascular disease (CVD) and mortality.3,4

CVD remains the leading cause of death in the United States and worldwide. Among cancer survivors, the risk for CVD is elevated because of the cardiotoxic effects of cancer treatments.5 A better understanding of the underlying mechanisms connecting cancer treatments to CVD can inform improvements in therapeutic and behavioral interventions; however, such mechanisms remain poorly understood. In a study reported in this issue of JACC: CardioOncology, Meng et al6 used EAA to investigate pathways linking cancer treatments (body region–specific radiotherapy and chemotherapeutic agents) to cardiometabolic risk factors (obesity, hypertension, and abnormal glucose metabolism) and CVD (cardiomyopathy and myocardial infarction) in survivors of childhood cancer. The investigators analyzed 2,939 cancer survivors of the St. Jude Lifetime cohort who received radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Using DNAm profiling from Illumina EPIC version 1 BeadChip array, the investigators calculated 3 established DNAm clocks: DunedinPACE, PCPhenoAge, and GrimAge2. The mediation analysis suggests that EAA may partially mediate the association between various cancer treatments and CVD outcomes, with the proportion of mediation ranging from 6.0% to 35.4%. The largest mediation was observed for DunedinPACE in relation to abdominal radiotherapy and abnormal glucose metabolism. To further validate the primary findings, the investigators conducted a series of secondary and sensitivity analyses, including restricting the study sample to survivors ≥18 years of age at the time of DNAm profiling or those with an attained age above the median of the cohort participants. Overall, this study is critical, as it highlights the clinical importance of integrating DNAm data in risk assessment among cancer survivors.

The findings of this study support the hypothesis that interventions aiming to slow epigenetic accelerated aging offers opportunities to reduce CVD risk among childhood cancer survivors. Several DNAm-based biomarkers and drugs have received U.S. Food and Drug Administration approval for disease detection, monitoring, or treatment.7,8 Clinical application of these biomarkers and drugs is still limited and occurs primarily in the field of oncology. One of the key knowledge gaps limiting the translation of these biomarkers and drugs for CVD is the lack of distinction between DNAm loci that causally contribute to a disease vs those that are the consequence of that disease. Although this study provides some evidence, its design limits the ability to draw conclusions about the causal relationship.

The DNAm clocks used in this study were developed using data from population cohort studies. They are widely used as aging biomarkers. In general, these biomarkers can predict mortality and morbidity in a population, but their prediction capacity at an individual level has not been robustly demonstrated.9 In addition, DNAm clocks are generally calculated with a supervised machine learning approaches such as elastic net regression to select a parsimonious set of CpGs. Specific disease outcomes are often not used to select CpGs in the model training phase; therefore, DNAm clocks may not be disease specific, which may limit their predictive capacity for diseases of interest. Furthermore, the biological functions of the selected CpGs for DNAm clocks are largely unknown. To provide mechanistic insights, additional bioinformatic analyses and experiments are needed.

Differential DNAm at a number of CpGs has been associated with cardiometabolic disease phenotypes. However, despite this evidence highlighting the importance of DNAm in disease monitoring and the development of CVD, most are cross-sectional (ie, based on a single DNAm measurement). Therefore, it is not surprising that a number of disease-associated CpGs have been found to be a consequence of the phenotypes studied and are not causal of disease10 and so cannot be directly used as preventive or therapeutic targets. Establishing a temporal relationship will provide more robust evidence for causal inferences. As pointed out by the investigators, incorporating longitudinal data with repeated DNAm measurements will further facilitate causal mediation analysis.

In large-scale human studies, DNAm marks are often measured using blood samples because of the minimal invasiveness, accessibility, and the critical role of blood-derived DNAm in crosstalk with other tissues. Cellular mechanisms vary in different types of cells and tissues. Cell type–specific or tissue-specific DNAm assessment is important to understand cellular heterogeneity, with important implications in the development of diseases. However, collecting such samples from less accessible tissues is clearly challenging. In the near future, in silico decomposition approaches and single-cell DNAm assessments may be used to improve interpretation of DNAm data.

The samples in the study were derived from participants of the St. Jude Lifetime cohort. The rich prospective medical assessments and longitudinal follow-up provide opportunities to refine our understanding of the effects of cancer treatment on the health of cancer survivors. Nonetheless, cancer treatments were determined on the basis of a combination of multiple factors, such as the stage of the cancer and patients’ characteristics. These treatment decision may have some impact on the findings. For example, in this study, the investigators compared EAA in patients who received specific treatment with EAA in those without such exposures. Patients who received such treatment may be different from those who did not receive the treatment; therefore, selection bias may be introduced, potentially limiting the interpretation.

Despite these challenges in translating DNAm-based EAA into clinical practice, ongoing research remains crucial. In this context, this study conducted by Meng et al6 represents a valuable contribution toward reducing CVD among cancer survivors.
