Comprehensive analysis of gp120 glycosylation to explore differences between transmitter/founder and chronic HIV-1 isolates
Authors: Stephen Temesy, Jordan Haly, Yingxue Sun, Mukti Patel, Najwa Zebian, Repon Saha, Sashini Loku Galappaththi, Yiying Zhang, Nick Twells, Lara K Mahal, Eric J Arts, Carole Creuzenet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe extraordinary genetic diversity of HIV variants and their differences in glycosylation of surface protein gp120 have hindered developing a universal vaccine. Via lectin-mediated interactions, the gp120 glycans may trap viruses at the mucosal interface, enhance trans-infection of CD4+ T cells, or enhance HIV uptake for antigen presentation while limiting such presentation by inhibiting gp120 proteolysis. Also, variations in numbers and location of glycosylation sites may allow escape from emerging neutralizing antibodies. Thus, specific gp120 glycans may be critical for transmission and immune control. As most transmission events arise from a single transmitted founder virus (T/F), we surmise that a unique glycosylation signature in T/F viruses supports optimal transmission and could be targeted for vaccination. By combining mass spectrometry, computational analyses, structural modeling and lectin microarrays, this study provides a comprehensive, quantitative and site-specific analysis of gp120 N-glycosylation and its functional impact. The 24 and 27 N-linked glycosylation sites of gp120 from a T/F and a chronic HIV-1 isolate were interrogated for 55 glycoforms representing glycosylation steps across the endoplasmic reticulum and the Golgi. We showed that gp120 is more glycosylated in T/F virions than in chronic virions, both having unique glycosylation hot spots that influence lectin binding. These site-specific glycosylation differences may correlate with differential transmission fitness of the isolates. This proof-of-principle study will enable large-scale comparisons of complex N-glycosylation patterns of T/F and chronic viruses to fully test if unique glycan signatures influence HIV transmissibility and can be exploited for vaccine development.Keywords: glycosylation, Gp120, HIV, lectins, mass spectrometry

Introduction

Despite over three decades of research, human immunodeficiency virus type-1 (HIV- 1) continues to be a significant global health burden and an effective vaccine is needed to curtail transmission and end the epidemic (Haynes et al. 2023; Boomgarden and Upadhyay 2025). During HIV transmission, despite exposure to a swarm or quasispecies of HIV-1 strains from the donor, typically only one HIV-1 clone termed the transmitted/founder (T/F) migrates from the recipient’s genital mucosa to establish a new systemic infection (Klein et al. 2018). In the absence of antiretroviral treatment, this infecting clone slowly diversifies to form a new diverse quasispieces in persons living with HIV (Shankarappa et al. 1999; Troyer et al. 2005), often resulting in new N-linked glycosylation sites and various other amino acid changes in the viral envelope (Env) protein complex to escape immune selective pressure (Zhu et al. 1993; Keele et al. 2008; Salazar-Gonzalez et al. 2009; Troyer et al. 2009; Klein et al. 2018). Transmissibility advantage of HIV-1 T/F founder-derived Env sequences, comprising the spike protein gp120 and its transmembrane anchor gp41, was demonstrated experimentally by cloning the Env coding region obtained from 20 acute infections in the “neutral” HIV-1 backbone (laboratory strain NL4–3) to generate the strains termed B1-B20 HIV-1. Sequences derived from chronic infections were cloned in the same system, including Q0 HIV-1 that was derived from an untreated person living with a chronic HIV infection (Troyer et al. 2005). These strains were tested in HIV-1 transmission studies done in various human primary cells (King et al. 2013), in rhesus macaques (Asmal et al. 2016; Krebs et al. 2016), and in an ex vivo model involving penile and cervical tissue (Klein et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2025). The HIV-1 B4 virus had consistently higher transmission fitness through genital mucosal tissues than the other 19 acute HIV-1 tested and than the 5 chronic HIV-1 which included Q0 HIV-1 (Klein et al. 2021).

The characteristics supporting transmission success of the T/F are unknown. All phenotypic analyses above, including viral transmission fitness, were performed with HIV-1 harboring the Env coding region from those infected with subtype B HIV-1, that have an average of 7% amino acid diversity and 10% nucleotide sequence diversity (Troyer et al. 2005;King et al. 2013 ; Asmal et al. 2016 ; Klein et al. 2021). Thorough diversity analyses of HIV-1 Env sequences have not revealed any Env signature sequence(s) linked to significant differences in transmission fitness or any other phenotypic property among the acutes or between acutes and chronic Env (King et al. 2013;Asmal et al. 2016 ; Klein et al. 2021). However, the Env sequences from acute infections tend to have fewer N-linked glycosylation sites than sequences derived from chronic infections (Asmal et al. 2016; Klein et al. 2021). This is relevant since the Env glycoproteins mediate viral entry into host cells and are the primary viral targets for neutralizing antibodies (Raja et al. 2003; Pilcher et al. 2004; Honnen et al. 2007; Alexandre et al. 2011; Lavine et al. 2012; Mouquet et al. 2012; Burton and Mascola 2015; Welbourn et al. 2022). Because gp120 is heavily glycosylated, with glycans estimated to cover 50–70% of Env’s surface (Wagh et al. 2018) and making up over 50% of its mass (Leonard et al. 1990), the conserved Env protein regions are largely hidden and protected from antibody recognition, including the CD4 binding site. Most anti-Env antibodies target hypervariable, disordered, exposed, and hyperglycosylated peptide loops in gp120. As such, the antibody response during a natural infection is not sufficient to control HIV-1, perpetuating escape mutations from newly emerging neutralizing antibodies (Burton and Mascola 2015) that result in altered content and positioning of N-linked glycosylation. The ever-evolving glycan layer shields conserved Env protein epitopes, keeping the infecting HIV always one step ahead of the humoral immune response, and ultimately, leading to viral persistence (Wang 2013; Doores 2015; Joseph et al. 2015; Klein et al. 2018). With every new transmission event/infection, only a single HIV-1 variant establishes infection from a diverse inoculating HIV-1 population. Prior to emergence of the adaptive anti-HIV immune response, HIV Env is under very different selective pressure with rare HIV-1 clones possibly being favoured for reduced and/or optimal glycan content and composition that support transmission (Alexandre et al. 2011; Akkouh et al. 2015; Doores 2015; Joseph et al. 2015).

The presence of abundant glycosylation also renders gp120 prone to being bound by lectins which may trap the virus at the tissue surface and prevent its translocation through tissues. Exogenous lectins such as algae Griffithsin and Cyanobacteria Cyanovirin-N that target high mannose glycans indeed bind to and inhibit HIV (Alexandre et al. 2011; Akkouh et al. 2015). However, some lectins can also increase host susceptibility to HIV infection by promoting HIV-1 spread between susceptible cells while avoiding glycan-mediated endocytosis and antigen presentation (Heindel et al. 2024). This suggests that there exists variation in HIV’s sensitivity to being targeted/trapped by lectins, likely imparted by a specific glycosylation pattern, which might drive the selection of viruses with specific transmissibility phenotypes (Joseph et al. 2015; Jan et al. 2019).

Most host lectins relevant to HIV transmission are C-type lectins that preferentially binds high-mannose glycoforms, such as soluble mannose binding lectins (MBLs) found within the extracellular matrix of the mucosa, the macrophage mannose receptor (MMR) on macrophages, or DC-SIGN and/or Langerin found on many cells in the myeloid lineage including dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, and Langerhans cells. The immune capture enabled by DC-SIGN is geared at supporting pathogen processing into antigens by the DCs for subsequent presentation to T cells. However, matured complex or hybrid glycans on gp120 can protect HIV viruses from degradation after capture by DC-SIGN, allowing transport and delivery of intact viruses to T cells (Geijtenbeek et al. 2000; Jan et al. 2019; Martín-Moreno and Muñoz-Fernández 2019). Consistently, we showed increased binding affinity of C-type lectins to chronic HIV-1 Env than to acute HIV-1 Env (Klein et al. 2021).

This cell-to-cell viral transfer from DCs to T cells may also involve other host lectins, such as the sialic acid-binding Ig-like lectin 1 (Siglec-1). The Siglec family recognizes terminal sialylated glycans and Siglec-1 is known to be up-regulated during HIV-1 infection (van der Kuyl et al. 2007; Pino et al. 2015). Interestingly, HIV-1 has been shown to utilize sialylated glycans on gp120 to facilitate viral entry into macrophages or DCs through interactions with Siglec-1, thus enhancing transmission to T cells (Zou et al. 2011; Izquierdo-Useros et al. 2012).

While the gp120 protein can be both N- and O-glycosylated, N-linked glycosylation has been demonstrated to be much more abundant (Ho et al. 2008). Also, certain studies have found that Env may lack O-linked glycosylation altogether in many HIV isolates (Stansell et al. 2015; Termini et al. 2017) and that HIV virus can retain full infectivity even when produced in a knockout cell defective for O-glycosylation (Termini et al. 2017). We thus decided to focus on gp120 N-glycosylation which is found at asparagine residues comprised in the NXT/S consensus sequence (X is any amino acid except proline).

Viral protein glycosylation relies on the glycosylation machinery from the infected host cell. The process of N-glycosylation begins in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) where a mannose-rich glycan is attached to the protein and is subsequently trimmed (Varki et al. 2015; Schoberer et al. 2018). Glycoforms that escape the glycosylation pathway from the ER are immature and present high mannose glycans. Glycoproteins carrying such immature glycan forms can be incorporated in the viral membrane. Glycoproteins that continue through the maturation pathway are transported from the ER to the medial- and trans-Golgi. In the medial-Golgi, the core glycan gets extended by N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) and galactose residues, forming “hybrid” glycoforms (Varki et al. 2015). In the trans-Golgi, complex glycoforms are created with incorporation of various sugars in various branching patterns, including the addition of sialic acid (N-acetylneuraminic acid/Neu5Ac) at terminal positions and of fucose on the basal GlcNAc (Varki et al. 2015). These constitute signatures for maturation in this compartment (Sosicka et al. 2022). Medial-Golgi glycoforms are considered hybrid as they combine features of both high mannose forms seen in the ER and complex glycans seen in the trans-Golgi (Varki et al. 2015). Gp160 is processed into its gp120 and gp41 forms in the trans-Golgi compartment by the furin protease, cleaving at the C-terminal end of the REKR motif which is ~50 amino acids from the last N-glycosylation site of gp120 (Zhao et al. 2022).

The nature, amount and location of N-glycans added at each site along the Env peptide chain, collectively referred to as the distribution of glycans, can differ between HIV isolates as a result of sequence variations that occur in different Env positions during the course of infection and that alter access of glycosidases and transferases to the N-linked sites (Wei et al. 2003). These changes in glycan content triggered by changes in primary Env sequence in turn greatly affect transmissibility to new hosts and subsequent immune escape. We hypothesized that a specific N-glycosylation pattern of gp120 is associated with transmission and is thus found in T/F HIV isolates, while a highly diversified N-glycosylation pattern emerges in chronic virions that are constantly changing due to escape mutations. To test this hypothesis, an in-depth, site-specific analysis of gp120 glycosylation is necessary.

Many studies assessed gp120 N-glycosylation by analysing the pool of glycans released from the glycoprotein/gp120 by chemical or enzymatic methods (Sanders et al. 2002; Go et al. 2011; Pritchard et al. 2015). This provided a global view of the extent of glycosylation and of the variability of the glycans present but did not inform on site-specific distribution. Other studies used deglycosylating enzymes (PNGaseF & Endo H, for example) that leave a typical mass “scar” on deglycosylated peptides to determine which sites were originally glycosylated and what type of glycans were present via mass spectrometry (MS) analysis of the deglycosylated peptides (Raska et al. 2010; Go et al. 2011; Struwe et al. 2018). This, however, resulted in loss of crucial information about the glycoforms found at each individual N-glycosylation site. To fully delineate the glycan distribution (nature, location and relative abundance) on a glycoprotein, the MS analysis of actual glycopeptides is necessary. This complex process requires combined methods to fragment and sequence the peptides and the glycans while also preserving at least one sugar on the glycosylation site.

Electron-transfer dissociation (ETD) generates c/z-type fragment ions from the peptide, allowing the identification of the peptide sequence while leaving the glycan intact and thus allowing the identification of the attachment site (Singh et al. 2012; Zeng et al. 2022). Higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) results in B/Y-type fragment ions arising from the glycan that are useful for identifying individual sugars and glycan chains (Singh et al. 2012; Zeng et al. 2022). HCD also produces b/y-type peptide backbone fragments that tend to lose their associated glycans but are helpful to ascertain the peptide origin of the glycans (Singh et al. 2012; Riley et al. 2020; Zeng et al. 2022). The coupling of HCD and ETD allows for better glycopeptide characterisation (Riley et al. 2020) and can be implemented in a “product dependent” (pd) HCD-pd-ETD mode whereby HCD scans are screened in real time for the presence of a glycopeptide by scouting for diagnostic sugar-derived oxonium ions. The detection of oxonium ions then triggers ETD analysis (Riley et al. 2020). A further improvement used in this study is HCD-pd-EThcD (Higher-energy Collisional Dissociation—product dependent—Electron Transfer higher-energy collisional Dissociation), where oxonium ion detection triggers several rounds of EThcD analysis (coupled ETD / HCD), allowing complete characterization of the glycans, peptides and attachment site (Čaval et al. 2019).

Downstream analysis of glycoproteomic MS data is complex and requires specialized software (Riley et al. 2020). Although many other MS glycopeptide analysis software exists, many struggle to combine glycan and peptide information, preventing site-specific analyses (Hu et al. 2017). Here, we used the GlycoPAT software to characterize the site-specific glycosylation of gp120 from T/F (B4) and chronic (Q0) HIV-1 isolates, which have the highest and lowest transmission fitness (respectively) as measured on human genital tissues and in macaques as described above. We interrogated each of the gp120 24–27 glycosylation sites for 55 glycoforms representative of the main glycoforms emerging in the ER, medial-Golgi and trans-Golgi. We compared the outputs of 2 GlycoPAT versions across viral isolates and identified differences that may relate to transmissibility. We further investigated if the glycosylation differences may have functional implications for virion binding to lectins using a lectin microarray. This may be relevant to binding to tissues during infection and to viral transmission. This work has proof-of-principle value as it sets the stage for in-depth analysis of complex N-glycosylation patterns in large scale studies of T/F versus chronic viruses to fully test whether a unique N-glycan signature of HIV transmissibility exists.

Results

Production of gp120 from chimeric HIV-1

We analyzed gp120’s glycosylation using gp120 produced from infectious, chimeric HIV-1. Although large volumes of virus propagation cultures were necessary, using gp120 from virions is preferable to using recombinant Env glycoprotein produced in insect or mammalian cells (Binley et al. 2000; Crooks et al. 2007) as in these systems, purified recombinant Env is mostly derived from the Env trapped in the ER/Golgi, with very little Env protein present on the plasma membrane. None of the recombinant Env would be tethered to HIV-1 matrix region of the Gag precursor, which selects for Env during the budding process for typical virus production. In this study, our HIV-1 isolates were chimeras with the entire genome derived from the laboratory strain NL4–3, but containing the gp120 coding region and the gp41 ectodomain of the T/F B4 and chronic Q0 HIV-1 (with the transmembrane and intracellular domain (TMIC) derived from NL4–3). The TMIC of NL4–3 was retained to avoid chimeric Tat and Rev proteins due to the second exon of these genes in the TMIC (Azizi et al. 2023). Large batch propagations of T/F (B4) or chronic (Q0) Env chimeric HIV-1 were performed with microglia U87.CD4.CCR5 cells. The virions were recovered by ultracentrifugation of the culture supernatants and were lysed using Empigen BB detergent.

Western blot analysis suggests differential glycosylation of gp120 from T/F vs chronic isolates

Western blotting using antibodies that react with various protein or glycan epitopes was used to highlight potential differences in glycosylation between the T/F and chronic isolates. First, the viral protein content was determined by Western blotting for integral viral protein p24 (Passaes et al. 2021) and was found to be 1.7 times higher in B4 samples relative to Q0 samples (Fig. S1, Panels B and G). This was despite a globally higher total protein content (viral and host proteins) in Q0 samples, which was mostly driven by low molecular weight protein bands as assessed by Ponceau S red staining of Western blotting membranes (Fig. S1, Panels A and C), and by quantitation on a matching Coomassie stained gel (Fig. S2).

Upon probing with the B13 antibody (Fig. S1 Panel D) that recognizes gp120 protein epitopes, especially the CD4 binding site (Kwong and Mascola 2012), very strong reactivity was detected in B4 samples across molecular weights of 75 to 150 kDa, which is consistent with extensive glycosylation that gives rise to multitude glycoforms of different sizes. In contrast, very weak reactivity was obtained for Q0 samples (Panel D) as previous described (Zhang et al. 2025) (Panel C). Subsequent probing of the same membrane (without stripping) with PGT121 antibody (Panel E) that recognizes glycans in the variable V3 loop (Julien et al. 2013; Pancera et al. 2017) yielded signal only for Q0 samples and only at ~160 kDa, which is consistent with glycosylated gp160 (uncleaved precursor). The sharp band indicates recognition of a homogeneously glycosylated form. Upon stripping of the membrane and probing with antibody 2G12 (Panel F) which recognizes high mannose glycans proximal to the V3 loop (Murin et al. 2014; Pancera et al. 2017), reactivity was observed in B4 samples only, and only for the highest molecular weight gp120 band. As these observations can not be fully explained by the slightly higher content in viral proteins in B4 samples determined above, these data suggest that differences of glycosylation may exist between B4 and Q0 samples and across gp120 forms within each virion type. This may encompass variations in the location and nature of glycans present in each protein. This was elucidated by a detailed MS-based analysis as described below.

Sample preparation for comprehensive glycosylation analysis of gp120

Though other studies describe enriching HIV lysates in gp120 by using a mannose binding lectin column (Go et al. 2013; Sun et al. 2018), we found that a significant portion of glycosylated gp120 does not bind to a mannose-binding lectin column (Fig. S3). Consequently, lectin-based purification could introduce a drastic bias in the data and was excluded from our workflow. Instead, total virion lysates were run on SDS-PAGE gels and all gel sections potentially comprising gp120 were excised and processed to extract glycopeptides (Fig. S2). This allowed the elimination of the prominent bovine serum albumin (BSA) contaminant (from tissue culture media) and of irrelevant low molecular weight proteins from the MS analysis. Overall, 3 glycopeptide pools (A, B and C) were analysed by MS for each sample as depicted in Fig. S2. Based on molecular weights of excised proteins, we surmise that pool A (> 150 kDa) comprises glycopeptides from the uncleaved gp160 precursor or oligomerized gp120; pool B (100 to 150 kDa) comprises glycopeptides from gp120 monomers with different levels of glycosylation; and pool C (50 to 100 kDa, excluding BSA band) may comprise glycopeptides from gp120 with low amounts of glycosylation or from truncated gp120 forms. This is not exclusive of other viral or host proteins.

Software and glycoforms used for MS data analysis

The MS data were analyzed using 2 versions of GlycoPAT following the workflow shown in Fig. S4. GlycoPAT v1.0 utilizes ETD and HCD spectra individually, returning a list of potential glycopeptides (Liu et al. 2017) while GlycoPAT v2.0 analyzes EThcD data (coupled ETD and HCD spectra), integrating peptide and glycan sequencing data to return a list of higher confidence glycopeptides. Both versions have complementary value as the higher stringency of v2.0 may lead to underestimation of the glycoform content, while the higher permittivity of v1.0 may lead to its overestimation. Importantly, GlycoPAT analysis is sequence-specific and thus only returns assignments for gp120, even if other proteins (gp41 or host proteins) were also present in the same pool.

The list of 55 glycoforms selected, though non exhaustive, comprises glycan signatures chosen based on the literature (Raska et al. 2010; Yolitz et al. 2018) and that are typical of the cellular compartment where they were added and/or last processed, such as the basal fucose and terminal Neu5Ac for trans-Golgi, the highly branched, high (terminal) mannose for ER, and the intermediate forms still devoid of basal fucose but having experienced extremity trimming and re-extension by sugars other than mannose for medial-Golgi. This allowed a classification for ER, medial-Golgi (MG), and trans-Golgi (TG) glycoforms (Fig. 1). The “ER” category also includes trimmed forms that may arise in the cis-Golgi but may also arise from bona fide ER forms upon fragmentation during mass spectrometry analyses. Other fragmented glycoforms without basal fucose may have arisen from any of the ER or MG glycoforms and were thus dubbed “unassigned ER/MG” (UA ER/MG). The “unassigned” small TG glycoforms (UA TG) that carry the basal fucose TG signature arose from any of the larger TG glycoforms upon MS fragmentation. As there is no possibility to track their specific origin amongst the TG glycoforms, they are dubbed “unassigned TG.”

B4 gp120 is more glycosylated than Q0 gp120 but the proportions of various glycoforms are similar in both virions

First, we determined the counts of each glycoform found in each of the A, B and C pools, with all 55 glycoforms stratified by biological compartment where their last processing occurred (Fig. S9). Except for outlier B4-2C, glycosylation was observed in all pools. As expected, the glycoform counts were higher with GlycoPAT v1.0 than with the more stringent v2.0, the latter also revealing higher glycoform counts for B4 pools relative to matching Q0 pools in all samples except outlier B4-2C. The higher glycoform counts in B4 were also apparent when all data were averaged together (~2.4 fold as per more stringent V.2, Fig. 3A and C). The differences were found statistically significant by two-sample t-test performed on the average glycoform group counts (ER, MG etc) for each GlycoPAT version (Fig. 3E).

The data from individual samples were also expressed as proportions to compensate for differences in glycoform counts that may be due to variations in sample processing. The proportions of the various glycoforms (ER, MG etc) were generally consistent and deemed non statistically different across technical replica (1,2 and 3) or across the A, B and C pools within each virion, as well as across B4 and Q0 samples (Figs S10 and S11, statistical analysis via Cohen’s h determination). Instances of low to medium effect size (0.2 < h < 0.8) across A, B or C pools were primarily driven by “unassigned” glycoforms and were mitigated by using the aggregated “TG All” glycoform category. This indicates excessive fragmentation that prevented definitive assignment of some glycoforms rather than actual sample differences. The fact that glycoform proportion differences between Q0 and B4 were generally minimal and non statistically significant was also apparent when all data were averaged to represent the proportions of glycoforms present in all gp120 forms produced by each virion (Fig. 3B, D and E).

In all samples, the TG forms (“assigned” + “unassigned”) represented 50–60% of all glycoforms (Figs S10 and S11, panels G and H, TG ALL and Fig. 3B, 3D), with no statistical difference between Q0 and B4 samples (Figs S10 I and S11 I). This indicates that gp120 mainly harbours complex glycans arising from passage through the TG and has thus progressed through the full glycosylation and maturation process. However, ~15–20% of gp120 features ER-type glycoforms, potentially suggesting early egress from the maturation pathway or incomplete processing of some glycosylation sites within each gp120 molecule. This egress may employ Golgi-independent trafficking following ER glycosylation, with extracellular release via endosomes (Bowen et al. 2017).

We have established that gp120 from the T/F virion B4 has more than double the glycoform counts (2.4 fold) than gp120 from the chronic virion Q0 when analysed with the more stringent GlycoPAT v2.0, and that these glycoforms are both similar and complex in nature in both virions. This was further evident when the number of individual sugars comprised in the various glycoforms identified was tabulated (Table S1). As the B4 samples comprise 1.7 more viral proteins than the Q0 samples based on p24 quantitation, we conclude that B4 gp120 has ~41% more glycosylation than Q0, where glycosylation is defined by the total sugar content that could be detected. Very hydrophilic glycans or very large glycans may not be captured in our analysis.

Analysis of gp120 glycosylation per glycosylation site

Though there was no major qualitative difference in glycoform proportions between Q0 and B4 samples, there was a quantitative difference whereby B4 is more glycosylated than Q0 and as such, we explored the possibility that there would be glycosylation hot spots that would differ between isolate types. Mapping differences in distribution of glycosylation along the sequence may help identify transmission-associated glycosylation signatures for future studies. To compare glycosylation across N-glycosylation sites of each isolate, their gp120 protein sequences were aligned to the Hxb2 sequence which is widely used as a reference (Grossmann et al. 2015). Structural modeling was used to refine the assignments of equivalent N-sites in areas where sequence-based alignments were ambiguous (Figs S12 and S13). The full list of equivalent N-glycosylation sites across the 3 structures is summarized in Table S2.

Comparing the numbers of glycoforms present across equivalent N-glycosylation sites of B4 and Q0 gp120 using the GlycoPAT v1.0 data was unsuccessful due to disproportionally high numbers of glycoforms proposed in the N-termini of Q0 and B4 gp120 and in the C-terminus of Q0 (Fig. S14). These were found on peptides that comprised multiple adjacent sites that could not be resolved on individual peptides either because no protease cut site exists or because the proteases could not cut due to steric hindrance from glycosylation. Though the data were curated to remove redundancy of assignment of glycosylation to these sites, the assignments to specific sites can not be confirmed without extensive manual annotation of the spectra.

As a result, the site-specific analysis was performed using the GlycoPAT v2.0 dataset revealed a significant difference (P < 0.05) in glycan distribution at 11 out of the 20 aligned N-glycosylation sites (Fig. 4). These were found in hypervariable regions V1, V2 and V4, but also in conserved regions C1, C2 and C5 (Cristian Apetrei et al. 2023). There were also qualitative differences on the nature of the glycoforms within each category as shown in pie charts for select site pairs (Fig. 4) and for all sites (Fig. 5). This is in addition to high glycosylation counts present at 4 of the B4 sites that have no equivalent in Q0 (K130, N141, N295 and N463, located in variable regions V1, V3 and V5), and at 6 of the Q0 sites that have no equivalent in B4 (mostly in N362 from conserved region C3 and in lower amounts at T49, N234, N406 and S461 a&b located in regions C1, C2, V4 and V5, respectively).

Specifically, the largest and most significant difference between the aligned N-glycosylation sites occurred at site N186 of the variable V2 region. N186 was found to be the most glycosylated N site in B4 overall while it was the least glycosylated N sites in Q0. The most abundant glycoforms at B4’s N186 site were complex type glycans, specifically TG6 and TG11 (Fig. 4, sample pie chart). In contrast, the most abundant glycoforms at Q0’s N186 were small, unassigned TG glycoforms, specifically TG36 and TG37. B4 also had a large number of high-mannose glycans at this site (e.g. ER1 to ER8). Striking differences were also observed at site N413 of the variable V4 region, with significantly higher counts in B4 and very different glycan distribution, especially regarding high-mannose ER forms being present in high proportions in B4 and absent in Q0. Such variability in glycoform nature was also obtained on several other N-glycosylation sites (Fig. 4, N356) though without statistical significance on their counts. Note that all sites were mostly present in their glycosylated forms in both isolates (>70% for most sites, Fig. S15), though the actual proportions of glycosylated vs unoccupied sites may be skewed by the HCD-pd-EThcD method that selects for glycopeptides.

The presence of hot spots for the amounts of glycosylation or for the nature of glycosylation across equivalent sites of both isolates is further apparent on a bubble plot (Fig. 6). A clear high mannose hot spot represented by ER1-ER5 forms and a hot spot for MG forms (MG2-MG6) are evident in the N-terminus of B4 samples while they are absent at equivalent positions in Q0 samples. Hot spots are also localized at the B4 N-terminus for TG forms, and at the very C-terminus and are absent from Q0. Of note, the hot spots do not map to the specific variable vs conserved areas typically used to map the gp120 sequence, and some hot spots actually overlap conserved and variable regions. For example, the ER, MG and TG hot spots in the N-terminus of B4 straddle C1, V1, V2 and C2 and the C-terminal TG hot spot straddles C5 and V5. Further, the hot spots concern “dense” glycan, i.e. glycans that have a higher count of individual sugars (≥ 8 sugars) compared with others. These include the ER1-ER4 chains, most of the TG1 to TG28 forms and the MG1-MG4 forms. While Q0’s glycosylation is overall less abundant, 5 small hot spots are still notable, including 2 that are unique to Q0 and do not overlap with B4 hot spots (full lines). These straddle regions C3, V4, C5 and V5.

However, the presence of hot spots indicates that glycosylation is not occurring on a random basis along the gp120 sequence. As structural factors may govern the accessibility of glycosylation enzymes while the gp120 protein folds and matures through the ER to Golgi pathway, we mapped the location and amount of the glycans from each glycan category (ER, MG and TG) on the gp120 Q0 and B4 structures (modeled on the Env trimer structure, PDB template 7n6u.1.A) with a view to detect glycosylation hot spots at the structural level. For all 3 categories, the few glycoforms present in Q0 are mostly confined to one half of the protein structure while glycosylation is evenly distributed along B4’s structure. Lectin microarray indicates potential functional significance of high-mannose glycosylation hot spot found in B4 T/F isolate

A lectin microarray comprising over 90 lectins was used to determine if the gp120 glycosylation differences between isolates determined by mass spectrometry may have functional implications on gp120’s ability to bind to lectins, which in turn would affect viral binding and transmission. The array used comprised plant lectins and not tissue lectins, but many of these lectins bind similar sugars/glycans as those bound by host lectins (See Tables S3 and S4 for details on array and lectin names and specificities). Also, though the lectin binding assays were performed with whole virion lysates which contain host proteins and other viral proteins in addition to gp120, the virion production system used is such that the main difference between the isolates tested is the gp120 protein sequence and its resulting glycosylation. For these reasons, the data from this array collectively provide a close approximation of potential differences of lectin-mediated binding to tissue imputable to differences in glycosylation of gp120. These differences would encompass both N- and O-glycosylation.

The B4 and Q0 gp120 samples were run against a biological reference consisting of all samples run on the array and labeled with an orthogonal fluorophore, and the data were expressed relatively to this reference (Figs S17 and S18). For each isolate, the lectins bound similarly between the 3 biological replica, indicating that the glycomes of each sample were conserved in each batch of virions produced. Clustering of the lectins identified both differences and similarities in the isolate binding patterns, showing glycans equally present in both isolates (O-glycan binding lectins MPA, HPL for example) or clusters where differential binding was apparent (for oligo- and high-mannose binding lectins HHL, GNA or Griffithsin for example).

Both strains bound equally to several lectins including MPL, AIA, HPA, PTA-II, showing the integrity and availability of glycans for lectin binding after fluorescent labeling of gp120. The strongest and equal binding of both sample types was seen for MPL (Fig. S18), which strongly binds core 1/3 O-glycans and tolerates sialic acid (Wu 2005; Bojar et al. 2021; Szczykutowicz et al. 2021; Bojar et al. 2022). This binding likely reflects O-glycosylation motifs present in the samples (on host or viral proteins) and conserved across isolates.

Significantly more binding was observed for B4 than for Q0 for 5 lectins (GNA 1 and 2, ASA, GNA and NPL) that have specificity for high mannose motifs (Fig. 8). There was also a trend for more binding of B4 with 3 other lectins (HHL, Griffithsin and BanLec H84T) that were also mannose-specific. Their specificity for various high mannose motifs stemming from the ER and cis-Golgi (Fig. 9B) correlated well with the lectin array patterns (Fig. 9C) and with the quantitative mass spectrometry data analysis that showed larger counts of each high-mannose ER/cis-Golgi glycoform in B4 than Q0 (Fig. 9A).

Other lectins showed near significance for binding more B4 compared to Q0. Although these lectins have limited global binding, they have specificity for fucose (Fig. 8, UEA-I, 1,2-fucosylated motifs, (Gomez et al. 1995; Bojar et al. 2021; Bojar et al. 2022)) which may be present at the termini of TG forms, or for terminal GlcNAc (GSL-II) which is present in some TG forms, thus suggesting that B4 samples contain more complex TG glycans than Q0 samples, as also seen in the mass spectrometry data analysis.

However, some areas of the lectin array did not directly match expectations based on the MS results. For example, while the slightly higher binding of B4 onto GSL-II (specific for GlcNAc), ECA (specific for LacNAc), UEA II (specific for GlcNAc) is consistent with the increase in LacNAc complex sugars, very little binding was obtained with UEA II overall, and no clear difference was observed with DSA which is also LacNAc-specific (Fig. S18). Likewise, the MS data point to an increase in sialylated complex N-glycans in B4 and consistently, lectin MAA-I (2 of the 3 sources tested, specific for sialylation and sulfation) showed an increase in B4. However, other lectins with similar specificity showed no difference (alpha 2,3 sialylation lectins SLBR N (1 and 2) and SLBR H; alpha 2,6 sialylation lectins SNA and rPSL1a), or even showed inverted trends (MAA-I, 1 of 3 sources tested) (Fig. S18). These differences in reactivity for lectins with the same predicted specificity depending on the original organism from which they were extracted and/or on the commercial source, could relate to slight differences in absolute specificity of these lectins. The discrepancies between lectins and MS data could also relate to the local environment of the gp120 target sites, whereby steric hindrance from other sugars in the vicinity may prevent the cognate sugar/lectin pair from binding.

Discussion

This study investigates if and how gp120 N-glycosylation differs between T/F and chronic HIV viruses, using isolates B4 and Q0 for a proof-of-principle comparison. Chimeric virions identical between both isolates except for their gp120 sequence were propagated in the CD4+CCR5+ U87 cell line which provides high viral titers (Dudley et al. 2009). As the final glycosylation pattern of gp120 incorporated in the virions depends both on the glycosylation capacity of the cell line used for propagation (Raska et al. 2010; Pritchard et al. 2015; Zhao et al. 2021), it will likely differ somewhat in the U87 cells compared with seminal or vaginal T cells or to macrophage populations that support HIV-1 replication at sites of donor to recipient transmission. Compared to various T cell lines, U87 cells express high levels of CD4 and CCR5 and are highly permissive to infection which allows for large-scale propagation of the virus. HIV-1 has only ~10 spikes per virus particle, a small number compared to the hundreds of spikes on most membrane encapsulated viruses (e.g. Influenza, SARS-CoV-2, etc), so high HIV particle yield is necessary to purify sufficient Env for mass spectrometry, lectin array analysis and transmission studies. Also, we have previously characterized the phenotypic differences in HIV-1 with acute and chronic Env using virus grown in various CD4+ T cell and monocytic cell lines, clones, or primary T cells (Quiñones-Mateu et al. 1998; Ball et al. 2003; Ariën et al. 2005; Troyer et al. 2005; King et al. 2013). When HIV-1 stocks are derived from U87 cells versus human CD4+ T cells, efficiency in host cell entry, replicative fitness, antibody mediated neutralization, and even lectin binding affinity remain similar between one virus relative to another. In addition, we routinely sequence these viruses before and after these short virus propagations and observed few if any synonymous substitutions but some nonsynonymous substitutions.

Since within a given production system, the final glycosylation pattern of gp120 incorporated in the virions also depends on the specific gp120 amino acid sequence tested, we exploited this system to identify sequence-specific differences between our T/F and chronic isolates. We dissected gp120 N-glycosylation according to the various glycoforms that are generated during the protein progress through the ER, medial-Golgi and trans-Golgi, and also examined gp120 N-glycosylation at a site-specific level along the protein sequence. Two versions of the GlycoPAT software were used, whereby version v1.0 likely overestimates gp120 glycosylation while version v2.0 may underestimate it, though both showed similar trends.

We analysed a wide array of gp120 forms as the biogenesis of HIV leads to rather heterogenous surface proteins, including gp120. The HIV-1 Env glycoprotein is translated from membrane ribosomes into ER where glycosylation is initiated and where folding into gp160 trimers occurs. With amino acid sequence diversity as high as 20% in the Env gene from different HIV-1 strains, the estimated mass of 160 kDa can differ based on amino acid content and based on the extent and nature of glycosylation that occurs in the ER and Golgi compartments on the 20 to 30 N-linked glycosylation sites present in a monomer of HIV-1 Env. Late in the Golgi compartment, the gp160 Env trimer is mostly cleaved by the Furin protease resulting in gp120/gp41 trimers being transported to the plasma membrane. About 10 gp120/gp41 trimers are incorporated on the virion surface when the plasma membrane is pinched off to produce a 100 nm HIV-1 particle containing a viral core. An intact gp120/gp41 Env trimer on the viral membrane is required to engage cell membrane proteins, CD4 and CCR5 to mediate viral and host membrane fusion for the disposition of the core into the cell cytoplasm. However, HIV-1 particles are not uniform in composition, with the presence of some uncleaved gp160 trimers and cellular transmembrane proteins along with gp120/gp41 Env trimers. The virus particle is also susceptible to cleavage by extracellular or membrane bound proteases. While the gp41 transmembrane domain and intracytoplasmic tail tether the entire Env to the membrane, ectodomains of the gp41 trimer fit within the protruding gp120 trimer. Due to the noncovalent interaction between gp41 and gp120, the gp120 trimer is readily shed from HIV-1 particle. Overall, the HIV-1 Env glycoproteins on a virus particle are very diverse in terms of numbers, amino acid sequence, glycan content, and in compositions of intact and degraded subunits. To account for this, all gp120 forms produced by each virion and present within ~50 to 160 kDa molecular weight bands on SDS-PAGE gels were assessed in our study. This avoided bias in glycosylation profile that may result from any lectin-based or other purification method. As expected, gp120 and its glycosylation were observed in all pools (except outlier B4-2C), and similar glycoform proportions were also observed in all pools. Consistently with the pool protein content proposed above, this suggests the presence of truncated gp120 products (Pool C), monomers (Pool B), and gp120 comprised in uncleaved gp160 and/or in oligomers (Pool A). Further, it excludes that differential glycosylation occurs in gp120 found in different pools, and suggests lack of influence of furin cleavage on the final glycosylation of gp120.

This analysis revealed that the T/F virus (B4) exhibited higher levels of glycosylation when compared to the chronic virus (Q0), the difference being more apparent with v2.0 than v1.0. It could be argued that B4 has more glycosylation (~41% more) because its amino acid sequence supports better peptide ionization than Q0. However, gp120 sequence conservation between isolates is very high (80.6% identity, 92.1% homology), with conservation even occurring in the most variable regions. Small differences in sequence would not be anticipated to affect peptide ionization drastically enough to double the glycan content found by MS, especially since our data show that the enhanced numbers are distributed throughout the sequence. Since the virions are generated from plasmid constructs where only the gp120 sequence differs, we can conclude that the subtle differences in amino acid sequences of B4 and Q0 gp120 resulted in this different glycosylation amounts.

The MS-based finding that B4 contains more sugars than Q0 is consistent with its reactivity with the 2G12 anti-glycan antibody (no prior antibody bound) while Q0 showed no response. However, the Western blot data obtained upon sequential usage of antibodies targeting protein (B13, applied first) and sugar epitopes (PGT21, applied without stripping B13 out) appear inconsistent with the MS finding as only Q0 responded to the sugar antibody and B4 responded much stronger to the protein antibody that was applied first. The estimated 1.7 fold less viral protein amount in Q0 samples does not appear sufficient to explain the drastic difference of B13 reactivity, from saturating signal in B4 to near zero signal in Q0. We thus surmise that the low B13 reactivity in Q0 samples may also be due to the masking of the B13 protein epitopes by glycans and/or to a potentially higher specificity of the B13 antibody for the B4 amino acid sequence. Either would result in less bound B13 antibody on Q0 protein bands. This would then allow better reactivity of Q0 bands with PGT121 antibody (for sugar epitopes) than of B4 bands that are already coated with the B13 antibody and that may not carry PGT121 specific glycan epitopes.

Regarding the nature of the glycans found, once the TG-derived unassigned forms are included with the TG-assigned forms, both virions comprise equivalent and preponderant proportions of TG forms than all other forms. This suggests progress of most gp120 through the full protein maturation and secretion process. Also, GlycoPAT v2.0 revealed that B4 had a higher count and higher proportion of ER, high-mannose forms and of medial-Golgi glycoforms, and the site-specific glycosylation analysis indicated that this enhanced high-mannose content was distributed across variable and conserved regions of the gp120 sequence. Even at B4 N-glycosylation sites where few glycans were found, high-mannose glycoforms were still more abundant than in Q0. The data are consistent with a prior study that annotated gp120 MS spectra manually and found that 20% of T/F N-glycosylation sites were populated with high-mannose glycans (Go et al. 2011). Overall, the T/F virus tested (B4) had a higher proportion of dense glycan chains at many N-glycosylation sites. Biologically, B4 may utilize more dense glycan chains to create an efficacious glycan shield that may mask immunogenic protein epitopes on viruses, and this may begin to explain why B4 has better transmission fitness.

This finding is consistent with the lectin array data that revealed functional (i.e. binding) differences of the Q0 and B4 glycans. More lectins were able to bind B4, indicating that the overall glycosylation was higher (or at least significantly different) in B4 despite having fewer potential N-glycosylation sites than Q0. Specifically, the differences in binding could be correlated mostly to the higher “high mannose” motif content of B4 based on lectin specificity. At this stage though, we can not exclude a contribution of O-glycans to this binding. However, other areas of the lectin array did not match expectations based on MS data, with globally limited and/or equal binding between isolates, and variability across lectins of similar specificity. This may relate to differences in the local environment of the targeted glycans that differentially affect lectin binding. Also, binding of lectins for glycans not included in our 55-glycoform list was observed. This was the case for UEA I that is specific for terminal alpha 1, 2 fucose. However, the binding was limited for the 3 UEA I lectins tested, especially Gene Tex UEA I. Also, very limited binding was observed with antibodies against Lewis structures that contain fucose in terminal and / or branched positions (B and Y, alpha 1,3 and alpha 1,4) and that were included in the array. Thus, we consider that the data on the lectin/antibody array are consistent with the MS data in that, when fucose was found by MS, it was mostly at the base of the glycan (alpha 1,6 fucosylation), on the chitobiose core as depicted in the glycan list we investigated and shown on annotated spectra (Fig. 2), as very limited binding was shown with lectin or antibodies probing for 2, 3 or 4 fucose.

Site-specific glycosylation analysis revealed sites with extreme differences in the count and the nature of glycoforms present between both strains, as exemplified with concatenated counts of all forms per site, with pie charts and a bubble plot delineating the specific proportion of each form at each site along the sequence. This analysis revealed the presence of isolate-specific glycosylation hot spots along the sequence. The more abundant high mannose glycosylation of B4 gp120 was essentially grouped in a hot spot at the N-terminus. A second area enriched mostly in Golgi forms is also present on B4 and shows absence of Golgi forms and much reduced numbers of TG forms (red ellipse, dashed line). Structural representation further demonstrates that hot spots exist for all glycoforms in B4 and map to defined areas of gp120. A clear high mannose hot spot (highlighted by red ellipse, solid line) is unique to B4 as the equivalent structural domain of Q0 gp120 is practically devoid of high mannose forms. The same area is also enriched in media Golgi and trans Golgi forms. This hot spot is oriented towards the outer surface of the trimer, forming an important surface for interactions with lectins as well as an efficient glycan shield against proteases and neutralizing antibodies. These hot spots do not overlap with the CD4 binding site and are thus unlikely to interfere with CD4 binding, thus supporting a role in steps preceding the interaction with CD4, which may comprise lectin binding for initial adherence to the mucosal epithelium and subsequent cross-epithelial transport, or protection against proteolytic degradation. The uneven distribution and globally limited presence of glycans in Q0 relatively to B4 leaves the Q0 gp120 more exposed to potential proteolytic degradation or antibody binding. It could be argued, that being a chronic isolate, the sequence variability of all gp120 isolates present concomitantly in people living with HIV chronically may counterbalance the ability to produce significant titers of appropriate antibodies against each of the isolates, thus negating the need for as efficient a glycan shield as in T/F founders.

DCs are among the first cells to interact with HIV after transmission and capture viruses by utilizing a C-type lectin, known as DC-SIGN that binds preferentially high-mannose motifs. Our finding of B4 having higher high-mannose content which shows functional effects in terms of lectin binding on our lectin microarray suggests that there could be functional implications in vivo to enhance transmissibility via favoring interactions with DC-SIGN. The matured complex or hybrid glycans that comprise 60–70% of its glycans could then protect the virus from degradation after capture by DC-SIGN, as reported by others (Geijtenbeek et al. 2000; Jan et al. 2019). Viruses that enter this pathway early enough can effectively evade the host’s immune response and begin transmission through enhanced trans-infection of CD4+ T cells. In tandem with our results, this points towards T/F gp120 needing a delicate balance of high-mannose and complex/hybrid glycoforms to enter the DC-SIGN pathway and not undergo degradation, which may be realized in B4 but not Q0.

Our analysis is conceptually akin to prior work performed on gp120 extracted from virions produced in infected T cells (Panico et al. 2016). Our final glycan compositions and distributions differ, especially regarding the very heterogenous glycosylation and the preponderance of complex glycans (trans-Golgi-type) that we observed at many sites. However, we found enhanced high-mannose content in B4 that was distributed across variable and conserved regions of the gp120 sequence, thus partially matching the high mannose content finding of the gp120 preparation stemming from T cells. The differences between both studies are potentially due to the fact that the nature of the cell line used for virion production influences the final glycosylation pattern as discussed above (U87 vs T lymphoid cell line), that the yield of virion production may affect the amount and nature of glycans incorporated, and that the gp120 sequence affects the glycan complement and differs in both studies. Also, differences could stem from the fact that we assessed a wide range of gp120 forms, representing all gp120 produced at a given time in the virion, instead of purifying a single, highly homogeneous gp120 band that may display a very different glycan content than other forms. Indeed, our Western blot analysis with PGT121 and 2G12 anti-glycan antibodies shows a distinct reactivity pattern of a high molecular weight band in each of Q0 (gp160) and B4 (gp120) relative to the rest of the gp120 population, suggesting a different specific glycan complement. Since differences in output inherent to each experimental set up can not be avoided, we alleviated system-specific issues via our experimental design based on a comparative analysis of 2 virions differing only in their gp120 sequence and produced under identical conditions, which allowed identifying sequence-mediated differences in N-glycosylation.

In conclusion despite differences in glycoform counts inherent to each GlycoPAT versions, the GlycoPAT v2.0 analysis confirmed and further emphasized the trends highlighted by GlycoPAT v1.0 analysis regarding the higher global level of glycosylation of B4 gp120 and the preponderance of trans-Golgi glycosylation forms in both virion types. It also revealed a higher proportion of ER, high-mannose glycoforms in B4 isolates than Q0, associated with a lower proportion of unassigned forms. The site-specific study identified isolate-specific hot spots at different locations in the sequence and structure of gp120 and with different glycan compositions. As isolate-specific glycosylation hot spots may be targetable for vaccine design, these proof-of-principle findings need to be recapitulated on several different chronic and T/F founder isolates.

Materials and methods

HIV virion propagation and processing

Viral propagation was completed in the biosafety level 2+ Imaging Pathogens for Knowledge Translation (ImPaKT) facility at the University of Western Ontario. Chimeric viruses where only the gp120 sequence differs between the T/F B4 and the chronic Q0 isolates were used. The T/F virus B4 was derived from people living with HIV (PLWH) with acute HIV-1 infections, while the chronic isolate Q0 was isolated from PLWH for over one year.

Chimeric virion cloning and viral propagation were done as previously described (Dudley et al. 2009). Briefly summarized, the Env genes of B4 and Q0 were cloned into the pREC-nfl-NL4–3-△env/URA3 HIV-1 backbone using a yeast recombination system and the resulting plasmid and its complementary vector pCMV-cplt were co-transfected into HEK 293 T cells using Fugene 6 Transfection Reagent (Promega, Canada) following the manufacturer’s instruction to generate the chimeric viruses expressing the B4 and Q0 Env gp140 proteins. The viruses were then propagated in T150 flasks using U87.CD4.CCR5 cells. Cell culture supernatants (22–25 ml) containing B4 and Q0 were collected from each flask 3 times when CPE was observed, centrifuged at 1225 x g for 10 minutes, and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter to remove cell debris. The supernatants were stored at −80 °C until further processing.

The viral solution was ultra-centrifuged at 101,000 x g for 1.5 hours at 4 °C. The viral pellets were resuspended in 300 μL of 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate at pH 8.0. Benzonase and MgCl2 were added to the virion suspension to final concentrations of 2.5 unit/ml and 2 mM, respectively. Empigen BB was then added at 0.25% (w/v, final concentration) and the mixture was vortexed for 10 seconds and incubated at room temperature for 2 hours to lyse the virions and release gp120 from the viral envelope. NaCl (45 μl at 0.5 M) was added to 650 mM final concentration and the viral lysate was centrifuged at 21,000 xg in a microcentrifuge for 15 minutes at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube and stored at -80oC until future gp120 processing.

Purification on GNA lectin

Initially, gp120 was purified using a column containing 1 ml agarose-conjugated GNA lectin (mannose binding lectin, see Table S4, Sigma-Aldrich, binding capacity 5–10 mg/mL). Ten column volumes (CVs) of equilibration buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 650 mM NaCl, 0.25% Empigen BB) was first passed through the column and the filtered viral lysate was loaded on the column by gravity. After washes with 10 CVs of 20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 650 mM NaCl to remove excess Empigen BB, 10 CVs of 20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 1 M NaCl to remove non specifically bound proteins, and 10 CVs of 20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl to remove excess salts, gp120 was competitively eluted using 10 CVs of 20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1 M methyl-α-D-mannopyranoside. The flow through and eluates were dialyzed overnight at 4 °C (12–14 kDa molecular weight cut off) in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate at pH 7.5 and the dialysed sample were frozen at -80 °C, lyophilized and resuspended in 75 μl of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate at pH 7.5 and stored in -20 °C till further processing. The samples were analysed on SDS-PAGE with Coomassie staining, band excision and MS analysis performed as described below for total viral lysates, though focusing on bands of 80 and 120 kDa in size only.

Digestion and extraction of glycopeptides from SDS-PAGE

Whole virion lysates (15 μl of sample per lane containing 30 μl of lysate above and 10 μl 4x SDS-PAGE loading buffer) were loaded on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and proteins were separated at 140 V for 60–90 minutes. After Coomassie blue staining, the gels were washed in water and the bands comprising oligomerized gp120/gp160 (150-250 kDa), gp120 itself (100-150 kDa), and gp120 fragments (50–60 kDa, 75–100 kDa) were excised (Bands 1 to 7, excluding BSA band, Fig. S2). Each gel slice was independently digested with trypsin and chymotrypsin after reductive alkylation by dithiothreitol and iodoacetamide treatment, and the resulting glycopeptides were extracted. Specifically, the gel pieces were cut into 1 mm2 pieces, washed with 1 volume (V, ~40 μL) of water, 1 V of a water:acetonitrile 1:1 (V:V) solution, 1 V of 0.1 M ammonium bicarbonate pH = 8.0 (ABC thereafter), and 1 V of acetonitrile, with 15 min incubation at room temperature between each wash. The samples were dried in a vacuum centrifuge at room temperature. The samples were then reduced and alkylated by first incubating with 1 V of ABC buffer containing 10 mM DTT at 56 °C for 45 minutes, removing excess fluid and incubating with 1 V of ABC buffer containing 55 mM iodoacetamide in the dark for 30 minutes. The samples underwent the washing steps outlined above, vacuum spun until dry, and frozen at −20 °C.

For in-gel enzyme digestion, the samples were rehydrated on ice for 45 minutes in 50 μL of digestion buffer comprising 50 mM ABC at pH 8, 5 mM CaCl2, and 10 ng/μl of each of sequencing grade SOLu-trypsin (porcine pancreas Sigma-Aldrich) and chymotrypsin (bovine pancreas, Sigma-Aldrich, Low specificity, cutting C-terminally to FYWML with the following exclusion criteria: F, L Y: not before P; W: not before M or P; M: not before P or Y; H: Not before D, M, P or W). After 45 minutes, the gel pieces were spun down, and the supernatant was discarded and replaced by 20 μL of digestion buffer without the enzymes. The samples were incubated at 37 °C overnight.

To recover the peptide fragments from the gel pieces, the liquid from the overnight incubation was collected (1 tube / sample) and the gel pieces were covered with 20 μL of 25 mM ABC pH = 8.0 and incubated for 15 minutes. The same volume of acetonitrile was then added and incubated again for 15 min. The gel pieces were then spun down and the supernatant was recovered and added into the respective collection tube. The gel pieces were then incubated for 15 minutes with 1 V of acetonitrile and 1 volume of 5% formic acid together and the supernatant was pooled in the same collection tube as above. The extraction in formic acid and acetonitrile was repeated once. The samples were then pooled together into pools of A, B and C as indicated in Fig. S2. The pooled samples were then dried in a vacuum centrifuge and stored at −20 °C.

Mass spectrometry analyses

The samples were submitted to the SPARC BioCentre at the SickKids Hospital in Toronto for liquid chromatography – mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis. Peptide samples (5 μL) were injected onto a Thermo EASY-nLC system (ThermoFisher Scientific) coupled to an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos hybrid quadrupole ion-trap Orbitrap mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific). Samples were trapped (20 μL min−1, 5 min) on a Symmetry C18 Trap Column, 5 μm, 180 μm × 20 mm (Waters) using 97% Mobile Phase A (H2O/0.1% formic acid (FA)), 3% Mobile Phase B (ACN/0.1% FA), then resolved on a Peptide BEH C18 Column, 130 Å, 1.7 μm, 75 μm × 250 mm (Waters) at 35 °C and a flow rate of 250 nL min−1 using a nonlinear gradient (3–20% B, 18 min, 20–35% B, 31 min; 35–100% B, 2 min). All liquid chromatography (LC) and mass spectrometry (MS) solvents used were of Optima LC/MS grade from Fisher Scientific.

The Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer was controlled by Xcalibur software v4.0 (ThermoFisher Scientific). Data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition mode (DDA) using Higher-energy Collisional Dissociation-product dependent-Electron Transfer higher-energy collisional Dissociation (HCD-pd-EThcD). Survey scans (MS1) were acquired in positive ion mode from m/z 300 to 3000 at a resolution of 120,000 with AGC (automatic gain control) set to 4 e5 and a maximum injection time of 50 ms. Multiply charged precursor ions were priority filtered for highest charge state and lowest m/z, isolated using a quadrupole window of m/z 2 and fragmented using HCD with a NCE (normalized collision energy) set to 28%. Fragment ion scans (MS2) were acquired from m/z 135 up to 2000 at a resolution of 15,000, with AGC set to 5 e4, a maximum injection time of 22 ms, and pd-EThcD triggering by ≥1 of the following oxonium ion (Top 20 product ion) fragments: 163.0600, Hexose; 138.0545, HexNAc fragment; 204.0867, HexNAc; 366.1396, HexNAc-Hex; 147.0658, Fucose; +/− 0.05 m/z. Oxonium-dependent precursor ions were isolated using a quadrupole window of m/z 2 or 3, and fragmented using EThcD with 3 separate scans acquired at a NCE of 25%, 50%, and 75%. Fragment ion scans (MS2) were acquired from m/z 120 up to 2000 at a resolution of 15,000 with AGC set to 2 e5 and a maximum injection time of 22 ms. Dynamic exclusion was set to 15 s, cycle time of 3.5 s, and lock mass ion was enabled at m/z 445.12003.

Glycopeptides from individual bands were pooled for MS runs as per pattern shown on Fig. S2, returning 3 data series per biological replica for each isolate. Preliminary analyses were done to assess that pooling 2–3 samples prior to MS analysis did not result in loss of data, obtaining similar numbers of scans and glycoform proportions (Fig. S19) as single samples. All pools also returned similar numbers of HCD (18,000-23,000) and ETD (6000-11,000) scans in most samples (Fig. S19), with 20,000–25,100 total scans per dataset (except Q0-3B, ~16,000), thus ensuring that outputs can be compared across samples.

Glycoproteomic data analyses

To analyze the MS datasets received as. RAW files, two different versions of the specialized glycopeptide analysis software GlycoPAT were used following the workflow shown in Fig. S4. Both versions are coded and run in MATLAB (downloadable at: https://www.mathworks.com/products/matlab.html). GlycoPAT v1.0 (downloadable at: https://sourceforge.net/projects/glycopat/) requires each dataset to be pre-converted to .mzXML format using the program MSConvert (downloadable at: https://proteowizard.sourceforge.io/download.html). GlycoPAT v1.0 also requires theoretical glycopeptides lists which were created using R software and that encompass peptides of 25 amino acids maximum surrounding each predicted N-glycosylation site and accounting for cuts with both trypsin and chymotrypsin, and for the presence of any of the 55 glycoforms selected. GlycoPAT v2.0 (Developper’s unsupported version, provided by Dr. Sriram Neelamegham, University at Buffalo, (Neelamegham 2025)) runs with. RAW format files and with pre-converted .mzML file format and automatically generates the theoretical glycopeptide list for each run. For both analyses, the false discovery rate was <1% and the ensemble score was set at >2 times the decoy score. The processed data were then compiled and analysed using Excel- and R studio-based graph templates. Spectra were manually annotated using GpAnnotate, a program accessible at https://sourceforge.net/projects/gpannotate/files/Example%20Files/ and compatible with GlycoPAT outputs (Cheng et al. 2020).

Since all GlycoPAT analyses are gp120-sequence specific, we deglycosylated 4 samples (EndoH followed by PNGase F) and analysed the resulting peptides under standard proteomics conditions (Struwe et al. 2018) to control for the presence of contaminants and their impact on GlycoPAT output. The data were searched against human and bovine genomes and revealed usual contaminants (BSA, keratine, fibrinogen, from expression system and user), but none of the sequences matched those of the gp120 glycopeptide candidates. This ascertains that the glycopeptides reported after GlycoPAT analysis stem from gp120.

Statistics for GlycoPAT analyses

A two-sample t-test was performed with Bonferroni correction to compare average glycoform group counts between B4 and Q0, while Cohen’s h was calculated to compare the glycoform group proportions because it quantifies effect size directly, allowing for an understanding of the practical significance of proportion differences regardless of sample size.

For site-specific analysis, sequences were previously aligned with HXB2 HIV clone from the Los Alamos HIV database to allow comparison of data across corresponding sites in Q0 and B4 samples. All v2.0 data for B4 were pooled, and all v2.0 data for Q0 were pooled. A two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons was performed based on the glycoforms found at each aligned N-glycosylation site for all GlycoPAT v2.0 data.

Sequence alignments and structural modeling

The amino acid sequences of Q0, B4 and HXB2 were first aligned using MultAlin software (http://multalin.toulouse.inra.fr/multalin/multalin.html). Areas of ambiguous alignments were corrected after structural examination. For this, the structures of Q0, B4 and HXB2 (Gallo et al. 1984; Ratner et al. 1985) gp120 were modeled using the Swiss modeling software (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/). While over 50 templates were found for each, we selected the models obtained using template 7n6u.1.A for trimeric envelope glycoprotein gp160. This reflected that our dataset comprises data from un-cleaved gp160 and gp120 oligomers. In this template the trimer is depicted in state U1 bound to small molecule HIV-1 entry molecule BMS-378806. PyMol was used for structure visualization and to highlight the location and numbers of glycoforms on the amino acid sequence and structure, as well to identify equivalent glycosylation sites in areas where sequence conservation was limited.

Lectin microarray

For the lectin microarray analysis, the samples underwent a buffer exchange to render them compatible with the required fluorescence labeling on amine groups. For this, 200 μl of virion lysate and 200 μl of a 0.25% w/v solution of Empigen BB in 100 mM ABC (pH 8.0) were added to a Centrifugal Filter Unit (Millipore) with 50 kDa cut off and the samples were spun at 9300 x g at 4 °C until ~100 μl of sample remained in the retaining cup. The samples were washed again with 200 μL of a 0.25% v/v solution of Empigen BB in 100 mM ABC. The samples were then washed once with 200 μl of PBS (137 mM NaCl, 2.6 mM KCl, 8 mM Na2HPO4, 1.4 M KH2PO4) at pH 6.8 containing 0.1% Triton-X and washed 4 times with 200 μl of PBS containing 0.08% Triton. The samples were spun down to 100 μl at each round. The 100 μl of sample remaining in retention cup was collected and 10 μl were run on 10% SDS-PAGE for quality control.10 μg of each sample was labeled with Alexa Fluor 555-NHS (Thermo Fisher) in Dr. Lara Mahal’s lab (University of Alberta) using standard procedures (Pilobello et al. 2007; Bojar et al. 2021; Tsui et al. 2024). Protein concentrations were determined by DC Protein Assay (Bio-Rad™) per manufacturer’s protocol. A reference was prepared by pooling 10 μg of each sample together and labeling with Alexa Fluor 647-NHS (Thermo Fisher) to enable a direct comparison of the relative binding affinity between samples on the same array (Pilobello et al. 2007; Tsui et al. 2024). Print conditions, hybridization conditions, and the print list for our lectin microarrays are shown in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4. For each lectin microarray experiment, only lectins that were active on >33% of all arrays were considered in the analysis. Log2 values of the average signals for each lectin were median-normalized over the individual subarray in each channel. Hierarchical clustering using the Pearson Correlation Coefficient and heatmap generation were performed in RStudio (Build 446). Data annotation was done using known specificities of the lectins (Bojar et al. 2021). A two-tailed Welch’s t-test was used to calculate p-values. All statistical tests were performed using Microsoft Excel.
