Background

Branchial cleft anomalies result from incomplete obliteration of the branchial apparatus during embryonic development. Among these, second branchial cleft anomalies are by far the most frequent, representing nearly 90% of all cases, typically manifesting as cysts, sinuses, or fistulas along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle [1, 2].

These anomalies are usually unilateral. Bilateral involvement is exceptionally rare, reported in fewer than 2% of cases, and is most commonly associated with syndromic conditions, particularly branchio-oto-renal (BOR) syndrome, which is characterized by branchial anomalies, hearing loss, and renal malformations [3, 4].

We herein describe an extremely rare case of a bilateral, non-syndromic second branchial cleft fistula in a child, emphasizing the importance of careful diagnostic evaluation and complete surgical management.

Case presentation

An 8-year-old Moroccan boy, born to non-consanguineous parents, presented to our department with a bilateral congenital cervical lesion that had been present since early childhood. The lesions were characterized by intermittent mucopurulent discharge and recurrent local infections, particularly during episodes of upper respiratory tract infection. The child had no previous medical or surgical history, and there was no family history of congenital malformations, hearing loss, or renal disease.

Clinical examination

On presentation, two symmetrical, low-lying paramedian fistulous openings were observed near the suprasternal notch (Fig. 1A). Thick purulent material was actively draining from both orifices.

A Preoperative view showing bilateral low-lying paramedian external openings with active mucopurulent discharge before targeted antibiotic therapy. B Preoperative appearance after antibiogram-guided antibiotic treatment, showing complete resolution of inflammation and absence of discharge

A swab culture and antibiogram were performed, and the patient was started on targeted antibiotic therapy. Following treatment, there was complete resolution of local inflammation and discharge, creating optimal conditions for surgical excision (Fig. 1B).

No cervical masses or other anomalies were palpable, and the rest of the physical examination was unremarkable.

Investigations

Given the bilateral presentation, a complete work-up was performed to rule out syndromic associations:

No additional anomalies were identified, thus ruling out BOR syndrome and other genetic conditions.

Surgical management

Under general anesthesia, both tracts were injected with methylene blue dye to delineate their pathways. Dissection revealed that each tract followed the classic anatomic course of a second branchial cleft fistula, passing between the internal and external carotid arteries and terminating in the tonsillar fossae.

To ensure complete excision of the internal openings, a bilateral tonsillectomy was performed during the same procedure. Both tracts were excised en bloc through separate transverse cervical incisions, with meticulous dissection to avoid injury to adjacent neurovascular structures (Fig. 2).

Intraoperative view demonstrating bilateral dissection of the fistulous tracts extending toward the tonsillar fossae before complete excision and simultaneous tonsillectomy

The wounds were closed in layers with absorbable sutures, and a compressive dressing was applied.

Outcome and follow-up

The postoperative period was uneventful, with no wound infection or complications. At six-month follow-up, the patient remained completely asymptomatic, with no recurrence of discharge or infection and excellent cosmetic results.

Discussion

Second branchial cleft anomalies arise from incomplete involution of the branchial apparatus during the fourth to seventh weeks of gestation [5]. They may present as cysts, sinuses, or complete fistulas, depending on the degree of persistence. The classic external opening is located along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, whereas the internal opening is typically in the tonsillar fossa [6].

Rarity of bilateral cases

Bilateral branchial cleft fistulas are extremely uncommon, accounting for less than 2% of all branchial anomalies [7]. When present, they are strongly associated with syndromes, such as BOR syndrome, which involves branchial anomalies, hearing impairment, and renal malformations caused by mutations in the EYA1 gene [8]. Therefore, a systematic preoperative evaluation including audiological and renal investigations is mandatory in all bilateral cases.

In our patient, absence of hearing loss, renal anomalies, and other dysmorphic features confirmed the non-syndromic nature of this anomaly, making the case exceptionally rare.

Management considerations

The definitive treatment for branchial cleft fistulas is complete surgical excision of the tract, including both the internal and external openings. Incomplete excision is associated with a high recurrence rate and persistent infections [9]. Preoperative imaging, such as ultrasonography or fistulography, may be useful for mapping the tract, although careful intraoperative dissection remains the most reliable approach.

In our case, methylene blue injection provided clear visualization of the tract, facilitating safe dissection. A single-stage bilateral procedure was chosen, which has been reported as safe and effective when performed by experienced surgeons [10].

Role of tonsillectomy

The internal opening of a complete second branchial cleft fistula is typically located in the tonsillar fossa. Failure to remove this opening during surgery is a major cause of recurrence [8]. For this reason, simultaneous tonsillectomy is strongly recommended, especially in pediatric cases or when the tract is complete, as in our patient.

Our approach of performing a single-stage bilateral excision with tonsillectomy allowed for complete removal and optimal healing without complications.

Literature review

A search of the English language literature revealed very few reports of bilateral, non-syndromic second branchial cleft fistulas. Most cases were linked to BOR syndrome or other genetic disorders [3, 4, 7]. Our case adds to this limited body of evidence and emphasizes the importance of considering a non-syndromic etiology even in bilateral presentations.

Conclusion

Bilateral second branchial cleft fistulas are exceptionally rare, and their occurrence in the absence of syndromic features is even more unusual. This case underscores the importance of thorough diagnostic evaluation, including hearing and renal assessments, to exclude associated anomalies. Complete surgical excision remains the gold standard for treatment, providing both definitive cure and prevention of recurrent infections.
