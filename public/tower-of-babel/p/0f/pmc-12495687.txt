Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes attenuate hepatic stellate cell activation through miR-223-3p-mediated mitophagy
Authors: Lijie Ma, Weidong Weng, Jie Chen, Hongdi Wu, Jiajia Liang, Fengbin Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLiver fibrosis is a common pathological process in chronic liver diseases and effective treatments are lacking. The activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) is a critical step in the development of liver fibrosis. Our previous research confirmed that bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (BMSC-exosomes) could regulate the level of miR-223-3p to alleviate intrahepatic inflammation, but whether they contribute to the protection of the liver against fibrosis remains unknown.MethodsIn this study, the antifibrotic function of BMSC-exosomes was validated through cell experiments. JS-1 cells (murine HSC line) were used as activated cells to simulate liver fibrosis. TGF-β is a stimulating factor for JS-1 cell activation. BMSC-exosomes were isolated via ultracentrifugation.ResultsOur results demonstrated that BMSC-exosomes internalized by JS-1 cells attenuated TGF-β-induced HSC activation and promoted HSC apoptosis. Moreover, BMSC-exosomes significantly reversed the upregulation of miR-223-3p levels and mitophagy induced by TGF-β. Luciferase activity reporter analysis further verified that hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 1 (HMGCS1) was a downstream target of miR-223-3p. By transfecting a miR-223-3p inhibitor, we found that downregulating miR-223-3p could increase the expression level of HMGCS1 and alleviate mitophagy, thereby reducing TGF-β-induced HSC activation and promoting HSC apoptosis.ConclusionsThese results suggest that BMSC-exosomes may have antifibrotic effects. The mechanism may be related to regulating miR-223-3p in HSCs to target HMGCS1 and mitophagy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12920-025-02228-y.

Introduction

Liver fibrosis, a necessary course in the development of chronic liver disease to cirrhosis, is usually caused by chronic liver injury. Its etiology includes viral hepatitis, metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), autoimmune liver disease, etc [1]. In recent years, the incidence of chronic liver disease has increased globally and has become a common cause of human death [2]. Moreover, despite extensive experimental studies of antifibrotic drugs, effective therapeutic approaches are still lacking clinically [3]. The pathogenesis of liver fibrosis is complex and involves the interaction of multiple cells and molecules. The activation of HSCs is a critical step in the development of liver fibrosis. Activated HSCs are capable of secreting large amounts of extracellular matrix (ECM), leading to liver structural destruction and dysfunction [4]. Thus, blocking the activation of HSCs is the key to treating liver fibrosis.

BMSC-exosomes are nanovesicles derived from bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMMSCs), and are characterized by low toxicity, low immunogenicity, and high biocompatibility [5]. BMSC-exosomes are widely used in tissue fibrosis therapy, immune regulation, and tissue regeneration by delivering biological information mediators, such as cytokines and regulatory miRNAs [6]. MicroRNA-223-3p (miR-223-3p), a highly conserved small noncoding RNA, plays an important role in the process of BMSC-exosomes therapy. Li et al. proposed that BMSC exosomes could treat airway remodeling and asthma by regulating miR-223-3p [7]. Additionally, Chen et al. reported that exosomal miR-223-3p from BMMSCs was able to target histone deacetylase 2 (HDAC2) and downregulate signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) phosphorylation to alleviate HBx-induced ferroptosis in podocytes [8]. In our earlier investigation, we established that BMSC-exosomes possess therapeutic potential for autoimmune hepatitis within an experimental framework. The underlying mechanism may involve the modulation of miR-223-3p concentrations and the expression of STAT3 in both hepatic tissues and macrophages [9]. Nonetheless, it remains to be elucidated whether BMSC-exosomes also play a role in safeguarding the liver from fibrosis. Recent studies have demonstrated that miR-223-3p expression levels were significantly elevated in cirrhotic models [10]. Li et al. reported that neutrophil- or NK cell-derived exosomes could exert antifibrotic effects through miR-223-3p, but the specific mechanisms remain to be investigated [11, 12].

Mitophagy is an intracellular self-depleting mechanism that degrades damaged or excess mitochondria via autophagy to maintain the stability of mitochondrial quantity and quality, thereby ensuring normal cellular function [13]. The liver is a crucial organ for systemic material metabolism. These complex physiological processes are performed mainly by hepatocytes, HSCs, Kupffer cells/macrophages, etc [14]. As the main organelles for energy production in these liver cells, mitochondria are closely related to the pathogenesis of liver diseases because of the dysregulation of their homeostasis, such as mitophagy [15, 16]. Currently, mitophagy has been reported to be overactivated in fibrotic liver tissue [17]. Additionally, Lee et al. reported that HSCs could be activated through parkin-mediated mitophagy, thereby promoting the progression of liver fibrosis [18]. Therefore, regulating mitophagy to reduce HSC activation is expected to become an important target for the treatment of liver fibrosis.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

In order to ensure sufficient cell numbers and effective Biological functional activity, we referred to previous studies and used passage 6 BMMSCs in our experiment. BMMSCs from C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (China). Cyagen Biosciences provided phenotype validation results for the BMMSCs. The cell lines were maintained in α-MEM (Sigma, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum sourced from Gibco (USA), along with 100 units/ml of penicillin and 0.1 mg/ml of streptomycin (Sigma, USA). Murine HSC JS-1 cells were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA), and cultured in DMEM (Gibco) with the same supplementation of 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 units/ml penicillin, and 100 units/ml streptomycin. All cultures were incubated in a Humidified environment at 37 °C with a CO2 concentration of 5%.

Extraction and identification of BMSC-exosomes

The process of extracting and identifying exosomes was conducted in accordance with previously established protocols [9]. When the BMMSCs were cultured in culture bottles to 80% confluence, the culture medium was changed to serum-free medium (Gibco, USA). After 24 h of cultivation, the supernatant was extracted and gradient centrifugation was performed at 4℃ (300 g 10 min; 2000 g 10 min; 10,000 g 30 min). The final supernatants were transferred into an exosome extraction centrifuge tube and subjected to ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g (4℃ for 70 min). The remaining sediment were then resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and underwent an additional ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g (4℃ for 60 min). Finally, the remaining precipitates were BMSC-exosomes. The characteristics of the exosomes were identified via western blotting and electron microscopy. As confirmed and reported in our previous research [9], the isolated exosomes showed cup-shaped membrane vesicles with diameters ranging from 40 to 100 nm. The results from the Western blot analysis demonstrated that these exosomes tested positive for the markers TSG101, CD9, CD63, and CD81, while they showed a negative result for cytochrome c (Abcam, UK).

Treatment of JS-1 cells with BMSC-exosomes

On the basis of previous studies, we constructed HSC activation models by stimulating JS-1 cells with different concentrations of TGF-β (MedChemExpress, China) and intervention times [19–21]. The most appropriate modeling schemes were screened by HSC proliferation and activation indicators. Finally, we constructed an HSC activation model by stimulating JS-1 cells with TGF-β (5 ng/ml for 12 h). TGF-β-treated JS-1 cells were respectively coincubated with BMSC-exosomes (100 µg/ml) or control medium for 12 h. After treatment, the cells were harvested for further experiments.

BMSC-exosomes uptake experiment

BMSC-exosomes were labeled utilizing the PKH67 Green Fluorescent Cell Linker Kit (Sigma, USA) in accordance with the instructions provided by the manufacturer. To eliminate any unbound dye from the labeled BMSC-exosomes, ultracentrifugation was performed at 100,000 g for 70 min at a temperature of 4 °C. The PKH67-labeled BMSC-exosomes were resuspended in 100 µl of PBS. For the uptake experiments, the green fluorescent dye PKH67-labeled exosomes were incultured with JS-1 cells for 6 h and Hoechst 33,342 was used to label nuclei of JS-1 cells according to previous literature [22]. Ultimately, the uptake of the BMSC-exosomes was analyzed using a confocal microscopy system (Olympus, Japan).

JS-1 cell transfection and intervention

The synthetic miR-223-3p inhibitor and inhibitor control were obtained from RiboBio (Guangdong, China). The synthetic miR-223-3p inhibitor and inhibitor control were transfected into JS-1 cells via Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) respectively when the cells reached 80% confluence according to the manufacturer’s procedures. After transfection, two JS-1 cell lines were obtained: JS-1miR−223−3p(−) and JS-1null. To further observe the effects of miR-223-3p on HSCs, transfected JS-1 cells were incubated with control medium or TGF-β (5 ng/ml) for 12 h, respectively. After treatment, the cells were harvested for further experiments.

Cell proliferation assay

JS-1 cells were cultured in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 10^3 cells per well and subjected to various treatments. The cell proliferation rates were detected with a Cell Counting Kit-8 (Beyotime, China), according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Apoptosis analysis

Apoptotic cells were analyzed via an Annexin V-APC/PI double staining procedure. Briefly, JS-1 cells were subjected to different groups. The JS-1 cells were digested into single cells via EDTA-free trypsin (MeilunBio, China), and the cells were then stained according to the instructions provided by the Annexin V-APC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (‌eBioscience, USA). The cells were immediately analyzed after 10–15 min by flow cytometry (Becton Dickinson, USA).

Luciferase reporter assay

The luciferase reporter assay was used to confirm the functional relationship between miR-223-3p and the 3’ untranslated region (UTR) of HMGCS1. Firstly, we directly synthesized the miR-223-3p target gene HMGCS1-3’UTR sequence according to the manufacturer’s suggested protocol, named HMGCS1-3’UTR-WT. At the same time, HMGCS1-3’UTR-MUT was synthesized after point mutation of the HMGCS1-3’UTR binding site (information concerning the gene sequences is shown in the Supplementary Figure S1). Then, we packaged the psiCHECK-2 luciferase reporter recombinant plasmids that contain the sequences of HMGCS1-3’UTR-WT and HMGCS1-3’UTR-MUT, respectively. Finally, 293 T cells (SAIOS, China) reaching 80% confluence were cotransfected with miR-223-3p mimics/mimics-NC, HMGCS1-3’UTR-WT/HMGCS1-3’UTR-MUT Luciferase constructs via Lipofectamine 3000. The Luciferase activity was assessed 12 h following the transfection using a luciferase reporter assay system (Promega, USA).

Western blot analysis

In accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol, total protein was extracted from JS-1 cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China), and quantified via the BCA method (HyClone-Pierce, China). Proteins were separated via gradient sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Immunoblotting was performed using specific primary and secondary antibodies (antibody information is shown in Supplementary Table S1-2). The intensities of the reactive bands were analyzed using chemiluminescent HRP substrates (Millipore, USA) and ImageJ software (GE, China).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction(qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells with TRIzol reagent (Sigma, USA). The expression of miR-223-3p and HMGCS1 were detected using a Hiscript QRT SuperMix for qPCR Kit (Vazyme, China) and an AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix Kit (Vazyme, China) as described by the manufacturer. U6 served as an internal control for the quantification of miR-223-3p, while GAPDH acted as the reference gene for HMGCS1. The relative expression level of each target gene was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCT method vis a VII7 real-time system (Applied Biosystems, USA). The primers used for qRT-PCR are shown in Supplementary Table S3.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0 (IBM, USA) and GraphPad Prism 5 (GraphPad Software, USA), with all data represented as mean ± standard deviations. The significance among multiple groups was assessed utilizing one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05. Each parameter was evaluated with a minimum of three replicates.

Results

Validation of the HSC activation model and selection of modeling conditions in vitro

JS-1 cells, as activated cells, were used to simulate liver fibrosis. TGF-β was often used to stimulate HSC activation to establish fibrosis models in vitro. In previous studies, the conditions for constructing an HSC activation model with TGF-β were inconsistent [23, 24]. Therefore, we activated JS-1 cells with different concentrations of TGF-β for different durations of stimulation. Then, we screened the most suitable modeling conditions through proliferation assays of JS-1 cells and Western blot analysis of fibrosis-related proteins. As shown in Fig. 1A, 5 ng/ml TGF-β significantly increased the proliferative activity of JS-1 cells compared with that of control cells at both 12 and 24 h of stimulation (P < 0.05, respectively). Moreover, the proliferative activity of JS-1 cells also progressively increased with increasing TGF-β concentration. A similar trend was also observed for the protein expression of α-SMA and collagen I (Col-1)(Fig. 1B-C). On the basis of the above results, to avoid the influence of JS-1 overactivation on the experiment, we ultimately constructed an HSC activation model with 5 ng/ml TGF-β and a stimulation time of 12 h.

BMSC-exosomes attenuated TGF-β-induced HSC activation and promoted HSC apoptosis

The effects of BMSC-exosomes on HSCs are shown in Fig. 2. Administration of TGF-β significantly increased the proliferative activity of JS-1 cells and the protein expression of α-SMA and collagen I (Fig. 2A-C). Furthermore, treatment with TGF-β obviously reduced the rate of apoptosis in JS-1 cells. However, the effects mediated by TGF-β described above were partly abolished when BMSC-exosomes were involved (Fig. 2D-E). The above results demonstrate that BMSC-exosomes attenuated TGF-β-induced HSC activation and promoted HSC apoptosis, which may contribute to the treatment of liver fibrosis.

BMSC-exosomes internalized by HSCs alleviated the upregulation of miR-223-3p and mitophagy induced by TGF-β

In order to illustrate the internalization of BMSC-derived exosomes, these exosomes were marked with PKH67 prior to being co-cultured with JS-1 cells. The results presented in Fig. 3A reveal that PKH67-labeled exosomes exhibited significant perinuclear accumulation in JS-1 cells (as indicated by the white box), with 50% of cells displaying cytoplasmic green fluorescence intensity exceeding background levels. This indicates that PKH67-labeled exosomes can be internalized into the cytoplasmic compartment of JS-1 cells. The mechanism by which BMSC-exosomes modulate JS-1 cells was further investigated. The qRT-PCR results revealed that miR-223-3p expression was significantly elevated after TGF-β-induced activation of JS-1 cells, whereas BMSC-exosomes obviously downregulated miR-223-3p expression. However, a comparison between the NC and the TGF-β + BMSC-exos treated group revealed that BMSC-exosomes were unable to reverse miR-223-3p to normal level after TGF-β intervention (Fig. 3B). In addition, Western blot analysis of mitophagy-related proteins revealed that TGF-β could stimulate the upregulation of p-PINK1, p-Parkin, p-ULK1, and LC3B protein expression in JS-1 cells, while BMSC-exosomes could reverse this effect (Fig. 3C-D). These results reveal that BMSC-exosomes can be taken up by HSCs and may exert their effects by downregulating miR-223-3p and inhibiting mitophagy.

HMGCS1 was the downstream target of miR-223-3p

The microRNA database was used to predict downstream targets of miR-223-3p, in order to further explore its mechanism of action (Fig. 4A, http://www.microrna.org). Subsequently, we evaluated a selection of potential candidates and discovered that miR-223-3p considerably reduced both the protein and mRNA expression levels of HMGCS1 in JS-1 cells (Fig. 4B-D). To further ascertain whether HMGCS1 functions as a downstream target of miR-223-3p, we conducted a luciferase activity reporter assay. The findings showed that transfection with miR-223-3p mimics markedly diminished the luciferase activity in cells harboring the HMGCS1-3’UTR-WT luciferase construct. Furthermore, the introduction of mutations at the binding sites negated the inhibitory effect, indicating that miR-223-3p directly interacts with HMGCS1 (Fig. 4E).

Downregulation of miR-223-3p inhibited TGF-β-induced HSC activation and promoted HSC apoptosis

To further confirm whether BMSC-exosomes exert their effects by downregulating miR-223-3p in JS-1 cells. The synthetic miR-223-3p inhibitor and inhibitor control were transfected into JS-1 cells respectively. After transfection, two JS-1 cell lines were obtained: JS-1miR−223−3p(−) and JS-1null. As shown in Fig. 5A, regardless of stimulation with TGF-β, the expression of miR-223-3p was significantly greater in JS-1null cells than in JS-1miR−223−3p(−) cells. Notably, the proliferative activity was significantly increased in both JS-1miR−223−3p(−) and JS-1null cells upon stimulation with TGF-β, but the proliferative activity in JS-1miR−223−3p(−) cells was lower than that in JS-1null cells(Fig. 5B). A similar trend was also observed for the protein expression of α-SMA and collagen I (Fig. 5C-D). In addition, we also found that TGF-β obviously reduced the late and total apoptosis rates in JS-1miR−223−3p(−) and JS-1null cells. However, when the expression of miR-223-3p was inhibited in JS-1 cells, these effects of TGF-β were counteracted (Fig. 5E-F), further supporting the possibility that BMSC-exosomes may inhibit TGF-β-induced HSC activation and promote HSC apoptosis by downregulating miR-223-3p.

MiR-223-3p regulated HSC activation and apoptosis by targeting the HMGCS1 and mitophagy pathways

To determine whether miR-223-3p exerts its effects via mitophagy and HMGCS1, we examined the mRNA level of HMGCS1 via qRT-PCR and the expression of mitophagy-related proteins using western blotting in JS-1 cells. As shown in Fig. 6A, the administration of TGF-β resulted in a significant reduction in the mRNA levels of HMGCS1 in JS-1 cells. Conversely, the application of a miR-223-3p inhibitor mitigated the TGF-β-induced decrease in HMGCS1 expression. Furthermore, western blot analysis examining proteins associated with mitophagy revealed that TGF-β promotes the enhanced expression of p-PINK1, p-Parkin, p-ULK1, and LC3B in both in JS-1miR−223−3p(−) and JS-1null cells. However, upon the use of miR-223-3p inhibitors, these effects were partially attenuated (Fig. 6B-C), indicating that miR-223-3p potentially regulates HSC activation and apoptosis through targeting the HMGCS1 and mitophagy pathways.

Discussion

HSCs play a crucial role in the progression of liver fibrosis, as their activated forms synthesize α-SMA and collagen I, which are recognized as key indicators of HSC activation. TGF-β, an important factor in HSC activation, has been widely used to model liver fibrosis in vitro [25]. Our study indicated that BMSC-exosomes significantly promoted HSC apoptosis and inhibited TGF-β-induced HSC proliferation and the expression of fibrosis-related proteins. These results suggested that BMSC-exosomes markedly inhibited HSC activation. In contrast to previous observations on exosome-targeted miRNA delivery, we found that BMSC-exosomes obviously downregulated miR-223-3p expression in HSCs [26]. We speculate that this phenomenon may be related to the presence of ceRNAs or signaling molecule in BMSC-exosomes, as reported by previous studies [27, 28]. Emerging evidence indicates that exosomes can regulate gene expression in recipient cells by delivering Cas9 ribonucleoprotein complexes, which contain nucleases [29]. Therefore, we also propose the hypothesis that BMSC-exosomes may downregulate the expression of miRNA-223-3p in HSCs by delivering natural cleavage enzymes targeting this miRNA. Future research is essential to verify the possible mechanisms mentioned above, which will be an important direction for our research. Furthermore, our experiments manifested that downregulation of miR-223-3p could promote HMGCS1 expression and inhibit mitophagy, which might inhibit the activation of HSCs and exert an antifibrotic effect.

Certain miRNAs, such as miR-122, miR-223 and miR-29, are specifically expressed or enriched in several types of hepatic cells or in the circulation and play important roles in the pathogenesis of liver diseases [30]. MiR-223-3p is highly conserved during evolution and has anti-inflammatory effects, participating in the pathogenesis of liver fibrosis [31]. Previous studies also reported that miR-223-3p was significantly upregulated in fibrosis models, which was consistent with our experimental results [32]. In addition, our findings showed that downregulating the level of miR-223-3p in HSCs could alleviate HSC activation and promote HSC apoptosis. Also, Li et al. demonstrated that downregulation of miR-223-3p levels inhibited LEMD3 expression, thereby alleviating carbon tetrachloride-induced liver fibrosis in mice [10]. Similarly, Xu et al. confirmed that upregulation of miR-223-3p expression promoted HSC proliferation, activation, and fibrosis development [33]. However, different results were discovered by Wang et al. They found that miR-223-3p could exert an antifibrotic effect by inhibiting the transcriptional activating factor TAZ [11]. The reasons for the discrepancy among these reports may be the different types of experimental cells, modeling methods, and liver diseases used [31, 34].

HMGCS1, which plays a critical role in liver cholesterol biosynthesis, was recognized as a potential target of miR-223-3p through both TargetScan analysis and a dual luciferase reporter assay in our study (http://www.targetscan.org). Recently, HMGCS1 was confirmed to play an important role in hepatocyte cholesterol homeostasis and was closely associated with liver diseases such as steatohepatitis and liver cancer [35, 36]. Interestingly, our experiments found that HMGCS1 was significantly downregulated in activated HSCs. Moreover, the expression of HMGCS1 progressively increased as HSC activation was inhibited. The above results suggested that HMGCS1 might have an antifibrotic effect. In our experiments, HSC activation was shown to be accompanied by elevated levels of mitophagy, which was found to be strongly associated with liver fibrosis [18]. Previous studies also proposed that HMGCS1 had a protective effect on the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria [37]. Therefore, we speculate that the antifibrotic effect of HMGCS1 may be associated with mitophagy, but the underlying mechanism needs further investigation.

The interactions between mitophagy and liver fibrosis are complicated [38]. Wang et al. demonstrated that XBP1 activation significantly inhibited BNIP3-mediated mitophagy in macrophages and exacerbated hepatic fibrosis [39]. However, mitophagy in hepatocytes might induce ferroptosis, leading to the progression of liver fibrosis [40]. These differences are also manifested in fibrosis models of different etiologies. Zhu et al. discovered that melatonin alleviated particulate matter-induced liver fibrosis by inhibiting ROS-mediated mitophagy [41]. However, in a model of carbon tetrachloride-induced liver fibrosis, the apoptosis-related protein BCL-B could induce mitophagy to reverse liver fibrosis [42]. In addition, the specific role of mitophagy in HSCs is also controversial. On the one hand, mitophagy has been suggested to facilitate HSC activation [18]. Our findings also revealed that mitophagy was a profibrogenic mechanism and that the inhibition of mitophagy alleviated HSC activation and liver fibrosis. Similarly, the latest research by Li et al. found that carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) could induce mitophagy to promote HSC activation and liver fibrosis [43]. Conversely, it has been proposed that mitophagy plays a protective role in the development of liver fibrosis. Li et al. proposed that lipotoxic hepatocyte-derived extracellular vesicles promoted the activation of HSCs by inhibiting mitophagy [44]. Consequently, the relationship between mitophagy and HSC activation within the context of liver fibrosis is intricate, influenced by various underlying causes and distinct cellular environments, necessitating additional research to elucidate these interactions.

This study mainly verified the possible antifibrotic effects and potential mechanisms of BMSC-exosomes based on cell models, but there are still some limitations in the research. First, this study does not incorporate in vivo experimental data to validate the distribution, metabolism, biological activity, and side effects of BMSC-exosomes, which may compromise accurate assessment of their therapeutic potential. Future studies can incorporate pharmacokinetic tracing and tissue-specific functional analyses to verify the biological functions of BMSC-exosomes through animal experiments or multidimensional models such as organoids/organ-on-a-chip [45]. Second, the exosomes utilized in this investigation were murine-derived mesenchymal stem cell products. Compared with human counterparts, their composition and functional characteristics are different, which is a critical consideration for cross-species therapeutic extrapolation [46, 47]. Further experimental validation is required to assess whether exosomes derived from human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSC-exosomes) have anti-fibrotic effects under physiologically relevant human conditions. Due to the high conservation of certain biological mechanisms between humans and animals, our experimentally validated novel mechanism of BMSC-exosomes therapy may provide critical insights for subsequent clinical research [48, 49]. Meanwhile, our research findings may also provide new strategies for the engineering modification of hBMSC-exosomes, thereby improving their targeting and therapeutic efficacy [50]. Third, in the experiment exploring the effect of miR-223-3p on mitophagy-related proteins, the inter-group differences in the blot images were not as significant as those in the bar graph. We speculate that this phenomenon could arise from limited inter-group mean value differences combined with low intra-group variability, evidenced by narrow standard deviations. Therefore, the result should be interpreted with caution, as repeated experiments may elevate intra-group variability and diminish inter-group statistical significance [51]. Finally, given the passage-dependent alterations in MSC biological status, exosomal biogenesis and molecular profiling exhibit dynamic variations across different culture generations [52]. Systematic characterization of exosomal cargo through quantitative proteomics and miRNA-seq is therefore required to establish passage-specific molecular signatures and promote mechanism research.

Conclusions

In summary, our research results demonstrated that the administration of BMSC-exosomes promoted HSC apoptosis and inhibited HSC activation. The mechanism may be related to the miR-223-3p-mediated regulation of HMGCS1 and mitophagy.
