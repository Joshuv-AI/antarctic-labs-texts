Risk and protective factors for musculoskeletal injury in youth track and field: a methodological scoping review
Authors: Kalani Weerasinghe, Ranil Jayawardena, Andrew Williams, Andrew P. Hills
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMusculoskeletal injuries are widespread among youth track and field athletes, largely due to the combined effects of growth, maturation, and escalating training loads. Yet, inconsistent definitions and measurement approaches for injuries, and for the factors that may increase or reduce risk, limit the comparability of findings across studies and impede progress toward effective prevention strategies.ObjectivesTo identify risk and protective factors for musculoskeletal injuries in youth track and field athletes, and to examine how these injuries and associated factors have been defined, measured, and aligned with consensus methodological standards.MethodsThis scoping review was conducted in accordance with Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and PRISMA-ScR reporting standards. Five databases (PubMed®, Web of Science®, Scopus®, SPORTDiscus®, and Embase®) were systematically searched from inception to March 2026. Studies were eligible if they included youth track and field athletes (mean age 15-24 years) and reported injury characteristics and/or risk or protective factors. Data were extracted on injury definitions, surveillance methods, exposure metrics, and factor measurement approaches. Methodological alignment with the athletics consensus statement by Timpka et al. was assessed using a structured framework.ResultsA total of 54 studies were included. Injury definitions varied widely, encompassing time-loss, medical-attention, symptom-based, and diagnosis-confirmed approaches, with substantial heterogeneity in surveillance methods and exposure quantification (48 studies). Factors associated with musculoskeletal injury outcomes spanned intrinsic (e.g., previous injury, biomechanics, strength, bone health) and extrinsic domains (e.g., training load, footwear, environment), with diverse measurement techniques (27 studies). Reporting of consensus-based methodological components varied across studies. Injury-definition elements were reported in a greater proportion of studies than exposure and surveillance elements. Variation was observed in the reporting of exposure metrics, prospective monitoring approaches, and the inclusion of overuse injuries across studies.ConclusionsConsiderable methodological heterogeneity exists in youth track and field injury research. Greater adoption of consensus-based definitions, standardised exposure measures, and robust surveillance systems may improve comparability and support the development of injury prevention strategies.Review registrationOpen Science Framework (Registration DOI: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/U4BSY).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01824-4.

Introduction

Track and field comprise a diverse range of disciplines including sprinting, middle- and long-distance running, jumping, throwing and combined events, each placing unique physical and physiological demands on athletes [1]. These activities require maximal force production, repetitive high-impact movements, and complex neuromuscular coordination [2], all of which contribute to a high risk of sport-related injury [3]. A recent systematic review and meta‑analysis reported an injury prevalence of 69.7% and pooled incidence rates of 68.8 injuries per 1,000 registered athletes across training and competition [4]. This elevated injury burden has multidimensional consequences, including persistent physical symptoms and recurrent time‑loss [5], psychological distress [6], and social disruption affecting academic and peer engagement, which may contribute to sport withdrawal [7].

Youth track and field athletes may be particularly vulnerable to musculoskeletal injuries due to the interaction of biological growth, maturation, and increasing training and competition demands during adolescence [8]. Periods of rapid growth may result in temporary imbalances in strength, flexibility, and neuromuscular control, potentially increasing injury susceptibility [9]. These injuries can result in substantial time‑loss from training and competition, negatively affecting athlete development and performance during a particularly sensitive developmental period [10].

Musculoskeletal injury risk in youth athletes is widely recognised as multifactorial, arising from a complex interaction of intrinsic and extrinsic determinants [11]. Intrinsic factors include biological and individual characteristics such as age, sex, growth and maturation status, biomechanical characteristics, muscle strength, flexibility, and previous injury history [12]. During adolescence, rapid growth and skeletal immaturity may increase vulnerability to injury due to physiological changes in bone, muscle, and connective tissues, particularly around periods of peak growth velocity [13]. Extrinsic factors related to the training and competition environment may also influence injury risk. These include training load, sport specialization, coaching practices, equipment use, and competition schedules [12]. High training volumes and intensities have been associated with greater injury occurrence in youth track and field athletes [14]. Huxley and colleagues reported that injured youth athletes undertook higher-intensity and higher-volume training than their uninjured peers [14].

Epidemiological research on musculoskeletal injuries in youth track and field is fundamentally shaped by how injuries are defined and measured [15], yet the literature demonstrates substantial variability in operational definitions [16]. Commonly used definitions include time‑loss injuries, medical‑attention injuries, and all‑complaint or self‑reported injuries [17], each capturing different aspects of the injury spectrum. Surveillance methods also differ widely [4], ranging from athlete self-report to medical-record and coach-report systems, resulting in differences in data capture and accuracy. Exposure quantification similarly lacks uniformity, with studies differing in the metrics used and in surveillance windows, limiting comparability of injury incidence and risk estimates.

To address such heterogeneity, sport science researchers have developed consensus statements recommending standardized definitions and surveillance procedures. For track and field specifically, the consensus statement by Timpka et al., [18] provides explicit definitions, structured guidance for characterizing injury nature and cause, and recommended procedures for exposure and severity recording, including baseline variables and appropriate denominators for calculating incidence and prevalence [18]. Adoption of these procedures may improve the comparability and utility of epidemiological evidence for injury prevention [19]. However, recent systematic and meta‑analytic work [4] indicates that many track and field studies continue to use heterogeneous definitions and inconsistent surveillance methods, with few explicitly adhering to the Timpka et al. recommendations [18].

Despite the growing body of research on musculoskeletal injuries in youth track and field athletes, the literature remains fragmented, with limited comprehensive mapping of risk and protective factors, injury definitions, and measurement approaches. This scoping review therefore aims to systematically map the tools, systems, and methodological approaches used to collect injury epidemiological data and associated factors in this population. Specifically, it aims to (i) identify reported risk and protective factors, (ii) examine how injuries and associated factors are defined and measured, (iii) assess alignment with consensus methodological recommendations, and (iv) identify key gaps requiring standardisation in youth track and field injury research.

Methods

Methodological approach

The protocol for this scoping review was prepared in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidance for scoping reviews [20]. The protocol was prospectively published on the Open Science Framework (registration DOI: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/U4BSY), and no deviations from the preregistered protocol were made during the conduct of this review. The review followed the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley [21], with further enhancements suggested by Levac and colleagues [22]. The review was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (Supplementary Material 1) [23].

Eligibility criteria

Population: Youth athletes participating in track and field (sprints, hurdles, middle/long-distance, jumps, throws, and combined events) with a mean age of 15-24 years, consistent with the United Nations definition of youth [24]. Inclusion up to 24 years reflects the prolonged physical, biological, and athletic development in track and field athletes [25], including ongoing musculoskeletal maturation, event-specific specialisation, and delayed peak performance in several disciplines [26]. Athletes within this age range often compete in youth or under-23 pathways and demonstrate distinct training loads, exposure patterns, and injury risk profiles compared with senior athletes [27]. Studies with broader age ranges were included only if youth-specific data were reported or extractable. Findings should therefore be interpreted across a developmental continuum rather than as a single homogeneous age group. Concept: Empirical investigation of risk factors, protective factors, or both, in relation to musculoskeletal injuries (acute or overuse) and/or injury-related outcomes (incidence, prevalence, burden, or time-loss). Context: Any setting (school, club, regional, national), training or competition, any country.

Study designs

Any study reporting injury characteristics and/or risk or protective factors using any injury data collection method was eligible. Narrative reviews, editorials, letters, case reports, conference abstracts without full texts, and animal or in vitro studies were excluded. Only English-language studies published from database inception to 1 March 2026 were included.

Information sources and search strategy

A three-step search strategy was employed: (i) an initial limited search to refine search terms, (ii) comprehensive searches of relevant databases, and (iii) reference and citation tracking of included studies. The search was conducted in the PubMed® (U.S. National Library of Medicine, USA), Web of Science® (v.5.4; Thomson Reuters, USA), SciVerse Scopus® (Elsevier Properties S.A., USA), SPORTDiscus® (EBSCOhost, USA) and Embase® (Elsevier, USA). Both controlled vocabulary terms and free-text keywords were used. The search strategy was based on three core concepts: (i) population (track and field athletes), (ii) musculoskeletal injury, and (iii) epidemiology and risk-related constructs (incidence, prevalence, risk factors). In PubMed, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were combined with title and abstract keywords using Boolean operators to maximise search sensitivity. In Embase, equivalent Emtree terms were incorporated alongside free-text keywords. In Web of Science, the search was conducted using the Topic (TS) field, while in Scopus the TITLE-ABS-KEY fields were used. In SPORTDiscus, subject headings and keywords were combined using database-specific operators. Truncation, phrase searching, and Boolean logic were adapted for each database to ensure comprehensive retrieval of relevant literature. Complete, database‑specific search strings, including controlled vocabulary terms and free‑text keywords, are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Study selection

Two reviewers (KW and RJ) independently conducted the study selection process. All retrieved records were first exported to Covidence (v.2024; Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia), where duplicate entries were identified and removed. Screening then proceeded in two stages: initial title and abstract screening, followed by full-text assessment, both conducted in accordance with the above-mentioned eligibility criteria. Calibration exercises were undertaken prior to each screening phase to ensure consistency between reviewers. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer (AW, APH) consulted when consensus could not be reached.

Data extraction

Following full-text selection, eligible studies were imported into EndNote (v.2025.3.1; Clarivate Analytics, USA). A JBI-aligned data-charting form was piloted and refined iteratively. Two reviewers (KW and RJ) independently extracted data, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. Extracted data included: (i) bibliographic details (author, year, country, study design); (ii) participant characteristics (age, sex, athletic discipline, performance level, sample size); (iii) injury definitions (type, ascertainment source, anatomical location, pathology classification, and severity measures); (iv) exposure metrics (athlete exposures, training/competition hours, sessions, mileage); (v) risk and protective factors (definitions, measurement methods, timing, and thresholds); and (vi) outcomes (incidence, prevalence, burden, and reported effect estimates with confidence intervals). Where multiple publications reported on the same surveillance system or dataset, the most recent and/or methodologically comprehensive report was included.

Assessment of alignment with athletics consensus statement and methodological gaps requiring standardisation

Methodological alignment with the athletics consensus statement [18] was assessed using a structured framework across two components. Part I (Injury-related methodological standards) evaluated injury definitions, classification, and severity reporting, while Part II (Exposure, surveillance, and reporting standards) assessed exposure measurement, surveillance design, and reporting practices. Studies were categorised as aligned, partially aligned, or not aligned based on predefined criteria. All studies reporting epidemiological injury data were included in the alignment assessment, while studies not involving injury surveillance were excluded from scoring but retained in the descriptive synthesis. Two reviewers (KW, RJ) independently assessed each study, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. Inter-rater agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa statistic (κ = 0.78). Detailed descriptions of the alignment framework, scoring criteria, and domain-level definitions are provided in Supplementary Material 3.

Methodological characteristics were examined by identifying domains that were not consistently reported or showed variation in alignment with consensus recommendations. These domains were grouped into key themes, including variation in injury definitions, differences in exposure metrics, approaches to overuse injury surveillance, and the use of classification systems. Findings were synthesised descriptively to summarise patterns in reporting and methodological approaches across studies.

Data synthesis

Extracted data were synthesised descriptively and narratively in accordance with JBI guidance for scoping reviews. Studies were grouped by design, injury definition and surveillance approach, exposure metrics, and investigated risk or protective factors. Injury definitions, measurement methods, and risk-factor operationalisation were compared to identify methodological approaches. These findings were integrated with the consensus-alignment assessment to characterise methodological patterns and areas of variability in approaches across the literature. Consistent with JBI and PRISMA-ScR guidance, no formal risk-of-bias or critical appraisal was undertaken, as the review aimed to map the breadth and methodological characteristics of the literature rather than evaluate causal effects. Given the heterogeneity in study designs, data sources, and outcome definitions, a single appraisal tool was not applied. Instead, methodological alignment with established athletics consensus standards was assessed to describe reporting practices and methodological characteristics across studies.

Results

Study selection

The database searches yielded a total of 9,135 records, comprising 3,391 from Scopus®, 1,862 from PubMed®, 1,286 from Web of Science®, 1,661 from SPORTDiscus®, and 935 from Embase®. An additional seven records were identified through other sources, including citation searching (n = 5) and manual searching (n = 2). After removing 2,207 duplicate entries, 6,935 unique records proceeded to title and abstract screening. Of these, 3,639 studies were excluded at the title‑level screening stage, and 158 studies proceeded to full‑text review. Following detailed full‑text evaluation, 54 studies met all eligibility criteria and were included in the scoping review. Of the included studies, 47 reported injury‑characteristics data, while 27 examined risk or protective factors associated with youth track and field athletes (Fig. 1).

How musculoskeletal injuries have been defined and measured in studies involving youth track and field athletes

Across the included studies, injury definitions were grouped into five approaches: time-loss, medical-attention, combined time-loss/medical-attention, symptom-based, and diagnosis-confirmed definitions. Time-loss definitions were the most used and frequently incorporated severity classifications [28–30]. Medical-attention criteria were often applied alongside time-loss definitions, whereas diagnosis-confirmed studies primarily focused on specific conditions such as bone stress injuries (BSIs) and hamstring strains [31, 32]. Injury onset (acute or gradual) was commonly reported across both time-loss and symptom-based frameworks [11, 28, 31, 33]. Injury data were most frequently collected through athlete self-report systems, although several studies incorporated clinician verification [11, 33]. Exposure was variably quantified using athlete exposures [31, 34], training hours, running mileage, or athlete-hours [11, 35], while some studies did not report exposure measures [36, 37]. What are the risk and protective factors for musculoskeletal injury, and how have they been measured in youth track and field athletes?

Protective factors

Protective factors were reported less frequently than risk factors and included physiological, neuromuscular, biomechanical, and behavioural characteristics (Table 2). Greater bone mineral density (BMD), bone mineral content (BMC), and lean mass were consistently associated with lower occurrence of BSIs and stress fractures [38–40]. Higher lower-body strength, improved movement quality, and greater trunk and hip endurance were also associated with lower injury rates [38–40]. Multicomponent injury-prevention programmes [11] and regular meal or snack consumption [44] were also associated with lower injury occurrence.

Risk factors

Reported risk factors were classified as intrinsic or extrinsic (Table 2). A history of prior injury was consistently reported as being associated with injury occurrence during follow‑up. Risk factors included previous injury [44, 51], unfavourable anthropometric characteristics [33, 38, 39], reduced lower-limb strength, strength asymmetries [50], faster race performances [47] and menstrual-health-related factors [38, 39]. Extrinsic factors were dominated by training-load variables, including greater training volume, mileage, and high-intensity exposure [11, 14, 47]. Footwear and environmental factors, such as spike use [42] and training on harder surfaces [47], were also associated with increased injury occurrence.

How these risk and protective factors have been measured?

Risk and protective factors were assessed using a combination of objective and self-report measures. Physiological, anthropometric, and bone-health variables were commonly evaluated using DEXA, anthropometric assessments, and strength-testing protocols. Neuromuscular and biomechanical characteristics were assessed using dynamometry, endurance tests, motion-analysis technologies, wearable sensors, force platforms, video-based techniques, Functional Movement Screen, landing-error scoring systems, and pressure insoles [12, 49]. Menstrual, nutritional, behavioural, injury-history, and training-related variables were primarily collected through questionnaires, athlete self-reports, training logs, coach reports, athlete diaries, and session-RPE measures. Stratified patterns in methodological approaches

Several methodological patterns emerged when studies were examined according to study characteristics.

Study design: Prospective cohort studies more frequently reported standardised injury definitions, quantified exposure, and implemented regular surveillance protocols. In contrast, retrospective and cross-sectional studies less frequently reported exposure denominators, and several exposure and surveillance components recommended by the Athletics Consensus Statement were not reported.

Athlete population and competitive level: Studies involving elite or highly trained athletes more commonly included clinician verification, structured surveillance systems, and standardised reporting frameworks. In contrast, studies conducted in school or recreational settings more frequently relied on questionnaire-based approaches and retrospective recall.

Athletic discipline: Endurance-based disciplines more frequently incorporated overuse injury definitions and prospective symptom-based surveillance approaches, whereas sprint and jumping disciplines more commonly focused on acute or diagnosis-specific injuries.

Discussion

Novelty and key findings

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first scoping review to comprehensively synthesise how youth track and field injury studies define musculoskeletal injury, measure injury burden, and operationalise risk and protective factors, while also examining methodological characteristics and alignment with the Athletics Consensus Statement [18]. Previous reviews have focused on injury definitions and surveillance systems across all sports disciplines [16, 54] or specifically on running-related injuries [55], whereas this review uniquely maps the methodological aspects used in sport injury epidemiology research in youth track and field.

Injury definitions and measurement approaches

A central finding is the marked variation in how injuries are defined and measured, which is consistent with findings from the broader athletics literature [41]. Additionally, time-loss definitions appear to underestimate overuse injuries compared with symptom-based approaches such as the Oslo Sports Trauma Research Centre (OSTRC) questionnaire [17, 56]. Similarly, Bahr [57] highlighted that traditional surveillance models fail to capture the burden of gradual-onset conditions, which are highly prevalent in endurance-based sports such as athletics. Consequently, symptom-based surveillance may identify injuries that are not captured by time-loss definitions.

Risk and protective factors: definition and measurement

This review identified a range of intrinsic and extrinsic risk factors reported in association with injury outcomes. These findings are consistent with those reported in the narrative review by Edouard and colleagues [41], where injury-related risk factors were also classified as intrinsic modifiable (performance level, maladaptive coping strategies such as self-blame), intrinsic non-modifiable (age, previous injury, biomechanical pole vaulting patterns), and extrinsic factors (training volume, spikes in training load, and environmental temperature). Among these risk factors, training load was the most frequently examined extrinsic factor domain. Similarly, the consistent reporting of previous injury aligns with the broader sports injury literature, where it is recognised as frequently associated with subsequent injury occurrence [58, 59], potentially reflecting incomplete recovery or premature return to sport [60]. The prominence of bone health and menstrual-related factors, particularly in female athletes, highlights the importance of energy availability and physiological maturation in this population, especially in relation to BSIs [61]. In contrast, other domains such as lower energy availability, Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S) and psychological factors recognised as critical in endurance athletes [62], remain underexplored in youth cohorts.

Alignment with consensus standards and methodological gaps

Although many studies reported core injury-definition components in accordance with the Athletics Consensus Statement [18], exposure measurement and surveillance practices varied across studies. This imbalance has also been reported in broader injury surveillance research, where prospective designs with standardised exposure metrics were less commonly reported [16, 54] including in studies specifically focused on running-related injuries [55]. A common study design approach involved retrospective and cross-sectional methods that frequently relied on self-reported injury data without exposure-adjusted outcomes. Similar concerns have been raised in youth sports epidemiology, where methodological characteristics may influence the interpretation of injury-risk relationships [63].

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this scoping review is the comprehensive synthesis of 54 studies spanning injury surveillance and risk-factor research in youth track and field. The review systematically evaluates injury definitions, measurement approaches, and alignment with consensus standards, providing a structured overview of current methodological practices. Inclusion of both intrinsic and extrinsic risk factors, along with their measurement methods, enables a multidimensional assessment of injury epidemiology in this population.

Key limitations include heterogeneity in study design and reporting, which may influence comparability across studies. Publication bias is possible due to reliance on peer-reviewed literature, with limited inclusion of grey literature and non-English studies. Although a structured framework and independent dual review process were used to assess consensus alignment, some subjectivity in interpretation remains. Finally, as a scoping review, this study does not estimate effect sizes or causal relationships; therefore, all reported associations should be interpreted as non-causal. Alignment scores reflect adherence to reporting and surveillance standards rather than overall study quality or evidence certainty.

Recommendations

Future studies should prioritise prospective surveillance systems and standardised exposure metrics. Incorporating clinician involvement and standard diagnostic coding systems may improve diagnostic validity, while more consistent measurement and reporting of neuromuscular, biomechanical, and training-load factors may enhance reproducibility and facilitate comparisons across studies.

Conclusions

This scoping review identified substantial variation in the methods used to define, measure, and report musculoskeletal injuries and associated risk and protective factors among youth track and field athletes. Variation in injury definitions, exposure measurement, and surveillance approaches may affect comparability across studies. Greater alignment with consensus-informed methodological practices may improve the quality and comparability of future research and support the advancement of injury epidemiology and prevention efforts in this population.
