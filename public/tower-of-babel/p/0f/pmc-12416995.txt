Hemodynamic Effects of Guideline-Based Sedation in Mechanically Ventilated Adults: A Multicenter Observational Analysis
Authors: Kiyan Heybati, Guozhen Xie, Jiawen Deng, Allan J. Walkey, Ognjen Gajic, Hemang Yadav
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IMPORTANCE:Propofol is a first-line sedative for adults receiving invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV). However, it can contribute to hemodynamic instability, especially during intubation. The magnitude, timing, risk factors, and variability of sedation-associated mean arterial pressure (MAP) changes remain poorly characterized in ICU settings.OBJECTIVES:To quantify MAP changes following propofol sedation, identify risk factors for hemodynamic instability, and characterize associated interventions.DESIGN:Retrospective cohort study. The primary outcome was MAP change within 2 hours following sedation. Secondary outcomes included vasopressor use and hypotension (MAP ≤ 60 mm Hg). Mixed-effects modeling was used to account for individual patient differences.SETTING AND PARTICIPANTS:Adults (≥ 18 yrs old) who required IMV and received greater than or equal to 6 consecutive hours of propofol infusion, between May 5th, 2018, and July 31st, 2024, in 11 ICUs across the Mayo Clinic, spanning 5 hospitals in 4 states.MAIN OUTCOMES AND MEASURES:The primary outcome was the change in MAP within 2 hours following the initiation of propofol-based sedation.RESULTS:Across 16,418 patients, 25.2% were on vasopressors before sedation initiation. Among the remaining 12,281 patients, 40.3% required vasopressors and 7.7% experienced hypotension within 2 hours of sedation. Propofol-based sedation was associated with a MAP reduction within the first 30 minutes (–6.58 mm Hg; 95% CI, –6.85 to –6.32; p < 0.001). There was substantial interpatient variability in both baseline MAP, and MAP decline after sedation (9.5 and 40.9% between-patient differences, respectively). Higher Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores (–0.31 mm Hg/point), older age (–0.04 mm Hg/yr), and male sex (–0.47 mm Hg) were associated with lower MAP. Patients with higher illness severity experienced progressively greater MAP decline over time (–0.20 mm Hg/hr/SOFA point; p < 0.001).CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE:Propofol-based sedation was associated with clinically significant hemodynamic effects requiring intervention in the early post-intubation period. The marked interpatient variability in hemodynamic responses highlights the importance of personalized management approaches, including risk stratification based on age, sex, and illness severity.

KEY POINTS

Question: What is the magnitude, timing, and risk factors for mean arterial pressure (MAP) changes following propofol-based sedation in patients receiving invasive mechanical ventilation?

Findings: In this retrospective study of 16,418 patients, of whom 12,281 were not initially on hemodynamic support, 40.3% required vasopressor initiation, and 7.7% experienced hypotension within 2 hours of propofol initiation. The magnitude of MAP decline within the first 30 minutes post-sedation was 6.6 mm Hg, with considerable variability (40.9%) between patients. Older age, male sex, and higher Sequential Organ Failure Assessment scores were associated with greater MAP reductions.

Meaning: Propofol sedation was associated with clinically significant hemodynamic changes in patients receiving invasive mechanical ventilation, especially those with higher illness severity. Given the substantial interpatient variability in MAP changes, personalized risk assessment is essential to guide sedation strategies.

Guideline-recommended sedation strategies for critically ill adults requiring invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) include propofol as a first-line sedative-hypnotic agent, favored for its rapid onset and short duration of action (1). However, post-intubation sedation management can create significant hemodynamic challenges, most notably hypotension (2, 3). A prior review demonstrated a higher mortality rate among those receiving propofol sedation vs. other agents, although it is unclear whether this finding is attributable to its hemodynamic effects (4).

Specifically, propofol is a sterically hindered phenol that allosterically activates the gamma-aminobutyric acid chloride channel and leads to central nervous system (CNS) depression (5, 6). This action inhibits sympathetic tone and disrupts cardiovascular regulatory mechanisms, including the baroreceptor reflex (5, 6). In critically ill patients who often have compromised compensatory mechanisms, alongside other contributing factors including intubation, these effects can manifest as clinically significant reductions in mean arterial pressure (MAP). This hemodynamic instability is particularly concerning because even brief episodes of hypotension have been associated with higher mortality, renal injury, myocardial damage, and other adverse outcomes (7–10).

Despite the widespread use of propofol infusion, the magnitude, timing, risk factors, and variability of sedation-associated MAP changes remain inadequately characterized in ICU settings (11–15). While propofol’s hemodynamic effects have been extensively studied in the operative setting, these findings may not translate to critically ill patients (11, 16, 17). Critically ill patients often have greater comorbidity burden, ongoing physiologic stress, complex polypharmacy, and concurrent interventions that may modify the effects of sedation (18–20). The temporal profile of blood pressure changes in the early period following intubation and the initiation of sedation, as well as risk factors associated with hemodynamic compromise remain poorly characterized.

Therefore, the objectives of this multicenter observational cohort study were to: 1) characterize the temporal relationship between the initiation of propofol-based sedation and changes in MAP during the early post-intubation period, 2) identify patient characteristics that correlate with hemodynamic instability, and 3) describe the patterns of associated vasopressor and fluid administration. By examining these relationships in a large, diverse ICU population, we aimed to provide clinically relevant insights that could inform sedation strategies and hemodynamic management in critically ill adults.

METHODS

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study analyzed data from 11 ICUs within the Mayo Clinic Enterprise, spanning 5 hospitals in 4 states (21). These sites include both large academic centers (Rochester, MN; Jacksonville, FL; Phoenix, AZ), as well as more community-oriented hospitals (Mankato, MN; Eau Claire, WI), representing diverse critical care practices within a single healthcare system with a unified electronic health record (EHR) and data collection platform. This study was reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines and REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data statement (Electronic Supplementary Materials [ESM] Table 1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B549) (22). This study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (No. 23-007416; August 9th, 2023) and conducted in accordance with institutional ethical standards and the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2024 (23). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by institutional review.

Patient Selection and Exposure

We included consecutive adults (≥ 18 yrs old) admitted to one of 11 ICUs between May 5th, 2018, and July 31st, 2024 (Fig. 1). Eligible patients required concurrent IMV and continuous propofol infusion as part of guideline-recommended sedation management for greater than or equal to 6 hours. The median time from intubation to initiation of sedation was 10 minutes. To avoid interdependence of observations, we only included the index (i.e., first eligible) ICU admission for each patient.

Patient flow diagram. IMV = invasive mechanical ventilation.

Outcomes of Interest

The primary outcome was the change in MAP within 2 hours following the initiation of post-intubation sedation, obtained from invasive arterial lines when present, and otherwise from noninvasive blood pressure measurements. Secondary outcomes included: 1) frequency and timing of hypotension, defined as at least two MAP readings less than or equal to 60 mm Hg within 15 minutes (3, 24); 2) use and timing of vasopressors; 3) fluid administration; 4) use of alternative or adjunct sedatives during the same time window; and 5) ICU and hospital length of stay (LOS), duration of mechanical ventilation, and mortality. Due to the complex interplay of vasopressor use and blood pressure, we analyzed those on vasopressors before sedation initiation separately.

Data Collection

Patients were identified through institutional data warehouses (Mayo Clinic ICU DataMart and Unified Data Platform; Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN) that provide a near real-time replica of Mayo Clinic’s EHR with comprehensive medication, vital signs, laboratory, and clinical data (25). Data were extracted by a data specialist from the Mayo Clinic Anesthesia and Critical Care Research Unit, as well as study investigators (K.H., G.X., H.Y.) trained in the use of Mayo Clinic’s data warehouses (Mayo Clinic ICU DataMart and Unified Data Platform). To ensure data quality and accuracy, a random sample of 80 patient records underwent manual chart review. Additional details are provided in ESM 2 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B549).

Data Analysis

Data analysis was performed in R 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the R Studio 2023.03 integrated development environment (PBC, Boston, MA) (26).

Descriptive Data Analysis

Patient characteristics were described using frequencies (percentages) or medians (interquartile range), as appropriate. The two primary analysis cohorts (i.e., vasopressors vs. no vasopressors within 2 hours of propofol-based sedation initiation) were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and chi-square tests for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. The characteristics of those who were receiving vasopressors before sedation were presented descriptively only, without statistical testing.

MAP trajectories were analyzed in 30-minute intervals from 0.5 hour before to 2 hours after sedation initiation. For patients receiving vasopressors during a specific interval, we calculated MAP values from the start of the interval until vasopressor initiation to prevent confounding by treatment. Similarly, patients receiving parenteral anti-hypertensives had their MAP values excluded from that interval. To facilitate analysis and consistent with prior literature (27), the dose of each vasopressor was converted into norepinephrine equivalents (NEEs), as outlined in ESM 2 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B549).

Time-to-Event Analyses

The cumulative incidence of the first and second vasopressor use, as well as hypotension, was modeled using the R cmprsk package (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and presented using adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To address the substantial proportion of missing ejection fraction (EF) data, we conducted a sensitivity analysis where patients without EF measurements were assigned to the greater than 40% category, based on the assumption that patients without recent echocardiograms were less likely to have significant left ventricular dysfunction.

Mixed-Effects Modeling

To account for the longitudinal nature of the data and within-patient correlation, we developed linear mixed-effects models using the R package lme4. These models examined the relationship between MAP and propofol-based sedation administration while adjusting for patient characteristics and time-varying interventions/covariates. Patient-level data included demographic and clinical variables: age, sex, weight, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, EF, and comorbidities including hypertension, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and diabetes mellitus. Missing EF values were imputed using the median value of the cohort, and a binary indicator variable for missing EF status was created to account for potential systematic differences in patients with missing EF data.

Additional details are presented in ESM 2 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B549). Briefly, baseline MAP was calculated as the mean of available MAP measurements in the time bin immediately preceding propofol administration. The propofol dose was incorporated as an actual body weight-adjusted continuous variable. To address the confounding effect of vasopressor administration on MAP, we calculated “effective MAP” using norepinephrine-equivalent dosing at each time point to adjust the raw MAP values. The main model used a “neutral” adjustment, estimating a 5 mm Hg MAP increase per 0.05 µg/kg/min NE, reduced to 3 mm Hg per 0.05 µg/kg/min after reaching a dose of 0.20 µg/kg/min. Fluid administration data were included to account for their potential influence on hemodynamics. To model the temporal relationship with sedation administration, we created time-related variables including a binary indicator for post-sedation time periods (i.e., within 0–30 min) and a continuous variable representing hours elapsed since propofol administration (set to 0 for pre-administration time bins). Individual variation in both baseline MAP and the rate of MAP change following propofol administration was accounted for through patient-specific random intercepts and slopes. We also investigated several interaction terms to examine how the effects of guideline-recommended post-intubation sedation management may vary based on patient characteristics and clinical context in the early post-intubation period (ESM 2, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B549).

RESULTS

Cohort Characteristics

Of 16,418 patients, 25.2% (n = 4,137) were already on vasopressors before sedation initiation (Fig. 1). Of the remaining 12,281 patients, 40.3% (n = 4,949) required vasopressors within 2 hours of sedation initiation, while 59.7% (n = 7,332) did not (Table 1). Patients requiring vasopressors were older (65.0 yrs [55.0–74.0 yrs] vs. 60.0 yrs [46.0–71.0 yrs]; p < 0.001), a higher proportion were male (64.6%, n = 3197 vs. 57.9%, n = 4243; p < 0.001), and had higher SOFA scores (10.0 points [7.0–12.0 points] vs. 7.0 points [5.0–9.0 points]; p < 0.001), and a higher prevalence of comorbid conditions including CKD (38.2%, n = 1912 vs. 28.1%, n = 2410; p < 0.001), prior myocardial infarction (28.5%, n = 1409 vs. 18.6%, n = 1364; p < 0.001), and heart failure (54.3%, n = 2685 vs. 34.6%, n = 2535; p < 0.001; Table 1; and ESM Table 2, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B549). There was a significantly longer ICU and hospital LOS across those in the vasopressor vs. no vasopressor cohort, as well as higher ICU (15.6%; n = 774 vs. 9.4%; n = 687; p < 0.001) and hospital (21.1%; n = 1046 vs. 13.8%; n = 1011; p < 0.001) mortality (ESM Table 3, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B549).

Changes in MAP and Interventions

Baseline MAP differed between patients who subsequently required vasopressors (80.5 mm Hg [72.0–92.5 mm Hg]) and those who did not (91.5 mm Hg [80.0–104.5 mm Hg]) (ESM Table 4, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B549). The first 30 minutes after sedation initiation saw the highest intervention rate, with 18.9% of patients (n = 3108) receiving their first vasopressor, 6.0% (n = 980) receiving fluid boluses, and 2.8% (n = 455) experiencing hypotension. Across those on vasopressors by the end of the first 30-minute period (n = 7245), the median administered dosage in NEE was 0.03 µg/kg/min (0.0–0.10 µg/kg/min). There was generally a higher dosage of vasopressors administered over the semi-hourly periods, with 0.0 µg/kg/min (0.0–0.05 µg/kg/min) in the 30 minutes before sedation (n = 4137) and 0.05 µg/kg/min (0.02–0.12 µg/kg/min) during the 1.5–2-hour period (n = 9086).

Among those who did and did not receive any vasopressors within 2 hours (n = 7332), the cumulative propofol dose within this first period was 38.7 µg/kg/min (23.0–50.0 µg/kg/min) and 40.0 µg/kg/min (25.0–60.0 µg/kg/min), with –5.9% (–17.7% to 5.5%) and –3.3% (–12.1% to 4.8%) change in MAP from baseline, respectively. Hemodynamic responses varied considerably. While some patients experienced MAP increases, others showed substantial decreases (ESM Table 4 and ESM Fig. 1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B549). Severe MAP reductions (i.e., ≥ 25% from baseline) initially affected 7.1% of the no vasopressor and 13.8% of the vasopressor group, increasing to 16.2% and 37.0%, respectively, by the 1.5–2-hour period.

Cumulative Incidence of Vasopressor Use and Hypotension

When compared with patients with an EF of less than or equal to 40%, those with an EF greater than 40% had a significantly lower cumulative incidence of initial vasopressor use (aHR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.80–0.96; p = 0.005; Fig. 2; and ESM Table 5, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B549) which remained consistent upon sensitivity analysis for missing EF values (ESM Fig. 2, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B549). ICU type was associated with a significant difference in hemodynamic interventions. Compared with cardiac/CT surgical ICU patients, those in medical/other surgical ICUs had a lower frequency of initial vasopressor use (aHR, 0.62; 95% CI, 0.57–0.67; p < 0.001) but were more likely to receive a second vasopressor (aHR, 1.91; 95% CI, 1.12–3.25; p = 0.017).

Across the 12,281 patients who were not initially receiving vasopressors before sedation, 944 (7.7%) experienced at least one episode of hypotension within 2 hours. When compared with those without hypotension (n = 11,337), the cumulative dosage of propofol within the 2 hours was comparable (30.7 µg/kg/min [20.0–47.3 µg/kg/min] vs. 39.1 µg/kg/min [24.1–52.9 µg/kg/min]; p = 0.42, for hypotension vs. no hypotension cohorts, respectively). However, there were several differences in the use of concomitant vasopressors, as well as sedative/analgesic infusions (ESM Table 6, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B549). The order of vasopressor and alternative sedative initiation among those who developed hypotension is outlined in ESM Table 7 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B549), with 78.9% (n = 745) of patients being started on at least one vasopressor within 0.6 hours (0.2–2.0 hr) following the first hypotensive episode.

Mixed-Effects model

In the mixed-effects models (Table 2; Fig. 3; and ESM Table 8 and ESM Figs. 3–5, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B549), post-intubation sedation initiation was associated with a significant MAP reduction within the first 30-minute period (–6.58 mm Hg; 95% CI, –6.85 to –6.32; p < 0.001). This effect was consistent across sensitivity analyses using different vasopressor adjustment methods (Table 3; and ESM Tables 9–14, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B549).

Baseline MAP was associated with post-propofol MAP (0.76 mm Hg per 1 mm Hg higher MAP baseline; 95% CI, 0.76–0.77; p < 0.001; Table 2). Higher SOFA scores (–0.31 mm Hg per point; 95% CI, –0.35 to –0.27; p < 0.001), older age (–0.04 mm Hg per year; 95% CI, –0.05 to –0.03; p < 0.001), and male sex (–0.47 mm Hg; 95% CI, –0.77 to –0.17; p = 0.002) were also associated with lower MAP.

Patients with higher illness severity experienced progressively greater MAP reductions over time (–0.20 mm Hg per hour per SOFA point; 95% CI, –0.26 to –0.15; p < 0.001). The propofol dosing relationship with MAP was complex. Larger doses were associated with a higher initial MAP (0.26 mm Hg per µg/kg/min; 95% CI, 0.24–0.28; p < 0.001), likely reflecting higher clinician dosing of propofol in those with higher baseline MAP. Subsequent propofol dosing was associated with larger MAP reductions over time (–0.08 mm Hg per hour per 1 µg/kg higher dose; 95% CI, 0.24–0.28; p < 0.001). New fluid boluses were associated with slightly lower MAP (–0.11 mm Hg per mL/kg; 95% CI, –0.14 to –0.07; p < 0.001), likely reflecting reactive fluid administration for falling blood pressure rather than a direct effect. In contrast, cumulative fluid administration was not associated with a change in MAP. The PCA model addressing fluid administration collinearity confirmed the robustness of the findings (ESM Table 12, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B549).

The random effects structure (ESM Table 8, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B549) revealed interpatient variability in baseline MAP (i.e., intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC]: 9.5% variation attributable to between-patient differences), with substantially higher variation in MAP changes over time (ICC = 40.9%) The high correlation between random intercepts and slopes indicates that patients with higher baseline MAP values tended to experience steeper declines in MAP during the early post-intubation period following propofol-based sedation. The substantial residual variance highlights considerable intrapatient variability in MAP measurements beyond what is explained by the measured covariates.

DISCUSSION

Summary of Findings

In this large multicenter study of critically ill adults receiving IMV, we found that approximately 40% of patients not already on vasopressors required vasopressor support within 2 hours of sedation initiation, and around 8% (i.e., one in 13 patients) had sustained hypotension. Lower EF was associated with a higher cumulative incidence of vasopressor initiation, but not hypotension. Early post-intubation sedation was associated with an approximately 7 mm Hg decline in MAP, although hemodynamic response varied considerably between patients. Several patient factors significantly influenced MAP following sedation initiation, including baseline MAP, illness severity (i.e., SOFA score), age, and sex.

Findings in Context

Our findings expand the current understanding of the associated hemodynamic changes during the early post-intubation period among critically ill patients receiving propofol-based sedation. While the current Pain, Agitation/Sedation, Delirium, Immobility, and Disrupted Sleep guidelines recommend the use of propofol as a first-line sedative, they provide limited guidance on managing its cardiovascular effects (1). Previous studies have primarily examined sedation impact during anesthesia induction, with limited data among critically ill patients receiving continuous infusions (11, 16, 17). In our cohort (n = 16,418), around 72% received a vasopressor at some point during their ICU stay. Of those not already on vasopressors (n = 12,281), approximately 40% received vasopressors in the first 2 hours after propofol, of whom 15% were also hypotensive (i.e., 6% of the total cohort), and 8% overall experienced hypotension. There was also a generally higher median vasopressor dose at later time points (i.e., closer to 2 hrs), which is consistent with the likely titration of these agents in practice to target a MAP goal. While comparative data is limited, a retrospective study of critically ill adults with COVID-19 found that around 11% (28/252) experienced hypotension requiring vasopressor initiation within 6 hours of propofol infusion, and the majority (81%, 204/252) received vasopressor support for over 12 hours at some point during their stay (28). However, it is unclear how hypotension was defined; some may have already been receiving vasopressor support before propofol infusion, and the timeline of vasopressor initiation among those who did not experience hypotension was not investigated. Furthermore, the relative effect of propofol vs. other agents and interventions (e.g., intubation) on these hemodynamic changes is unclear, given the retrospective nature of these investigations.

Additionally, the relationship between EF and vasopressor use in the ICU has not been well described (29). Interestingly, our study revealed that a higher proportion of those with lower EF received vasopressors, with no significant difference in the cumulative incidence of hypotension. These observations may reflect the goal of keeping MAP within a specific range (i.e., over 65 mm Hg) to avoid adverse outcomes associated with hypotension, especially in those with cardiac dysfunction. Our mixed-effects modeling revealed that after controlling for baseline MAP and other factors, EF itself was not independently associated with the magnitude of MAP reduction, even after adjustment for vasopressor dosing. However, the positive coefficient of having a missing EF value (i.e., those with a missing EF had higher MAP) suggests that such missingness may be informative.

Furthermore, patients in cardiac/CT surgical ICUs received earlier vasopressor support with lower rates of subsequent hypotension and additional vasopressor use compared with medical/other surgical ICUs. While this may reflect different risk stratification and management approaches across ICU settings, further investigation is warranted to better understand underlying etiologies for these observations.

It has been suggested that higher doses of propofol are associated with a larger magnitude of harm (14). While we did not identify any significant difference in propofol dose (µg/kg/min) between those who did and did not experience hypotension within the first 2 hours of propofol infusion, mixed-effect models revealed that higher propofol dose was associated with higher baseline MAP. This may reflect clinicians’ tendency to administer lower doses of propofol to patients with lower baseline MAP to avoid hypotensive episodes.

Furthermore, patient characteristics such as advanced age, comorbid conditions, and higher illness severity have been shown to influence the association between propofol-based sedation and MAP in prior smaller studies (18, 20, 30, 31). A prospective study of 100 patients who received propofol noted that higher illness severity (i.e., Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II score ≥ 15 vs. < 10) translated to a 4.8-fold higher risk of hypotension (24). This is consistent with our study, which found a –0.31 mm Hg lower MAP for every 1-point increase in the SOFA score. Furthermore, we found a synergistic effect between SOFA score and time from propofol initiation, which implies that patients with high illness severity show a greater decrease in MAP over time. Older age and male sex were also associated with larger reductions in MAP; however, there were no significant associations between the preexisting chronic conditions and changes in MAP after accounting for baseline MAP.

Strengths and Limitations

This study is among the first and largest analyses to quantify granular changes in MAP following guideline-recommended post-intubation sedation with propofol. The inclusion of data from 11 ICUs across diverse practice settings enhances generalizability, while the precise capture of medication timing and vital sign measurements allowed for detailed temporal analysis. Our mixed-effects modeling approach accounted for both interpatient and intrapatient variability and incorporated multiple sensitivity analyses to ensure the robustness of findings.

However, this study has several limitations that should be considered. Given the analysis of retrospective data, there is potential for misclassification and residual confounding. Additionally, while we were able to capture the relevant timing of MAP measurements and interventions, we were unable to determine the clinical context for decisions such as vasopressor initiation. While we attempted to capture the type of shock, we were unable to determine the primary cause of MAP reductions and hypotensive episodes given the retrospective design. Propofol initiation closely follows intubation (median 10 min in our cohort), creating inherent challenges in isolating propofol’s specific hemodynamic effects. Intubation and mechanical ventilation independently cause physiologic alterations that cannot be readily disentangled, including blunting of endogenous catecholamines, loss of sympathetic tone, and reduced preload from positive pressure ventilation. While these limitations are inherent to the clinical reality of the care of critically ill patients, they likely contribute to the substantial interpatient variability observed in our mixed-effects models and underscore the need for the granular data our study provides. Ideally, prospective studies are needed to better understand the changes in MAP in different clinical scenarios, but these are challenging to practically do at scale. Our analyses were subject to missing data, and while this was generally low for most variables, around 40% of patients did not have an available EF measurement within 3 months of admission. We attempted to mitigate this by modeling missingness as a fixed effect variable, suggesting that missing EF is unlikely to be missing at random and quantifying the nature of that effect. Future investigation is also needed to better characterize outcomes among those with multiple ICU admissions, given that our analyses only included the initial ICU stay during the study period. These findings should also be assessed across different healthcare systems for better generalizability. Finally, as our analyses primarily focused on the immediate 2-hour period following propofol initiation, we could not account for the possible effect of induction agents due to data limitations, and did not assess longer-term outcomes specifically attributable to such early hemodynamic changes.

CONCLUSIONS

Among critically ill adults receiving IMV, around 40% were started on at least one vasopressor and 8% experienced hypotension within 2 hours following intubation and the initiation of propofol-based sedation. Patients with reduced EF received more vasopressors, without experiencing higher rates of hypotension. Post-intubation sedation was associated with an average MAP reduction of 6.6 mm Hg within the first 30 minutes, with greater effects in patients with higher illness severity, advanced age, and male sex. These findings have several clinical implications. First, they highlight the importance of anticipating hemodynamic changes following propofol-based sedation in the early post-intubation period, particularly in high-risk patients. Second, they suggest that different management approaches across ICU types may influence outcomes, with potential benefits to earlier intervention in vulnerable patients. Finally, they underscore the significant heterogeneity in hemodynamic responses, suggesting that personalized rather than protocolized approaches may be warranted. Given the designation of propofol as a first-line sedative in current guidelines, future prospective studies should examine whether patient-specific risk stratification and tailored preventive strategies can reduce associated hypotension and improve outcomes. Additionally, comparative effectiveness research between different sedation strategies that account for hemodynamic effects may help optimize care for mechanically ventilated critically ill adults.
