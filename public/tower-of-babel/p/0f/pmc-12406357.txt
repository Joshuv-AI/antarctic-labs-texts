Caffeic acid-related gene expression and antioxidant activity enhance drought tolerance in three bean cultivars
Authors: Zahra Rashidi, Khosro Azizi, Hamid Reza Eisvand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCaffeic acid, one of the important phenolic compounds in plants, plays a significant role in enhancing the defense mechanisms and adaptation of plants to environmental stresses, including drought. This study aimed to investigate the effect of drought stress on the expression of genes involved in the biosynthesis of caffeic acid, photosynthetic mechanisms, and antioxidant enzyme activity in three cultivars of beans. The experiment was conducted in a split-plot design within a randomized complete block design, with three irrigation levels (50, 75, and 100% of water requirement) as the main factor and three bean cultivars (red, white, and pinto) as the sub-factor, with three replications.ResultsThe results showed that drought stress increased the expression of caffeic acid biosynthesis genes and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities (CAT, SOD, POD) in all bean cultivars. These responses were particularly stronger in pinto and red beans. For instance, caffeic acid content increased by more than 60% under severe drought in pinto bean tissues, contributing to improved antioxidant capacity. While drought stress reduced seed yield in all cultivars, the decline was less pronounced in pinto bean (about 14%), indicating its higher drought tolerance compared to the white bean, which showed a larger yield reduction.ConclusionsThese results suggest that the increased expression of genes involved in caffeic acid biosynthesis and the enhancement of antioxidant activity are key mechanisms in plant adaptation to drought. Identifying and utilizing these mechanisms can provide effective strategies for genetic improvement and the production of drought-resistant cultivars. Moreover, the findings can contribute to optimizing irrigation management and improving production sustainability in arid and semi-arid regions.Keywords: DPPH, Drought tolerance, Legumes, Photosynthetic mechanisms, Seed yield, PAL

Introduction

Caffeic acid, a major phenolic compound recognized for its strong antioxidant properties, plays a vital role in various physiological processes in plants [1]. It acts as a natural protector under abiotic stress conditions such as drought, salinity, and pollution by mitigating oxidative damage through reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging mechanisms [2]. Under drought stress, the production of ROS such as hydrogen peroxide and superoxide radicals increases sharply, which can lead to lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and disruption of cellular functions. Caffeic acid contributes to reducing these effects by enhancing the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and peroxidase (POD), thereby preventing damage to cell membranes and tissues [3].

Legumes, particularly common beans (Phaseolus vulgaris L.), are considered a vital food source in developing countries due to their high protein and micronutrient content [4]. Besides their nutritional value, beans play a critical role in sustainable agriculture by fixing atmospheric nitrogen through symbiotic relationships with Rhizobium bacteria, thereby improving soil fertility [5]. However, the productivity of beans is significantly threatened by drought stress [6]. Due to their relatively shallow root systems and high-water demand during flowering and pod filling stages, beans are highly sensitive to water shortages [7]. Drought stress limits water availability, alters photosynthetic performance, enhances oxidative stress, and ultimately reduces yield [8].

In recent years, the role of secondary metabolites in stress resistance has gained considerable attention. Among these, caffeic acid has been reported to increase under stress conditions, contributing to enhanced antioxidant defense and cellular stability [9]. Moreover, caffeic acid is synthesized via the phenylpropanoid pathway, involving key enzymes such as phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (C4H), and p-coumarate 3-hydroxylase (C3H) [10]. Studies in crops like rice and wheat have shown that upregulation of these genes under drought conditions leads to increased caffeic acid biosynthesis, which in turn improves plant resilience by preserving membrane integrity, reducing malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, and enhancing water retention capacity [11, 12].

Despite these findings, there remains a gap in knowledge regarding the regulation of caffeic acid biosynthesis and its link to drought tolerance in legumes, especially in beans. While research in model plants has provided insights into the protective functions of this compound, its specific role in different bean cultivars with varying drought sensitivity has not been fully elucidated.

To address this, the current study investigates the expression of caffeic acid biosynthetic genes (PAL, C4H, and C3H) and their physiological implications in three bean cultivars (red, white, and pinto) under different irrigation regimes (100%, 75%, and 50% of water requirement). These cultivars were selected due to their contrasting responses to water stress. It is hypothesized that cultivars with higher expression of caffeic acid biosynthetic genes will show stronger antioxidant activity and improved physiological responses, resulting in greater drought tolerance.

The specific objectives of this study were to: (1) quantify caffeic acid content and antioxidant enzyme activities in different bean cultivars under varying drought levels; (2) assess biochemical and physiological responses such as proline, MDA, SPAD, and photosynthetic traits; (3) analyze the expression of PAL, C4H, and C3H genes involved in the caffeic acid biosynthetic pathway; and (4) investigate the relationships between gene expression, antioxidant mechanisms, and drought tolerance in common beans.

Results

Gene expression

Drought stress, bean types, and their interaction significantly affected the expression of the PAL, C4H, and C3H genes. The results showed that the expression of all three genes (PAL, C4H, and C3H) increased with the intensity of stress in all three bean cultivars, with the highest gene expression observed in the pinto bean. The expression of PAL in the pinto bean under 75% and 50% stress increased by 39% and 87%, respectively, and in the red bean, it increased by 44% and 88% compared to the 100% irrigation treatment, while the increase for the white bean was 33% and 78%, respectively (Fig. 1-A). The expression of C4H in the pinto bean under 75% and 50% stress increased by 51% and 101%, respectively, compared to the control treatment, in the red bean by 55% and 109%, and in the white bean by 49% and 98% (Fig. 1-B). Additionally, the expression of the C3H gene in the pinto bean under 75% and 50% stress increased by 36% and 91%, respectively, compared to the control treatment. In the red bean, the increases were 40% and 100%, and in the white bean, they were 32% and 89%, respectively (Fig. 1-C).

Levels of caffeic acid in the root, stem, and leaf tissues

The effects of drought stress, bean types, and their interaction on the content of caffeic acid in root, stem, and leaf tissues were significant. Caffeic acid increased in all three tissues with the severity of stress, although the amounts of caffeic acid produced in the leaf tissue were higher compared to the stem and root tissues. In the pinto bean, the levels of caffeic acid were higher under all three stress treatments. In all three tissues (root, stem, and leaf), the amount of caffeic acid under the 75% stress treatment increased by 49%, 25%, and 22%, respectively, compared to the control treatment, while under the 50% stress treatment, the increase was 75%, 60%, and 43%, respectively. In the red bean, the increase in caffeic acid in the 75% stress treatment compared to the control was 36%, 27%, and 18%, respectively, and in the 50% treatment, the increase was 65%, 60%, and 33%, respectively. In the white bean, the levels of caffeic acid in the root, stem, and leaf tissues under the 75% treatment increased by 31%, 22%, and 18%, respectively, compared to the 100% irrigation treatment, while these increases for the 50% irrigation treatment were 63%, 42%, and 36%, respectively (Fig. 2). Levels of DPPH, MDA, proline and RWC

The levels of DPPH, MDA, proline, and RWC were significantly influenced by drought stress, bean types, and their interaction. In all three types of beans, as the intensity of the stress increased, the levels of DPPH, MDA, and proline also increased. The DPPH value in the pinto bean increased by 36% and 62% under 75% and 50% stress conditions, respectively, compared to the 100% treatment. For the red bean, these values were 30% and 58%, while for the white bean, they were 23% and 53%, respectively (Fig. 3-A). The MDA levels in pinto beans increased by 35% and 78% under 75% and 50% stress treatments, respectively, compared to the control. In red beans, these increases were 43% and 80%, and in white beans, they were 44% and 83%, respectively (Fig. 3-B). Additionally, the proline levels in pinto beans increased by 67% and 112% under 75% and 50% stress conditions, respectively, compared to the control. In red beans, these increases were 47% and 99%, and in white beans, they were 38% and 84%, respectively (Fig. 3-C). As the stress intensity increased, the RWC in all three types of beans decreased. The RWC in pinto beans decreased by 4% and 36% under 75% and 50% stress treatments, respectively, compared to the 100% treatment. In red beans, these decreases were 13% and 58%, and in white beans, they were 16% and 63%, respectively (Fig. 3-D). The correlation analysis showed that DPPH and proline had a significant positive correlation with antioxidant enzymes, caffeic acid, and the genes PAL, C4H, and C3H (Fig. 7).

Antioxidant enzymes

The results of the analysis of variance for the traits indicated that the effects of drought stress, bean types, and their interaction on the activities of catalase, peroxidase, and superoxide dismutase enzymes were significant. Application of drought stress led to an increase in antioxidant enzymes. On the other hand, the bean types showed similar behavior. Catalase activity in pinto beans was higher than in red and white beans at all stress levels. The catalase content in pinto beans increased by 35% and 77% under 75% and 50% stress conditions, respectively, compared to the control. However, the catalase content in red beans under 75% and 50% stress increased by 33% and 65%, respectively, and in white beans, it increased by 24% and 56%, compared to the control (Fig. 4-A). In pinto beans, superoxide dismutase activity content increased by 28% and 57% under 75% and 50% stress, respectively, compared to the control, while in white beans, superoxide dismutase content increased by 24% and 52%, respectively, under 75% and 50% stress (Fig. 4-B). Similar to catalase and superoxide dismutase, peroxidase activity levels increased under drought stress in all three bean cultivars, with the highest levels observed at 50% irrigation. Peroxidase content in pinto beans increased by 50% and 119% under 75% and 50% stress, respectively, compared to the control. However, peroxidase content in white beans increased by 36% and 87% under 75% and 50% stress (Fig. 4-C). The correlation analysis showed that antioxidant enzymes had a significant positive correlation with caffeic acid present in the root, stem, and leaf (Fig. 7).

Photosynthetic characteristics

The photosynthetic characteristics were significantly affected by drought stress, bean types, and their interaction. The values of net photosynthesis, stomatal conductance, transpiration rate, and intercellular CO2 concentration decreased at different drought stress levels, while stomatal resistance increased. In pinto beans, the values of net photosynthesis, stomatal conductance, transpiration rate, and intercellular CO2 concentration decreased by 28%, 23%, 25%, and 14%, respectively, under 75% stress compared to the control. These values decreased by 25%, 22%, 25%, and 13% in red beans, and by 29%, 29%, 40%, and 16% in white beans. On the other hand, under 50% stress compared to the control treatment, the values of net photosynthesis, stomatal conductance, transpiration rate, and intercellular CO2 concentration decreased by 43%, 42%, 55%, and 26% in pinto beans, by 40%, 43%, 59%, and 26% in red beans, and by 63%, 52%, 65%, and 27% in white beans. Stomatal resistance in pinto beans increased by 30% and 78% under 75% and 50% stress conditions, respectively, compared to the control. However, stomatal resistance in white beans increased by 27% and 63%, respectively, compared to the control (Fig. 5). The correlation analysis showed that seed yield had a significant positive correlation with RWC, transpiration rate, and net photosynthesis, while it had a significant negative correlation with stomatal resistance (Fig. 7).

Seed and biological yield

The results showed that seed and biological yields were significantly influenced by drought stress, bean types, and their interaction. Both seed and biological yields decreased with increasing stress intensity, with the greatest reduction observed in beans exposed to 50% stress (Fig. 6). For pinto beans, seed and biological yields decreased by 9% and 14% for seed yield, and 7% and 10% for biological yield under 75% and 50% stress, respectively, compared to the control. In red beans, seed yield decreased by 12% and 25%, and biological yield decreased by 7% and 17% under 75% and 50% stress conditions, respectively, compared to the control. For white beans, seed yield decreased by 30% and 39%, and biological yield decreased by 19% and 24% under 75% and 50% stress, respectively, compared to the control (Figs. 6 and 7).

Discussion

Drought stress is a major challenge for crop plants, significantly impacting their physiological and molecular processes. In this study, the effects of drought stress on various physiological and biochemical traits were evaluated in three bean types (red, white, and pinto). The findings revealed that red and pinto beans exhibited greater drought tolerance compared to white beans, primarily due to the activation of complex defense mechanisms in these species. This section explores the different molecular and physiological mechanisms and their interconnections.

Caffeic acid is one of the important secondary metabolites in plants, playing a critical role in resistance to environmental stresses such as drought [9]. The production of caffeic acid is primarily mediated through the phenolic biosynthesis pathway, which involves the activity of several key enzymes. Within this pathway, the genes PAL (Phenylalanine Ammonia-Lyase), C4H (Cinnamate-4-Hydroxylase), and C3H (Cinnamate-3-Hydroxylase) play essential roles [12]. Our experimental results indicated that the expression levels of PAL, C4H, and C3H increased in all three bean types under elevated drought stress conditions. PAL is the initial enzyme in the phenolic metabolite biosynthesis pathway, catalyzing the conversion of phenylalanine to cinnamic acid [13]. During drought stress, the expression of PAL is significantly elevated in many plants because phenolic acids, particularly caffeic acid, protect cells against oxidative stress induced by drought [1, 14] and [15]. Increased production of caffeic acid in drought-stressed plants has been reported to mitigate damage caused by free radicals and improve drought tolerance [16]. For instance, Liang et al. [10] reported that drought-stressed rice plants exhibited elevated PAL expression, which enhanced phenolic acid production and subsequently improved drought resistance. C4H is another critical enzyme in the phenolic biosynthesis pathway, acting downstream of PAL and converting cinnamic acid into p-coumaric acid. Under environmental stresses, particularly drought, plants often selectively upregulate C4H expression to boost the production of phenolic compounds such as caffeic acid [14]. In the present study, the high expression of C4H in red and pinto beans led to increased production of caffeic acid, contributing to improved drought resistance in these species. Similarly, researchers have reported that in wheat plants subjected to drought, enhanced C4H expression increased the production of caffeic acid and other phenolic compounds, which alleviated drought-induced damage and enhanced drought resistance [17]. Like PAL and C4H, the expression of C3H also significantly increased in red, pinto, and white beans in response to drought stress, indicating the role of this gene in enhancing caffeic acid production and improving drought tolerance. C3H is the enzyme responsible for converting p-coumaric acid into caffeic acid in the subsequent steps of its biosynthetic pathway [18]. Khakdan et al. [19] reported increased C3H expression in drought-stressed basil plants, leading to enhanced caffeic acid production, consistent with the findings of this study.

Caffeic acid, as one of the secondary metabolites in plants, is particularly produced in response to environmental stresses such as drought and salinity [20]. The results showed that red and pinto beans exhibited significantly higher production of caffeic acid compared to white beans. This increased production of caffeic acid may function as a defensive mechanism against oxidative stress induced by drought. Caffeic acid contributes to the preservation of cellular membrane integrity by mitigating free radical-induced damage and preventing lipid peroxidation [21]. Furthermore, it can be inferred that in beans with higher caffeic acid production, such as red and pinto beans, the reduction in oxidative damage supports the maintenance of biological and photosynthetic processes. Ultimately, this leads to improved performance traits. It has been reported that increased caffeic acid production in potato under salinity stress significantly enhances plant resistance to environmental stresses and ultimately improves yield [22].

Caffeic acid, as a phenolic antioxidant compound, has a significant impact on the activity of antioxidant enzymes and plays a crucial role in mitigating oxidative stress caused by drought [14]. This compound enhances the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and peroxidase (POD), thereby reducing free radicals in plants. SOD, as the first line of defense against superoxide radicals (O₂⁻), converts them into H₂O₂ [23]. Subsequently, CAT prevents the accumulation of these harmful radicals by breaking down H₂O₂ into water and oxygen [24]. Additionally, POD plays a vital role in combating oxidative stress by reducing lipid peroxidation and protecting cellular membranes from damage caused by free radicals [25]. Caffeic acid, by boosting antioxidant enzyme activities, not only reduces malondialdehyde (MDA) levels a marker of lipid peroxidation damage but also maintains cellular membrane integrity and mitigates oxidative stress damage [26]. Thus, caffeic acid enhances plant resistance to drought stress by improving the antioxidant system’s performance and minimizing free radical-induced damage, ultimately contributing to better plant performance under adverse environmental conditions. Moreover, the higher activity of these enzymes in red and pinto beans leads to lower MDA levels (indicating reduced oxidative damage) and improved relative water content (RWC). These mechanisms protect cellular structures and maintain photosynthetic function, which positively impacts plant yield. Several studies have reported that increased antioxidant enzyme activity in plants under drought stress improves drought resistance and enhances grain yield [27, 28].

MDA is produced as a byproduct of lipid peroxidation due to oxidative stress and serves as an indicator of cellular membrane damage [29]. In this study, it was observed that red and pinto beans had lower MDA levels compared to white beans, indirectly indicating a higher resistance of these species to oxidative stress and a reduction in drought-induced damage. This decrease in MDA levels in the resistant beans could be attributed to enhanced antioxidant production and repair mechanisms in these species. It has been reported that drought stress treatments led to an increase in malondialdehyde (MDA) content, a product of lipid peroxidation, in the roots and shoots of canola cultivars, which was negatively and significantly correlated with seed yield [30]. A study on the effects of different drought stress levels on two bean cultivars showed that lipid peroxidation increased with the severity of drought conditions in all genotypes, whereas the fresh weight of the aerial parts, plant height, leaf number and area, and relative water content (RWC) decreased under various drought stress levels [31]. On the other hand, proline is recognized as an osmotic compound in plants that accumulates under stress conditions, including drought [32]. The increased proline levels in pinto and red beans reflect their higher capacity to maintain osmotic balance and reduce osmotic pressure caused by drought. Proline can help prevent cell dehydration, preserve enzymatic activities, and stabilize protein structures [33]. Additionally, proline acts as an antioxidant, protecting against oxidative damage. This mechanism, along with other features such as antioxidant enzyme activity and the production of caffeic acid, contributes to the greater drought resistance of red and pinto beans compared to white beans. It has been reported that increased proline accumulation in plants under drought stress enhances plant resilience and performance [34].

Drought resistance in plants is primarily achieved through physiological and biochemical adjustments that affect seed performance and biological functions [35]. In this study, photosynthetic characteristics were significantly improved in red and pinto beans, which helped these species become more resistant to drought. The relationship between these traits and the plant’s defense mechanisms is intricately interconnected. Caffeic acid, by enhancing the activity of antioxidant enzymes, can reduce damage caused by free radicals and provide a better environment for photosynthetic activities. This compound influences the regulation of net photosynthesis and can increase photosynthetic efficiency in plants by reducing oxidative stress induced by drought. Net photosynthesis depends on the production of carbohydrates and energy in the plant, which is directly influenced by environmental factors, including soil moisture and light intensity [36]. Caffeic acid, by reducing cellular membrane degradation and improving the antioxidant system’s performance, aids in maintaining cellular structures during the photosynthetic process, leading to increased efficiency [21].

Net photosynthesis, as the most important process for energy production in plants, is directly influenced by stomatal conductance [37]. Under drought conditions, stomatal closure leads to a reduction in stomatal conductance, resulting in a decrease in net photosynthesis [38]. While the reduction in photosynthesis due to stomatal closure is observed in most plants, red and pinto beans have managed to compensate for this decrease through various mechanisms. In these beans, which show higher intercellular CO₂ concentration under drought conditions, this trait indicates the plant’s greater ability to optimize the use of available CO₂ resources and reduce the damage caused by stomatal closure. In these plants, stomatal opening leads to an increase in intercellular CO₂ concentration, which continues photosynthetic activity in an optimal manner. These physiological mechanisms directly impact seed and biological performance [39]. A reduction in photosynthesis is typically associated with decreased seed and biological performance. However, in plants that have increased drought resistance through the enhanced production of antioxidant compounds such as caffeic acid and antioxidant enzymes like SOD, CAT, and POD, or by increasing proline production and reducing MDA, photosynthetic performance and, ultimately, seed performance are better maintained. Thus, red and pinto beans, by improving their physiological and biochemical performance, were able to reduce drought-induced damage and maintain or even enhance seed performance under water-limited conditions. Similar studies, such as those by Fang and Xiong [40] and Yang et al. [41], have shown that plants capable of using physiological mechanisms like better stomatal control, CO₂ balance maintenance, and reduced evaporation under drought conditions can sustain better performance and higher drought resistance. These studies demonstrate that plants able to continue photosynthetic activities while maintaining stomatal function under water scarcity exhibit higher performance [40 and 41].

Despite the superior physiological and biochemical responses observed in red and pinto beans under drought stress—including higher levels of caffeic acid, antioxidant enzyme activity, and photosynthetic efficiency—the highest yield was recorded in the white bean variety. This apparent discrepancy can be attributed to the intrinsic genetic yield potential of the varieties. Red and pinto beans activate stronger defense mechanisms under drought conditions, which primarily contribute to stress tolerance and survival (e.g., maintenance of membrane integrity and photosynthetic activity), rather than maximizing reproductive output. In contrast, the white bean, despite its lower stress tolerance indicators, seems to possess a higher genetic capacity for yield production, likely due to more efficient assimilate partitioning, a greater allocation of resources to reproductive organs, and a higher harvest index. Similar findings have been reported in other crops, where genotypes with greater physiological resilience under stress did not necessarily produce higher yields [51, 52]. These observations suggest that yield performance under drought is not solely determined by stress tolerance traits but also by the genetic predisposition of each genotype for reproductive efficiency. Therefore, the observed yield differences reflect a complex interplay between stress mitigation capacity and inherent productivity potential.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that red and pinto beans exhibit greater drought resistance than white beans, primarily through enhanced antioxidant responses, increased caffeic acid and proline levels, and improved photosynthetic efficiency. Elevated expression of PAL, C4H, and C3H genes contributed to higher caffeic acid biosynthesis, supporting oxidative stress mitigation. These mechanisms helped preserve membrane integrity and physiological function under drought conditions. Despite these advantages, white beans showed higher yield potential, likely due to intrinsic genetic traits. Overall, selecting genotypes with both high stress tolerance and yield potential can support sustainable production in drought-prone environments, and these findings provide a basis for future breeding and genetic improvement programs.

Materials and methods

Time and location of the experiment

To investigate the role of caffeic acid produced in different bean varieties (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) under drought stress conditions, a field experiment was conducted during the 2020–2021 growing season at the research farm of the Faculty of Agriculture, Ferdowsi University of Mashhad. The farm is located 10 km southeast of Mashhad, at a latitude of 36°16’ North and a longitude of 59°36’ East, with an elevation of 985 m above sea level. The region receives an annual rainfall of 233.8 mm, and the average annual temperature is 14.5 °C.

Experimental design and treatments

The experiment was conducted using a split-plot design based on a randomized complete block design with three replications. The main plots included different irrigation levels (100%, 75%, and 50% of the plant’s water requirement), while the subplots consisted of different bean varieties (red bean cultivar “Yaqut”, white bean cultivar “Dorsa”, and pinto bean cultivar “Kousha”). The bean seeds were obtained from the National Bean Research Center of Khomein.

Land Preparation and planting

Land preparation operations, including plowing, disking, and leveling, were carried out in accordance with regional practices in the spring of 2021. All seeds were disinfected before planting using Benomyl (2 g. kg⁻¹ of seed). Subsequently, the seeds were sown in each subplot in six rows, each 4 m long, with a row spacing of 50 cm and a plant spacing of 10 cm, at a depth of 5 cm.

Field operations

Seed sowing was carried out in late May. The first irrigation occurred immediately after planting, and subsequent irrigations were performed every four to seven days until the onset of drought stress, based on the environmental conditions of the region. Drought stress treatments were applied at the third trifoliate leaf stage of the bean plants and continued until the final irrigation.

The required irrigation water volume for each stage of irrigation for the non-stress treatment (100% of plant’s water requirement) was calculated using an A-class evaporation pan. The daily evaporation from the pan was measured, and based on the pan coefficient and crop coefficient, the irrigation water volume for each stage was determined using the following equation:

V = irrigation volume (m³), PE = evaporation from the A-class evaporation pan (mm), KC = crop coefficient, A = irrigated area (m²) and Ei = irrigation efficiency.

The crop coefficient for beans varies throughout its growth stages: 0.15 during early growth, 1.15 during mid-growth, and 0.26 at harvest. For the treatments with 75% and 50% irrigation, 75% and 50% of the irrigation volume required for the non-stress treatment were applied, respectively. The plots were irrigated using polyethylene pipes, and the water volume entering the plots was monitored using water meters.

Measurement of traits

To measure physiological and biochemical traits and investigate the expression of genes involved in caffeic acid synthesis, sampling was carried out at the beginning of the flowering stage.

Caffeic acid measurement

Samples of roots, stems, and leaves of the bean plants were collected and dried at 45 °C. For caffeic acid extraction, 0.5 g of powdered dry tissue was mixed with 10 mL of 80% methanol (v/v) and incubated in a water bath at 40 °C for 1 h with constant shaking. The mixture was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter prior to HPLC analysis. For measuring caffeic acid content, High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) was used with an Agilent 1260 Infinity model. This system was equipped with a binary pump and a Zorbax Eclipse Plus C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, 5 μm). A UV-VIS detector set at 325 nm was used to detect caffeic acid. Separation was achieved using a mobile phase consisting of solvent A (water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile), with a flow rate of 1 mL/min in gradient mode [42].

Antioxidant enzymes

To measure the activity of antioxidant enzymes, 100 mg of leaf sample was homogenized using a cold-ice extraction method with 1000 µL of 0.1 M potassium-phosphate buffer (pH = 7.8) containing 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). After centrifugation at 12,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C, the clear supernatant was separated. Catalase activity (EC 1.11.1.6) was measured by assessing the initial reduction of hydrogen peroxide, following the method of Velikova et al. [43]. The reaction solution included 1 mL of 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH = 7), the extracted enzyme, and 33 mM hydrogen peroxide. The reduction in absorbance at 240 nm per minute was considered as one unit of catalase activity (extinction coefficient 40 mM⁻1 cm⁻1). Peroxidase activity (EC 1.11.1.7) was calculated based on the method of Srinivas et al. [44] by measuring the formation of tetraguaiacol through absorbance at 470 nm, using an extinction coefficient of 26.6 mM⁻1 cm⁻1. The reaction mixture contained 1 mL of 20 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH = 6), 5 mM guaiacol (2-methoxyphenol), 1 mM hydrogen peroxide, and the extracted enzyme. The reaction was allowed to proceed for one minute, and one unit of peroxidase activity was defined as the oxidation of 1 µmol of guaiacol per minute. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was measured according to the method of Giannopolitis and Ries [45]. The enzyme activity was assessed photometrically. A spectrophotometer was calibrated at 560 nm.

Photosynthetic parameters

Photosynthetic parameters were measured using the Li6400XT device. Theywere net photosynthesis rate, intercellular CO2 concentration, stomatal conductance, stomatal resistance, and transpiration rate. The third and fourth leaves from the terminal part of the plant were selected for these measurements, and representative plants with optimal growth were chosen for each treatment. Measurements were carried out between 8:50 and 11:00 AM, with the LED light intensity set at 800 µmol m² s⁻¹.

Malondialdehyde (MDA)

Lipid peroxidation was measured by determining MDA content using the method of Heath and Packer [46]. For this purpose, 100 mg of fresh leaf tissue was homogenized in 1000 µL of trichloroacetic acid (TCA). After centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, 200 µL of the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was then mixed with 800 µL of 20% TCA containing 0.5% thiobarbituric acid. After incubating the mixture in a water bath at 95 °C for 30 min, absorbance at 532 nm and 600 nm was recorded. The difference in absorbance at these two wavelengths was used to calculate the MDA content, with an extinction coefficient of 1.55 × 10⁵ M⁻¹ cm⁻¹. Proline

For proline measurement, 100 mg of fresh leaf sample was homogenized in 1 mL of sulfosalicylic acid in a 1.5 mL microtube using a homogenizer. After centrifugation at 3000 g for 5 min, the insoluble material was separated, and 200 µL of the clear upper solution was collected. To this solution, 200 µL of ninhydrin and 200 µL of glacial acetic acid were added. For preparing the ninhydrin reagent, 5 mg of ninhydrin was mixed with 800 µL of phosphoric acid and 120 µL of acetic acid. The absorbance was then read at 520 nm [47].

2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH)

Antioxidant activity was assessed using the free radical 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH). A 0.1 mM DPPH solution was prepared in methanol. For the assay, 2 mL of the DPPH solution was added to 1 mL of the sample extract and mixed thoroughly. The resulting mixture was incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. The absorbance of the sample was then measured at 517 nm using a spectrophotometer, and the percentage of free radical inhibition was calculated [48].

Gene expression

RNA extraction was performed following the method of Chang et al. [50]. To construct cDNA, 2 µg of RNA was transferred into a tube, and 1 µL of Oligo DT (5 mM) and 2 µL of dNTP (5 mM) were added. The volume of the mixture was adjusted to 13 µL. The tubes were incubated at 65 °C for 5 min, then placed on ice for 1 min and centrifuged for 5 s. Afterward, 4 µL of FSB buffer, 1 µL of each DTT (0.1 mM), RNase Out (1 mM), and SSIII (1 mM) were added. The tubes were incubated at 50 °C for 60 min, followed by 15 min at 70 °C. Subsequently, 0.5 µL of RNaseH was added, and the tubes were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Finally, deionized MQ water was added to reach a final volume of 100 µL, completing the cDNA synthesis.

For qRT-PCR, the synthesized cDNA was diluted 1:10. Then, in each reaction, 10 µL of Master Q-RT-PCR (Invitrogen), 10 µL of diluted cDNA, and 1 µL of primer were used. Primers were designed using Primer 3 and Oligo Analyzer software and are listed in Table 1. The Rotor-Gene Q (Qiagen) was used to perform the qRT-PCR.

The qRT-PCR steps included an initial 4-minute denaturation at 94 °C, followed by 50 cycles. Each cycle consisted of 10 s at 95 °C, 15 s at 60 °C, and 20 s at 72 °C. After each cycle, a melting curve program (ranging from 72 °C to 95 °C with a 5-second pause at each temperature) was performed, with fluorescence being recorded at 72 °C. The gene expression levels were analyzed quantitatively using comparative analysis against the reference gene with Rotor-Gene Q Series software. Control samples using water instead of the RNA samples were used to ensure no primer dimers affected the results. Data were presented as the mean of four samples (four replicates), and differences were compared using standard error.

Statistical analysis

The SAS 9.4 software was used for data analysis, and for plotting, Excel (Ver. 2019) was utilized. The comparison of means was carried out using Duncan’s multiple range test, and a 95% confidence level was considered for all tests and analyses. Correlation analysis was performed in R (v. 4.0.2) using the corrplot package.
