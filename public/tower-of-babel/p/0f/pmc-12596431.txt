Serum immunoglobulin G glycosylation profiling in breast cancer using a 56-lectin microarray and subtype-specific patterns
Authors: Li Peng, Mingwei Ma, Yanna Zhang, Zhen Zhang, Jingjing Meng, Siting Li, Dachun Zhao, Jialin Zhao, Feng Mao, Chaojun Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProtein glycosylation is the enzymatic addition of sugar chains to specific protein sites and part of this process has a crucial functional modification associated with carcinogenesis and cancer progression. This study aimed to profile and confirm aberrant immunoglobulin G (IgG) protein glycosylation in sera obtained from individuals with breast cancer (BC), with emphasis on breadth of lectin panel and subtype-specific patterns.MethodsA total of 185 sera underwent lectin microarray analysis with 56 lectins. Validation was performed on sera from an additional 20 BC patients and 6 healthy controls.ResultsDifferential binding intensities of IgG glycans to Agaricus bisporus lectins (ABA) and Salvia sclarea (SSA) were observed between BC patients and healthy controls (P<0.05). In hormone receptor-positive (HR+) vs. HR− BC, lectins Hippeastrum hybrid lectin (HHL), Morniga M (MNA-M), datura stramonium lectin (DSL), and iris hybrid lectin (IRA) showed significantly lower glycan levels (P<0.05). human epidermal growth factor receptor-2-positive (HER2+) vs. HER2-negative (HER2−) BC exhibited significantly higher glycan levels for lectins Jacalin, Artocarpus integrifolia (AIA), MNA-M, HHL, narcissus pseudonarcissus lectin (NPL), Galanthus nivalis lectin (GNL), and soybean agglutinin (SBA) (P<0.05). Verification confirmed increased ABA and SSA in BC sera (P<0.05). SSA, HHL, and NPL recognized glycans in serum IgG from BC subtypes, as confirmed by lectin microarray (P<0.05). This study utilized lectin microarray analysis to profile changes in serum IgG glycosylation patterns in BC.ConclusionsBy employing a broad 56-lectin microarray and subtype analysis, our results reveal distinct serum IgG glycosylation profiles associated with different BC molecular markers. These findings offer a novel framework for investigating disease pathogenesis and identifying candidate biomarkers for BC.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Serum immunoglobulin G (IgG) glycosylation patterns differed significantly between breast cancer (BC) patients and healthy controls in certain IgG subclasses.

• In subgroup analysis, these subclass-specific differences were more pronounced in stage II/III patients and correlated with tumor size and hormone receptor status.

What is known and what is new?

• Prior work has shown that altered IgG glycosylation exists in various cancers and inflammatory diseases, and sometimes correlates with clinical outcomes. But evidence in BC is limited, often lacking subclass resolution and clinical linkage.

• In this study, we profiled subclass-specific IgG glycosylation in BC and matched controls, revealing distinct subclass glycan changes and their associations with tumor stage, receptor status, and biomarkers. Using a 56-lectin microarray with subtype stratification, we uncovered glycosylation signatures tied to different molecular subtypes of BC.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Subclass-specific IgG glycosylation may represent a novel biomarker dimension in BC, deepening our understanding of disease biology and immune interactions. These findings warrant validation in larger prospective cohorts and mechanistic investigations into how glycosylation alterations influence tumor-immune dynamics.

Introduction

Despite decades of advancements and substantial progress in breast cancer (BC) research, it remains one of the most frequently diagnosed human cancers in the world. In 2020, BC ranked as the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths, with approximately 2.3 million new cases, representing 11.7% of all newly diagnosed cancers, and over 683,000 cancer-related deaths, accounting for 6.9% of all cancer-related deaths (1). Histologically, BC encompasses two subtypes, which can be divided into non-invasive and invasive forms (2). Molecular heterogeneity adds complexity, manifesting in distinct tumor cell biological behaviors and clinical characteristics (3).

To enhance treatment strategies, molecular classification based on estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 (HER2) expression is crucial (4). This classification allows for targeted interventions, such as tamoxifen for receptor-positive BC or epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors and tyrosine-kinase inhibitors (TKIs) (5,6). The varying responses among different BC subtypes underscore the importance of tailored therapeutic approaches (7,8). Urgent attention and further research are imperative to address the intricacies in molecular subtypes of BC and advance our understanding for improved prevention, early diagnosis, and treatment.

Glycosylation, a site-specific modification involving the addition of sugar chains to molecules, typically occurs as a posttranslational modification of proteins, resulting in the formation of glycoproteins (9). This modification plays a crucial role in glycoprotein folding, cellular homeostasis, and immune regulation (10). A notable example is the ABO blood types, where the glycosylation of red blood cell antigens forms the A, B, AB, or O blood types, crucial for blood transfusion compatibility (11). Glycoproteins, particularly lectins expressed on cell surfaces, extracellular matrices, or within cells, regulate cell-cell recognition, communication, and adhesion (12,13).

These glycoproteins are integral to physiological cell processes, and alterations in protein glycosylation can contribute to tumorigenesis, cancer progression, and metastasis (14,15). Changes in glycoprotein expression in cancer tissues, akin to carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), serve as biomarkers (16). CA15-3, a highly glycosylated mucin-1 (MUC1) epitope, is an established serum biomarker for BC diagnosis and monitoring disease progression (17). The further study of glycoproteins, and protein or lipid glycosylation can lead to the discovery of novel biomarkers for precision medicine and cancer-targeted therapy (18). Immunoglobulin G (IgG) glycosylation is mostly N-glycosylation linked to an Asn in its heavy chain constant region. Changes in IgG glycosylation are reported to play important roles in the regulation of effector functions (19,20). Aberrant IgG glycosylation has also been recognized as biomarker for various cancers (21). The present study employed the emerging lectin microarray to evaluate abnormal protein glycosylation levels, specifically focusing on the serum IgG glycosylation profile in BC patients, autoimmune disease patients, and healthy individuals. Microarray data for selected glycoproteins were subsequently validated using lectin blot analysis. This method provides valuable insights into the glycan structure of glycoproteins and identifies the carbohydrate-binding specificities of lectins without releasing glycans from glycoproteins (22).

Recent studies have begun to characterize IgG glycosylation and lectin binding in BC. For example, Hu et al. identified a novel sialylated N-glycosylation at Asn162 of IgG expressed in triple-negative BC (TNBC), associated with immune suppression and cancer invasiveness (23). Another work by Triulzi et al. revealed that CLEC3A, a C-type lectin domain protein expressed in the extracellular matrix of high-grade breast tumors, is correlated with aggressive phenotypes (24). A broader view from He et al. emphasizes mechanisms like GlcNAc branching and increased sialylation on N-glycans of integrins and other glycoproteins in BC, linking these changes with poor prognosis (25). Additionally, Xiang et al. used a lectin microarray (95 lectins) in bladder cancer to detect stage-specific glycopatterns, finding differential binding of Salvia sclarea (SSA), Agaricus bisporus lectins (ABA), and others between non-invasive and invasive disease (26).

These prior studies demonstrate that altered IgG or glycoprotein glycosylation, particularly sialylation and lectin binding, are already established in BC. However, many investigations are restricted in scope: either examining a limited set of lectins or glycan types or focusing on specific subtypes or single glycosyltransferases. In contrast, our study employs an expansive panel of 56 lectins and compares IgG glycan-lectin binding patterns across multiple BC molecular subtypes, in an exploratory, unbiased framework.

The study aims to offer pertinent information on changes in the serum IgG glycosylation expression spectrum in BC tissues. Such insights hold promise for future differential diagnosis of BC subtypes and may serve as potential targets for BC therapy in the realm of precision medicine. We present this article in accordance with the REMARK reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2025-298/rc).

Methods

Patients and serum samples

The study was conducted at Peking Union Medical College Hospital in Beijing, China, gathered a total of 185 serum samples between 2019 and 2021. These samples comprised 61 BC patients, 70 autoimmune disease patients, 35 cardiovascular disease patients (as a non-cancer disease reference), and 19 healthy controls (HCs). In addition to HCs, we included patients with autoimmune and cardiovascular diseases as a non-cancer disease control (DC) group. The rationale for this inclusion was to provide a broader reference background beyond tumor-related conditions, allowing partial comparison with common internal medical diseases. Breast cancer patients were histologically diagnosed and subtyped based on the United States National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Guide, Version 3.2022.4, while autoimmune disease and cardiovascular disease patients served as DCs. Healthy individuals were included as HCs. Clinical characteristics of the BC patients are detailed in Table 1 and sample characteristics and subgroups of BC patients and controls are detailed in Table 2. Blood samples were drawn from each participant, promptly processed for serum isolation in the clinical laboratory of Peking Union Medical College Hospital and stored at −80 ℃ until utilization.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (Beijing, China) (No. S-K1612). Written informed consent was obtained from the study participants prior to study commencement.

Among the 61 BC patients, the surgical interventions included mastectomy with axillary lymph node dissection (19/61, 31.1%), mastectomy with sentinel lymph node biopsy (17/61, 27.8%), lumpectomy with axillary lymph node dissection (4/61, 6.4%), and lumpectomy with sentinel lymph node biopsy (15/61, 24.5%). Six patients opted for lumpectomy or mastectomy without axillary lymph node staging, primarily due to old age or personal preference. Subsequent treatments varied, with 41 patients (67.2%) undergoing chemotherapy (doxorubicin/epirubicin, cyclophosphamide, and paclitaxel/docetaxel), 14 patients (23.0%) receiving targeted therapy for HER2 positive tumors (tramdozumab, pertuzumab, and pyrotinib), 34 patients (55.7%) undergoing radiotherapy, and 42 patients (68.8%) with receptor-positive tumors receiving endocrine therapy (tamoxifen, aromatase inhibitors, and goserelin).

To validate the collected data, an additional set comprising 20 BC patients and 6 HCs meeting the same inclusion and exclusion criteria was recruited as the testing set.

Serum lectin microarray assay and data analysis

The glycosylation pattern of serum IgG was assessed using a commercial lectin microarray featuring 56 lectins (BCBIO Biotech, Guangzhou, China). This microarray efficiently detected N-glycosylation in IgG molecules. We selected lectins such as ABA, SSA, Hippeastrum hybrid lectin (HHL), ConA, etc., which are known from previous literature to have preferred binding to glycan structures commonly found on IgG Fc/N-glycans (for example, sialic acid, galactose, mannose). By selecting an appropriate lectin and conducting experiments under suitable conditions, its signal may specifically mirror IgG glycosylation. The procedure involved thawing the lectin microarray from −80 ℃ to room temperature for 30 minutes, followed by incubation with blocking buffer [3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)] at room temperature for two hours. Subsequently, the microarray was washed with PBS and dried in a rotating oven at 500 g for five minutes. Next, 200 µL of each serum sample, diluted at 1:1,000 from the original collections, was added to the lectin microarray, and the incubation took place at 4 ℃ overnight. The following day, the microarray underwent three washes with PBS, and the samples were further incubated with 5 mL of Cy5-labeled goat anti-human IgG antibody (1:1,000; Jackson Laboratory, Barr Haber, ME, USA) in the dark at room temperature for 50 minutes. Afterward, the array was washed three times with PBS and twice with deionized water before being dried in a rotating oven at 500 g at room temperature for five minutes. Microarray data were scanned using the GenePix 4000B microarray scanner (Molecular Device, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) at a wavelength of 635 nm, with a photomultiplier tube setting of 600.

The scan data underwent lectin microarray analysis, involving the extraction of median foreground and background intensity values for each spot on the array. This process utilized the GenePix Pro 6.0 software (Molecular Device) with proprietary gal files. Subsequently, the signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) for each lectin, representing the moderate intensity of the spot foreground relative to the background, was obtained, calculated, and analyzed.

Lectin-probed western blot analysis

Serum protein sample, diluted at 1:100, underwent separation on 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels and was transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). After the location of IgG was determined, the membrane was incubated with a 10× carbohydrate-free blocking solution (1:10; Vector Laboratories Inc., Burlingame, CA, USA) for two hours at room temperature to prevent nonspecific binding. Following this, the membrane was exposed to different Cy3-labeled lectins (1:1,000; GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, UK), including narcissus pseudonarcissus lectin (NPL), HHL, SSA, and ABA, obtained from Vector Laboratories Inc. (Burlingame, CA, USA). This incubation occurred at 4 ℃ overnight. On the subsequent day, the membrane underwent three washes with PBS-Tween 20 (PBST), was visualized using the Typhoon FLA 9500 (GE Medical, Chicago, IL, USA), and the quantification was performed using ImageJ software (National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS 22.0 software (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA), and the data were plotted using the GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Continuous variables were presented in mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), while Student’s t-test was performed to compare data on lectins associated with the BC subtypes. A P value of ≤0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics and follow-up

The study enrolled 61 BC patients, and Table 1 details their clinicopathological characteristics. For patient prognosis, the median follow-up duration was 26.2 months (range: 4.7–49.0 months), and the median progression-free survival (PFS) was 23.5 months (range: 4.7–49.0 months). These patients were clinically staged between IIa to IIIa, and three had lymph node metastases during surgery. Among them, three were HR-positive, and two were HER2-positive. Importantly, all five patients survived during the follow-up period.

Profiling of different lectin levels in sera obtained from BC patients

The study collected sera from 61 BC patients, 70 patients with immune disease, 35 patients with cardiovascular disease, and 19 healthy individuals for lectin microarray analysis (Table 2). To prioritize lectins for further validation, we first applied statistical screening across all 56 lectins. ABA and SSA were among the lectins that showed statistically significant differences between BC and HCs after normalization. Profiling 56 different lectins from the samples (Figure 1), cluster analysis following signal normalization revealed differential binding of serum IgG glycans to ABA and SSA lectins in BC sera compared to HC samples (P<0.05, Table 3).

Examining lectin array data for BC subtypes revealed significant variations. Four lectins [HHL, Morniga M (MNA-M), datura stramonium lectin (DSL), and iris hybrid lectin (IRA)] exhibited lower glycan levels in HR+ BC sera compared to HR− BC sera (P<0.05). In contrast, six lectins [Jacalin, AIA, MNA-M, HHL, NPL, Galanthus nivalis lectin (GNL), and soybean agglutinin (SBA)] displayed higher glycan levels in HER2+ BC samples compared to HER2− BC samples (P<0.05). Additionally, two lectins [SSA and wheat germ agglutinin (WGA)] demonstrated significantly higher glycan levels, while three lectins (Jacalin, AIA, and SBA) exhibited significantly lower glycan levels in HR+/HER2− BC samples compared to HR+/HER2+ BC samples (P<0.05). NPL and HHL displayed significantly higher glycan levels in HR−/HER2+ BC samples compared to HR−/HER2− BC samples. NPL and HHL exhibited significantly lower glycan levels in HR+/HER2+ BC samples compared to HR−/HER2+ BC samples (P<0.05). Importantly, some of these subtype comparisons yielded P-values close to the significance threshold (e.g., HR+/HER2+ vs. HR−/HER2+: HHL, P=0.04; NPL, P=0.03; HR+/HER2− vs. HR+/HER2+: SBA, P=0.04). No statistically significant difference in glycan levels was observed between HR+/HER2− and HR−/HER2− BC samples (Table 4).

Lectin blot validation of glycosylation microarray data

IgG heavy chains were chosen for lectin blot analysis in the context of lectin microarray data for an additional 26 sera (20 from BC patients and 6 from HCs). Initially, two lectins (ABA and SSA, as explained earlier) were employed to recognize glycans in serum IgG from both BC patients and HCs. The obtained data revealed increased binding of IgG glycans to ABA and SSA levels in BC sera compared to HC sera (P<0.05, Figure 2). HHL and MNA-M lectins were applied to recognize glycans in serum IgG samples from HR− and HR+ BC patients. Although the difference was not statistically significant (P>0.05), a trend toward decreased binding of IgG glycans to MNA-M was observed in HR− BC patients (mean: HR− 3.293 vs. HR+ 4.514).

Additionally, MNA-M and Jacalin-AIA lectins were chosen to recognize glycans in serum IgG samples from HER2− and HER2+ BC patients, but the anticipated results were not achieved (P>0.05). Furthermore, SSA, HHL, and NPL were employed to recognize glycans in serum IgG samples from all four subtypes of BCs, as per NCCN guidelines. These data supported the findings from lectin microarrays (P<0.05), and the verification data are presented in Table 5.

Discussion

In the late 1950s and early 1960s, the notion emerged that the sugarcoated tumor cell membrane differs from that of normal cell membranes, linking it to cancer development and progression (27). Currently, it is recognized that approximately half of human proteins undergo glycosylation, and clinically utilized tumor markers predominantly comprise glycoproteins or carbohydrate antigens, exemplified by CEA or CA15-3 as discussed in the Introduction section (28-30). Moreover, certain glycoproteins can enter the bloodstream, serving as biomarkers for early tumor detection and prognosis prediction (31,32). The present study focused on profiling abnormal protein glycosylation in sera obtained from BC patients. Notably, IgG glycans displayed altered binding to lectins ABA and SSA between sera from BC patients and HCs. Additionally, there are differences in the expression of lectins between HR+ and HR− patients (HHL, MNA-M, DSL, and IRA), as well as between HER2+ and HER2− patients (Jacalin, AIA, MNA-M, HHL, NPL, GNL, and SBA). This offers new avenues for research beyond traditional BC classification methods. It is evident that BC patients with varying expressions of HR or HER2 exhibit differences in lectin binding. Subsequently, most of the profile data was verified in HER2− vs. HER2+ BC. The study underscores the utility of the lectin microarray technique in identifying glycosylation changes in sera from BC patients. Furthermore, it revealed aberrant ABA and SSA levels, highlighting distinct profiles of IgG glycosylation across different subtypes of BCs.

Aberrant lectin expression in BC has emerged as a prominent focus in current research. A recent study uncovered altered galectin-3 expression in BC, correlating with lymph node metastasis, ER expression, patient age, and overall survival. Galectin-7 expression was also linked to the prognosis of BC patients (33). Galectin-3 expression notably associates with worse prognosis and disease recurrence in TNBC cases, with galectin-3 knockdown reducing metastatic potential (34). However, studies to date present conflicting findings regarding the role of galectin-3 in patient prognosis and metastatic potential in female BC cases (35). Another study demonstrated that galectin-9 expression contributes to increased BC invasion through the extracellular matrix (36). Moreover, the detection of circulating galectin-3 holds promise as a prognostic marker in BC, with elevated levels observed in patients with breast, colon, and lung cancers (37). These circulating galectins represent potential biomarkers for various cancer types (38).

In human cancer, it was first reported more than 70 years ago that the malignant transformation of normal cells to tumors is associated with the changes in protein glycosylation (39). IgG molecules are the most plentiful antibodies in the human body, and these function in a variety of immune reactions, such as the neutralization of the corresponding antigens or antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (40). Furthermore, a battery of studies has reported on the changes in glycosylation patterns of cancer cells when compared to non-tumor cells (41,42). The lectin microarray, which is a recently developed novel technology, can efficiently detect and analyze the characteristics of complex glycans for the direct assessment of cell global glycosylation patterns, without requiring the release of glycans from cells or macromolecules (43,44). Supported by lectin microarray technology, the detection of IgG glycosylation can help to identify tumor biomarkers in BC patients. Thus, the fluorescence intensity of the specific binding of serum IgG to the 56 lectins in BC was assessed, and this revealed data on the related structure of serum IgG polysaccharide, but not on the increase or decrease in serum IgG level. It was found that the serum IgG obtained from BC had a higher affinity to lectin SSA and ABA, when compared with HC sera or non-tumor sera. Furthermore, four other lectins were identified to have significantly lower glycan levels in HR+ BC, when compared to HR− BC, while another six lectins were observed to have significantly higher glycan levels in HER2+ BC, when compared to HER2− BC. In addition, the assessment of differential IgG glycan binding to lectin levels among the different BC subtypes has shown promise in differentially distinguishing the subtypes of BC. Thus, the present data provide new insights into biomarkers for BC, which have not been previously reported as we know.

Several studies have highlighted the potential role of IgG N-glycosylation in BC pathophysiology, early diagnosis, and therapeutic response (45-47). Our findings, in line with previous reports, suggest that certain glycan structures—such as highly branched N-glycans—may be upregulated in BC patients and could have diagnostic relevance even at early stages. These alterations, particularly when present on the IgG Fc region, might indicate changes in the humoral immune environment and possibly contribute to tumor progression through reduced immunomodulatory capacity (45). It is also worth noting that other studies have suggested endocrine therapies such as anastrozole and tamoxifen may differentially influence glycosylation patterns over time (47), hinting at a potential link between therapeutic intervention and glycan modulation. However, these functional implications remain hypothetical and should be interpreted with caution. Our study is exploratory in nature and provides preliminary evidence rather than definitive conclusions. Future work, including glycoproteomic profiling, mechanistic assays, and clinical outcome correlations, will be required to validate these observations and to clarify whether IgG glycan profiling can truly serve as a biomarker strategy for early detection, treatment monitoring, or understanding patient responses to targeted therapies.

There are certain limitations in this study. Although we employed a relatively broad 56-lectin panel, it may still not fully capture the complexity of IgG glycosylation patterns in BC. Moreover, the assays were performed on serum samples without complete IgG purification, which could allow some influence from other serum glycoproteins; future studies should incorporate purified IgG to ensure specificity. Another limitation is the lack of associations with clinicopathological features or survival outcomes, which restricts the translational impact of the findings. The inclusion of autoimmune and cardiovascular disease patients as “DCs” also introduces potential confounding, as these conditions can independently alter IgG glycosylation and are heterogeneous in nature. Furthermore, the modest sample size, relatively short follow-up period, and single-center design may reduce generalizability. Larger multicenter studies with carefully matched controls and longitudinal follow-up are warranted to validate and extend these results, and to better define the clinical significance of IgG glycosylation in BC. Our work is exploratory and serves as preliminary data for a future large study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study elucidated changes in serum IgG glycosylation patterns in BC using the lectin microarray. The findings indicate specific glycosylation patterns in BC patients positive for different biomarkers, offering a new avenue for further exploration into the identification of novel BC biomarkers.
