Transgender Identity Dimensions and Unmet Dental Care Needs Among Korean Transgender Adults
Authors: Min-Joo Shin, Yongsun Choi, Yuyi Park, Hyun-Jae Cho, Dong-Hun Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransgender individuals experience oral health disparities, yet the contribution of transgender identity-related psychosocial experiences to unmet dental care remains poorly understood in Asian populations. This study examined associations between four dimensions of the Transgender Identity Survey (TIS) – Pride, Passing, Alienation, and Shame (each scored on a 1-7 scale) – and unmet dental care among Korean transgender adults, using Andersen's Behavioral Model.MethodsCross-sectional data were analysed from 333 Korean transgender adults recruited through community-based sampling. The primary outcome was self-reported unmet dental care since first disclosing or recognizing gender identity. The validated 26-item TIS assessed four identity dimensions. Hierarchical logistic regression adjusted for predisposing, enabling, and need factors, with all subscales entered simultaneously. Stratified analyses examined hormone therapy status and gender identity.ResultsUnmet dental care was reported by 211 participants (63.4%). In the fully adjusted model, Pride (aOR 1.79, 1.28-2.49), Passing (aOR 1.50, 1.19-1.88), and Shame (aOR 1.56, 1.12-2.17) were associated with unmet dental care. Sensitivity analyses identified classical suppression between Pride and Shame (r = −0.80), with only Passing robustly associated across specifications. Gender-affirming hormone therapy was strongly protective (aOR 0.26, 0.15-0.45).ConclusionsPassing emerged as the most robust psychosocial predictor of unmet dental care among Korean transgender adults, with Pride and Shame exhibiting classical suppression in joint models. Gender-affirming hormone therapy was a strong enabling factor. These findings suggest that trans-affirming dental care should address interpersonal competency and anticipated identity exposure barriers, tailored by transition stage and gender identity.

Introduction

The right to the highest attainable standard of health is recognized as a fundamental human right.1 Yet, this right is not equally realized. Discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity continues to generate marked inequalities within health systems worldwide,2 and transgender individuals – whose gender identity differs from the sex assigned at birth – are among the most structurally marginalized groups in this regard. Healthcare systems that have been organized around a binary model of sex and gender frequently fail to accommodate transgender patients, producing a cascade of access barriers that ultimately compromise health.3,4 These structural exclusions are not abstract; they may translate into measurable disparities in service utilization, quality of care, and clinical outcomes across every domain of medicine.

Transgender populations consistently report higher rates of unmet medical care than cisgender peers.5 Within this broader pattern, oral health disparities are particularly pronounced: a recent systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that transgender individuals exhibit poorer oral hygiene, higher rates of untreated dental disease, and lower utilization of dental services compared with the general population.6 A scoping review of 2SLGBTQ+ oral health found that transgender people scored significantly higher on the decayed-missing-filled teeth (DMFT) index and reported greater dissatisfaction with their oral health.7 The dental encounter is, however, distinct from most other healthcare encounters: it requires patients to remove face coverings, lie supine with the mouth open, and submit to close visual and tactile inspection of the head, jaw, and neck – anatomical regions that may be cues for gender perception. For transgender individuals who experience body-incongruence-related distress (gender dysphoria), the dental chair may therefore function not merely as a clinical site but as a setting in which gender identity is involuntarily exposed. The psychological threshold to entering this setting may, accordingly, be higher than in any other branch of medicine.

Most existing research on transgender oral health has concentrated on external and structural barriers. These include discriminatory attitudes of healthcare providers, misgendering during dental encounters, refusal of treatment, low income, lack of insurance coverage, and unstable legal status.8, 9, 10, 11 A US study reported that approximately 48% of transgender and gender non-binary participants avoided dental visits because of concerns related to their gender identity, and identified provider behaviours such as misgendering and incorrect pronoun use as key contributors.10 A Brazilian cross-sectional study among LGBTQIA+ adults found that discrimination experienced in dental services was associated with worse self-perceived oral health and oral-health-related quality of life.12 Andersen's Behavioral Model of Health Services Use13,14 has been applied extensively to dental utilization in general populations,15, 16, 17 and within these applications, financial enabling factors – income, insurance, out-of-pocket cost – have repeatedly emerged as the dominant determinants of access. This structural framing has been valuable, but it has implicitly positioned transgender individuals primarily as recipients of external barriers rather than as actors whose own internalized experiences of stigma also shape engagement with care.

Two important gaps in the literature follow from this emphasis. First, internalized transphobia – the extent to which negative societal attitudes about transgender identity are absorbed into the self18 – has rarely been examined as a distinct predictor of dental care utilization, despite extensive evidence from the broader minority stress framework19,20 that internalized stigma can drive avoidance of healthcare independently of any encounter with overt discrimination. Shame may operate as a form of self-imposed exclusion: individuals may decline to seek care not because they have been refused but because they anticipate that their identity will become visible in a setting where they cannot control its expression. Second, gender-affirming care – particularly hormone therapy – has been studied primarily as a therapeutic intervention for gender dysphoria, with comparatively little attention to its potential downstream effects on broader healthcare engagement. It is plausible that gender-affirming care, by enhancing congruence between identity and embodied appearance, also reduces the psychological cost of medical encounters and thereby functions as an enabling factor in Andersen's sense – a hypothesis that has not been tested in dental contexts. Furthermore, empirical data on transgender oral health from East Asia, where the social meaning of transgender identity, the structure of healthcare delivery, and cultural norms around bodily exposure differ from Western contexts, are virtually absent from the international literature.

This East Asian gap is particularly consequential for South Korea, where transgender individuals face a sociocultural environment that is notably more hostile than those of most Western countries in which existing transgender oral health research has been conducted. On the 2019 Franklin & Marshall Global Barometer of Gay Rights, South Korea was one of only three OECD countries to receive a grade of F, a designation indicating active persecution of LGBTQ communities.21 Public opinion data are consistent with this assessment: a 2023 Pew Research Center survey found that 56% of South Koreans opposed the legalization of same-sex marriage, substantially higher than levels of opposition reported in regional comparators such as Japan and Vietnam.22 Despite at least eleven legislative proposals submitted to the National Assembly since 2007, South Korea has not yet enacted a comprehensive anti-discrimination act that would explicitly protect against discrimination based on gender identity, leaving transgender individuals with limited legal recourse in employment, housing, education, or healthcare settings. The institutional dimensions of this hostility were starkly illustrated by the case of Hui-Soo Byun, the country's first openly transgender soldier, who was forcibly discharged from the Republic of Korea Army in January 2020 after undergoing gender-affirming surgery in Thailand and was found dead by suicide in March 2021; a subsequent court ruling deemed her discharge unlawful, and in 2024 a state review committee posthumously recognized her death as having occurred in the line of duty.23 Such delayed and posthumous vindications, however, do not undo the years during which Korean transgender individuals must navigate the everyday consequences of institutional rejection, including in healthcare encounters. The internalized stigma examined in the present study cannot, therefore, be understood adequately without reference to this distinctive sociocultural backdrop.

To address these gaps, the present study applies Andersen's Behavioral Model to a community-based sample of Korean transgender adults, with three specific aims: (1) to characterize the prevalence of unmet dental care needs in this population; (2) to examine whether the four dimensions of transgender identity-related psychosocial experience measured by the validated Transgender Identity Survey24 – Pride, Passing, Alienation, and Shame – are independently associated with unmet dental care after adjustment for predisposing, enabling, and need factors; and (3) to evaluate whether gender-affirming hormone therapy and other indicators of healthcare engagement function as enabling factors that mitigate this disparity, with attention to potential effect modification by transition stage and gender identity.

Materials & methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design to investigate the determinants of healthcare utilization among transgender individuals in South Korea. A total of 333 transgender adults participated in the survey, providing data on their clinical experiences and psychosocial perceptions. To ensure the inclusion of this marginalized population while maintaining participant anonymity, recruitment was conducted via snowball sampling through community-based organizations and online platforms dedicated to transgender rights. The survey was administered online between July 2024 and June 2025. The study protocol and data collection procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University School of Dentistry (IRB No. S-D20230018). All participants provided informed electronic consent before entering the survey.

Eligibility required participants to meet at least one of three criteria, following the operational definition of Lee et al (2018)25: (1) having undergone one or more gender-affirming surgical procedures; (2) having received a clinical diagnosis of gender identity disorder (ICD-10 F64.0) following gender identity assessment; or (3) having ever received gender-affirming hormone therapy. Additional eligibility criteria were age 18 years or older and Korean residency with sufficient Korean-language proficiency.

Sample size adequacy was evaluated with reference to the fully adjusted model (Model 4), which contained 13 predictor variables (four TIS subscales, age, two gender identity indicators, education, urban residence, subjective social position, employment, gender-affirming hormone therapy, and poor self-rated oral health). With 211 unmet-care events, the events-per-variable ratio was 16.2, exceeding the conventional minimum of 10 recommended for stable logistic regression estimates.26 Green's heuristic (N ≥ 50 + 8k)27 yields a minimum required sample of 154 for a 13-predictor model, which the analytic sample of 333 also substantially exceeded. While the sample was therefore adequately powered for the primary model, parameter estimates for less common categorical levels carry wide confidence intervals and are interpreted with corresponding caution.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was self-reported unmet dental care since first disclosing or recognizing one’s gender identity. This was operationalized from a multi-category item asking participants whether, since first disclosing or recognizing their gender identity, they had received needed dental and general medical care: (1) received both dental and general care, (2) needed but did not receive dental care, (3) received dental but not general care, or (4) needed but did not receive both dental and general care. We coded responses (2) and (4) as unmet dental care and responses (1) and (3) as met dental care.

Transgender identity related psychosocial experience: Transgender identity survey

Transgender identity related psychosocial experience assessed with the 26-item Transgender Identity Survey (TIS) developed and validated by Bockting et al24 in a sample of 1,333 transgender adults. The TIS comprises four subscales: Pride (8 items), Passing (7 items), Alienation (3 items), and Shame (8 items). Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree). Each subscale was computed as the mean of constituent items; we did not reverse-score Pride because subscales were treated as distinct constructs rather than aggregated into a total score. Higher scores on each subscale indicate stronger endorsement of its content. Internal consistency in the present sample was excellent for Pride (Cronbach α = 0.94), Passing (α = 0.87), and Shame (α = 0.96), and acceptable for Alienation (α = 0.64).

The English-language TIS was translated into Korean by a bilingual researcher with subject-matter expertise and independently back-translated into English by a second bilingual translator blinded to the original instrument; discrepancies in meaning, idiom, and culturally specific expressions were reconciled through committee discussion to produce the final Korean version administered in this study.

Because the TIS was developed and validated in English-speaking North American samples, we additionally evaluated its psychometric properties in the present Korean sample. Sampling adequacy was excellent (Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin = 0.944; Bartlett's test of sphericity P < .001), and exploratory factor analysis (principal axis factoring with Promax rotation) supported four factors by the Kaiser criterion; 22 of 26 items (84.6%) loaded most strongly on their theoretically expected factor. Full psychometric results are provided in the Supplementary Table 6. The implications of the items that did not replicate the original structure are considered in the discussion section.

Covariates

Covariates were organized according to Andersen's Behavioral Model of Health Services Use.13,14 Predisposing factors comprised demographic and social characteristics: age (continuous variable), gender identity (trans woman, trans man, non-binary; analysed using two indicator variables), and education (high school or less vs. college or higher).

Enabling factors captured resources facilitating healthcare access: urban residence (Seoul or metropolitan area vs. other regions), subjective social position assessed with the 10-point MacArthur Scale of Subjective Social Status,28 employment status (regular/temporary employment vs. other), and current use of gender-affirming hormone therapy (Yes vs. No). Personal income and insurance type were also collected; however, because the sample was demographically homogeneous (mean age 30.9 years, 89.5% employed, 97.0% covered by National Health Insurance, and income tightly clustered around the population median), these objective financial variables offered limited discriminatory power. We therefore selected the MacArthur subjective social position ladder – validated as a sensitive predictor of health outcomes in demographically homogeneous samples – as the primary socioeconomic indicator in the enabling block, and examined income and insurance in sensitivity analyses. Hormone therapy was conceptualized as a personal-level enabling resource: individuals receiving it have already engaged with the medical system, suggesting both willingness and access to navigate transgender-related care.

Need factors captured perceived requirement for dental care, operationalized as poor self-perceived oral health (response of fair, poor, or very poor vs. good or very good).

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics were summarized with means and standard deviations for continuous variables and counts with percentages for categorical variables, stratified by unmet dental care status. Group differences were assessed with independent-samples t-tests and chi-square tests as appropriate.

Hierarchical multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals. All four TIS subscales were entered simultaneously in every model to identify their independent contributions. Model 1 included only the four TIS subscales. Model 2 added predisposing factors. Model 3 added enabling factors (including gender-affirming hormone therapy). Model 4 added the need factor (the fully adjusted model). Model fit was evaluated using −2 log-likelihood and Nagelkerke pseudo-R². Prior to interpretation, we assessed multicollinearity among the simultaneously entered TIS subscales using the inter-subscale Pearson correlation matrix and variance inflation factors (VIF) for Model 4. To verify robustness of the TIS estimates to the omission of objective financial variables, we conducted sensitivity analyses adding income and insurance type to Model 4. Full diagnostic and sensitivity results are reported in the Supplementary tables.

To examine effect modification by transition stage and gender identity, we conducted stratified analyses by repeating the fully adjusted model within strata defined by gender-affirming hormone therapy status (not currently using vs. currently using) and gender identity (trans women vs. trans men). Non-binary participants (n = 18) were excluded from the gender identity stratification due to small sample size. Within each stratum, the stratifying variable itself was excluded from the covariate set, while all other Andersen covariates (predisposing, enabling, and need factors) were retained.

As an exploratory analysis of the relationship between the TIS dimensions and direct healthcare-related discrimination, we conducted a sub-analysis restricted to participants who had used any healthcare service since gender identity disclosure (n = 144), in which perceived healthcare discrimination (mean of three items measuring anticipated prejudicial remarks, perceived likelihood of mistreatment, and perceived prevalence of transgender discrimination in Korean healthcare; α = 0.80) and enacted healthcare discrimination (whether the participant endorsed having directly experienced discrimination as a barrier to care) were added to the fully adjusted model. Because this sub-analysis included only 22 unmet-care events, it is substantially underpowered; its results are reported in the Supplementary Table 1 and interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating only.

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0. Two-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Unmet dental care since first disclosing or recognizing one’s gender identity was reported by 211 of 333 participants (63.4%). Compared with those who had received needed dental care, those with unmet need were less likely to be using hormone therapy (22.3% vs. 45.9%, P < .001), reported higher Passing scores (4.44 vs. 4.10, P = .012), were more likely to be employed (92.4% vs. 84.4%, P = .035), and were less likely to report poor oral health (26.5% vs. 39.3%, P = .021). The two groups did not differ significantly in age, gender identity distribution, education, urban residence, or social position (all P ≥ .10; Table 1). The higher employment rate in the unmet-care group runs counter to the usual Andersen expectation; as detailed below, this bivariate association did not persist after multivariable adjustment.

These associations remained substantially stable as predisposing, enabling, and need factors were added (Models 2-4). In the fully adjusted Model 4, each one-point increase in Pride was associated with 79% greater odds of unmet dental care (aOR 1.79, 95% CI 1.28-2.49), each one-point increase in Passing pressure was associated with 50% greater odds (aOR 1.50, 95% CI 1.19-1.88), and each one-point increase in Shame was associated with 56% greater odds (aOR 1.56, 95% CI 1.12-2.17). Alienation again showed no association.

Among Andersen covariates, gender-affirming hormone therapy was the strongest single predictor: hormone therapy users had approximately 74% lower odds of unmet dental care than non-users (aOR 0.26, 95% CI 0.15-0.45). Poor self-perceived oral health was associated with lower odds of unmet need (aOR 0.52, 95% CI 0.29-0.92), an inverse pattern consistent with symptom-driven utilization. No other covariate reached statistical significance. Model fit improved substantially with the addition of covariates (Nagelkerke pseudo-R²: 0.065 in Model 1, 0.075 in Model 2, 0.190 in Model 3, and 0.209 in Model 4; Table 2).

Multicollinearity diagnostics for Model 4 were acceptable: the maximum variance inflation factor was 4.93 (Shame), below the conventional threshold of 5, although the four subscales were intercorrelated—most notably a strong negative correlation between Pride and Shame (Pearson r = −0.80). Sensitivity analyses that additionally adjusted for income and insurance type left the TIS estimates essentially unchanged (|ΔaOR| ≤ 0.04), and neither income (aOR 1.01, P = .82) nor Medical Aid coverage (aOR 0.25, P = .06) was independently associated with unmet dental care. Full diagnostics and sensitivity results are provided in the Supplementary tables 2-4.

To address the strong negative correlation between Pride and Shame (r = −0.80), we conducted additional sensitivity analyses entering each subscale separately and testing an identity-valence composite (Pride − Shame). When Shame was removed, Pride’s coefficient attenuated substantially (aOR 1.28, 95% CI 1.03-1.59, P = .024); when Pride was removed, Shame lost significance (aOR 1.00, 95% CI 0.81-1.24, P = .97). In single-entry models with each TIS subscale alone, both Pride (aOR 1.13, 95% CI 0.94-1.37) and Shame (aOR 1.07, 95% CI 0.90-1.25) were non-significant, whereas Passing remained robustly associated (aOR 1.42, 95% CI 1.15-1.75, P = .001). The identity-valence composite was not associated with unmet dental care (aOR 1.07, 95% CI 0.96-1.20, P = .23). These patterns are consistent with classical suppression between Pride and Shame in the joint model and identify Passing as the most clearly independent psychosocial predictor (Table 4).

Effect modification by hormone therapy status and gender identity

These stratified analyses were prespecified as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and the resulting subgroup estimates – based on smaller strata with correspondingly wide confidence intervals – should be interpreted with caution. Stratified analyses revealed substantial effect modification of the TIS associations across hormone therapy status and gender identity (Table 3). Exploratory sub-analysis: Healthcare-related discrimination

In an exploratory sub-analysis restricted to the 144 participants who had used any healthcare service since gender identity disclosure (Supplementary Table 1), perceived and enacted healthcare discrimination were added to the fully adjusted model. Because this sub-sample contained only 22 unmet-care events, the model is substantially underpowered, and its coefficients are numerically unstable. We therefore refrain from drawing substantive inferences from this analysis; the point estimates are reported in the Supplementary tables for transparency only.

Discussion

This study examined associations between transgender identity-related psychosocial dimensions and unmet dental care needs in a community-based sample of 333 Korean transgender adults, framed within Andersen's Behavioral Model of Health Services Use. Three findings stand out. First, the prevalence of unmet dental care was strikingly high: more than six in ten participants (63.4%) reported needing but not receiving dental treatment since first disclosing or recognizing their gender identity. Second, Passing was robustly associated with unmet dental care across all model specifications, with Pride and Shame functioning as related but distinct identity-related dimensions whose coefficients exhibit classical suppression in joint models. Together, these findings indicate that identity-salience and identity-related psychosocial barriers operate above and beyond demographic, socioeconomic, and discrimination-related factors. Third, gender-affirming hormone therapy emerged as the strongest single protective factor, reducing the odds of unmet dental care by approximately 74%.

Our high prevalence estimate aligns with broader evidence that transgender populations experience marked oral health service disparities.6,7 An earlier US study reported that approximately 48% of transgender and gender non-binary respondents avoided dental visits because of gender identity-related concerns,10 and a Brazilian cross-sectional study found that discrimination in dental services and self-perceived treatment need were associated with worse oral health-related quality of life among LGBTQIA+ individuals.12 Our findings extend this evidence base in two important ways. First, we document that TIS-measured transgender identity dimensions – measured with a validated multidimensional instrument – predicts unmet dental care independently of perceived and enacted discrimination, addressing a gap noted in recent reviews.6,29 Second, we provide, to our knowledge, the first analysis of these relationships in an East Asian setting, where the social meaning of transgender identity, the structure of healthcare delivery, and cultural norms around bodily exposure differ markedly from Western contexts. For broader context, a recent nationwide multilevel analysis of Korean adults reported that self-perceived unmet dental needs remain prevalent in the general population and are patterned by social and functional determinants30; the markedly higher burden observed in our transgender sample underscores the additional psychosocial barriers this population faces.

A central conceptual contribution of this study is the integration of two complementary frameworks. Within Andersen's Behavioral Model, the four TIS dimensions are most appropriately classified as predisposing factors – specifically, health beliefs that exist prior to and independently of any individual healthcare encounter. The Minority Stress Model18,19 provides the missing psychological mechanism by which these health beliefs come to function as access barriers: chronic exposure to societal anti-transgender stigma is internalized as Shame and amplifies Passing-related vigilance, which then operates as a psychosocial barrier that may effectively neutralize objective enabling resources (e.g., insurance coverage, geographic proximity to providers). In other words, even when structural enabling factors are favourable, TIS-measured psychosocial barriers can render dental services psychologically inaccessible. This intersection helps explain why the protective effect of hormone therapy – an enabling factor that simultaneously reduces body-incongruence-related distress – was substantially larger than the effects of conventional enabling factors such as urban residence or employment in our sample. The combined framework thus offers a more complete account of unmet dental care among transgender populations than either model can provide alone.

Why are Passing and Shame such consistent predictors of unmet dental care? We propose that the dental encounter is unusually demanding from a passing perspective. Unlike most other healthcare encounters, dental care requires the patient to remove face coverings, lie supine with the mouth wide open, and submit to close visual and tactile inspection of the head, jaw, and neck – areas that are salient cues for gender perception. For transgender individuals who experience gender-incongruence-related distress, the dental chair may function not as a clinical site but as a setting in which body features they have worked to manage become involuntarily visible. The fear is anticipatory: it does not require any actual discriminatory event to operate as a barrier. This may explain why, in our healthcare-user sub-sample, the association of transgender identity dimensions with unmet dental care persisted after adjusting for both perceived and enacted discrimination experiences (Supplementary Table 1).

The unexpected positive direction of the Pride association warrants particular attention. The TIS Pride subscale captures not only internal affirmation of identity but also outward visibility – items such as 'I have a strong sense of being transgender' and 'I am proud to be transgender' reflect openness rather than concealment of transgender identity. Individuals scoring high on Pride are therefore more likely to be visibly identifiable as transgender in everyday encounters, which can paradoxically heighten – rather than buffer – their sensitivity to anticipated identity-based judgment in clinical settings. In the dental context specifically, where the patient's face and body must be exposed to a provider whose response to that visible identity is unpredictable, greater identity expression may translate into greater anticipatory stigma sensitivity. This interpretation is consistent with prior minority stress research suggesting that visibility of a stigmatized identity can have both protective effects (through social support and identity affirmation) and exposing effects (through increased perceived risk of judgment), depending on the setting and the audience. The Pride finding therefore warrants replication in independent samples and ideally with longitudinal designs that can disentangle identity expression from anticipated discrimination.

A second, complementary mechanism may also contribute to the Pride association. In our sample, Pride was positively correlated with subjective social position (Spearman ρ = 0.30) and income (Supplementary Table 5), indicating that higher-Pride participants tended to occupy more socioeconomically advantaged positions and likely possess greater oral health literacy. This pattern is consistent with the well-documented 'perception–need paradox' in dental services research, in which individuals with greater oral health literacy and higher expectations report unmet needs at higher rates even when objective need is similar.31 A Korean difference-in-differences study similarly found that expanding dental insurance was paradoxically associated with an 8.8 percentage-point increase in perceived unmet need among older adults, attributable to heightened awareness rather than worse access.32 Higher Pride may therefore co-occur with greater awareness of, and higher expectations for, adequate dental care, increasing the likelihood that needs are reported as unmet (Supplementary Table 5).

The stratified analyses (Table 3) added a clinically important nuance to these findings. Among participants not currently receiving hormone therapy, the dominant barriers were internal psychological dimensions of identity – Pride and Shame – suggesting that when external transition has not begun, the psychological burden of transgender identity itself is the primary determinant of dental care avoidance. In contrast, among hormone users, anticipated visibility (Passing) became the principal barrier, consistent with the dental encounter's specific demands on facial and oral exposure becoming more salient when external transition is in progress and identity-related body modifications are at stake. A parallel pattern was evident across gender identity: Pride and Shame dominated among trans women, while Passing pressure dominated among trans men, possibly reflecting different anatomical concerns and different experiences of being read in clinical settings. The opposing pattern for Alienation across the two hormone-status strata is reported for transparency but interpreted with caution, given the brevity of this subscale (3 items, α = 0.64) and the small number of unmet-care cases in the hormone-using subgroup (n = 47); this finding warrants dedicated investigation in future studies designed to characterize transgender community connection as both a normative reference and an enabling resource at different transition stages. Furthermore, the stratified analyses within the hormone-using (n = 103) and trans men (n = 71) subgroups are constrained by relatively modest sample sizes, yielding wider confidence intervals across coefficients. The effect modifications identified here must therefore be viewed as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Rigorous validation in adequately powered samples is essential before leveraging these subgroup-specific insights for stage-tailored intervention.

Together, these stratified findings suggest that internalized barriers to dental care are not uniform across the transgender population but shift in character with transition stage and gender identity. This has direct implications for intervention design: psychological support targeting internalized shame may be most effective for individuals who have not yet initiated medical transition, whereas environmental modifications addressing visibility concerns may be more relevant for those already receiving hormone therapy.

The strong protective effect of hormone therapy is consistent with prior work suggesting that engagement with gender-affirming medical care is associated with broader healthcare integration.7,11 Andersen-based studies of dental utilization in general populations have repeatedly identified income, insurance, and education as the primary enabling factors,15,17 whereas in our transgender-specific sample, the most powerful enabling factor was access to gender-affirming care itself – a finding with direct policy implications. Two mechanisms could underlie this protective association, which the cross-sectional design cannot fully disentangle: a direct body-congruence pathway, in which hormone therapy reduces dysphoria-related avoidance of dental settings, and a healthcare-engagement selection pathway, in which hormone-therapy users have already overcome systemic barriers and possess greater general healthcare access. Two observations are more consistent with a contribution of the direct pathway: the hormone-therapy point estimate remained protective and of similar magnitude after adjusting for perceived and enacted discrimination in the exploratory sub-analysis, and stratified models suggested that Passing-related concerns predominated among hormone users whereas Pride and Shame predominated among non-users—consistent with a transition-stage-specific shift in the qualitative character of barriers. Prospective longitudinal designs are nonetheless required to distinguish these mechanisms definitively.

The protective effect of gender-affirming hormone therapy raises an important policy implication that requires interpretation within the specific Korean healthcare context. The Republic of Korea operates a universal National Health Insurance (NHI) system that covers most general medical and dental care, yet gender-affirming care has historically been excluded from coverage. Hormone therapy must be paid out-of-pocket through private clinics, and gender-affirming surgeries were, until very recently, largely unavailable domestically – many Korean transgender individuals have travelled to Japan or Thailand to access such procedures. The recent opening of dedicated gender-affirming clinics in Korea marks a structural shift, but the legacy of medical-tourism-as-necessity has produced a generation of transgender adults who learned to navigate healthcare as a privatized, foreign, and inaccessible domain. This macro-level structural exclusion does not exist in isolation from the micro-level psychological barriers we documented; the two are mutually reinforcing. A healthcare system that has historically refused to recognize transgender bodies as legitimate objects of care produces, over time, an internalized expectation that one's gender identity will be unwelcome in any clinical encounter – including the dental chair, where the connection to gender-affirming care is not even direct. Strengthening NHI coverage of gender-affirming services may therefore yield benefits well beyond the procedures it covers, by signalling that transgender patients belong within the healthcare system as a whole and reducing the anticipatory shame that prevents engagement with general medical and dental care.

At the level of dental practice, our findings reinforce existing calls for transgender-affirming clinical environments. Cultural competency training that addresses correct pronoun use, gender-neutral intake forms, and visible markers of inclusivity has been recommended in several reviews.11 However, our results suggest that interventions confined to interpersonal behaviour may be insufficient if internalized stigma and anticipated identity exposure remain unaddressed. Complementary strategies – for example, allowing patients to wear masks during procedures where clinically possible, offering preliminary virtual consultations, or providing peer-navigator support – may help to reduce the psychological cost of dental encounters specifically. Stratified findings further suggest that such interventions may need to be tailored: psychological support and identity-affirmation programs for individuals at earlier transition stages, and physical-environment modifications addressing visibility concerns for those further along.

The unexpected bivariate association between employment and unmet dental care (higher employment in the unmet-care group) deserves comment. This association did not persist in the fully adjusted model (aOR 1.55, 95% CI 0.64-3.78), attenuating after adjustment for age, education, and Pride (employed participants reported higher Pride scores). To the extent that any residual signal exists, plausible explanations include time constraints on working individuals seeking elective dental care, and greater workplace-related concern about identity exposure when requesting leave for appointments. We regard this as an anomalous bivariate finding that warrants future investigation rather than a robust independent effect.

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first study to examine the multidimensional structure of TIS-measured transgender identity dimensions as a predictor of unmet dental care in an East Asian transgender population, and one of relatively few applications of Andersen's framework to LGBTQ+ oral health. The sample size (n = 333) is substantial relative to comparable transgender oral health studies. Use of the validated TIS24 allowed disaggregation of transgender psychosocial barriers into theoretically distinct dimensions, with internal consistency in the present sample comparable to or exceeding values reported in the original validation study. Stratified analyses revealed clinically meaningful effect modification that would be missed in a single pooled model.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; the strong association between hormone therapy and reduced unmet dental need may partly reflect reverse causation or shared determinants such as overall healthcare engagement. Longitudinal studies are needed to establish temporal precedence. Second, all variables – including the primary outcome of unmet dental care, oral health status, and psychosocial measures – were self-reported and are therefore subject to recall and social desirability bias. More importantly, our outcome captured perceived rather than normative need: participants reported whether they felt they had needed dental care without receiving it, but we did not have access to clinical examination data that would have established objective treatment need based on caries, periodontal disease, or other dentist-evaluated indicators. The two constructs can diverge substantially: an individual with severe undiagnosed periodontal disease who has avoided all dental encounters may not have any 'perceived need' to report despite having very high normative need. The internalized stigma effects we documented may therefore be even larger when assessed against normative need, as the very mechanism we are studying – anticipatory avoidance – also suppresses recognition of one's own need. Future studies pairing self-reported unmet need with clinical oral examinations would substantially strengthen the evidence base. Third, recruitment relied on community-based and online snowball sampling through transgender community organizations and online platforms. Although this approach is widely used and often necessary for accessing this hidden population, it inevitably over-represents transgender individuals who are sufficiently socially active to be reachable through community networks: those who have disclosed their identity to others, those who participate in advocacy or peer-support spaces, and those with internet access and willingness to engage with research. The most marginalized transgender individuals – those who remain entirely closeted, experiencing homelessness or severe poverty, isolated from any community connection, or having the most severe internalized transphobia – are precisely the group least likely to be reached, and likely the group with the greatest unmet dental care needs. Our 63.4% prevalence estimate should therefore be interpreted as a lower bound on the population-level burden, and our findings on TIS-measured psychosocial barriers as conservative estimates of effects that may be even stronger in unreached subpopulations. Fourth, the stratified analyses (Table 3) – particularly within the hormone-using (n = 103) and trans men (n = 71) subgroups – had reduced statistical power and wider confidence intervals; these findings should be regarded as exploratory and warrant replication in larger samples. Fifth, the primary outcome used a since-disclosure or since-recognition recall window rather than a fixed 12-month period. While this framing is appropriate for capturing identity-related barriers across the transition trajectory, it limits direct comparability of our prevalence estimate with the 12-month estimates commonly reported in the general dental literature, and the indeterminate recall period may introduce variable recall error across participants at different transition stages. Sixth, although the Korean TIS showed internal-consistency reliabilities closely matching the original English validation, exploratory factor analysis indicated that the four-factor structure did not fully replicate in this sample: four items cross-loaded, two of the three Alienation items loaded on other factors (leaving the Alienation factor weakly defined), and Pride and Shame were strongly negatively correlated (r = −0.80), suggesting they may behave as opposite poles of a single identity-valence dimension in this cultural context. The Korean-language factor structure of the TIS therefore has not been formally validated, and cross-population comparisons of subscale scores should be made with caution; formal validation (including confirmatory factor analysis and measurement-invariance testing) is an important direction for future research. Seventh, sensitivity analyses revealed classical suppression between Pride and Shame: each subscale lost significance when the other was removed, and the identity-valence composite (Pride − Shame) was not associated with unmet dental care (Table 4). Conclusion

In a community-based sample of Korean transgender adults, more than six in ten reported unmet dental care since first disclosing or recognizing their gender identity. Passing was identified as the most robust transgender identity-related psychosocial predictor of this disparity, while Pride and Shame exhibited classical suppression in joint models; together, these effects persisted after full adjustment for predisposing, enabling, and need factors organized within Andersen’s Behavioral Model. These stratified patterns, which should be regarded as exploratory, suggested that the configuration of identity-related psychosocial barriers shifts with transition stage and gender identity: internal psychological dimensions dominate prior to medical transition, while anticipated visibility dominates among those receiving hormone therapy. Gender-affirming hormone therapy emerged as the strongest single protective factor. Crucially, these findings underscore that the journey to dental care is obstructed not solely by the external realities of prohibitive costs and systemic discrimination but is profoundly hindered by psychosocial burdens related to anticipated identity exposure. Reducing oral health disparities for transgender populations will, therefore, require complementary, stage-tailored interventions at the levels of clinical practice (trans-affirming dental care), structural integration (gender-affirming care as a gateway to general healthcare), and community-based identity affirmation.
