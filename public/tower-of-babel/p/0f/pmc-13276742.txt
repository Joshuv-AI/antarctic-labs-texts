Kidney Transplant Candidates With High Frequency Deceased Donor Offers
Authors: Jesse D Schold, Susana Arrigain, S Ali Husain, Miko Yu, Rocio Lopez, Deena Brosi, Ryan LaVanchy, Thomas Pshak, James Cooper, Trevor Nydam, Sumit Mohan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChanges in organ allocation and regulatory policy have led to significant expansion of deceased donor kidney offers for transplantation in the United States. Transplant rates have increased, however inefficiencies in allocation have also evolved including increased donor kidney nonutilization, resources, and complication rates.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective observational study of deceased donor kidney offers in the United States with the Scientific Registry Of Transplant Recipients (SRTR) data between January 1, 2021 and December 31, 2024 using multivariable logistic and negative binomial models.ResultsThere were 7,608,578 donor kidney offers to 205,780 candidates with an average 19.4 months follow-up on the waiting list. The median offer per candidate was 17 (interquartile range: 6–46); however 28% of offers (n = 2,128,812) were to 5% of candidates (n = 10,285), and 55% of offers (n = 4,212,693) were to 15% high-frequency candidates (HFC; n = 31,049), who had ≥ 74 offers over the period. HFC were disproportionally aged from 50 to 59 years (incident rate ratio [IRR] = 1.26, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.24–1.29, relative to candidates aged 18–39), male (IRR = 1.14, 95% CI:1.12–1.16), Black (IRR = 1.30, 95% CI:1.28–1.31, relative to White), type-O blood (IRR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.67–1.70, relative to type-A), and body mass index (BMI) ≥ 35 kg/m2 (IRR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.10–1.14, relative to BMI = 20–24 kg/m2). There was a wide heterogeneity in HFC proportions by transplant center (median = 9%, interquartile range: 2%–17%), and centers with the highest HFC proportions had significantly low transplant rates and were more likely underperforming for offer acceptance ratios (OARs). Among candidates who received transplants during the study period, HFC recipients received transplants from older donors, including both deceased and living donors.ConclusionResults indicate that organ offers are disproportionately explained by a minority of candidates. Efforts to identify reasons for repetitive offer declines, strategic use of offer filters, and transition of applicable candidates to inactive status may dramatically improve the efficiency of deceased donor kidney allocation.Keywords: allocation, candidates, deceased donors, donor offers, kidney transplantation, policy

Allocation policy of deceased donor organs for transplantation incorporates numerous factors and has evolved significantly over time. The aims of allocation policy are to balance aspects of medical need, utility, and equity, incorporating different weights and hierarchy of factors to distribute deceased donor organ offers to candidates across the United States in a codified, transparent manner.1,2 Prominent changes in the national kidney allocation policy in the United States occurred in 2014 with the Kidney Allocation System and the subsequent broadening of geographic access to transplantation in 2021 with Kidney Allocation System-250.3, 4, 5, 6 In addition, the Conditions of Coverage policy, enacted by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services for organ procurement organizations strongly incentivized increased donation and transplantation rates in the United States.5,6 The cumulative effect of these changes has been a dramatic increase in donor kidney offers for transplantation across the country, including an almost 4-fold growth of offers since the implementation of the Kidney Allocation System.7 Deceased donor transplants have also increased significantly in this period, but the efficiency of the allocation process, measured as number of donor organ offers, sequence number on the allocation match run at transplantation, the proportion of organs procured but not used for transplantation, and the distance between donor hospitals and recipients, has been significantly impacted.3,8, 9, 10, 11, 12

The growing complexity and inefficiencies of organ allocation have led to greater regulatory scrutiny in the field. The Membership Professional and Standards committee within the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network incorporate analyses by the SRTR to identify underperforming transplant programs based on low OARs.13,14 Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services have incorporated these OARs to evaluate performance of kidney transplant centers in the national Increasing Organ Transplant Access model.15 United Network for Organ Sharing has expanded efforts to improve organ allocation efficiency by promoting organ offer filters, allowing programs to opt out of offers for the entire waiting list at their program or for individual candidates based on select donor criteria they are unlikely to accept.16,17

Cumulatively, efforts to understand and improve efficiencies are of increased importance to maximize the use of deceased donors and expedite potential transplants. As some candidates may receive multiple offers before transplantation or removal from the waiting list, further evaluation of the degree to which organ offers are clustered among certain candidates may be an important factor affecting the allocation process. The primary aim of the current study is to evaluate the distribution of organ offers among waitlisted candidates in the United States and identify candidates with higher frequency offers. We sought to evaluate candidate characteristics and center-level heterogeneity associated with patients with numerous donor kidney offers over time. Finally, we aimed to evaluate the association of the proportion of candidates with a high frequency of offers with center transplant and offer acceptance rates.

Methods

This study used data from the SRTR. The SRTR data system includes data on all donors, wait-listed candidates, and transplant recipients in the US, submitted by the members of the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network. The Health Resources and Services Administration, US Department of Health and Human Services provides oversight of the activities of the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network and SRTR contractors. We used standard analytical files describing the solitary kidney transplant candidate population, as well as the potential transplant recipient files, which include all offers to candidates among deceased donors that were ultimately transplanted.

The study was a retrospective observational study of deceased donor kidney offers in the United States. We used potential transplant recipient files from the SRTR, including donor kidney offers for kidneys that were eventually transplanted between January 1, 2021 and December 31, 2024. We aggregated the number of offers per candidate over this period and merged these data with the kidney candidate files. We excluded bypassed offers and only included candidates with at least 1 offer over the study period. The terminology “bypass” in this context refers to donor kidney offers in which candidates appeared on the allocation match run but the center was never formally presented with the offer. This generally happens for 2 reasons. The most common reason is that the center filters the offers (centers indicate that they are not willing to accept donor offers with certain characteristics for that patient or for their entire waitlist). Another reason would be that the organ was allocated out of sequence; thus, candidates were skipped in the allocation process to be able to allocate the organ to a candidate further down the match-run list. We did not consider these as turndowns given that there was no active decision to make on behalf of candidates that were skipped.

We excluded candidates who were also listed for organ transplants other than kidney-alone. We evaluated the distribution of deceased donor kidney offers per candidate over the study period and compared patients based on the number of offers using chi-square tests. We used multivariable negative binomial models to evaluate independent factors associated with the frequency of offers over the study period using IRR. Based on the distribution of offers to candidates over the study period, we characterized HFCs as those receiving the top 15% of offers. We used multivariable logistic models to evaluate candidate characteristics associated with HFC.

We aggregated offers by transplant center and evaluated the distribution of HFC by center. We used publicly available SRTR data to identify centers that were classified as underperforming, based on an OAR < 0.30 over the study (which included 7 SRTR reporting periods) consistent with the current Membership Professional And Standards Committee standards.18 We evaluated the association of center transplant rates (transplants/100 patient follow-up years) and average OAR with the proportion of HFC over the period using Spearman’s correlation. The average OAR per center was based on the SRTR reports, which included all organ offers used for reports between 2021 and 2024. We performed several sensitivity analyses based on candidates who may have different reasons for offer turndowns, including restricting the analyses to candidates ≥ 18 years, excluding candidates with a calculated panel reactive antibody (PRA) percentage over 95%, excluding patients listed in 2024 (with less potential follow-up), and excluding candidates who received a living donor transplant within the study period. We repeated the analyses excluding calculated PRA and blood type in risk adjustment, given these factors were more directly associated with donor availability. We also generated a multivariable hierarchical logistic model adjusted for the same candidate factors while incorporating transplant center as a random effect. From this model, we estimated the median incidence rate ratio to evaluate the effect of center location on the rate of donor kidney offers. Missing covariate data was included as a categorical level for multivariable models. Two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant for all analyses. The study was approved by the University of Colorado Institutional Review Board. All analyses were conducted with SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

There were 7,608,578 deceased donor offers for kidneys to 205,780 waitlisted candidates with an average follow-up of 19.4 months in the study population. We included offers of kidneys that were eventually transplanted in the study period, representing donor organs that were perceived as viable for transplantation for at least 1 candidate. The median offers per candidate was 17 (interquartile range: 6–46). The distribution of offers was highly skewed, including 28% of offers (n = 2,128,812) allocated to 5% of candidates (n = 10,285) and 55% of offers (n = 4,212,693) to 15% of candidates (n = 31,049). Candidates with the top 15% offers had ≥ 74 offers over the period and were classified as HFC (Figure 1).

Distribution of national deceased donor kidney offers by candidate frequency of offers.

The distribution of candidate characteristics by frequency of offers is displayed in Table 1. HFCs were more likely ages 50–59 relative to other age groups; were disproportionally male; Asian, Black, and from Other race groups; had Hispanic ethnicity; higher body mass index BMI; had Medicaid as primary insurance; lower PRA percentage; shorter duration of dialysis before listing; type-O blood group; and did not have a previous transplant. These factors remained significant independent risk factors in the multivariable logistic model (Table 2). Prominent independent risk factors for HFC included age group 50–59 (adjusted odds ratio[aOR] = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.37–50, relative to candidates ages 18–39, Asian race (aOR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.86–2.06), BMI > 35 kg/m2 (aOR = 1.33, 95% CI :1.27–1.39), and type-O blood (AOR = 2.00, 95% CI: 1.94–2.06). Candidates with the highest PRA percentage (aOR = 0.05, 95% CI: 0.05–0.06), longest prelisting dialysis (aOR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.36–0.43), and type-B blood (aOR = 0.17, 95% CI: 0.15–0.19) had a dramatically lower likelihood of HFC. Based on the relative frequency of offers, Figure 2 displays the IRR of donor offers per candidate. Independent factors associated with the adjusted IRRs of offers were generally consistent with previous results. Candidates aged 50–59 had an IRR of 1.22 (95% CI: 1.20–1.23, relative to candidates 18–39), and candidates with type-O blood had an IRR of 1.69 (95% CI: 1.67–1.70, relative to candidates with type-A blood).

There was wide heterogeneity in the proportion of HFC by individual transplant center. As displayed in Figure 3, the median proportion of HFC by center was 9%; however, 25% of centers had a proportion of HFC greater than 17%, and the maximum proportion of HFC was 42%. There was a statistically significant negative correlation between the proportion of HFC by center and transplant rates during the period (Figure 4) as well as a statistically significant negative correlation with OAR (Figure 5). During the study period, over 7 SRTR program-specific reports, 33 out of 238 (14%) transplant centers had at least 1 OAR < 0.30, indicating low performance based on membership professional and standards committee criteria. As illustrated in Figure 6, there was an increasing likelihood of low performance evaluations for OARs based on the quintile of HFC at transplant centers. Over the study period, HFC candidates had lower transplant rates than non-HFC candidates (12.2 transplants/100 follow-up years vs. 35.3 transplants/100 follow-up years, respectively). In addition, HFC had lower mortality rates over the study period, with 2.7 deaths per 100 follow-up years compared with 5.2 deaths per 100 follow-up years for non-HFC candidates. For candidates that received a transplant during the study period, HFC received transplants from donors with older age for both deceased donors (42.9 years vs. 40.2 years, P < 0.001) and living donors (46.6 years vs. 44.8 years, P < 0.001) compared with non-HFC.

For sensitivity analyses, we repeated the multivariable logistic models for factors associated with HFC using different exclusion criteria (Supplementary Tables S1–S4). In each of these additional analyses, the effect of independent risk factors modestly changed and remained statistically significant. The model excluding blood type and calculated PRA also resulted in similar findings for the remaining factors including statistically significantly higher likelihood of high frequency candidates among males and nonWhite candidates. Results of the hierarchical model using transplant center as a random effect, the median IRR was equal to 3.18. This indicates that the estimated rate of offers is more than triple for a candidate to be listed at a transplant center with a higher proportion of HFC relative to a lower proportion of HFC.

Discussion

The primary findings of the study indicate that deceased donor kidney offers are highly concentrated among a minority of waitlisted candidates. HFC were disproportionally male, 50 to 59 years old at listing, type-O blood, not highly sensitized, Black and other race, had shorter time on dialysis, and higher BMI. There was significant center-level heterogeneity in the proportion of HFC, and centers with the highest proportion of HFC were associated with lower deceased donor transplant rates, lower OARs, and were more likely to be identified as underperforming for offer acceptance based on Membership Professional and Standards Committee criteria. For candidates who received transplants during the study period, HFC received transplants from older donors as compared with non-HFC candidates. Cumulatively, the study illustrates that efforts to improve the efficiency of the deceased donor allocation system may prioritize identifying reasons for repeated offer declines for HFC and developing potential policies and interventions to manage these candidates prospectively.

A potential extension of the study findings is to consider automated processes to identify HFC for updated shared decision-making. For candidates with repeated declined offers, understanding the rationale for repeated declines and aligning expectations and preferences with caregivers may be particularly important. In general, outcomes following offer declines are not favorable including high rates of mortality, waitlist removal, or ultimate acceptance of offers with higher donor risk factors.7,19 In addition, as many patients are interested in understanding organ offer declines, but the logistical complexities of notifying all candidates about every organ offer decline may be untenable, notifying candidates with a certain threshold number of offers may be a reasonable compromise.20 The intent of this interaction could be to realign preferences with candidates and to discuss prospective decision-making for offers. Informing patients about offer declines has been proposed within the increasing organ transplant access model and limiting the interaction to HFC may be a reasonable compromise rather than all candidates on the waiting list.

From a policy perspective, 1 potential consideration is automating a transition to inactive status for candidates who receive a certain threshold of offers. The rationale of such a policy would be to improve efficiency of the allocation system and allow candidates and transplant centers to re-engage in discussions about prospective candidacy. This strategy would be preferable to the current center practice of using “internal holds” on actively listed candidates, which results in the generation of organ offers for candidates who are not actually ready for transplant. Ultimately, HFC likely account for a disproportionate number of longer organ allocation match runs that may also lead to nonutilization of some deceased donor organs. Although 1 of the potential reasons for repetitive offer turndowns for candidates may be increased selectivity of donor offers for candidates that may benefit long term with a higher quality organ despite longer waiting time, HFC that received transplants were from older age donors than for non-HFC. Thus, identifying reasons for repetitive turndowns for candidates is important for the efficiency of the transplant system and for improving the propensity for donor organs to be successfully placed for transplantation.

There were numerous patient-level factors associated with high-frequency donor offers. These associations may have different explanations. Repeated offers may represent the perceived likelihood that candidates may receive a “better” donor offer over time, the readiness of candidates to receive a transplant, or shared decision-making between candidates and the transplant team regarding specific preferences of donor characteristics. Results may also reflect clinical expertise regarding the suitability of donor offer characteristics for candidates. For example, the higher frequency offers for candidates with high BMI may reflect the need for donor organs with specific morphological characteristics.

The most common age group of HFC was from 50 to 59 years. This association may reflect a cohort that is unlikely to receive numerous offers from very low risk donors (i.e., they would not routinely qualify for low estimated post-transplant survival offers); yet there may be nuanced opinions about suitability for donor offers with the highest cumulative risk factors. However, even in this age category the most common prognosis for candidates is relatively poor when waiting for a significant amount of additional time for an improved donor match.21 Patients with a relatively short duration of dialysis may also be perceived as able to wait longer for a more optimal donor match, whereas patients with the longest prelisting dialysis time are more likely to receive an offer of higher quality more rapidly with top waiting list priority. Blood-type O candidates generally have a longer time to transplant and thus may receive numerous offers over time that are not considered viable for transplantation by programs. The increased association of HFC with moderate levels of sensitization may reflect the increased priority of these candidates in allocation despite the relatively lower proportion of viable donor organs.22 Candidates who are not highly sensitized are more likely to receive additional compatible offers in the future, whereas those with calculated PRA > 95% may not. The association of nonWhite race with HFC is not entirely clear, but may represent differences in waiting time, comorbid conditions, and patient readiness, resources, or preferences. Notably, the associations of nonWhite race, age, and gender remained consistent without adjusting for blood type or calculated PRA, which may be more reflective of availability of donors. In addition, the primary analyses did not account for center-level clustering of patients, such that patients with different characteristics (e.g., race, gender, and dialysis time) may be disproportionately represented at centers that have different proportions of HFC.

For each of these circumstances and patient characteristics, there may be an opportunity to improve allocation efficiency by using center-level or individual candidate filters to bypass offers that would not meet criteria. The ability to tailor offers to individual candidates may be an important intervention to attenuate repeated offer declines balanced with the resources necessary to document individual candidate characteristics and specific donor offers. Notably, the donor offers in the current analysis were limited to donor kidneys that were ultimately transplanted, which suggests that many of the offers were viable for certain candidates. However, the data does not reflect the multifold additional donor offers that were not ultimately transplanted, and thus the cumulative frequency of offers for these candidates is not reflected in these analyses.

For transplant centers, the study results indicate that understanding the frequency and proportion of HFC is important for operational efficiency. Results of the hierarchical model illustrated an approximate 3-fold higher rate of offers for candidates listed at centers with higher proportions of HFC. This suggests that the practice patterns, structural factors, or environment of the transplant center significantly contribute to the frequency of offer turndowns beyond individual characteristics. High proportions of HFC were also associated with lower quality metrics, as reported by the SRTR, and a higher likelihood of lower performance evaluations. For transplant centers randomized into the national increasing organ transplant access model, OARs represent 20% of center’s performance scores. Improving OARs can have direct impact on likelihood of upside payments based on the policy. An important aspect of OAR is that the unit of analysis is every offer to each candidate on the waiting list. Thus, candidates receiving multiple offers and organ turndowns over time disproportionally contribute to this metric. This data also highlights the ongoing urgency of waitlist management, given that some HFC candidates may not be considered ready for transplantation, despite active status, leading to numerous offer declines on their behalf.

There are several limitations of this study that should be acknowledged for the appropriate interpretation. Associations between candidate characteristics and high-frequency offers cannot be considered causal relationships because of the retrospective study design. We did not systematically evaluate the risk factors of donor offers (e.g., distribution of kidney donor profile index); thus, though some candidates may be more likely to receive numerous offers, the relative risk of these donor offers may vary between candidate characteristics. Although HFC overall had lower mortality and transplant rates, these data should be interpreted with caution, as, by definition, candidates who receive numerous kidney offers are more likely to have remained on the waiting list for a longer period. We did not specifically evaluate rates of donor offers (offers per time) given the purpose of our analysis was to understand patient factors that lead to a higher absolute number of offers and lower efficiency of the allocation system. However, rates of offers may have different connotations related to decision-making and likelihood of subsequent offers. We also did not evaluate reasons for offer turndowns among HFC, which may illustrate potential interventions to address higher turndown rates. However, further understanding of sources of variation related to candidates with high frequency of turndowns is important to patients, programs, and the national transplant system.

Cumulatively, this study identifies the distribution of organ offers by individual kidney transplant candidate in the United States. The primary finding indicates that a minority of candidates explain the majority of offers, and a better understanding of reasons for repeated offer declines may be critical to improving the efficiency of the organ allocation system. Further consideration of center-level efforts to identify and intervene on candidates with repeated offer declines may be important for programs and may be an effective mechanism to improve center metrics. Policies addressing HFC may also be important to improve the efficiency of the transplant system. Improving the efficiency of the transplant system is a highly laudable goal and may better honor the wishes of deceased donors and their families, while also creating more transplant opportunities for prospective candidates. Findings of the present study indicate an important novel mechanism to address inefficiencies in the transplant system and improve lives for prospective transplant candidates.
