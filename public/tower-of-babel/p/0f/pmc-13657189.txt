Cheek tooth extraction in geriatric versus non‐geriatric horses
Authors: Elias Mair, Omid Nekouei, Patricia Trummer‐Schug, Rhea Haralambus, Nora Mareike Biermann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAge‐related dental changes are major contributors to dental disorders in older horses, and cheek tooth extractions are often required to maintain masticatory function and oral comfort. However, limited information is available on clinical and treatment characteristics in this older patient population.ObjectiveTo describe clinical features, treatments, complication rates and short‐term outcomes of horses undergoing oral cheek tooth extraction and treatment of odontogenic sinusitis, and to compare findings between geriatric and non‐geriatric horses.Study DesignRetrospective analysis of clinical records.MethodsData of horses undergoing cheek tooth extractions (2018–2022) were collected and analysed. Clinical features, treatments and outcomes were recorded and analysed by age (per year) as well as by comparisons between non‐geriatric (<20 years) and geriatric (≥20 years) horses.ResultsIn total, 442 horses undergoing 710 dental extractions were included: 154 (34.8%) were geriatric and 288 (65.2%) non‐geriatric. Geriatric age was significantly associated with the reason for extraction, with periodontal disease being the most frequent reason (53.3% vs. 12.9%; p < 0.001). Geriatric horses more often required extraction of multiple teeth (53.8% vs. 28.5%; p < 0.001) and extraction of mandibular cheek teeth (42.9% vs. 25.7%; p < 0.001). They were also more likely to have concurrent diseases (29.2% vs. 11.8%; p < 0.001) and to receive long‐term medication (14.9% vs. 1.4%; p < 0.001). On the other hand, increasing age was significantly associated with lower odds of receiving antibiotics (OR 0.95 [95% CI 0.92–0.98] p < 0.001) and requiring invasive extraction technique (OR 0.96 [95% CI 0.93–0.99] p = 0.011). Complication rates did not differ (OR 0.98 [95% CI 0.98–1.01] p = 0.201).Main LimitationsRetrospective single‐centre design.ConclusionsGeriatric horses more frequently required extraction of multiple teeth, primarily for periodontal disease, whereas non‐geriatric horses more often required invasive extraction techniques while complication rates did not differ between age groups and the short‐term outcome was favourable in geriatric horses.Keywords: ageing, antibiotics, complications, dentistry, horse, sinusitis

INTRODUCTION

Ongoing advancements in veterinary care, husbandry and owner commitment have led to an increasing life expectancy in horses. Consequently, a growing proportion of the equine population now reaches geriatric age, with an estimated 11%–13% being older than 20 years in regions with good access to veterinary care. 1 , 2 As in other species, 3 ageing is associated with a higher prevalence of chronic diseases such as osteoarthritis, laminitis and pituitary pars intermedia dysfunction (PPID). 3 , 4

Equine dentition undergoes continuous structural remodelling throughout life. 5 The hypsodont cheek teeth of horses erupt continuously, and this way maintain a relatively constant clinical crown despite an annual occlusal wear of 2–3 mm. 5 , 6 With advancing age, however, the reserve crown is reduced progressively, the cross‐sectional area of the cheek teeth decreases, the infundibulae of the maxillary teeth wear out, and the enamel infoldings of mandibular teeth become less pronounced, eventually leaving only a thin shell of peripheral enamel. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 These changes decrease the teeth's resistance to abrasion and shear forces and further predispose to accelerated attrition. 9 Ageing also alters occlusal contact, with the mandibular cheek teeth becoming more curved rostrocaudally, while the maxillary teeth change their mesio‐occlusal angle. 11 Together, these structural and positional changes inevitably result in an age‐related increase in prevalence and severity of dental disease. 12 However, clinical signs of dental pain, such as quidding, often become apparent only in advanced stages of disease, partly due to the stoic nature of horses. 13 Although the horse's wellbeing was identified as the single most important factor for owners of geriatric horses, 14 they underwent veterinary examinations less frequently than their non‐geriatric counterparts. 15 , 16 , 17 Misinterpretation of chronic pain, financial restrictions and the fear of complications may contribute to this delay.

Progression of dental disease often requires extraction to alleviate pain and enable efficient mastication. Equine dental practice has undergone major advancements, and the oral approach to cheek tooth extraction is now considered the gold standard due to its lower complication rates in comparison with older techniques such as repulsion. 18

Despite this, particularly in geriatric horses, owners and veterinarians may hesitate out of concern for potential complications. Age‐related physiological changes, including immunosenescence and chronic systemic inflammation (‘inflammaging’), may impair immune function and healing in geriatric horses. 19 Nevertheless, studies have shown that geriatric horses even undergoing invasive procedures such as colic surgery have comparable short‐term complication rates and outcomes to non‐geriatric horses. 20 , 21 To date no study has systematically compared geriatric with non‐geriatric horses undergoing dental extraction with or without concurrent signs of sinusitis regarding clinical characteristics, comorbidities, treatment and complications in a large horse population.

The objectives of this study were to describe the clinical features, treatment, complication rates and short‐term outcomes of horses undergoing oral cheek tooth extractions and to compare these findings between geriatric and non‐geriatric horses. We expected that geriatric horses would have complication rates and short‐term outcomes comparable to non‐geriatric horses, would less frequently require invasive extraction techniques, and would be less commonly affected by odontogenic sinusitis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Medical records of horses undergoing one or more cheek tooth extractions, with or without odontogenic sinusitis, at the Centre of Equine Health and Research at the University of Veterinary Medicine Vienna, Austria, between January 2018 and January 2022, were reviewed Figure 1. Owners' consent for the use of clinical data was obtained upon admission. Cases involving extraction of incisors, canine teeth, wolf teeth and/or deciduous teeth, as well as cases of sinusitis without dental involvement, were excluded. Information from clinical records was included up to the time of hospital discharge, and all available data from any readmissions during the study period were also recorded.

Flowchart showing the inclusion and exclusion of horses in this study, between January 2018 and January 2022.

Data collected from medical records included patient signalment (age, breed, gender), single most relevant reason for extraction (including apical or endodontic infections, tooth fractures without obvious signs of apical infection, periodontal disease and other disorders such as developmental abnormalities, neoplasia and headshaking) which was diagnosed based on clinical and oral examination as well as radiographic findings in all cases, method of dental extraction (oral forceps extraction, tooth sectioning, or buccotomy), total number of extracted teeth and the location of extracted teeth (mandibular or maxillary cheek teeth).

In cases of sinusitis, the treatment method was recorded, including transnasal sinus endoscopy and lavage, trephination and surgical enlargement of the nasomaxillary aperture (SENMAP) and minimally invasive techniques (e.g. endoscopic‐guided conchotomy, transendoscopic laser fenestration of the nasal conchae), as well as the amount of sinusoidal lavages performed postoperatively.

Additionally, the presence of concurrent diseases (such as endocrine disorders (e.g., PPID), gastrointestinal disorders, respiratory diseases and other conditions), length of hospitalisation (days), duration from admission to surgery (days), short‐term complications (any adverse deviations from the clinical course prior to discharge from the hospital) and administration of antibiotics, non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), opioids and long‐term medications (defined as medications intended for prolonged or even lifelong administration, e.g., Pergolide, Firocoxib) were documented. When antibiotics were administered, the following information was recorded: antibiotic drug used, total duration of antibiotic administration (days, during hospitalisation and after discharge) and the time of administration (pre‐, peri‐, post‐operative or when complications occurred).

Routinely, all procedures were performed in standing, sedated horses using detomidine and butorphanol (intravenous, 0.01 mg/kg each; Domidine 10 mg/mL, Dechra; Butomidor 10 mg/mL, Richter Pharma), with the horse restrained in stocks. An intravenous catheter was placed for constant‐rate infusion (CRI) sedation with detomidine and butorphanol, as well as for intravenous crystalloid fluid administration (lactated Ringer's solution, Vetifundin®, OÖG GmbH). Peri‐ and postoperative analgesia was provided by administration of 1.1 mg/kg Flunixin Meglumine (Niglumine® 50 mg/mL, Vana) intravenously. Local anaesthesia was performed using either a mandibular extraoral, vertical approach 24 or maxillary (extraperiorbital fat body insertion technique—EFBI 25 ) nerve block with Mepivacaine (3 mL/100 kg bodyweight; Mepinaest purum® 2%, Gebro Pharma). For trephination, the skin was additionally desensitised using a line block with 2% Mepivacaine around the respective surgical site.

Dental extractions were performed via oral forceps extraction or, in some cases, via tooth sectioning using a dental drill, or via buccotomy. 26 , 27 Treatment of sinusitis was performed either by transnasal sinuscopy when openings to the affected sinus compartments were sufficiently large to accommodate a 6‐mm diameter endoscope (5.9 mm paediatric gastroscope of 1 m length; Karl Storz), or via surgical approaches, including modified trephination techniques with SENMAP 28 or minimally invasive transnasal approaches as recently described. 29 For transnasal surgical approaches, a maxillary nerve block (EFBI technique) was combined with splash blocks using Mepivacaine delivered via the endoscope's working channel onto the nasal mucosa at the proposed surgical site.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata v19.5 (StataCorp). A total of 442 horses admitted for dental extraction with or without signs of odontogenic sinusitis were included in this study. Frequency distributions of all collected variables, such as horse signalment, diagnosis, treatment, complications and outcome, were described and/or tabulated. For continuous variables, the median and interquartile range (IQR) were presented, and for categorical variables, the number of samples within each category (%) was reported. Although physiological or functional age vary between individuals, an age of 20 years is widely considered as geriatric. 20 , 30 , 31 , 32 Therefore, for comparisons, cases were categorised into ‘geriatric’ (horses ≥20 years old), and ‘non‐geriatric’ (horses <20 years old). The associations between age group (geriatric vs. non‐geriatric) and the clinical variables of interest were assessed using either the Chi‐square test (for categorical variables) or the Kruskal–Wallis test (for continuous variables). Further, logistic regression analysis with outcomes of interest (e.g., occurrence of complications, antibiotic treatment, extraction technique, diagnosis and treatment of sinusitis) and age as a continuous variable (per year) was performed. Additionally, odds ratios for 5‐year increments were calculated by exponentiating the regression coefficient (β) by a factor of five (i.e., OR5 = OR1 5), with corresponding transformation of the 95% confidence intervals (CI). The level of significance was set at 0.05.

RESULTS

A total of 442 horses underwent 710 cheek tooth extractions (median = 1; IQR: 1–2 teeth), and 100 of these horses (22.6%) were additionally treated for sinus disorders associated with dental disease. The median age of the horses was 16.7 years (IQR: 11.3–23.3), ranging from 0.2 to 39.1 years. Based on the age cutoff of 20, 154 horses (34.8%) were classified as geriatric and 288 as non‐geriatric (65.2%). Most horses were Warmbloods (240; 54.3%), followed by other large‐breed horses (94; 21.3%), ponies (90; 20.4%) and draught horses (18; 4.1%). Two hundred and forty‐eight horses were geldings (56.1%), 172 were mares (38.9%) and 22 were stallions (5.0%).

Diagnosis and treatment of sinus disease

Computed tomography (CT) examinations were performed in 29/442 horses (6.6%), and this was carried out more frequently in non‐geriatric horses (27/288; 9.4%) than in geriatric horses (2/154; 1.3%) (p < 0.001; Table 2). Similarly, a diagnosis of sinusitis and subsequent sinus treatment was more common in non‐geriatric horses (76/288; 26.4%) compared to geriatric horses (24/154; 15.6%) (p = 0.001; Table 2), however, age was not associated with diagnosis and treatment of sinusitis (OR 0.98 [95% CI 0.95–1.01] p = 0.106; Table 3). Transnasal endoscopy and sinus lavage were the most commonly performed procedures (70/100; 70%), followed by trephination and SENMAP (23/100; 23%) and minimally invasive approaches (7/100; 7%). After initial treatment, horses underwent a median of 3 (IQR: 2–5) follow‐up transendoscopic sinus lavages during hospitalisation.

Treatment of dental disease

Overall, tooth fractures were the most common reason for dental extraction (170/442; 38.5%), followed by apical infections (134/442; 30.3%), periodontal disease (119/442; 26.9%) and other dental disorders (19/442; 4.3%). Age group was significantly associated with the reason for dental extraction (p < 0.001; Table 2). Periodontal disease (82/154; 53.3%) was the main reason for cheek tooth extraction in geriatric horses, followed by tooth fracture (47/154; 30.5%), apical infection (22/154; 14.3%) and other reasons (3/154; 1.9%).

Oral extraction using dental forceps was used in most cases (353/442; 79.9%), tooth sectioning was performed in 56/442 cases (12.7%) and buccotomy in 33/442 cases (7.5%). Significant differences in the extraction techniques utilised were found between non‐geriatric and geriatric horses (p < 0.001; Table 2). Oral extraction using forceps was performed more frequently in geriatric horses, whereas non‐geriatric horses more commonly underwent either dental sectioning (14.9% of non‐geriatric horses 43/288 vs. 8.4% of geriatric horses 13/154) or buccotomy (10.4% of non‐geriatric horses 30/288 vs. 1.9% of geriatric horses 3/154). For every 5‐year increase in age, the odds of requiring an invasive extraction technique decreased by approximately 18% (OR 0.96 [95% CI 0.93–0.99] p = 0.011; Table 3).

Furthermore, the number of extracted teeth and their location differed significantly between non‐geriatric and geriatric horses (p < 0.001; Table 2). Most non‐geriatric horses had only one tooth extracted (205/288; 71.1%), whereas more than half of the geriatric horses underwent extraction of more than one tooth (82/154; 53.2%). In addition, compared with non‐geriatric horses, a greater proportion of geriatric horses had mandibular cheek teeth extracted (25.7% of non‐geriatric horses 74/288 vs. 42.9% of geriatric horses 66/154.).

Additional clinical information, complications and outcomes

Concurrent diseases were noted in 34/288 (11.8%) non‐geriatric horses and in 45/154 (29.2%) geriatric horses (p < 0.001; Table 2) and long‐term medication was more commonly administered in geriatric horses (p < 0.001; Table 2).

Analgesics were administered to nearly all patients. NSAIDs were given to 438/442 horses (99.1%) with a median duration of 4 days (3–7), and opioids were administered to 39/442 horses (8.8%). Among cases that did not receive NSAIDs, two were diagnosed with chronic renal failure and received Methadone instead, and in two cases, analgesia beyond local anaesthesia during the extraction process was deemed unnecessary as the extracted teeth were very loose and easily removed.

Antibiotics were administered to 327/442 horses (74.0%). Non‐geriatric patients received antibiotics more commonly than geriatric horses (78.8% of non‐geriatric horses 227/288 vs. 64.9% of geriatric horses 100/154; p = 0.002; Table 2) and increasing age was significantly associated with lower odds of antibiotic administration, with a 24% decrease in odds per 5‐year increase in age (OR 0.95 [95% CI 0.92–0.98] p < 0.001; Table 3). Further, non‐geriatric horses were more likely to be discharged whilst on antibiotic therapy (p = 0.017; Table 2; OR 0.97 [95% CI 0.94–0.99] p = 0.030; Table 3). However, the time of initiation and total duration of antibiotic treatment (Median of 5 days; IQR: 3–8) did not differ between the two groups (Table 2). Only 7 cases (2.1%) received antibiotics due to the development of complications. Most commonly, Trimethoprim‐sulfadiazine was the antibiotic chosen initially (310/327; 94.8%), followed by a combination of Benzylpenicillin and Gentamicin (11/327; 3.4%), Doxycycline (4/327; 1.2%) and Marbofloxacin (2/327; 0.6%). Metronidazole was administered additionally in 10/327 cases (3.1%).

Short‐term complications during hospitalisation occurred in 131 (29.6%) of the cases. Most cases (99; 22.4%) experienced mild complications, while 32 cases (7.2%) were classified as severe as they required medical intervention. The complication rate did not differ between non‐geriatric and geriatric horses (Table 2) and similarly, age had no significant effect on occurrence of complications (OR 0.98 [95% CI 0.98–1.01] p = 0.201; Table 3) regardless of whether they received antibiotics or not (with antibiotic treatment; p = 0.305; without antibiotic treatment; p = 0.509).

The median duration of hospitalisation was 7 days (IQR: 4–12; range: 0–61), and the median time from admission to surgery was 1 day (IQR: 0–2; range: 0–32); neither differed between geriatric and non‐geriatric horses (p = 0.665; Table 2). Furthermore, other important outcome variables, including the occurrence of complications, the need for more invasive extraction techniques, and antibiotic treatment, either did not differ between groups or were less frequently required in older horses (Table 2, Table 3).

Of all 442 horses admitted for dental extraction, 437 (98.9%) were discharged from the hospital, while 5 horses (1.1%) were euthanised. Two horses showed severe progressive mandibular abscessation, which in both cases already existed prior to dental extraction but did not respond to treatment. One horse was euthanised due to severe neurologic signs during extraction 1 month after sinus trephination and SENMAP surgery; pathologic examination revealed an abscess of the pituitary gland. One horse was euthanised due to severe progressive clinical signs of cauda equina syndrome and in one horse, recurrent oesophageal impaction resulted in oesophageal perforation.

DISCUSSION

The present study provides insight into the clinical characteristics and short‐term outcomes of horses undergoing oral cheek tooth extraction, with a specific focus on age‐related differences. Approximately one‐third of the study population was classified as geriatric, which highlights the clinical relevance of this age group in equine dental practice. In line with our expectations, geriatric horses did not experience higher complication rates or worse short‐term outcomes compared with younger horses, despite more frequently requiring extraction of multiple teeth. Furthermore, geriatric horses less commonly required invasive extraction techniques and antibiotic treatment.

A variety of conditions can necessitate dental extraction, including fractured cheek teeth, apical infection, displacement, periodontal disease, supernumerary cheek teeth and wear abnormalities. In contrast to previous reports, which consistently reported apical infection as the leading cause, 33 , 34 , 35 tooth fractures were the most common reason for extraction in the present study, followed by apical infection. However, for geriatric horses, periodontal disease was the predominant reason for extraction. This observation may reflect progressive anatomical and functional changes in equine cheek teeth such as the development of senile diastemata. 36 Diastemata allow food to accumulate, resulting in inflammation and progressive loss of periodontal‐supporting structures, including the periodontal ligament, gingiva and alveolar bone. 37 , 38 When left untreated, periodontal disease can advance and tooth extraction may become necessary. 35 Both periodontal disease and the occurrence of diastemata were found to be more prevalent in the mandibular arcade. 39 This is consistent with the results of this study, where mandibular cheek tooth extractions were more common in geriatric horses, with 43% of the extracted teeth belonging to the mandibular arcade, whereas only 26% of the extracted teeth in non‐geriatric horses were mandibular teeth. Findings from other studies support this age‐related pattern. Kennedy et al. reported that, in a relatively young population with a mean age of 11.6 years, maxillary cheek teeth were extracted more frequently than mandibular teeth (290 vs. 138 extractions). 33 Additionally, geriatric horses required more teeth to be extracted than their younger counterparts as the cumulative nature of age‐related dental pathology increases the likelihood of multi‐tooth extractions. 12 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44

In the present study, sinusitis was more frequently observed in non‐geriatric horses; however, age itself was not significantly associated with a diagnosis of sinusitis, likely due to the relatively small number of horses diagnosed with odontogenic sinusitis. While it has been suggested by others 10 , 45 , 46 that sinusitis is more common in younger horses, our data do not allow us to draw a final conclusion, and therefore we cannot answer this question at this time. The combination of odontogenic sinusitis being less frequently diagnosed in geriatric horses, the fact that CT examination can only be performed under general anaesthesia at our institution, and potential financial constraints are likely reasons for the lower rate of CT scans in geriatric horses.

Geriatric horses were less likely to require more invasive extraction techniques. It can be assumed that shorter roots and weakened periodontal ligaments resulted in a higher success rate of simple oral forceps extractions in geriatric horses, whereas non‐geriatric horses more often required intraoral sectioning or access via buccotomy. The technically easier, faster and less traumatic extractions in geriatric horses could have influenced clinicians' decisions to reduce or omit antibiotic therapy in these cases. Overall, the less frequent use of antibiotics in older horses may also reflect differences in case complexity (e.g., invasiveness of tooth extraction, diagnosis of sinusitis, number and location of extracted teeth) and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

While there is growing evidence that antibiotic treatment may not be necessary in oral cheek tooth extraction 22 , 23 , 47 antibiotics were still used routinely during the study period and were in many cases only started after cheek tooth extraction had been completed. This practice has changed since, and as elimination of the underlying cause, lavage and mechanical debridement are the most important treatment modalities 48 in cases suffering from odontogenic sinusitis, antibiotics are no longer administered routinely. Additionally, if antibiotic therapy is deemed necessary, treatment is initiated only after culture and sensitivity testing.

In this study, antibiotic administration and selection were based on clinician preference. Although most horses received first‐line agents, these primarily consisted of Trimethoprim–sulfadiazine. The choice of using a broad‐spectrum antibiotic could be viewed critically, as narrower‐spectrum first‐line antibiotics (e.g., Penicillin) may represent more appropriate options for prophylactic treatment. Ease of oral administration and cost considerations may have influenced these decisions; however, as the medical records did not provide a rationale for antibiotic selection, this interpretation remains speculative. In addition, two horses were treated with Marbofloxacin, a WHO‐classified critically important antibiotic that should be used only after careful consideration in the context of rising antibiotic resistance. 49 Recently implemented clinical guidelines, aligned with national and international legislation 50 now require that such critically important antibiotics are only administered following sensitivity testing and when disease progression precludes alternative therapeutic options.

The overall short‐term complication rate observed in this study population was higher than previously reported (29.6% vs. 13.5%–18.4%) 22 , 33 , 35 but this likely reflects the inclusion of horses with odontogenic sinusitis and their treatment contributing to a higher complication rate. Additionally, the occurrence of complications such as orosinusal fistulation in cases with sinusitis could also be related to more advanced stages of dental disease and alveolar bone plate infection rather than the extraction itself. Furthermore, as horses were hospitalised for an average of 7 days, this relatively long duration likely allowed detection of some minor complications which, in other settings, may have been treated by referring veterinarians or on an ambulatory basis and therefore remained undocumented.

Overall, short‐term outcome in terms of similar duration of hospitalisation and occurrence of complications did not differ between age groups, combined with the knowledge that geriatric horses required less frequently invasive extraction techniques or antibiotic treatment although they required more commonly multi‐tooth extractions was in line with our expectations.

Direct complications following oral extraction included sequestrum formation, delayed healing and root fragmentation, which is consistent with previous reports 33 and was observed regardless of age. In light of these findings, it is notable that geriatric horses had a higher prevalence of concurrent diseases and particularly endocrine conditions such as PPID have been associated with weakened immune status and delayed healing. 51 While we can conclude that concurrent disease did not affect overall short‐term complication rates significantly, our results do not allow us to draw conclusions on its impact, since other factors, for example, the higher rate of sinusitis and associated procedures in non‐geriatric horses, could have obscured these effects. Additionally, it should be mentioned that geriatric horses were much more likely to receive long‐term medications, primarily Pergolide. Potentially, this ongoing medical therapy could have successfully alleviated impairments caused by chronic disease, 51 further contributing to a favourable outcome in geriatric patients. However, not all horses were tested for PPID, and undetected and untreated disease might also have had a negative effect on outcome of some cases.

One of the main limitations of this study is its retrospective, single‐centre design. All cases that underwent dental extraction during the study period were included in the initial case selection. However, horses that may have required extraction but did not undergo the procedure for reasons such as severe concurrent disease or owners' financial constraints could not be assessed. Additionally, concurrent disease was recorded only as present or absent, without standardised categorisation, which should be considered when interpreting potential age effects.

Furthermore, selection bias towards horses considered clinically fit for extraction may also be present. At our institution, owners of geriatric horses are generally willing to pursue advanced and costly treatments irrespective of age, as evidenced by the fact that approximately 40% of horses undergoing colic surgery are older than 20 years (unpublished data). Nevertheless, this does not eliminate the aforementioned bias, although the large number of cases may partially mitigate its impact. 52 Loss of clinical information regarding very mild and therefore potentially unrecorded complications is also likely and could only be addressed in a prospective study design. Age‐related differences observed in this study may be influenced by underlying differences in case characteristics, such as sinusitis status, extraction technique and number of teeth extracted. We explored adjusted analyses for selected outcomes, including antibiotic treatment and occurrence of complications, and the overall conclusions regarding age‐related effects did not change. As it was not possible to perform these analyses in a standardised manner with the same variables included in each model for all case characteristics, we chose to report unadjusted analyses in this manuscript. The observed associations should therefore be interpreted cautiously and cannot be assumed to represent effects of age alone.

The hospital's data protection policy did not permit research‐directed follow‐up after discharge, resulting in a median follow‐up time of only 7 days and likely leading to an underestimation of delayed postoperative complications. Although some horses with more severe complications returned for further treatment and were therefore included in the analysis, this remains a major limitation of the study, as many long‐term complications following dental extraction and sinusitis treatment may only become apparent weeks or months after the initial intervention.

CONCLUSION

Geriatric horses represent a substantial proportion of equine dental patients and present distinct clinical features compared with younger horses. Non‐geriatric horses more frequently required invasive extraction techniques, while geriatric horses more commonly required extraction of multiple teeth, most often due to periodontal disease. Notably, complication rates did not differ between age groups and short‐term outcomes were favourable in geriatric horses.
