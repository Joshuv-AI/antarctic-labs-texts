Reply to: Utility in prognostication of continuous EEG monitoring in postanoxic coma: finding the right balance
Authors: Stefan E Knapen, Wouter H Hinsenveld, Marcus L F Janssen, Werner H Mess, Judith Drenthen, Martijn R Tannemaat
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We thank Ayasse et al. for their interest in our study and their insightful comments regarding the complexities of neuroprognostication [1, 2]. We appreciate the opportunity to clarify the scope and implications of our findings regarding the real-world utility of continuous EEG (cEEG) in postanoxic coma.

Ayasse et al. correctly point out that our study did not report long-term neurological outcomes or detailed sedation parameters. While these variables are crucial for validating new prognostic markers, the primary aim of our study was to evaluate the clinical yield and incremental value of cEEG as currently implemented under national guidelines [3]. These guidelines rely on EEG patterns previously validated to have a false-positive rate of near 0% for poor outcome [4, 5]. Our study operates on the premise that these established patterns are reliable; our goal was to determine how often cEEG provides a definitive prognosis that is not already evident through standard clinical examination or necessitated by the patient’s hemodynamic status.

Regarding the comparison between cEEG and routine EEG (rEEG), Ayasse et al. suggest that intermittent rEEG might not suffice and that cEEG sensitivity could be improved by analyzing temporal evolution. We agree that EEG patterns evolve over time. However, in the context of poor prognostication, patterns typically evolve toward improvement or remain poor; it is exceptionally rare for a “poor” pattern meeting strict guideline criteria (e.g., suppression or burst-suppression with identical bursts < 24 h) to evolve into a marker of good outcome. Therefore, a poor prognosis established within the first 24 h is often sufficient for decision-making [6]. We argue that while cEEG is superior for seizure detection, its added value for prognostication specifically—relative to the resources required—was low in our multicenter cohort.

Finally, regarding hemodynamic instability, the authors note this is not a marker of brain injury. We agree. However, in an analysis of resource utility, it is highly relevant. If a patient succumbs to refractory shock (hemodynamic instability) prior to the 72-hour mark, the prognostic information provided by cEEG becomes clinically redundant. Our data highlight that in many poor prognosis cases, the decision to limit care is multifactorial, often driven by clinical signs or physiological failure rather than the cEEG alone.

We fully support the multimodal approach endorsed by the ERC-ESICM [7]. Based on our findings, we do not advocate abandoning cEEG completely, but rather suggest that a “one-size-fits-all” application may be inefficient. We hope our study prompts further research into targeted strategies—perhaps utilizing clinical prediction scores—to identify patients for whom continuous monitoring offers genuine additional value over routine clinical assessment.
