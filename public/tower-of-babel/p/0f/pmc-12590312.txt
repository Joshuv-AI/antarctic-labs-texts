Development and implementation of a multifaceted strategy to reduce excessive fasting in an acute surgical setting using the behavior change wheel framework
Authors: Sharon Carey, Rachel Hardy, Kiah Waller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExcessive fasting for tests and procedures within the hospital setting remains commonplace, despite clear evidence of the benefits related to reduced fasting. There is minimal research addressing the issue of prolonged preoperative fasting. The aim of this study was to undertake a pre/post implementation study using multifaceted interventions to reduce excessive fasting on acute surgical wards within a quaternary hospital in Sydney, Australia, using the behavior change wheel (BCW) framework.MethodsThe BCW was used to identify key strategies to address barriers to implementing evidence‐based fasting guidelines. A pre/post implementation study using multifaceted interventions to reduce excessive fasting on acute surgical wards was implemented over a 3‐month period. The primary outcome was preoperative fasting time in hours. Clinician interviews were conducted to assess the benefit of each of the interventions within the study.ResultsOn completion of preimplementation data collection, strategies were implemented including ward education, guideline promotion, patient stories, patient‐facing flyers, and audit and feedback. There was no significant difference between fasting times pre and post intervention. Clinician interviews showed increased awareness and increased confidence.ConclusionThis pre/post implementation study was unable to significantly reduce fasting times, but did show increased clinician awareness and a trend towards improved patient‐reported outcomes. INTRODUCTION

It is well established that recommended requirements for fasting before anesthesia include a 6 h fast from solids and 2 h fast from fluids. This recommendation has been published in multiple anesthesiology and surgical guidelines worldwide. 1 Reduced fasting, compared with traditional fasting from midnight, has shown favorable outcomes including improved immune function, improved wound healing, reduced catabolism of protein and muscle stores, 2 and overall reduced morbidity. 3 Reduced fasting also reduces the incidence of dehydration, where dehydration is linked to poorer outcomes and higher anesthesia risk, 4 and reduces patient‐reported distress. 5 However, implementation of fasting guidelines has proven to be challenging.

Excessive fasting continues to be commonplace, with a plethora of studies showing patients fast longer than needed. 6 , 7 , 8 More recently a multicentre prospective audit across five British health services indicated patients fast from solids for 16 h and 6 h for clear fluids. 8 Patients fasting for emergency surgery have the longest fasting times. 8 These studies confirm that there continues to be a large gap between best practice and current practice.

Although it seems like a simple change in clinical processes, the practice of fasting within the hospital system is far more complex than it may appear. Qualitative research using the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) 9 has highlighted substantial barriers to implementation of fasting guidelines, including barriers related to systems, culture, and knowledge. 10 Findings indicate that the barriers clinicians face in attempting to follow fasting guideline recommendations are too enormous to tackle, and, hence, clinicians revert back to fasting patients from midnight, which in turn leads to excessive fasting. 10

There are thousands of people fasted every day worldwide for tests, procedures, and surgeries requiring anesthesia. There remains a dearth of research attempting to address this practice gap. A systematic review in 2020 highlighted the lack of interventional studies aimed at reducing fasting times within the acute care setting. 11 Studies to date have focused on unidimensional interventions, and the majority of studies were of moderate to high risk of bias. 11 No studies have utilized an implementation framework to plan and study change in fasting practices. 11 Furthermore, there is also a lack of guidance related to implementation of fasting guidelines. This was highlighted in a 2016 systematic review that showed fasting guidelines provide very little or no guidance on implementation and applicability. 1

The use of implementation frameworks has allowed a greater exploration and understanding of the complexities of behavior change. The behavior change wheel (BCW) is one such framework, 12 which allows identification of interventions most likely to bring about behavior change. The BCW starts with identification of domains that may be seen as a barrier or enabler to behavior change, including capabilities, opportunities, and motivations. Each of these domains are linked to specific interventions that are most likely to bring about change in behavior based on current evidence. 12 Given the multifaceted challenges of implementing evidence‐based fasting guidelines, the use of an implementation framework, such as the BCW, would allow identification of strategies and interventions most likely to promote behavior change.

The aim of the present study was to undertake a pre/post implementation study using multifaceted interventions to reduce excessive fasting on acute surgical wards within a quaternary hospital in Sydney, Australia, using a BCW framework. Mixed methodologies were used to evaluate the impact of the intervention, where the primary outcome was preoperative fasting time in hours.

METHODS

Theoretical framework

Ethics approval was gained through the Human Research Ethic Committee (Royal Prince Alfred Hospital) with a waiver of consent. Results from previous qualitative interviews with multidisciplinary clinicians working within acute surgical wards at a quaternary hospital in Sydney, Australia, were used to identify key enablers and barriers to implementation of evidence‐based fasting guidelines. These enablers and barriers were mapped against the 14 domains of the TDF (see Carey et al. 10 for full details). These were then linked to the BCW capabilities, opportunities, and motivations domains, 12 allowing the identification of effective intervention types and specific intervention strategies. Study design

A prospective pre/post implementation study with multiple interventions was undertaken across two gastrointestinal surgical wards using a mixed methods evaluation. The surgical wards consisted of upper gastrointestinal and colorectal specialties which both receive tertiary and quaternary referrals from across the state. Each specialty had three surgical teams consisting of two consultant surgeons, a surgical fellow, a surgical registrar, and a junior medical officer. Placing diet orders into the electronic medical record is undertaken by ward nurses and ward assistants, under the instruction of the treating surgical team.

The study timeline can be seen in Figure 2, with data collection over three time periods (baseline, postintervention, and 1‐month postintervention). The study was originally planned to run over a 6‐month period but was scaled down to 3 months with a 6‐week intervention period because of reduced funding as a result of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) pandemic.

Participants

All patients admitted to the study wards and undergoing a test, procedure, or surgery during the study period were included in the study. Patients were identified through the hospital electronic medical record, when a diet order of nil per os (NPO) was placed, and then the reason for fasting was collected from a review of the electronic medical record. Fasting time was determined by the time in which the NPO diet order was placed into the electronic medical record, and fasting time ended when the NPO diet order was removed. Fasting was defined as the withholding of oral, enteral, or parenteral nutrition. Basic demographics, use of intravenous fluids, cancellation (and reason for cancellation) of tests/procedures/theatre, use of preoperative clear fluid diet, and early breakfast orders were all collected from the electronic medical record.

Surgeons, radiologists, nurses, and dietitians were recruited to undertake short, structured interviews at the end of the study period to evaluate process measures and individual intervention strategies. Recruitment was using a convenience sample of staff available on the wards to undertake interviews. Interview answers were recorded using paper and pen.

Interventions

Executive engagement

Before the development of the intervention an executive committee was established. This committee was chaired by the hospital director of medical services, and planned interventions aimed at reducing excessive fasting based on known barriers (highlighted in previous work). 10 This committee included surgical, radiology, theatre, and endoscopy heads of departments, nursing executive, nurse unit managers, foods services management and consumer representation. During the intervention executive memorandums were emailed out to all staff from the director of medical services reminding them of fasting recommendations, roles, and responsibilities.

Ward in‐servicing

A standardized in‐service was developed and delivered at three varying times to each ward at the start of the intervention period. In‐services were for all ward staff available at the time of the in‐service including nursing, ward assistants, and medical/surgical officers. The in‐service included a video recorded patient story, evidence for reduced fasting, where to find hospital fasting guidelines, fasting flipcharts, how to order food outside working hours, and roles and responsibilities related to fasting. Each in‐service ran for 30 min.

Guidelines and fasting flip charts

Hospital fasting guidelines were updated in consultation with all relevant departments across the hospital and were readily available on the hospital intranet. A fasting flipchart was developed based on the guidelines. The flipchart gave fasting times for people on oral, enteral, and parenteral nutrition for all tests and procedures in radiology, endoscopy, and magnetic resonance imaging; planned, and emergency theatre lists; and details on ordering preoperative fluids, and out‐of‐hours meals. Each ward was provided with flipcharts to place in the main nurses' station and at medical stations. Flipcharts were also given to theatre, radiology, and endoscopy units.

Role delineation

With agreement from the hospital medical executive, senior surgical registrars were deemed responsible for making decisions around fasting of patients and liaising with surgical theatres around theatre schedules and delays. This was communicated to all staff through hospital‐wide memos, emails, and during ward in‐services.

Audit and feedback

At the end of each week within the intervention period graphs of average fasting times were provided to each ward, clinical team, and consultant surgeon. Wards could compare their fasting times with other ward fasting times. Clinical team and consultant surgeon fasting reports showed other deidentified clinical teams and consultant surgeon fasting results as a comparison. These graphs were emailed at the end of each week to relevant surgeons and ward nurse managers.

Patient flyers

In collaboration with the hospital executive and the hospital consumer representatives, a patient‐facing flyer was developed to explain the need for fasting, fluids allowed on a preoperative clear fluid diet, and how to escalate should they have concerns about the amount of time they have been fasting.

Dedicated Fasting Liaison Officer

A dietitian was allocated to attend morning and lunchtime radiology planning sessions and have electronic visibility of theatre lists. The dietitian role was to liaise with ward nurses and surgical registrars to flag patients that were fasting but unlikely to proceed with a test, procedure, or surgery that day so that appropriate food or fluids could be ordered.

DATA COLLECTION

Clinical

The primary outcome of this study was to assess preoperative fasting for tests, procedures, and surgery, as defined above. Other clinical data collection included number and reasons for cancellation of test/procedure/surgery (including documentation of adverse events related to inadequate fasting), use of intravenous fluids, and documentation of fasting orders in electronic medical records.

Patient reported

All nursing staff were encouraged to complete the validated Thirst Distress Questionnaire 5 with patients who were fasting. This questionnaire included the following questions: (1) When did the patient last eat? (2) When did the patient last drink? (3) On a scale of 0–10, how intense is the patients’ thirst at the moment (with a score of 0 indicating no thirst and 10 indicating the worst possible thirst). Nursing staff would then place the completed questionnaire into a box collected by the research team.

Process evaluation

To assess exposure to the interventions, short, structured interviews were undertaken using a convenience sample of medical, nursing, and dietitians working on the wards and in the units involved in the study. Attendance at in‐services was not recorded.

Statistical analysis

Because of the nonparametric nature of the data, results were presented as median and range. Analysis between groups was undertaken using Kruskal‐Wallis tests using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS), version 29. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics

Ethical approval was granted by the Local Health District (RPA) Human Ethics Review Committee (Protocol No: X21‐0137 & 2021/ETH00898). A waiver of consent was approved for this research to allow data for all individuals fasting within the specified timeframe to be included in the study.

RESULTS

A total of 95 episodes of preoperative fasting during the three study periods were captured, with 39 episodes in the baseline data collection, 31 in the immediate postintervention period, and 25 in the 1‐month postintervention period. DISCUSSION

The aim of this study was to undertake a pre/post implementation study using multifaceted interventions to reduce excessive fasting on acute surgical wards within a quaternary hospital in Sydney, Australia, using the BCW framework. Despite a very comprehensive multifaceted intervention strategy, no difference in fasting times was seen between the baseline, postimplementation, and 1‐month postimplementation time periods. No difference was seen in any of the other outcome measures, which spanned across clinical and patient‐reported outcomes. There were trends towards improved documentation of fasting instructions within electronic medical records and reduced patient‐reported thirst distress. Short, structured interviews with clinicians showed greater awareness of fasting and good awareness and exposure to the strategies employed within the study. However, this increased awareness did not equate to change in behavior.

There are two key factors to explain the lack of significant clinical change following the multifaceted intervention. Firstly, the habit of “fasting from midnight” has been embedded into hospital systems for decades. 13 Changing this behavior is likely to require sustained positive reinforcement over time. Although there is limited research assessing sustainability of behavior change in the healthcare worker, previous research looking at sustainability of individual behavior change in people with complex long‐term illness has shown behaviour change is only sustained when an intervention is longer than 6‐months. 14 Hence, changing a long standing habit likely requires a much longer and sustained intervention period. Secondly, the reduced duration of the study and reduced sample size led to the study being underpowered. Future research needs to ensure adequate recruitment to enable the possibility of detecting significant changes. This could be done with a larger cohort or to run the study over a longer period, which would also address the challenge of sustainability.

The simple idea of changing fasting practices within a large hospital is actually an enormous shift in power dynamics within the healthcare team. 15 Previous qualitative research has shown that senior surgical staff make decisions around fasting, leaving junior doctors and nurses fearful to challenge these decisions. 10 Implementation of a change in clinical practice is likely to disturb the current status quo equilibrium. 15 Establishing a new equilibrium related to decision making around fasting requires healthcare professionals to be open to “unlearn” current hierarchical systems. 15 This change would provide nurses and junior doctors the ability to manage decisions around patient fasting.

Despite no statistically significant change in fasting times post‐implementation, staff interviews indicate strategies were of benefit, and there was a trend towards reduced patient‐reported thirst distress and increased documentation of fasting plans in individual patient medical records in line with best practice. Given the study was underpowered, this is encouraging as an indication that staff are proactive in attempting to reduce fasting for their patients. However, as shown in previous qualitative research, the barriers to reducing fasting, specifically patient need for emergency tests and procedures and long surgical wait lists, are not only related to culture and knowledge but also complex systems and unpredictability of workloads. 10 Limitations in the electronic medical record and diet ordering system within the study hospital does not allow for flexibility to support reduced fasting. However, when hospitals have opportunities to utilize systems to positively enable reduced fasting capabilities, these should be explored.

There are limitations with this research that need to be acknowledged. Relying on the electronic medical record to record NPO orders assumes the order is entered into the system, and that fasting and cessation of fasting occur in line with these orders. This also assumes patients do not consume food external to the hospital food service system, such as when family members bring food in or when food is obtained from the hospital cafeteria. There was also no blinding during the intervention. As discussed above, a major limitation is the small sample size because of a scaled down study meaning the intervention period was halved. The study was conducted because surgical activity was increasing after the COVID‐19 pandemic, and this was likely a period of high staff fatigue, 16 possibly leading to a reluctance to take on new ideas and change. 17

More recent strategies to reduce preoperative fasting have included allowing preoperative clear fluids (Sip Til Send) 18 or water (Think Drink) 19 up until patients are sent for their procedure. These strategies have been shown to improve patient satisfaction 20 while not increasing risk of harm. Although these strategies provide a solution to managing complex ordering systems, they still need to address barriers related to culture.

With such a dearth of research to guide interventions to reduce excessive fasting, there is a need for ongoing research in this area. Future research needs to incorporate these novel approaches to fasting, alongside larger scale studies, with a 6‐month intervention period to allow for practices to be embedded as usual care. Although blinding is not possible, a stepped wedge study design may be pragmatic and robust solution to reduce risk of bias. 21

CONCLUSION

This multifaceted pre/post intervention was not able to show a statistically significant improvement in reducing preoperative fasting. The majority of interventions were seen as beneficial to clinicians and resulted in increased awareness and increased clinician confidence to manage fasting on the surgical wards. Given the complexity and dearth of research in this area, ongoing studies are required with a larger sample size. Further research should factor in long‐term interventions, incorporating emerging strategies such as “sip‐to‐send”or “think‐drink.” The use of implementation frameworks allows future work to build on this, one of the first implementation studies to attempt to address such a long standing deleterious clinical issue.
