Nuances in the surgical treatment of gangliocytomas of the spinal cord: illustrative cases
Authors: Hassan H Amhaz, Christina Abi Faraj, Adam S Wu, Ganesh Rao, Maria A Gubbiotti, Ian E McCutcheon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDIntramedullary gangliocytoma, a tumor rarely seen in the spinal cord, presents unique diagnostic and operative challenges. The authors present 2 cases illustrating these complexities.OBSERVATIONSThe first patient, a 34-year-old woman with cerebral palsy, had 6 years of ascending numbness and worsening leg weakness, bladder incontinence, and thoracic radicular pain. MRI revealed a slightly enhancing area of cord enlargement extending from T5 to the conus. The second patient, a 45-year-old man with unilateral vestibular schwannoma, presented with slowly progressive left leg weakness and an intramedullary tumor at C7–T1. Each patient underwent surgical tumor removal, with one resected completely and the other subtotally. Both surgical specimens demonstrated clusters of ganglion cells strongly immunopositive for neurofilament proteins amid prominent neuropil islands, with no evidence of a glial component, confirming a diagnosis of gangliocytoma. The first patient had a follow-up period of 13 years without tumor recurrence, while the second patient had a follow-up of 3 months.LESSONSAlthough these tumors have a pseudocapsule that may assist safe resection, their interface with normal cord is sometimes focally indistinct. Subtotal resection in some cases may prevent new neurological deficit while permitting long-term maintenance of preoperative levels of function with stable residual disease. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE2570 Keywords: gangliocytoma, ganglion cell tumor, spinal cord, surgery, case reportABBREVIATIONS: GCT = ganglion cell tumor, MEP = motor evoked potential, SSEP = somatosensory evoked potential

Ganglioglioma and gangliocytoma are distinct varieties of mature ganglion cell tumor (GCT) that occur primarily in children and young adults.1–10 Among primary tumors of the CNS, gangliocytoma is extremely rare and accounts for only 0.1%–0.5% of all intracranial lesions. Such tumors usually arise in the temporal lobe, with a smaller subset originating in the frontal or parietal lobes, the hypothalamus, or the floor of the third ventricle.11,12 Isolated dysplastic gangliocytoma of the cerebellum, termed Lhermitte-Duclos disease, is a separate entity. Gangliocytomas arising in the spinal cord are even more unusual and account for < 1% of intramedullary tumors.2 They present a significant surgical challenge due to their rarity, the inconsistency of definition of the tumor margins, and the sensitivity of their anatomical location. As long-term follow-up has never been reported, it has not hitherto been possible to judge the success of surgical techniques in this disease.

We present the cases of 2 patients with gangliocytoma of the thoracic spinal cord associated with slowly progressive myelopathy, in one of whom good tumor control was achieved during extended follow-up, and describe nuances of the surgical technique and histopathological diagnosis of such tumors.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

A 34-year-old female was diagnosed with cerebral palsy at age 4 years and progressive scoliosis at age 10 years and had posterior spinal stabilization with Harrington rods and allograft bone fusion at age 14 years. Her condition was stable for the next 15 years, during which time she functioned independently and walked with the aid of crutches. During this period, she had stable numbness of the left foot and mild left lower leg weakness, with normal function on the right side. At age 29 years, she started experiencing a band of shock-like hyperesthesia at the T4–7 dermatomal levels bilaterally. This was initially responsive to gabapentin, but her pain syndrome became less responsive and more severe over time. At age 32 years, she began to develop myelopathy, with new ascending numbness in both legs, including profound loss of pain and temperature sensation, progressive bilateral leg weakness, and worsening of bowel and bladder incontinence. Her ability to walk deteriorated to the point that she required a wheelchair most of the time. After removal of the Harrington rods to allow for a definitive MRI, a diffuse enlargement of the spinal cord was discovered that began at T6 and extended to the conus medullaris (Fig. 1).

Case 1. A: Sagittal T2-weighted MR image showing expanded cord below T4 with a relatively sharp signal transition between the rostral end of the tumor and normal spinal cord. The tumor was not contrast enhancing and showed no signal demarcation at its boundaries on T1-weighted images. B: It extended all the way to the conus, visible within an expanded spinal canal within dystrophic, scalloped vertebrae at each level. A posterior fusion mass is evident.

She underwent thoracic laminectomy with subtotal excision of the intramedullary mass from T5 to T7. The spinal cord was clearly enlarged and friable beneath a superficially normal-appearing surface. A distinct plane of separation between the dorsal half of the lesion and the cord allowed resection, but the plane became indistinct in the ventral portion; thus, complete removal was not possible. Resection was limited to this portion of the cord to allow preservation of leg and sphincter function. Somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs) were absent throughout the operation, but motor evoked potentials (MEPs) were preserved. Frozen section taken from the center of the intramedullary lesion demonstrated only reactive changes, but permanent sections confirmed a diagnosis of gangliocytoma, WHO grade I (see below). The histology is displayed in Fig.2. No neoplastic glial component was identified, nor were any high-grade features present.

Her neurological status was unchanged compared to her preoperative examination. Her hyperesthetic pain resolved almost entirely immediately after surgery. However, despite no further tumor growth on MRI, her discomfort, in the form of shocking and burning pain to the torso and bilateral lower extremities, gradually returned during 13 years of subsequent follow-up. In addition, she requires gabapentin, duloxetine, and baclofen and had placement of a spinal stimulator for the control of pain and spasticity, and her bilateral leg weakness has worsened; however, she remains ambulatory.

Case 2

A 45-year-old male presented with a 10-year history of mild left leg weakness with a dragging foot, and a recent worsening that interfered with his gait, with several near falls. Concurrent with this was a history of slowly progressive hearing loss on the left side and intermittent dizziness leading to a diagnosis of unilateral vestibular schwannoma. The patient had no back pain. On examination, he had normal strength in both arms and in the right leg. In the left leg, he had 4/5 power in all muscle groups, with obvious atrophy compared with the right. Sensation was intact, and reflexes were normal.

MRI of the brain demonstrated a left vestibular schwannoma with intracanalicular location, but no additional intracranial tumors to suggest neurofibromatosis type 2. MRI of the spine revealed an intramedullary lesion at C7–T1 (Fig. 3A and B). The posterior fossa tumor was managed conservatively. For the symptomatic spine mass, the patient underwent C6–T1 laminectomy and tumor resection. After midline myelotomy, an intramedullary lesion with a distinct pseudocapsule was readily identified and distinguished from normal cord by its dark color. The lesion was removed in piecemeal fashion and a gross-total resection was obtained (Fig. 3C). Significantly decreased SSEPs from the left leg occurred during the dissection, while SSEPs on the right side remained stable throughout the procedure.

B: T2-weighted image showing hyperintense signal within the area of expansion, but also tapering to the T2 level. Surgical pathology revealed a tumor composed exclusively of mature ganglion cells arrayed as single cells and small clusters in a fibrillary matrix that was strongly positive for synaptophysin and neurofilament protein (Fig. 4). The ganglion cells exhibited prominent cell body decoration by synaptophysin, cytoplasmic positivity for neurofilament protein, and nuclear positivity for Neu-N. Immunostaining for glial fibrillary acidic protein revealed only scattered entrapped processes of reactive astrocytes; no neoplastic glial component was identified. No mitotic figures, vascular proliferation, or necrosis was seen. A diagnosis of gangliocytoma (WHO grade I) was rendered.

Case 2. Gangliocytoma histology. The tumor was composed of clusters of mature ganglion cells (A; H&E, original magnification ×200) and exhibited strong positivity for the neuronal markers synaptophysin (B; original magnification ×200), neurofilament proteins (C; original magnification ×200), and Neu-N (D; original magnification ×200). GFAP immunostaining revealed scattered entrapped glial processes, with no reactivity seen in the tumor cell population (E; original magnification ×400).

Postoperatively, the patient had decreased sensation below T3 and a new left foot drop. Three months later, his foot drop had resolved, but numbness persisted on the left below the T9 dermatome. He has since been lost to follow-up.

Discussion

Observations

Here we add 2 cases of gangliocytoma of the thoracic spinal cord to the 9 cases of intramedullary gangliocytoma extant in the literature.2–10 Table 1 shows all previously reported cases of pure gangliocytoma of the spinal cord, 4 of them in children, all of whom had scoliosis.3,4,6,10 Additional cases were mentioned by Matsuyama et al. and Cossu et al., but as no details were given, they are excluded here.13,14 The apparent association with scoliosis (also seen in our case 1) is consistent with the chronicity of these tumors and their frequent location in the thoracic cord, and most patients present with bilateral leg weakness and/or sensory change. Jacob et al. reported a gangliocytoma in a 19-year-old woman who presented with a long history of hyperhidrosis and limb paresthesia.5 Indeed, in our case 1, the clinical diagnosis of “cerebral palsy” in childhood may simply reflect the presence of the spinal cord tumor from an early age. Ganglioneuroma has also been described within the spinal cord, sometimes in association with neurofibromatosis type 1,15 and has sometimes been classified together with spinal cord gangliocytoma,5 but it is now considered a closely related but distinct entity.

Imaging

Gangliocytomas have no uniform imaging characteristics that distinguish them from gangliogliomas, their GCT counterpart, and may show nonuniform enhancement with variable degrees of cyst formation and calcification on CT.2,4–6,8 MRI typically reveals a heterogeneous mass of low intensity on T1-weighted images and high intensity on T2-weighted images.2,4,5,9,10 Gangliocytomas may show moderate heterogeneous or focal enhancement or may fail to enhance altogether. As a result, definitive diagnosis relies on histological analysis of the operative tissue specimen.

Neuropathology

Gangliocytomas belong to the family of primary CNS tumors containing mature ganglion cells, a group that also includes gangliogliomas, ganglioneuromas, and ganglioneurocytomas.1,2,16,17 Supplemental Table 1 discusses key differences in pathology, management, and prognosis among gangliogliomas, gangliocytomas, and ganglioneuromas. The neoplastic elements in gangliocytomas are atypical ganglion cells, and such tumors are distinguished from gangliogliomas by a normocellular, nonneoplastic astrocytic network.4,6 In gangliogliomas, by contrast, both the neuronal and glial networks show neoplastic features.1 Ganglioneuromas are mature GCTs usually originating from spinal nerve roots and supported by an underlying Schwann cell network.18 These lesions typically grow in a dumbbell fashion with extradural (and sometimes transforaminal) extension, but they may show intradural extension or exist within the spinal cord.15 They are identified by their pathognomonic whorled appearance on T2-weighted MRI. In contrast, spinal cord gangliocytomas are purely intramedullary, although there is one reported case of leptomeningeal dissemination from an unusual multicentric mixed neurocytoma/gangliocytoma arising in the cerebellum, brainstem, and spinal cord (specifically, at C7–T1).19 Ganglioneurocytomas are neurocytomas with a ganglion cell component; only 6 cases have been reported in the spinal cord.17,18,20–23 Pure gangliocytomas (like the 2 cases reported here) lack the component of small cell neurocytic differentiation.

Diagnosis by intraoperative frozen section can be difficult in patients with gangliocytoma. Our own case 1 yielded a frozen-section diagnosis of reactive tissue, quite different from the tumor revealed on permanent sections. Removal in that case was guided by the distinctly abnormal gross appearance of the lesion. An experienced neuropathologist is essential for accurate interpretation of such cases, and if this diagnosis is suspected from imaging, it may be necessary to send slides to a referral center to ensure it is properly excluded or confirmed. Although the clinical presentation and surgical nuances of gangliocytoma mirror those of ganglioglioma, precision of diagnosis is important as the future may reveal distinctions of tumor biology not now apparent.

Treatment

Gangliocytomas are well-differentiated indolent tumors with little propensity for accelerated growth.2–6 Like gangliogliomas, they are typically resistant to radiation therapy and chemotherapy, and resection is the primary treatment.4,5 For both spinal cord gangliocytoma and ganglioglioma, gross-total resection is believed to provide the most favorable long-term outcome.1,5 No studies have shown differences in outcome between radical and subtotal resection in either tumor type.1,5 Jallo et al. reported a 30% rate of recurrence for both complete and incomplete resection in a large series of gangliogliomas, but no comparable series exists for gangliocytomas, which are much less common.1 For intramedullary tumors in general, freedom from recurrence occurs much more often when a detectable plane is present between the tumor and spinal cord.24 In addition, gross-total removal of tumor is more commonly achieved in patients who present with better neurological function.13 Finally, Gupte et al. identified a BCR-NTRK2 fusion and overexpression of BRAF in their case, suggesting that molecular testing could guide targeted treatment options to complement resection.10

One of our patients (case 2) presented with minimal myelopathy and a good tumor-cord interface; he received gross-total resection and has remained free of tumor. In our other patient, preoperative neurological function was more severely affected, the tumor did not have a uniformly well-developed pseudocapsule, the deeper margins with normal spinal cord were indistinct, and the risk of inducing new neurological deficits with gross-total resection was high. A more conservative resection was performed with good maintenance of function after surgery, and to date no further growth of the tumor remnant has occurred.

The results of surgery are not given in the extant literature, where follow-up is either absent2,6–8 or ranges from 2 to 24 months.3–5,9,10 The much longer follow-up of 156 months reported here for our case 1 provides, for the first time, evidence of the good long-term surgical outcomes possible for spinal gangliocytoma.

Surgical Technique

Resection of intramedullary gangliocytoma is achieved via midline incision and laminectomy of the appropriate levels, with monitoring of spinal cord function using SSEP and MEPs throughout. Since both of our cases occurred in the thoracic spinal cord, laminoplasty was not deemed necessary for maintenance of spinal stability. Tumor localization is confirmed by ultrasound, which is most helpful in the sagittal plane. If the tumor breaches the pial surface at a readily accessible point, that location may be used for starting the tumor exposure. Typically, however, a midline myelotomy (used in both of our patients) leads most safely and quickly to the tumor. The pia is divided sharply, and the myelotomy is gently deepened with microdissection until the relatively dark tumor surface is identified. Pial retraction sutures allow a gentle and optimal display of the tumor.

In some patients the tumor is well circumscribed and surrounded by a pseudocapsule, identifiable by a change in texture and often by a darkening of color. Because gangliocytomas tend to be large and to thin the cord significantly, the pseudocapsule should be opened and the central portion of the tumor debulked by piecemeal removal or by low-intensity ultrasonic aspiration. Gentle circumferential microdissection is then undertaken (when possible) to separate the pseudocapsule from the surrounding spinal cord. Gross-total resection can be achieved if the pseudocapsule remains distinct from the cord over the entirety of the tumor’s surface (as in our case 2). If an easily definable plane between the tumor and the surrounding spinal cord cannot be identified in some regions (as in our case 1), direct stimulation can be used to identify functional cord in order to direct the dissection. However, in the absence of such a plane, the indolent nature of these tumors makes subtotal removal with maintenance of function preferable to complete removal at the cost of new neurological dysfunction.

Lessons

Rarely seen within the spinal cord, gangliocytomas are mature, indolent GCTs characterized histologically by atypical ganglion cells in a nonneoplastic astrocytic background. Although gross-total resection is sought when a distinct tumor-cord interface is present, the indolent natural history and uncertain border of some of these lesions suggests that subtotal resection even in the absence of adjuvant therapy may be sufficient or even necessary for good prognosis and maintenance of neurological function.
