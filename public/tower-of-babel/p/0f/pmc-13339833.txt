Using HIV antibody measurements to detect viral load rebound: An analysis from an analytic treatment interruption study in the United States
Authors: Anna Bershteyn, Shiying You, Adam Epstein-Shuman, Zoe Gotthold, Hae-Young Kim, Nao Yamamoto, David Kaftan, Tae-Wook Chun, Oliver Laeyendecker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives:HIV viral load (VL) testing using polymerase chain reaction is a mainstay of treatment monitoring but is technically demanding, time-consuming, and costly. We investigated whether antibody measurements can detect VL rebound, leveraging a recent analytic treatment interruption (ATI) study, NCT03225118.Methods:We tested longitudinal specimens in N = 22 ATI participants (91% male, 38–61 year age range, 2.1–15.9 years VL suppression pre-ATI) using limiting antigen (LAg) antibody assays, measured as normalized optical density (ODn). We used Bayesian inference to fit linear mixed-effects models, including the time between first detectable VL and ODn increase, the time between peak VL and peak ODn, and the ODn rate of change after VL re-suppression.Results:LAg ODn increased 4.36 weeks (95% confidence interval [CI]: 3.58, 5.79) after treatment interruption and 2.78 weeks (95% CI: 1.94, 4.15) after VL became detectable. ODn peaked 1.03 weeks (95% CI: 0.25, 1.93) after VL peaked, increasing 1.70 fold (95% CI: 1.39, 2.08) compared with pre-interruption levels. After VL re-suppression, ODn declined by −0.015 units per week (95% CI: −0.017, −0.013) over 1 year of follow-up.Conclusion:LAg antibody levels increased in all participants 2–4 weeks after VL detection during treatment interruption. Longitudinal antibody measurements could support facile, rapid, and low-cost HIV treatment monitoring.Keywords: HIV, Antibodies, Rapid test, Triage test, Viral load, Antiretroviral therapy

Introduction

HIV viral load (VL) testing by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is a mainstay of routine monitoring for over 30 million people receiving HIV treatment globally. VL testing is currently recommended every 3–4 months in the United States [1] and every 6–12 months in resource-limited settings [2] to verify treatment adherence and identify potential signs of drug resistance. Maintaining an undetectable VL is considered the primary goal of HIV treatment, as it signifies prevention of HIV disease progression [3] and transmission [4]. Beyond clinical care, VL testing is essential for measuring progress toward the widely adopted “95–95-95” HIV programmatic targets: diagnosing ≥95% of people living with HIV, providing treatment to ≥95% of those diagnosed, and achieving undetectable VL in ≥95% of those on treatment [5]. Large-scale population-based studies have used VL to assess progress toward these targets and identify gaps.

Despite its clinical and public health importance, VL testing has important drawbacks. The technical complexity of PCR requires specialized laboratory infrastructure, incurring costs, delays, and barriers to access in remote settings. Studies have found that if VL results could be returned faster, for example, within the same clinical encounter as sampling, individuals with unsuppressed VL would experience significantly improved odds of re-suppression [6–8]. However, point-of-care PCR instruments are infrequently used, likely due to high per-specimen costs [9], low throughput, and—despite being considered “rapid”—requiring an hour or more to return results [10].

Another limitation of PCR testing is that it only measures VL “in the moment” and may fail to reflect between-test viral rebounds. Studies indicate that HIV treatment adherence often increases just before clinic visits [11]. This phenomenon, known as “white coat compliance,” is observed for numerous oral therapies beyond HIV [12–14] due to factors such as the social desirability of being perceived as adherent by a medical professional [15]. Failure to detect between-visit viral rebounds would be particularly concerning in resource-limited settings, where VL testing is relatively infrequent (typically annual) even when guidelines are fully implemented [2]. Furthering this issue, clusters of differentiations (CD4+) T cell counts—a longer-term measure of immune deterioration due to HIV replication—have become less common in routine treatment monitoring to reduce costs [16]. Low-cost tests that provide information about treatment effectiveness over a longer retrospective time window could usefully complement VL testing, similarly to how hemoglobin A1c testing complements “in-the-moment” glucose testing for diabetes [17].

Finally, tests that reflect VL over retrospective time windows could help assess harms in the wake of systemic shocks that simultaneously disrupt both care access and data systems—such as natural disasters, pandemics, conflict, and funding withdrawal. For example, in 2025, abrupt United States bilateral aid withdrawals and “stop work” orders disrupted both HIV care and record-keeping in resource-limited settings that had relied on international aid for these functions [18]. The number of HIV-positive people who experienced viral rebounds during this disruption is currently unknown but could be ascertained with a retrospective viral rebound test such as the one proposed.

Antibody-based tests offer a promising avenue toward rapid, affordable, and facile “hemoglobin A1c-like” tests that reflect VL over a retrospective time window. One such test is the commercially available limiting antigen (LAg) antibody assay, a modified immunosorbent assay available in multi-well or rapid lateral flow test formats that detects the quantity and strength of human immunoglobulin G binding to HIV glycoprotein 41 [19]. LAg is widely used to assess recency of HIV infection testing because,in the initial months of infection, the test’s read-out—normalized optical density (ODn)—increases sharply as increased viral stimulus drives a stronger and more specific antibody responses [20]. Studies have found that LAg ODn decreases in individuals who have received long-term HIV treatment, requiring that these individuals be carefully screened out of HIV incidence estimation algorithms, otherwise, they may be misclassified as recently infected [21]. We hypothesized that LAg ODn declines observed among those on treatment could potentially be harnessed for the novel application of retrospective VL rebound detection, with applications for HIV treatment monitoring and population-based surveys. Promisingly, previous studies suggest that LAg ODn increases following HIV treatment failure (i.e., periods of detectable VL), but due to infrequent specimen collection (at most semi-annually), those studies could not discern the dynamics of LAg ODn relative to HIV VL [22].

Analytic treatment interruption (ATI) studies provide a unique opportunity to study the dynamics of LAg ODn before, during, and after treatment interruption. These studies typically recruit participants with long-term VL suppression on treatment and conduct a planned interruption of treatment, with frequent VL testing to inform when treatment should be re-started [23]. We used biobanked specimens from a recent ATI study[24] to compare trajectories of VL and LAg ODn using Bayesian linear mixed-effects modeling. We hypothesized that LAg ODn would increase soon after viral rebound during treatment interruption and that ODn would gradually decline following VL re-suppression—providing a “hemoglobin A1c-like” counterpart to VL testing.

Methods

Study population

We tested specimens from a previously completed ATI study (NCT03225118) conducted by the US National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases [24]. Briefly, the study enrolled 22 adults receiving HIV treatment in the United States. Demographically, participants were predominantly male (20/22), with a median age of 51 (range: 38–61), most self-identifying as either White or Caucasian (10/22) or Black or African American (9/22). The majority (20/22) were receiving integrase strand transfer inhibitor-based HIV treatment, and all (22/22) had undetectable VL at enrollment, which had been maintained for a median of 7.7 years (range: 2.1–15.9 years). The median CD4+ T cell count at enrollment was 767 cells per μL (range: 466–1778).

Study participants began their ATI by discontinuing treatment at a designated time and ended their ATI by re-initiating the same treatment regimen when any of the following criteria were met: plasma viremia exceeding 1000 copies/mL for ≥4 consecutive weeks, a large decline in CD4+ T cell percentage (>30% decline) or count (to <350 cells/μL), onset of HIV-related symptoms, or pregnancy.

Participants provided plasma specimens for VL testing and biobanking weekly during treatment interruption and for 4 weeks afterward, then monthly until 6 months post-interruption, and at 1 year post-interruption. Data on VL and CD4+ T cell count for each participant at each timepoint, along with additional study details, were published elsewhere [24].

The original study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (protocol number 17-I-0106). All subjects provided written informed consent. Data and specimens for the current investigation were de-identified for further analysis following the original study’s data and sample-sharing protocols.

LAg ODn measurement

We tested specimens using the commercially available Maxim HIV-1 LAg Avidity Enzyme Immunoassay (Maxim Biomedical, Bethesda, MD) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, we incubated 1:100 diluted plasma in pre-coated microwell plates for 1 hour at 37°C. After four washes, we added dissociation buffer to remove low-avidity antibodies. To bind to the remaining immunoglobulin (Ig)G, we added goat anti-human IgG peroxidase, followed by a tetramethylbenzidine substrate. Color development was stopped with sulfuric acid after 15 minutes, and OD was read at 450 nm with 650 nm as a reference using a SpectraMax iD3 spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA). Normalized ODn was calculated as the OD in each well divided by the mean of triplicate calibrator samples run on the same microwell plate.

Data analysis

We fit a segmented linear mixed-effects model to longitudinal trajectories of LAg ODn and VL. VL (Figure 1, red curve) was modeled as a triangular function consisting of two linear segments: the first representing an increase from undetectable levels to peak, and the second representing a decline from peak to undetectable levels. Antibody levels in ODn (Figure 1, blue curve) were modeled as three linear segments: the first representing a baseline trend, the second representing an increase to a peak, and the third representing the trend after peak.

Schematic of the segmented linear mixed-effects regression model used for viral load (red) and LAg antibody levels (blue). Primary outcomes were time interval t1 from detectable viral load until LAg antibody increase, time interval t2 from peak viral load until peak LAg antibody level, and the slope of LAg antibodies after the peak.

LAg, limiting antigen.

We fit as model parameters the slope of each of the three segments of the ODn trajectory, the time of each of the two breakpoints where the segments intersected, and the y-intercept of the first segment—the latter of which served to shift the entire trajectory up or down. Parameters were specified as either random effects (i.e., fit separately for each participant in the study) or fixed effects (i.e., fit as a collective value across all participants). We defined the y-intercept of the first segment and the slopes of the first two segments as random effects, and the slope of the third segment as a fixed effect. The latter allowed us to estimate a mean rate of ODn decline in response to treatment resumption.

The time of the two breakpoints were alternatively modeled as either fixed effects or as random effects as follows: Model A—both breakpoints fixed, Model B—first breakpoint fixed, second breakpoint random, Model C—first breakpoint random, second breakpoint fixed, and Model D—both breakpoints random. We compared models using the Widely Applicable Information Criterion (WAIC), a model selection criterion that is used for hierarchical models because the regularity assumption (that the likelihood function is approximately Gaussian) used in other common model selection criteria (Akaike Information Criterion, Bayesian Information Criterion) is less likely to hold [25].

For each model, we compared fitted ODn breakpoints with key time points along each participant’s observed VL trajectory (Figure 2, red curves): detectability, peak, and re-suppression. VL rebound was defined as the midpoint between the first VL measurement above the limit of detection (≥40 HIV RNA copies/mL) and the preceding measurement below the limit of detection (<40 HIV RNA copies/mL). This method accounts for VL crossing the threshold for detectability earlier than the time of sample collection. VL peak was defined as the first time point with a higher VL than its preceding time point and higher than the next two consecutive time points, which was generally the highest VL observed during ATI unless “double-humped” VL curves (e.g., Figure 2, upper right) were separated by a “trough” of ≥2 weeks. Re-suppression was defined as the first of at least two consecutive undetectable VL results (<40 HIV RNA copies/mL) observed after treatment reinitiation. Primary outcomes were the time interval between VL rebound and increase in ODn (t1 denoted in Figure 1), the time between the VL peak and ODn peak (t2 denoted in Figure 1), and the rate at which ODn declined after peaking.

HIV viral load (red) and LAg assay results in units of normalized optical density (ODn, blue) in four illustrative study participants. Pink regions indicate the period of antiretroviral treatment interruption.

LAg, limiting antigen.

We tested the normality of the modeled Bayesian posterior estimates using three methods. First, we compared posterior means and medians. Second, we performed a Shapiro-Wilk test for normality. Third, we visually inspected posterior distributions for unimodal and symmetric properties and visually inspected Q-Q plots for alignment with expected normal quantiles. Additional details of these tests are provided in the Supplementary Appendix.

Analyses were performed in R version 4.4.0 using the package brms (v2.21.5), which provides an interface to the Stan C++ programming language to perform Bayesian inference for linear multi-level models [26]. Models were executed through the cmdstanr interface (v0.8.0), which communicates with CmdStan (v2.37.0), the command-line C++ implementation of Stan used to compile and run the sampling algorithms. Computations were conducted on macOS 15.5 (Apple M1).

Results

All participants initially had undetectable VL, aligned with study inclusion criteria. After treatment interruption, VL became detectable in all participants and returned to undetectable levels after treatment re-initiation (Figure 2, red curves). The median time from treatment interruption until detectable VL was 1.5 weeks (range: 0.5–4.6 weeks). The median time from treatment interruption until VL peak was 4.8 weeks (range: 2.3–9.3 weeks), peaking at a mean of 764,705 RNA copies/mL (median: 149,743 copies/mL; range: 1804–6,315,485 copies/mL). The median time from treatment re-initiation to VL re-suppression was 5.9 weeks (range: 2.3–15.6 weeks).

Initial LAg ODn values varied widely among participants, with a median first ODn of 2.44 (range: 0.28–3.83). ODn increased during treatment interruption in all participants (Figure 2, blue curves), peaking at a median of 1.03 weeks (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.25, 1.93) after VL peaked and increasing 1.70-fold (95% CI: 1.39, 2.08) compared with pre-interruption levels. Across all participants, ODn was observed to decline over the remainder of the study but too gradually to reach baseline levels by 1 year of follow-up (Figure 2, blue curves).

Segmented linear regression models with two VL segments and three ODn segments converged for all study participants and all four model specifications. Parameterizing the time of the two breakpoints in the ODn trajectory as random effects (i.e., separate values fit for each participant) resulted in the best model selection score (Model D, WAIC = −96.18, see Table 1). This model showed ODn beginning to increase at a median of 4.36 weeks (95% CI: 3.58–5.79) after treatment interruption, and 2.78 weeks (median; 95% CI: 1.94–4.15) after VL became detectable, with ODn peaking 1.03 weeks (median; 95% CI: 0.25–1.93) after the peak in VL (Figure 3). Across all model specifications (Table 1), ODn consistently increased within 5 weeks of VL rebound (longest t1 in Model A: 4.99 weeks, 95% CI: 4.03–5.21).

The slopes of the three ODn trajectory segments demonstrated a delayed but steep increase during treatment interruption, followed by a gradual decline after treatment re-initiation (Figure 3). The slope of the initial ODn trajectory segment was slightly positive (slope = 0.045, 95% CI: 0.017–0.070); the slope of the second segment increased by more than 19-fold (slope = 0.868, 95% CI: 0.523–1.386); and the slope of the final segment was significantly negative (slope = −0.015, 95% CI: −0.017 to −0.013) (Table 1). Bayesian posterior estimates were approximately normally distributed for all four model specifications, with nearly identical means and medians and distributions that appeared unimodal and symmetric (see Supplementary Appendix).

The effect of treatment interruption on ODn remained detectable for several months post-antiretroviral therapy re-initiation. Among the 20 participants retained for 24 weeks after treatment re-initiation, ODn ranged from 2.44–4.23 and was significantly elevated relative to ODn at study enrollment (P <0.001). For the 17 participants retained for 52 weeks after treatment interruption, ODn ranged from 1.99–4.15 and remained significantly elevated compared with baseline (P = 0.001).

Discussion

This study revealed predictable LAg ODn patterns before, during, and after HIV treatment interruption. ODn increased sharply approximately 1 month after treatment interruption, which was approximately 2.5 weeks after VL become detectable. ODn peaked, on average, 1 week after peak VL. Unlike VL, which dropped precipitously below the limit of detection once treatment was reinitiated, ODn declined gradually, remaining significantly above pre-interruption levels for at least 1 year.

Our findings suggest that LAg ODn could be developed into a novel long-term indicator of HIV treatment interruptions, analogously to how hemoglobin A1c provides a long-term indicator of blood glucose. In addition to the practical advantages of antibody-based measurements compared with VL PCR, LAg assays could be particularly useful when assisting clients who struggle with treatment adherence except when a clinical encounter is nigh (“white coat adherence”) [11], and for assessing impacts when HIV care and VL testing are concurrently disrupted (e.g., pandemics, conflict, natural disasters, funding freezes) [18].

Alongside these promising attributes, LAg ODn has potential drawbacks as a treatment monitoring tool. We found that ODn responded to treatment interruptions more slowly than VL (1 month vs 2 weeks), implying a longer “window period” for detecting HIV treatment failure. Absolute ODn values varied widely across participants (range of 0.28–4.84 across 22 study participants), as expected given the wide diversity of humoral immune responses in human populations [27]. This implies that a single ODn measurement may be inadequate to distinguish previous treatment interruption from high baseline ODn value. The use of longitudinal ODn measurements could compensate for this shortfall, since ODn trajectories exhibited a predictable increase and decline relative to baseline. However, longitudinal measurements introduce complexity avoidable by PCR-based VL testing. Further studies are needed to assess whether the wide variation in baseline values is observed for other HIV antibody tests, and whether algorithmic combinations of multiple test read-outs could “normalize” the variation in baseline ODn measurements.

Our study has several important limitations. First, we used a laboratory-based multi-well plate LAg assay to rapidly test a large number of samples and quantify assay outputs. However, many practical advantages of LAg testing—such as speed and ease of use—would be enhanced with the assay’s 20-minute lateral flow test format. Both test formats are commercially available and have been shown to correlate well when comparing ODn to the darkness of the band on the assay strip measured with a digital camera [28,29]. Future work should directly evaluate rapid LAg test strips for detecting HIV treatment interruptions, including test strip digital read-out modalities for optimal accuracy and ease of implementation in clinical settings.

Second, as a first study of its kind, our study focused on fitting longitudinal trajectories to understand patterns of LAg ODn relative to VL but did not formally test the clinical effectiveness of antibody testing for HIV treatment monitoring in a large or prospective cohort. Future research, potentially involving interventional trials, is needed to understand the clinical and implementation-related implications of using LAg assays for treatment monitoring.

Third, study participants underwent a single, full, prolonged treatment interruption lasting a mean of 6.8 weeks (range: 2.3–15.6), which may impact antibody levels differently than shorter, partial, or sporadic treatment interruptions. Future studies should analyze antibody trajectories in individuals receiving HIV treatment who experience a wider range of VL patterns, such as repeated viral blips or intermittent low-level viremia. Such studies could clarify questions such as the minimum VL necessary to increase ODn, the minimum duration of viral rebound necessary to increase ODn, and how multiple intermittent viral rebounds affect ODn trajectories. Our analysis of full and prolonged treatment interruptions could serve as a foundation for interpreting antibody trajectories in these more complex scenarios.

Finally, we analyzed specimens from a cohort of predominantly male individuals in North America, all of whom were stable on HIV treatment with high baseline CD4+ T cell counts. There is a dearth of ATI studies for population segments including women and residents of low- and middle-income countries, despite these constituting a majority of people living with HIV globally [30]. Longitudinal cohorts with frequent specimen collection in the context of treatment interruption could help to test the generalizability of our findings to other populations, settings, and HIV subtypes.

Conclusion

A commercially available LAg assay, conventionally used to measure recency of HIV infection, has the potential to provide a rapid, low-cost, and longer-term measurement of HIV treatment success compared with PCR-based VL measurement. Further research is needed to assess the utility of this test for clinical decision-making and population-based monitoring tool and to expand this domain research to additional assays and populations.
