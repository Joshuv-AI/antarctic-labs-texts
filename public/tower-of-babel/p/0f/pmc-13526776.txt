Failure mechanisms and recycling potential of railway sleepers damaged during the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes
Authors: Muslum Murat Maras, Hamza Tas, Hüseyin Gazi Yigit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring the modernization of railway infrastructure, key structural components such as rails, ballast, and concrete sleepers particularly those used in heavy freight transportation often reach the end of their service life or suffer severe damage due to natural disasters. This process results in the generation of substantial amounts of construction and demolition waste along railway corridors, making effective waste management and recycling increasingly critical from both environmental sustainability and economic efficiency perspectives. The 7.7 and 7.6 magnitude earthquakes that struck Kahramanmaraş in 2023 caused extensive damage across a wide geographical region, including significant destruction to railway infrastructure. In this context, the mechanisms of damage to railway lines and the recycling potential of the resulting waste materials were systematically investigated. This study aims to assess the feasibility of recycling seismically damaged and end-of-service-life reinforced concrete railway sleepers as raw materials for the production of green high-strength concrete in the construction sector. B58 and B70 type concrete sleepers, obtained as waste from the TCDD 5th Regional Directorate railway lines in Türkiye, were processed using environmentally friendly techniques to regenerate recycled aggregates and produce sector-applicable high-strength green concrete. The findings of this study demonstrate that the recycling of earthquake-damaged railway sleepers into green concrete plays a significant role in reducing environmental impacts and contributes effectively to sustainability goals in the built environment.Keywords: Recycled railway sleepers, High-strength concrete, Sustainable railway, Circular economySubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Natural hazards

Introduction

Natural disasters are of critical importance in railway transport systems, and it is essential that load-bearing elements are durable, particularly for the safe continuation of heavy freight transport and transport infrastructure1,2. They are of critical importance in the post-disaster period, especially during the earthquake recovery process, in terms of the rapid and safe transport and delivery of aids3. In these rail transport systems, sleepers with high strength and energy absorption capacity are required to reduce seismic effects4. With the increase in railway usage in recent years, new generation concrete sleepers are preferred in high-speed trains and heavy freight transport. These sleeper elements provide a safe transport network by damping dynamic loads5. Concrete sleeper elements, which ensure that these loads are transferred to the ground correctly, suffer damage in outdoor environments and undergo permanent deformation6. Significant work is being carried out to improve the performance characteristics of these elements7. New-generation sleepers are being produced with emerging technology to reduce maintenance costs and impart high strength properties. These types of sleepers dampen dynamic loads, thereby ensuring the safety of the railway system8.

As new generation high-strength prefabricated sleepers are exposed to these load effects, high-strength and longer-lasting rail structure elements are being designed9,10. These concrete sleeper elements are increasingly preferred in long-distance systems due to their quick and easy replacement11. These ready-made production elements minimise labour errors that may occur in field applications and eliminate defects that may arise during casting12. In rail transport, a large amount of construction and demolition waste (CDW) is generated from sleepers that have reached the end of their service life due to seismic effects. Recycling this waste and reducing its environmental impact is extremely important13. In addition, a wide variety of materials such as concrete, timber, steel, brick, asphalt, glass and plastic are released from structures damaged after earthquakes, and this constitutes a significant environmental problem14. Especially after large-scale and destructive earthquakes, the need for reprocessing and recycling of construction and demolition waste (CDW) is increasing15,16. Since natural disasters cause serious environmental impacts by generating large amounts of CDW, post-disaster recovery and reconstruction processes are extremely critical. Current studies show that there is a significant potential for reducing CO2 emissions in earthquake-related waste management through life cycle assessment and material recycling and reuse processes.[18–20]. However, in this period where product sustainability has become a fundamental requirement, the integration of current studies on post-earthquake CDW management into structural decision-making processes has remained limited. [18,21,22]. In the name of sustainability, proposes a framework to allow for environmentally friendly and scalable reuse of construction debris and achieve the trade-off between seismic safety and circular economy principles.

The 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes caused extensive damage to transportation infrastructure, including railway systems, resulting in significant economic losses and disruptions to regional mobility. Beyond their immediate structural impacts, such large-scale seismic events generate substantial quantities of construction and demolition waste, posing serious environmental and waste management challenges. Therefore, the post-earthquake management of railway infrastructure waste presents not only a reconstruction challenge but also an opportunity to promote sustainable construction practices through the recovery and reuse of valuable concrete resources. Within this context, the utilization of recycled aggregates obtained from damaged railway sleepers offers a promising approach for producing environmentally friendly and high-performance concrete while supporting circular economy principles in the construction sector.

Unlike conventional construction and demolition waste (CDW), B58 and B70 railway sleepers are manufactured using high-strength, prestressed concrete specifically designed to withstand repeated dynamic loading, impact effects, vibration, and severe environmental exposure throughout their service life. Furthermore, while previous studies have primarily focused on recycled aggregates derived from conventional building demolition waste, limited research has investigated the valorization of earthquake-generated railway sleeper waste and its application in the production of high-strength green concrete. Accordingly, the present study contributes to the literature by: (i) evaluating the recycling potential of damaged B58 and B70 prestressed railway sleepers, (ii) characterizing the properties of recycled aggregates obtained from these specialized railway components, and (iii) assessing their suitability for producing sustainable high-strength concrete. These aspects distinguish the current work from conventional CDW recycling studies and constitute the principal novelty of the research.

Earthquake damage mechanisms in concrete railway sleepers

On 6 February 2023, two major earthquakes measuring Mw 7.8 and Mw 7.5 respectively occurred in Turkey, with epicentres in Pazarcık and Elbistan. These earthquakes formed along the Anatolian, Arabian and African plates. The seismic sources occurred on both left-lateral (East Anatolian Fault Zone) and right-lateral (Dead Sea Transform Fault) strike-slip faults. This type of faulting is associated with strike-slip ruptures on both left-lateral and right-lateral faults, indicating that the East Anatolian Fault Zone and the Dead Sea Transform Fault were formed by the Arabian Plate’s movement towards the African-Eurasian plates.[24.

This study examines the damage analyses of railways damaged in two major earthquakes centred in Pazarcık and Elbistan, Kahramanmaraş, on 6 February 2023, and the recycling processes of damaged elements. The primary causes of the damage observed in the railway infrastructure during the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes were a combination of strong ground motions, soil-related effects, and structural deficiencies. The high accelerations and prolonged shaking during the earthquake caused significant horizontal and vertical displacements in the rail-sleeppad-ballast-substructure system. Lateral spreading, particularly in weak and liquefaction-prone soils, led to displacements along the rail axis, level differences, and distortion of the track geometry. In addition, surface fractures and fault displacements directly cut through the railway lines, causing sudden and large displacements, resulting in serious geometric discrepancies at the rail joints. The joint seals and connection systems used at the rail connections could not withstand the large relative displacements caused by the earthquake; they were damaged in both vertical and horizontal directions and became dysfunctional. In engineering structures such as tunnels and bridges, lining damage, stone and concrete spalling, and structural discontinuities occurred due to soil-structure interaction, movement of the surrounding rock mass, and inadequate seismic design details. When all these factors are considered together, it has been determined that the damage to the railway in the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes was critically influenced by seismic effects, ground conditions, inadequate structural details, and connection elements in terms of earthquake design criteria. The connections between rails and sleepers, which transfer loads to the ground in railways, are of great importance. The joint seals and rail connection systems used in rail connections vary depending on external load effects. The positioning arrangements of the seals used in the connections between rails, sleepers and ballast examined in railway lines are important. Fig. 2 shows the connection length (L) distributed symmetrically on both sides of the connection in straight and curved railway lines, and the positioning arrangements of the joints used in the examined rail connections are given in detail under boundary conditions. For the opposed joint, it is positioned with a maximum permissible gap (70 or less) between the adjacent rail ends. In contrast, for stepped joints, both straight and curved rail conditions are considered, and the maximum stepped slip limit is restricted to 55. These limit values are important for the distance of rail joints in the uniform transfer of loads.[26].

Positioning arrangements of seals used in the rail joints examined in straight and curved railway lines[26].

Following the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes, railway field observations revealed gaps in rail joints, lateral displacement and bending damage to rails (Fig. 3). Field photographs showed horizontal deviations and vertical displacements in rail joints. It was observed that the permitted displacement limits at the connection points were exceeded and wide gaps formed. Seismic effects caused sleepers displacement at the rail joints, resulting in extensive deformation of the railway tracks. Damage observed on the railway line included longitudinal and transverse rail displacements, vertical level differences, and geometric inconsistencies at the joints. In addition, surface fractures have led to significant horizontal displacements in transportation corridors, including railway lines, in many areas.

Damage and deformations occurring in the railway line as a result of lateral spreading caused by seismic impact.

In curved track sections, centrifugal and axial stresses, already geometrically present in the rails, combined with earthquake-induced horizontal accelerations, created additional shear and tensile stresses at the rail splices. This prevented the expected rigid behavior of the splice region and led to uncontrolled displacements at the rail ends. In spliced track sections, the increase in the gap between the rail ends to exceed the allowed limit of ≤ 70 mm (Fig. 2) caused the rails to separate during the earthquake, thus resulting in the loss of track integrity. In ballasted (classic) pavements, the sudden change in soil rigidity and the seismic rearrangement (replacement) of the ballast material disrupted the rail-sleeper system interaction. This disruption prevented the symmetrical behavior assumed under boundary conditions from occurring. Consequently, the railway damage observed in the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes resulted from the failure to meet design boundary conditions such as splice gap, stepped shear, and symmetrical splice length defined by shapes, due to large earthquake-induced displacements (Fig. 3). This situation has determined that connection point limit values are quite critical in regions with weak ground conditions and that damage occurs in these regions due to larger displacements.

The Kahramanmaraş earthquakes also caused structural damage to the railway tunnel systems. Field studies revealed block stone detachments at the entrance of stone railway tunnels, falling rock fragments, and damage at rail connection points (Fig. 4). In stone arch railway tunnels, collapses in the side supporting wall elements, joint openings, horizontal displacements, and displacements in block elements in different areas occurred.

When seismic waves arrive at an angle, sections such as those in Fig. 5b show oval displacement.

The damage observed in a reinforced concrete railway tunnel (illustrated in Fig. 6) following the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes resulted from the simultaneous and combined interaction of the tunnel deformation mechanisms schematically presented in Fig. 5. These mechanisms, which define the tensile, compressive, bending, and shear forces generated in the tunnel cross-section through soil-tunnel interaction, collectively explain the observed failure modes. Specifically, the longitudinal seismic waves shown in Fig. 5a created successive tension and compression zones along the tunnel axis. The failure including the spalling and collapse of the concrete lining at the tunnel portal, separations at the lining-soil interface, and cracks that change direction along the lining is attributed to these tensile stresses exceeding the concrete’s capacity. Although the concrete lining is robust in compression, its limited ductility against the increased tension, especially near the portal due to the free surface effect, caused significant concrete disintegration. Furthermore, the oblique wave effects (Fig. 5b) led to the ovalization of the cross-section. Evidence such as the localized disintegration of the lining concrete, non-uniform fractures across the lining thickness, and differential movement traces at the rock-concrete interface aligns with the asymmetrical stress distribution caused by waves reaching the cross-section at an angle.

Damage observed in reinforced concrete railway tunnels after the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes.

The observed pattern is consistent with a shear deformation mechanism. As shown in Fig. 5c, the irregular propagation of longitudinal waves has caused bending deformation, which is particularly evident in the entrance section. Bending damage occurs due to seismic longitudinal load effects, followed by crushing and spalling of concrete in the upper region of the tunnel elements. In particular, this beam-like behaviour is most pronounced in the entrance and exit sections of the tunnel.

In reinforced concrete tunnel entrances, spalling and delamination of the lining concrete were observed, and in some areas, reinforcement rupture and buckling damage were also detected. Furthermore, cracks formed in the side wall and ceiling joints, and localized damage due to shear stresses and lining discontinuities were also observed. Furthermore, irregular deformations and localized stiffness losses occurred along the lining due to the negative impact of tunnel-soil interaction. These types of damage demonstrate that the high accelerations experienced during earthquakes in reinforced concrete tunnels, the variation in ground motions, and the dynamic behavior of the surrounding rock mass create significant stresses on the lining systems.

Fig. 7 shows the experimental and computational boundary conditions and loading arrangement of prestressed monoblock concrete sleepers according to the EN 13230–1 standard. The sleeper is modeled on support and connection elements defined to represent the rail-sleep-ballast interaction. In the figure, (1) the rigid support represents the fixed support used to idealize the vertical load-carrying capacity of the sleeper provided by the ballast. (2) the articulated support allows for rotational freedom of the sleeper, enabling a more realistic modeling of the ballast behavior. (3) the resilient pad is the intermediate layer between the rail and the sleeper, aiming to distribute loads evenly, dampen vibrations, and reduce contact stresses. (4) the prestressed monoblock concrete sleeper is the main load-bearing structural element and transfers the vertical loads from the rail to the ballast. The force Fc shown in the figure represents the vertical wheel load applied from the rail to the middle of the sleeper, and the bending behavior along the sleeper under this load is investigated. The distance Lc, defined along the sleeper span, is a characteristic length that determines the bending moment and shear force distribution of the sleeper depending on the loading and support conditions. These boundary conditions ensure that the stresses occurring in both the mid-section (positive moment) and support regions (negative moment) of the sleepers are safely evaluated according to EN 13230–1[28].

The strong ground movements and prolonged shaking during the earthquake caused significant vertical and horizontal displacements in the track-sleeper-ballast system (Fig. 8). In these two major earthquakes, loosening and settlement damage occurred in the ballast and ties/crossties layers and in the ground. In general, damage to the railways consisted of bending damage to the rail axes and axial displacements in the ties elements. Permanent damage occurred at the connection points of crossties elements with low ground bearing capacity under the influence of horizontal earthquake forces. Furthermore, plastic hinges formed at the connection areas between these ties elements and rail elements, causing severe damage due to rotational and bending moments.

Line geometry distortions that occurred in concrete sleepers as a result of the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes.

At the connection points, the hinged sections allowed the ties/crossties-ballast system to move freely. However, since it did not allow horizontal displacement in the support areas with rigid nodes, torsion and bending damage occurred on the rail axes. Seismic effects caused large deformations by exceeding the displacement limit, particularly in areas with weak soil classes. The results once again highlighted the importance of using designs with high energy absorption capacity, particularly concrete sleepers exhibiting ductile behaviour.

Materials and methods

Source and selection of damaged sleepers

Following the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes, concrete sleeper elements sustained severe damage, significantly reducing their load-bearing capacity. These sleeper elements, which hold the ballast and rails together, caused severe damage to the transport network due to settlement and lateral displacement in the ground under the effects of the earthquake. During this process, the severely damaged sleeper elements were removed and replaced for road safety (Fig. 9). This replacement resulted in a large amount of waste sleeper elements. High-strength green concrete was developed to reuse as aggregate these wastes and return them to production. The recycling processes and reuse of these sleepers are intended to provide significant contributions in terms of environmental impact and sustainable design. These removal operations are considered a necessary intervention to restore safety in the railway infrastructure after the earthquake, correct the track geometry, and maintain long-term operational performance.

Following the earthquake, dismantling and track renewal operations were carried out on concrete sleepers based on the causes of damage.

As a consequence of these earthquakes, the concrete sleepers, which serve as the primary load-bearing elements of the railway track, suffered severe damage. As a result of these comprehensive studies, a large amount of waste material emerged, particularly from B58 and B70 type reinforced concrete sleepers damaged after the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes (Fig. 9).

Material preparation

Processing of sleeper materials from recycled aggregates

The types of damage to railway infrastructure elements resulting from the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes are detailed in Fig. 10a. The most common waste generated from these elements is concrete waste from sleeper elements. The evaluation of this waste for reuse as recycled aggregate is of considerable importance. These wastes are not only caused by seismic effects but also by heavy freight transport and harsh external conditions, which cause deterioration and damage to concrete elements. It is crucial to reuse these concrete wastes from the railways to produce concrete and sustainable, high-strength building materials. Accordingly, damaged concrete blocks obtained from the TCDD 5th Regional Directorate, generally showing cracks, excessive displacements, and deterioration in concrete integrity, were processed in a laboratory environment to obtain Recycled Concrete Aggregate (RCA). In this process, the concrete blocks were first subjected to sizing, removal of foreign materials, and sieving; the particle size distribution characteristics of the RCA were determined in accordance with TS EN 933-1 standards 29. In addition to the recycled aggregates, other components such as cement, additives, and water to be used in the experiments were also selected to produce new concrete with high-performance “green concrete” properties. Concrete sleepers collected from railway lines after the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes reveal the extent of the destructive impact of seismic loads. This situation demonstrates that these materials cannot be reused in railway infrastructure and urgently need to be recycled.

Waste management plan for elements damaged by earthquakes, as well as the recycling process for various sleeper types.

The Kahramanmaraş earthquakes generated a substantial amount of waste material from B58 and B70 type railway sleepers, which are widely used high-strength prestressed reinforced concrete elements in the Turkish railway network (Fig. 10b). Initially, the sleepers were crushed using a jaw crusher to reduce their size into manageable fragments. Subsequently, secondary crushing was performed using an impact crusher to obtain the target aggregate gradation. The prestressed steel reinforcement embedded within the sleepers was separated from the crushed concrete using electromagnetic separation systems and manual inspection to ensure complete removal of metallic components. The resulting recycled aggregates were then screened and classified according to the required particle size distributions. Fine particles and impurities were removed through washing, and the aggregates were stored under laboratory conditions prior to use. To minimize the influence of the higher water absorption capacity of RCA, the aggregates were pre-soaked to a saturated surface-dry (SSD) condition before concrete production. The physical and mechanical properties of the recycled aggregates were determined in accordance with relevant aggregate testing standards, and the aggregate grading complied with the requirements specified in standards for concrete aggregates. The processed recycled concrete aggregates were subsequently incorporated into the concrete mixtures used in this study.

Optimum mixtures were designed to evaluate the suitability and performance criteria of the RCA obtained for use in concrete production. During this design phase, the particle size distribution and the physical, chemical, and mechanical properties of the RCA were determined. As a result of these recycled aggregate experiments, the reuse of construction concrete waste was investigated. It is aimed that the recycling and reuse of this waste and the reduction in the need for natural aggregates will make significant contributions to sustainability in the railway transport system (Fig. 11).

Concrete mix design and production

Although the exact cement type used in the original sleepers could not be identified due to the unavailability of production records, B58 and B70 railway sleepers are prestressed concrete elements typically manufactured in accordance with stringent railway specifications using high-strength Portland cement-based concrete. These sleepers generally possess characteristic compressive strengths exceeding 50 MPa, ensuring high durability and mechanical performance under repeated dynamic loading and environmental exposure. Consequently, the recycled concrete aggregates (RCA) obtained from these sleepers originate from a high-quality concrete source, distinguishing them from conventional construction and demolition waste aggregates and enhancing their potential suitability for high-performance concrete applications.

Concrete mix proportions and production methods were determined by establishing the granulometry curve of concrete fragments separated from sleepers. In general, the binder: aggregate: water ratios were used in the order of nearly 1:3:0.45 in the mixture ratios. In some mixture percentages, the binder ratios were reduced to determine the effect of the binders. The water/cement ratio (w/c) was mostly kept within a narrow range (approximately 0.40 to 0.46) to maintain the workability of the mixture, and the water ratios were adjusted according to the consistency. Superplasticiser (SP) was used as an additive in all mixtures due to the high water absorption capacity of the recycled aggregates. In the last five mixtures, the effect of silica fume in the concrete mixture on strength and durability properties was also determined.

To facilitate a clearer interpretation of the experimental program, the concrete mixtures were categorized into three groups according to their design objectives and the variables investigated. Group A (M1–M16) was developed to evaluate the effect of progressively increasing recycled aggregate content on the fresh and hardened properties of concrete. In this group, the amount of coarse recycled aggregate (CRA) was systematically increased while maintaining a constant total aggregate content, allowing the influence of aggregate replacement ratio to be examined in a controlled manner. Group B (M17–M25) was designed to investigate the combined effects of fine recycled aggregate (FRA) and coarse recycled aggregate (CRA) incorporation at different replacement levels. These mixtures represent various combinations of natural and recycled aggregates and were intended to assess the influence of aggregate type and replacement strategy on concrete performance. Group C (M26–M30) consisted of performance-enhanced recycled aggregate concretes incorporating silica fume (SF) and varying dosages of superplasticizer (SP). The superplasticizer dosage was maintained at a constant level in mixtures M1–M25 because the final mix proportions were established based on a series of preliminary trial mixtures conducted prior to the main experimental program. These preliminary studies were used to determine suitable water-to-cement ratios and admixture contents that would provide adequate workability and allow successful casting of the specimens. Therefore, the selected superplasticizer dosage was considered sufficient for the intended laboratory production conditions.

Experimental program

Characterization and quality control of recycled aggregates

The characterisation and use of recycled aggregates have been investigated. Chemical, physical and mechanical tests were conducted to determine the suitability of these waste materials for use as aggregates. The granulometric curve was determined according to TS EN 933-1 standards to determine the particle size distribution of the aggregates [29]. This distribution is crucial in terms of the durability, density, and workability of the concrete mixture. When determining the physical properties in concrete mix calculations, water absorption and density tests were performed according to TS EN 1097−6 [30]. To measure the recycled aggregate’s resistance to abrasion and fragmentation, the Los Angeles abrasion test was applied according to the TS EN 1097−2 standard 31. The determination of harmful clay and fine matter in these recycled aggregates was determined according to the TS EN 933-9 methylene blue test32. Additionally, the sand equivalent test33 examined the amount of clay, silt, and organic content in the fine aggregate according to the TS EN 933-8 + A1 standard. The total sulphur content in the concrete was determined in accordance with the TS EN 1744−1 [60] standard. Through these multi-stage aggregate tests, the aim is to use recycled aggregates in concrete and reduce potential strength losses in the future. The goal is to recycle and reuse these aggregates to produce high-strength green concrete elements.

Concrete compressive strength testing methodology

The compressive strength values and mechanical performance of concrete specimens produced using recycled aggregates at different percentage ratios were determined. The compressive strength values of the prepared concrete mix designs were determined in accordance with TS EN 12-390 standards34. In the test method, the recycled aggregate percentage was determined according to the total aggregate percentage, and the concrete quality classes were determined according to the concrete strength results.

Throughout the test, the surfaces were cleaned and dried to ensure that the load transfer of the samples was performed correctly. The compressive strength values of each sample were positioned in the centre of the plate to ensure proper load transfer. Immediately before starting the test, the dimensions of the concrete specimens were selected and the compressive strength values were calculated automatically. The maximum force recorded by the machine at the moment of failure was subsequently used to calculate the concrete’s ultimate compressive strength.

Results and discussion

Sieve analysis results of recycled concrete aggregate (RCA)

According to the determined sieve analysis results, concrete aggregates were classified into different fractions based on the general particle size distribution characteristics given in Table 3. The cumulative percentages of fine and coarse concrete aggregate samples passing through different sieve openings were calculated, and the resulting granulometric curves are shown in Fig. 12. Sieve analysis results show that the fine recycled aggregate (FRA) is predominantly concentrated in the 0–4 mm fraction and meets the fine aggregate classification criteria. The fact that 98.6% of the material in the FRA sample is below 4 mm indicates a consistent grain distribution and a suitable granulometry for effective use in the mortar phase, a finding generally consistent with other studies on recycled fine aggregates35. However, the 6.0% ratio of very fine material below 63 μm is within the limits reported in the literature for recycled aggregates [36and may only slightly increase the binder demand of the resulting concrete mixture. The coarse recycled aggregate (CRA), on the other hand, is mainly in the 4–12.5 mm range, and the fact that all particles pass through the 12.5 mm sieve indicates that the maximum grain size is suitable for concrete design. The recycled coarse aggregate ratios were obtained at 15.2% below 4 mm, which is within the acceptable limits for these aggregates according to similar studies [37].The sieve analysis results show that the proportion of fine materials below 63 μm is quite low, indicating that the aggregates do not contain harmful materials. In concrete design, this ratio clearly affects the workability and strength properties of the concrete. Hoffmann et al.[38] and Sobuz et al39. stated in their studies that low fine particle analysis results indicate the absence of harmful materials. In contrast, while FRA’s general particle distribution provides a homogeneous structure with binding materials, CRA’s narrower particle distribution and low fine material content minimise the segregation effect within the concrete, contributing significantly to the production of high-strength concrete. The results also showed that recycled concrete aggregates have a significant effect on this particle distribution40.

Chemical analysis results of recycled concrete aggregate (RCA)

The chemical analysis results of Recycled Fine Aggregates (FRA) have been determined in accordance with standards. The Methylene Blue test, in accordance with the TS EN 933-9 standard, and the Sand Equivalent Test, in accordance with the TS EN 933-8+A1 standards, were applied to determine the harmful components in the fine materials. These tests are an important parameter in determining the chemical properties of fine-grained aggregates and reveal the quality of aggregates obtained under boundary conditions as a result of these experiments41,42. The Methylene Blue test results for FRA samples were obtained as 0.88, meeting the boundary conditions and yielding a very low chemical substance determination value. It was determined that recycled aggregates meeting these limit conditions did not contain harmful clay or undesirable organic matter in the concrete and did not have an effect that could reduce its strength properties. [43–45]. The Sand Equivalent Test analysis results of other FRA samples also determined that the amount of fine dust, organic matter, and other contaminants was quite low. According to TS EN 933-8, a minimum sand equivalent value of 40% or higher is generally accepted for fine aggregates, and the test results obtained a value of 79% in the aggregate test (Fig. 13). These results indicate that recycled fine aggregates meet the standards in chemical analysis results and do not pose any obstacle to the production of high-strength concrete, as determined by the analyses conducted.

The chemical analysis results for Fine RCA.

Three different tests were conducted to determine the presence of harmful substances in recycled coarse concrete aggregates (RCA). Firstly, the methylene blue test method was used to determine the aggregate quality by measuring the harmful clay content and contamination rate. The analyses revealed that the methylene blue value of RCA aggregates was 0.65, which was determined to be quite low compared to the limit value for harmful substance determination (Fig. 14). Similar studies have indicated that a low methylene blue value does not adversely affect the hydration of binding materials over long periods. [46,47].

The chemical analysis results for coarse RCA.

The second method commonly used in the chemical analysis of aggregates is the Sand Equivalent test method. Although these tests are generally used to determine harmful substances in fine aggregates, they are also used to determine harmful substances in coarse aggregates. The test results obtained for coarse aggregates yielded a Sand Equivalent value of 0.88. The obtained value indicates that coarse RCA aggregates are clean in terms of harmful and fine particles. Similar studies have also indicated that recycled aggregates, which do not contain harmful substances, have a positive effect on the strength and workability of concrete48. Additionally, the Total Sulphur Content was also determined to establish the chemical properties of the recycled aggregates. The analysis results showed that the Total Sulphur Content was 0.10; this value is well below the regulatory limits, which are generally accepted as less than 1.0% (and even below the stricter limit of less than 0.1% required for sensitive applications). The results indicate that recycled coarse aggregates meet the required quality standards and do not pose any obstacles to their use in concrete.

Mechanical and physical analysis results of RCA

The mechanical and physical properties of RCA play a critical role in its use within concrete. The wear percentage was calculated in the mechanical test results, and it was determined that RCA meets the boundary conditions. It has been determined that the wear percentage of RCA is 26.1% and that the wear percentage meets the boundary conditions in similar studies49. The low wear percentage contributes positively to the strength effect, particularly in screening and crushing processes, where the bond with the binding materials is high.

The physical properties of the RCA samples were also determined. The analysis results were found to be in accordance with the TS-1097-6 standard, with the density of coarse aggregates obtained as 2.38 g/cm³ and the water absorption rate determined as 8.1%. The density value in these samples is compatible with traditional natural aggregates and does not affect the unit weight of aggregates in concrete mix proportions. The water absorption rate is high compared to traditional aggregates, and similar values have been obtained in studies, with the results being consistent50,51. Experimental results have determined that aggregates with high water absorption rates have a negative effect on the strength properties of concrete, but that these strength losses can be reduced with additives. By considering these parameters in the prepared mix ratios and using chemical additives to reduce water content, high-strength concrete specimens were obtained.

Finally, the Flatness Index value of the recycled aggregates has been determined in accordance with the TS-EN 933-3 standard. The analysis results indicate that the Flatness Index value is 15.2%. These results indicate that the shape of the aggregates is not excessively flat or elongated. The limit conditions for concrete aggregates are between 15% and 20%, and these aggregates meet the limit conditions. Samples with high flatness index ratios can negatively affect workability, requiring more water and resulting in strength losses. The analysis results show that the proximity to the lower limit conditions requires attention to placement and compaction processes in concrete strength performance (Fig. 15).

Performance evaluation of concrete containing recycled railway sleeper aggregates

The concrete designs utilised waste-based concrete sleepers elements, which were reused as recycled aggregate, with the recycled aggregate percentages detailed in Table 4. The analyses determined the effect of recycled concrete aggregate (RCA) on concrete strength. Fig. 16 details the compressive strength values and strength classes of mixtures from M1 to M30 according to their RCA percentage. The results showed high strength properties, particularly in concrete mixtures with low to medium RCA content ranging from 25% to 50%. The M1 sample used 47.45% RCA and achieved a compressive strength value of 51.4 MPa. The M8 sample used 66.73% RCA and achieved compressive strength values of 53.8 MPa.

Scientists also stated in their study57,58 that concrete samples produced with traditional aggregates showed higher strength and density properties compared to RCA concrete samples. Concrete specimens, which generally have a heterogeneous structure59,60, exhibited low strength properties due to high water absorption and weak interface transition zones (ITZ). This result confirms the suitability of sleeper-derived RCA for structural or semi-structural concrete applications when used in appropriate ratios (such as 30%–50%) and with necessary optimizations. Therefore, this opens a promising avenue for the sustainable management of railway waste resulting from disasters such as earthquakes and the application of circular economy principles in high-performance concrete production.

Following 24 h of casting, the specimens were demolded and cured under standard water-curing conditions until the testing age. Compressive strength tests were performed after 28 days of curing, and the corresponding results are presented in Fig. 16. To improve the reproducibility and clarity of the study, the specimen dimensions, casting procedure, compaction method, curing conditions, and testing age have been explicitly described in the revised manuscript.
