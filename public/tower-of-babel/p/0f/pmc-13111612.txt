Chromosomal-level genome assembly of Ichthyurus bourgeoisi Gestro using PacBio HiFi and Hi-C sequencing
Authors: Yuxia Yang, Yiyang Zhen, Zheng Yang, Jiliang Wang, Jianxin Hua, Haoyu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIchthyurus bourgeoisi Gestro is a representative species of the tribe Ichthyurini within the beetle family Cantharidae. This tribe is particularly noteworthy because of its brachelytrous characteristics. However, the lack of high-quality genomic resources hinders our understanding of the evolution and ecological adaptions associated with this beetle group. In this study, we present a chromosome-level genome assembly for I. bourgeoisi constructed using a combination of PacBio HiFi and Hi-C sequencing data. The genome spans 664.72 Mb, with a scaffold N50 of 98.12 Mb, and is organized into seven pseudo-chromosomes, including a chromosome X validated through analyses of genome collinearity and sequencing depth. Repeat sequences account for 65.35% of the genome, and 13,386 protein-coding genes are identified. The high-quality genome assembly and annotation has been corroborated by multiple metrics, including genome size, reads mapping rate, and BUSCO completeness (98.6%). This comprehensive genomic resource provides a foundation for elucidating the ecological adaption of I. bourgeoisi and advancing our understanding of morphological evolution in Ichthyurini within Cantharidae.Subject terms: Genomics, Taxonomy

Background & Summary

The family Cantharidae, commonly known as soldier beetles, comprises 157 genera over 6,000 species across eight tribes of six subfamilies1–3. Among them, the tribe Ichthyurini is a mainly pantropical group containing six genera and approximately 300 species1,4–23. This tribe is particularly interesting because of their characteristic morphology, such as slender body, mixed yellow with black in coloration, strongly abbreviated elytra and well-developed while exposed hind wings, and fork-like terminal abdominal tergite3,5–7,10.

As a group of soft-bodied beetles characterized by a notably reduced degree of sclerotization24, the elytra of Ichthyurini exhibit relative thinness and softness, along with significant abbreviation. These characteristics appear to deviate from the typical body plan observed in most beetles. Elytra, which represent the conversion of forewings into hardened protective covers, are widely recognized as a key evolutionary innovation within Coleoptera due to their substantial enhancement of mechanical protection25. It is therefore intriguing to investigate how brachelytrous Ichthyurini beetles can successfully endure selection pressure despite lacking this critical attribute that offers numerous advantages25,26. To compensate for the diminished mechanical protection afforded by elytra, species within Ichthyurini have adopted various alternative defense strategies, such as chemical protection and mimicry or aposematic coloration. The distinctive coloration exhibited by Ichthyurini species, often featuring contrasting yellow/orange-to-dark elements alongside exposed metathoracic wings, probably creates an appearance reminiscent of hymenopterans like wasps, as observed in other beetle groups27. This resemblance may confer benefits in evading predators, or signal toxicity or unpalatability27,28. It is highly probable that these alternative defense strategies serve to offset the limited protective function provided by their elytra28. Another hypothesized factor contributing to this phenomenon is enhanced flexibility in the uncovered abdomen coupled with improved maneuverability, facilitating their predation or mating behaviors27.

Although there are many potential explanations for brachelytry29, the direct drivers and underlying mechanisms remain largely unexplored30,31. Most current knowledge of insect wing formation comes from studies in the dipteran model Drosophila melanogaster32–34, which have elucidated a complex network of interacting genes known as the Wing Gene Network (WGN). This framework has been extended to beetles through research on the red flour beetle (Tribolium castaneum) as a model, revealing that the elytron—despite its highly modified appearance—represents the default developmental state of the coleopteran wing. In Tribolium, the Hox gene Ultrabithorax (Ubx) acts specifically in the third thoracic segment (T3) to suppress elytral characteristics and promote hindwing formation35. Further studies have demonstrated how conserved wing genes have acquired divergent functions in beetles, contributing to elytron-specific traits such as exoskeletalization36 and distinct morphological shaping37. More recent RNA interference screens have identified novel regulators involved in pigmentation, sclerotization, sensory development, and venation—processes that collectively shape the unique architecture of the beetle elytron38.

Given that the factors driving elytral reduction likely vary among different beetle groups30,39, targeted studies are necessary to elucidate the evolutionary background and implications of this significant modification within Ichthyurini. To address these issues and establish a foundation for future genetic research, we have constructed the first chromosome-level reference genome for Ichthyurini, utilizing Ichthyurus bourgeoisi Gestro, 1892 (which is restricted to southern China) as a representative species. This was achieved by integrating high-fidelity (HiFi) long-read sequences with high-throughput chromatin conformation capture (Hi-C) data. Up to date, genome assemblies for 6 genera and 15 species of soldier beetles are available in the NCBI database, but none of these belongs to Ichthyurini. Here, the newly obtained high-quality reference genome is expected to provide valuable genetic resources that facilitate a deeper understanding of the morphological characteristics and adaptive mechanisms of Ichthyurini while serving as an important reference for further phylogenetic and evolutionary studies with the family Cantharidae.

Methods

Samples collection

Male and female samples of I. bourgeoisi were collected in June 2025, from Wudaoxia National Nature reserve, Xiangyang, Hubei Province, China (31.74°N, 111.21°E). The specimens were morphologically identified and meticulously cleaned under a stereomicroscope to remove surface contaminants, thereby minimizing potential interference with downstream molecular experiments. Subsequently, samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent transcriptomic and genomic analyses. All sequencing was conducted at Berry Genomics (Beijing, China) and BenaGen (Wuhan, China).

Library construction and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from a single male individual of I. bourgeoisi using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method, followed by purification with a Grandomics Genomic kit according to the manufacturer’s standard protocol. The purified DNA was subsequently used for sequencing to generate the reference genome.

SMRTbell library with a 20 kb insert size was prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Pacific Biosciences #PN 101-853-100, CA, USA) for HIFI sequencing. This library was sequenced on the PacBio Sequel Revio sequencing platform at Berry (Beijing, China).

The DNBSEQ-T7 library (for second-generation genome sequencing, WGS) was constructed using the Agencourt AMPure XP-Medium Kit, with an insert size of 200–400 bp. The sequencing was performed on the DNBSEQ-T7 (BGI) platform at Berry (Beijing, China).

The ONT PromethION library (for third-generation transcriptome sequencing, extracted using the (DP441) RNA prep Pure Plant Plus Kit) was generated using the SQK-PCS109 + SQKPBK004 kit at BenaGen (Wuhan, China). The RNA-sr library (for second-generation transcriptome sequencing, extracted using the TRIzolTM Reagent Kit) was constructed using the VAHTS mRNA-seq v2 Library Prep Kit at Berry (Beijing, China).

The Hi-C library was constructed at Berry (Beijing, China), which involved several steps: DNA cross-linking with formaldehyde, chromatin digestion using the restriction enzyme MboI, end repair, DNA cyclization, and DNA purification.

The different sequencing platforms of various libraries, the output quantity of the original sequence and the sequencing depth are detailed in Table 1. The sequencing data were generated, comprising approximately 44.80 Gb (67.40×) of BGI short reads, 64.59 Gb (97.17×) of PacBio CLR reads, 78.42 Gb (117.97×) of Hi-C data, as well as 22.57 Gb of RNA-sr data and 9.93 Gb RNA-ONT data (Table 1). The PacBio reads achieved a scaffold N50 of 17.11 kb, with an average length of 17.28 kb. Meanwhile, the RNA-ONT achieved a scaffold N50 of 1.57 kb, with an average length of 1.36 kb.

Genome survey

The primary purpose of the genome survey analysis is to estimate the genome size, heterozygosity, and the proportion of repetitive sequences. This information is essential for selecting appropriate assembly tools and adjusting relevant parameters in subsequent steps. To ensure high quality data, the obtained BGI second-generation data underwent quality control and trimming using fastp v0.23.440 with the following parameters (‘-q 20 -D -g -x -u 10 -5 -r -c’): retaining sites with base quality scores no less than 20 (>Q20), removing duplicate sequences (-D), trimming poly-G/X ends (-g -x), the proportion of unqualified bases not exceeding 10% (-u 10), and correcting bases using overlapping reads (-c).

The survey draws inferences based on the distribution of k-mer frequencies. The evaluation of k-mer frequencies is conducted using khist.sh (a component of BBTools v39.0141), while simultaneously assessing the genome with a sequence length set to 21 k-mers. Additionally, GenomeScope v2.042 is employed for genome feature analysis, with the maximum k-mer coverage cutoffs established at 10,000, and the parameters as ‘-k 21 -p 2 -m 10000’. The results are shown in Table 2 and Fig. 1.

Chromosomal-level genome assembly

High-quality HiFi reads were generated using pbccs v6.4.0 (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/ccs), retaining sequences with a base quality above Q20. The initial assembly was performed using Hifiasm v0.24.043 with default parameters. Low-depth sequences are likely to be contaminants or erroneous sequences, so the Hifiasm assembly excluded those with a depth lower than 1/10 of the sequencing depth (5X). Given the extremely high base quality of the assembly, evidenced by QV values exceeding 60 (as demonstrated in subsequent Merqury analysis of the genome), no conventional error correction step (polishing) was deemed necessary. Both Merqury analysis and BUSCO completeness assessment indicated an absence of significant heterozygosity within the assembly, so no conventional procedure for removing heterozygosity was undertaken.

Chromosome scaffolding was conducted using Hi-C data in conjunction with the YAHS v1.244 pipeline. Initially, quality control on the Hi-C data was performed using chromap v0.2.645 (i.e., read aligning, duplicate removal, and Hi-C contact extraction). Subsequently, two rounds of chromosome mounting were executed using the default parameters provided by YAHS v1.2. Following the first round of mounting, manual correction were made to address errors (i.e., misjoins, translocations, and inversions) through Juicebox v1.11.0846 before proceeding to a final round of mounting. The sequencing depth of each pseudo-chromosome was evaluated using SAMtools v1.1047, with the input alignment BAM files generated via minimap2 based on HiFi (‘-ax map-hifi’) or second-generation WGS (‘-ax sr’) reads. The determination of sex chromosome was based on comparative analysis of sequencing depth across chromosomes, with the theoretical depth of the male sex chromosome being half that of the autosomes. As shown in Table 4, the sequencing depth of chromosome 7 was significantly lower than that of the other chromosomes, indicating its identification as the X chromosome. Furthermore, as depicted in the Hi-C mounting heatmap presented in Fig. 2, the quality of chromosome mounting achieved an exceptionally high standard, culminating in seven chromosome-level assemblies.

Genome integrity was evaluated using BUSCO v5.7.148, referencing the insecta_odb10 database (n = 1,367 single-copy genes). This tool employs insect near-universal single-copy orthologous genes (USCOs) to predict the completeness of existing genes within the genome. Meanwhile, raw sequences of genome (the second-generation) and transcriptome (both the second and third generations) were mapped back to the genome assembly to examine the utilization rate of raw data as well as the completeness of the assembly. The mapping tool used was Minimap2, with mapping rate calculated by SAMtools. Potential contamination in the assembly was investigated using MMseq. 2 v1349 through a blastn-like search, utilizing NCBI nt and UniVec as comparison databases. Additionally, single-base quality scores (QV values) and k-mer spectra of the genome were analyzed with merqury v1.350.

The detailed metrics of the final genome assembly are shown in Table 3. The total genome size is 664.72 Mb, comprising 28 scaffolds and 44 contigs. The longest scaffold and contig measure 150.585 Mb and 95.125 Mb, respectively. The N50 lengths for scaffold and contig are recorded at 98.117 Mb and 43.916 Mb, respectively. The GC content is 34.39%. These statistics reveal an extremely high level of continuity within the assembly. Seven pseudo-chromosomes account for 661.99 Mb in total, achieving a mapping rate of 99.59%. Detailed information regarding the length, sequencing depth, and QV value for each chromosome can be found in Table 4. The BUSCO assessment indicates that the integrity of the genome assembly is at an impressive rate of 98.6%, with only a minimal presence (1.4%) of multi-copy BUSCOs, suggesting negligible redundancy within the dataset. Re-mapping rates from various sequencing approaches, including second-generation survey (99.24%), second-generation RNA (99.63%), third-generation RNA (99.72%), and third-generation HiFi data (99.75%), all exceed the threshold of 99%, indicating extremely high sequencing quality throughout. Furthermore, the QV values for bases across each chromosome mostly exceed a score of 60, corresponding to an error rate equal to or less than of 1e-6. Analysis of k-mer spectra reveals no significant heterozygosity, and any intermediate peaks observed are attributed to sex chromosome (Fig. 1b). Collectively, these metrics indicate that this genome assembly has achieved an extremely high standard concerning both continuity and integrity.

Repeat sequence annotation

First, a de novo repeat library was constructed utilizing RepeatModeler v2.0.551 with the additional LTR search option (‘-LTRStruct’). This library was merged with the Dfam 3.852 and RepBase-2018102653 databases to create a custom repeat database. Subsequently, repeat sequences were predicted using RepeatMasker v4.1.554 based on the custom repeat database. The results indicate that there are 1,599,218 repeat sequences totaling 434,405,063 bp, accounting for 65.35% of the total genome, a typical high ratio of repetitive structure. The six types of repetitive sequences with the highest proportions are: Unknown (25.30%), DNA (19.78%), LINE (11.49%), LTRs (4.57%), Rolling-circles (2.55%), and SINEs (0.82%). As illustrated in Fig. 3, there are three indicators of transposon expansion within the genome as follows. Ancient expansion is primarily attributed to LINE repeats, evidenced by a peak at approximately 40% on the right side of the graph. Subsequent expansion is represented by a peak at around 12%, which arises from the combined contributions of Unknown and DNA transposons. Recent expansion predominantly involves DNA, LTR and LINE repeats collectively contributing to the observed peak on the left side.

Non-coding RNA annotation

rRNA, snRNA and miRNA were annotated utilizing Infernal v1.1.555 based on the Rfam database. tRNA were identified using tRNAscan-SE v2.0.1256 and low-confidence tRNAs were subsequently filtered by the built-in ‘EukHighConfidenceFilter’ script. A total of 958 non-coding RNAs were identified, including 219 rRNAs, 63 miRNAs, 78 snRNAs, 330 tRNAs, 8 ribozymes, and 2 lncRNAs. The snRNAs category includes 55 spliceosomal RNAs (U1, U2, U4, U5, U6, U11), along with 2 minor spliceosomal RNAs (U4atac, U6atac), as well as 13 C/D box snoRNAs, and 5 HACA-box snoRNAs.

Protein-coding gene structure prediction

The protein-coding genes were identified utilizing the MAKER v3.01.0457, which integrated ab initio gene prediction, gene structure prediction by transcriptome data, and alignment with homologous proteins.

For ab initio gene prediction, it was conducted using BRAKER v3.0.658 and GeMoMa v1.959, with results merged as input file for MAKER to expand the potential range of candidate coding genes. Transcriptome alignments were generated by comparing RNA-sr data to the genome, using minimap2 to produce BAM alignment files (‘-x splice:sr’). Augustus v3.4.060 and GeneMark-ETP61 were trained using RNA sequence alignments and reference proteins from the OrthoDB12v1 database62. Long-read transcriptome reads were aligned with minimap2 with the ‘-x splice’ option, followed by genome-guide assembly of RNA-sr and RNA-ONT data using StringTie v3.0.163. Predictions based on protein homology and conserved intron position were carried out using GeMoMa, applying parameters “GeMoMa.c = 0.4 GeMoMa.m = 82000”, with protein sequences from five species which have high-quality genomes with robust assembly and annotation obtained from NCBI, including Drosophila melanogaster (Diptera), Chrysoperla carnea (Neuroptera), as well as three Coleoptera species: Tribolium castaneum, Onthophagus taurus, and Dalotia coriaria.

A total of 13,386 protein-coding genes were identified, with an average gene length of 13,668.7 bp (Table 5). On average, each gene comprises an average of 5.8 exons, 4.7 introns, and 5.5 coding sequences (CDS), with an average exon length of 389.5 bp. 2,595.5 bp, and 301.2 bp, respectively. BUSCO analysis of the predicted protein-coding gene sequences indicated a completeness score of 98.5% (n = 1,367). This result is close to the 98.6% completeness estimated from the genome (Table 3), demonstrating high annotation quality for the identified protein-coding genes.

Protein-coding gene function predication

The annotation for protein-coding gene functions was performed using multiple tools. Diamond v2.1.7.16164 was used to search the UniProtKB v202503 (SwissProt + TrEMBL) database (‘--very-sensitive e 1e-5’). InterPro 5.70–102.065 was used to query the Pfam66 database. EggNOG-mapper v2.1.1267 was employed to search the eggNOG v5.0.268 database. Finally, the results obtained from the aforementioned methods were integrated to derive the final gene function predictions, typically presented in gff file format.

A total of 12,658 genes (94.56%) were aligned in the UniProtKB database. InterPro successfully identified protein domains in 11,124 protein-coding genes. In comparison, the eggONT annotation results assigned 12,274 genes, 11,600 COG categories, 9,176 GO items, 8,275 KEGG ko terms, 5,173 KEGG pathways, and 3,075 Enzyme Codes. Furthermore, both InterPro and eggNOG-mapper collaboratively assigned GO terms to 10,429 genes and identified KEGG pathway entries for 5,173 genes (Table 5). Additionally, TBTools-II v2.33069 was used to create a visual graphic for genomic characteristics (Fig. 4). Notably, the abundance of retrotransposons (SINE/LINE/LTR) appears to be elevated at both ends of the chromosome.

Technical Validation

The chromosome-level genome of the I. bourgeoisi assembly was 664.72 Mb with a scaffold N50 length of 98.12 Mb. BUSCO assessment demonstrated that that more than 98% of BUSCO genes were identified in the genome assembly (Table 3), indicating a high level of completeness of the I. bourgeoisi genome assembly. Furthermore, the assembly accuracy was measured by calculating the re-mapping rates for various sequencing approaches (Table 3), including second-generation survey (99.24%), second-generation RNA (99.63%), third-generation RNA (99.72%), and third-generation HiFi data (99.75%), all of which exceed the threshold of 99%, indicating extremely high sequencing quality throughout. Moreover, the Hi-C heatmap showed obvious grouping, and the intensity of the interaction was higher than that of the off-diagonal position, indicating a high degree of interaction between neighboring sequences in the chromosomal results of the Hi-C assembly, while the interaction signals between non-adjacent sequences were weak, consistent with the principle of Hi-C auxiliary assembly, indicating better genomic anchoring effect. The genome of I. bourgeoisi was divided into 7 groups, indicating 7 pseudo-chromosomes (Fig. 2). Collectively, these evaluations demonstrate that the chromosome-level genome of I. bourgeoisi was of high quality and reliability.
