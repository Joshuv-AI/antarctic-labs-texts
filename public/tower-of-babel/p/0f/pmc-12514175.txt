The role of biogenic aluminium oxide nanoparticles (Al2O3 NPs) synthesised from Solanum virginianum L in combating microbial vaginal pathogens
Authors: Mohammad Zubair, Yazeed Albalawi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrobial vaginosis results from an elevation in biofilm-forming vaginal bacteria. They provide a considerable clinical concern due to the rising global antibiotic resistance. This work investigated the antibacterial and anti-biofilm characteristics of Al₂O₃ NPs synthesised from Solanum virginianum L. Fourier. The characterisation techniques Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy (UV–Vis), and Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) validated the synthesis and structural integrity of the nanoparticles. Marked antibacterial and antifungal efficacy was noted against 108 isolates of various microorganisms (G. vaginalis, S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and C. albicans). Al₂O₃ displayed minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) ranging from 1000 to 3000 µg/mL and MBC at 2000–5000 mg/mL against study strains. Al₂O₃ signicantly inhibited biofilm formation by 50%-75%, cell surface hydrophobicity by 50–60%, outer membrane disruption by 60%-70% and preform biofilm disruption by 50%-80% in the test strains at respective 0.5xMIC. Elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation in the Al₂O₃ -treated G. vaginalis, S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and C. albicans could be the possible cause of cell death leading to biofilm inhibition. The research identified a significant correlation between biofilm positive and age, exhibiting an odds ratio of 6.33 and a risk ratio of 2.28. Vulvar pruritus and oedema were correlated with biofilm positive, although secretion colour exhibited no significant relationships. These results may inform clinical care and therapeutic approaches. The findings indicate that biogenic Al₂O₃ NPsderived from S. virginianum offer an environmentally sustainable alternative to traditional antimicrobials for vaginal washing in the treatment of biofilm-mediated vaginal infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19268-0.Keywords: Al2O3 nanoparticles, S. virginianum, Vaginosis, G. vaginalis, S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, C. albicansSubject terms: Microbiology, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Microbial vaginal infections, particularly bacterial vaginosis (BV) and candidal vaginosis, are among the most common gynecological disorders affecting women worldwide. These infections arise from a disruption in the vaginal microbial ecosystem, characterized by a decline in protective Lactobacillus species and an overgrowth of anaerobic pathogens such as Gardnerella vaginalis (Arroyo-Moreno et al., 2022)1. This microbial imbalance, or dysbiosis, leads to clinical symptoms including abnormal vaginal discharge, unpleasant odor, and vulvar discomfort, significantly impacting women’s health and quality of life2. The nosocomial organisms are a major threat to biofilm infection in admitted patients with BV3–5. These organisms can adhere to both biotic and abiotic surfaces, resulting in biofilm formation. Despite the availability of antibiotic treatments such as metronidazole and clindamycin, the increasing prevalence of antibiotic resistance and frequent relapses highlight the limitations of current therapeutic approaches7. This underscores the urgent need for innovative strategies to effectively manage and prevent these infections.

One of the major challenges in treating BV is the high rate of recurrence following standard antibiotic therapy. Many women experience a relapse within months of initial treatment, indicating that conventional antibiotics often fail to address the underlying causes of dysbiosis7. A healthy vaginal microbiome, dominated by Lactobacillus species, plays a crucial role in maintaining vaginal health by producing lactic acid, which lowers the vaginal pH and inhibits the growth of pathogenic microorganisms. Disruptions in this microbial balance, often characterized by a reduction in lactic acid-producing bacteria and an increase in anaerobic bacteria such as G. vaginalis, Atopobium vaginae, and Mobiluncus spp., can lead to recurrent infections and long-term complications5. Therefore, interventions that not only eliminate the current infection but also restore and sustain a healthy vaginal microbiome are essential for effective management of BV.

The persistence of BV and its associated complications are further exacerbated by the formation of microbial biofilms, which are structured communities of bacteria encased in a self-produced extracellular polymeric matrix. These biofilms confer increased resistance to antibiotics by hindering drug penetration and facilitating horizontal gene transfer among bacterial cells8. While biofilms are not inherently harmful and can play a role in maintaining healthy bodily functions, their presence in infections or on medical devices can lead to chronic, difficult-to-treat conditions that often require prolonged antibiotic regimens or surgical intervention9,10. In the context of BV, biofilms formed by pathogens such as G. vaginalis protect resident bacteria from antibiotic action, contributing to treatment failure and recurrence. Moreover, conventional treatments can disrupt the native vaginal flora, predisposing patients to secondary infections and long-term complications. Disturbances in the vaginal microbiota and host immune interactions can also increase susceptibility to sexually transmitted infections and adverse pregnancy outcomes11. Therefore, there is a critical need for interventions that selectively target pathogenic bacteria without disrupting the commensal flora, thereby preserving vaginal health5.

In recent years, nanotechnology has emerged as a promising field for developing targeted drug delivery systems and antimicrobial agents to combat various infectious diseases, including those involving biofilms8. Nanoparticles, due to their small size and unique physicochemical properties, can interact with biological systems at the cellular and molecular level, offering targeted drug delivery and enhanced antimicrobial efficacy4,12. Among these, biogenic nanoparticles synthesized using natural plant extracts have gained attention for their biocompatibility, stability, and reduced toxicity compared to chemically synthesized nanoparticles9. These nanoparticles can disrupt bacterial membranes, generate reactive oxygen species, and interfere with essential metabolic pathways, providing a multifaceted approach to antimicrobial therapy. Furthermore, the ability of nanoparticles to deliver drugs directly to bacterial biofilms, protect antimicrobial agents from degradation, and disrupt biofilm structure represents a significant advancement in antimicrobial therapy13.

S. virginianum L., also known as Surattense nightshade, is a medicinal plant native to the Asir and Hejaz regions of Saudi Arabia. It has been traditionally used for its antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and wound-healing properties14,15. The plant’s bioactive compounds, including alkaloids, flavonoids, and terpenoids, are thought to contribute to its therapeutic efficacy. These compounds can also mediate the reduction and stabilization of metal ions into nanoparticles, making S. virginianum L. a promising candidate for the green synthesis of biogenic nanoparticles3,12. The use of plant extracts in nanoparticle synthesis not only enhances the biocompatibility and stability of the nanoparticles but also reduces the environmental impact of the synthesis process16,17. Aluminium oxide nanoparticles (Al₂O₃ NPs) have been widely studied for their antimicrobial properties, particularly against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria17. The high surface area and reactivity of Al₂O₃ NPs enable them to interact with bacterial cells and disrupt their membranes, leading to cell death18. Additionally, Al₂O₃ NPs can generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) that damage bacterial DNA and proteins, further enhancing their antimicrobial efficacy19. In the context of biofilms, Al₂O₃ NPs can penetrate the extracellular matrix and deliver antimicrobial agents directly to the bacterial cells, thereby overcoming the resistance mechanisms associated with biofilms20.

By leveraging the unique properties of nanoparticles, such as their high surface area and reactivity, this research aims to develop a sustainable and effective alternative to conventional antibiotics. This research aims to develop an alternative of antimicrobial agents Owing to the pharmaceutical importance of S. virginianum, we have performed synthesis of Al2O3 NPs using the extract of S. virginianum as a stabilizing agent to assess its potential in reducing the biofilm bacterial and fungal isolated from vagina.

Materials and methods

Patients’ selection

Adult Patients aged 18–40 years presenting to University Clinic, University of Tabuk, Saudi Arabia, with a culture-confirmed, biofilm-forming infection due to a drug-resistant pathogen, from vaginal swab specimens who have not received systemic antibiotics in the 14 days before collection, who can provide informed consent, and who are not immunocompromised, pregnant or breastfeeding, and allergic to aluminum compounds, were included. Healthy volunteers without any active infection, no antibiotic use in the past 14 days, no history of chronic illness or immunosuppressive therapy were recruited as controls. This study received clearance from the Local Research Ethics Committee (LREC) of the University of Tabuk, approval number UT-517-334-2025, in accordance with the norms of the National Committee of Bioethics (NCBE), Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. Informed permission was acquired from each participant in the study. All techniques employed in this study were conducted in compliance with the guidelines and rules sanctioned by the LREC. An informed consent was obtained from study participants.

S. Virginianum sample collections

This study was performed from February to May 2025 in the Faculty of Medicine, Department of Medical Microbiology, University of Tabuk, Saudi Arabia. Specimens of S. virginianum ripe fruit, commonly known as Roselle and locally referred to in Arabic as biadhinjan (الباذنجان), were collected from the local market of Arar region of Saudi Arabia (GPS site location 30.880843, 41.161914). The collecting approach was meticulously executed to guarantee the procurement of high-quality and representative samples for the study. The specimen was identified by Prof Nasir M Khan of Faculty of Applied, University of Tabuk, and subsequently preserved at the ultra-low temperature storage facility of the Molecular Microbiology and Infectious Disease Research Unit, University of Tabuk, KSA.

Green synthesis of Al2O3 nanoparticles from S. virginianum

Initially, fresh fruits of S. virginianum were placed in sterile containers. Dust and other particles were removed through extensive washing with double-distilled water. Following washing, they were dried in a shaded room. A mortar and pestle were then used to grind the fruits. Aluminium nitrate (purity 99.5%) used for nanoparticle synthesis was procured from Fisher Scientific. The extract was filtered to obtain a clear filtrate, which was subsequently used for nanoparticle synthesis. In a container, 3 mM Aluminium nitrate was combined with 50 mL of filtrate and heated to 100 °C for half an hour. Upon reaction, the suspension turned from green to brown, confirming the formation of aluminium oxide nanoparticles (Al₂O₃ NPs). The resulting greenish-brown solid was recovered by evaporation and subsequently dried at 120 °C to enhance its structural integrity. A schematic diagram of the Al₂O₃ NPs preparation procedure is shown in Fig. 1. The synthesis of Al2O3 nanoparticles involved optimisation of pH, calcination temperature, and duration to ensure high purity and consistent nanoparticle size. Preliminary trials revealed that maintaining a pH of 7.0 and calcination at 120 °C for 2 h yielded nanoparticles with uniform morphology and enhanced antimicrobial activity. Adjustments to reaction time and temperature were based on visual and spectral analyses21,22.

Characterisation of Al2O3 NPs

The biosynthesised Al₂O₃ NPs(NPs) made from S. virginianum were characterised by spectroscopic and microscopic techniques to evaluate their chemical, crystallographic, and morphological properties. FTIR spectra were recorded on a Thermo Fisher Nicolet FTIR spectrometer over a range of 4,000–400 cm⁻¹ at 1 cm⁻¹ resolution. Absorption bands corresponding to Al–O vibrations and residual phytoconstituent functional groups were identified, confirming both oxide formation and capping by bioorganic compounds24. XRD patterns were collected on a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) over 2θ = 20°–80° at 40 kV/40 mA. SEM micrographs were used to assess particle morphology and size distribution. Images were analysed using ImageJ, which showed uniform morphology. The mean particle diameter aligned with the XRD-derived crystallite size aggregation26. The UV–Vis absorption spectrum of the Al₂O₃ NPs was recorded on a Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer over the 200–800 nm range. Before measurement, the nanoparticles were dispersed in distilled water, and the instrument baseline was corrected using a distilled water blank. EDX was conducted with SEM to quantify the elemental composition of the Al₂O₃ nanoparticles. This approach yielded both qualitative and quantitative information on the elements present, enhancing our understanding of the sample’s chemical profile and its potential environmental interactions. The combination of EDAX and SEM provided a detailed view of surface morphology and elemental distribution, which is important for modifying nanoparticle properties for diverse applications27.

Preparation of Al2O3 NPs water suspension

To prepare the aqueous suspension, 100 mg of Al₂O₃ powder was added to 10 mL of sterile Milli-Q water and vigorously shaken. The mixture was then sonicated (100 W, 40 kHz) for 30 min to disrupt nanoparticle agglomerates and ensure a stable, uniform dispersion prior to use.

Antimicrobial, anti-ESBL, anti-MBL and anti-MRSA studies

Collection of bacterial samples and antibiotic sensitivity

108 clinical isolates were obtained from 40 female patients with vaginal infections. This is an exploratory study aimed at investigating the antimicrobial and antibiofilm properties of biogenic Al₂O₃ NPs synthesized from S. virginianum L. in the context of drug-resistant, biofilm-forming vaginal infections. Given the exploratory nature of the research, the sample size was determined based on practical considerations, including the availability of eligible participants, resource constraints, and the need to generate preliminary data that could inform future, more extensive studies. These strains, which had been previously characterized and confirmed by the hospital laboratoy, were selected based on their demonstrated resistance to at least three classes of antimicrobial agents. Susceptibility testing was conducted using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method on Mueller–Hinton agar, following the guidelines established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute28 and Zubair et al.10. The panel of antibiotics and antifungal agents tested is used are Amikacin, Aztreonam, Imipenem, Piperacillin, Ceftriaxone, Ciprofloxacin, Levofloxacin, Norfloxacin, Ampicillin, Azithromycin, Ofloxacin, Erythromycin, penicillin, metronidazole, chloremphinecol, clindamycin, clotrimazole, fluconazole, voriconazole, itaconazole, and ketoconazole. Zones of inhibition were measured and interpreted in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (Hi-Media Labs, Mumbai, India). Fungal isolates were macro and microscopically identified based on colony morphology on media. Gram’s staining and germ tube test was performed on mucoid yeast like growth and followed by urease29. In a suspected candida sp with budding yeast like positive gram reaction, we perform the germ tube test. C. dubliniensis along with C. albicans gives GTT positive, we use CHROM agar (HiMedia, Mumbai, India) differentiate morphologically by appearance of growth type and colour. Manufacturers instruction guideline were used to identify Candida species: Candida albicans (Green)29.

Double disk synergy test

Phenotypic confirmation of ESβL producers was performed by placing a 30 µg cefotaxime disk and a 30/10 µg cefotaxime–clavulanic acid disk 20 mm apart (on a Mueller–Hinton agar plate, as per CLSI recommendations28,30. After overnight (18 h) incubation at 37 °C, an increase of ≥ 5 mm in the inhibition zone around the combination disk compared to the cefotaxime disk alone was considered indicative of ESβL production. Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 (non-ESβL producer) and Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700603 (ESβL producer) served as quality control strains.

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) test

A sterile swab was dipped in an S. aureus suspension adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard and streaked evenly onto Mueller–Hinton agar plates. 1 µg of oxacillin discs were applied to the agar surface. Plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight. Isolates exhibiting an inhibition zone of ≤ 14 mm were classified as oxacillin-resistant (MRSA). S. aureus ATCC 25,923 was used as the quality-control strain31.

Detection of MβL activity test

The E-test strip method was used to detect MβL production32. Bacterial suspensions were standardised to 0.5 McFarland in Mueller–Hinton broth, then uniformly swabbed on Mueller–Hinton agar plates. Two E-test strips (one containing imipenem alone and one combining imipenem with EDTA) were placed on the inoculated agar. A reduction of at least 8-fold in MIC with the EDTA-containing strip, relative to the Imipenem-only strip, was indicative of MBL production. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was read at the point where the inhibition ellipse intersected the strip for each test.

MIC and MBC determination of Al2O3 NPs

Overnight cultures of each bacterial strain were prepared on Mueller–Hinton agar (MHA) at 35 °C. To determine the MIC of Al₂O₃ NPs, the agar-dilution method was used. Serial two-fold dilutions of Al₂O₃ NPs (ranging from 3,500 to 100 µg/mL) were incorporated into MHA plates. Each plate was then inoculated with approximately 2 × 10⁸ CFU/mL of bacteria and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The MIC was defined as the lowest nanoparticle concentration that completely inhibited visible bacterial growth. Each MIC determination was performed in triplicate for reproducibility. After MIC testing, 25 µL aliquots from all nanoparticle-containing tubes showed no visible growth onto fresh, nanoparticle-free MHA plates. After incubating these plates at 37 °C for 24 h, the MBC was recorded as the lowest Al₂O₃ NP concentration that achieved a 100% kill rate as indicated by the absence of colony formation33.

Time-dependent growth Inhibition assay

To perform the assay using a microplate method, fresh colonies grown on MHA plates were used to inoculate 50 mL of LB broth and incubated until reaching an optical density (OD600) of 0.1, corresponding to approximately 108 CFU/mL described elsewhere with slight modifications4,8. The culture was then diluted to achieve a concentration of 2 × 108 CFU/mL. Subsequently, 100 µL aliquots of this bacterial suspension were transferred into each well of a 96-well microplate containing 100 µL of LB broth with Al₂O₃ NP at various concentrations (0, 500, 1000, 1500, 2000, 2500, 3000, or 3500 µg/mL). Each condition was performed in triplicate, and wells containing bacteria without nanoparticles served as controls. The plates were incubated at 37 °C with shaking conditions, and bacterial growth was monitored by measuring OD600 every two hours up to 18 h using a microplate reader.

Anti-biofilm study

Biofilm formation

Biofilm formation was quantified as previously described8 using 96-well with slight modifications using flat-bottom microplates. Assays were performed in triplicate, and the mean absorbance at 590 nm was calculated. Based on OD₅₉₀ values, biofilm producers were classified as follows: No biofilm: OD ≤ ODc, Weak biofilm: ODc < OD ≤ 2 × ODc, Moderate biofilm: 2× ODc < OD ≤ 4 × ODc, Strong biofilm: OD > 4 × ODc.

Effect of Al2O3-NPs on the colony-forming unit (CFU)

To determine the impact of Al₂O₃ NP sub-inhibitory concentrations on bacterial growth, cells were inoculated into 100 mL Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB). They were then exposed, in triplicates, to the following concentrations: MIC, 0.5 × MIC, and 0.25 × MIC. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C, and growth was monitored by measuring OD₆₀₀ at 2-hour intervals over an 18-hour period.

Effect of Al2O3 NPs on biofilm formation

The biofilm-forming ability of study isolates was assessed using a colorimetric microtiter plate assay with crystal violet staining. For biofilm formation, bacterial cultures were grown in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth supplemented with 1% glucose to enhance adherence. The inoculum was prepared by adjusting overnight bacterial cultures to an OD600 of 0.1, followed by a 1:100 dilution in fresh medium. A 200 µL aliquot of the diluted culture was added to each well of a 96-well flat-bottom microtiter plate. For negative controls, the wells contained only BHI with 1% glucose without bacterial inoculation. The plates were incubated under static conditions at 35–37 °C for 24–30 h. After incubation, the wells were gently washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.2) to remove non-adherent cells. The remaining attached biofilm was fixed using methanol (150 µL/well) for 15 min and air-dried at room temperature. The biofilms were then stained with 150 µL of 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min, followed by three washes with PBS to remove excess dye. To quantify biofilm formation, the retained stain was solubilized using 150 µL of 95% ethanol, and the plate was incubated for 30 min at room temperature without shaking. The optical density (OD) was measured at 590 nm using a microtiter plate reader. The cut-off OD (ODc) was calculated as the mean OD of the negative control wells + (3 × standard deviation of the negative control OD values)8.

Effect of Al2O3-NPs on exopolysaccharides (EPS)

Cell-free supernatants were harvested from bacterial cultures exposed to Al₂O₃ NPs at MIC, 0.5×MIC, and 0.25×MIC, as well as from untreated controls. To precipitate EPS, each supernatant was combined with chilled ethanol in a 3:1 ratio and incubated at 4 °C for 16–18 h. The resulting EPS was quantified via the Dubois method, using a glucose standard curve for calibration34. Untreated cultures were used as negative controls, whereas samples treated with DNase I (10 µg/mL) were utilized as positive controls to benchmark EPS reduction by the nanoparticles.

Effect of Al2O3-NPs on cell surface hydrophobicity (CSH)

Cell surface hydrophobicity was assessed by the Microbial Adhesion to Hydrocarbons (MATH) assay. Overnight bacterial cultures (1 mL) were mixed with 100 µL xylene and the appropriate sub-inhibitory concentrations of nanoparticles. Untreated cultures acted as controls. After vigorous vortexing for 2 min, samples were left at room temperature for 10 min to allow phase separation. The absorbance of the aqueous layer was then measured at 530 nm. Effect of Al2O3-NPs on disruption of preformed biofilm

Biofilm formation was measured in 96-well plates. Test strains were grown for 24 h at 37 °C, then wells were washed twice with PBS to remove free-floating cells. Fresh medium, with or without 0.5× MIC of nanoparticles, was added, and plates were incubated statically for another 24 h at 37 °C. After a second wash to remove non-adherent cells, adherent cells, representing biofilm, were stained with 0.1% crystal violet. The amount of biofilm was quantified by measuring absorbance at 585 nm, following de Sousa et al.36 and Schlichter Kadosh et al.37.

Statistical analysis

The experiments were conducted in triplicate unless otherwise indicated. The means + standard deviation of all the obtained values is presented using sigma plot 16. A comparison of the means of various sets of data was carried out through a t test.

Results

In this study, we evaluated the basic biochemical and metabolic parameters in individuals with microbial vaginal infections (n = 40) compared to healthy controls (n = 20). The details of the basic biochemicals and metabolic parameters were mentioned in supplementary file of results and supplementary Table 1. The distribution of microbial isolates by infection type is shown in Table 1. The analysis of isolates from vaginal infections revealed distinct patterns of microbial growth depending on the oxygen requirement. Among aerobic isolates (31.4%), a total of 2 (5.8%) were monomicrobial, two species by 19 (55.8%), and 23(67.6%) were polymicrobial, indicating a predominance of multispecies infections in the aerobic environment. These figures translate to a total of 23 (67.6%) exhibiting polymicrobial growth when considering all aerobic isolates. In contrast, anaerobic isolates showed a higher proportion of polymicrobial growth, with 31 (51.6%) out of 60 total isolates displaying multiple species, including 2 (3.3%) monomicrobial and 26 (43.3%) involving two species. Monomicrobial anaerobic infections were less common, accounting for only 4 (3.3%) isolates. Fungal isolates predominantly demonstrated mixed infections with aerobic and anaerobic bacteria, with 64.2% with single bacteria and 55.5% with more than two bacteria. Overall, two and polymicrobial infections accounted for 96.2% of all isolates across all groups, reflecting a significant trend towards mixed-species colonization or infection in vaginal samples. These findings highlight the complex microbial ecology of vaginal infections, with polymicrobial communities being especially prevalent in anaerobic and fungal infections compared to aerobic groups.

The infrared (IR) spectrum of biogenic Al₂O₃ NPs synthesised from S. virginianum reveals important insights into its molecular structure and functional properties (Fig. 2 A). The FTIR spectrum of Al₂O₃ shows diagnostic absorption features that are due to different vibrational modes of its constituent functional groups. The broadband observed between 3200 and 3600 cm⁻¹ is assigned to O–H stretching vibrations arising from surface-bound hydroxyl species or adsorbed water. Such hydroxyl functionalities are commonly observed in metal oxides and may influence the reactivity of aluminium oxide in biological systems. The absence of strong peaks in the carbonyl region (around 1700 cm⁻¹) indicates a lack of carbonyl functional groups, which is consistent with the inorganic nature of the oxide. Furthermore, peaks in the range of 1000–1300 cm⁻¹ can indicate potential C-O stretching from organic residues that might be associated with the biogenic synthesis process. These organic components could influence the material’s surface properties and provide sites for further chemical interaction. The overall spectrum underscores the distinctive features of biogenic Al₂O₃, enhancing understanding of its potential applications in biomedicine, catalysis, and environmental remediation. Thus, the IR analysis effectively illustrates the chemical characteristics and functional relevance of Al₂O₃ derived from S. virginianum.

In XRD analyses (Fig. 2B), the abscissa represents the diffraction angle (2θ), whereas the ordinate corresponds to the diffracted-beam intensity, reflecting the relative number of X-ray photons scattered at each angle. Each peak at a specific 2θ value corresponds to different crystal planes of Al₂O₃, reflecting its orderly arrangement of atoms. For Al₂O₃, common diffraction peaks can be associated with the alpha phase (α-Al₂O₃) at around 25.5°, 35.1°, 37.2°, 43.4°, 49.4°, and 67.1° 2θ, which denotes its stable and thermodynamically favoured form. If the peaks indicate the gamma phase (γ-Al₂O₃), they generally occur at lower angles, suggesting different properties and behaviours. The intensity of these peaks provides insights into the relative abundance of each phase present in the sample. Higher intensity peaks indicate a greater proportion of crystalline material, which often correlates with better mechanical strength and catalytic activity. Additionally, any extraneous peaks observed in the pattern may imply the presence of impurities or secondary phases, such as aluminium hydroxides, which could affect the material’s properties and functional efficacy. It is crucial to match the observed diffraction peaks against recognised database standards such as those from the Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS), to definitively determine the crystalline phases present. Overall, the XRD results not only help in identifying the structure of biogenic Al₂O₃ but also provide a foundation for understanding its potential performance in practical applications, acknowledging that the crystal structure significantly influences properties like reactivity, surface area, and thermal stability. This comprehensive insight makes XRD an invaluable tool in characterising nanomaterials for targeted biomedical and industrial uses.

Discussion

This study evaluated the antibacterial and antibiofilm efficacy of Al₂O₃ NPs synthesized via an environmentally benign route employing S. virginianum L. extracts. Due to their high surface area, enhanced reactivity, and tunable surface chemistry, metal oxide nanoparticles are being used as alternatives to conventional antimicrobials18,19. In this context, biogenically derived Al₂O₃ NPs were investigated for their ability to inhibit drug-resistant vaginal pathogens and disrupt established biofilms, addressing the urgent need for novel strategies against recalcitrant infections.

Women with bacterial vaginal infections in this study showed significant metabolic disturbances compared to healthy controls. Fasting serum glucose and HOMA-IR values were high in the infection group. This suggests an insulin-resistant state that can compromise innate immune defences at the mucosal surface. Hyperglycemia has been shown to impair neutrophil degranulation38phagocytosis39and cause oxidative stress40which can promote microbial colonisation. At the same time, vitamin D levels were significantly lower in infected participants, which could further weaken antimicrobial peptide production. This may result in a compromised defence against vaginal infection. Vitamin D also stimulates keratinocyte differentiation and proliferation in the epidermis, implying it may help preserve epithelial integrity in the genital tract41.

This study characterised biogenic Al₂O₃ NPs synthesised from S. virginianum and confirmed their α-phase crystalline structure, surface hydroxyl functionalities, and heterogeneous morphology. These physicochemical features, such as the surface hydroxyl groups, are thought to promote interactions with microbial cells42. Nanoparticles can increase reactive oxygen species levels, which can lead to the damage of cellular components, which could cause cell death43. Such mechanisms of action suggest that Al₂O₃ NPs could disrupt microbial viability through membrane disruption, oxidative stress, and ionic imbalances.

The XRD findings of this study are similar to those reported by Ikhioya and Nkele44who described biogenic Al₂O₃ NPs synthesized using Azadirachta indica exhibiting 2θ peaks at 18.38°, 38.71°, 44.72°, 61.22°, and 69.89°. The narrow diffraction bands and prominent peaks show the crystalline nature of the metal oxide45. Moreover, FTIR results are in line with previous studies synthesizing Al₂O₃ NPs from plants, which showed peaks at 3600 cm⁻¹ and 1700–1800 cm⁻¹46. Additionally, while SEM images in this study showed loose, three-dimensional aggregates several micrometres across, Babiker47 observed more uniform clusters when using Mentha pulegium, implying that the choice of plant extract influences morphology and, therefore, surface area and reactivity. In EDAX analysis, the prominent peaks for aluminium (Al) and oxygen (O) confirm that aluminium oxide is the primary compositional element, similar to the results was also observed in 2024 48. Additional peaks for elements such as sodium (Na), and sulfur (S) may indicate the presence of these elements in trace amounts or as contaminants.

The antibiotic susceptibility testing showed a high prevalence of multidrug-resistant organisms in both aerobic and anaerobic isolates. S. aureus strains showed high resistance to macrolides, fluoroquinolones, and third-generation cephalosporins. E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and G. vaginalis also showed significant resistance to many therapeutic agents. These patterns show the global trend of rising antibiotic resistance in pathogens due to overuse48 and demonstrate the urgent need for alternative or adjunctive antimicrobial therapies. These findings underscore the increasing challenge of antibiotic resistance in common vaginal pathogens, which is consistent with trends observed in other clinical settings49. The elevated resistance to commonly used antibiotics such as metronidazole, clindamycin, and fluoroquinolones among vaginal isolates, especially G. vaginalis and P. anaerobius, raises concerns about treatment efficacy and highlights the urgent need for alternative therapeutic strategies50. The detection of resistance among Lactobacillus spp., which are typically considered beneficial vaginal flora, is particularly worrisome because it may affect the ecological balance of the vaginal microbiome and potentially exacerbate dysbiosis11. The results highlight the complex and evolving nature of antimicrobial resistance among vaginal pathogens, calling for continued monitoring and exploration of new therapeutic interventions. The escalating prevalence of multidrug-resistant organisms presents a formidable challenge in the effective management of diverse infectious processes, including those affecting the delicate balance of the vaginal microbiome51. This situation necessitates a comprehensive approach that encompasses enhanced surveillance systems, rigorous antibiotic stewardship initiatives, and the relentless pursuit of innovative therapeutic modalities to effectively combat resistance52.

The emergence of resistance to antifungal agents among Candida species, including C. albicans, further complicates the management of vaginal infections; therefore, constant screening of drug resistance is needed53,54. Alternative approaches, such as those involving biogenic nanoparticles, may provide innovative solutions to combat resistant microbial strains and restore the balance of the vaginal ecosystem55. The urgent need for new treatments is highlighted by the rise in drug-resistant species like Candida auris and Candida glabrata56. As drug resistance rises, new treatment strategies are needed, such as those that stop resistance from happening in the first place4. Current antifungals have side effects and drug interactions that can make them less effective, so it is important to overcome these issues too57. The alarming increase in resistance to azole antifungals, a cornerstone in the treatment of fungal infections, underscores the critical need for the development and implementation of innovative antifungal strategies58,59. The rise of antifungal drug resistance presents a significant obstacle to effective clinical management, as it curtails the available treatment options. This underscores the importance of devising strategies to limit the development of resistance in Candida strains53. Biofilm-associated infections pose a significant global health burden, contributing to an estimated 100,000 deaths each year and highlighting the urgent need for innovative therapeutic interventions9.

The classification of isolates according to their biofilm activity was evaluated in this study, encompassing both aerobic and anaerobic organisms as well as fungal isolates (Table 3). Biofilm formation occurred as an important factor in the persistence of these infections. Strong biofilm producers, including S. aureus, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa, were associated with methicillin resistance, ESBL production, and metallo-β-lactamase phenotypes. Biofilms create a protected niche that reduces antibiotic penetration60promotes horizontal gene transfer61and promotes chronicity62. In this clinical analysis, biofilm positivity was found to be significantly associated with age, polymicrobial infection, monomicrobial infection, multidrug resistance, elevated fasting glucose, and vitamin D insufficiency. Among the total of 108 isolates, different levels of biofilm formation were observed, categorised as strong, moderate, weak, and negative. Among the aerobic bacteria, which comprised 31.4% of the total isolates, the distribution of biofilm production was notably varied, pointing to diverse adaptive capabilities within this group. Specifically, 8.8% of the aerobic bacteria demonstrated strong biofilm production. This subset of strong biofilm producers is particularly significant because biofilms can enhance bacterial survival by protecting against environmental stresses and antimicrobial agents. Furthermore, 23.5% of aerobic isolates exhibited moderate biofilm production, suggesting a substantial ability to form structured communities which could potentially contribute to persistent infections. The presence of 20.5% of isolates showing weak biofilm production indicates a spectrum of biofilm-forming potential even among closely related organisms. Remarkably, 47.0% of aerobic bacteria were negative for biofilm production, emphasizing that not all members possess this capability, which could influence their role in the infection process and their responsiveness to treatment. Similar patterns of biofilm formation variability were noted among anaerobic bacteria and fungal isolates. The development of biofilms can lead to reduced susceptibility to antifungal drugs and evasion of the host’s immune response63. The strong biofilm-producing C. albicans exhibited the highest minimum inhibitory concentration value towards fluconazole, followed by ketoconazole, itraconazole, and amphotericin B, highlighting the heightened resistance of biofilms to common antifungal treatments10. Microorganisms embedded within biofilm structures are inherently more recalcitrant to antimicrobial agents compared to their planktonic counterparts64,65. Biofilm formation represents a critical virulence factor, enabling pathogens to withstand host immune defenses and resist antimicrobial therapies66,67. Biofilms enhance bacterial survival, offer protection against environmental stressors and antimicrobial agents, and allow for increased antibiotic resistance68. Biofilms, complex microbial communities encased in a self-produced matrix, exhibit increased resistance to antimicrobial agents and host immune defences69,70. This resilience is conferred by a multitude of mechanisms, including the restricted penetration of antimicrobial agents through the dense extracellular matrix, the metabolic quiescence of cells within the biofilm leading to reduced drug susceptibility, and the presence of persister cells that exhibit innate tolerance to antimicrobial treatments23. The biofilm architecture hinders the diffusion of drugs, while the altered metabolic activity of biofilm-embedded cells reduces their susceptibility to antibiotics71. The formation of biofilms is influenced by genetic and environmental factors, allowing microbes to resist physical and chemical stressors72. The heightened antimicrobial resistance displayed by biofilm-producing bacteria, contrasted with their non-biofilm-producing counterparts, accentuates the significant clinical implications of biofilm formation in the context of recalcitrant infections73. Certain bacterial species, such as *Propionibacterium acnes*, exhibit enhanced biofilm formation in invasive isolates, indicating that the ability to produce biofilms is a crucial factor in their virulence74. Therefore, the intricate dynamics of bacterial-fungal interactions within biofilms necessitate rigorous investigation to fully elucidate their combined resistance mechanisms against host4,8.

In-vitro testing demonstrated that Al₂O₃ NPs possess strong antimicrobial activity against all five test organisms, which is consistent with prior studies showing that metal oxide nanoparticles have shown promising antibiofilm properties and they can also effectively overcome the problems associated with the emergence of resistance in bacteria75. These values place Al₂O₃ NPs in a similar efficacy range to zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) against standard strains, which shows a MIC of 1,000 µg/mL76. This, however, is less effective than silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs), where MICs fall to 500 µg/mL for fungal strains under comparable conditions77. MBC/MIC ratios between 1.5 and 2.0 indicate that only minute concentration increases above the inhibitory threshold are required for bactericidal or fungicidal effects. CFU assay and growth-curve assays further showed that higher nanoparticle concentrations delayed microbial replication, reduced maximal growth rates, and, at threshold levels, abolished detectable growth in most cases. Focusing on the mechanisms by which Al₂O₃ NPs disrupt biofilm-related virulence traits, the findings of this study regarding exopolysaccharide (EPS) reduction mirror those of Muzammil et al.78who demonstrated a decrease in EPS when a microbe was treated with Al₂O₃ NPs. These results can be attributed to the nanoparticle-induced suppression of the pel and psl operons which are involved in EPS production79. In line with Pati et al.80this study showed that cell-surface hydrophobicity decreased when cells were exposed to metal oxide nanoparticles at their MIC, suggesting that a biogenic synthesis route may promote strong surface interactions and thus greater hydrophobicity impairment. CSH is an essential virulence factor that is responsible for adhesion. Therefore, the reduction of CSH by Al₂O₃ NPS can prevent the formation of the biofilm when the adhesion stage has just begun81.

At the MIC, an almost complete loss of membrane integrity was observed, which likely arises from multiple nanoparticle–membrane interactions. First, surface hydroxyl groups on the nanoparticles can form strong hydrogen bonds with phospholipid head groups, effectively destabilizing the lipid bilayer. Simultaneously, electrostatic attractions between charged alumina moieties (Al–O) and positively charged R–N(CH₃)₃⁺ or negatively charged phosphate groups in the membrane further disrupt membrane structure. In living cells, these effects are compounded by nanoparticle–biomolecule contacts that increase oxidative stress, leading to membrane disruption and even activation of apoptotic pathways82. Finally, regarding pre-formed biofilm eradication, this study showed 100% removal at the MIC, which may be explained by some metal oxide nanoparticles catalysing the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that damage both cellular components and the biofilm matrix83. This property is far superior to conventional antifungals like fluconazole, which failed to reduce or disperse the preformed C. albicans biofilm84.

When comparing the epidemiological findings of this study to those from other regions, some contrasts are observed. For example, Saleh et al.85 reported an OR of 0.46 (95% CI: 0.24–0.87) for biofilm-positive urinary tract infections among Yemeni individuals aged 16–30 years. Although their focus was on UTIs rather than bacterial vaginosis (BV), their OR remains lower than the OR of 6.33 (95% CI: 1.54–26.04) for BV in patients aged ≥ 30 years in our study. This suggests potential regional or pathogen‐specific differences in biofilm‐associated risk. Similarly, Mojtahedi et al.86 found that vitamin D deficiency (serum level < 10 ng/mL) conferred an OR of 4.34 (p = 0.011) for BV in an Iranian cohort. In contrast, our cohort, using a deficiency cut-off of < 25 ng/mL, showed an OR of 6.33. Part of this discrepancy may be because of different vitamin D thresholds, but geographic, demographic, and methodological differences might also play a role.

In this study, hyperglycemia was significantly associated with biofilm positivity (p = 0.03). Elevated glucose levels are known to enhance microbial virulence by promoting tissue adherence and biofilm formation which can lead to chronicity87. Moreover, our chi-square test for multidrug resistance (p = 0.03) aligns with data from Olana et al.88 in Ethiopia, where biofilm‐forming isolates also demonstrated higher rates of MDR (p = 0.015). Together, these findings show the roles of host metabolic status and regional antimicrobial pressures in determining biofilm‐related infection patterns. The findings of this study demonstrate a new therapeutic strategy that involves correcting host metabolic imbalances through blood glucose management and vitamin D repletion to strengthen mucosal defences. This should be done alongside the use of biogenic Al₂O₃ NPs to target biofilm-associated, drug-resistant pathogens. This strategy could be valuable in managing recurrent or refractory vaginal infections where standard antibiotics do not work.

Limitations: However, this study is not exempt from limitations. All current data are in-vitro, and factors like vaginal pH, mucus interactions, and complex microbiota may reduce in-vivo efficacy. SEM images revealed micrometer-scale nanoparticle aggregates, which could lower antimicrobial potency, hinder consistent dosing, and risk local tissue retention. Toxicological profiling such as hemolysis testing, cytokine release assays, and long-term biodistribution studies, has not yet been performed. Only five species were tested in this study, not considering other BV pathogens such as Mobiluncus, and Atopobium vaginae, which was not isolated in out set of patients and due to which the full antimicrobial spectrum of microbial communities remain unknown. Variability in the phytochemical composition of S. virginianum extracts as a result of season, geography, or extraction methods, may lead to inconsistent nanoparticle properties. Finally, the molecular mechanisms, whether Al₂O₃-GNPs act via physical membrane disruption, ROS generation, or specific protein interactions, are not elucidated in this study, and the risk of resistance arising under sub-MIC exposure was not explored.

Nevertheless, the antimicrobial and antibiofilm activity of biogenic Al₂O₃ NPssynthesised from Solanum virginianum provides directions for future research. In-vivo studies should evaluate their efficacy and safety in animal models of bacterial vaginosis or wound infections by assessing biodistribution, clearance, and cytotoxicity in vaginal epithelial cells. Moreover, embedding nanoparticles into chitosan- or carbopol-based hydrogels, mucoadhesive suppositories, or PEG-modified nanosuspensions could improve mucosal adhesion, controlled release, and colloidal stability. Combining sub-MIC levels of Al₂O₃-GNPs with antibiotics such as metronidazole and clindamycin may reveal synergistic effects against multidrug-resistant strains. Co-administration with probiotics like Lactobacillus crispatus could help restore healthy microbiota. Future studies should also employ transcriptomic and proteomic profiling, ROS assays, and confocal microscopy to determine whether membrane disruption or oxidative stress is the reason behind the antimicrobial and antibiofilm properties of Al₂O₃ nanoparticles.

Conclusion

This study gives strong evidence that biogenically synthesised Al2O3 NPs from S. virginianum could be a promising and versatile way to treat bacterial vaginosis (BV) and other infections that go along with it. One of the most important findings is that the drug was able to kill a wide range of clinically relevant pathogens, such as G. vaginalis, S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and C. albicans, in vitro. It was especially effective against strains that were resistant to other drugs. This antimicrobial action works by affecting several things: it changes the hydrophobicity of the cell surface, damages the membrane, and stops the production of EPS, which are important for biofilm formation and survival. The scientific importance of these results is that they show a new, plant-based way to make Al2O3 NP and describe its antibacterial properties in great detail. The study explains how these nanoparticles work with bacterial cells and biofilms, which gives us an idea of how they might be able to fight antibiotic resistance. The study also shows how important it is to think about host factors. It found strong links between biofilm positivity in BV patients and metabolic imbalances (high fasting glucose and insulin resistance) and vitamin D deficiency. This study could have a lot of different effects. Biogenic Al2O3 NP could be a good alternative or addition to traditional BV antibiotic treatments, especially for people who keep getting infections or have organisms that are resistant to multiple drugs. Being able to target several bacterial virulence factors at once might make it less likely that resistance will develop. The study’s focus on host metabolic factors also suggests that BV management should be more comprehensive, using nanoparticle therapy along with other methods to fix underlying problems, like controlling blood sugar levels and taking vitamin D supplements. This combined approach might lead to better and longer-lasting treatment results. Future research should focus on in vivo studies to find out how safe and effective these nanoparticles are, as well as on creating formulations that are best for vaginal delivery, like mucoadhesive hydrogels or suppositories. These results make it possible to create new future research with larger sample size and multicentric and multi-countric study to manage complicated and recurring vaginal infections.
