A single-chain mRNA vaccine co-expressing GPC and NP provides complete protection against lethal Dabie bandavirus challenge in mice
Authors: Entao Li, Haoyi He, Xiaoping Guo, Weiqi Wang, Qizan Gong, Da Chen, Yanqiong Shen, Zunhao Zhou, Jiachen Zhang, Zhihao Li, Liping Zhao, Chao Wang, Yucai Wang, Sandra Chiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is an emerging viral hemorrhagic fever caused by Dabie bandavirus (DBV), with a fatality rate of up to 30%. However, no approved vaccines or specific therapeutics against the virus are available yet. Here, we engineered a novel mRNA vaccine (GFN-LNP) featuring a single-chain design that incorporates both glycoprotein precursor (GPC) and nucleoprotein (NP) antigens connected by a (G4S1)3 flexible linker. A comparative evaluation was performed against three control formulations GPC (GPC-LNP), NP (NP-LNP), and physically mixed GPC-LNP/NP-LNP (Mix). Immunological assessments revealed that both GPC-LNP and GFN-LNP formulations induced robust humoral and cellular immune responses in mouse models. Remarkably, all multiantigen formulations (GPC-LNP, Mix, and GFN-LNP) conferred complete protection against lethal DBV challenge in A129 mice, as evidenced by the absence of weight loss, mortality, or detectable infectious virions in major organs. Immunohistochemistry analysis further demonstrated the superior efficacy of GFN-LNP, with no viral antigen detected in organ sections post-challenge. The dual-antigen GFN-LNP formulation additionally presented the unique advantage of maintaining hematological parameters within normal ranges following viral exposure. These findings collectively establish that our innovative single-chain, dual-antigen mRNA design (GFN-LNP) combines manufacturing efficiency with exceptional protective efficacy, positioning it as a leading vaccine candidate against DBV.Keywords: MT: Delivery Strategies, Dabie bandavirus, mRNA vaccine, immunity, challenge

Introduction

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is a viral hemorrhagic fever caused by Dabie bandavirus (DBV), also known as SFTS virus (SFTSV), Huaiyangshan virus, or new bunyavirus, a novel tick-borne virus that belongs to the bunyaviral family Phenuiviridae.1,2 The major clinical symptoms are fever, myalgia, gastrointestinal symptoms, and local lymph node enlargement. The laboratory findings include thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, and elevated serum liver enzyme levels.3,4 DBV was first isolated from a patient in China in 2009 and is now endemic to Korea, Japan, Vietnam, Myanmar, Thailand, and Pakistan, with fatality rates ranging from 5% to 30% in East Asia.5,6 A virus similar to DBV was isolated in the United States in 2012 and named Heartland virus.7 Tick-to-human transmission is the primary route of DBV infection through a tick bite by an Asian longhorned tick (Haemaphysalis longicornis), which functions as a vector and reservoir.8,9 Unfortunately, the Asian longhorned tick is a well-reported invasive species throughout the world and has been detected in the United States, Russia, Australia, and the Western Pacific regions.10 There is a possibility that DBV outbreaks outside Asian countries will occur in the future. In 2018, DBV was listed as a prioritized pathogen by the World Health Organization (WHO).11 In particular, the incidence and prevalence rates of SFTS have been increasing annually in China.12 However, there are no approved vaccines or specific therapeutics against DBV. Therefore, the development of antivirals and vaccines against DBV is urgently needed.

DBV is an enveloped, negative-sense single-stranded RNA virus that carries three gene segments, designated large (L), medium (M), and small (S), which encode the L protein, glycoproteins Gn (the N-terminal half) and Gc (the C-terminal half), and nucleoprotein (NP) and nonstructural protein (NSs), respectively. Two recent cryo-electron microscopy methods revealed that Gn and Gc form heterodimers that form the viral lipid bilayer envelope, which is produced by a glycoprotein precursor (GPC) undergoing proteolytic maturation.13,14 The DBV glycoprotein Gn, a type I transmembrane protein, plays a major role in binding host receptors and internalizing viral particles into the endosome.15 The DBV glycoprotein Gc, a class II fusion protein, is responsible for mediating membrane fusion between the host cell membrane and the viral envelope to release the viral genome into the host cell cytoplasm.16 Like many kinds of bunyaviruses, including the Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever virus,17 Rift Valley fever virus,18 and hantavirus,19 Gn and Gc are two major antigenic components on the surface of DBV. A recent study reported that high-titer Gc-specific serum could partly protect mice from DBV challenge.20 Owing to the DBV diversity across the six genotypes (A–F), variations in the Gn and Gc glycoproteins across the genotypes were limited to less than 6% of the amino acid sequence. The full-length DBV GPCs containing both Gn and Gc are considered attractive targets for DBV vaccines.21,22

In addition to the important role of anti-envelope (Gn and Gc) antibodies in protective immunity, nonenvelope-specific T cell responses could also contribute to protection against DBV infection.22 Kwak and colleagues reported that individual DNA vaccines containing NP, NSs, or RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) can contribute to partial protection against DBV infection.22 Kang and colleagues developed a plasmid DNA vaccine encoding the DBV envelope glycoproteins Gn and Gc, as well as NP-NS fusion antigens.23 However, the absence of antibodies against surface glycoproteins in the serum and antigen-specific cellular immunity was confirmed through enhanced antigen-specific T cell responses in immunized mice. Strikingly, vaccination with plasmid DNA provided partial protection to mice upon lethal DBV challenge.23 The above studies indicate that antigen-specific T cell responses, especially nonenvelope responses, also play major roles in protection. DBV NPs polymerize into large oligomers and bind with viral RNA (vRNA) to make viral ribonucleoprotein protect the entire length of the viral genome RNA from degradation.24 Furthermore, Song et al. demonstrated that the absence of virus-specific IgG responses targeting both Gn and NP serves as a distinctive immunological hallmark in fatal cases of SFTS caused by DBV infection.25 These findings further highlight the critical protective roles of NP- and Gn-specific antibody responses in controlling viral pathogenesis and disease progression. However, most vaccine studies on DBV have focused on Gn/Gc or the head region of Gn, and only one study has reported protective immunity with a full-length glycoprotein.21 Therefore, developing a bivalent mRNA vaccine encoding DBV GPC and NP warrants further exploration.

The mRNA vaccine platform provides innovative solutions for immunization against various infectious diseases, particularly the overwhelming success in the rapid development of COVID-19 vaccines.26 The efficacy and safety of mRNA vaccines have been substantiated by extensive real-world evidence during the COVID-19 pandemic.27,28,29 Here, we developed mRNA vaccine candidates expressing different antigens of DBV, including GPC (GPC-LNP), NP (NP-LNP), and GPC and NP with a flexible linker (G4S1)3 in a single sequence (GP-flexible linker-NP [GFN]-LNP). Then, the immunogenicity and protective efficacy of GPC-LNP, NP-LNP, mixtures (GPC-LNP and NP-LNP), and GFN-LNP in mice were systematically evaluated. Our results showed that both GPC-LNP and GFN-LNP could elicit strong humoral and cellular immune responses in mice. GPC-LNP, Mix, and GFN-LNP provided complete protection against lethal DBV infection in A129 mice, whereas immunization with NP-LNP resulted in partial protection, with a 20% survival rate (1/5). This study provides valuable information for the design of DBV mRNA vaccines and suggests a promising vaccine candidate for defending against DBV infection.

Results

Design and expression of the DBV antigen-specific mRNA vaccine

For DBV mRNA vaccine preparation, the GPC (GenBank: YP_009666134.1) and NP (GenBank: YP_009666136.1) gene sequences of the SFTS virus HNXH were chosen and three mRNA sequences were designed, which separately expressed the GPC, NP, and a fusion protein of GPC and NP with a flexible linker. The mRNA sequences were subsequently cloned and inserted into a vector containing the 5′ UTR and 3′ UTR after codon optimization to match human codon usage. Then, linearized and amplified DNA templates were utilized for co-transcriptional N1-methyl-pseudo UTP incorporation, 5′ capping, and 3′ poly-A tails. A schematic diagram of DBV mRNA vaccines is shown in Figure 1A. The expression of antigens was detected by western blotting and an immunofluorescence assay (IFA) after mRNA transfection in HEK293T cells in vitro. As shown in Figure 1B, both Gn and Gc were detected in the GPC-mRNA and GFN-mRNA samples and NP was detected in the NP-mRNA and GFN-mRNA samples. Furthermore, IFA (Figure 1C) revealed that GPC-mRNA- and GFN-mRNA-transfected HEK293T cells presented positive expression of Gn and Gc and NP-mRNA- and GFN-mRNA-transfected HEK293T cells presented positive expression of NP. Next, the mRNAs were encapsulated in lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) via a microfluidics device. The size, polydispersity index (PDI), and encapsulation efficiency (EE) of the mRNA vaccines were detected, with an average size of 85∼95 nm, a PDI ranging from 0 to 0.25, and an encapsulation efficiency greater than 95% (Figure S1). Collectively, these data indicate that DBV mRNAs display efficient expression levels in transfected cells and appropriate uniformity and stability after being encapsulated within LNPs.

Humoral immune responses of immunocompetent mice vaccinated with DBV mRNA vaccines

To investigate the immunogenicity of the DBV mRNA vaccines, immunocompetent C57BL/6J mice were intramuscularly (vaccinated with GPC-LNP, NP-LNP (containing 1 μg of each antigen-encoded mRNA), Mix-LNP (a mixture containing 0.5 μg of each GPC-LNP and NP-LNP), or GFN-LNP (containing three doses of 0.2, 1, and 5 μg). Mice in the two control groups were injected with PBS or LNP. After receiving a two-shot vaccination regimen at 3-week intervals, the mice were sacrificed and analyzed for both humoral and cellular immune responses 10 days after the second vaccination. Serum samples were collected at the indicated time points post-immunization. The immunization and the sample collection schedule for the DBV mRNA vaccines is shown in Figure 2A. The 50% neutralizing titer (NT50) against BDV was determined via a high-content imaging system via IFA (Figure 2B). As expected, the GPC-LNPs, Mix, and GFN-LNPs elicited high neutralizing antibody titers after booster vaccination with 1 μg/mouse, reaching 1:2,113, 1:162, and 1:832, separately. And the NT50 of GFN-LNP showed a dose-dependent manner respectively. The NT50 of GFN-LNPs was dose dependent. Additionally, the BDV-Gn/Gc/NP-specific IgG titers in mouse serum on day 10 after booster immunization were assessed via ELISA. GPC-LNP, Mix, and GFN-LNP elicited high specific binding titers against BDV Gn and Gc, and the IgG titers in GFN-LNP increased in a dose-dependent manner (Figures 2C–2E). The BDV-Gn-specific IgG titers did not significantly differ among the GP-LNP, Mix, and GFN-LNP groups at the same dose (Figure 2C). The BDV-Gc-specific IgG titers in the 1 μg/mouse GFN-LNP group were significantly higher than those in the Mix group (p < 0.01) (Figure 2D). However, the BDV-NP-specific IgG titers in the NP-LNP group were much higher than those in the Mix and GFN-LNP groups (Figure 2E). Collectively, these data showed that GPC-LNP, Mix, and GFN-LNP could elicit high specific binding titers against the BDV antigenic protein, resulting in a high level of neutralizing antibodies against BDV in mice.

Cellular immune responses elicited by DBV mRNA vaccines in immunocompetent mice

The DBV-GPC/NP-specific cellular responses to DBV mRNA vaccines were analyzed at 10 days after boost immunization and restimulated with the respective antigen peptide pools for 8 or 24 h to assess cytokine expression via an enzyme-linked immunospot assay (ELISpot) or T cell activation via flow cytometry. The ELISpot results revealed that both GPC-LNP and GFN-LNP induced robust type 1 helper T (Th1) cytokine (interferon gamma [IFN-γ], tumor necrosis factor alpha [TNF-α], and interleukin [IL]-2) production after restimulation with the GPC peptide pool (Figure 3A). However, only the NP-LNP induced robust IFN-γ and IL-2 production in response to stimulation with the SFTSV NP peptide pool (Figure 3B). Flow cytometry was used to detect the proportions of Th1 cells (CD3+CD4+IFN-γ+), cytotoxic T cells (CD3+CD8+IFN-γ+), and central memory CD8+T cells (Tcm, CD3+CD4+CD44+CD62L+). GPC-LNP elicited a high proportion of Th1 cells, cytotoxic T cells, and CD8+ Tcm cells, followed by GFN-LNPs (Figure 3C). Additionally, only the NP-LNPs induced a high proportion of cytotoxic T cells and CD8+Tcm cells, while the GFN-LNPs elicited a high proportion of Th1 cells (Figure 3D). Collectively, these data showed that GPC-LNP, NP-LNP, and GFN-LNP could elicit strong Th1-biased cellular immune response in immunocompetent mice.

Humoral immune responses of A129 mice vaccinated with DBV mRNA vaccines

The type I interferon receptor-deficient (A129) mouse model, which develops fatal hemorrhagic fever with hematological manifestations mirroring human disease pathogenesis, was used to evaluate BDV mRNA vaccine efficacy and drugs.20,21,30,31 The mice received prime-boost intramuscular immunizations (5 μg/dose) at 3-week intervals, with serum collection at the indicated time points detailed in Figure 4A. As shown in Figures 4B–4D, the GPC-LNP, Mix, and GFN-LNP elicited robust specific binding titers against DBV Gn and Gc. The levels of antigen-specific IgG subtype antibodies in the serum were assessed 14 days post-booster vaccination at a dilution of 1:100. As expected, the GPC-LNP, Mix, and GFN-LNP also elicited robust levels of SFTSV Gn- and Gc-specific IgG1 and IgG2b antibodies. The IgG-, IgG1-, or IgG2b-binding antibodies from the NP-LNP, Mix, and GFN-LNP against the NP were consistent with those against Gn and Gc. Thus, these results indicate that DBV mRNA vaccines could also elicit a strong humoral immune response in A129 mice.

BDV challenge of vaccinated A129 mice

Following booster immunization (day 14), the mice were intraperitoneally challenged with BDV at a dose of 100 TCID50. Clinical parameters, including body weight and survival rates, were recorded daily for 21 days post-infection (dpi). Three mice per group were humanely euthanized at 4 dpi for comprehensive analysis, including virological assessment, complete blood cell count (CBC), and tissue collection (Figure 5A). The PBS-, LNP-, and NP-LNP-treated groups exhibited progressive body weight loss following challenge. All PBS- and LNP-treated mice died from infection by 6 dpi, with only 20% survival (1/5) in the NP-LNP group. In contrast, GPC-LNP-, Mix-, and GFN-LNP-vaccinated mice maintained stable body weights and demonstrated 100% survival through 21 dpi (Figures 5B and 5C). Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction revealed significantly lower viral loads (p < 0.01) in the brain, kidney, lung, heart, spleen, liver, and blood samples from the GPC-LNP, Mix, and GFN-LNP groups than in those from the control group at 4 dpi (Figures 5D‒5J). IFA analysis revealed the complete absence of detectable infectious viral particles in kidney, lung, heart, and spleen tissues from the GPC-LNP, Mix, and GFN-LNP treatment groups, with statistically significant reductions (p < 0.0001) compared with both the PBS and LNP control groups (Figures 5K–5Q). Notably, the viral titers in the brain, liver, and blood samples fell below the detection limits (limit of detection = 1:10) across all the groups.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis corroborated the IFA findings, revealing widespread BDV antigen-positive cells in multiple organs (spleen, kidney, lung, and heart) in the LNP control group (Figures 6 and S2). Notably, while IFAs failed to detect infectious viral particles in splenic tissues from Mix-vaccinated mice, IHC examination revealed persistent BDV antigen presence in corresponding paraffin-embedded spleen sections (Figure 6). Furthermore, heart tissue analysis revealed residual BDV antigens in both the GPC-LNP and Mix treatment groups (Figure S2), suggesting incomplete viral clearance despite the absence of detectable infectious particles. Histopathological evaluation of the splenic tissues revealed complete resolution or marked attenuation of virus-induced pathological alterations in the GPC-LNP-, Mix-, and GFN-LNP-immunized groups (Figure 6). In contrast, the LNP- and NP-LNP-treated cohorts presented significant disruption of the splenic architecture characterized by white pulp atrophy, apoptotic lymphocyte infiltration, and multiple areas of local necrosis. Notably, the preserved splenic microarchitecture in the vaccinated groups correlated with enhanced viral clearance efficiency, as observed through complementary immunohistochemistry analyses. The persistent necroinflammatory changes in the control groups suggest a failure to contain viral cytopathic effects at the tissue level. Consistent with the viral loads, pathological injuries to the heart, lung, and kidney tissues were eliminated or alleviated by the GPC-LNP, Mix, and GFN-LNP vaccines (Figure S2). Furthermore, CBC analysis revealed the following: (1) platelet counts (PLTs) in the protected groups (GPC-LNP, Mix, and GFN-LNP) remained comparable to those in the uninfected control (Normal) group, whereas those in the control groups (PBS and LNP) revealed significant thrombocytopenia (p < 0.0001; Figure S3A); (2) the lymphocyte percentages (LYM%) in the PBS and LNP groups were significantly lower than those in the normal group (p < 0.05), with partial recovery in the NP-LNP and GPC-LNP groups (Figure S3B); and (3) the granulocyte ratios (GRAN%) were inversely correlated with the lymphocyte profiles (Figure S3C). These findings demonstrate the complete protective efficacy of the GPC-LNP, Mix, and GFN-LNP vaccines against lethal BDV challenge, with the NP-LNP providing partial protection. The robust correlation between virological clearance, hematological stability, and histopathological preservation confirms vaccine-mediated immune protection.

Discussion

DBV, an emerging tick-borne pathogen endemic to East Asia,6 poses increasing global health risks because of the worldwide invasion of its primary vector, Asian longhorned tick.10 With no approved vaccines or therapeutics available, DBV has been designated a priority pathogen by the WHO. To address this critical gap, we developed an mRNA vaccine encoding GPC and NP with a flexible linker (G4S1)3 in a single sequence (GFN-LNP) and compared this vaccine with mRNA vaccine candidates encoding GPC (GPC-LNP) or NP (NP-LNP). We found that the GPC-LNP and GFN-LNP induced robust humoral immunity and potent T cell responses in mice. Immunohistochemistry analysis revealed that the GGN-LNPs provided complete protection against lethal DBV infection in A129 mice without any positive viral antigens in the main organs. Notably, GFN-LNP immunization resulted in stable LYM% and GRAN% percentages post-DBV challenge compared with those resulting from GPC-LNP immunization alone, demonstrating preserved hematological homeostasis despite viral exposure. The observed hematological profile suggests that the dual-antigen design of GFN-LNPs mitigates the systemic inflammation typically associated with single-glycoprotein vaccines. The use of a flexible linker to connect GPC and NP proteins in a single sequence in the vaccine design may enhance efficacy, with improved immunogenicity potentially attributable to (1) the increased molecular weight of the fusion antigen (sum of the Gc and NP proteins, Figure 1B) and (2) the exposure of the immunodominant epitopes.

Our findings align with prior studies demonstrating that mRNA vaccines targeting the DBV glycoprotein (Gn/Gc) or receptor-binding domain (Gn head domain) induce robust humoral and cellular immunity, achieving complete protection against SFTSV infection.21,32,33 This study further validated the effectiveness of DBV GPC-derived nucleic acid vaccines, while revealing novel advantages of a multigenic approach incorporating both GPC and NP. Crucially, our dual-antigen vaccine maintained hematological homeostasis after viral challenge (Figure S3), addressing a critical gap in DBV countermeasures. Kim et al. reported that NP alone can offer partial protection against DBV infection in a ferret model (1/3 survival),22 while NP-LNP vaccination in mice shows a similar trend (1/5 survival post-challenge) (Figure 5C). Notably, LYM% and GRAN% were partially recovered in the NP-LNP group, similar to the GPC-LNP group (Figure S3), at 4 dpi. Notably, Song et al. demonstrated the absence of NP-specific IgG in deceased patients, suggesting the key role of the NP-specific antibody response in the control of viral pathogenesis.25 Our study highlights the critical role of combining GPC (Gn/Gc) and NP antigens in DBV vaccine development. Compared with mixed GPC-LNP and NP-LNP formulations, the GFN-LNP construct, which employs a flexible linker for dual-antigen co-expression, demonstrates superior efficacy, safety, and manufacturing simplicity. Single-sequence antigen linkage ensures uniform LNP encapsulation and intracellular delivery, whereas mixed mRNA formulations introduce variability in particle fate and antigen processing. Our work provides a template for optimizing multigenic mRNA vaccines through co-antigen design, addressing both immunological synergy and production scalability.

The rapid development of mRNA vaccines during the COVID-19 pandemic has revolutionized immunization strategies. While efficacy is well documented, safety evaluation remains critical for these novel platforms. Clinical data demonstrate an overall favorable safety profile for authorized mRNA vaccines, with predominantly mild-to-moderate local reactions (pain, erythema, swelling) and systemic effects (fatigue, headache, myalgia) occurring more frequently after the second dose.34,35,36,37 Although pericarditis and myocarditis occur at very low frequencies (1 < 10,000), they have been identified in post-authorization surveillance of COVID-19 mRNA vaccines.38 Our preclinical study with DBV mRNA candidates revealed no injection site reactions or behavioral changes in the mice, which is consistent with prior findings in which MPXV-1103 with the same LNP exhibited comparable hematological and organ pathology to that of the controls.39 These observations confirm that while mRNA vaccines have characteristic immune-related effects, their safety profile remains acceptable relative to public health benefits, with ongoing pharmacovigilance further elucidating their risk-benefit balance.

A previous study demonstrated that individual DNA vaccines containing NSs or RdRp can provide partial protection against DBV infection, whereas DNA vaccines containing N/NSs/RdRp confer complete protection in a ferret model.22 The use of combinations of multiple antigens, such as Gn, Gc, NP, NSs, or RdRp, in DBV mRNA vaccine candidates should be detected in the future. Lu et al. reported that the full-length DBV glycoprotein mRNA vaccine provided complete cross-protection against other bandaviruses, such as the Heartland virus and Guertu virus, in lethal challenge models.21 The GFN-LNP formulation in this study theoretically confers robust protective efficacy against other bandaviruses. Durable antiviral immunity is a key vaccine development priority, and confirmation of the long-term protective efficacy of this vaccine against viral challenge is needed for clinical validation. Although the A129 mouse model aids early research, its immune limitations necessitate ferret challenge studies that better replicate human disease. Current two-dose protocols should be compared with single-dose regimens to optimize immunization strategies.

Conclusion

Through these studies, we developed an mRNA vaccine encoding DBV full-length GPC and NP with a flexible linker (G4S1)3 in a single sequence. GFN-LNP elicits strong humoral and cellular immune responses in mice and provides complete protection against lethal DBV infection in A129 mice. In addition, our dual-antigen vaccine maintained hematological homeostasis post-DBV challenge. Compared with mixed GPC-LNP and NP-LNP formulations, our single-chain, dual-component mRNA vaccine design strategy enables streamlined large-scale manufacturing and high efficacy. Collectively, our findings provide a robust framework for advancing multiantigen vaccine platforms against DBV and related pathogens, highlighting the transformative potential of innovative immunization strategies in mitigating both emerging and persistent infectious disease threats.

Materials and methods

Animals and ethics statement

Female C57BL/6J mice (5–6 weeks old) were obtained from Jiangsu Huachuang Xinnuo Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. (Taizhou, China). The IFN-α/β receptor knockout mice (B6.129S2-Ifnar1tm1Agt/Mmjax, RRID: MMRRC_032045-JAX) were kindly provided by the Wuhan Institute of Virology of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and subsequently housed and bred under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in the animal facility of the Division of Life Sciences and Medicine at the University of Science and Technology of China (USTC). The protocols for the animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the USTC (USTCACUC26100122094). Studies with DBV were conducted under biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) and animal biosafety level 3 containment (ABSL-3).

Cells and virus

Vero E6 cells (African green monkey kidney epithelial cells, ATCC: CRL-1586) were cultured in DMEM (VivaCell, cat# C3103-0500; Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum ([FBS] VivaCell, cat# C04001-500; Shanghai, China; Shanghai, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Biosharp, BL505A; Hefei, China). HEK293F cells were cultured in HEK293 cell complete medium (Sino Biological, Inc., Beijing, China) supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All the cells were grown at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. An authentic DBV (Phlebovirus WCH/97/HN/China/2011) sample was preserved and obtained from the National Virus Resource Center, Wuhan Institute of Virology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, and propagated with Vero cells.

Generation and characterization of the mRNA-LNPs

The mRNAs were produced via in vitro transcription (IVT) from linearized DNA templates from plasmids encoding sequences of the target antigens (GP, NP, and GFN). The IVT reactions were performed via T7 RNA polymerase-mediated transcription (Vazyme, cat# TR101-01/02; Nanjing, China) in the presence of a trinucleotide cap1 analog (TriLink) with the complete replacement of uridine with N1-methyl-pseudouridine. The length and purity of the in vitro-transcribed mRNAs were assessed via capillary gel electrophoresis (Fragment Analyzer 5200; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The mRNA sequences were named GP-mRNA, NP-mRNA, and GFN-mRNA.

Formulation and characterization of the mRNA-LNPs

The mRNAs were encapsulated in LNPs via a microfluidics device (Aitesen, MPE-L2; Suzhou, China) as previously described. Briefly, an ethanolic lipid mixture of ionizable cationic lipids, 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine, cholesterol, and polyethylene glycol-lipid (molar ratio = 50:10:38.5:1.5), was rapidly mixed with an aqueous solution (acidic citrate buffer, pH 4.5) containing mRNA. The two phases were uniformly mixed at a 5:1 flow rate (aqueous to organic) via the microfluidic chip, and the resulting mixture (LNP solution) was collected. The collected LNP solution was then diluted 10-fold with purified water, followed by ultrafiltration to remove residual ethanol and unencapsulated free mRNA. The hydrodynamic size and PDI of the LNPs were measured via a Nano ZS90 Zetasizer (Malvern Instruments, Shanghai, China). The concentrations of mRNAs and the encapsulation efficiency of the LNPs were determined via a Quant-iT RiboGreen RNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, cat# R11490; Carlsbad, CA, USA). The mRNA-LNPs were named GP-LNPs, NP-LNPs, and GFN (GP-flexible linker-NP)-LNPs.

Transfection analysis

mRNA transfection was carried out via Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, cat# 11668019; Carlsbad, CA, USA) to measure antigen expression. HEK293T cells were seeded in a 12-well plate until they reached 70% confluence, after which 2.5 μg of mRNA was added to each well. After 48 h of incubation, the supernatant was collected, and the cells were subsequently subjected to treatment with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF (Beyotime). The resulting samples were combined with 6× protein loading buffer (TransGen Biotech) and boiled at 100°C for 10 min. SDS-PAGE was performed to separate the proteins, which were subsequently transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane for western blot analysis. The PVDF membrane was incubated with Gn rabbit polyclonal antibodies, Gc rabbit polyclonal antibodies, or NP rabbit polyclonal antibodies initially at 4°C overnight and subsequently at room temperature for 1 h with a goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody and horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Invitrogen). In addition, an IFA was conducted. After 48 h of transfection, the cells were fixed at room temperature for 30 min with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized for 1 h at 37°C with 0.5% Triton X-100/5% BSA. Following washing with PBS, the cells were incubated with specific antibodies targeting SFTSV glycoproteins (anti-Gn and anti-Gc polyclonal antibodies, kindly provided by Academician George Fu Gao and Associate Investigator Yan Wu, CAS Key Laboratory of Pathogenic Microbiology and Immunology, Chinese Academy of Sciences) or nucleocapsid proteins (anti-NP polyclonal antibodies generated in-house) for 1 h at 37°C. The cells were subsequently incubated at room temperature for 1 h with goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488, Abcam), followed by 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining for 20 min. Fluorescence was observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Vaccination of mice

C57BL/6J mice (5–6 weeks old, female, SPF) were randomly divided into eight groups (n = 5). The mice were immunized intramuscularly with GPC-LNPs, NP-LNPs (containing 5 μg of each antigen-encoded mRNA), GFN-LNPs (containing three doses of 0.2, 1, and 5 μg), and Mix-LNPs (a mixture containing 2.5 μg of each GPC-LNP and NP-LNP) and boosted with the same dose after 2 weeks. The mice in the two control groups were injected with PBS or LNPs. Serum samples were collected at 0, 14, and 28 days after the initial immunization.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The IgG, IgG1, and IgG2b antibody titers in the serum samples were determined via classical ELISA. The SFTSV Gn (GenBank: KY440776.1, 20–340 amino acid [aa]), Gc (GenBank: KY440776.1, 563–996 aa), and NP (GenBank:OM451688.1, 1–245 aa) proteins were expressed in HEK293F cells, and all the proteins were purified via a Ni-NTA agarose column according to standard protocols. First, 96-well plates were coated overnight at 4°C with the SFTSV-specific antigens Gn (1 μg/mL), Gc (1 μg/mL), or NP (1 μg/mL). The plates were subsequently washed with 1× PBST for 5 min four times and blocked with 5% skim milk powder-PBS for 2 h at 37°C. The serial 3-fold gradient dilutions of serum starting at 1:100 (sera were diluted 1:100 for IgG1 and IgG2b antibody analysis) were added to the wells, diluted with 5% skim milk powder-PBS, and incubated for 2 h at 37°C. Next, the plates were washed with 1× PBST for 5 min four times and treated with HRP-conjugated AffiniPure goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Proteintech, Cat# SA00001-1; Chicago, IL, USA) for 1 h at 37°C. Afterward, the plates were washed with 1× PBST for 5 min four times and incubated with 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (Beyotime, cat# P0211; Shanghai, China) for 25 min at room temperature in the dark before the reaction was terminated with sulfuric acid. The absorbance at 450 nm was recorded via a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA, USA). The ELISA endpoint titer was defined as the dilution of vaccinated serum that resulted in an absorbance no less than 2.1-fold higher than that of the average negative control.

Level of 50% neutralizing titer

Antibody neutralization assay and determination of the antibody titer via IFA: Briefly, Vero E6 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured overnight. The next day, serially diluted sera (beginning with a 1:30 dilution) in DMEM were incubated with 400 TCID50 of SFTSV at 37°C for 1 h. The serum-virus mixture was then added to three duplicate wells and incubated with the cells for an additional hour. After incubation, the mixture was discarded, the cell monolayer was washed with PBS, and then, DMEM containing 2% FBS was added. Three days later, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 for 15 min. Then, the cells were blocked with 5% skim milk powder-PBS, followed by treatment with a primary antibody (house-generated rabbit polyclonal antibody recognizing DBV NPs) at a 1:1,000 dilution. After the plates were washed with 1× PBST, the cells were stained with the secondary antibody, fluorescein (fluorescein isothiocyanate [FITC])-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Proteintech, Chicago, USA). Nuclei were stained with DAPI. The cells were imaged via a MetaXpress high-content imaging system (Molecular Devices), and the percentages of infected and DAPI-positive cells per well were calculated via the associated Harmony 3.5 software. The NT50 was calculated via GraphPad Prism v.9 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA).

ELISpot assays

Splenocytes from vaccinated mice were released into RPMI 1640 media through a 70-μm mesh filter, followed by suspension in RBC lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 10 min at room temperature. The secretion of the antigen-specific IFN-γ, TNF-α (Mebtech, Bristol, USA), IL-2, and IL-4 by splenocytes was measured via mouse ELISpot kits (Dakewe, Shenzhen, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 5×105 cells were cultured with the SFTSV GPC or NP peptide pools (2 μg/mL of each peptide; GenScript, Nanjing, China) in ELISpot plates at 37°C for 24–36 h. After incubation, spots indicative of IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-2, and IL-4 secretion were detected via the ELISpot Plus assay protocol and were counted via AID ELISpot READER (AID; D-72479 Strassberg, Germany).

Intracellular cytokine staining assay and flow cytometry

For the intracellular detection of IFN-γ, splenocytes (2×106 cells/well) were stimulated ex vivo with peptide pools of GP, or NP containing a protein transport inhibitor cocktail (eBioscience, cat#00-4980-93). After incubation for 6 h at 37°C, the cells were blocked for 15 min in PBS supplemented with 1% anti-CD16/CD32 (BD Pharmingen, cat# 553141) and then incubated with the following antibodies: Fixable Viability Stain 700 (BD Pharmingen, cat# 564997), PerCP-Cy5.5 rat anti-mouse CD8a (BD Pharmingen, cat# 551162), APC-Cy7 hamster anti-mouse CD3e (BD Pharmingen, cat# 557596), and BV605 rat anti-mouse CD4 (BD Pharmingen, cat# 563151). After fixation with 1× fixation/permeabilization buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat#00-5123 and cat#00-5223), the cells were stained with FITC-conjugated rat anti-mouse IFN-γ (BD Pharmingen, cat#554411). A CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) was used to acquire cells, and the data were analyzed with FlowJo software.

Animal immunization and challenge

A129 mice (aged 6–10 weeks, n = 8/group) received intramuscular vaccinations according to a prime-boost regimen at 3-week intervals. Serum samples were collected biweekly until challenge. The mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (RWD, R510-22-10; Shenzhen, China) and infected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 100 TCID50 of SFTSV at 14 days after booster vaccination. The weight loss of the mice was monitored for 20 days. Animals that lost more than 20% of their initial body weight were humanely anesthetized. Some infected animals (n = 3/group) were sacrificed at 4 dpi for tissue harvesting, virological analyses, and CBC.

Measurement of infectious virus particles and the viral genome

After the mice were sacrificed, blood, spleens, livers, lungs, brains, kidneys, and hearts were collected for tissue homogenization. The tissue homogenates were subsequently clarified via centrifugation at 8,000 rpm for 10 min. Infectious virus particles in the supernatants in the form of TCID50 values were detected via IFA in Vero E6 cells, and the number of copies of vRNA in the supernatants was measured via real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction. Total RNA was isolated via the Virus DNA/RNA Extraction Kit (Vazyme, cat# RM401; Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. SFTSV RNA was measured with the following primer sets: SFTSV-S-F: 5′-ACCTGTCTCCTTCAGCTTCT-3′ and SFTSV-S-R: 5′-TGTCAGAGTGGTCCAGGATT-3’. Amplification and quantification of the viral genome were performed via the HiScript II One Step qRT-PCR SYBR Green Kit (Vazyme, cat# Q221-01; Nanjing, China) with a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA, USA).

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry

Tissues dissected from the animals were immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, G1101-500ML). The fixed tissues were subsequently dehydrated with an ethanol gradient and embedded in paraffin. Sections (4-μm thick) were cut from the embedded tissues, dried at 60°C, dewaxed, and stained with H&E. The stained sections were dehydrated, sealed, and imaged via a Nikon Digital Sight DS-FI2 microscopic imaging system (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). For IHC, mouse lung tissue sections were immunostained with a primary antibody (1:1,000, house-generated DBV NP rabbit polyclonal antibody) and subsequently incubated with a secondary antibody (Zsbio, Cat. no PV-6000). Finally, 3,3′-diaminobenzidine solution (Zsbio, Cat. no ZLI-9018) was used for the color reaction, which was visualized with a microscope system (Nikon, Nikon Eclipse 50i).

Hematology

To collect hematological data, the animals were euthanized with isofluorane and bled via the retro-orbital sinus. Blood was prepared in tubes coated with 4% sodium citrate anticoagulant (Coolaber, SL6690-100 mL), and 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, G1101-500 mL) was added at a 1:1 ratio to inactivate the virus.40 The hematological data were analyzed via the Zoolab-V6 Hematology System (Maanshan, China).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism 9.0. All the data are presented as the means ± SEMs. Statistical differences were analyzed by ordinary one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons. All tests were considered statistically significant when the p value was <0.05.
