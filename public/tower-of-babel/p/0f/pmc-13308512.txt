Thrombospondin-1 regulates synaptic transmission in hippocampal field CA2 and social behavior in mice
Authors: Gerardo Leana-Sandoval, Alexis Madrid, Johanna Extrémet, Ester Aso, Javier Díaz-Alonso
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThrombospondin 1 (TSP1) is a secreted extracellular matrix glycoprotein, which mediates cell-to-cell and cell-to-matrix interactions. In humans, mutations in THBS1, which encodes TSP1, are associated with neurodevelopmental disorders, but the underlying neurobiological mechanisms are unclear. TSP1 is secreted by astrocytes, and has been shown to promote synaptogenesis in vitro, but knowledge about its function in vivo is limited. In this study, we assessed TSP1’s modulation of synaptic function and cognitive and emotional processes using TSP1 knockout mice. Deletion of TSP1 led to decreased perineuronal net density and strengthened synaptic transmission in hippocampal field CA2, but not CA1. Accordingly, TSP1 KO mice showed severely disrupted social interaction, as well as emotional alterations, but normal spatial memory. Collectively, our findings identify TSP1 as a crucial modulator of synaptic function in CA2 and social and emotional processes. Furthermore, our work delineates a mechanism linking altered TSP1 signaling with psychiatric conditions with altered social and emotional function.Keywords: Thrombospondin 1, Social behavior, AMPA receptor, Hippocampal field CA2, Autism spectrum disorder

Introduction

In the mammalian central nervous system (CNS), chemical synapses are the primary sites of communication among neurons. Synapse formation, remodeling and elimination are tightly regulated to safeguard appropriate circuit assembly and brain function. While synapses remain highly dynamic throughout life, the early postnatal period is a critical window for synapse formation and specification. Altered function of synaptic organizing proteins can lead to perturbations of synaptic development, and are a common feature of neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders, including autism spectrum disorder (ASD) (Delorme et al., 2013; Nelson and Valakh, 2015).

Emerging evidence highlights the critical role played by glia-derived molecules in shaping synaptic connectivity. Glial factors modulate synapse formation, maintenance, and plasticity (Eroglu and Barres, 2010; Allen and Eroglu, 2017; Chung et al., 2024). Thrombospondins (TSPs) represent one of the best characterized families of astrocyte derived secreted factors. Several TSP isoforms (TSP1–5) are expressed in the CNS, with TSP1 and TSP2 enriched during early postnatal development, coinciding with excitatory synaptogenesis (Iruela-Arispe et al., 1993). TSPs are large, oligomeric, extracellular matrix (ECM) glycoproteins, which have been shown to promote synaptogenesis (Christopherson et al., 2005). Specifically, TSP1 application to cultured neurons increases excitatory synapse density. Synapses induced by TSP1 are structurally normal, but devoid of AMPA receptors (AMPARs), thus considered “silent synapses” (Christopherson et al., 2005; Eroglu et al., 2009). Despite the synaptogenic capacity of TSP1 in vitro, our understanding of TSP1 signaling in vivo is incomplete. Specifically, the sites of TSP1 action have yet to be identified, and its contribution to brain function remains poorly understood. Notably, TSP1 KO mice exhibit hyperactivity and attention deficits (Nagai et al., 2019), and polymorphisms in the THBS1 gene have been linked to ASD and other neuropsychiatric conditions in humans (Park et al., 2012; Lu et al., 2014; Lim et al., 2017). These findings suggest that TSP1 may modulate discrete synaptic and circuit-level processes relevant to neuro-developmental disorders.

In the CNS, TSPs are secreted primarily by astrocytes, and act as ligands of neuronal surface proteins (Christopherson et al., 2005; Risher and Eroglu, 2012; Allen and Eroglu, 2017). For example, TSP1 can interact with the cell adhesion molecule neuroligin 1 (Xu et al., 2010), although the best characterized TSP1 receptor in the CNS is the voltagegated calcium channel (VGCC) auxiliary subunit α2δ-1 (Christopherson et al., 2005). Work from our lab has identified a direct interaction of α2δ-1 with the extracellular AMPAR subunit GluA1 amino-terminal domain (Leana-Sandoval et al., 2024). Consistent with a role in excitatory synaptic regulation, we found that mice lacking α2δ-1 in hippocampal field CA3 have decreased CA1 LTP, and show substantial deficits in spatial learning and memory (Leana-Sandoval et al., 2024).

In this study, we explored the regulation of hippocampal excitatory synapses and cognitive function by TSP1 using constitutive TSP1-deficient mice. Electrophysiological recordings revealed a defect in synaptic transmission in the relatively understudied hippocampal field CA2 in TSP1-deficient mice. CA2 plays a crucial role regulating social behavior and memory and its function is altered in some psychiatric disorders (Chevaleyre and Piskorowski, 2016). Consistent with altered CA2 function, TSP1 KO mice show severely impaired social interaction, but no defects in spatial learning and memory. Altogether, our findings identify a specific circuit regulated by the ASD-associated glycoprotein TSP1 and open the possibility of targeting this signaling system to further understand social dysfunction in neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions.

Methods

Animals

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of California, Irvine (protocol number AUP-23-091). All mice were group-housed and maintained in a 12-h light/dark schedule and had access to food and water ad libitum. B6.129S2-Thbs1tm1Hyn/J [(TSP1 KO strain # 006141)] mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories. WT mice were obtained from Charles River. Genotyping was carried out by TransnetYX Inc.

Confocal microscopy and image analysis

Brains from WT and TSP1 KO mice (P40) were sectioned (40 μm, coronal) following fixation in 4 % paraformaldehyde. After blocking tissue with 5 % swine serum (Jackson Immuno Research, # 014–000-121) and 2 % BSA (Cell Signaling, #9998S) in permeabilizing conditions (0.1 % Triton X-100, Sigma-Aldrich, # T8787), samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: GluA1 (rabbit, Cell signaling, #13185, 1:500), PSD-95 (mouse, Synaptic Systems, #124 011, 1:500), and Parvalbumin (chicken IgY, Synaptic Systems, #195 009, 1:500), followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse (Invitrogen, #A-11001, 1:500), Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-rabbit (Invitrogen, #A21245, 1:500) and Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti Chicken IgY (Abcam, #ab150173) secondary antibodies for 1.5 h at RT.

For PNN staining, slices were incubated with biotinylated Wisteria floribunda agglutinin (WFA, Vector laboratories # B-1355-2, 1:1000 dilution) overnight at 4 ° C. Subsequently, slices were incubated with Streptavidin, Alexa Fluor-568 conjugate (Invitrogen, # S11226, 1:500).

Slides were mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Reagent with DAPI (Cell Signaling, # 8961S).

Confocal images were collected using a Leica Sp8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Images of field CA1 and CA2 of the dorsal hippocampus were acquired using a 63 x oil objective as a series of 20 z-steps, with a z-step size of 0.2 μm, at a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels, scanning frequency of 400 Hz. The optical resolution (voxel size) per image was 180 nm in the xy-plane and 0.2 μm in the z-plane.

Analysis of synaptic localization was performed using Imaris 9.9.1 (Bitplane, South Windsor, CT, USA) and MatLab Runtime R2022b (Mathworks, Natick, MA, USA), as previously described (Leana-Sandoval et al., 2024). Briefly, the “Spots” tool was utilized to assign representative three-dimensional ellipsoid shapes to individual synaptic-like puncta of the excitatory postsynaptic marker PSD-95 as well as GluA1. For confocal imaging analysis, the minimum xy and z puncta diameters of GluA1 were set to 0.356 μm and 0.712 μm, respectively. Then “Background Subtraction” was applied prior to the addition of spots using a Gaussian filtered channel (Gaussian filtered by ¾) minus the intensity of the original channel Gaussian filtered by 8/9th of the punctum radius. A region of interest (ROI) was created to restrict the colocalization quantification to solely within the SR of each image. This ROI spanned an average area of approximately 20,196 ± 207 μm2 for CA1 and 12,877 ± 852 μm2 for CA2 samples. The number of spots was adjusted qualitatively using the automatically generated and interactive “Quality” filter histogram to select dense signal while excluding faint puncta likely to be background signal. To ensure an accurate spot segmentation of the underlying puncta determined by size, the “Different Spots Sizes” selection was utilized. Within this setting, the “Local Contrast” tool was used. The corresponding histogram was manually adjusted to ensure each spot covered as much of the puncta as possible. Spots were then rendered. Once optimal settings for each of these parameters were established for the GluA1 and PSD-95 channels, a batched protocol to automate spot detection on every image was run. For confocal imaging analysis, threshold for colocalization was established at 0.7 μm from the center of neighboring puncta.

Analysis of WFA fluorescence intensity was performed as previously described by (Carstens et al., 2021). Briefly, a contour was drawn in the respective sublayers (i.e. stratum oriens, pyramidale, radiatum) of hippocampal field CA2 and the ‘mean gray value’ was measured within those ROIs using ImageJ software. For WFA fluorescence intensity calculations in PV+ cells in hippocampal fields CA3, CA2 and CA1, a contour was established using the PV staining, and then WFA was measured within that contour. The ‘mean gray value’ of those cells was then averaged for each subregion and genotype.

Electrophysiology

Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were obtained from CA1 and CA2 pyramidal neurons (PNs) in acute ~P21-P35 mouse brain slices. Additional recordings were obtained from CA1 PNs in acute ~P9-P11 mouse brain slices to test possible effects of TSP1 deletion in younger mice. 300 μm acute horizontal slices were acquired using a vibrating microtome (Precisionary Instruments, MA, USA) in ice-cold, oxygenated NMDG recovery solution containing (in mM): 110 NMDG, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 30 NaHCO3, 20 HEPES, 25 glucose, 2 thiourea, 5 Na-ascorbate, 3 Na-pyruvate, 0.5 CaCl2•2 H2O, and 10 MgSO4•7 H2O. pH was adjusted to 7.4 and osmolarity to 310–316 mOsm. Slices were then incubated for at least 30 min. at 34 ° C in artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) composed of (in mM): 119 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1 NaH2PO4, 26 NaHCO3, 11 glucose, 2.5 CaCl2 and 1.3 MgSO4. aCSF was bubbled with 95 % O2 and 5 % CO2 to maintain pH. Osmolarity was adjusted to 307–310 mOsm. For recordings, slices were transferred to a perfusion stage on an Olympus inverted BX51WI microscope (Evident Scientific, Japan) and perfused at 2.5 mL/min. With aCSF containing 100 μM picrotoxin. Recording pipettes of 3–6 MΩ resistance were made using borosilicate glass capillaries using a P-1000 puller (Sutter Instrument, CA, USA) and filled with internal solution. The internal solution contained (in mM): 135 CsMeSO4, 8 NaCl, 10 HEPES, 0.3 EGTA, 5 QX-314, 4 Mg-ATP, 0.3 Na-GTP, and 0.1 spermine. Osmolarity was adjusted to 290–292 mOsm, and pH at 7.3–7.4. Membrane holding current, input resistance and pipette series resistance were monitored throughout experiments. Data were gathered through a IPA2 amplifier/digitizer (Sutter Instrument, CA, USA), filtered at 5 kHz, and digitized at 10 kHz. Postsynaptic currents were elicited with a tungsten bipolar stimulating electrode (Microprobes for Life Science, MD, USA) placed in the stratum radiatum (SR) or stratum oriens (SO), as indicated in each figure. Electric pulses were delivered at 0.2 Hz. AMPAR EPSCs were obtained while clamping the cell at − 70 mV, NMDAR currents were obtained at +40 mV, both in the presence of picrotoxin (100 μM). The peak evoked AMPAR response and the NMDAR component 100 ms after the stimulation artifact (to avoid contribution of the AMPAR EPSC) were used to calculate the AMPAR/NMDAR ratio. GABA A mediated IPSCs were recorded at 0 mV in the absence of pharmacological blockers. A subset of AMPAR recordings were performed in the absence of picrotoxin to calculate AMPAR/IPSC ratios. For paired-pulse ratio (PPR) experiments, stimulation was delivered at an inter-stimulus interval of 50 ms. PPR was calculated by dividing the second EPSC or IPSC by the first.

Electrophysiology data was gathered and analyzed using Sutterpatch (Sutter Instrument, CA, USA) and Igor Pro (Wavemetrics).

Behavior

3–6 month-old male and female mice were used in behavior experiments. Mice were handled for 3 consecutive days prior to all behavioral testing. At the beginning of each testing day, mice were allowed to acclimate to the behavior room for at least 30 min before the start of the experiment. Behavioral chambers and objects were cleaned and deodorized between mice. Behavioral scoring was done by a researcher blind to the genotype. Mice were subjected to different behavioral tasks, in the following order: three-chamber test, open field test, novel object recognition task, spatial novelty preference Y-maze task, light/dark transition test, and forced swim test. Individual data points from male and female mice are represented using different symbols where pertinent. We did not detect statistically significant sex differences or interactions between sex and genotype in all behavior tests applied; therefore, data from male and female mice were pooled. Number of mice tested in each experiment is indicated in the figure legend. Estimation of the number of mice tested for behavioral experiments was based on prior studies from our lab and others. Each task was replicated in at least two separate cohorts of mice.

Three-chamber test: social interaction and social memory of mice were evaluated in the three-chamber test (Kaidanovich-Beilin et al., 2011). The apparatus consists of a box divided into three chambers separated by two removable walls to limit access to the lateral chambers. Mice underwent a habituation phase in which they were placed in the center chamber, with doors leading to the other 2 areas closed, and allowed to explore for 5 min. Next, during the sociability test, a novel mouse (mouse 1), the same sex and age as the test mouse, was placed inside a wire cage in one of the lateral chambers and a novel object (identical but empty wire cage) in the opposite chamber. Time spent exploring mouse 1 (TM) or the novel object (TO), was analyzed during 10 min. The social memory and novelty test (session 2) began immediately after the sociability test. In this test, mouse 1 (now familiar mouse) remained in its wire cage and a novel mouse (mouse 2), also of the same sex and age as the test mouse, was placed in the wire cage in the opposite side. Again, time spent exploring the familiar mouse (mouse 1, TF) or the novel mouse (mouse 2, TN), was manually recorded during 10 min. Then, Social interaction index and Social recognition indexwere respectively calculated for the first and the second sessions: Social interaction index = (TM−TO)/(TM + TO); Social recognition index = (TN−TF)/(TN + TF).

Open field test: Mice were placed in a white Plexiglas chamber (30 × 23 × 23 cm) and allowed to explore the arena for 5 min. The total distance traveled and time spent in the center were measured.

Novel objection recognition (NOR) task: Following handling, mice were habituated to a white Plexiglas chamber (30 × 23 × 23 cm) for 5 min daily for 4 days. On training day, mice were placed in the chamber with two identical objects (2 conical tubes or 2 plastic 3-D printed blocks) and allowed to explore them for 10 min. The following day (test day), the mice were placed back in the chamber with one familiar and one novel object and allowed to explore for 5 min. The nature of objects employed was counterbalanced between genotypes. The animal’s behavior was recorded using an overhead camera and exploration time with each object was scored using the criteria described by (Vogel-Ciernia and Wood, 2014). Discrimination index (DI) was calculated as follows: (Novel Object Time – Familiar Object Time) / (Novel Object Time + Familiar Object Time) x 100. DI was calculated for both training and test day. Exclusion criteria: Mice that showed preference (DI ≥ 20) for an individual object on training day were excluded from the analysis. Mice that spent less than 3 s exploring the objects on training or test day were also excluded. Distance traveled per minute was calculated as: distance traveled (m) / session duration (min). Total object exploration per minute was calculated as: total object exploration time (sec) / session duration (min).

Spatial novelty preference Y-maze: The spatial novelty preference task was performed using an opaque Plexiglas Y-maze. Each arm was 36 l x 21 h x 10w cm. On the training trial, mice were placed into a starting arm, facing the center of the maze, and allowed to explore two of the arms for 5 min, while the third arm was blocked. After an inter-trial interval of 1 min, mice were placed back in the maze at the same starting arm and allowed to explore all three arms for 5 min. The starting arm and blocked arm were counterbalanced across mice. Total number of arm crossings and time spent in each arm was scored using a mouse tracking software (Any-Maze, Stoelting Co). Mice were required to enter an arm with at least 2/3 of its body to be considered a crossing. DI was calculated as Novel Arm Time / (Novel Arm Time + Non-Starting Arm Time) x 100.

Light/dark transition test: The light-dark apparatus consisted of an opaque acrylic box (42 × 21 × 25 cm) divided into two zones (2/3 light, 1/3 dark) with a small opening connecting the two chambers. The light zone was made of opaque white walls and lit by an overhead lamp, while the dark zone was unlit and made of black non-transparent acrylic walls with a lid overhead to prevent light from shining through. Mice were placed in the light zone and allowed to freely explore both zones for 10 min. The time spent in each chamber, number of crossings, and the latency to enter the dark zone was recorded using Any-Maze (Stoelting Co).

Forced swim test: Mice were individually placed in a clear plastic cylinder (25 h x 23d cm) filled with water at 23 C for 6 min. The total time spent immobile in the last 3 min of the task was individually scored by a researcher blind to the genotype. Floating and idle swimming were considered immobility.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis throughout the study was done blind to the experimental condition when possible. Results shown represent the mean ± SD. The number of independent experiments or biological samples, and the statistical test employed, are indicated in every case. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. In addition to summarized statistics in figure legends, complete statistical analyses are provided in Suppl. Table 1.

Results

Normal excitatory synaptic transmission in CA1 PNs in TSP1 KO mice

TSP1 is an astrocyte-secreted ECM glycoprotein, which has been shown to promote excitatory synapse formation acting through the GPI-anchored VGCC auxiliary subunit α2δ-1 (Christopherson et al., 2005; Eroglu et al., 2009; Risher et al., 2018). We recently established that α2δ-1 regulates AMPAR transmission and cognitive function in the mouse hippocampus (Leana-Sandoval et al., 2024), raising the possibility that its endogenous ligand TSP1 modulates synaptic AMPAR function. To test this hypothesis, we first quantified excitatory synapse organization using confocal microscopy in CA1 SR of TSP1 KO and WT mice (Fig. 1A). The density of neither PSD-95 nor GluA1 puncta were altered between genotypes (Fig. 1B, C, respectively). Their colocalization was not altered either (Fig. 1D). Altogether, these data indicate that excitatory synapse organization is not strongly affected in hippocampal field CA1 in TSP1 KO mice. Then, to directly assess TSP1’s role in synaptic transmission, we turned to whole-cell patch-clamp recordings from acute TSP1 KO and WT hippocampal slices. We patched CA1 PNs, while stimulating Schaffer collaterals, thereby probing the role of TSP1 at CA3➔CA1 synapses (Fig. 1E). We observed no significant differences in AMPAR/NMDAR ratios between TSP1 KO and WT CA1 PNs (Fig. 1F). We found no differences either in paired-pulse ratios (PPR), suggesting that glutamate release probability is unaffected by loss of TSP1 at CA3➔CA1 synapses (Fig. 1G). TSPs expression levels are highly developmentally regulated, peaking during the major synaptogenesis period and dropping drastically by postnatal day 21 (Johnson-Venkatesh and Umemori, 2010). To test the role of TSP1 at peak expression, we conducted additional recordings at early postnatal stages (P9-P11). However, these recordings revealed no effect of TSP1 deletion on AMPAR/NMDAR ratios (Suppl Fig. 1). Together, these findings indicate that excitatory synaptic transmission is largely intact at CA3➔CA1 synapses in TSP1 KO mice.

Altered synaptic transmission in CA2 PNs in TSP1 KO mice

Imaging and electrophysiology data suggest that TSP1 does not play a necessary role regulating synapse numbers or synaptic transmission in CA1. To explore the possibility that TSP1 function may be circuit–specific, we next asked whether TSP1 loss might affect synaptic transmission in neighboring hippocampal field CA2. Similar to CA1, confocal analysis showed no significant alterations in AMPAR localization or excitatory synapse numbers in field CA2 SR of TSP1 KO mice (Fig. 2A-D). We next performed whole-cell patch-clamp recordings from CA2 PNs, evoking postsynaptic responses with a stimulating electrode placed in SR, where CA2 PNs receive excitatory synaptic inputs primarily fromCA3 PNs (Fig. 2E). Strikingly, we found a significant increase in AMPAR/NMDAR ratios in CA2 PNs from TSP1 KO mice (Fig. 2F). We also observed a significant decrease in PPR (Fig. 2G), indicative of increased glutamate release probability.

A substantial fraction of the extrahippocampal inputs CA2 PNs receive impinge onto basal dendrites in SO. These include afferents from hypothalamic nuclei, which convey critical information relevant to social interaction and novelty (Chevaleyre and Piskorowski, 2016; Smith et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2020). To assess the regulation of these inputs by TSP1, we obtained additional whole-cell patch-clamp recordings from CA2 PNs, this time placing the stimulating electrode in SO (Fig. 3A). We observed normal AMPAR/NMDAR ratios (Fig. 3B), but reduced EPSC PPR (Fig. 3C). To assess possible changes in inhibitory transmission, we recorded evoked IPSCs in CA2 PNs. AMPAR/IPSC ratios were unchanged between genotypes (Fig. 3D). However, IPSC PPR were significantly smaller in TSP1 KO mice (Fig. 3E), consistent with increased GABA release probability.

Loss of TSP1 affects perineuronal nets in CA2

A possible explanation to the selective synaptic alterations in CA2 of TSP1 KO mice may involve perineuronal nets (PNNs). PNNs are specialized ECM which surround the somata of parvalbumin (PV)-positive interneurons (INs) (Favuzzi et al., 2017; Fawcett et al., 2022; Mattioni et al., 2024). In CA2, PNNs gate synaptic transmission and plasticity onto PNs as well (Carstens et al., 2016; Carstens and Dudek, 2019). We hypothesized that loss of the ECM-associated glycoprotein TSP1 may affect PNN organization. To test this possibility, we examined PNN density with Wisteria floribunda agglutinin (WFA). We found that WFA staining intensity was decreased in CA2 of TSP1 KO mice compared with WT controls, especially in stratum oriens (SO, Fig. 4A-D). Noteworthy, lack of TSP1 did not affect PNNs in PV+ INs in CA3, CA2 or in CA1 (Fig. 4E-H).

Severely disrupted social behavior in TSP1 KO mice

Growing evidence points to area CA2 as a crucial hub regulating affective and social memory (Hitti and Siegelbaum, 2014; Dudek et al., 2016; Oliva et al., 2020). The specific synaptic deficits observed in field CA2 of TSP1 KO mice suggested a potential involvement of TSP1 signaling in social function. To test whether loss of TSP1 affected social interaction and memory we applied the well-characterized three-chamber test (Kaidanovich-Beilin et al., 2011)(Fig. 5A). In the first phase of the test, we found that TSP1 KO mice showed a dramatic lack of preference for interacting with a conspecific of the same sex vs. an inanimate object compared to their WT counterparts (Fig. 5B-D). TSP1 KO mice spent significantly less time exploring a peer (Fig. 5C). While WT mice showed very significant preference for the mouse vs. the object, TSP1 KO mice showed no detectable preference in this test (Fig. 5B, C). Total exploration time was reduced in TSP1 KO mice (Fig. 5D). In the subsequent social discrimination test, TSP1 KO mice showed an apparent reduction in social memory (Fig. 5E-G). TSP1 KO mice spent similar time as WT controls exploring the familiar mouse, but explored the novel mouse significantly less time than their WT counterparts (Fig. 5E-G). While WT mice showed marked preference for the novel mouse, TSP1 KO mice displayed no preference between familiar and novel mice (Fig. 5G). Overall, TSP1 KO mice spent significantly less time interacting with mice in this phase of the test (Fig. 5H). Together, these results suggest that TSP1 deletion severely disrupts sociability in mice, and hint at potential social memory defects in these mice. However, the limited social exploration TSP1 KO mice exhibited in the initial phase of the experiment prevents the conclusive interpretation of the social discrimination test in TSP1 KO mice.

Affective behavior is altered in TSP1 KO mice

We next tested whether other affective and cognitive tasks were impacted in TSP-1 KO mice. In the open field test (Fig. 6A), TSP1 KO mice showed significantly reduced percentage of time spent in the center of the arena compared to WT controls (Fig. 6B), which suggested thigmotactic behavior and heightened anxiety. Total locomotion was similar between TSP1 KO and WT mice (Fig. 6C) during the test, and both WT and TSP1 KO mice showed similarly decreased locomotion in the open field arena over consecutive sessions, with no significant interaction between genotype and time in the habituation test (Suppl. Fig. 2A), suggesting no bias due to locomotion alterations on anxiety testing. To further assess anxiety in TSP1 KO mice, we applied the light/dark transition test (Fig. 6D), used to measure anxiety behavior induced by bright light in rodents (Crawley and Goodwin, 1980; Haile et al., 2023). TSP1 KO mice spent more time in the light compartment relative to their WT counterparts (Fig. 6E), which surprisingly suggests reduced anxiety in bright lit conditions. Latency to enter the dark zone was not significantly affected in TSP1 KO mice (Fig. 6F). In the forced swim test (FST, Fig. 6G), frequently applied to measure despair-like behavior in rodents, TSP1 KOs spent less time immobile than WT mice (Fig. 6H), while their latency to immobility did not differ between genotypes (Fig. 6I). These findings indicate that TSP1 deletion induces altered responses to different emotional stressors.

Spatial and object recognition memory is intact in TSP1 KO mice

In the spatial novelty preference Y-maze test (Fig. 7A), a task used to measure spatial working memory which relies on intact CA1 function, TSP1 KO performed comparably to their WT counterparts (Fig. 7B). The total number of crossings was similar between genotypes (Suppl. Fig. 2B). We next assessed long-term object recognition memory using the novel object recognition task (Fig. 7C), which involves hippocampal circuits as well as cortical areas. TSP1 KO mice performed at comparable levels relative to their WT counterparts (Fig. 7D). Total object exploration time and locomotion during training and test were not significantly different between genotypes (Suppl. Fig. 2C-F).

Discussion

A small number of astrocyte-derived signaling molecules are known to modulate synapse formation and function. Glypicans, for example, induce excitatory synapse formation through the AMPAR subunit GluA1 (Allen et al., 2012). The astrocyte-secreted ECM glycoprotein TSP1, in contrast, promotes the formation of ultrastructurally normal, but post-synaptically silent, synapses lacking AMPARs (Christopherson et al., 2005; Eroglu et al., 2009). Yet, the regulation of synapses, brain circuits and behavior by TSP1 in mice is relatively unexplored. Here we show that TSP1 KO mice exhibit severely impaired social interaction, along with altered anxiety- and depression-related behavior, while spatial and object recognition memory remain intact. At the synaptic level, we found an alteration in synaptic transmission in hippocampal field CA2, which regulates social behavior (Meira et al., 2018; Oliva et al., 2020), but not CA1, involved in spatial and episodic memory, in TSP1 KO mice. Intriguingly, we observed a simultaneous decrease in AMPAR PPR and an increase in AMPAR/NMDAR ratios, suggesting both enhanced post-synaptic potentiation and presynaptic glutamate release at excitatory synapses in CA2 SR. In CA2 SO, PPR was decreased both at excitatory and inhibitory synapses. Taken together, our findings demonstrate that TSP1 is a critical regulator of social behavior, and suggest that TSP1 acts by modulating synaptic transmission in hippocampal field CA2.

CA2 is distinguished from adjacent hippocampal fields CA1 and CA3 by its unique gene expression profile, connectivity and regulation by hormones (Dudek et al., 2016). Pyramidal neurons in CA2 receive synaptic inputs from adjacent CA3, dentate gyrus and entorhinal cortex through their apical dendrites in SR and stratum lacunosum-moleculare (Chevaleyre and Siegelbaum, 2010). Additionally, they are unique among hippocampal fields in that they also receive connections from hypothalamic nuclei, which convey information related to novelty and social events (Chevaleyre and Piskorowski, 2016). CA2 PNs also establish recurrent collaterals with ipsilateral CA2, particularly in SO (Insausti et al., 2023). CA2 PNs show uniquely high threshold for longterm potentiation (LTP) (Simons et al., 2009; Evans et al., 2018; Carstens and Dudek, 2019), likely reflecting differences in the mechanisms underlying social memory from other forms of memory (Dudek et al., 2016; Oliva, 2022). CA2 PNs are critically regulated by local inhibitory circuits (Piskorowski and Chevaleyre, 2013; Botcher et al., 2014; Loisy et al., 2022), and inhibitory synaptic plasticity strongly controls CA2 output and social memory processes (Piskorowski et al., 2016; Dominguez et al., 2019).

A key element regulating CA2 physiology are PNNs, which in CA2 ensheathe PNs, in contrast to other hippocampal and cortical areas in which they are restricted to PV+ interneurons (Carstens and Dudek, 2019). PNNs limit diffusion of extracellular molecules and membrane proteins (including receptor trafficking), thereby gating LTP in CA2 PNs (Carstens et al., 2016). Consequently, PNNs can control CA2-dependent memory processes (Fawcett et al., 2022). Here, we observed a reduction in PNN density in dorsal CA2 in TSP1 KO mice compared to control mice. Reduced PNN density may account for the decrease in both EPSC and IPSC PPR observed in TSP1 KO mice, consistent with reported decreases in IPSC PPR at inhibitory synapses between Purkinje cells and deep cerebellar nuclei neurons upon PNN digestion (Hirono et al., 2018). These changes may ultimately distort the threshold for synaptic potentiation at CA2 synapses, subsequently interfering with social behavior, in TSP1-deficient mice.

In the adult brain, TSP1 signaling remains basally latent, but can be reactivated by astrocyte activity. In the striatum, for example, astrocytic GABA B receptor activation enhances TSP1 release, driving synaptogenesis and hyperactive behavior (Nagai et al., 2019). It is therefore conceivable that TSP1 expression can be reactivated in other behaviorally-relevant conditions in other brain regions. The robust social deficits we observed in TSP1 KO mice suggest a role of acute, activity-dependent TSP1 signaling on synaptic transmission in hippocampal field CA2. Alternatively, the specific regulation of CA2 physiology and social behavior by TSP1 we describe here may reflect early developmental consequences of constitutive deletion. Future studies will disentangle constitutive vs acute roles of TSP1 in the context of social behavior, as well as other brain functions. The association between CA2 dysfunction and social alterations is well established (Hitti and Siegelbaum, 2014; Chevaleyre and Piskorowski, 2016; Dudek et al., 2016; Oliva, 2022), therefore, the synaptic alterations in CA2 described herein offer a rationale for the defective sociability observed in TSP1 KO mice. However, our findings do not rule out a possible contribution of TSP1 to the normal functioning of other brain regions regulating social behavior, such as the medial prefrontal cortex (Li et al., 2025).

Reduced TSP1 expression has been reported in Fmr1 KO mice and C58/J mice, rodent models used to study Fragile X syndrome (FXS) and ASD (Moy et al., 2008), respectively, which share the impaired social behavior observed in TSP1 KO mice (Cheng et al., 2016; Baron-Mendoza et al., 2018). THBS1 has been identified as an ASD-associated gene in humans (Lu et al., 2014; Lim et al., 2017). However, no changes in TSP1 serum levels were observed between children diagnosed with ASD and neurotypical controls (Karatoprak et al., 2024), suggesting that local, circuit-specific TSP1 may be critical in the context of ASD. Our findings highlight a role for TSP1 in the regulation of hippocampal circuits relevant to social behavior and memory, and add additional mechanistic evidence underlying the possible link between TSP1 malfunction and ASD.

The GPI-anchored, epilepsy and intellectual disability-associated protein α2δ-1 is the best characterized TSP1 receptor in the brain (Eroglu et al., 2009), as well as a key molecular target of gabapentinoid drugs (Gee et al., 1996). Although α2δ-1 was initially identified as a VGCC auxiliary subunit, increasing evidence points to additional, VGCC-independent α2δ-1 functions. We recently identified α2δ-1 as an extracellular AMPAR interactor, the loss of which affects plasticity-dependent synaptic AMPAR trafficking, spatial memory and seizure susceptibility (Leana-Sandoval et al., 2024). While α2δ-1’s expression is relatively low at hippocampal field CA2, other members of the α2δ family, chiefly α2δ- 3, are enriched in CA2 and hence may play a similar role at CA2 synapses. Loss of function mutations in α2δ-3 are associated with ASD in humans (De Rubeis, He et al., 2014; Iossifov et al., 2014; Satterstrom et al., 2020), and deletion of α2δ-3 produces ASD-related behavioral deficits in mice (Landmann et al., 2019; Shao et al., 2023). Interestingly, ASD-associated mutations identified in α2δ-1 and α2δ-3 do not affect calcium channels (Haddad et al., 2024), suggesting that ASD-relevant α2δ functions are VGCC-independent. While the molecular pathological mechanisms underlying the contribution of mutant α2δ- to ASD are still unknown, this work suggests the involvement of a circuit-specific regulation of synaptic transmission by TSP1/α2δ signaling.

Collectively, the present findings establish TSP1 as a critical regulator of synaptic transmission in CA2 and social behavior in mice, and suggest that disrupted CA2 PNNs may underly the synaptic and behavioral alterations observed in TSP1 KO mice. Our work provides additional support for a model where latent astrocyte-secreted factors like TSP1 can be acutely re-activated during highly demanding cognitive and emotional tasks and suggests that TSP1 may be a suitable molecular target for interventions to tackle neurodevelopmental conditions including ASD.
