The independent and combined effects of smoking and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease on body mass index trajectories
Authors: Spencer J Keene, Johanna H M Driessen, Rachel E Jordan, Alice Sitch, Peymane Adab, Frits M E Franssen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLow body mass index (BMI) is a common feature of severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) but in the general population, cigarette smoking is also associated with low body weight. Many people with COPD remain smokers after diagnosis, and it is unclear whether low BMI is because of the disease itself or its most common risk factor. We aim to assess the independent and combined effects of smoking and COPD on BMI trajectories. 27,651 patients without COPD and 25,990 with COPD from The Health Improvement Network (2005–2019) were grouped into: never-smokers, former smokers, sustained quitters, intermittent smokers, and continuous smokers (ten total COPD-smoking status groups). BMI trajectories over 10-year time horizon were modeled by these status groups using multivariable mixed-effect models adjusted for age (in continuous years), sex, Townsend score (a measure of material deprivation), alcohol consumption (yes/no), exacerbation history (yes/no, only for COPD patients) and any history of asthma, cancer, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, or cardiovascular disease (yes/no). Individuals with COPD who smoked at baseline (intermittent, sustained quitter, or continuous smokers) had a lower initial BMI (27.1 kg/m² [26.9–27.3]; 26.6 [26.4–26.9]; 26.2 [26.0–26.4], respectively) than non-COPD controls in the same smoking categories (28.0 [26.6–28.2]; 27.6 [27.2–27.9]; 26.7 [26.4–26.9]). Current smokers had lower initial BMIs than never and former smokers, regardless of COPD status. In individuals with COPD, compared to former smokers, continuous smokers lost weight faster (-0.071 kg/m²/year [-0.097 to -0.045]; p < 0.001), while quitters gained weight (0.266 [0.233 to 0.298]; p < 0.001). Non-COPD controls showed similar but less pronounced patterns when continuous smokers and quitters (-0.059 [-0.090 to -0.028] and 0.213 [0.173 to 0.254], respectively; both p < 0.001) were compared to former smokers. Those with a baseline BMI of < 30 also showed a decrease in longitudinal BMI, especially among COPD patients. COPD patients had lower baseline BMI than controls, but BMI trajectories were similar between groups, with continuous smokers losing weight faster and quitters gaining weight. These findings suggest that smoking behaviour significantly influences weight loss in COPD, emphasizing its importance in clinical evaluations and nutritional support consultations. Introduction

Low body mass index (BMI) was one of the earliest recognized extrapulmonary features in individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)1. It is associated with more severe stages of the disease2 and is identified as an independent contributor to COPD mortality3. In addition, weight gain by nutritional supplementation is one of few interventions associated with improved survival among COPD patients4. Low BMI in COPD results from a variable combination of reduced dietary intake5, increased work of breathing6, and increased energy expenditure at rest and during exercise7. Thus, prior evidence has suggested that disease processes primarily drive weight loss in COPD. On the other hand, in the general population, cigarette smoking is associated with weight loss - attributed to the effects of nicotine on increasing metabolic rate, decreasing metabolic efficiency, or appetite suppression - while quitting smoking often results in weight gain8. Cigarette consumption is a main cause of COPD, and many people remain smokers after diagnosis. Therefore, it is difficult to disentangle to what extent low BMI in those with COPD is because of the disease itself and/or its most common risk factor. Although it was previously demonstrated that trajectories in body weight were similar between elderly people with airflow limitation and smokers with normal lung function9, it is still unclear whether continued smoking or disease processes play a primary role in those with COPD.

Our objective was to understand how individual BMI trajectories vary according to smoking status in those with and without COPD to disentangle the role of each factor on nutritional status among those with COPD.

Methods

Study population and measurements

Individuals diagnosed with COPD (diagnosed at 40 years of age or older) with one or more Read codes (Supplementary text), assigned by general practitioners from UK primary care practices that were contributing to The Health Improvement Network database10, were matched by age (+/- one year), sex, and practice at a 1:1 ratio to those without COPD (non-COPD, defined as no COPD Read codes in their records). For computational efficiency, all COPD and non-COPD controls were sampled using a 25% random sample by practice for practices with at least 20 registered individuals, otherwise, all individuals from the practice were included. For both groups, the date of the first recorded BMI measurement had to be within one year of the baseline smoking status assessment and individuals had to have at least one additional BMI measurement at least 3 months after the initial measurement to be included. Follow-up for those with COPD diagnosed before 1 April 2006 began from 15 months after practice registration whereas, for those diagnosed after this date, follow-up was 15 months after their diagnosis date (to ensure relevant baseline information is recorded). Non-COPD controls were assigned the index date of their matched individual with COPD. Individuals with non-missing smoking status were followed from their index date until the earliest of their date of death, the date they transferred out of the practice, the last practice collection date, or 30th September 2019. Five smoking status groups were assigned to both individuals with and without COPD, defined as follows: never-smoker, former smoker (i.e. not a current smoker but has any history of smoking), intermittent smoker (i.e. those who had at least one transition from current to former smoking as well as a transition from former to current smoking during follow-up), continuous (i.e. current smokers at baseline who did not quit during follow-up), and sustained quitters (i.e. current smokers at baseline who quit smoking during follow-up and remained former smokers throughout the remaining follow-up).

Statistical analysis

We built linear mixed-effect models (to account for bias from missing repeated BMI measurements) separately for COPD and non-COPD groups to investigate BMI trajectories over a 10-year time horizon in each smoking status group, using former smokers as the reference group. Each model was fitted with a random intercept and a random time slope with groupings at the individual and practice level and contained smoking group, time, and an interaction term between the smoking status group and time (status group×time) The following baseline variables were adjusted for based clinical knowledge of their associations with smoking status and BMI: age (in continuous years), sex, Townsend score (a measure of material deprivation that is calculated using a combination of four census variables for any geographical area), alcohol consumption (yes/no), exacerbation history (yes/no, only for COPD patients) and any history of asthma, cancer, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, or cardiovascular disease (yes/no). We repeated this procedure by building new mixed-effect models for COPD patients with prior exacerbations and by baseline subgroups of age (≥ 65 years old versus < 65 years old), sex, obesity status (BMI ≥ 30 versus < 30 kg/m2), and diabetes status. We did not model fat-free muscle mass and did not take account of pack-years, years of smoking cessation, and COPD severity (e.g. lung function) as these variables are not commonly measured in routine data.

Results

53,641 individuals were included from across the UK, including 25,990 individuals with a Read code for COPD and 27,651 without one (Table 1). The median follow-up was 7.8 years (interquartile range: 5.4–10.2). Those with COPD were more deprived, had a lower BMI, were less likely to be never smokers and have diabetes, and were more likely to have excessive alcohol consumption than non-COPD controls. Individuals with COPD and with current smoking behaviour at baseline (i.e. intermittent, sustained quitter, continuous smokers) had a lower initial BMI level (27.1 kg/m2, 95% confidence interval [26.9 to 27.3]; 26.6 [26.4 to 26.9]; 26.2 [26.0 to 26.4], respectively) than non-COPD controls from the same smoking groups (28.0 [26.6 to 28.2]; 27.6 [27.2 to 27.9]; 26.7 [26.4 to 26.9]) (Fig. 1). Those with current smoking behaviour at baseline had lower initial BMI levels than never and former smokers regardless of COPD status. Among those with COPD, compared to individuals who were former smokers, those who continued to smoke lost weight more rapidly (-0.071 kg/m2/year [-0.097 to -0.045]; p < 0.001) while those who quit smoking gained weight (0.266 [0.233 to 0.298]; p < 0.001). For non-COPD controls, the trajectories were less pronounced (-0.059 [-0.090 to -0.028] and 0.213 [0.173 to 0.254], respectively) but still different (p < 0.001) compared to non-COPD controls who were former smokers. COPD patients with prior exacerbations showed similar BMI patterns to all included COPD patients (Fig. 1). We also examined BMI trajectories according to subgroups of age, sex, baseline BMI, and diabetes status (Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5). Older individuals (≥ 65 years old) with COPD tended to have more pronounced weight loss (Fig. 2). There was very little difference in BMI trajectories between males and females (Fig. 3). Importantly, those with a BMI of < 30 at baseline, especially those with COPD, still lost weight with continuous smoking behaviour and gained weight after quitting (Fig. 4). Those with diabetes had higher BMI levels, especially those with COPD (Fig. 5).

Discussion

We found that people with COPD had a slightly lower baseline BMI than non-COPD controls with the same smoking behaviour. Smoking status was more influential; former smokers from both groups had the highest BMI, followed by never-smokers, with the lowest baseline BMI measurements observed among COPD patients who were recently or currently smoking. There was a significantly more rapid decline in BMI among COPD patients who continued to smoke. Conversely, sustained quitters with COPD experienced a significant increase in BMI over time.

On average, body weight tended to decrease over time in all people regardless of COPD status. This is similar to findings in another study of older (mean age about 73 years) men and women, some with obstructive lung disease9. This is in contrast to many general population studies which include younger people and have shown that overall, adults gain approximately 0.3 kg to 1.2 kg/year11–17. Weight gain is more pronounced in healthy young adults whereas with increasing age, the rate of weight gain decreases18. Age-related decline in body weight in older adults is often due to decreases in skeletal muscle, fat mass, lean mass, and bone mineral content9. Additionally, general practitioners may be more likely to perform weight measurements if they are worried about a patient losing weight over time in the presence of disease. Overall, the estimated effects of smoking and COPD on BMI trajectories shown in the present study are relevant in older patients who experience an age-related decline in body weight.

In this study, the greater increase in BMI after quitting smoking in those with COPD seemed to be mirrored by the greater rate of BMI decline among continuous smokers with COPD when compared to those without COPD. Importantly, we also found this to be true in those who were not obese at baseline, suggesting that those at relatively healthy or below healthy weight can still lose weight with continuous smoking behaviour. These findings agree with evidence that nicotine may have a short-term effect on increasing energy expenditure and a reduction in appetite8. This effect of smoking on body weight reduction may be worsened in COPD because there is more work associated with breathing when a patient has impaired lung function and respiratory symptoms19. In addition, dyspnoea while eating or preparing meals as well as COPD-related depression and anxiety may alter a patient’s eating habits and appetite20,21 and result in weight loss. Also, low-grade systemic inflammation22,23 and prolonged physical inactivity24 may result in a loss in skeletal muscle mass and contribute to muscle wasting and reduced body weight25. On the other hand, smoking cessation is associated with a short-term increased dietary intake8 and the effect may even remain past one year of follow-up26. Weight gain after quitting may be due to an increased energy intake and decreased energy expenditure8. People with COPD who quit smoking may gain weight more rapidly than similar people without COPD of quit, likely because of a rebound effect after weight loss that may have been due to both smoking and COPD Quitting smoking may not only improve nutritional status in COPD patients but it also alters the natural history of COPD27. Smoking cessation was also accompanied by yearly weight gain in a nationally representative sample of nearly 17 thousand Australian adults who were studied between 2006 and 201428. Importantly, this weight gain was not associated with an increased risk of chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease and cancer, nor did it attenuate the mortality benefit of cessation.

Several studies have investigated the effect of smoking on body composition in COPD9,29–32. However, results are mixed as to whether smoking or COPD status plays a primary role in body composition changes over time or if these two factors have a joint effect. A longitudinal analysis of 3,075 Health, Aging and Body Composition (ABC) cohort participants from the US showed that smoking may compromise body composition regardless of COPD status attributing this similarity to a common smoking-related insult earlier in life9. The trajectories of body weight over time were similar to patients from THIN, however, our conclusion is more consistent with a joint effect of smoking and COPD on BMI trajectories. This may be due to our ability to distinguish smoking status groups within COPD which was not possible in the Health-ABC cohort due to a smaller sample (n = 260). Also, the Health-ABC study included only participants aged 70–79 years old and COPD was not clinically diagnosed. On the other hand, a study using the ECLIPSE cohort found that the percentage of patients with an increase (> 0.5 kg/m^2) or a decrease (< -0.5 kg/m^2) in fat-free mass index and fat-mass index over three years was similar for COPD patients and smoking and non-smoking controls29, which suggests that smoking and COPD may not have a large role in changes in body composition. Also, ECLIPSE participants with a continuous decline in fat-free mass had only a slightly higher number of pack-years of smoking compared to the remaining patients (45 versus 43, respectively)33. However, changes in smoking status were not examined in ECLIPSE. Next, In 32 smokers (smoking history > 10 pack/years), 32 mild/moderate COPD (current smokers or former smokers), and 32 never smokers, smoking and early COPD both jointly reduced body composition (fat-free mass) in a Brazilian population30. Finally, in 64 patients with stable COPD, the decline in fat-free mass was associated with continued smoking31, although no individuals without COPD were included. These studies were unable to assess smoking behaviour in COPD and people without COPD due to low sample sizes and often lacked generalisability. Also, some studies were cross-sectional while the others often had shorter follow-up times with relatively few longitudinal body composition measurements.

Strengths of the current study include participants having characteristics similar to those with access to primary care. We built models that showed within individual BMI measurements over time and summarised these into trajectories according to subgroups defined by smoking and COPD status. Previous studies often showed cross-sectional mean BMI measurements at different time points. There are several limitations in this study. First, the recording of smoking may be less frequent in the non-COPD cohort unless some of these individuals were also suffering from a smoking-related condition34 that rewards GPs for recording smoking status. People without COPD may quit smoking, and these may not be recorded, biasing BMI trajectories of smokers toward a less rapid decline. Next, we are relying on reported weight measures, and know that GPs do not routinely weigh patients and are more likely to weigh them in the context of chronic disease or if worried about excess weight gain or loss. The likelihood that GPs weigh patients may also vary by the type of disease individuals are diagnosed with. For instance, we found that people without COPD are more likely to have diabetes than COPD patients. People without COPD may be more likely to be treated with diabetes as their primary condition where weight is a bigger concern for general practitioners and so these patients would receive more weight measurements (and were more likely to meet our inclusion criteria). Therefore, these patients would be more likely to be included in our study. Next, smoking tends to increase abdominal obesity but not overall obesity35. This indicates that smokers have different fat distributions and/or decreased muscle mass which may be more appropriate measures than BMI when describing adverse health. BMI may not be as clinically relevant for COPD patients as muscle mass and skeletal muscle mass (i.e., muscle wasting). Cachexia and muscle wasting is a common and reversible feature in COPD that contributes to decreased exercise capacity and health-related quality of life, and increased mortality36. However, muscle and fat measurements are not possible in certain healthcare settings. With this in mind, two different definitions of cachexia were established37: Consensus cachexia (“weight-loss > 5% in 12-months or low BMI in addition to 3/5 of decreased muscle strength, fatigue, anorexia, low FFMI, and inflammation”) and weight loss cachexia (“weight-loss > 5% or weight-loss > 2% (if low BMI) in 12-months”). Importantly, cachexia defined using either definitions resulted in subgroups that had a similarly strong and independent association with mortality. Finally, and related to the above, we did not take account of pack-years, years of smoking cessation, COPD severity, and did not model fat-free muscle mass as these variables are not commonly measured in routine data.

In conclusion, individuals with COPD had lower baseline BMI than non-COPD controls. However, BMI trajectories across smoking status groups were similar for COPD and non-COPD controls, with continuous smokers losing weight faster and quitters gaining weight, on average. These patterns were not restricted to those who were already obese at baseline. The results of this study suggest that weight loss in COPD is significantly influenced by smoking behaviour, not just by the disease itself, and this should be emphasised in clinical evaluations and nutritional support consultations.
