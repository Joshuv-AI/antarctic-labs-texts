Single-Stage Flexor Tendon Reconstruction Using Tendon Grafting Versus Turnover Tendon Split-Lengthening Technique
Authors: Eleni Karagergou, Maria Baxevanou, Anastasia Moysiadou, Alexandros Givissis, Nikolaos Platon Sachinis, Panagiotis Givissis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the long-term functional outcomes and complication rates of single-stage flexor tendon reconstruction using either traditional tendon grafting or the turnover split-lengthening technique in patients with flexor tendon injuries that were unsuitable for primary repair.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 14 patients who underwent single-stage flexor tendon reconstruction between 2014 and 2022. Seven patients received palmaris longus tendon grafts, and seven underwent the turnover split-lengthening procedure. Inclusion criteria comprised adequate soft tissue coverage and absence of excessive scarring; two-stage reconstructions were excluded. Functional outcomes were assessed by total active range of motion (AROM) using the modified Strickland formula, grip strength, and patient-reported function via the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (QuickDASH) score. Complications, including tendon adhesions, joint contractures, and bowstringing, were recorded.ResultsAt a mean follow-up of 5.7 ± 3 years, the mean total AROM was 67.6 ± 14.5%, with outcomes classified as excellent in three patients, good in four, fair in six, and poor in one. Mean grip strength was 90.7 ± 9.2% of the contralateral hand, and the mean QuickDASH score was 2.9 ± 5. There were no statistically significant differences between tendon grafting and turnover split-lengthening in AROM, grip strength, or QuickDASH scores (P = .69, .59 and .26, respectively). However, a greater proportion of patients treated with tendon grafting achieved excellent or good outcomes according to the Strickland criteria (5/7 vs 2/7); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance. Complications were observed in 50% of patients, predominantly in those with suboptimal functional results, but did not differ by reconstruction technique.ConclusionsSingle-stage flexor tendon reconstruction using either tendon grafting or turnover split-lengthening, provides comparable functional outcomes, grip strength, and patient-reported satisfaction. The turnover technique is a viable alternative, particularly when the palmaris longus is absent or donor-site morbidity is a concern. Both techniques are associated with a high incidence of complications, emphasizing the challenges of secondary tendon reconstruction.Type of study/level of evidenceTherapeutic IV.

Primary and prompt repair of divided flexor tendons in the hand, followed by an early active motion (EAM) therapy protocol, is the gold standard for treating these injuries.1 Advances in tenorrhaphy techniques and the recognition that a strong four- or six-strand core repair allows early active motion have contributed to improved functional outcomes.2 However, in certain circumstances, primary flexor tendon repair is not possible. In cases of failed primary repair because of rupture or adhesions, closed ruptures (such as “jersey finger”), or neglected injuries where myostatic shortening has occurred, secondary flexor tendon reconstruction remains an option for selected and highly motivated patients who wish to restore the full active range of motion (AROM) of their injured finger.3

Options for secondary flexor tendon reconstruction, depending on the zone of injury, include intercalated tendon grafts, tendon transfers, and lengthening procedures.4 All these techniques can be performed in one or two stages. One-stage grafting is indicated when no extensive scarring or contracture of the finger is present; otherwise, two-stage grafting is recommended.5 In 2013, Cerovac and Miranda described a turnover split-lengthening technique in which half of the tendon is harvested and folded over a pivot point 0.5–1 cm from the end of the proximal stump, thereby bridging the tendinous gap.6 This is particularly useful when the palmaris longus is absent and harvesting a distant donor tendon, such as one from the lower limb, is undesirable. However, there are no published data directly comparing tendon grafting with the turnover split-lengthening technique for secondary flexor tendon reconstruction.

Our retrospective study aims to evaluate the long-term functional outcomes and complication rates of single-stage flexor tendon reconstruction by using either traditional tendon grafting or the turnover lengthening procedure.

Materials and Methods

All patients who underwent flexor tendon reconstruction over a 9-year period between 2014 and 2022 were retrospectively reviewed. Demographic and clinical data were extracted from patients’ medical records following approval by the local scientific committee. Patients with divided flexor digitorum profundus or flexor pollicis longus (FPL) tendons who underwent single-stage flexor tendon reconstruction using either tendon grafting or the turnover split-lengthening technique were included. Patients with extensive scarring and an inadequate pulley system requiring two-stage reconstruction were excluded. Data collected included age, mechanism of injury, zone of injury, time from injury to surgery, type of reconstruction, and duration of follow-up. Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients for publication of this data and accompanying images.

Operative technique

Bruner incisions were used to expose the flexor tendons, followed by removal of the pseudotendon and debridement of the tendon stumps, while attempting to preserve at least 50% of the A2 and A4 pulleys (Fig. 1). In cases requiring tendon grafting, the palmaris longus (PL) tendon was harvested and used to bridge the tendinous gap by direct end-to-end tenorrhaphy with a four-strand Kessler core suture and a running epitendinous suture, using 4-0 and 6-0 polypropylene, respectively (Fig. 2).Figure 1Identification of the proximal and distal stumps of the flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) while preserving the A2 and A4 pulleys.Figure 2Retrieval of the PL tendon graft through the flexor sheath to perform proximal and distal end-to-end tenorrhaphies.

When the PL was absent, or when considered preferable by the surgeon —for example, to avoid distal donor-site morbidity and additional scarring or to reduce operative time — a turnover split-lengthening technique was used. This involved creating a reverse L-shaped longitudinal incision at the proximal tendon stump and turning it over around a pivot point located 0.5–1 cm from the end of the proximal stump (Fig. 3). The length of the turnover segment was equal to the measured tendinous gap (Fig. 4). Distal tenorrhaphy was performed using a four-strand Kessler core suture and a running epitendinous suture. Two interrupted 4-0 Prolene sutures were placed at the turnover point to prevent further splitting of the tendon, while avoiding tubularization of the turnover pedicle with a continuous epitendinous suture, as originally described by Cerovac and Miranda.6 After surgery, a dorsal blocking splint was applied for 6 weeks. All patients followed a controlled EAM protocol, based on the modified Belfast regimen, initiated within the first postoperative week and supervised by certified hand therapists. The protocol included, protected passive flexion and active extension within the splint, progressing to active flexion exercises after week 4.Figure 3Illustration of the turnover tendon split-lengthening technique, which involves creating an L-shaped longitudinal split of the proximal tendon stump and turning it over at the pivot point.Figure 4The split tendon used to bridge the tendinous defect.

All patients were invited for follow-up and assessment of their functional outcome. Total AROM was calculated using the modified Strickland formula and subsequently classified according to the Strickland and Glogovac criteria.7,8 Grip strength was measured using a Jamar dynamometer with patients seated, elbows flexed to 90°, and wrists in a neutral position. The average of three trials was recorded for both the injured and noninjured hands. Grip strength percentage was calculated by dividing the grip strength of the injured side by that of the contralateral, noninjured side and multiplying the result by 100.9 Secondary outcomes included patient-reported function assessed using the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (QuickDASH) score and documentation of complications such as bowstringing, tendon adhesions and joint contractures.

Statistics

All data were recorded and analyzed using Python (version 3.13) with the pandas, NumPy, and SciPy libraries. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics and outcomes overall and by treatment group. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range, IQR), as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared between groups using Fisher exact test. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and group comparisons were performed using either independent sample t tests, with Welch’s correction applied when variances were unequal or to account for the small sample size, or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

Discussion

Secondary flexor tendon reconstruction is indicated when primary repair is not possible, such as in cases of delayed presentation, rupture of the initial tenorrhaphy, or segmental tendon loss.10 Traditionally, the decision to proceed with a single- versus two-stage procedure depends on the integrity of the pulley system, the degree of scarring, and the adequacy of soft tissue coverage.4,10 In this study, we included only patients with adequate soft tissue coverage and absence of excessive scarring who were suitable candidates for single-stage reconstruction using either tendon grafting or the turnover split-lengthening technique.

Functional outcomes after secondary reconstruction vary among studies but, in general, are inferior to those achieved after primary tendon repair.11, 12, 13 In a large retrospective case series including 401 patients who underwent single-stage flexor digitorum profundus reconstruction with a tendon graft, excellent or good outcomes according to the American Society for Surgery of the Hand criteria were achieved in 63% of cases.11 Similarly, Moeller et al12 reported excellent-to-good results in 60% and 50% of patients undergoing single-stage and two-stage reconstruction, respectively. In another study, 16 patients who underwent two-stage FPL reconstruction with a tendon graft demonstrated significantly reduced AROM at the interphalangeal joint of the injured thumb compared with the uninjured side (mean AROM 32°, range 10°–70°), along with a mean Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand score of 11.13 In our study, the mean AROM among all patients who underwent single-stage flexor tendon reconstruction, irrespective of the technique used, was 67.6% and half of the patients (7 out of 14) achieved excellent or good outcome according to the Strickland criteria. Our final outcomes are comparable to those reported in previous studies and underscore the suboptimal results typically achieved after secondary reconstruction, in contrast to primary repair.

Recent studies have reported successful outcomes following delayed primary flexor tendon repair when surgery is performed within 3–5 weeks after injury, thereby avoiding the need for secondary tendon reconstruction and its associated complications.2,14 Satisfactory outcomes of direct flexor tendon repair have also been reported when surgery was performed more than 3 months after trauma; however, in these cases, adjunctive procedures such as musculotendinous junction release or Z-lengthening were often required to achieve adequate tendon excursion.15,16 In our current practice, the upper time limit for direct flexor tendon repair is approximately 3–4 weeks after injury, depending on the zone of injury. Further high-quality studies are required to establish clear indications, timing thresholds, and outcome expectations for delayed primary flexor tendon repair.

Flexor tendon reconstruction is most commonly performed using intercalated tendon grafting. Although intrasynovial grafts have shown superior biologic and biomechanical properties in experimental models, this advantage has not been consistently confirmed clinically, and harvesting such grafts is associated with increased donor-site morbidity.11,17,18 Therefore, the PL tendon remains the most frequently used graft. When absent or of insufficient diameter, alternative graft sources include the plantaris tendon or toe extensor tendons, at the cost of additional morbidity and operative time.19 Various tendon-lengthening procedures have been proposed as alternatives to tendon grafting in an effort to avoid the aforementioned drawbacks. Chong and Chen20 described the use of multiple step cuts at the tendinous portion of the FPL, providing approximately 1 cm lengthening of the proximal stump and limiting its applicability to short gaps of up to 2 cm. Z-lengthening of flexor tendons in zones II or V has also been described, allowing varying degrees of advancement; however, its application may be limited by synovial adhesions or the insertion of the lumbricals.21, 22, 23

The turnover split-lengthening technique described by Cerovac and Miranda6 is based on the knowledge that preservation of 50% of the circumference of a flexor tendon is adequate for strength and function. It was initially applied in two cases with a 2 cm flexor tendon defect and a 1.5 cm extensor tendon defect. Both patients reported good postoperative outcome and returned to daily activities without significant limitations. In a recent experimental study using ovine flexor profundus tendons, the biomechanical testing between tendon turnover tenorrhaphies and direct repairs showed no difference in the load to failure.24 We have been using the turnover lengthening when PL is absent or when additional donor-site morbidity and scarring need to be avoided. In contrast to the originally described technique, in which the donor site and the turnover stump are tubularized with continuous epitendinous sutures, we omit this step to avoid compromising the tendon’s blood supply. In the current study, we show similar functional outcomes with tendon grafting in terms of total AROM, grip strength, and QuickDASH. This technique can be applied in any flexor tendon zone, provided that the turnover point is not located within the confined compartment of flexor zone II, because of the bulkiness of the tendon repair at this site. To overcome this limitation, the elongation rail technique was described, in which the rail segment is fully detached and advanced to bridge the defect , creating two end-to-end tenorrhaphy sites.25

In our patient series, a four-strand core suture combined with an epitendinous suture was selected to secure both the tendon grafts and the turnover pedicles. A six-strand core suture or a Pulvertaft weave technique was not feasible because of the small tendon caliber of the grafts and turnover pedicles. Although four-strand repairs are biomechanically less strong than six-strand constructs, clinical evidence indicates that four-strand core suture techniques provide sufficient tensile strength to support EAM rehabilitation protocols with satisfactory functional outcomes and low rupture rates.26

Postoperative rehabilitation after flexor tendon reconstruction remains controversial. Tonkin et al27 showed that the final motion after tendon grafting was independent of postoperative management, whether it involved immediate controlled motion or immobilization for 3–4 weeks. Although it is generally believed that prolonged immobilization might lead to tendon adhesions, several authors believe that early motion can lead to increased gap between the graft and the flexor tendon stump, thereby recommending a period of postoperative immobilization that ranges from 1 to 3 weeks.5,11 In the present series, all patients followed the same controlled EAM protocol with initial splint protection, allowing comparison between reconstruction techniques without the confounding effect of different rehabilitation regimens.

All patients (7 out of 14) with poor or fair outcomes according to the Strickland and Glogovac criteria presented with complications such as joint contractures, tendon adhesions, or bowstringing, irrespective of the reconstruction method used. This finding highlights other potential factors contributing to suboptimal outcomes, including injury severity, delay to surgery, rehabilitation-related issues, and patient noncompliance.

Despite its retrospective design and small sample size, this study is among the few to directly compare traditional tendon grafting with the turnover tendon split-lengthening technique for single-stage flexor tendon reconstruction. The turnover split-lengthening technique remains a valuable alternative, particularly in the absence of a PL tendon or when donor-site morbidity should be minimized. Future prospective and randomized studies are required to further define the indications and comparative effectiveness of these techniques.
