Enhanced brain delivery of oleanolic acid by GSH-PEGylated liposomes and its amelioration of scopolamine-induced cognitive dysfunction via multi-target regulation
Authors: Yihuo Luo, Xiaokun Hong, Xiaohui Chen, Yanqi Liang, Feng Li, Xian'ai Shi, Xiongmei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oleanolic acid (OA) exhibits pronounced neuroprotective effects in the central nervous system; however, its clinical translation is limited by poor solubility and low bioavailability. To overcome these limitations, we developed glutathione-PEGylated OA liposomes (GSH-PEG-OA-LIP) via a post-insertion method, achieving an load efficiency of 94.2%. In vivo imaging demonstrated that GSH-PEG modification significantly prolonged blood circulation and enhanced brain accumulation compared to unmodified liposomes. In a mouse model of scopolamine (SCOP)-induced cognitive deficit, GSH-PEG-OA-LIP treatment effectively reversed short- and long-term memory impairments. Biochemical analyses revealed that the treatment restored hippocampal cholinergic function by increasing acetylcholine (ACh) levels and choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) activity, while simultaneously alleviating oxidative stress through reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) content and increased superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity. Furthermore, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations confirmed stable interactions between OA and key signaling targets—specifically PKC, PI3Kγ, MEK, and IP3R—suggesting a multi-targeted mechanism of action. Collectively, these results highlight GSH-PEG-OA-LIP as an efficient brain-targeted delivery system and a promising therapeutic strategy for cognitive disorders.

Introduction

Cognitive dysfunction refers to varying degrees of cognitive decline caused by multiple factors, manifested as impairments in memory, language, visual space, execution, and other cognitive domains (Lin, 2018). It is a continuous process spanning from mild cognitive impairment to dementia (Chan et al., 2024), imposing a substantial burden on patients and public health authorities, and becoming a research hotspot in neurology and clinical medicine.

The cholinergic system is closely associated with cognitive function, and cholinergic synapses are even termed “memory synapses” (Barnstedt et al., 2016; Deutsch, 1971). Dunbar et al. (1993) found that hippocampal choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) activity and choline uptake were higher in rats with good water maze performance. Numerous studies have confirmed that anticholinergic drugs significantly impair learning and memory (Gage et al., 2013; Salahudeen et al., 2015; Inge and Arjan, 2010), while M receptor agonists can reverse scopolamine-induced spatial memory deficits. Cholinergic hypofunction is a core pathological feature of Alzheimer's disease (AD), a major cause of cognitive dysfunction (Masliah et al., 2003). Although synthetic cholinergic drugs such as tacrine and donepezil are widely used clinically, their side effects (e.g., nausea, diarrhea, anorexia) limit long-term application (Dunn et al., 2000). Thus, natural active compounds with high safety have attracted widespread attention for treating cognitive disorders.

Pentacyclic triterpenoids derived from plants have gained increasing interest due to their excellent health benefits and safety (Li et al., 2017), exhibiting anti-tumor (Li et al., 2024), anti-oxidative (Yan et al., 2006), and hypoglycemic effects (Lv et al., 2009), among others. Oleanolic acid (OA), a representative pentacyclic triterpenoid, has shown prominent neuroprotective activity: it alleviates oxidative and glycative stress in aging mice (Tsai and Yin, 2012), upregulates ERK and CREB phosphorylation in hippocampal neurons (Yi et al., 2014), and ameliorates scopolamine-induced memory impairment via the TrkB-dependent BDNF-ERK-CREB pathway (Jeon et al., 2017). Its derivative maslinic acid also enhances cognitive function through BDNF activation (Bae et al., 2020). However, OA's clinical translation is severely limited by poor water solubility and extremely low bioavailability (<5%) (Jeon et al., 2017), coupled with the blood-brain barrier (BBB)—a major hurdle for CNS drug delivery due to tightly connected brain capillary endothelial cells restricting paracellular transport.

One of the most important hurdles for drug delivery to the brain is the blood-brain barrier (BBB), in which brain capillary endothelial cells are tightly connected, limiting the paracellular transport. To address this, various sophisticated nanoplatforms, including liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, and biological vesicles, have been developed. Recent advances have highlighted the potential of engineered exosomal macromolecules as natural delivery vehicles, which can cross the BBB through surface modifications to provide neuroprotection and anti-inflammatory effects in Alzheimer's disease (AD) models (Jain et al., 2025). Similarly, the development of innovative lipid-based systems for siRNA delivery underscores the critical need for efficient strategies to overcome BBB restrictive transport and achieve precise gene silencing of pathological factors (Jain et al., 2024). A PEGylated liposome has good biocompatibility and now has been used as an excellent drug carrier in cancer treatment and other fields (Qin et al., 2017; Li et al., 2025). Glutathione (GSH) is an endogenous tripeptide with several important physiological functions. The GSH transporter is preferentially expressed in the central nervous system (CNS) or the BBB, and can promote drug transport to the brain (Gaillard et al., 2012; Lindqvist et al., 2013; Maussang et al., 2016) (Fig. 1a). In the present study, OA (Fig. 1b) was loaded in GSH-coated PEGylated liposomes (GSH-PEG-OA-LIP, Fig. 1c) using an post-insertion method to address OA's solubility and delivery challenges. We analyzed the central drug delivery efficiency of GSH-PEG-OA-LIP and its ameliorative effects on scopolamine-induced cognitive dysfunction in mice via maze behavioral analysis and central cholinergic level detection. In addition, we investigated the potential targets of OA involved in the modulation of the cholinergic signaling pathway using molecular docking and 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations, and to discuss the underlying mechanisms of action.

Materials and methods

Materials

Oleanolic acid (OA), soy lecithin (SPC), scopolamine (SCOP), galantamine (Gala) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). DSPE-PEG2000 was purchased from Solabao Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), acetylcholine (ACh), acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) analysis kits were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Research Institute (Nanjing, China). 1,1′-Dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethyl indotricarbocyanine iodide (DiR) was purchased from US Everbright Inc. Other chemicals or reagents employed were of analytical grade from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), unless otherwise specified.

Animals and treatment

All animals used in this work were handled under the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All possible efforts were made to reduce the suffering and the number of animals used. The experimental protocol and animal handling were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Fuzhou University (2021-SG-054).

Male ICR mice (6 weeks old) were supplied by Shanghai Slac Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Number of animal license: SCXK (HU) 2022-0004). After one week of acclimation, the animals were randomly distributed to the control and the treatment groups of seven and housed in polypropylene rodent cages (3–4 animals each). The animal experiment was performed under standard controlled environmental conditions. The temperature of the animal room was 23–25 °C and the relative humidity ranged from 50 to 70%. The animal room was set on a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle (lit from 6:00 to 18:00). A standard rodent maintenance diet and water were provided to the animal ad libitum.

Preparation of liposome

GSH-PEG-OA-LIP was prepared according to the reported post-insertion method with minor modifications (Gaillard et al., 2012). Briefly, 10 mg OA, 12.5 mg cholesterol, and 100 mg SPC were dissolved in 20 mL absolute ethanol. The organic phase was then injected drop by drop into 20 mL PBS (pH 7.2, 10 mM) and stirred for 2 h at room temperature. The ethanol was removed by vacuum rotary evaporation to obtain the OA-LIP. The formed liposomes were then extruded through 450 and 220 nm filters to homogenize the particle size. GSH-PEGylated liposomes were prepared by improved post-insertion technique reported by Gaillard et al. (2012). In short, GSH reacts with DSPE-PEG2000-MAL in a molar ratio of 2: 1 for 6 h to ensure complete combination. Then, GSH-PEG-DSPE micelles with 4% of total lipids were incubated with preformed liposomes for 3 h, thus preparing GSH-PEGlyated liposomes (GSH-PEG-OA-LIP). For the preparation of PEGylated liposomes, the same post-insertion procedure was followed using DSPE-PEG2000 micelles without the GSH ligand.

To evaluate the brain-delivering efficiency in vivo, fluorescent agent-loaded liposomes were prepared using a similar method as above described. In short, for the DiR-loaded liposomes, 1 mg of DiR was added to the lipid mixture in place of OA. For the OA/DiR dual-loaded liposomes, 10 mg of OA and 1 mg of DiR were incorporated into the lipid phase simultaneously during the initial film formation stage.

The loading efficiency (LE) of OA was determined by separating the unassociated drug using Sephadex G-75 microcolumn centrifugation. Briefly, a microcolumn was prepared using fully swollen Sephadex G-75 in a 2.5 mL syringe (bed volume 1.5 mL) and pre-centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 3 min to remove interstitial liquid. 200 uL of the liposome suspension was loaded onto the column and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 5 min. The collected eluate (purified liposomes) was disrupted with a 10-fold volume of methanol and sonicated for 10 min to ensure complete drug release. The concentration of OA was then quantified by a reported HPLC method (Li et al., 2017). The LE was calculated as the ratio of the drug amount in the eluate to the total drug amount in the original suspension.

Characterization of liposomes

The size and the ζ-potential of the liposomes were measured using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments Ltd., U.K.). The stability of the liposomes in serum was evaluated by monitoring the changes of liposome size in the presence of fetal bovine serum (FBS). Briefly, 100 μL of different liposomes were added into 2 mL of DMEM medium containing 10% FBS and incubated at 37 °C with slow shaking at 50 rpm. The size of the liposomes was measured at different incubation time points.

The in vitro release study was conducted according to a dialysis method with a minor modification (Wang, 2017). Briefly, 1 mL of OA-loaded liposomes was added into a dialysis bag (MW 10 kDa), and the dialysis bag was placed in 40 mL of release buffer (PBS containing 0.4% Tween 80, pH 7.4). The release study was carried out at 37 °C for 96 h with a gentle shaking (50 rpm). 500 μL of the release buffer was sampled at designed time intervals, and replaced with the same volume of fresh release buffer. The amount of OA in dialysis samples was analyzed using the HPLC method after mixing the dialysis samples with methanol (with a volume ratio of 1:3).

Evaluation of the central delivery of liposomes in vivo

Near-infrared fluorescence imaging in vivo was used to evaluate the central delivery capacity of liposomes. The semi-quantification of DiR in liposomes was obtained by empirical estimation based on the loading efficiency of OA (94.2%). The DiR lipsome purified was converted into the concentration of DiR in liposomes according to 94.2% of the amount of DiR put into liposome preparation, and the dose of DiR for intravenous injection in vivo imaging (0.1μg/g body weight) was adjusted according to this concentration. Its central delivery ability was compared with that of free DiR (dispersed in 0.5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose). DiR-loaded liposomes were injected intravenously into mice at a 0.1 μg/g body weight of DiR dose. At 1, 2, 6, 12, and 24 h after DiR administration, the mice were anesthetized and imaged in a live imaging system (Bruker MISE, USA) with a NIR filter set (excitation, 745 nm; emission, 780 nm). Finally, 24 h after injection, the mice were euthanized. The fluorescence intensity of various organs was measured.

Experimental design

After one week of acclimatization, the mice were allocated to 7 groups of 7 animals each using a random number table generated via GraphPad Prism 7.0 to ensure unbiased group allocation. During the randomization process, only the principal investigator was aware of the group allocation to ensure the integrity of randomization. All other researchers and technicians involved in the experiment were kept unaware of the group assignments until the completion of data analysis.

Group 1: The normal control group (CON), the mice were subjected to a 21-day treatment of saline (0.9% NaCl, 15 mL/kg BW, daily, i.p.);

Group 2: The model group (SCOP), the mice were administered with scopolamine (1 mg/kg BW, daily, i.p.) for 21 d + a 21-day treatment of saline (15 mL/kg BW, daily, i.p.) simultaneously;

Group 3: The positive drug control group (Gala), the mice were administered with scopolamine (1 mg/kg BW, daily, i.p.) for 21 d + a 21-day treatment of galantamine (2 mg/kg BW, daily, i.p.) simultaneously;

Group 4: Free OA group (OA), the mice were administered with scopolamine (1 mg/kg BW, daily, i.p.) for 21 d + a 21-day treatment of OA (5 mg/kg BW, daily, i.p.) simultaneously;

Group 5: The GSH-PEG-OA-LIP 1 mg/kg BW group (G-P-O-L 1), the mice were administered with scopolamine (1 mg/kg BW, daily, i.p.) for 21 d + a 21-day treatment of GSH-PEG-OA-LIP (1 mg/kg BW, daily, i.p.) simultaneously;

Group 6: The GSH-PEG-OA-LIP 2 mg/kg BW group (G-P-O-L 2), the mice were administered with scopolamine (1 mg/kg BW, daily, i.p.) for 21 d + a 21-day treatment of GSH-PEG-OA-LIP (2 mg/kg BW, daily, i.p.) simultaneously;

Group 7: The GSH-PEG-OA-LIP 5 mg/kg BW group (G-P-O-L 5), the mice were administered with scopolamine (1 mg/kg BW, daily, i.p.) for 21 d + a 21-day treatment of GSH-PEG-OA-LIP (5 mg/kg BW, daily, i.p.) simultaneously.

Free OA was administered as a suspension in 0.5% (w/v) CMC-Na prepared in sterile saline with a content of 0.5 mg/mL. The mice were given free access to food and water during the experimental period, and body weights were measured daily. Y-maze test

The short-term working memory of the animals was assessed using the Y-maze spontaneous alternation test, which was conducted as previously described with minor modifications (Bae et al., 2019). Briefly, the Y-maze used in this study was composed of three stainless steel arms (30 × 5 × 15 cm, length × width × height) forming a “Y” shape, with a 120°angle between each pair of adjacent arms.

To minimize order bias during behavioral testing, the Y-maze test was conducted in a fixed order: normal control group (CON) → scopolamine model group (SCOP) → treatment groups (Gala, Free OA, G-P-O-L 1, G-P-O-L 2, G-P-O-L 5). This order was preset to avoid cumulative stress or learning effects from sequential testing affecting the results.

All tests were carried out in a quiet and poorly lit room to minimize environmental stress, and the apparatus was thoroughly cleaned with 75% ethanol between animals to eliminate residual olfactory cues. Each mouse was initially placed at the end of one randomly selected arm, and the sequence of arm entries (e.g., 1 → 2 → 3 or 1 → 3 → 2) and total number of entries were recorded continuously over a 5-min period. An “arm entry” was defined as the mouse entering an arm with all four paws crossing the arm threshold, and “alternation” was defined as consecutive entries into three distinct arms. The alternation rate, reflecting short-term working memory ability, was calculated using the formula:Alternation%=Number of alternationsTotalarmentries−2×100.

Morris water maze test

The spatial learning and memory abilities of the mice were assessed using the Morris water maze test. The test was conducted in an opaque water tank with a 1.2 m diameter, the water tank was filled with water (22 ± 1 °C) to a depth of 30 cm. The tank was conceptually divided into four quadrants, with a hidden platform (6 cm diameter, 28 cm high) fixed in one quadrant. The mice underwent 3 training trials per day for 5 consecutive days. In each training test, the animals were put gently into the water at different starting positions distributed equidistantly around the tank.

Each mouse was given a maximum of 60 s to find the hidden platform. After reaching the platform, the animal was allowed to stay for 10 s. If the animal failed to find the platform within 60s, the training test was stopped, with the highest value of 60 s. Then the mouse was placed on the platform and allowed to stay for 10 s, and the time for finding the platform was recorded. Following five days of training, a spatial reference memory probe test was carried out by removing the platform. Each animal was allowed to swim freely in the water for 60 s. The process of the formal test was recorded by video. The video data was analyzed by ImageJ to analyze the number of times each mouse swam across the target quadrant and the swimming time spent in the target quadrant during the test.

Biochemical analysis

Upon completion of the behavioral tests, mice were euthanized under anesthesia. The cerebral cortex and the hippocampus were carefully separated, with tissues allocated for histopathological analysis and for biochemical analysis. The samples were homogenized with saline at 4 °C, and the homogenate was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The levels of AChE, ChAT, ACh, SOD, and MDA were measured according to the manufacturer's protocols (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). All levels of the biochemical indexes were normalized to the total protein contents of the samples. The total protein content was determined by the Bradford method.

Histopathological analysis

For microscopic histopathological analysis, samples of the cerebral cortex or hippocampus were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, then dehydrated using a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylol, and embedded in paraffin. After routine processing, 5 μm paraffin sections were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin before microscopic examination.

Molecular docking

The binding free energies and ligand efficiency were investigated in the molecular docking study. The ligand OA structure was obtained from the ZINC database (https://zinc15.docking.org/, ID: ZINC3785416), and optimized with Chem3D 14.0 to generate a *.pdb file. Total 15 protein molecules closely associated with central cholinergic signaling were selected as potential targets for molecular docking. The 15 PDB files for central cholinergic-related biomacromolecule were obtained from the Brookhaven Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). The crystal structures of extracellular regulated protein kinases (ERK, PDB ID: 6G9J); protein kinase C (PKC, PDB ID: 2I0E); cAMP-response element binding protein (CREB, PDB ID: 5ZKO); Calmodulin-dependent protein kinase (CaMK, PDB ID: 1HKX); brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF, PDB ID: 1B8M); Fos proto-oncogene (c-Fos, PDB ID: 2WT7); Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase γ(PI3Kγ, PDB ID: 4PS7); Heterotrimeric G proteins i/o (Gi/o, PDB ID: 6OIK); Rat sarcoma (Ras, PDB ID: 1X1R); Mitogen-activated extracellular signal-regulated kinase (Mek, PDB ID: 4LMN); Phospholipase C (PLC, PDB ID: 3QR0); Heterotrimeric G proteins q/11 (Gq/11, PDB ID: 6OIJ); Adenylate Cyclase (AC, PDB ID: 4USU); Inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor (IP3R, PDB ID: 3UJ0); Protein kinase A (PKA, PDB ID: 2JDS) were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. The reported ligands and water molecules were removed from the protein complex structure for the later docking simulation using Pymol 2.4.1. Automated docking simulation was performed using AutoDockTools 1.5.6 (ADT). A Lamarckian genetic algorithm method implemented in Autodock 4.2.1 was employed. For docking calculations, Gasteiger charges were added by default, the rotatable bonds were set by the ADT, and all torsions were allowed to rotate. The grid maps were generated by Autogrid 4.2.1 which the grid box size of 120 × 120 × 120 had a default spacing of 0.375 Å. The grid centers (X, Y, Z) were 4.921, 5.008, 42.244 for ERK; 1.848, −29.43, −30.814 for JNK; 35.895, 53.05, 27.892 for PKC; 19.775, −43.734, 168.161 for CREB; 14.668, 24.556, 48.883 for CaMK; −2.055, 25.689, 19.228 for BDNF; 29.399, −24.458, −11.522 for c-Fos; 22.434, 56.410, 26.37 for PI3Kγ; 119.717, 104.178, 101.949 for Gi/o; 5.952, 8.089, 8.085 for Ras; 27.295, 23.778, −15.835 for Mek; −8.616, −30.927, −25.329 for PLC; 110.317, 91.822, 102.052 for Gq/11; −17.029, −31.809, −2.625 for AC; 32.288, −21.256, 37.367 for IP3R; 14.588, 9.400, −0.904 for PKA. The GA parameters are set as the number of GA runs: 50, population size: 300, the maximum number of evals: 2500000, maximum number of generations: 27000. The other parameters were set by defaults of ADT. The conformations were ranked by free energy binding. The conformations with the lowest binding free energy were analyzed and visualized using Pymol 2.4.1.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics simulations of the protein-ligand complexes were performed following molecular docking to assess binding stability. All simulations were performed using AMBER 22, and system topology and coordinate files were generated with the LEaP module employing the Amber FF14SB and GAFF2 force fields (Xu et al., 2026). Each complex was solvated in a TIP3P water box with a 10 Å buffer, and counterions were added to neutralize the system. After energy minimization, NVT and NPT equilibration steps were performed, followed by 100 ns of production MD simulation. Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) values were calculated using the CPPTRAJ module in AMBER 22.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons were performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's test was performed for multiple comparisons to assess statistical significance among groups. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant, with significance levels denoted as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 (vs. the control group or the model group); #P < 0.05, ##P < 0.01, ###P < 0.001 (vs. other experimental groups, if applicable). Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. The specific sample size (n) for each experiment is indicated in the corresponding figure legends. All calculations were performed using GraphPad Prism 7 or Origin 2021.

To validate the use of DiR as a representative tracer for OA-loaded liposomes, we further evaluated the in vivo biodistribution of dual-loaded formulations (DiR/OA-LIP, PEG-DiR/OA-LIP, and GSH-PEG-DiR/OA-LIP) by living images. The brain accumulation trends were consistent with those of DiR-only liposomes (Supplementary Fig. S4), supporting the reliability of DiR-based imaging for assessing OA liposome delivery.

In order to separate the specific brain targeting effect of GSH ligand from the long-term circulation effect of PEGylation, we further evaluated the brain accumulation of PEG-DiR/OA-LIP (GSH-free) as a control by in vivo imaging. Although it is known that PEGylation alone can enhance systemic circulation, our results show that the brain fluorescence intensity of GSH-PEG-DiR/OA-LIP group is significantly higher than that of PEG-DiR/OA-LIP group (Supplementary Fig. S5). This comparison confirms that the superior brain delivery is mainly driven by GSH-mediated endocytosis, not just by the extended circulation time provided by PEG.

The significantly enhanced brain accumulation of GSH-PEGylated liposomes compared to the conventional version underscores the critical role of the GSH ligand. According to previous report (Rip et al., 2014), GSH-mediated brain targeting is primarily driven by the interaction with specific glutathione transporters (such as the Na+-dependent GSH transporters) that are highly and constitutively expressed on the luminal membrane of brain capillary endothelial cells. This receptor-mediated transcytosis pathway has been well-documented as a highly efficient route for bypassing the BBB. By leveraging this established transport mechanism, our formulation successfully increased the cerebral bioavailability of OA, thereby enabling its multi-target neuroprotective effects.

Oxidative stress is an important factor in memory impairment, often leading to neuronal dysfunction in the hippocampus. We analyzed MDA levels and SOD activity in the hippocampus to assess this (Fig. 8d–e).

Compared with the control group, SCOP significantly increased MDA level (P < 0.01) and decreased SOD activity (P < 0.05) in the hippocampus, indicating that SCOP can lead to oxidative stress in the hippocampus. Compared with the SCOP group, GSH-PEG-OA-LIP significantly increased SOD activity and decreased MDA level in a dose-dependent manner. Free OA (5 mg/kg) only significantly increased SOD activity (P < 0.05). Galantamine showed no significant effect on MDA level and SOD activity. The above results indicate that GSH-PEG-OA-LIP can significantly alleviate the oxidative stress in the hippocampus of mice induced by SCOP.

The hippocampus CA1 region is closely related to the ability of learning and memory and is one of the important parts of long-term potentiation (LTP) formation. In the present study, histopathological analysis of the hippocampus CA1 region was carried out to evaluate the damage of this region (Fig. 8f). In the control group, neurons in the hippocampus CA1 region were intact in morphology and connected closely with each other (Fig. 8f(1)). Compared with the control group, the CA1 region of the SCOP group showed obvious neuronal damage, loose cellular organization, and evidence of neuronal loss (Fig. 8f(2)). Compared with the SCOP group, GSH-PEG-OA-LIP (1, 2, and 5 mg/kg) can effectively alleviate the neuron damage in the CA1 region induced by SCOP.

To rule out the alternative hypothesis that GSH-PEG-OA-LIP might alleviate cognitive impairment by sequestering SCOP in systemic circulation (thereby reducing SCOP's central bioavailability), we conducted a dedicated experiment to assess plasma SCOP concentrations. Mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 3): the SCOP alone group (1 mg/kg, i.p.) and the SCOP+GSH-PEG-OA-LIP co-administration group (SCOP 1 mg/kg + GSH-PEG-OA-LIP 5 mg/kg OA equivalent, i.p.). At 24 h post-administration, orbital sinus blood was collected, plasma was isolated by centrifugation, and SCOP concentrations were quantified via HPLC. Results showed that the plasma SCOP concentration in the co-administration group was not significantly different from that in the SCOP alone group (Supplementary Fig. S7). This finding indicates that GSH-PEG-OA-LIP does not interfere with the absorption, distribution, or systemic stability of SCOP. Thus, the cognitive improvement observed in GSH-PEG-OA-LIP-treated mice is unlikely due to peripheral neutralization of SCOP, but rather reflects the central neuroprotective effects of OA following GSH-PEG-mediated BBB penetration.

Discussion

In recent years, the central neuroprotective activity of OA has gained increasing attention, but its poor water solubility and low bioavailability limit its application. This study prepared GSH-PEGylated OA liposomes (GSH-PEG-OA-LIP) to enhance OA delivery across the BBB. Moreover, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations revealed that OA exhibits high binding affinity and stability with PKC, PI3Kγ, MEK, and IP3R, which provides a new basis for exploring its pro-cholinergic and cognitive-improving mechanisms.

The particle size of GSH-PEG-OA-LIP (114.80 ± 3.02 nm) is consistent with the optimal range for the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect (Nguyen-Huu et al., 2024), ensuring passive targeting of nanoparticles. The BBB, composed of tightly connected brain capillary endothelial cells, severely limits paracellular drug transport (Wilhelm et al., 2013). GSH-PEG modification significantly increased OA accumulation in the brain and prolonged blood retention (verified by near-infrared fluorescence imaging), which is the premise for OA to exert central effects. The in vitro release profiles at pH 7.4 demonstrate the robust stability of GSH-PEG-OA-LIP, ensuring its integrity during systemic circulation and BBB penetration. However, the therapeutic efficacy also depends on the responsiveness to intracellular and pathological microenvironments. Following receptor-mediated endocytosis into brain cells, liposomes typically encounter an acidic environment (pH 4.5–6.0) within endo-lysosomes. This pH shift, potentially synergistic with the high intracellular GSH concentration, may trigger the accelerated release of OA from the liposomal bilayer. Such localized release is essential for OA to reach its intracellular targets, including PKC and PI3K-γ, thereby effectively modulating downstream signaling pathways to restore cognitive function. Pharmacological experiments in scopolamine (SCOP)-induced cognitive impairment mice confirmed that GSH-PEG-OA-LIP enhances cholinergic signaling by increasing acetylcholine (ACh) synthesis/release, which is closely related to the regulation of the newly identified targets.

Given that SCOP is a small, lipophilic molecule that can cross the BBB passively (Inge and Arjan, 2010), we reasoned that GSH-PEG-OA-LIP (∼110 nm) would not compete with SCOP for BBB penetration. This is supported by our plasma SCOP data (no change in systemic levels). Our biochemical data (increased hippocampal ACh/ChAT, reduced MDA) and histopathological results (protected CA1 neurons) reflect OA's central neuroprotective effects, consistent with OA's binding to brain-specific targets (PKC, PI3Kγ, etc., via molecular docking/MD simulations). No evidence supports SCOP-OA interactions in the brain, as SCOP's mechanism (muscarinic receptor antagonism) is distinct from OA's multi-target regulation.

Cholinergic signaling (mediated by muscarinic receptors, mAChRs) relies on downstream G protein-coupled pathways (Maeda et al., 2019), and our data further clarify the multi-step regulation of this pathway by OA. Regarding PKC, a key effector of mAChR/Gq/11 signaling, direct molecular interactions were validated via molecular docking and MD simulations: OA fits well into PKC's catalytic pocket, forming stable π-alkyl interactions with Met473, Leu348, Val356, Ala483, Ala369, Lys371, and Phe353 (Fig. 9a; Supplementary Fig. S2a), while the complex maintains structural stability from 20 ns to 100 ns of simulation (Fig. 10). This direct binding serves as the molecular foundation for OA's regulatory effect on PKC. The downstream functional consequences involve PKC activation, which promotes PKC translocation to the cell membrane (Govoni et al., 1993) and enhances mAChR-mediated synaptic transmission-consistent with findings that PKC/ERK pathway activation ameliorates cognitive impairment in SAMP8 mice (Zeng et al., 2025). Biochemical data corroborate this: GSH-PEG-OA-LIP treatment significantly increased ChAT activity (2 and 5 mg/kg doses) and ACh levels (1, 2, 5 mg/kg doses) in the hippocampus (Fig. 8a–b), directly reversing SCOP-induced cholinergic hypofunction and ultimately translating to improved short- and long-term memory in behavioral tests (Fig. 6, Fig. 7).

As for PI3Kγ, a PI3K subtype implicated in cholinergic neuron survival and cognitive regulation, direct molecular interactions were characterized by four hydrogen bonds between OA and Arg277, Asp788, Gln291, His658 of PI3Kγ (Fig. 9b), alongside reduced solvent exposure of Arg849, Lys298, Leu211, His295, and Trp292 (Supplementary Fig. S2b), ensuring stable binding throughout the 100 ns MD simulation (Fig. 10). The downstream functional consequences include activation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway (Xu et al., 2022), a pathway whose diminished activity is associated with sleep deprivation-induced cognitive impairment (Xu et al., 2022). This activation protects cholinergic neurons from SCOP-induced damage, as evidenced by histopathological analysis showing reduced neuronal loss and improved cellular organization in the hippocampal CA1 region (Fig. 8f), which further underpins the restoration of cholinergic function (Masliah et al., 2003).

Turning to MEK, the upstream kinase of ERK in memory-related signaling, direct molecular interactions were verified through docking results: OA nestles into MEK's catalytic pocket, forming two hydrogen bonds with Lys175 and Phe371 (Fig. 9c), while reducing solvent exposure of Ile171, Tyr125, Ala172, Lys168, and Gly202 (Supplementary Fig. S2c), with the complex achieving structural stability after 80 ns of MD simulation (Fig. 10). The downstream functional consequences entail MEK activation to promote ERK phosphorylation (Yang et al., 2025), which upregulates downstream CREB/BDNF signaling, key mediators of synaptic plasticity and memory consolidation (Jeon et al., 2017). This aligns with our behavioral data, where GSH-PEG-OA-LIP treatment reversed SCOP-induced deficits in spatial learning and memory (Fig. 6, Fig. 7), as synaptic plasticity constitutes the core basis for memory formation (Jeon et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2025).

With respect to IP3R, an ER-located Ca2+ channel closely linked to cognitive impairment, direct molecular interactions were identified via docking: OA binds to IP3R's catalytic pocket, forming one hydrogen bond with Ser128 (Fig. 9d), accompanied by reduced solvent exposure of Asp35, Lys130, Glu153, Phe483, and Phe445 (Supplementary Fig. S2d), with stable binding observed after 80 ns of MD simulation (Fig. 10). The downstream functional consequences involve regulating IP3R-mediated Ca2+ release to restore intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis (Hisatsune and Mikoshiba, 2017), which mitigates mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS production—pathological processes associated with IP3R dysregulation (e.g., hyperactivation of IP3R1) that exacerbate amyloid-β(Aβ)-induced cognitive deficits (Park et al., 2019). Biochemical data confirm this: GSH-PEG-OA-LIP treatment dose-dependently decreased MDA levels and increased SOD activity in the hippocampus (Fig. 8d–e), alleviating oxidative stress and protecting cholinergic neurons from damage, an essential supplement to the restoration of cholinergic function (Hisatsune and Mikoshiba, 2017; Park et al., 2019). Although the docking and binding energy of ERK with OA met the screening criteria, 100 ns MD simulations showed that the complex conformation was unstable (Supplementary Fig. S3), suggesting that ERK may not be the key stable target through which OA exerts its effects.

The in silico findings of stable binding between OA and PKC, PI3Kγ, MEK, and IP3R are strongly corroborated by prior experimental evidence, reinforcing the credibility of our proposed multi-target mechanism. Specifically, Yi et al. (2014) demonstrated via Western blotting and pathway inhibition assays that OA activates the MEK-ERK-CREB-BDNF cascade in the mouse hippocampus, consistent with our MD simulation showing stable OA-MEK binding. Dong et al. (2025) further validated that OA modulates intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis through regulating Ca2+ channel-related proteins, supporting our prediction that OA targets IP3R to restore Ca2+ balance. Together, these preclinical studies confirm that OA's binding to the identified targets translates to functional regulation of downstream signaling pathways critical for cognitive function. Our work builds on this foundation by optimizing OA's brain delivery via GSH-PEGylation, ensuring these intrinsic neuroprotective mechanisms are effectively engaged in the CNS to ameliorate scopolamine-induced cognitive impairment.

Based on the findings from molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and pharmacological experiments, we propose a potential mechanism by which OA ameliorates central cognitive impairment (Fig. 11). OA binds to PKC and PI3Kγ to activate downstream pathways of muscarinic acetylcholine receptors (mAChRs), thereby promoting the synthesis and release of acetylcholine (ACh) and enhancing cholinergic signaling; simultaneously, OA activates MEK to drive the ERK/CREB/BDNF pathway, supporting memory formation and consolidation to boost synaptic plasticity; in addition, OA regulates IP3R to maintain intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis, reducing neuronal damage induced by reactive oxygen species (ROS) and thus alleviating oxidative stress. This multi-target regulatory mode centered on PKC, PI3Kγ, MEK, and IP3R can concurrently mitigate multiple pathological processes linked to cognitive impairment (including cholinergic hypofunction, synaptic damage, and oxidative stress), ultimately exerting a significant ameliorative effect in scopolamine (SCOP)-induced cognitive impairment model mice.Fig. 11Schematic diagram of potential mechanism of OA ameliorating central cognitive impairment. Conclusion

In summary, the present study successfully developed glutathione-polyethylene glycol-modified oleanolic acid liposomes (GSH-PEG-OA-LIP) with favorable physicochemical properties and enhanced BBB penetration, which significantly ameliorated SCOP-induced cognitive impairment in mice. Molecular docking combined with molecular dynamics simulations further revealed that OA exhibits high binding affinity and stability with PKC, PI3Kγ, MEK, and IP3R, and exerts neuroprotective effects through multi-targeted pathways, including potentiating cholinergic neurotransmission via PKC/PI3Kγ-mediated mAChR signaling, enhancing synaptic plasticity and memory processes by activating the MEK/ERK/CREB/BDNF cascade, and alleviating ROS-mediated neuronal damage by modulating IP3R to maintain intracellular calcium homeostasis.

Notably, this study advances our understanding of the neuroprotective mechanisms of OA and validates GSH-PEG modification as an effective strategy to enhance the central nervous system delivery of poorly bioavailable natural products. However, as this study is limited to preclinical mouse models, future research should prioritize evaluating the efficacy and safety of GSH-PEG-OA-LIP in larger animal models and clinical trials, while exploring potential synergistic interactions among the identified targets to further elucidate the therapeutic potential of OA. Ultimately, these efforts may provide valuable insights for the development of OA-based formulations and blood–brain–barrier–penetrating nanomedicines for neurological disorders.
