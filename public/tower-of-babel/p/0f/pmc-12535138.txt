Biogeographical patterns and ecological cascades of soil nutrients, plant nutrients, and population traits of Neotrinia splendens grasslands in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau
Authors: Jiayue Liu, Shuaizhi Lu, Rui Zhang, Nan Li, Dongjie Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, regarded as an ecological security barrier for Asia, has grassland ecosystems that are highly sensitive to climate change and anthropogenic disturbances. Neotrinia splendens, known for its remarkable nutrient and geographical distribution adaptability, plays a crucial role in regulating the structure and function of the plant community in the northern Qinghai-Tibet Plateau. However, the biogeographic patterns and underlying driving mechanisms of the nutrients and population traits remain poorly understood. Based on 83 N. splendens grassland sites in the northern Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, the aboveground nutrient concentrations and population traits of N. splendens were measured and analyzed, combined with soil physicochemical and climatic factors.ResultsAlong the longitude gradient, soil organic matter, total nitrogen, total phosphorus, available nitrogen (SAN) and phosphorus (SAP), plant Carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P) concentrations significantly increased, whereas these decreased with increasing latitude and altitude gradients. Population height, cover, and density showed the same biogeographical pattern as soil and plant nutrients. Mean annual temperature (MAT) and mean annual precipitation (MAP) induced by longitude, latitude, and altitude gradients indirectly altered plant C, N, and P concentrations by directly regulating SAN and SAP concentrations, affecting plant height and cover.ConclusionsThis study enhances the understanding of the biogeographic patterns of plant nutrients and population traits on the grasslands of Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, provides insights to deepen multiple adaptations of plants to environmental stresses, and elucidates the multi-scale driving mechanisms of nutrient cycling in grassland ecosystems through the climate-soil-plant-population ecological cascades.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07449-y.

Introduction

Ecological stoichiometry refers to the requirements of plants for different elements and the mechanisms underlying the balance and interactions of these chemical elements [1], especially for Carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P). Plants adapt to environmental changes or stresses by adjusting nutrient concentrations and their stoichiometry [2], which is an important nutrient allocation strategy. Meanwhile, the “Growth Rate Hypothesis” points that the plant growth rate is closely related to plant C, N, and P concentrations and their stoichiometries, with some studies supporting its applicability to higher plants [3, 4]. This indicates that plant nutrient concentrations and their stoichiometry may alter population traits by regulating plant growth rates. For example, plants with higher nutrient concentrations and lower C/N and C/P may have higher growth rates, altering leaf or stem traits in grazed grassland ecosystems [5]. However, this relationship between nutrient stoichiometry and population traits remains poorly understood across environmental stresses. Exploring this relationship bridges the gap between species-level nutrients and macro-level population traits, providing new perspectives and methods for understanding plant adaptation mechanisms to environmental changes from a stoichiometric viewpoint.

Natural environmental stresses created by heat and water gradients in a region affect plant nutrient strategy and population traits. Studying the biogeographic patterns of plant nutrients is crucial for revealing plant nutrient adaptation strategies across environmental gradient stress. Numerous studies have explored the variations in plant nutrients at different spatial scales, but have lacked their related research on population traits [6–9]. For example, some studies have shown that plant N and P concentrations significantly increase with latitude gradient in eastern China’s forests [9]. Meanwhile, studies in the arid northwest of China have found that plant N and P concentrations decrease with latitude gradient and increase with longitude and altitude gradients [10]. Additionally, there are also some small regional studies of a single geographic gradient and lack of studies of multiple geographic gradients [11–13]. For instance, a previous study observed that plant N and P concentrations significantly increase with aridity in northern China’s grasslands [12]. Research on the eastern Qinghai-Tibet Plateau has shown that plant N and P concentrations increase with altitude gradient [14]. Differences in soil (e.g., soil nutrients, moisture, pH, and texture) and climatic (e.g., temperature and precipitation) factors account for these controversial biogeographic patterns of plant nutrients [15–17]. Recent studies have also shown that N and P concentrations among different plant taxa had divergent biogeographic patterns with longitude and latitude gradients [18]. Studying regional multi-species assemblages (n >10) may not only obscure the true regional nutrient patterns (due to the nutrient differences among species), but also prevent a quantitative exploration of the relationship between stoichiometry and population traits (due to the different traits among various species). Thus, selecting a species with wide geographical distributions and strong ecological adaptability has emerged as a new approach to studying regional plant nutrient adaptation to environmental stress, as demonstrated in regions like the Qilian Mountains or Qinghai-Tibet Plateau [19, 20]. More importantly, focusing on a single species allows for a deeper exploration of the relationship between plant nutrient stoichiometry and population traits, enhancing the understanding of the link between plant stoichiometry and population dynamics.

The Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, located in the interior of Asia, is the highest and largest plateau on Earth, referred to as the “Third Pole” [21]. Grasslands, which are a vital component of the vegetation on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, are widely distributed across its northern areas [21]. These grasslands play crucial roles in ecological services, production, and the preservation of ethnic cultures [22]. However, under the influences of the natural environment (e.g., extreme cold, poor soil nutrients, and intense radiation), global climate change (e.g., rising regional temperatures, altered precipitation patterns, and increased extreme precipitation events), and human activity [11, 23], plant growth suffers from multiple environmental stresses. The Neotrinia splendens grasslands, widely distributed in the northern Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, are a typical grassland type that plays a vital role in regulating regional ecological security [24]. As a dominant species, N. splendens has a wide range of distribution (longitude, latitude, and altitude gradients) and exhibits strong ecological adaptability, such as drought tolerance, cold resistance, and nutrient adaptability [25]. Therefore, N. splendens serves as an ideal species for studying the biogeographic patterns of plant nutrients and population traits in the grasslands of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau to reduce the effect of differences in nutrients and population traits among different plant taxa.

In this study, 83 field sites were established in N. splendens grasslands across the northern Qinghai-Tibet Plateau. The population traits and aboveground nutrients of dominant species (N. splendens) were investigated and measured. We addressed the following questions: (1) how the biogeographic patterns of plant nutrients and population traits are distributed in different spatial patterns; (2) how soil and climatic factors drive the biogeographic patterns of nutrients and population traits. We hypothesized that (1) plant nutrients and population traits increased significantly with increasing longitude due to better water and heat conditions, but these indicators decreased significantly with increasing latitude and altitude due to poor water and heat conditions; (2) longitude, latitude, and altitude gradients influenced temperature and precipitation, which directly affected soil available nutrients; soil available nutrients affected plant nutrients, thereby affecting population traits. Our study reveals the biogeographic patterns of plant nutrients and population traits and their driving factors in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau grasslands and strengthens the link between species-level stoichiometry and macro-level population traits.

Materials and methods

Study sites

The study area is located in the grassland region of the northern Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (96.8°E-101.7°E, 34.8°N-37.6°N), characterized by significant elevation variations ranging from 2534 to 4061 m. The topography includes high plains, basins, hills, and gentle mountains. The region belongs to a continental plateau climate, with long, cold winters and short, cool summers. Precipitation and heat occur simultaneously, accompanied by intense solar radiation, large diurnal temperature fluctuations, and frequent winds. The Mean annual temperature (MAT) is 4.6 °C, with winter lows reaching − 28.5 °C and summer highs up to 33.5 °C. Mean annual precipitation (MAP) ranges from 200 to 350 mm, decreasing from east to west. The aridity index varies between 1.34 and 1.94 [24]. The soil type is classified as chestnut soil (according to the Chinese Soil Classification), with relatively thin layers and parent materials primarily composed of sandy gravel and loamy sand.

Field survey and sampling

Based on the distribution of N. splendens grasslands documented in China Vegetation and supplemented by regional field vegetation surveys [24], field investigations and sample collections were conducted from July to August 2023. A total of 83 field sites were established (Fig. 1). N. splendens grasslands (covering at least 500 m × 500 m) with ungrazed disturbance were selected, and sampling sites were established at least 100 m away from roads to minimize other human disturbance. Geographic information (e.g., longitude, latitude, and elevation) was recorded for each sampling site. Within the sampling site, a 100-meter transect was established in areas with flat terrain and uniform vegetation. Along the transect, three 1 m × 1 m quadrats were evenly established. In each quadrat, the cover of N. splendens was estimated visually, followed by measuring its density based on bunches. Five N. splendens were randomly selected and measured their height. Finally, the aboveground parts of 5 to 10 healthy, mature N. splendens were collected and stored in envelopes. The voucher specimens for N. splendens of each site have been stored in the Herbarium of Inner Mongolia University and all specimens were identified by Prof. Liqing Zhao of Inner Mongolia University. Detailed collection information and corresponding herbarium accession numbers are provided in Table S1.

Distribution of sampling sites of Neotrinia splendens grasslands in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (n = 83)

After plant sampling, soil samples were collected from each quadrat by a five-point sampling method. A 3-cm-diameter soil auger was used to collect soil from a depth of 0–10 cm. Roots and stones were removed, and the soil was homogenized before being stored in sealed bags at 4 °C.

Sampling measurement

Plant samples were dried at 65 °C to a constant weight, then cut into 1 to 2 cm segments and ground using a mill (MM400, Retsch). Soil samples were air-dried in the laboratory. A portion of the soil samples was sieved through a 2 mm mesh for soil available nitrogen (SAN) and phosphorus (SAP), while the remaining soil was sieved through a 0.15 mm mesh for soil organic matter (SOM), total nitrogen (STN), and total phosphorus (STP). In this study, soil and plant nutrient were determined according to the methods described in Methods of Soil Analysis [26]. SOM was measured using the potassium dichromate oxidation-external heating method. STN was determined using the Kjeldahl method after digesting subsamples in H2SO4-K2SO4-CuSO4. STP and SAP were measured using the molybdenum antimony colorimetric method, and SAN was determined using the alkaline diffusion method. Plant C and N concentrations were measured by an elemental analyzer (vario ELIII CHNOS Elemental Analyzer, Elementar Analysensysteme GmbH). Plant P concentration was digested in H2SO4-K2SO4-CuSO4 and determined by using ICP-OES (iCAP 6300 ICP-OES Spectrometer, Thermo Scientific).

Climatic data

Two meteorological indicators, MAT and MAP, were selected for analysis. Data for the two variables were obtained from WorldClim (https://www.worldclim.org), with a temporal range of 1970–2020 and a spatial resolution of 1 km × 1 km.

Statistical analysis

MAT and MAP for each sampling site were interpolated using the Kriging method in ArcGIS 10.3 (ESRI Inc., California, USA). Data normality and homogeneity of variance were tested, and plant and soil nutrient data were found to conform to a normal distribution. Univariate linear regression models were used to analyze the relationships between SOM, STN, STP, SAN, SAP, plant C, N, and P concentrations, and population height, cover, and density with longitude, latitude, and altitude. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was employed to explore the relationships among climatic factors, soil nutrients, plant nutrients, and population traits. Models were constructed using maximum likelihood estimation and evaluated based on chi-squared values, P-values, CFI, RMSEA, and AIC. Based on correlations among climatic factors, soil nutrients, plant nutrients, and population traits, variables with no significant relationships or collinearity were excluded. The study focused on the relationships among MAT, MAP, SAN, SAP, plant C, N P concentrations, and population height and cover, integrating previous research and current knowledge to construct the initial SEM model. Statistical analyses were conducted at a significance level of 0.05 by IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Amos 24.0 (Amos Development Co., Greene, Maine, USA), with figures generated in R 4.4.1. The data was indicated by mean ± standard error.

Relationships among climatic factors, soil factors, plant nutrients, and population traits along geographic gradients

Longitude, latitude, and altitude altered the biogeographical patterns of plant nutrient population traits through different ecological processes. Along longitude gradient, both MAT and MAP exhibited significant positive correlations with increasing longitude (Fig. 7; P < 0.05). Increased MAT enhanced SAP and SAN concentrations. Additionally, Increased MAP also promoted plant cover and height. Elevated SAN and SAP levels further increase plant N and P concentrations, thereby promoting C accumulation (Fig. 7; P < 0.05). This cascade of nutrient responses ultimately enhanced plant cover and height.

In contrast to the regulating pathways of longitude on the biogeographical patterns of plant nutrient and population traits, both MAT and MAP demonstrated significant declines with increasing latitude (Fig. 8; P < 0.05). These latitude gradients exhibited strong positive correlations between MAT and MAP. Meanwhile, MAT and MAP had significant correlations with SAN and SAP concentrations (Fig. 8; P < 0.05). Enhanced SAN and SAP concentrations drove the increases in plant N and P concentrations, thereby stimulating plant C accumulation process (Fig. 8; P < 0.05). This nutrient cascade ultimately elevates plant cover and height. Furthermore, MAP not only directly modulates plant height and cover but also exerts indirect control through its sequential effects on plant cover, consequently shaping height differentiation patterns.

Discussion

Biogeographical patterns of soil nutrients in the northern Qinghai-Tibet Plateau grasslands

This study reveals that in the grasslands of the northern Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, the SOM, STN, STP, SAN, and SAP concentrations were 2.31%, 0.14%, 0.10%, 15.09 mg/kg, and 6.81 mg/kg, respectively. The relatively low soil N and P concentrations align with previous findings [13, 27], indicating a deficiency of these nutrients in the soils of the grasslands of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau. Soil available nutrients are influenced by both the soil material and climatic factors such as MAT and MAP [28]. The harsh environmental conditions of the grasslands of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, characterized by low temperatures, strong winds, aridity, and intense radiation, suppress microbial decomposition and mineralization, leading to slow biogeochemical cycling rates and consequently low availability of soil nutrients. The soil nutrients in this region are lower than those in the grasslands of Inner Mongolia and the Loess Plateau [29, 30], but higher than those in the alpine meadows of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau [31]. The higher MAT and MAP in Inner Mongolia and the Loess Plateau facilitate microbial decomposition and mineralization, accelerating the breakdown of soil materials and litter, thereby promoting the transformation of soil N and P and the accumulation of SOM. In contrast, the more severe environmental conditions in alpine meadows in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau impede biogeochemical cycling, resulting in even lower soil nutrient concentrations.

Variations in environmental factors such as MAT and MAP, driven by geographical spatial heterogeneity, create different environmental gradients that influence the biogeographical patterns of soil nutrients [27, 32]. This study found that SOM, STN, STP, SAN, and SAP concentrations significantly increase with increasing longitude in the northern Qinghai-Tibet Plateau grasslands, consistent with previous findings in other regions of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau [33]. Changes in MAP are the primary driver of the biogeographical patterns of soil nutrients. Increased precipitation with longitude enhances microbial activity, promoting the mineralization of soil materials and the decomposition of plant litter, thereby facilitating the accumulation of soil nutrients [33, 34]. However, this study also found that these soil nutrient concentrations significantly decrease with increasing latitude and altitude. Lower temperatures at higher latitudes and altitudes reduce microbial activity and the rates of soil mineralization and litter decomposition [35]. Thus, MAT is the dominant factor driving the latitudinal and altitudinal patterns of soil nutrients in the northern Qinghai-Tibet Plateau grasslands.

According to the “Soil Substrate Age Hypothesis”, plant nutrients are closely linked to soil nutrients [36], and biogeographical patterns of soil nutrients will further affect biogeographical patterns of plant nutrients. As a region highly sensitive to global climate change, the study of soil nutrient patterns in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau provides critical data for global change research, enhances our understanding of soil nutrient responses to global climate change, and supports the development of global-scale ecological models [34, 35]. Future studies could leverage these data to uncover complex ecological relationships, build more accurate predictive models, and innovate ecological research paradigms.

Biogeographical patterns of plant nutrients and population traits in the northern Qinghai-Tibet Plateau grasslands

Plants adapt to environmental changes and stresses by adjusting their nutrient concentrations and stoichiometry [11], which in turn affects their growth rates and population traits. The plant C concentration in this region was lower than that in plants from Inner Mongolia but similar to that in other grassland ecosystems worldwide, as well as in other regions of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and Xinjiang (Table 3). C, as a product of photosynthesis, is primarily influenced by photosynthetic and respiratory rates [3]. The harsh environmental conditions of the northern Qinghai-Tibet Plateau result in lower photosynthetic and higher respiratory rates compared to Inner Mongolia, and plants may allocate a portion of photosynthetic products to adapt to environmental stresses such as low temperatures and aridity, leading to lower C concentrations [37]. Similar environmental conditions in other regions of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and Xinjiang also result in relatively low plant C concentrations. In contrast, the plant N concentration in this region is significantly higher than the global and Chinese averages for terrestrial ecosystems and grasslands, and slightly higher than those in Inner Mongolia, Xinjiang, and other regions of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (Table 3). The P concentration is also higher than the global and Chinese averages and comparable to that in other regions of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (Table 3). These findings suggest that plants in the northern Qinghai-Tibet Plateau grasslands exhibit “high N and high P concentrations”. Our study also supports the “Temperature-Plant Physiology Hypothesis” [36]. Plants adapt to environmental stresses such as low temperatures and aridity by increasing the production of secondary metabolites like proline, sorbitol, and sugar alcohols, or by enhancing cellular osmotic potential, leading to higher N and P concentrations [11, 38]. Previous studies have also shown that under environmental stresses, plants adopt a “storing strategy” by increasing N and P concentrations [1], to adapt to the harsh environment, consistent with the nutrient adaptation strategies of plants observed in other arid regions.

Understanding the biogeographical patterns of plant nutrients is critical for elucidating ecosystem nutrient cycling processes and understanding how plants adapt to environmental stresses by adjusting nutrient concentrations and stoichiometry. This study found that plant nutrients significantly increase with longitude, consistent with previous findings [11, 42]. Based on the “Soil Substrate Age Hypothesis”, the nutrients required for plants are closely linked to the soil, as plants absorb N and P through their roots and engage in material exchange with the soil [36, 43]. Consequently, the biogeographical patterns of plant nutrients are closely linked to those of soil nutrients. Moreover, higher MAP with increasing longitude promotes plant growth rates. This results in higher N and P concentrations, lower C: N and C: P ratios [2], and consequently, a greater overall accumulation of plant carbon. In contrast, lower temperatures at higher latitudes and altitudes hinder plant growth, leading to lower growth rates and consequently lower N and P concentrations, as well as higher C/N and C/P ratios [36]. However, some studies have reported that plant N and P concentrations increase with altitude, contrary to our findings [14, 44, 45]. These studies, conducted over a wide altitudinal range (1800–4200 m), suggest that plants increase N and P concentrations to adapt to environmental stresses such as aridity and low temperatures, consistent with the “Temperature-Plant Physiology Hypothesis” [36]. In our study, the altitudinal range is narrower (2500–3500 m), with less pronounced environmental gradients and milder environmental stresses.

Population traits are key factors affecting community structure and function, and understanding their biogeographical patterns is essential for exploring community characteristics and their spatial distribution. Similar to the factors influencing plant nutrient biogeographical patterns, population traits in the northern Qinghai-Tibet Plateau grasslands significantly increase with longitude and decrease with latitude and altitude. Variations in nutrients along geographical gradients account for these changes. The consistency of plant stoichiometry and population trait changes supported our hypothesis that plants altered population traits by adjusting nutrients and their stoichiometry. Revealing the biogeographical patterns of plant nutrients and population traits along longitude, latitude, and altitude in the northern Qinghai-Tibet Plateau grasslands helps clarify plant nutrient adaptation strategies to environmental stresses, provides a basis for ecosystem function assessment, and offers key parameters for the precise construction of ecosystem models, improving predictions of the structure and function of Qinghai-Tibet Plateau grassland ecosystems.

Driving paths among soil nutrients, plant nutrients, and population traits in the northern Qinghai-Tibet Plateau grasslands

Climatic and soil factors are key driving factors of the biogeographical patterns of plant nutrients and population traits [17, 39]. This study found that soil factors directly regulate plant nutrient concentrations, while climatic factors induced by geographic gradient changes indirectly influence plant nutrient concentrations by affecting soil available nutrients, which is consistent with our previous hypotheses. MAT and MAP, as climatic factors, alter the activity and intensity of soil microorganisms, influencing the processes of soil mineralization and litter decomposition, and thereby affecting soil nutrient availability. Since plant nutrients are derived from soil nutrients, soil nutrients directly regulate plant nutrient concentrations. These findings are consistent with previous studies on the driving mechanisms of plant nutrient biogeographical patterns in arid ecosystems of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, forest ecosystems in eastern China, and grassland ecosystems in Inner Mongolia [9, 11, 39], indicating nutrient biogeography of plant nutrients in China’s terrestrial ecosystem is mainly regulated by climatic factors. However, some studies have indicated that the biogeographical patterns of plant nutrients in desert ecosystems of the arid Qinghai-Tibet Plateau are solely influenced by soil factors [11], suggesting that the drivers of plant nutrient biogeographical patterns vary across different terrestrial ecosystems. The “Growth Rate Hypothesis” points that plant growth rates are closely related to plant C, N, and P concentrations and their stoichiometry [2]. Therefore, plant nutrients may affect population traits by regulating plant growth rates, ultimately forming the biogeographical patterns of nutrients and population traits, a relationship that has received limited attention in previous studies. Our study found significant positive correlations between plant nutrients and population traits such as plant height and cover, indicating a close relationship between plant nutrients and population traits. Higher N and P concentrations and lower C/N and C/P ratios result in higher growth rates, leading to greater population traits such as plant cover and height, supporting the “Growth Rate Hypothesis” [2]. By analyzing the synergistic effects of temperature, precipitation, and soil physicochemical properties along geographical gradients on plant nutrient concentrations and stoichiometry, this study transcends the limitations of traditional single-factor explanatory frameworks and establishes a theoretical model of plant adaptation strategies under multi-scale environmental stresses. Moreover, our study also provides a scientific basis for implementing spatially differentiated grazing management strategies. For instance, regions exhibiting enhanced nutrient availability (e.g., eastern zones) may sustain moderate grazing pressure, whereas nutrient-limited areas (e.g., western and high-altitude zones) necessitate more stringent conservation measures (e.g., reduced grazing intensity and seasonal exclusion) to reduce degradation risks. These insights further advocate for grassland restoration, ultimately enhancing the resilience of these fragile ecosystems in the face of climate change.

Our analysis examined longitude, latitude, and altitude as independent gradients to ensure statistical accuracy. We know that these geographic factors interact synergistically to shape hydrothermal conditions and ecological processes across the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau. For example, the decreasing temperature associated with high latitude is often intensified at higher altitudes, while the eastward rise in precipitation (longitude) likely interacts with thermal gradients (latitude or altitude) to differentially regulate nutrient availability. Efforts to incorporate these interrelated geographic variables into a unified SEM were constrained by severe multicollinearity, resulting in model instability and ambiguous parameter estimates. This statistical limitation highlights the inherent difficulty of disentangling covarying environmental drivers in large-scale observational studies.

Conclusion

The northern Qinghai-Tibet Plateau grasslands support a wide distribution of N. splendens, demonstrating remarkable ecological resilience in this extreme environment. Through systematic analysis of 83 sampling sites across the region, our investigation identified a distinctive “high N and high P” nutrient strategy in N. splendens. Notably, plant nutrient concentrations (C, N, and P) and population traits (height, coverage, and density) significantly increase with longitude but decrease with latitude and altitude. The same pattern occurred in soil nutrient concentrations. Our path analysis further deciphered the hierarchical regulation mechanisms: MAT and MAP directly controlled soil nutrient availability through synergistic effects and indirectly influenced plant nutrient acquisition strategies. This dual regulation ultimately cascaded to population-level adaptations.
