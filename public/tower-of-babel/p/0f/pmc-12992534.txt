Therapeutic effects of Tetramethylpyrazine on Cartilage in Rat Model of Post-traumatic Osteoarthritis
Authors: Zhengjiao Duan, Hongpan Gao, Panpan Li, Si Tang, BenXiang He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-traumatic Osteoarthritis (PTOA) is a common secondary joint disease characterized by cartilage degradation, subchondral bone remodeling, and synovial inflammation following joint trauma. Current therapeutic options are limited in reversing pathological progression. Tetramethylpyrazine (TMP), an active alkaloid extracted from traditional Chinese medicine Ligusticum chuanxiong, exhibits anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and cartilage protective properties. This study aimed to investigate the therapeutic effects of TMP in a rat model of PTOA.MethodsSixty-three male Sprague–Dawley rats were randomly divided into sham and model groups. PTOA was induced using a modified Hulth method (anterior cruciate ligament and medial collateral ligament transection plus medial meniscectomy). Four weeks post-surgery, rats were intra-articularly administered TMP (2, 4, or 8 mg/kg) or hyaluronic acid sodium (HA, positive control) for 5 weeks. Functional recovery was assessed using grip strength and gait analysis. Cartilage and subchondral bone changes were evaluated by Micro-CT and histopathological staining (HE and safranin O-fast green). Immunohistochemistry was used to detect cartilage matrix components (COL II, MMP13 and ADAMTS5) at the protein level, while qRT-PCR was performed to validate the gene level of Col2a1, Mmp13 and Adamts5. ELISA was used to determine inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-18) in synovial fluid and serum.ResultsTMP significantly improved grip strength and normalized gait parameters in PTOA model rats. Micro-CT revealed that TMP attenuated subchondral bone loss and improved trabecular microarchitecture. Histological analysis showed that TMP restored cartilage surface integrity, increased chondrocyte density, and enhanced matrix staining. TMP significantly downregulated MMP13 and ADAMTS5 protein expression while upregulating COL II protein. Similarly, TMP restored Col2a1 mRNA expression and reduced Mmp13 and Adamts5 mRNA level in cartilage. TMP also decreased IL-1β and IL-18 levels in both serum and synovial fluid.ConclusionIntra-articular administration of TMP effectively restored joint function and ameliorated PTOA progression in model rats by promoting cartilage matrix repair, inhibiting inflammatory responses, and improving subchondral bone microstructure. These findings suggest TMP as a potential therapeutic agent for PTOA treatment.Keywords: Post-traumatic Osteoarthritis, Tetramethylpyrazine, Cartilage repair

Instruction

Post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA), a secondary subset of osteoarthritis (OA), is triggered by identifiable joint trauma such as ligament rupture, meniscal tear or intra-articular fracture [1]. Its core pathological features are characterized by decreased articular cartilage integrity, aberrant subchondral bone remodeling, chronic synovial inflammation and intra-articular fibrosis, which collectively produce clinical phenotypes of persistent joint pain, restricted mobility and mechanical instability that substantially impair quality of life [2]. Escalating sports-related injuries and an increasingly youthful patient demographic have rendered PTOA a leading cause of joint disability in adults aged < 50 years, imposing a considerable socioeconomic burden [3].

Current management strategies are purely symptomatic, aiming to relieve pain, delay structural degeneration and preserve joint function. Standard regimens include oral non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and intra-articular hyaluronic acid sodium (HA) [4]. HA, a natural constituent of synovial fluid, provides visco-supplementation, shock absorption and cartilage surface protection, and is therefore widely employed as a positive-control intervention in pre-clinical studies [5]. Nevertheless, long-term oral pharmacotherapy is frequently complicated by gastrointestinal adverse events and fails to interrupt cartilage catabolism, whereas HA efficacy is highly variable because it is modulated by the extent of initial trauma and individual biological factors, and it does not reliably reverse established cartilage damage [6,7]. Consequently, safe, mechanism-based, disease-modifying pharmacological agents represent an urgent unmet need in PTOA research.

Tetramethylpyrazine (TMP) is the principal bioactive alkaloid isolated from Ligusticum chuanxiong. It possesses well-documented anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidant properties [8]. Recent investigations indicate that TMP exerts protective effects in skeletal disorders, attenuating inflammatory joint destruction in rheumatoid arthritis and ameliorating bone loss in osteoporosis via modulation of osteoblast and osteoclast activities [9,10]. However, the efficacy and molecular mechanisms of TMP in PTOA have not yet been clearly defined by rigorous experimental evidence.

Articular cartilage integrity is maintained by the dynamic equilibrium between chondrocyte anabolism and ECM turnover, and disruption of this balance following mechanical insult is a central driver of PTOA [11]. Cartilage degradation is fundamentally mediated by abnormal activation of proteolytic pathways, with interleukin-1β (IL-1β) serving as a master regulator. IL-1β suppresses type II collagen (COL II) synthesis, which is the primary fibrillar component of cartilage ECM, thereby compromising biomechanical competence, while simultaneously inducing catabolic enzymes that accelerate matrix breakdown and stimulating synoviocytes to release additional inflammatory mediators [12,13].

Building upon these insights, the present study employed the modified Hulth method to establish a rat PTOA model. The modified Hulth method simulates clinical common combined ligament and meniscal injuries, which can rapidly induce joint instability, leading to chondrocyte apoptosis, matrix degradation, subchondral bone sclerosis, and synovitis [14]. Behavior and histopathological changes were observed via Gait analysis, Micro-CT, HE and safranin O-fast green staining. The molecular aspects were detected by immunohistochemistry, qRT-PCR and ELISA. This study explored the therapeutic effect of TMP on PTOA, providing a theoretical basis for its potential clinical application. We hypothesized that TMP was likely to be a candidate therapeutic agent for the prevention and treatment of PTOA.

Materials and methods

Animals

This animal experiment was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Sichuan Academy of Chinese Medicine Sciences (approval no. DWSYLL-2024-188). Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, aged 2 months and weighing 200-220 g, were purchased from Shu Daqiang Biotech Co.,Ltd. (certificate no. NO.110324241106738475). The animal room was ventilated and kept dry with a relative humidity of 55%-70% and room temperature of 20 °C-25 °C. All experimental animals successfully completed the experimental cycle. No instances of abnormal body mass or other diseases were observed.

Establishment of PTOA rat model and validation

Animals were acclimated in the housing facility for 1 week. During the second week, they were randomly assigned to five groups. Prior to modelling, the animals were fasted for 12 h. Anaesthesia was induced by intraperitoneal injection of urethane (20 %, 5 mL kg-1). After loss of reflexes had been confirmed, each rat was placed in the supine position; the hair surrounding the right knee joint was removed with an electric clipper, and the surgical field was disinfected with 70 % ethanol.

A longitudinal skin incision was made from the distal pole of the patella to the proximal tibial plateau. The joint capsule medial to the patellar tendon was then opened; the incision was gently enlarged with micro-scissors to fully expose the intra-articular structures. The patellar tendon was retracted laterally with forceps, and the infrapatellar fat pad was bluntly dissected to reveal the medial meniscus, medial collateral ligament and anterior cruciate ligament (ACL). Subsequently, the medial collateral ligament and ACL were transected with a scalpel, and the medial meniscus was excised. Animals in the Sham group underwent identical anaesthesia and arthrotomy without any ligamentous or meniscal injury.

The joint cavity was irrigated with sterile 0.9 % saline, and the wound was disinfected with povidone-iodine. The joint capsule and skin were closed in layers with standard sutures. For the next 3 days, rats received daily intramuscular injections of penicillin G (200 000 IU) to prevent infection. Body weight, food intake and general activity were monitored daily. The surgical site was inspected for erythema, swelling or exudate, and any abnormalities were treated promptly [15].

Four weeks after surgery, three model rats were randomly selected, euthanized with an overdose of urethane, and perfused transcardially with 4 % paraformaldehyde. The knee joints were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned coronally at 5 μm. Sections were stained with HE. Histopathological evaluation revealed evident cartilage degeneration. Only animals exhibiting a Mankin score > 5 were considered to be successfully modelled and were included in subsequent analyses [16] (Supplementary file 1).

Animals group

Sixty-three male Sprague–Dawley rats were allocated by a random number table to either the Sham group (Sham, n=10) or the Model group (n=53). Four weeks after surgery, three animals from the model group were randomly selected for model validation; the remaining fifty were re-randomized into five experimental groups (n=10 each): (1) PTOA model group, (2) 2 mg/kg TMP, (3) 4 mg/kg TMP, (4) 8 mg/kg TMP, (5) HA positive control (HAG). Based on the clinical dose for osteoarthritis patients (80 mg) [17], the equivalent rat dose was calculated via body surface area normalization as follows: (80 mg/70 kg) × 6.3 (Km conversion factor for human-to-rat dose scaling) ≈ 7.2 mg/kg [18,19]. To evaluate a clear dose-response relationship, the low, medium and high doses were established at 2 mg/kg, 4 mg/kg and 8 mg/kg, respectively. In line with previous study [20]and to minimize inter-individual variability inherent to behavioral assessments, 10 SD rats were assigned to each group.

Guided by previous studies, intra-articular administration was selected as the route of delivery [17,21]. TMP (HY-N0264, purity 99.87 %) and HA (HY-B0633, purity 98.0 %) were purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). TMP treatment groups were administered once weekly for 5 weeks. The HAG group was injected with 0.03 mL HA once weekly for 5 weeks [21]. Sham and PTOA groups received an equivalent volume of sterile saline (Fig.1).

Experiment schedule

Hindlimb grip strength

Hindlimb grip strength was measured by rat grip strength meter. Each animal was tested three times per session, and the mean value was recorded. Measurements were obtained at four time-points: immediately before surgery, 4 weeks post-surgery, and at the end of the 2nd and 5th weeks of intervention.

Gait analysis

Gait parameters were recorded with a GaitScan system that analyses ventral-view videos of SD rats walking on a transparent runway. A high-speed digital camera positioned beneath the plate captured the print impressions. In a sound-attenuated cabinet, each rat was released at one end of the 50 cm × 8 cm central recording zone; Only footprints acquired within this area were processed. The following variables were extracted from the video files: swing duration (ms), stance duration (ms), print area (%), and paw pressure (N cm-2) of the right hind limb.

Prior to every recording session, the animals were allowed three habituation crossings. For each test, three valid runs per rat were obtained and averaged. Gait analyses were performed at the same four time-points as the hindlimb grip strength measurements.

Tissue samples

After the final intervention, rats were euthanized with an overdose of urethane. Blood (2-2.5 mL per rat) and synovial fluid were collected from 10 rats per group. Blood was drawn from the abdominal aorta and centrifuged (4 °C, 3,000 rpm, 15 min) to obtain serum. Synovial fluid was obtained by intra-articular perfusion of 0.1 mL PBS followed by suction. Subsequently, the knee joints and cartilage were dissected from the rats, with the joint samples allocated for subsequent assays: 3 rats for micro-CT scanning, 3 for pathological staining, and 3 for immunohistochemistry.

Micro-CT

After excision, each joint was fixed in 10 % neutral-buffered formalin for 24 h and subsequently cleared of excess soft tissue. The scanning protocol was set to 55 kV, 200 μA, with an isotropic voxel size of 10 μm. Samples were positioned in the micro-CT gantry and the acquisition sequence was initiated. During scanning, X-rays were transmitted through the specimen and the attenuated beam was recorded by a flat-panel detector, generating a series of two-dimensional projection images.

Upon completion, the projection dataset was imported into N-Recon software for reconstruction, yielding a three-dimensional volumetric model. The tibial plateau was then isolated and subjected to quantitative analysis using CT-An software. The following trabecular bone parameters were computed: Tb.BV/TV (bone volume fraction), which reflects absolute bone mass; Tb.BS/TV (bone surface-to-tissue volume ratio), an indirect index of bone quantity; and Tb.N (trabecular number), defined as the mean number of intersections between bone and non-bone voxels, providing an estimate of trabecular density [22].

Histopathological analysis

Knee joints were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution, decalcified in 20% EDTA solution and embedded in paraffin wax. The specimens were then sectioned in the sagittal position and stained with HE and safranin O-fast green staining. The degree of cartilage was assessed by Mankin’s score. All sections were scored independently by two experienced pathologists who were blinded to the experimental grouping.

Immunohistochemistry

The immunohistochemical staining procedure was conducted as follows: Tissue sections were first deparaffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was achieved using an enzymatic method. To eliminate background staining, an endogenous peroxidase inhibitor was applied to quench the activity of endogenous peroxidase. Subsequently, the sections were blocked with bovine serum albumin (BSA) to prevent nonspecific binding. The sections were then incubated with the following primary antibodies: anti- ADAMTS5 (1:100, BS-3573R, Biosharp Co.,Ltd, Beijing, China), anti-COL II (1:100, ab34712, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), and anti-MMP13 (1:50, 18165-1-AP, Proteintech Group, Rosemount, USA). Following the removal of unbound primary antibodies by washing, the sections were treated with an IHC Detection Reagent. A DAB chromogenic reaction was subsequently performed to visualize the antigen-antibody complexes, followed by counterstaining of the cell nuclei. The sections were then dehydrated and mounted with coverslips. High-resolution images of the stained sections were captured using a digital microscope (Eclipse Ci, Nikon, Chiyoda, Japan). The percentage of positive cells was quantitatively analyzed using Image-Pro Plus software (version 6.0).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Synovial fluid was collected from the knee joints of rats in each group, and the levels of IL-β, IL-18 were measured according to the instructions of the ELISA kit (ZC-37565, ZC-56098, Zci Bio, Shanghai, China). The sensitivities were 1.0 pg/mL for IL-1β and 1.0 pg/mL for IL-18, respectively, with intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) < 10%.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from cartilage using the RNA Extraction Kit (Biosharp Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Subsequently, 1 μg of the extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Takara RNA PCR Kit (AMV) Ver. 3.0 (Takara Bio, Ohtsu, Japan). The primers used for the quantitative analysis of matrix Col2a1, Mmp13, Adamts5 and Actb were designed and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) (Table 1). Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the SYBR Green PCR Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) on the Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. The relative mRNA expression levels were determined using the 2-ΔΔCT method, with Actb serving as the endogenous reference gene. (Table 1)

Statistical Analysis

The experiments were conducted at least three times. Results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPad Prism 10.0 were used to analyze the data. Two-way repeated measures ANOVA was applied to the behavioral data for statistical analysis, with the mixed-effects model applied as the alternative for nonparametric testing. One-way ANOVA was adopted for the analysis of the remaining data, and the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used instead for nonparametric testing. Post-hoc comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni method. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Validation of PTOA model rats

Following modified Hulth surgery, the animals were monitored daily; no mortality occurred in any group. Rats in the Model group exhibited slightly dull fur with reduced sheen and diminished locomotor activity.

Three knee joints randomly selected from the operated animals were processed for HE staining (Fig.2). Histological examination revealed full-thickness cartilage disruption and marked architectural disarray. Chondrocyte density was markedly decreased, with extensive vacuolation; Matrix staining was faint, and lesions extended into the subchondral bone. The mean Mankin score for these specimens was 10.33±0.47, confirming successful induction of PTOA (Supplementary file 2).

Gait behavior

Gait analysis revealed that the Sham group exhibited orderly gait patterns, consistent velocity, and uniform stress distribution. In contrast, the PTOA group displayed a biased gait, uneven walking speed, and imbalanced stress distribution due to pain in the affected limb, consistent with the typical manifestations of post-traumatic arthralgia. To assess the effect of TMP on gait improvement in PTOA model rats, we analyzed and compared the swing time, stance time, paw print area, and paw pressure of the right hind limb across groups and across-time (Fig.3b-e and Supplementary file 4). In terms of swing time, a significant main effect of intervention group was observed (F=5.77, P<0.05); A significant main effect of observation time was observed (F=14.03, P<0.05); No significant interaction effect was found between intervention group and observation time (F=1.02, P>0.05). Post-hoc tests revealed that compared with the PTOA group, all intervention groups showed significantly decrease(P<0.05). The intervention with TMP exhibited a dose-dependent effect. The 8 mg/kg TMP group demonstrated the most prominent improvement, which was significantly lower than that of both the 2 mg/kg and 4 mg/kg groups (P<0.05); the 4 mg/kg group also showed better outcomes than the 2 mg/kg group (P<0.05). Compare to swing time at 2 weeks, each intervention group showed a significant decreasing trend at 5 weeks (P<0.05). The stance time, paw print area, and paw pressure exhibited similar recovery trend as swing time.

TMP alleviated cartilage damage in PTOA model rats

Micro-CT results

Micro-CT analysis of the knee joints (Fig.4) revealed that the Sham group exhibited smooth articular cartilage surfaces without osteophyte formation, along with well-organized and dense trabecular bone at the articular ends. In contrast, the PTOA group displayed rough articular cartilage surfaces, marked bone defects in the tibial plateau, and evident osteophyte formation. All intervention groups showed significantly alleviated pathological cartilage changes and reduced bone defects compared with the PTOA group. After 5 weeks of treatment, the TMP subgroups demonstrated significantly increased trabecular bone volume fraction (Tb.BV/TV), bone surface density (Tb.BS/TV), and trabecular number (Tb.N) compared with the PTOA group (P<0.05), indicating a reversal of bone loss pathology.

Pathological staining results

We further evaluated the extent of tissue damage through HE staining and safranin O-fast green staining (Fig.5). In the Sham group, the surface layer and fiber layer were well-aligned and intact. In contrast, the PTOA group exhibited significant cartilage degeneration, loss of proteoglycan, and disruption of the surface and fiber layers. TMP treatment induced a marked improvement in the histological appearance of the cartilage in a dose-dependent manner. The surface layer appeared smoother, and the fiber layer was more orderly arranged compared to the PTOA group, with a notable restoration of the structural integrity of the cartilage tissue. Compared to the Sham group, the PTOA group had a significantly higher Mankin’s score (P<0.01) (Fig.5c), which was markedly reduced in the TMP treatment groups (P<0.01). These findings suggested that TMP effectively alleviated the pathological symptoms in PTOA model rats.

TMP inhibited ECM degradation in PTOA model rats

We used immunohistochemical staining and qRT-PCR to assess ECM-related protein and mRNA expression in cartilage (Fig.6). The levels of cartilage protective protein COL II significantly decreased in PTOA groups (P<0.01) (Fig.6a, b). COL II expression elevated after TMP treatment (P<0.01). COL II expression increased more in group 8 mg/kg than in group 2 and 4 mg/kg TMP (P<0.01). Besides, expression of MMP13 and ADAMTS5 was higher in groups PTOA than in group Sham (P<0.01) (Fig.6a, c, d). The upregulated expression of MMP13 was mostly restored by group 2, 4, 8 mg/kg TMP (P<0.05), whereas the upregulated expression of ADAMTS5 was reversed by 8 mg/kg TMP (P<0.05). Similarly, the qRT-PCR analysis showed that Col2a1 mRNA levels were significantly reduced in the PTOA group relative to the Sham group (P<0.01) (Fig.6e). Mmp13 and Adamts5 were significantly elevated in the PTOA group relative to the Sham group (P<0.01) (Fig.6f, g). However, Col2a1 mRNA expression were significantly restored in the 2, 4, 8 mg/kg TMP group compared to the PTOA group (P<0.01), while Mmp13 and Adamts5 mRNA levels significantly reduced after 2, 4, 8 mg/kg TMP treatment (P<0.05). Our results indicated that 8 mg/kg TMP provided the maximal protection of joint cartilage.

Discussion

In this study, a modified Hulth method was employed to construct a PTOA model in SD rats by transecting the medial collateral ligament and anterior cruciate ligament, as well as excising the medial meniscus of the right hind limb knee joint. The therapeutic effects of TMP on PTOA were systematically investigated using behavioral, imaging, histopathological, and molecular biological analyses. The results of this study confirmed the hypothesis that TMP effectively alleviated PTOA symptom in rats.

The dosage of TMP employed in this study did not elicit local irritant responses upon intra-articular administration and effectively ameliorated joint pathological manifestations. The selection of intra-articular injection was predicated upon experimental objectives and targeting requirements. Distinct from the systemic administration achieved via oral gavage or intraperitoneal injection, intra-articular injection enables direct drug delivery to articular cartilage, thereby minimizing potential adverse effects associated with systemic distribution while enhancing local drug concentrations and targeted therapeutic efficacy. This approach represents a more precise drug delivery strategy specifically directed toward localized pathological lesions [23].

The core clinical manifestations of PTOA include joint pain, decreased joint stability, and impaired motor function following joint trauma [24]. Structural damage to the medial collateral ligament, anterior cruciate ligament, and medial meniscus after joint injury directly reduces knee stability, while cartilage damage and synovial inflammation lead to persistent joint pain. The transmission of pain signals via the spinal–brain pathway induces a pain-avoidance movement pattern in rats, characterized by reduced active weight-bearing and restricted range of motion in the affected limb. Over time, this not only directly impairs joint motor function but also leads to disuse atrophy of the skeletal muscles in the affected limb, especially key muscle groups such as the quadriceps and gastrocnemius, manifesting as thinning of muscle fibers, weakened contractility, and ultimately a significant decline in limb grip strength [25,26]. At the same time, the loss of joint stability and pain-induced movement avoidance further disrupt gait rhythm, specifically manifested as prolonged swing time (increased duration of affected limb lifted off to avoid weight-bearing) and shortened stance time (insufficient weight-bearing support, with quick lifting due to pain avoidance). Moreover, the paw print area is reduced (limited weight-bearing range), and paw pressure distribution becomes uneven, with pressure concentrated on the unaffected limb or non-painful areas of the affected limb, resulting in a typical PTOA gait imbalance phenotype [27,28]. In this study, after successful PTOA modeling, the PTOA group and all intervention groups showed decreased grip strength in the affected limb and significant abnormalities in gait parameters (swing time, stance time, paw print area, and paw pressure). This confirmed that under PTOA pathological conditions, knee pain and functional limitation reduced the activity level of rats, thereby leading to declined muscle function in the affected limb. Simultaneously, the reduced weight-bearing capacity and aberrant gait of the affected limb further corroborated the impairment of motor function in PTOA, which aligned with previous studies reporting that PTOA model rats developed pain-avoidance movement patterns due to joint injury-induced pain [29].

TMP effectively ameliorated gait performance in PTOA model rats, with the improvement of all gait-related indicators following 5 weeks of intervention being significantly superior to that after 2 weeks of intervention. One possible reason was that the cumulative long-term effects of TMP more effectively delayed cartilage degeneration, suppressed synovitis, and further restored joint stability, thereby reducing abnormal mechanical stimulation during movement [30]. Another reason was that sustained pain relief enabled the rats to gradually resume a normal movement pattern, with enhanced weight-bearing capacity of the affected limb, expanded paw-print area, and restored uniformity in paw pressure distribution [31]. Although the 2 mg/kg TMP group improved grip strength (indicating partial recovery of muscle function), its effect on modulating joint stability remained limited, resulting in a weaker gait improvement compared to the 4 mg/kg and 8 mg/kg TMP groups. Furthermore, no significant differences in behavioral parameters were observed between the 8 mg/kg TMP group and the HAG group at any time point, suggesting that 8 mg/kg TMP produced a comprehensive effect on restoring muscle function and correcting gait comparable to that of the positive drug.

Joint structural damage constitutes the pathological basis of PTOA. Its key distinction from ordinary osteoarthritis lies in its trauma-triggered onset, leading to multi-structural cascade injury and chronic inflammatory progression [32]. When trauma occurs, mechanical damage not only directly disrupts joint stability but also activates local inflammatory responses [33]. Even when anatomical structures are restored through surgical repair—such as ligament suturing, reconstruction, or artificial meniscus replacement—the long-term abnormalities in the postoperative intra-articular biomechanical environment, such as uneven joint surface loading and cartilage overload, cannot be fully corrected. Furthermore, the surgical procedure itself may induce synovial injury and local hemorrhage, exacerbating inflammatory stress [34]. These factors collectively drive the progression of PTOA, ultimately manifested as full-thickness cartilage degeneration, disordered subchondral bone remodeling, and osteophyte formation.

The subchondral bone, as a mechanical support structure for cartilage, is closely linked to cartilage function through its metabolic balance [35]. During the onset and progression of PTOA, degradation products released from cartilage degeneration are transmitted through the cartilage–bone interface to the subchondral bone, activating osteocytes and osteoclasts [36]. On one hand, inflammatory cytokines promote the differentiation and maturation of osteoclasts, accelerating bone resorption. On the other hand, uneven joint loading leads to localized stress concentration in the subchondral bone, stimulating abnormal proliferation of osteoblasts and resulting in osteophyte formation. Ultimately, this manifests as bone loss and disorganized trabecular architecture [37]. From the perspective of subchondral bone remodeling, Micro-CT results showed that the PTOA group exhibited rough articular cartilage surfaces, severe bone loss in the tibial plateau, and evident osteophytes. This was consistent with previous findings that under PTOA conditions, aberrant cartilage–bone signaling due to cartilage degeneration leaded to subchondral bone loss and disorganized trabecular structure [38]. After TMP intervention, the aforementioned microstructural parameters significantly increased, indicating that TMP could delay the structural progression of PTOA by inhibiting subchondral bone loss and improving trabecular bone arrangement. This effect may be associated with the ability of TMP to regulate bone metabolic balance. First, TMP may inhibit inflammation and cartilage degeneration, reducing the activation of osteoclasts by inflammatory cytokines, lowering the rate of bone resorption, and suppressing osteoclast activity. Second, TMP may directly act on osteoblasts, promoting their proliferation and mineralization functions, thereby repairing damaged trabecular structure, while also inhibiting abnormal osteoblast proliferation to reduce osteophyte formation.

The pathological mechanism of PTOA involves an imbalance in cartilage metabolism and amplification of intra-articular inflammation. COL II, MMP13, ADAMTS5, and the inflammatory factors IL-1β and IL-18 serve as key molecular markers reflecting these processes. COL II is the primary structural protein of the cartilage matrix, and its expression level directly reflects the integrity of the cartilage matrix. In contrast, MMP13 and ADAMTS5 are key proteases responsible for degrading the cartilage matrix, and their overexpression is a central driver of cartilage degeneration [39]. Trauma leads to increased chondrocyte apoptosis and suppressed synthetic function in surviving cells, directly resulting in a decreased synthesis rate of COL II and proteoglycans [40]. Concurrently, the mechanical damage triggers the excessive release of IL-1β and IL-18 from chondrocytes, synoviocytes, and infiltrating immune cells, which in turn upregulates the transcriptional activity of MMP13 and ADAMTS5. This inflammatory cascade further exacerbates the imbalance between matrix synthesis and degradation, accelerates the loss of cartilage extracellular matrix, and ultimately promotes the progression of PTOA from early cartilage damage to irreversible joint dysfunction. Moreover, the sustained elevation of these inflammatory factors and matrix-degrading proteases can also induce synovial inflammation and bone remodeling abnormalities, forming a vicious cycle that perpetuates joint pathology [41].

PTOA is not merely confined to the joint as a localized disease.Tthe release of intra-articular inflammatory factors (such as IL-1β and IL-18) can trigger systemic inflammatory responses through the bloodstream [42]. The ELISA results of this study showed that the expression levels of IL-1β and IL-18 in the serum and synovial fluid of rats in the PTOA group were significantly higher than those in the Sham group, indicating that the PTOA model successfully replicated the pathological feature of local inflammation leading to systemic inflammation. This aligned with the observed elevation of inflammatory factors in the serum of clinical PTOA patients [43]. Our results demonstrated that TMP not only suppressed local inflammatory responses in PTOA but also reduced the systemic inflammatory burden. This provided experimental support for the local administration of TMP in the treatment of PTOA.

The limitations of the present study are as follows. First, although the SD rats used in this study developed PTOA successfully via the modified Hulth method, which induced intra-articular inflammation, cartilage damage, and bone remodeling, there are notable anatomical differences between quadrupeds such as SD rats and upright-walking humans. SD rats distribute their body weight across all four limbs, resulting in substantially lower vertical stress on the knee joints compared with humans [44]. Second, although antibiotics were administered after modeling to prevent infection, bleeding and inflammation during the surgical procedure may still have influenced the onset and progression of PTOA. Third, this study lacked pharmacokinetic characterization of TMP following intra-articular administration, including local biodistribution and systemic exposure profiles, future investigations should include pharmacokinetic studies to compare systemic exposure between intra-articular and systemic administration routes. Forth, future experiments could employ additional assessment methods, such as comparing weight-bearing ratios between the injured and contralateral hind limbs, to further observe pain-related responses following traumatic osteoarthritis.

Conclusion

The therapeutic effects of TMP on PTOA mainly involved improving joint function and cartilage structure, as well as restoring the balance of extracellular matrix metabolism and eliminating inflammation. TMP showed potential as a candidate therapeutic agent for PTOA and warranted further investigation.
