Delayed aspergillus endocarditis and septic pulmonary embolism caused by a retained transcardiac foreign body
Authors: Lu Tong, Cheng Zhou, Nianguo Dong, Fei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Cardiac foreign bodies are rare and may enter the heart via various routes [1]. The clinical presentation exhibits marked heterogeneity, ranging from completely asymptomatic to acute cardiac tamponade and sudden death. Plain radiography, computed tomography, and echocardiography are the primary diagnostic modalities. Treatment strategies should be individualized, taking into account the size, shape, location of the foreign body, and hemodynamic status. Treatment options include surgical removal, percutaneous removal, or conservative management [2].

A literature search of PubMed and Web of Science over the past ten years, using the keywords “cardiac foreign body” and “endocarditis”, yielded 43 relevant articles, including 31 case reports. The basic information of these cases is summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Notably, none of the reported cases involved a cardiac foreign body traversing the liver and subsequently leading to delayed fungal endocarditis and pulmonary embolism, which further supports the novelty of our case. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a retained transcardiac foreign body traversing the heart and liver leading to delayed septic pulmonary embolism.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old female farmer presented with persistent fever and cough for one month, maximum temperature of 39.4 °C, which worsened over three days with progressive dyspnea. She received cephalosporin antibiotic treatment at a local hospital without clinical improvement and was subsequently transferred to our institution. On admission, her axillary temperature was 38.5 °C. Laboratory findings showed several abnormalities, white blood cell count (WBC) 15.09 × 10⁹/L (reference range 3–9 × 10⁹/L), hemoglobin (Hb) 89 g/L (reference range 112–149 g/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) 129 mg/L (reference range 0–4 mg/L), procalcitonin(PCT) within normal limits, serum albumin(ALB) 34.7 g/L (reference range 35–50 g/L), D-dimer 1,880 µg/L (reference range < 500 µg/L). Serum (1,3)-β-D-glucan (G test) and galactomannan (GM test) were performed on admission, prior to empirical antibiotic therapy, and both yielded negative results. Blood cultures for bacteria and fungi, as well as sputum cultures, were negative. Chest radiography demonstrated a linear metallic foreign body in the right lower lung field (Fig. 1 panel A).

Further history taking revealed that two years earlier, the patient had been struck by a foreign body while operating a lawnmower. She experienced chest discomfort at that time but did not seek medical evaluation due to the absence of bleeding. Over the subsequent two years, she had an unintentional weight loss of 15 kg.

Transthoracic echocardiography showed a rod-shaped hyperechoic foreign body in the right atrium, with vegetations visible around the rod-shaped structure, extending into the right ventricle (Fig. 1 panel B). Empirical anti-infective therapy with cefoperazone/sulbactam plus vancomycin was administered for one week, with no clinical improvement. Further imaging with computed tomography angiography was performed, which identified a metallic foreign body traversing the diaphragm from the liver into the right heart (Fig. 1 panel C) and revealed emboli in the distal branches of the right pulmonary artery (pulmonary embolus, Fig. 1 panels D).

Following multidisciplinary team discussion, the patient underwent a median sternotomy. The operation was performed under mild hypothermic (34–35 °C) beating-heart cardiopulmonary bypass, with arterial cannulation of the ascending aorta and venous drainage via the superior vena cava and femoral vein. Surgical exploration revealed a metallic rod measuring 10 cm in length and 0.5 cm in diameter, penetrating the liver, diaphragm, and right heart. The metallic foreign body was extracted via a right atriotomy, and the pulmonary emboli were removed through a direct pulmonary arteriotomy. Both were successfully retrieved (Fig. 1, panels E and F). The total operative time was 90 min. Pathological analysis of the debrided vegetations and pulmonary emboli showed positive staining for periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) and Gomori methenamine silver (GMS). The microscopic findings of septate hyphae with acute-angle branching (~ 45°) and uniform width findings were highly consistent with Aspergillus infection (Fig. 1F, inset, H&E stain, ×20). The patient was discharged uneventfully on postoperative day 10 and continued oral voriconazole (200 mg twice daily) for a planned duration of 12 weeks, with therapeutic drug monitoring (target trough, 1–5.5 µg/mL) and monthly liver function tests, along with regular medical follow‑up.

Follow-up at 3 months demonstrated satisfactory clinical recovery, with normalization of body temperature, WBC, CRP, fungal blood cultures, and echocardiographic findings. At more than 11 months postoperatively, the patient remained asymptomatic, resumed normal daily activities, showed no signs of infection, and achieved a 6‑minute walk test distance of 625 m.

Discussion

Retained cardiac foreign bodies are rare, with most cases resulting from penetrating trauma, intravenous migration, or iatrogenic retention. Complications caused by cardiac foreign bodies include mechanical injury, arrhythmias, infection, and embolism. The risk of these complications is closely related to the size, location, type, and duration of retention of the foreign body. The extent of impact also varies depending on the location where the foreign body lodges [1]. Although the clinical spectrum ranges from incidental findings to life-threatening cardiac tamponade or systemic embolism, delayed complications occurring years after the initial insult remain exceptional. To our knowledge, the present case is the first to describe a transcardiac foreign body traversing the liver and diaphragm, presenting with delayed septic pulmonary embolism secondary to Aspergillus infection two years after the penetrating injury.

Two unique features of this case warrant particular attention. First, despite a metallic rod penetrating the liver, diaphragm, and right atrium, the patient remained completely asymptomatic for two years after the initial injury. The delayed presentation may be partly attributed to the possible mechanism that the foreign body was fixed and stable, and did not cause life‑threatening complications such as hemorrhage or cardiac tamponade. Second, the possibility of Aspergillus endocardial infection should not be ruled out by negative serum fungal biomarkers and cultures. Serum fungal biomarkers may remain negative in localized Aspergillus endocarditis because of the limited fungal burden within the bloodstream and the absence of disseminated disease. In the present case, both the vegetations and pulmonary emboli showed Aspergillus infection, an extremely rare finding in penetrating cardiac foreign bodies. Given the patient’s occupation as a farmer and the circumstances of the lawnmower injury, the source of Aspergillus was likely environmental. Unlike bacterial endocarditis, Aspergillus cardiac infections are often characterized by normal procalcitonin levels, negative blood cultures, and a subacute or indolent course, as observed in this patient. These features can easily lead to misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis, emphasizing the need for a high index of suspicion for fungal etiology in patients with negative routine cultures and a history of penetrating trauma.

Regarding management, surgical removal was unequivocally the preferred option in this case. The foreign body was long, sharp, and carried vegetations. It traversed multiple organs and had already caused septic pulmonary embolism. Surgical exploration allowed en bloc removal of the foreign body and pulmonary emboli under direct vision. Postoperatively, oral voriconazole was administered as antifungal therapy, which is the standard treatment for Aspergillus endocarditis [3–4]. The optimal duration remains uncertain, but current guidelines generally recommend at least 12 weeks, followed by long‑term surveillance for relapse [5–6].

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that penetrating cardiac foreign bodies may be initially non‑fatal but carry a latent risk of late‑onset fungal infection and septic embolism. Clinicians should be aware of this rare but potentially fatal complication, particularly in patients with an indolent course, negative routine cultures, and a history of penetrating trauma. Meanwhile, it highlights the value of a high index of suspicion, timely multimodality imaging, and aggressive surgical intervention combined with targeted antifungal therapy.
