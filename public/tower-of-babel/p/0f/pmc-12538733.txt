Immunological characteristics and functions of macrophages in Mycobacterium leprae infection
Authors: Xiaodong Zhang, Haiqin Jiang, Weijun Liu, Hongsheng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveOur research aimed to clarify the roles of M1 and M2 macrophages in leprosy, focusing on their polarization states, phagocytic capacities, and the survival of M. leprae within these macrophage subsets.MethodsM1-like macrophages were induced by IFN-γ, IL-4 induced M2-like macrophages, and then they were compared with M. leprae-induced macrophages regarding cell-surface antigen expression and cytokine secretion. The phagocytic capabilities of M1-like and M2-like cells were assessed using a laser-scanning confocal microscope. Simultaneously, their bactericidal capacities were evaluated by 16S rRNA/RLEP qPCR to determine the viability of M. leprae.ResultsM1-like macrophages induced by IFN-γ were characterized by CD86 and CD68 expression and an elevated expression of the IRF-5 gene. In contrast, M2-like macrophages induced by IL-4 were distinguished by enhanced expression of CD163 and CD206 markers and the upregulation of the IRF-4 gene. M. leprae-induced macrophages encompass CD86⁺CD68⁺(M1 markers ) and CD163⁺CD206⁺( M2 markers ) subpopulations, potentially displaying characteristics of both M1-like and M2-like phenotypes. M1-like macrophages secreted Th1 cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-15, and TNF-α, whereas M2-like macrophages secreted IL-10 and TGF-β. At different stages, macrophages induced by M. leprae released Th1 and Th2 cytokines. The phagocytic ability of M2-like macrophages exceeded that of M1-like macrophages. Nevertheless, M. leprae viability was notably higher in M2-like macrophages, indicating a weaker sterilizing capacity than in M1-like macrophages. Conversely, M1-like macrophages demonstrated potent bactericidal activity, although their phagocytic ability was relatively lower than that of M2-like macrophages.ConclusionOur findings suggested the notable significance of macrophage polarization in leprosy. M1 macrophages exhibited a relatively strong bactericidal effect against M. leprae, while M2 macrophages might have been somewhat associated with pathogen persistence.Keywords: Macrophage polarization, Leprosy, M1 macrophages, M2 macrophages, Mycobacterium leprae

Introduction

Leprosy is an ancient disease that mainly affects the skin and nerves, causing damage to the body. It is categorized as an insidious, chronic condition and persists endemic in over 120 countries, revealing an annual increase of over 200,000 new cases [1, 2]. Leprosy is a chronic mycobacterial infection with high infectivity but low pathogenicity. The clinical presentation of leprosy comprises a spectrum influenced by the interplay between the immune response and infection [3]. Based on histological and immunological features, leprosy is classified into five types. At the same time, depending on the number of skin lesions and nerve involvement, leprosy can be divided into polypustular and oligopustular [3]. In the cutaneous lesions of tuberculoid-type patients, the predominant cytokines expressed are IL-1β [4], IL-2 [5], IL-7, IL-15 [6], TNF-α [5], and IFN-γ [5, 7]. In the lepromatous type, IL-4, IL-6, IL-5 [7], and IL-10 [5, 7] are predominantly expressed [8, 9].In the skin lesions of leprosy patients with unstable borderline leprosy, a significant population of reactive and non-reactive T cells polarizes towards type 1-like T cells, which mainly produce IFN-γ and TNF-α. In contrast, type 2-like T cells primarily produce IL-4, IL-5, IL-10 [5], and IL-13 [4, 7, 10]. However, the exact role of these immune responses in leprosy infection and the specific functions of IL-4 and IFN-γ in this process remain undetermined [7, 9, 11]. Leprosy is widely acknowledged as an ideal model for investigating the intricate interplay between pathogen load and diverse host immune responses during infection. Nonetheless, leprosy’s pathogenesis remains incomplete due to the absence of a suitable animal model.

Macrophages can differentiate into two distinct phenotypes: classically activated (M1) and alternatively activated (M2) macrophages, which are closely associated with Th1 and Th2 immune responses [12, 13]. In vitro, the M1 polarization is stimulated by IFN-γ [14] and LPS [15], while M2 polarization is induced by IL-4 [16]. M1 macrophages primarily participate in the immune response to pathogenic microorganisms and tumors. Conversely, M2 macrophages primarily participate in the anti-inflammatory process, contribute to tissue regeneration, and help to combat parasitic infections [12, 13, 15]. GM-CSF plays a crucial role in activating macrophages into the M0 state. M0 is an unpolarized state lacking distinct functions. It is in a basal condition and is an M1/M2 precursor [17]. In this study, we stimulated macrophages using GM-CSF and subsequently polarized them towards an M1-like phenotype by employing IFN-γ and towards an M2-like phenotype using IL-4. We investigated their morphological and secretory differences compared to those of M. leprae-infected macrophages. Additionally, we evaluated the phagocytic and bactericidal activity of these two cell populations against M. leprae.

Materials and methods

Cells and bacteria

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from healthy adults aged 18 to 40 who provided informed consent and had no history of infections or positive PPD test results. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated, as mentioned in the previous studies [6, 18], using Ficoll Paque (GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ, USA). The cells were then suspended in an AIM-V medium (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA) and cultured in a 24-well plate (Corning, Tewksbury, MA, USA). Monocytes adhered to the plate for one hour and were subsequently incubated in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) and 20% fetal calf serum (FCS, HyClone, Waltham, MA, USA) for 5 days at 37 °C [18, 19]. Thus, the monocytes differentiated into macrophages. On the fifth day, stimulation was achieved by separately adding IFN-γ (5 ng/ml) and IL-4 (50 ng/ml) or M. leprae (MOI 50:1) [19], and the cultures were replenished with the corresponding stimulants (the M. leprae group was replenished only with medium) every 2 days. Based on the various stimuli, the subjects were categorized into 5 groups: control group (medium), GM-CSF group, GM-CSF + IFN-γ group, GM-CSF + IL-4 group, and M. leprae group. All cytokines were purchased from PeproTech (Rocky Hill, NJ, USA).

The M. leprae strain (Thai-53), obtained from the Leprosy Research Center of the National Institute of Infectious Diseases in Japan, was cultured in nude mice foot pads and identified through Hsp65 DNA sequencing [18, 19]. The footpads of freshly sacrificed nude mice were disinfected and rinsed. The muscles and skin within the footpads were meticulously minced and ground to a fine consistency. After washing with Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), these tissue samples were subjected to digestion with 0.5% trypsin (Amresco, LLC, Solon, OH, USA) at 37 °C for 1 h. Upon completion of the digestion process, the samples were centrifuged and treated with 2% NaOH (Shanghai Shiyi Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Subsequently, the reaction was neutralized using a cold HBSS solution. After being washed twice with HBSS, the bacterial samples were stored in 10% FCS-RPMI 1640 medium at −80 °C in a freezer and were intended to be utilized within one month. Before use, the M. leprae suspension was rewarmed to room temperature and treated with 2000 IU/ml penicillin (Harbin Pharmaceutical Group, Harbin, Heilongjiang, China) for 30 min. After centrifugation, the precipitate was resuspended in 10% FCS-RPMI 1640 medium. A uniform suspension of M. leprae was achieved by repeatedly passing the suspension through a 1-ml syringe (Shandong Weigao Group, Weihai, Shandong, China) fitted with a fine needle. According to the manufacturer’s instructions (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), the acid-fast staining technique was employed to visualize individual bacilli and enumerate their numbers.

Nude mice (BALB/c-nu/nu) were obtained from Charles River Laboratories, Inc. For euthanasia, 1 ml of 3% pentobarbital sodium (Harbin Pharmaceutical Group, Harbin, Heilongjiang, China) was administered intraperitoneally, and the response was monitored. When the mice exhibited decreased activity, muscle relaxation, no reaction to external stimuli (e.g., no autonomous reflex to toe pinching), stable and slowed breathing, closed eyes, and no response to light, they were deemed deep anesthetic. 10% KCl (0.3–0.5 ml/kg body weight, from Harbin Pharmaceutical Group, Harbin, Heilongjiang, China) was intravenously administered, and the cessation of heartbeat and respiration, along with complete muscular relaxation, confirmed the euthanasia of the mice [20].

Ethical approval for collecting human tissue samples was obtained from the ethics committee of the Dermatology Hospital of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (2021-KY-013). Written informed consent was obtained from the participants, and the study adhered to the guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Animal experiments receive support from the same organization (202 Animal No. 025).

Instrumental analysis

Cell surface antigens were analyzed using flow cytometry and confocal microscopy. After 5 and 10 days of cultivation, macrophages were harvested for analysis of cell surface antigens associated with macrophage polarization CD68, CD86, CD163, and CD206 (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), utilizing a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) [18, 19]. Cells exhibiting positive staining with 7-amino actinomycin D (Shanghai Biyun Tian Biological Company, Shanghai, China), indicating necrotic cell death, were excluded from data analysis. The monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) utilized for antigen analysis included PerCP-CyTM 5.5-conjugated mAb against CD86, fluorescein phycoerythrin-conjugated mAb against CD68 and CD206, and phycoerythrin-conjugated mAb against CD163. The quadrant-specific percentages represented the proportion of cells that exhibited positive staining for the variable antigens. On day 10, we stained the cells with labeled antibodies to CD68 and CD163 and stained the cell nuclei with the Hoechst 33,258 solution(Shanghai Biyun Tian Biological Company, Shanghai, China). The morphology of the cells and the expression of surface CD molecules were observed using confocal microscopy (Olympus, Tokyo, JP).

Real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent (ELISA) assays

The expression levels of the cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-15, IFN-γ, TGF-β, and IL-10) were assessed by Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction. First, total cellular RNA was isolated with the RNeasy Mini kit (QIAGEN, Düsseldorf, NRW, Germany) to analyze cytokine expression. These RNAs were then reverse-transcribed via a reverse transcription system (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). RT-PCR was carried out with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer [18, 19]. The purification and amplification of DNA were carried out as previously published [18, 21]. The DNA levels were measured on the ABI 7300 instrument with ABI software (Bio-Systems, Grand Island, NY, USA) [21]. ELISA kits (NeoBioscience, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) were used to quantify the concentrations of various cytokines present in the supernatant, and the results were expressed in picograms of protein per milliliter (pg/ml), following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer [18, 19].

Phagocytosis of macrophages

On days 8 and 10, cellular adhesion to the well was established, as previously described [6, 18]. M. leprae (MOI 50:1) was introduced to the cells and incubated for one hour. Subsequently, the staining of bacilli (Auramine O, Shanghai Rujibio, Shanghai, China), cell membrane (Cellmask, Life Technology, California, USA), and nuclei (Hoechst 33258) was performed as instructed. The observation was directly conducted using a fluorescence microscope. A total count of one hundred cells was recorded for both macrophage phagocytosis occurrence and non-occurrence within randomly selected areas; additionally, the number of bacteria engulfed by these macrophages was simultaneously recorded. This procedure was repeated three times to ensure accuracy.

Determination of M. leprae Viability

The Viability of M. leprae was evaluated by RT-PCR [22]. After co-culturing M. leprae (MOI 50:1) with macrophages (1 × 10e5) from each experimental group for 8 h, the macrophages were lysed using 0.1% NaOH and neutralized with 0.1% HCl (Shanghai Shiyi Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) to isolate M. leprae. The levels of 16 S rRNA and the RLEP gene from that sample were extracted by TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, California, USA) following the manufacturer’s guidelines and detected by RT-PCR using the procedures detailed in the previous section. The viability of M. leprae was then reflected by the levels of the ratio of 16 S rRNA to RLEP [22, 23].

Statistical analysis

The experiments were replicated three times to ensure accuracy, and the measurement of target gene mRNA expression was determined using the 2-ΔΔCt method. The statistical analysis was performed utilizing SPSS version 13.0 software (SPSS Inc, Chicago, USA). One-way ANOVA and the general linear model were applied to evaluate the significance of the results. The standard errors of the mean for the three replicates were represented by horizontal bars.

Discussion

Macrophages can enhance cell-mediated immunity and play a pivotal role in providing an essential niche for intracellular bacteria, allowing them to persist and evade host defense mechanisms [12, 24]. M1 promotes type 1 cell immunity, while M2 supports type 2 immunity [15, 25]. After incubation with GM-CSF, macrophages were treated with IFN-γ to differentiate them into M1-like macrophages (CD68 + CD86+) and with IL-4 to differentiate them into M2-like macrophages (CD163 + CD206+). Our results were consistent with previous studies on the distinctive features of M1 and M2 cells [16]. Macrophages stimulated with M. leprae were both CD68 + CD86 + and CD163 + CD206+. It is known that M1 cells predominate in TT leprosy [26] and M2 cells predominate in LL lesions [26, 27].

IRF-5 plays a crucial role in promoting the differentiation of macrophages into the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and is important in regulating macrophage function [28, 29]. It upregulates pro-inflammatory genes and inhibits anti-inflammatory mediators, thereby polarizing macrophages towards a pro-inflammatory phenotype [28]. IRF-4 also differentiates various types of immune cells to influence immunity [30, 31]. Our research found that within the first five days of GM-CSF induction, macrophages upregulated IRF-5 and IRF-4 gene expression. However, there is some controversy regarding the regulatory role of GM-CSF on IRF-4 [32–34]. These results indicate that GM-CSF had the potential to allow the transformation into both M1 and M2 phenotypes after activation. A significant upregulation of the IRF-5 gene occurred in M1-like macrophages, whereas no notable alterations were detected in the expression of the IRF-4 gene. The expression level of the IRF-4 gene in M2-like macrophages was upregulated, but the expression of the IRF-5 gene was unchanged. After M. leprae infection, the expression of IRF-5 and IRF-4 in macrophages increased significantly, suggesting a possible “coexistence” of M1 and M2 macrophages.

IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-1β are crucial for innate and adaptive immunity in combating pathogen infections. IL-6 and TGF-β promote the differentiation of Th17 cells and the secretion of IL-17, which initiates leprosy reactions [35]. IL-6 is important during the maintenance phase of leprosy [36]. IL-15, similar in structure to IL-2, can lead to limited phagocytic activity against M. tuberculosis and provides the best host defense against microbial infections [37]. IL-15 induces Langhans giant cells to participate in the leprosy immune response [6].IL-10 can induce macrophages to enhance mycobacteria phagocytosis and promote the uptake of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) to form foam cells. However, it fails to trigger the vitamin D-dependent antimicrobial pathway, thus creating a favorable environment for mycobacteria survival. In leprosy, it is associated with progressive multibacillary lesions and promotes the progression of chronic infection [37]. The expression level of IL-4 remained consistently low across all groups. Previous studies have also reported the absence of IL-4 in PBMCs co-cultured with M. leprae. It is speculated that the induction of M2-like cells by M. leprae may not only rely on the IL-4 pathway but also through alternative mechanisms. These cells exhibit similar characteristics as those induced by IL-4, but they may encompass additional subtypes alongside M2 [19]. In our study, macrophages stimulated by M. leprae exhibited M1 and M2 characteristics in their secretory functions, with a tendency towards M1-like cells.

M1 macrophages have a bactericidal effect and secrete many pro-inflammatory factors; the M2 phenotype limits inflammatory responses and type I adaptive immunity but clears debris, promotes blood vessel formation and is involved in tissue remodeling and repair. Consistent with traditional views, our study found that the phagocytic ratio and phagocytic index of M2-like cells induced by IL-4 were significantly elevated, whereas the M. leprae group had high phagocytic activity. The phagocytic ratio and phagocytic index of M1-like cells induced by IFN-γ showed no increase. However, on the 10th day, the phagocytic index of M1-like cells exhibited a significantly higher value than the GM-CSF group, whereas the viability of M. leprae diminished. It is speculated that M2-like macrophages (IL-4 induced) enhance phagocytic capacity within a specific range while diminishing bactericidal efficacy; conversely, M1-like macrophages (IFN-γ induced) enhance bactericidal efficacy without compromising phagocytic capacity.

Interestingly, in contrast to previous studies that utilized GM-CSF and IL-4 to differentiate macrophages into dendritic-like cells [38], in our observations, macrophages induced in the GM-CSF + IL-4 group were observed to have a significant increase in IL-10 and TGF-β secretion, as well as an increase in IRF-4 expression levels. Flow cytometry analysis revealed elevated CD163 and CD209 expression levels in these cells. These findings suggest a propensity towards M2a-type macrophage characteristics.

Conclusion

M1-like macrophages induced by GM-CSF and IFN-γ are characterized by increased expression and secretion of pro-inflammatory factors, thereby enhancing their ability to engulf M. leprae and display augmented bactericidal efficacy. In contrast, the GM-CSF and IL-4-induced M2-like macrophages are characterized by enhanced phagocytic capacity, reduced bactericidal activity, and elevated expression and secretion of inhibitory inflammatory factors. The phagocytic capacity of M2-like cells was enhanced, while their bactericidal ability was attenuated. M. leprae-activated macrophages exhibited morphological and functional similarities with GM-CSF and IFN-γ-induced M1-like macrophages while showing some of the characteristics of M2-type macrophages induced by the combination. Upon contracting leprosy, macrophages play a significant role in the immune response. As the disease progresses, macrophages undergo polarization changes to enhance their phagocytic capacity and effectively restrict bacterial dissemination.

Limitations

This study explored macrophage polarization in M. leprae infection, yet it has limitations. Firstly, Our research represents a relatively stable in-vitro immune model of multibacillary leprosy but does not fully replicate the body’s complex microenvironment. Upon stimulated culturing, macrophages can generate diverse cell types, requiring further in-depth analysis. Despite diverse detection methods, the cytokine network’s complexity left some factor interactions unclear, constraining research depth and breadth. Animal experimentation and more in-depth research should be conducted to offer precise targets and strategies for leprosy immunotherapy.
