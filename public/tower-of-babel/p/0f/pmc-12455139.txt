Longitudinal associations between bilateral versus unilateral knee osteoarthritis and physical performance measures
Authors: Jennifer S Hanberg, Faith Selzer, Elena Losina, Jeffrey N Katz, Jamie E Collins
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBilateral knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is common, but evidence for the effect of bilateral versus unilateral KOA on functional outcomes is conflicting. We aimed to examine the association between bilateral versus unilateral KOA and physical performance measures over two years.MethodsOur cohort included adults with symptomatic, radiographic KOA. Study assessments at baseline, 12 and 24 months included the 30-s chair sit-to-stand task (CST) and 40-m walk (40 ​MW). We defined bilateral KOA as Kellgren-Lawrence grade ≥2 and Knee Osteoarthritis Outcome Pain Scale ≥17/100 in both knees. We used linear mixed models to (1) assess the cross-sectional association between the presence of bilateral versus unilateral KOA at each timepoint and performance measures, and (2) assess the association between bilateral versus unilateral KOA at baseline and longitudinal changes in performance measures. Models were adjusted for clinical and demographic covariates.Results101 participants were included. At baseline, 43/101 (43 ​%) had bilateral KOA. In adjusted models, bilateral, versus unilateral, KOA was associated with 1.0 fewer stands on the CST (95 ​% CI: 2.2–0.1), and a 0.03 ​m/s slower 40 ​MW (95 ​% CI: 0.10–0.04). Average 2-year change in CST was −0.6 stands in those with baseline bilateral KOA and −0.7 in those with unilateral KOA (between-group difference, 0.1 stands (95 ​% CI: 1.7–1.8)). The baseline bilateral KOA group had greater worsening in 40 ​MW time (between-group difference −0.10 ​m/s (95 ​% CI: -0.20–0.00)).ConclusionsWe did not find clinically significant associations between bilateral vs unilateral KOA and performance on two physical performance tasks over two years of follow-up in this cohort study.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Knee, Bilateral, Physical performance

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common type of arthritis and is a leading cause of disability and healthcare expenditure in the United States, with OA of the knee occurring in approximately 9 ​% of the US population by the age of 60 [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. The clinical course of OA is heterogeneous and individualized prognostication is difficult. Given the clinical heterogeneity in OA progression over time, there is a need to develop better tools to predict the progression of OA in individual patients [6,7].

Bilateral involvement occurs in about 1/3 to 2/3 of patients with knee OA (KOA), depending on the population examined [[8], [9], [10]]. Bilateral KOA is of clinical interest due to its association with reduced quality of life and the potential for bilateral joint replacement, and because it could represent a practical and clinically accessible prognostic factor for predicting progression [11]. Previously published definitions of bilateral KOA are heterogeneous, with some authors defining bilateral KOA by the presence of radiographic KOA only [12], others by the presence of both pain and radiographic KOA [10,13], and others [8,14] by bilateral pain with radiographic change on at least one side. Bilateral knee osteoarthritis is more common among individuals with low educational attainment and is associated with higher rates of widespread pain [15,16]. Existing evidence for the clinical importance of prevalent bilateral KOA with respect to symptoms and physical functioning is conflicting. Some studies have reported no difference in pain, walking speed, and gait parameters [2,12,13] while others have found worse chair sit-stand performance, higher pain sensitization, and greater risk for joint replacement among participants with bilateral KOA [8,17,18].

In addition to gaps in knowledge regarding the effect of prevalent bilateral KOA on pain and function, there are also important unanswered questions concerning the longitudinal progression of OARSI-endorsed [19] (Osteoarthritis Research Society International) objective performance measures over time. Existing cross-sectional studies reveal weak correlations between more frequently collected subjective measures (pain, quality of life) and objective, physical performance measures (including chair sit-to-stand performance and walk speed) [16]. Furthermore, although many studies have attempted to describe and quantify trajectories of pain and structural change in KOA [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25]], few studies have reported physical performance measures at multiple timepoints [12,26]. Sharma and colleagues [12] investigated prognostic factors for poor physical function measured by chair-stand performance over 3 years. They found that 66 ​% of participants had a poor outcome on the chair sit-to-stand task, defined as either no change in the quintile of performance if the participant was in the 1st-3rd quintiles at the baseline visit, or worsening in the quintile of performance (e.g., from 2nd to 1st (worst) quintile). Baseline radiographic bilateral knee OA was one of 14 candidate risk factors examined and the authors did not find associations between this and chair stand performance over 3 years. However, 90 ​% of participants had bilateral knee OA at baseline. Baumbach et al. [26] aimed to build high-dimensional models to predict individual changes in several outcomes including 40-m walking speed. Outcomes were measured over just 6 weeks and the authors did not include presence of bilateral disease as a predictor. Additional prospective studies are required to examine longer-term changes in these physical performance outcomes and to assess whether bilateral disease is associated with worsening physical function.

In this investigation, we sought to examine the association between bilateral versus unilateral symptomatic, radiographic knee osteoarthritis and physical performance measures over two years of follow-up in a cohort of patients with KOA. We hypothesized (1) that bilateral (as compared with unilateral) disease is associated with poorer physical performance cross-sectionally, and (2) that participants with bilateral KOA at baseline experience greater worsening in physical performance over two years.

Methods

Study design and sample: This was a secondary analysis of prospectively collected data from the Osteoarthritis Registry of Biomarker and Imaging Trajectories (ORBIT) cohort, which has been described previously [20]. The ORBIT study recruited participants from the Brigham and Women's Hospital Orthopedic and Arthritis Center in Boston, MA between 2018 and 2020. Inclusion criteria included: age ≥40 years, knee pain for >4 weeks, prior radiograph with Kellgren-Lawrence (K-L) grade 2 or 3 KOA of either knee within 2 years of screening, fluency in English, and a modified Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) pain score ≥2/12. To calculate the modified KOOS score, participants were asked to rate their level of pain in the more bothersome knee while walking on flat surfaces, walking up or down stairs, and standing upright, with each question scored from 0 (no pain) to 4 (extreme pain); a score of at least 2/12 was specified as a minimum pain threshold for inclusion in the ORBIT study; i.e., at least mild pain on two of three items or moderate pain on one item. Exclusion criteria were: prior radiograph with K-L grade 4 disease in either knee, planned knee replacement within 1 year, prior knee replacement, inflammatory arthritis, neurological conditions precluding questionnaire completion, pregnancy, nursing home residence, contraindication to MRI, unwillingness to provide blood specimen, or receipt of Worker's Compensation.

Participants were followed over 48 months, with study visits at baseline, 12, and 24 months incorporating physical performance measures, questionnaires, and imaging, including standing bilateral radiographs. Follow-up questionnaires were administered at 48 months. Measurements were carried out by trained and certified research staff.

Exposures: Our primary exposure of interest was bilateral, compared to unilateral, KOA. We defined bilateral KOA as (1) the presence of K-L grade 2 or higher on bilateral knee radiographs and (2) the presence of pain in both knees, defined as a score ≥17/100 on the full KOOS Pain Scale. This threshold is similar to the study inclusion criterion: mild pain on greater than 2/3 of KOOS Pain items or moderate pain on greater than 1/3 of KOOS Pain items. Subjects who did not fulfill both of these criteria were labeled as having unilateral KOA. We assessed the presence of bilateral KOA at each timepoint. Our primary analyses included all participants. In secondary analysis, we excluded participants whose bilateral vs. unilateral status changed over time.

Outcomes: The primary outcome was performance on the 30-s chair sit-to-stand task [19]. This test measures the number of times a participant can complete a full transfer of weight from sitting to standing within 30 ​s. The secondary outcome was walking speed on the 40-m walk task, which assesses the amount of time it takes for a participant to walk a 10-m distance four consecutive times at their fastest walking pace. Both measures are part of the set of tests recommended by OARSI to assess physical function in people diagnosed with hip or knee OA [19]. The 30-s chair sit-to-stand task was chosen as the primary outcome based on prior findings from Riddle and Stratford, who identified that performance on the chair sit-to-stand task, but not the 20-m walk, was associated with bilateral versus unilateral knee pain in a cohort of participants with radiographic KOA in at least one knee [8]. 40-m walk speed was chosen as the secondary outcome due to its relevance to performance in daily life, understanding that it is also influenced by non-musculoskeletal factors such as cardiopulmonary disease. Participants completed the KOOS Pain and Quality of Life (0–100, 100 worst) questionnaire at each visit. We assessed the outcomes as continuous measures and also dichotomized changes over time as worsening by the minimally clinically important difference (MCID) or greater. The MCID thresholds were 2.6 stands and 0.3 ​m/s for the 30-s chair sit-to-stand and 40-m walk, and 8 points out of 100 for KOOS Pain and Quality of Life [27,28]. Outcomes measured after a knee replacement were excluded from analyses.

Covariates: We determined a priori that several clinical covariates might confound the observed association between bilateral vs unilateral knee OA and physical performance outcomes. These included age, sex, baseline duration of pain in the more bothersome knee (2 years or more versus <2 years), education (associate's degree or higher versus some college or less), body mass index, widespread pain (assessed via a modified Widespread Pain Index, with knee scores omitted), and the use of therapeutic injections within 3 months prior to each measurement of physical performance (reported by the participant). Age and duration of pain were chosen as covariates due to the higher likelihood of developing bilateral symptoms with time, while education may be associated with occupational exposures that increase the risk of injury and unilateral OA. The widespread pain index was hypothesized to be associated with risk of diffuse pain and therefore a higher likelihood of reporting bilateral knee pain, and the presence of injections was included as a covariate as it may influence the presence of pain at the time of questionnaire (and hence classification of a participant as having bilateral versus unilateral knee KOA).

We also calculated the baseline Charlson Comorbidity Index, which was reported as a variable of interest and used for multiple imputation but not expected to be a confounder of the primary exposure-outcome relationship [29]. A history of knee injury was assessed by questionnaire, indicating whether the participant had ever sustained a knee injury that caused difficulty walking for at least one week.

Ethics Approval: The ORBIT study was approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board.

Statistical Analysis: For categorical variables, we calculated and reported frequencies and percentages, and for continuous variables, we assessed measures of central tendency and spread as either mean (standard deviation) or median (25th-75th percentile) as appropriate.

We used linear mixed modeling to assess the bivariable and multivariable-adjusted relationship between bilateral (vs. unilateral) symptomatic and radiographic KOA and the outcomes of interest. The primary exposure and covariates were considered as fixed effects, with each individual participant included as a random effect to account for repeated measures over time. All analyses included up to three timepoints per participant.

The first set of analyses examined the cross-sectional association between the presence of bilateral disease and physical performance, assessed at each visit. Covariates in the multivariable model included study visit, age, sex, education, BMI, baseline duration of pain, the baseline modified widespread pain, and the presence of therapeutic injection in either knee within 3 months prior to a given study visit.

The second hypothesis addressed whether the presence of bilateral disease at baseline was associated with a faster rate of worsening physical performance over time. For this set of analyses, the presence of bilateral disease was defined at baseline and we added an interaction term between time and the baseline bilateral indicator variable. In the primary analysis, we only considered baseline bilateral disease status.

In pre-specified sensitivity analyses for both hypotheses, we excluded participants whose bilateral vs. unilateral status changed over time.

Missing data: Linear mixed effects models are robust to data missing at random (MAR) [30]. However, given the relatively high frequency of missing follow-up data, we performed several sensitivity analyses, including multiple imputation of missing outcomes by chained equations, to assess the potential bias due to non-random missing data on our primary findings [31]. The imputation model included age, sex, BMI, modified widespread pain scores, education level, the Charlson comorbidity index, and an indicator for bilateral KOA at baseline. We also imputed missing covariates (education level, presence of bilateral vs nonbilateral disease at follow-up visits, and history of joint injection within 3 months prior to missed follow-up visits). We considered a variety of scenarios under both the MAR and missing not at random (MNAR) assumptions. Under the MAR assumption, we assume that missing outcomes can be imputed from observed outcomes and covariates; under MNAR we assume that actual outcomes may differ from those imputed based on observed data alone [32]. We used a tipping point approach in MNAR sensitivity analyses, imputing missing outcomes under a range of assumptions to determine how severe the missing data imbalance must be to change our conclusions. We ranged imputed data between ±2 standard deviations higher or lower than was predicted based on observed outcomes and covariates alone, including scenarios where imputed data was higher/lower than predicted for all participants with missing data, for participants with bilateral KOA only, and for participants with unilateral KOA only. We generated 20 imputed datasets and combined the results using Rubin's rules [31].

Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value of <0.05. We performed the analyses in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with RStudio version 2024.04.2 (Posit Software).

Results

The study included 101 participants who completed a baseline visit, of whom 72 and 62 completed the primary outcome assessment at the 12- and 24-month follow-up visits (Table 1). Details of cohort assembly are reported in Fig. S1. The mean age was 64 years and 63 ​% of patients were female. At baseline, 43 ​% of participants were categorized as having bilateral KOA. Participants with bilateral KOA at baseline were on average 3 years younger than those in the unilateral group (62 years compared to 65 years), and 33/41 (80 ​%) had a body mass index of at least 25 ​kg/m2 compared to 43/57 (75 ​%) in the unilateral group (Table 1). The distribution of sex and educational level was similar between groups (Table 1). A history of knee injury was present in 44 ​% (N ​= ​19) of patients with bilateral OA at baseline, compared to 59 ​% (N ​= ​34) of patients with unilateral OA. Median modified widespread pain index score (omitting pain in the knees) was 4 [25th-75th percentile, 3–8] in the unilateral group and 3 [2–5] in the bilateral group, with the quartiles suggesting substantial overlap in the distribution between groups. While most participants with bilateral or unilateral KOA at baseline remained in the same category throughout follow-up, 22 participants (22 ​%) met criteria for both bilateral and unilateral KOA at different times during the study. 10 of 43 participants classified as bilateral at baseline did not meet the criteria for bilateral KOA at one or more subsequent timepoints, and 22 of 58 participants classified as unilateral at baseline did meet the criteria for bilateral KOA at one or more subsequent timepoints.

Cross-sectional between-group differences

Chair sit-to-stand performance was similar between the groups at each visit (Fig. 1). At baseline, the median number of stands was 11 (25th-75th percentile, 9–13) in the group with bilateral KOA compared to 12 (9–15) among those classified as having unilateral KOA. At 24 months the median number of stands was 11 in both groups (Table 2). The 40-m walk speed was similar between groups at baseline (mean 1.5 (0.4) m/s vs. 1.5 (0.3) m/s) and at the 12- and 24-month visits (Table 2). KOOS pain and quality-of-life scores were worse at all 3 timepoints among participants with bilateral vs. unilateral KOA (Table 2).

Change over time between groups by baseline bilateral KOA

Of the participants with 24-month data, a net worsening by the MCID of 2.6 stands or more [27] occurred in 5 of 26 (19 ​%) participants with baseline bilateral KOA compared to 10 of 36 (27 ​%) of those with unilateral baseline KOA. Among participants with follow-up data at 24 months, net worsening by the MCID of 0.3 ​m/s occurred in 4 of 25 (15 ​%) participants with baseline bilateral KOA compared to 3 of 36 (8 ​%) participants with unilateral disease. On average, the bilateral group demonstrated a slight net worsening in walk speed over 24 months (worsening by 0.03 (0.26) m/s) compared to a slight improvement (0.05 (0.23) m/s) in the unilateral group.

Unadjusted linear mixed models did not show an association between bilateral versus unilateral KOA and more rapid change over time in 30-s chair sit-to-stand performance. The estimated change in mean chair sit-to-stand performance from 0 to 24 months was −0.6 stands (95 ​% CI, −1.9 to 0.6) in the bilateral group compared to −0.8 stands (95 ​% CI, −1.9 to 0.3) in the unilateral group. Over the 24-month follow-up period, the estimated decrease in mean chair sit-to-stand performance was 0.1 stands fewer in the bilateral group compared to the unilateral group with a between-group difference of 0.1 stands (95 ​% CI, −1.5 to 1.8); similar results were observed in multivariable-adjusted models (Table 4). After adjusting for potential confounders, the estimated mean 40-m walk speed decreased by 0.04 ​m/s over 24 months in the bilateral group (95 ​% CI, −0.12 to 0.04) but increased by 0.06 ​m/s (95 ​% CI, 0.00–0.13) in the unilateral group. The estimated between-group difference in 24-month change scores was −0.10 ​m/s (95 ​% CI, −0.20 to 0.00; Table 4), indicating a small decrease in walk speed for the bilateral group compared to the unilateral group.

Sensitivity analyses

After performing multiple imputation of missing outcomes, we found that the effect size estimates for our primary and secondary outcomes were stable with imputation of between-group differences for missing values up to 1 standard deviation, suggesting that results are robust to MNAR dropout. The 95 ​% confidence intervals for models on imputed data were widened compared to our primary estimates and included no difference between groups in all cases. The tipping point at which some outcomes demonstrated evidence for an interaction between time and bilateral KOA status was at ​± ​2 standard deviations, suggesting that results are robust to all but an extreme MNAR missing data mechanism.

Results of sensitivity analysis excluding the 22 participants whose unilateral vs. bilateral status changed over time are shown in Table 5. In this sensitivity analysis, bilateral vs unilateral KOA was associated with an adjusted between-group difference of −2.3 stands (95 ​% CI, −4.5 to −0.2) at each timepoint, but the confidence intervals for between-group difference in 40 ​m walk included no difference (estimated between-group difference −0.10 ​m/s, 95 ​% CI -0.25 to 0.06 ​m/s). The estimated between-group difference in 24-month change in chair sit-to-stand performance was −0.6 stands (95 ​% CI -2.6 to 1.3). Bilateral KOA was associated with a greater rate of worsening in walk speed over time compared to unilateral KOA (estimated 24-month between-group difference, −0.16 ​m/s, 95 ​% CI -0.28 to −0.04).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, we did not find that the presence of bilateral, compared to unilateral, symptomatic and radiographic knee osteoarthritis was associated with worse physical performance on the 30-s chair sit-to-stand or 40-m walk. We also did not find that participants with bilateral compared to unilateral KOA worsened faster with respect to physical performance measurements over two years of follow-up.

Few studies have examined physical performance measures specifically among persons with bilateral vs. unilateral KOA. Messier and colleagues [13] did not find differences in kinetic and kinematic parameters, such as stride length, support time, and hip and knee forces, among participants with bilateral symptomatic and radiographic KOA, but did not specifically examine physical performance tests. White et al. [33] examined questionnaire-based measures of physical functioning and reported that both low physical function at baseline, and improvement in function over follow-up, were more common in participants with unilateral than bilateral pain. Riddle and Stratford [8] found that bilateral KOA with severe pain in at least one knee was associated with a worse chair sit-to-stand performance (adjusted between-group difference of 0.06–0.09 stands per second, or 1.8 to 2.7 stands per 30 ​s); this difference was not observed in participants reporting mild or moderate pain in both knees, and was not observed for the outcome of 20-m walk speed [8]. Our estimated between-group difference in the primary analysis of 1.0 stands was smaller than this, but the effect size among participants with consistently bilateral or consistently unilateral KOA was of a similar magnitude to the result reported by Riddle and Stratford. Additionally, our cohort included a higher proportion of female participants (63 ​% versus 50 ​%) than that reported by Riddle and Stratford, and included participants with Kellgren-Lawrence grade 2 or higher radiographic KOA (versus 3 or higher in Riddle and Stratford's cohort). It is possible that the exclusion of participants with milder radiographic KOA in the Riddle and Stratford study may have led to greater observed between-group differences.

Our study adds to the existing literature with a longitudinal modeling approach that quantifies the association between bilateral disease and objective physical performance measurements over time. We found that neither chair sit-to-stand performance nor 40-m walk speed was associated with bilateral versus unilateral KOA status, and estimated between-group differences did not approach MCIDs in the primary analysis [27]. We also observed that performance on both the chair sit-to-stand and 40-m walk was overall stable over 2 years, with the vast majority of participants exhibiting change less than the test-specific MCID. These data advance our understanding of the prognosis of KOA-related physical function impairment – namely, that performance is unlikely to decrease in a clinically important fashion over 2 years of follow-up, and that the presence of bilateral versus unilateral KOA at baseline or at any given timepoint is not a poor prognostic marker for such outcomes. Therefore, future risk prediction tools may not need to incorporate the presence of bilateral or unilateral KOA as a risk factor for worsening physical function.

Of note, worsening by the MCID in chair sit-to-stand performance was numerically more common among participants with unilateral disease, while worsening in walk speed was more common among participants with bilateral KOA, although these differences were not statistically significant and these results, which are based on study completers, should be interpreted with caution. Multivariable adjusted models including all participants did not identify bilaterality as a correlate of these differences, suggesting the observed unadjusted between-group difference may be due to confounding (such as by BMI, education, or other demographic and clinical factors) or missing data. In light of these results, participants with symptomatic, radiographic bilateral KOA that have not progressed to K-L grade 4 may be counseled that their physical performance is unlikely to worsen dramatically over the next two years.

There is no universally accepted definition for bilateral KOA, with some author groups defining bilateral disease by the presence of symptoms only (in the context of radiographic disease in at least one knee), [8,33] versus radiographic KOA only [12], versus both symptoms and radiographic KOA. [10,17,13] We chose to evaluate bilateral KOA by the presence of both structural KOA (K-L grade ≥2) and symptoms in both knees, as the absence of bilateral structural KOA in a participant with bilateral pain suggests a different mechanism, such as central sensitization, for bilateral pain.

Our study has several strengths. The ORBIT cohort is clinically well-characterized and importantly collected data concerning longitudinal change in physical performance measurements, which are described less frequently than subjective outcomes in the existing literature [20,34]. This investigation also has certain limitations. Participants were recruited from a single academic referral center, and this may limit generalizability. There was a high rate of loss to follow-up in this cohort likely due to the COVID-19 pandemic; however, we used multiple imputation methods to better understand the worst-case scenarios for missing data and confirmed that for our primary findings to change, missingness not-at-random would need to be present with a between-group difference of more than one standard deviation. While model diagnostics were overall reassuring against gross violations of key assumptions, we have used linear modeling for the primary outcome of chair sit-to-stand, which is most accurately described as a count variable. Fourth, while our definition of bilateral KOA is clinically relevant, incorporating both structural change and symptoms, it does allow participants to change from bilateral to unilateral over time if pain improves; this reduces the stability of classification within an individual participant over time. Additionally, this study observed participants over a course of two years, while progression in OA is often seen over longer time periods. Future analyses may evaluate these associations in additional data sources with larger sample sizes and/or longer follow-up. Finally, the relatively small sample size and loss-to-follow-up limit our power to detect interactions in the primary analysis, or to undertake sex-stratified analyses.

In summary, we did not observe a clinically significant association between bilateral vs unilateral KOA with performance on two physical function tasks over two years of follow-up. Future studies may investigate the associations between bilateral KOA and objective and subjective function measures over longer time periods.
