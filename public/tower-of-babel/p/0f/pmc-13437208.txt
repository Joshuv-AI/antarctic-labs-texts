Comments on “Prognostic Significance of Pretreatment 18F-FDG PET/CT Parameters in Patients With ER+/HER2- Metastatic Breast Cancer Treated With CDK4/6 Inhibitors Plus Endocrine Therapy”
Authors: Huiting Liu, Xue Liu
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the article by Suh et al. [1] evaluating the prognostic significance of pretreatment 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET/CT parameters in 374 patients with estrogen receptor-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (ER+/HER2-) metastatic breast cancer (MBC) treated with cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 (CDK4/6) inhibitors plus endocrine therapy. The authors elegantly demonstrated that maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax), metabolic tumor volume (MTV), and total lesion glycolysis (TLG) were independently associated with both progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS). To the best of our knowledge, this represents the largest cohort study in this setting, and their findings provide important evidence supporting the integration of metabolic imaging biomarkers into risk stratification frameworks.

While the study is noteworthy, we would like to raise several methodological and clinical considerations that may warrant discussion.

First, the authors excluded 137 of 511 potentially eligible patients (26.8%) because their PET/CT scans were acquired on Siemens scanners, which exhibited differing SUV profiles compared with the GE Healthcare scanners that were retained. Although intra-scanner harmonization within the GE platform is commendable, this exclusion raises two concerns. On one hand, it may introduce selection bias if the distribution of metabolic or clinical characteristics systematically differed between patients scanned on Siemens versus GE platforms. On the other hand, it limits the generalizability of the findings to institutions using non-GE scanners, which represent a substantial proportion of clinical practice worldwide. In an era of multicenter precision oncology, the application of guideline-based, cross-scanner harmonization strategies—such as those recommended by the European Association of Nuclear Medicine (EANM) or the use of phantom-based calibration—would have preserved a larger sample size and strengthened the external validity of the results [23].

Second, metabolically active tumors were segmented using a fixed SUV threshold of ≥2.5. While this approach is widely used, it is known to be sensitive to image noise, partial volume effects, and background activity, particularly in lesions with low FDG avidity or small metastatic deposits [4]. Alternative segmentation methods, such as gradient-based techniques, 41% of SUVmax thresholding, or artificial intelligence–driven delineation, may yield more reproducible MTV and TLG measurements [5]. It would be valuable for the authors to clarify whether a sensitivity analysis using alternative segmentation strategies was performed and whether the prognostic associations remained consistent.

Third, although the authors adjusted for several clinicopathological covariates, the prognostic models did not incorporate emerging molecular biomarkers that are increasingly recognized as drivers of CDK4/6 inhibitor resistance, such as PIK3CA, ESR1, and RB1 alterations [6]. Preclinical evidence indicates that activation of the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B/mammalian target of rapamycin pathway not only enhances glycolysis but also promotes cyclin D-CDK4/6 complex activity, linking metabolic phenotype directly to the therapeutic target [7]. Integrating comprehensive genomic profiling with baseline PET metrics could potentially refine risk stratification and identify biologically distinct subgroups with differential metabolic trajectories.

Fourth, the study focused exclusively on baseline pretreatment PET parameters. Emerging evidence suggests that early metabolic response assessment—typically performed 3 months after treatment initiation—may provide superior prognostic information by capturing tumor chemosensitivity and clonal adaptation dynamics [89]. The incremental value of combining baseline and early response PET parameters, as well as circulating tumor DNA kinetics, deserves exploration in future prospective studies.

Finally, we agree with the authors that the retrospective, single-center design is an important limitation. Given the heterogeneous treatment landscape of ER+/HER2- MBC and the expanding role of CDK4/6 inhibitors across multiple lines of therapy, prospective multicenter validation employing harmonized acquisition protocols, standardized segmentation methodologies, and integrated molecular profiling is essential before these PET metrics can be adopted into routine clinical decision-making.

In summary, we congratulate Suh et al. [1] on this important contribution to the metabolic imaging literature. The prognostic associations of baseline PET parameters with PFS and OS are robust and clinically meaningful. However, addressing the issues of cross-scanner harmonization, segmentation standardization, and molecular integration in future studies will be critical to translating these promising baseline PET biomarkers into clinically actionable tools for personalized treatment of ER+/HER2- MBC.
