Management of a liver hydatid cyst patient who fistulized the diaphragm and subsequently presented with thorax rupture: a case report
Authors: Hüseyin Yönder, Hasan Elkan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Hydatid cysts, caused by Echinococcus species, are zoonotic diseases frequently observed in endemic regions worldwide, including Turkey. It is most commonly localized in the liver, followed by the lungs. Anaphylaxis and peritonitis, which develop after cyst rupture, are the most significant complications observed in these patients. Early diagnosis and prompt surgical intervention are extremely important. This study presented an extremely rare case of a hepatic hydatid cyst ruptured into the thorax, which may have high morbidity and mortality. A 24-year-old male patient presented to our emergency department with cough, dyspnea, fever, and allergy symptoms. He had a history of hepatic hydatid cyst surgery 5 years ago. Physical examination revealed urticarial lesions on the skin. The body temperature was 39 °C, and the pulse rate was 120 beats/min. Laboratory tests revealed leukocytosis and elevated C-reactive protein. Contrast-enhanced thoracic and abdominal computed tomography was performed. Diagnostic tests revealed that the patient had a ruptured hepatic hydatid cyst in the right thoracic cavity; hence, he was scheduled for emergency surgery. Laparotomy was performed via a right subcostal incision, and the ruptured cyst was located. It was understood that this cyst had fistulized into the diaphragm during a chronic process and subsequently ruptured acutely into the thorax. Partial cystectomy was performed for a ruptured liver cyst. The diaphragm was repaired, and catheters were placed in the thorax and abdomen for drainage. There were no other postoperative complications. Rupture of hydatid cyst disease is frequently observed in endemic regions. The rupture of a hepatic hydatid cyst into the thorax is an extremely rare event. In patients presenting with pulmonary symptoms who have a history of hepatic hydatid cyst surgery, the abdomen should always be thoroughly evaluated, and emergency surgery should be performed if required.

Introduction

Cystic echinococcosis, also known as hydatid disease, is a zoonotic disease primarily caused by the parasite Echinococcus granulosus [1]. It is most commonly localized in the liver, followed by the lungs. However, it may also rarely localize the spleen, kidneys, brain, pancreas, heart, and bones [2, 3]. Although clinical symptoms vary depending on the organ involved, the most critical and potentially fatal complication is the cyst rupture, leading to the dissemination of its highly allergenic contents throughout the body [4]. Sometimes, urticaria and skin symptoms associated with it may be the only manifestation of a ruptured hydatid cyst [5].

Rupture of a hepatic hydatid cyst into the abdominal cavity is a rare complication [6]. Rupture of the hepatic hydatid cyst into the thoracic cavity is an even rarer condition [7]. Hence, limited information is available regarding this condition, and the studies conducted on this topic are mostly case reports. Generally, such patients also have biliobronchial fistula [7, 8]. However, in this study, a biliobronchial fistula was not detected. This rare complication, characterized by the direct passage of cyst contents from liver rupture into the thoracic cavity, can mimic pulmonary hydatid cyst rupture, leading to delayed diagnosis and treatment. Patients usually present with respiratory symptoms, including dyspnea, fever, and cough [9]. In this case report, we aimed to present our approach for the diagnosis and treatment of a patient with ruptured hepatic hydatid cyst into the thorax who presented with an atypical clinical picture without acute abdominal symptoms.

Case report

A 24-year-old male sought emergency care at another hospital with complaints of sudden-onset cough, shortness of breath, chest pain, itching, and redness of the skin 3 days before presenting to our clinic. The patient was treated for lower respiratory tract infection. The complaints partially regressed at first, but later, the patient was presented to our hospital’s emergency department with fever and weakness in addition to previously observed symptoms. During the physical examination, he was conscious, alert, oriented, and cooperative. Areas of erythema resembling urticaria were present on the neck and trunk. No tenderness, guarding, or rebound was detected during abdominal examination. Auscultation revealed decreased right lung sounds. Rales were present in the lower-middle lobe. Body temperature was 39 °C, pulse rate was 120 beats/min, blood pressure was 100/70 mmHg, and SpO2 was 90%. The patient had a right subcostal incision scar caused by a liver hydatid cyst and gallbladder surgery performed before 5 years. The patient was not living in a rural area and had no contact with animals. Following the initial surgery, he had a history of treatment with an anthelmintic medication for 6 months, but did not receive further treatment for hydatid cyst.

Laboratory tests revealed leukocytosis with neutrophil dominance (total leukocyte count: 11.2 × 103/uL, normal: 3.84–9.84 × 103/uL). C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were elevated (CRP: 5.5 mg/dL, normal: <0.5 mg/dL). However, the patient had no eosinophilia (eosinophils: 0.23 × 103/uL, normal: 0.03–0.44 × 103/uL).

Chest X-ray revealed pleural effusion in the right lung and complete disappearance of the costophrenic angle (Fig. 1). Thoracic and abdominal computed tomography (CT) were performed because the patient had a history of upper abdominal surgery. On thoracic CT, a pleural effusion extending from the apex to the base of the right lung, reaching a thickness of 5.5 cm at its most prominent location, was observed. Passive atelectatic appearance was also noted in the lung parenchyma adjacent to the effusion. The lower lobe of the right lung appeared almost completely collapsed at the base, showing dense consolidation and infiltrative areas. There were focal areas of consolidation and infiltration in the posterior aspect of the right middle lobe of the lung. There was a defect of approximately 1.5 cm in the posterior aspect of the right hemidiaphragm. No pathology was detected in the left lung (Figs. 1 and 2).

Abdominal CT shows a thick-walled hypodense lesion in the subdiaphragmatic–subcapsular area in segment 7 of the liver. At segment 8 of the liver, a second lesion measuring 47 × 20 mm was observed, containing solid areas and hyperdense calcified foci, consistent with a stage IV or V hydatid cyst according to the Gharbi classification. Both cysts were unocular. An effusion reaching a depth of approximately 2 cm in the pelvic region (Fig. 2).

The hepatic hydatid cyst in the patient had fistulized into the diaphragm due to chronic adhesions and may have ruptured into the thorax following a sudden increase in intra-abdominal pressure. The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit for emergency surgery. All examinations were completed within few minutes in the emergency department, and the patient was prepared for surgery. The patient, whose fasting period was also appropriate, was taken to surgery.

The patient was placed on the operating table in supine position under general anesthesia. The abdomen was entered through a right subcostal incision over the previous scar. There was no abdominal contamination. The cyst in the dome of the liver (segment 7), which had lost tension, adhered to the diaphragm. Using hypertonic saline-soaked sponges, adhesions were carefully dissected while protecting the liver and abdomen against spillage of the cyst contents, revealing perforation of the cyst through the defect in the diaphragm into the thoracic cavity (Fig. 3). The cyst contents and germinative membrane were removed. A hypertonic saline solution containing 3% NaCl was introduced into the thoracic cavity through the diaphragmatic opening, left for 10 min, and then aspirated. A drainage catheter was placed percutaneously into the pleural cavity. The diaphragm was repaired transabdominally using a 1/0 vicryl suture. Partial cystectomy was performed for the ruptured hepatic cyst. The cyst pouch was filled with hypertonic saline solution, left for 10 min, and then aspirated. A drain was placed in the abdomen, extending toward the cyst pouch. No intervention was made for the calcified cyst. After hemostasis, the fascia was closed with a continuous 1/0 polydioxanone suture, and the skin was closed with interrupted 2/0 prolene sutures. No blood product transfusion was administered during surgery. The patient was admitted to the postoperative intensive care unit.

The patient was monitored in the intensive care unit for 2 days postoperatively, and there were no additional issues during follow-up. On the second postoperative day, the patient’s chest X-ray returned to normal (Fig. 4). The patient was admitted to the ward on the third day, and laboratory values were completely normalized on the fourth day (total leukocyte count: 8.2 × 103/uL, normal: 3.84–9.84 × 103/Ul, CRP: 0.3 mg/dL, normal: <0.5 mg/dL). There were no postoperative complications. The patient was monitored in the ward for 2 days and discharged on the fifth postoperative day after drainage catheters were removed. The patient was started on anthelmintic treatment with albendazole (10 mg/kg/day) from the first postoperative day. Treatment was continued for at least 6 months. The patient visited the clinic for monthly checkups. The patient was given medication for 3 weeks, followed by a 1-week break from treatment. Liver enzymes levels were assessed before starting each medication course. Medication was continued only if the enzyme levels were normal. If enzyme levels were observed to be elevated, the medication was temporarily discontinued or the dose was reduced (5 mg/kg/day).

Discussion

Hydatid cysts are transmitted to humans through direct contact with intermediate host animals or consumption of contaminated food that has not been properly washed after being contaminated with host feces. Eggs ingested through the enteric system enter the portal and lymphatic circulation, leading to the formation of the disease most commonly in the liver of adults and in the lungs of children [5]. Cysts can grow up to 1–50 mm per year, but they can also remain stable in size over time. Most patients are asymptomatic and diagnosed incidentally during imaging performed for another reason. The patient presented in this study did not have a history of living in a rural area or prolonged contact with animals. Detailed information about the patient’s dietary habits and living hygiene conditions could not be obtained.

Ruptured hepatic hydatid cysts can present with a wide range of clinical symptoms, including abdominal pain, weakness, jaundice, urticaria, palpable mass, compression of the bile ducts, rupture of the bile ducts, and anaphylaxis [10, 11]. In the literature, rupture of hepatic hydatid cysts into the peritoneal cavity has been reported at rates 1–16%. Rupture can occur spontaneously or secondary to trauma. Rupture of the cyst does not always present with conspicuous symptoms. Although it can sometimes present with mild symptoms, in about 2% of cases it can result in anaphylaxis [11]. Factors triggering cyst rupture include cyst size > 10 cm, increased cyst pressure, young age, and exophytic location of the cyst [12]. Our case fulfilled three of these four criteria. The diameter of the ruptured cyst was measured intraoperatively at 8 cm. A 24-year-old patient, the cyst located at the dome of the liver with an exophytic growth pattern, a history of previous hepatic hydatid cyst surgery, adherence of the cyst to the diaphragm limiting its ability to expand, and consequently increasing intra-cystic pressure were factors that increased the risk of rupture into the thorax [13]. The fact that our patient previously underwent open surgery for hydatid cyst raises the possibility of cyst via seeding.

After the initial hydatid cyst surgery, the patient received medical treatment for 6 months and subsequently did not attend follow-up appointments at the outpatient clinic. The lack of continued treatment also facilitated the subsequent formation of fistulas. Adhesions secondary to previous surgery led to the fistulization of the cyst into the diaphragm. What distinguishes our case from ruptured cases in the literature is that the hepatic hydatid cyst did not open into the peritoneal cavity but instead opened into the thoracic cavity through the diaphragm. This completely changed the clinical picture. Peritonitis caused by infected cyst was not observed because the cyst opened into the lungs. The primary symptoms were cough and dyspnea due to pleural effusion and irritation. Again, leukocytosis, increased CRP levels, and a fever of 39 °C indicated the progression of the infectious condition. Although our patient exhibited mild allergic symptoms, an anaphylactic reaction did not develop. Nevertheless, it should be kept in mind that anaphylaxis may occur in patients with hydatid cyst presenting with allergic symptoms, and these patients may require emergency surgery.

For treating hydatid cysts, medical therapy, percutaneous treatment, and surgical procedures can be used depending on the characteristics of the cyst. Albendazole is commonly used in medical treatment, but the puncture-aspiration-injection-reaspiration procedure is performed in percutaneous treatment. Surgery is classically performed using laparoscopic and open surgical techniques [14]. The decision to perform surgery should be made more cautiously compared to other methods. Albendazole (10 mg/kg/day) is used as an antihelminthic treatment. It is recommended to be used for one month prior to elective surgery. Continued treatment for six months after surgery reduces recurrence. Surgery is the primary treatment option for ruptured and complicated hydatid cysts [15, 16]. It is not possible to start antihelminthic therapy in these patients before surgery. However, after surgery, treatment should be administered and monitored regularly. Ruptured hydatid cyst surgery is more complicated than elective hydatid cyst surgery. The recurrence rate is reported in the literature to be 6.7–28.6%. Therefore, these patients should be followed up meticulously, and in cases of rupture, the abdominal cavity should be thoroughly cleaned with scolicidal agents [16]. The case presented in this report was a delayed case. The patient had neglected to attend follow-up. When the patient was evaluated preoperatively, it was decided that laparoscopy was not suitable, and to avoid any delay, direct open surgery was deemed appropriate. Rapid and accurate diagnosis significantly reduced the patient’s hospitalization period. This approach not only reduced morbidity but also prevented all the adverse outcomes associated with unnecessary prolonged hospital stays.

In conclusion, it should be kept in mind that hydatid cysts can rupture from the liver to the lung via neighboring tissues. The risk is higher in individuals with a history of open hepatobiliary system surgery. Allergic symptoms, associated with anaphylaxis risk, must always be considered in patients with a history of hydatid cysts. In regions endemic to hydatid cyst disease, patients with respiratory complaints should also undergo abdominal screening for hydatid cysts.
