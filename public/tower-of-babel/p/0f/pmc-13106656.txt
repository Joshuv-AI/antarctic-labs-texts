Physicochemical properties of soy protein isolate–beet polysaccharide mixtures and phase inversion of their emulsion composites
Authors: Shirong Dong, Shanshan Guo, Wei Xu, Chun Bian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the impacts of heat treatment and mass ratio on the physicochemical and emulsion attributes of soy protein isolate (SPI) and beet polysaccharide (BSP) mixtures. In addition, the phase inversion of their emulsions in soy oil was examined. The mixtures were prepared at SPI: BSP mass ratio of 1:0, 1:1, 2:1, 5:1, and 0:1 with heated or unheated treatment. Meanwhile, their emulsions were synthesised at different oil contents of 20, 50, 100, 200, 400, 600, 800 and 1000 mL, and the microscopic morphology, appearance, conductivity, and rheology of these emulsions were assessed. Compared with the SPI − BSP mixtures produced with unheated treatment, these produced with heated treatment showed a larger particle size distribution, lower zeta-potential value, and a higher content of β-sheet structures. The synergistic effects of heat treatment and BSP addition resulted in these variations. When the amount of oil added reached 600 mL, the emulsions formed from the heated mixtures showed higher storage modulus and loss modulus. Phase inversion (i.e., from oil-in-water (O/W) to water-in-oil (W/O)) was observed in partial emulsions formed from mixtures as the oil content increased. This behaviour is governed by both the heat treatment and the SPI: BSP ratio. These results provide important theoretical insights for the design and development of novel emulsion-based systems via using SPI − BSP mixtures.Subject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

SPI is considered a significant protein resource for sustainable development in the future because of its high nutritional value and functional properties1. SPI contains more than 90% protein, and it can be obtained from defatted soybean meal2. 11 S glycinin and 7 S β-conglycinin are the main components of SPI. The primary amino acids contained in SPI include basic amino acids such as arginine (7.7%), lysine (6.8%), cysteine (2.5%) and thiol-containing amino acids. SPI shows advantageous functional properties, especially in terms of gelation and emulsification, which can be attributed to its special amino acid profile and structural composition3. However, food processing is an extremely complicated process involving in multiple factors. For example, one or more factors among thermal treatment, ionic strength, pH, and interactions with other food components affect the functional properties (damage or improvement)4. Emulsification is an especially crucial property of protein among its numerous functional properties5. Therefore, exploring ways to further enhance emulsification and other related functions of proteins through processing methods is of great research significance. Oil-in-water (O/W) emulsions are essential in food science and technology, serving as the fundamental structure in a wide variety of common foods such as salad dressings, mayonnaise, ice cream, cream liqueurs, and processed meat products like bologna. Electrical conductivity, being highly sensitive to ion concentration, serves as an effective indicator for detecting the phase inversion from O/W to water-in-oil (W/O) emulsions. Measured in microsiemens per centimeter (mS/cm), conductivity depends directly on the concentration of dissolved ions, including charged proteins that act as macromolecular electrolytes in protein-stabilized emulsions. As a result, monitoring electrical conductivity provides valuable insight into the underlying mechanisms of the phase inversion process6.

In food processing, multiple components often coexist in a physically mixed state rather than undergoing true thermal cross-linking. These physically mixed states still interact with and influence each other’s functional performance. Consequently, it is particularly necessary to systematically study the physicochemical properties and functional characteristics of mixtures of different components under various conditions.

Almost all foods contain two essential nutrients: protein and polysaccharides. During food processing, these components inevitably come into contact or interact with each other, thereby influencing the quality of the final product. Amino groups in proteins can react with carbonyl groups in polysaccharides, resulting in improvements in the protein functions, including solubility, gelation, and rheological attributes. Food texture, steadiness, and general product quality are positively influenced by these enhancements7. With regard to SPI and polysaccharide mixtures, interplay may encompass electrostatic forces, steric hindrance8, and various physical and chemical bonds, with the Maillard reaction representing one of the core chemical routes9. BSP provide some physiological advantages, such as the control of glucose and lipid metabolism, decline in the cholesterol values, and relief from constipation, assisting in the prevention of obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and some cancers10. Mixtures of SPI and BSP can be prepared by heated and unheated treatment. To date, however, the specific influence of heated treatment, compared with unheated treatment, on the physicochemical and functional properties of SPI-polysaccharide mixtures remains unclear. Hence, a systematic investigation into the interaction between SPI and BSP under heated and unheated conditions is justified11–14.

Accordingly, mixtures were prepared at different mass ratios (1:0, 1:1, 2:1, 5:1, and 0:1) of SPI and BSP under either heated or unheated treatment. The physicochemical properties (i.e. particle size distribution, zeta potential, pH, and conductivity) of these mixtures were then compared. Further structural analyses were conducted by using scanning electron microscope (SEM), fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), fluorescence spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction (XRD). Subsequently, emulsions were prepared by mixing 100 mL these SPI-BSP composites with varying oil volumes (20, 50, 100, 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 mL). The microscopic morphology, appearance, conductivity and rheological properties of the prepared emulsions were also systematically evaluated. Overall, a tailored design of SPI-BSP-based emulsifiers and gelling agents can be produced based on the theoretical foundation of this study.

Results

Ten mixtures of SPI and BSP at different ratios (1:0, 1:1, 2:1, 5:1, 0:1) were prepared using two different methods: with heated (75 °C for 20 min) and unheated treatment. The particle size distribution was strongly influenced by the preparation method as shown in Fig. 1A–E. A bimodal particle size distribution was observed for all the mixtures except SPI-BSPH2:1. For size distribution, the volume percentages of small particles (d < 100 nm) in SPI-BSPH1:0, SPI-BSPH1:1, SPI-BSPH2:1, SPI-BSPH5:1, and SPI-BSPH0:1 were 99.98%, 99.54%, 85.72%, 99.93%, and 76.23%, respectively. This aggregation behavior was also evident in the SEM images (Fig. 2), despite the expected differences in absolute size. Smaller particle sizes generally correlate with higher stability in solutions. Zeta-potential

A zeta potential value reflects the change in the electrostatic charge on the surface of the SPI − BSP mixtures16. As shown in Fig. 1F, the zeta potential value of heated SPI and unheated SPI was − 12.10 mV and − 23.18 mV, respectively, indicating that heated treatment resulted in the formation of more positively charged groups on its surface. Moreover, the zeta potential value of heated BSP and unheated BSP was − 15.57 mV and − 16.70 mV, respectively, indicating that heated treatment had a limited influence on changes in the charged groups of BSP. For the same BSP concentration, compared with the unheated mixtures, the heated SPI − BSP mixture exhibited a lower absolute zeta potential value, suggesting the formation of various mixtures. The zeta potential value partly determines the stability of the mixtures17. At an SPI-to-BSP ratio of 5:1, the potential measured − 24.55 mV under heated conditions and − 26.67 mV under unheated conditions, indicating relatively high stability of the composite nanoparticles prepared under both conditions at this specific ratio. A higher negative charge also results in stronger local and overall repulsion effect7, and the zeta potential results showed that the stability of the SPI − BSP mixture was affected by heated treatment17.

Conductivity and pH

The conductivity and pH of the prepared SPI − BSP mixtures varied with the mixture preparation method and the BSP concentration, as shown in Fig. 1G and H. Under both heated and unheated conditions, the addition of different ratio of BSP altered the conductivity and pH. When the SPI-to-BSP ratio was 1:0, 1:1, 2:1, 5:1, and 0:1, the conductivity of the mixtures first increased and then decreased under both heated and unheated conditions. However, at the same ratio, the conductivity of the mixtures prepared under heated conditions was consistently higher than that prepared under unheated conditions. Regarding pH, both SPI-BSPUH1:0 and SPI-BSPH1:0 both exhibited the highest pH value, whereas both SPI-BSPUH0:1 and SPI-BSPH0:1 demonstrated the lowest values under the heated and unheated conditions. Among the mixtures, the highest pH was observed for SPI-BSPUH5:1 and SPI-BSPH5:1. These results suggested that both the conductivity and pH jointly influenced the mixture system. These factors might lead to changes in the ultimate form and properties of these mixtures.

SEM analysis

Using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) at a high resolution is an effective way to reveal the detailed surface morphology and microstructure of the SPI-BSP mixtures by using the images with high resolution.

Figure 2 shows the SEM images of SPI-BSPH1:0, SPI-BSPH1:1, SPI-BSPH2:1, SPI-BSPH5:1, SPI-BSPH0:1, SPI-BSPUH1:0, SPI-BSPUH1:1, SPI-BSPUH2:1, SPI-BSPUH5:1, and SPI-BSPUH0:1. For the mixtures, the distinct microstructural differences were observed between the heated and unheated SPI–BSP mixtures across various mixing ratios. For individual components, while both heated and unheated BSP exhibited fragment-like structures with a smooth surface7, the latter exhibited a smoother surface. Similarly, unheated SPI demonstrated large fragments, while its heated induced the formation of a necklace-like, ‘bead-on-a-string’ complex18. In 1:1 SPI-BSP blends, the unheated sample exhibited a large sheet-like polymers structure, which transformed into spherical granules upon heated treatment. At a 2:1 ratio, the unheated sample displayed a primarily block-like structure, with some spherical particles, transitioning to spherical or elliptical polymers after heated treatment. Finally, at a 5:1 ratio, the unheated complexes exhibited necklace or strand-like structures. Heated promoted the formation of large spherical polymers. However, their surface smoothness was notably lower than that of the complexes formed at the 2:1 and 1:1 ratios.

FTIR analysis

FTIR was employed to investigate conformational changes in the protein secondary structures19. As shown in Fig. 3A, both the heated and unheated SPI samples exhibited characteristic absorption peaks at 3280 cm−1, 2931 cm−1, 1635 cm−1, 1515 cm−1, 1446 cm−1, 1394 cm−1, 1233 cm−1, 1077 cm−1, and 1043 cm−1. These peaks corresponded to –CH2 stretching vibrations, amide I band (–C = O stretching), amide II band (–N–H and –N–H bending), amide III band (–N–H bending), –C–N stretching, and aromatic C–H out-of-plane vibrations, respectively. This suggested that more hydrogen bonds were formed between SPI and BSP, thereby enhancing their intermolecular interactions20. Polysaccharides such as BSP contain abundant hydroxyl and other hydrophilic functional groups. In both heated and unheated BSP samples, peaks at 3294 cm−1, 2926 cm−1, 1631 cm−1, 1360 cm−1, and 1015 cm−1 were attributed to O–H stretching (overlapping with N–H stretching), amide I band vibrations, and asymmetric bending of quaternary methyl groups21. In FTIR analysis, the regions at 1664–1681 cm−1 and 1637–1648 cm−1 are associated with protein secondary structures such as α-helices, β-sheets, β-turns, and random coils3. Absorption at 1660 cm−1, indicative of an α-helical conformation, was weakest at an SPI-to-BSP ratio of 0:1. New absorption peaks emerged for all mixtures containing varying BSP concentrations, though BSP addition did not disrupt the secondary structure of SPI. Both pure BSP and various SPI–BSP complexes showed broad absorption bands in the 3200–3500 cm−1 range, attributed to intra- and intermolecular hydrogen bonding. After incorporating different proportions of BSP, the peak of SPI at 2935 cm−1 remained intact, whereas the signals at 1546 cm−1 and 1404 cm−1 weakened or disappeared, likely because of SPI-BSP electrostatic interactions and shielding of hydrophobic frameworks, resulting in improved composite hydrophilicity. Nevertheless, as the BSP ratio decreased, the intensity of the wide band in the 3200–3500 cm−1 region changed, possibly indicating an increased water content or improved hydrophobic interplay. According to the FTIR results, the addition of varying BSP amounts influenced SPI conformation, though the absence of the role of BSP in the tertiary structure of SPI was not implied. Large variations in the FTIR spectra were induced by heated treatment.

The secondary structure composition of unheated SPI comprisedα-helix (21.78%), β-sheet (41.60%), β-turn (24.49%), and random coil (12.13%) (Fig. 3B). Upon heated treatment, these related values did not vary, demonstrating values of 21.78%, 41.60%, 24.49%, and 12.13%, respectively. The secondary structure of SPI was also influenced by the inclusion of BSP at varying ratio. After heated treatment, the protein structure was inversed towards a more ordered state7. When the SPI-to-BSP ratio was 1:1, the heated samples contained 26.11% α-helix, 33.21% β-sheet, 27.97% β-turn, and 12.71% random coil, while the unheated samples contained 25.00% α-helix, 32.52% β-sheet, 29.77% β-turn, and 12.71% random coil. At a ratio of 2:1 (SPI: BSP), the heated sample showed 22.90% α-helix, 38.13% β-sheet, 27.05% β-turn, and 11.92% random coil contents. The corresponding unheated samples contained 23.90% α-helix, 38.66% β-sheet, 25.32% β-turn, and 12.12% random coil. For a 5:1 SPI-to-BSP ratio, the heated samples contained 23.90% α-helix, 36.44% β-sheet, 27.67% β-turn, and 11.99% random coil, while the unheated samples contained 23.86% α-helix, 37.41% β-sheet, 26.42% β-turn, and 12.30% random coil. According to these outcomes, the secondary framework of SPI was jointly modulated by heated treatment and BSP addition, indicating a complicated interplay during structural re-layout.

Changes in fluorescence intensity and XRD

Owing to the sensitivity of its fluorescent groups, such as tryptophan (Trp) and tyrosine (Tyr) residues, fluorescence spectroscopy was employed to investigate changes in the tertiary structure of SPI to environmental variations21. The effect of BSP addition on the fluorescence emission spectrum of SPI is shown in Fig. 4A. As depicted in Fig. 4A, the maximum fluorescence emission peak (λmax) of both heated and unheated SPI appeared at approximately 348 nm. In contrast, λmax for the heated and unheated BSP samples was observed at two regions—317 and 345 nm, respectively. A similar bimodal emission pattern was observed for the SPI–BSP complexes under unheated condition, with peaks observing at 317 nm and 345 nm, suggesting a more pronounced tertiary structure of BSP under this condition3. In both conditions, as the ratio of BSP increased, the λmax of SPI slightly redshift from 349 nm to 351 nm. The partial unfolding of SPI may indicate that BSP causes the discovered fluorescence improvement, exposing non-polar amino acid residues and leading to a more open tertiary framework.

The XRD patterns of the SPI–BSP mixtures are shown in Fig. 4B. All samples showed wide and diffuse peaks, characteristic of an amorphous framework18. A wide diffraction envelope with discernible peak positions approximately 2θ = 20.4° and 17.89° was observed for both raw and heated BSPs, respectively. Similarly, a wide peak at 2θ value of approximately 20.32° and 19.36° was observed for unheated and heated SPI, respectively, which is typical for native and heated globular proteins and many polysaccharides in solid, dry form. At SPI: BSP ratio of 5:1, 2:1, and 1:1, the peak positions of the mixtures—irrespective of produced under heated or unheated circumstances—changed, with the 2θ value increasing by 1.09°, 0.60°, and 0.69°, respectively. There slight shifts in the broad diffraction envelope may suggest changes in the short-range molecular packing or intermolecular distances within the amorphous composites.

Emulsions inversion analysis

As shown in Fig. 5A, the appearances of the emulsions were characterized. As the oil content increased from 20 mL to 1000 mL, the emulsion color gradually shifted from milky white to yellowish (Fig. 5A). Various SPI-BSP mixtures emulsion showed different state. When the content of the oil lower 200 mL, the emulsion prepared from SPI-BSPUH1:0 or SPI-BSPH1:0 displayed a uniform state, but presented a pronounced color change at 400 mL. Similarly, SPI-BSPUH1:1, SPI-BSPUH2:1 and SPI-BSPUH5:1 all showed homogeneous sate, as the oil content increased to 400–600 mL. At higher oil contents (800–1000 mL), these emulsions turned distinctly yellow. Notably, the SPI-BSPH1:1 mixture formed emulsions did not exhibit significant stratification at 20 mL, and the emulsion formed from SPI-BSPH1:1 were stable across all tested oil levels, with a marked color shift occurring at 400 mL. At 200 mL and 400 mL, this formulation developed an oil-gel structure, effectively preventing phase separation. Similarly, emulsions prepared with SPI-BSPH2:1 and SPI-BSPH5:1 remained stable at oil contents of 200 mL, 400 mL, and 600 mL, and also formed a gel-like state. The SPI-BSPH2:1 remained formulation produced stable emulsions at all oil levels tested. These visual changes showed that different SPI-BSP mixtures undergo alterations in emulsion type upon blending with varying amounts of oil, implying the existence of a potential phase inversion critical point dependent on the composition and oil content.

The microscopic morphology of these emulsions was observed using optical microscopy (Fig. 5B). At low oil contents, all samples formed stable and uniform emulsions with well-defined droplets. Compared to emulsions stabilized by SPI-BSPUH1:0, the SPI-BSP mixtures system produced smaller droplets with a more compact morphology. This indicates that the SPI-BSP mixtures can encapsulate a larger amount of oil and exhibits superior emulsifying capacity relative to simple protein solutions. However, when the oil content exceeded a critical threshold (e.g., > 400 mL), phase inversion occurred, transforming the emulsions from oil-in-water (O/W) to water-in-oil (W/O) type. This inversion was confirmed by a drop test: O/W emulsions readily dispersed in water but not in soybean oil, whereas the opposite behavior was observed for W/O emulsions (see Methods for details). These results underscore the key role of protein concentration in the formation and stabilization of emulsions for product applications7.

On the other hand, conductivity (measured in microsiemens per centimetre, mS/cm) reflects the concentration of dissolved ions in a system, such as charged protein molecules acting as macromolecular electrolytes in a protein-stabilized emulsion. Thus, conductivity measurements can offer valuable insight into the underlying mechanism underlying phase inversion6,23. Conductivity approaches zero when proteins are fully adsorbed at the interface, indicating phase inversion from O/W to W/O. In this study, to a fixed 100 mL solution of SPI–BSP mixtures was used, followed by the addition of 20, 50, 100, 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 mL of oil to prepare emulsions. The conductivity of the resulting emulsions was then measured separately. As the amount of oil added increased, the conductivity of all samples showed a decreasing trend Fig. 5C. When the amount of oil added increased from 10 to 1000 mL, the conductivity of SPI-BSPUH1:0 decreased from 447.67 mS/cm to 1.56 mS/cm, and that of SPI-BSPH1:0 decreased from 599.87 mS/cm to 0.53 mS/cm. When 400 mL oil was added, the conductivity decreased to 2.82 mS/cm and 0.59 mS/cm, indicating that a phase inversion occurred under the homogenization of the heated or unheated SPI sample24. However, the conductivity of SPI-BSPUH1:1 and SPI-BSPUH2:1 decreased to a minimum value of 51.7mS/cm and 99.85 mS/cm, respectively, indicating that no significant phase inversion occurred between the two. Meanwhile, the conductivity of sample SPI-BSPUH5:1 decreased from 364.45 mS/cm to 0.28 mS/cm, indicating a significant phase inversion when 1000 mL of oil was added. The conductivity of SPI-BSPH2:1 decreased from 633.87 mS/cm to 104.27 mS/cm, indicating that no phase inversion occurred. SPI-BSPH1:1 underwent phase inversion when added with 800 mL of oil, and SPI-BSPH5:1 underwent a phase inversion when added with 400 mL of oil. The formation of different types of emulsions depended on the influence of heat and the comprehensive properties of polysaccharides. This further proved that there were differences in the structure of these complexes, which can lead to variations in some binding abilities.

Rheological analysis

The SPI–BSP mixtures prepared under both heated and unheated conditions at various ratios formed stable emulsions when 600 mL of the oil phase was added. Rheological analysis confirmed that these emulsions exhibited the gel‑like behaviour and belonged to the category of non‑Newtonian fluids. As shown in Fig. 6A and B, the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) of the different SPI–BSP mixtures varied with the angular frequency. Under both preparation methods, all samples exhibited similar trends: both G′ and G″ increased with increasing angular frequency, indicating their clear frequency‑dependent viscoelastic behaviour25. This increase in the moduli with frequency might be attributed to the progressive structural deformation of SPI or SPI-BSP aggregates under shear, along with the weakening of intramolecular or intermolecular interactions, which collectively reduced the flow resistance26. Moreover, heat treatment helped in achieving higher G′ and G″ values for the SPI-BSP mixtures, compared with unheated treatment. Shahbazi et al.25 reported that increasing the mass fraction of sugar beet pectin from 1% to 6% led to a corresponding increase in the G′ and G″ values of SPI‑based emulsion gels, with G′ reaching approximately 100 Pa at a 1% pectin content.

Discussion

Based on the above results, no phase inversion occurred in the SPI-BSPUH2:1, SPI-BSPH2:1, and SPI-BSPUH1:1 samples as the oil content increased from 20 mL to 1000 mL. In contrast, other SPI–BSP mixtures underwent a phase inversion from O/W to W/O emulsions. This discrepancy can be more explicitly linked to the distinct molecular and microstructural features of these resistant composites. For instance, the SPI-BSPH2:1 composite exhibited a relatively high β-sheet content (38.13%, Fig. 3B) and a well-defined spherical/elliptical polymers morphology (Fig. 2), suggesting a stable, ordered interfacial structure that may enhance emulsion droplet rigidity and resistance to inversion. Similarly, the unheated SPI-BSPUH2:1 and SPI-BSPUH1:1 mixtures, which showed block-like or large sheet-like structures in SEM analysis, may possess interfacial layers with different mechanical strengths or packing densities that stabilize the O/W interface against takeover by the oil phase at high fractions. This structural robustness likely impedes the catastrophic reorganization required for phase inversion, aligning with their sustained higher conductivity values even at elevated oil volumes (Fig. 5). Therefore, the resistance to phase inversion is not arbitrary, but it appears to be governed by the specific secondary structure profile and the resultant microstructural architecture formed under particular SPI: BSP ratios and thermal histories.” Based on this, a model illustrating changes in the protein molecular structure within W/O or O/W emulsions was proposed, as detailed in Fig. 7.

Conclusion

Different mixtures of SPI and BSP were prepared at various mass ratios (0:1, 1:1, 2:1, 5:1, and 0:1, respectively) with heated or unheated treatment. Different treatment methods and ratios jointly influence the physicochemical properties of the prepared mixtures. In addition, these mixtures were mixed with different oil contents (20, 50, 100, 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 mL) to produce various emulsions, and their appearance, microscopic morphology, conductivity, and rheology were assessed. Compared with mixtures produced with unheated, the SPI–BSP mixtures produced under heated condition showed a larger particle size, lower zeta-potential value, and a higher content of β-sheet structures. The synergistic effects of heat treatment and BSP addition resulted in these variations. When the amount of oil added reached 600 mL, the resulting emulsions formed from the heated mixtures showed higher G’ and G”. Phase inversion (i.e. from oil-in-water (O/W) to water-in-oil (W/O)) occurred in partial emulsions formed from mixtures as the oil content increased. This behaviour was governed by a combination of the heat treatment and the SPI: BSP ratio. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for the rational design of protein-polysaccharide–based emulsifiers and gelling agents, with potential applications in food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic products.

Methods

Materials

SPI with a moisture content of < 7.0% and protein content of > 90% was purchased from the Soybean Research Institute of Northeast Agricultural University, China. Sugar beet pectin, referred to as beet polysaccharide (BSP), with a relative molecular weight of 100,000, and the presence of abundant neutral sugar side chains (primarily arabinan and galactan), was purchased from Shanghai Source Leaf Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other reagents were of analytical grade.

Preparation of SPI-BSP mixture

SPI was dispersed in deionized water to prepare a 3% (w/w) protein suspension, which was stirred overnight at 100 rpm and 4 °C to ensure complete hydration. The pH of the suspension was then adjusted to 7.0. A 3% (w/v) BSP solution was similarly prepared by dissolving BSP powder in deionized water. To obtain SPI-to-BSP mass ratios of 1:0, 5:1, 2:1, and 1:1, four aliquots of 100 mL of the 3% SPI suspension were each mixed with 0, 20, 50, or 100 mL of the 3% BSP solution, respectively, under continuous magnetic stirring at 800 rpm for 1 h. A BSP-only sample (SPI: BSP = 0:1) was prepared by taking 100 mL of the 3% BSP solution without SPI. Each resulting mixture was divided into two portions: one portion was left unheated and designated as SPI-BSPH followed by the corresponding mass ratio (e.g., SPI-BSPH1:0, SPI-BSPH1:1, etc.), while the other portion was heated at 75 °C for 20 min and labeled as SPI-BSPUH with the same ratio designations. Particle size and zeta potential measurements

The particle size distribution and zeta potential values were determined by a dynamic light scattering (DLS) analyser (Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK). All the samples were diluted to 6 mg/mL using distilled water and then equilibrated at 25 °C.

Determination of pH

The pH of the prepared mixtures was measured using a calibrated benchtop pH meter (FE28, Mettler Toledo) equipped with a standard glass combination electrode.

Determination of conductivity

A conductivity meter (DDSJ-318T Conductivity Meter Shanghai Yidian Science Instrument Co., Ltd) was used to measure the conductivity of the prepared samples. All measurements were conducted at a constant temperature (25 °C) to eliminate thermal effects on conductivity.

SEM determination

The morphologies of these freeze-dried mixtures were evaluated using SEM (SU8010, Hitachi, Japan). The samples were fixed onto conductive carbon tape and prepared for imaging by sputter-coating with gold using a Dest-1 TSC system (Denton Vacuum LLC, Moorestown, NJ, USA). SEM observations were performed at a voltage of 5 kV.

FTIR spectroscopy measurement

The freeze-dried SPI-BSPH1:0, SPI-BSPH1:1, SPI-BSPH2:1, SPI-BSPH5:1, SPI-BSPH0:1, SPI-BSPUH1:0, SPI-BSPUH1:1, SPI-BSPUH2:1, SPI-BSPUH5:1, and SPI-BSPUH0:1, respectively, were individually mixed with KBr and ground into fine powders. Subsequently, these powders were compressed into transparent sheets using a hydraulic tablet press. Their fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were acquired using an FTIR spectrophotometer (Nicolet 6700, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Each spectrum was obtained by averaging 32 scans at a spectral resolution of 4 cm−1 in the wavenumber range of 4000 to 400 cm-113. Then, the samples were analyzed for secondary structure content using PeakFit 4.12 (Systat Software Inc., San Jose, CA, USA)27.

Fluorescence spectroscopy measurement

An RF 6000 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan) was used for fluorescence measurements, with an excitation wavelength of 280 nm, an emission wavelength range of 300–500 nm, an excitation and emission slits of 5 nm, a data interval of 0.5 nm, and a scanning speed of 200 nm/min28.

XRD determination

X-ray diffraction analysis was performed on freeze-dried SPI-BSPH1:0, SPI-BSPH1:1, SPI-BSPH2:1, SPI-BSPH5:1, SPI-BSPH0:1, SPI-BSPUH1:0, SPI-BSPUH1:1, SPI-BSPUH2:1, SPI-BSPUH5:1, and SPI-BSPUH0:1, respectively, using a Rigaku UltimaIV diffractometer (40 kV/40 mA) with Cu-Kα radiation. The scans covered a range of 5°–90° 2θ range at 5°/s scan rate20.

Preparation of emulsions

The SPI-BSPH1:0, SPI-BSPH1:1, SPI-BSPH2:1, SPI-BSPH5:1, SPI-BSPUH1:0, SPI-BSPUH1:1, SPI-BSPUH2:1, and SPI-BSPUH5:1 were, respectively, dissolved in water to make 1% (w/v) protein solutions, and then the pH was adjusted to 7.0. To each of the mixture solutions (100 mL), eight aliquots of oil at 20, 50, 100, 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 mL were added. Then these mixtures were homogenized and mixed (20,000 r/min, 2 min) (IKA, Germany) at a high speed29. Freshly prepared emulsions were immediately subjected to EC measurement.

Microscopic morphology

The microscopic morphologies of the emulsions were examined using an electron microscope at a magnification of 20 × 40 (i.e., 800×). All observations were performed immediately after sample preparation to ensure the freshness and integrity of the emulsions.

Appearance determination

The visual appearance of the emulsions was recorded using a digital camera immediately after all emulsion samples were prepared.

Phase inversion of the emulsions determination

Phase inversion from O/W to W/O was determined using two complementary methods. First, conductivity was measured using a conductivity meter (DDSJ-318T Conductivity Meter Shanghai Yidian Science Instrument Co., Ltd). A sharp decrease in conductivity to near-zero values indicated inversion to W/O. All measurements were conducted at a constant temperature (25 °C) to eliminate thermal effects on conductivity. Second, a drop test was performed: a drop of emulsion was gently placed into either water or soybean oil. O/W emulsions dispersed readily in water but not in oil, whereas W/O emulsions showed the opposite behaviour.

Rheological determination

The rheological properties were determined using a rheometer (MARS40, Haake, Germany) equipped with 35-mm parallel plates. Changes in the rheological properties of the samples were evaluated via apparent viscosity and temperature scans. The gap between the parallel plates was 1 mm, and the initial temperature was 25 ℃. One mL of the SPI solution (15% w/ w) was loaded between parallel plates30.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated three times and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The statistical significance of mean values between multiple treatment groups was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s test. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
