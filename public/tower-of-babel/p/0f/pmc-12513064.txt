Burden of inflammatory enteritis caused by diarrheagenic Escherichia coli in Spain (2016–2022)
Authors: Rafael Garcia-Carretero, Valentin Hernandez-Barrera, Ruth Gil-Prieto, Angel Gil-de-Miguel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDiarrheal diseases caused by diarrheagenic Escherichia coli (DEC) are a significant public health challenge, particularly among vulnerable populations such as children and the elderly. This study aimed to analyze the burden of DEC-related inflammatory enteritis in Spain, focusing on hospitalization rates, mortality, and associated risk factors.MethodsUsing data from a national registry, we conducted a nationwide, retrospective analysis including hospitalized patients with DEC from 2016 to 2022. We used descriptive statistics and multivariable logistic regression to assess predictors of mortality.ResultsWe identified 265,754 hospitalizations related to DEC. Individuals aged ≥80 years exhibited the highest rates of hospitalization (516 per 100,000). Mortality rates increased with age. The elderly show the highest mortality rate (53.8 % of deaths). Women had higher mortality rates than men, especially in older age groups. Multivariable logistic regression identified age ≥ 80 years, comorbidities such as malignancy (odds ratio [OR] = 2.23, 95 % CI = 2.15, 2.3) and sepsis (OR = 3.6, 95 % CI = 3.47, 3.74), and coinfections with Staphylococcus aureus (OR = 3.46, 95 % CI = 2.1, 5.69) and COVID-19 (OR = 2.35, 95 % CI = 2.21, 2.49) as significant predictors of mortality. Regional disparities in hospitalization and mortality rates were also observed.ConclusionThese findings underscore the growing burden of DEC-related diarrhea in Spain, particularly among aging populations, and highlight the need for targeted public health interventions, improved diagnostic protocols, and integrated care for patients with comorbidities.Keywords: Escherichia coli, Mortality, Hospitalization burden

Introduction

Diarrheal diseases remain a significant global health challenge, contributing to substantial morbidity and mortality, particularly among vulnerable populations such as children under five years of age and the elderly (Kyu et al., 2024). According to the World Health Organization (WHO), diarrheal diseases are the second leading cause of death in children under five, accounting for approximately 525,000 deaths annually worldwide (Hartman et al., 2022). Mortality rates are lower in developed countries, but diarrheal illnesses still pose a considerable burden due to associated healthcare costs (Kyu et al., 2024; Reiner et al., 2020). Although there have been advances in diagnostic techniques and public health policies, infectious diarrhea remains as a significant contributor to diarrheal disease burden worldwide (Liang et al., 2024).

Among the causative pathogens of infectious diarrhea, diarrheagenic Escherichia coli (DEC) represents a major cause of both sporadic cases and outbreaks (Ballal, 2016; Akinlabi et al., 2023; Saka et al., 2019). Diarrheagenic E. coli can be classified into five major pathogenic categories on the basis of mechanism of pathogenesis: enterotoxigenic (ETEC), enteroinvasive (EIEC), enteropathogenic (EPEC), enterohemorrhagic (EHEC), and enteroaggregative (EAEC). Each type has its own virulence mechanism, clinical presentation and course, geographical patterns, and epidemiological trends. Except enterotoxigenic strains, the rest of E. coli strains can cause inflammatory enteritis (Kaper et al., 2004).

Inflammatory enteritis is a pathologic condition characterized by ileal and/or colonic inflammation. Its clinical presentation can range from slight, superficial involvement of the enteric mucosa to more severe, ulcerative wounds affecting the entire enteric wall. Therefore, inflammatory enteritis can present with abdominal pain, dysentery, watery diarrhea, fever and blood or mucus in the stools (Kaper et al., 2004). Inflammatory enteritis caused by DEC poses several challenges, including mortality and morbidity among immunocompromised patients and individuals with underlying comorbidities (Miller and Cushman, 2011; Shane et al., 2017). In Spain, the epidemiology of DEC remains understudied, particularly in terms of age and sex distribution, mortality, and associated comorbidities. In a recent, Spanish, multi-center, prospective study including 1769 individuals with diarrhea, the authors reported that DEC was detected in 23.2 % of patients, and affected most significantly to patients ≤5 years old (27.7 % vs. 13.3 %) compared with those >5 years old (Llorente et al., 2023).

Public health strategies to prevent inflammatory enteritis caused by DEC focus on hygiene, food safety, and safe water practices. These strategies include thorough handwashing, proper food preparation and storage, avoiding contaminated water and unpasteurized beverages, and practicing caution around animals. Additionally, public health interventions like access to clean water and sanitation, food safety regulation. Infrastructure improvement to ensure access to clean water, public awareness campaigns to educate people about the risks of DEC, and conducting campaigns on how to prevent infections are also useful public health strategies. By implementing these interventions, we can significantly reduce the incidence and impact of DEC-related infections and their associated inflammatory enteritis.

Understanding the epidemiological trends of DEC is key for public health strategies, improving diagnostic protocols and testing, and guiding clinical management. Therefore, the aim of this study is to describe the features and epidemiology of diarrheagenic Escherichia coli in Spain between 2016 and 2022, in terms of age and sex distribution, mortality, geographical distribution, and comorbidities. By analyzing national surveillance data and clinical records, our aim is to provide an overview of the burden of DEC in Spain and identify potential risk factors associated with mortality. Such insights are essential for developing targeted interventions to reduce the impact of DEC-related diarrhea in healthcare settings.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective study utilized centralized data from the Spanish Ministry of Health. The Minimum Basic Data Set at hospitalization (MBDS-H) is a mandatory, centralized registry of the Spanish National Health System that compiles discharge reports from hospitals across Spain. This database is a standardized registry covering nearly 95 % of Spanish hospitals (including both public and private settings) and includes approximately 97 % of all hospital discharge reports in Spain. The registry contains demographic and clinical information, such as age, sex, admission/discharge dates, hospital type, residence, ICU admission, mortality, diagnoses, procedures, and cost. Diagnostic information is coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM). Using these codes, we identified patients diagnosed with diarrheagenic Escherichia coli (codes B96.20 [unspecified DEC], B96.21 [Shiga toxin-producing E. coli, STEC, O157], B96.22 [Other specified Shiga toxin-producing E. coli], B96.23 [Unspecified Shiga toxin-producing E. coli], and B96.29 [other specified DEC]) between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2022. We also included the following codes: A04.0 (enteropathogenic E. coli infection), A04. (enteroinvasive E. coli infection), A04.3 (enterohaemorrhagic E. coli infection) and A04.4 (other intestinal E. coli infections). The code A04.1 for enterotoxigenic E. coli infection was not included in our analyses, since it is not a cause of inflammatory enteritis. The study was approved by the Ethics and Research Board of our institution. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective and anonymized nature of the data.

Study population

The study population included all hospitalized patients with a primary or secondary diagnosis of DEC, as identified by the relevant ICD-10-CM codes from 2016 to 2022. Our study included a total of 265,972 hospitalizations. Those with incomplete demographic and clinical information, including age, sex, or type of discharge, were excluded from the analysis (n = 218). Consequently, 265,754 participants were included in the final analysis.

Variables

For each patient, we extracted data on sex, age, comorbidities, and mortality. Age was stratified into predefined groups to evaluate age-specific trends. Comorbidities were identified using specific ICD-10-CM codes and categorized for analysis. As independent variables, we included demographics (age, sex), comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, cerebrovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, chronic liver disease, malignancy, dementia, rheumatoid disease, peptic ulcer, and HIV infection), and different acute infections (urinary, respiratory, digestive, and sepsis). Several pathogens were identified as causative of coinfections (P. aeruginosa, Staph. aureus, Klebsiella spp., and COVID-19). Cardiovascular disease was recorded as a composite variable encompassing myocardial infarction and, congestive heart failure, without further subclassification. All variables of interest and chronic conditions were identified using the applicable code for ICD-10-CM, as mentioned. As dependent variables we considered mortality (defined as all-cause in-hospital death).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the study population. Absolute numbers and rates per 100,000 population were calculated for hospitalizations and mortality to provide a standardized measure of disease burden. Age-specific population data for rate calculations were obtained from the Spanish National Institute of Statistics.

To assess the significance of trends over time in DEC-related admissions and mortality for each variable, we used Poisson regression models. Poisson regression models with year as a continuous variable can estimate rate changes, avoiding the need for multiple comparison adjustments. For post hoc categorical comparisons (i.e., between age groups or comorbidities), unadjusted p-values are reported with the understanding that these exploratory analyses warrant validation in future studies. A formal adjustment for multiple comparisons (i.e., Bonferroni) was not applied to primary regression models, as our hypotheses focused on overall trends rather than individual pairwise tests. The resulting p-values indicate whether a significant linear trend existed across the entire study period (2016–2022). So, these are not p-values for pairwise comparisons between individual years.

To identify risk factors associated with mortality, we performed a multivariable logistic regression analysis. Variables included in the model were age, sex, and comorbidities. Results were expressed as adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95 % confidence intervals (CI). A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.1 and SPSS version 28.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the local Ethics and Research Board. Data were obtained through a formal request to the Spanish Ministry of Health. Patient data were provided anonymized and de-identified, ensuring confidentiality and privacy.

Sex-specific trends

Men accounted for 122,386 hospitalizations (46.1 % of the total), with an overall hospitalization rate of 75.8 per 100,000 (95 % CI: 75.4, 76.3). The HR for men increased significantly from 72.1 in 2016 to 90.0 in 2022 (p < 0.001). Women accounted for 143,368 hospitalizations (53.9 % of the total), with an overall HR of 85.6 per 100,000 (95 % CI: 85.2, 86.1). The HR for women also increased significantly, from 91.5 in 2016 to 99.2 in 2022 (p < 0.001). Men consistently exhibited higher hospitalization rates than women across most age groups, particularly in infants under 1 year and older adults. In individuals aged 60–69 and 70–79 years, women's hospitalization rates remained relatively stable over time (p = 0.289 and p = 0.532, respectively), while men showed a significant upward trend (see Supplementary Table 2).

Age-specific trends

Hospitalization rates showed a significant increasing trend, particularly in older age groups. Fig. 1 illustrates the trends in hospitalization and mortality rates by age. The highest HRs were consistently observed in individuals aged ≥80 years, with a peak of 636 per 100,000 in 2022. Infants under 1 year had the highest HR among pediatric age groups, with an overall rate of 227.1 per 100,000 (95 % CI: 221.4, 232.9). Although the HR declined from 343.5 in 2016 to 198.1 in 2022, it remained significantly higher than in other pediatric groups (p < 0.001). Patients aged ≥80 years had the highest HR of all age groups, with an overall rate of 516.7 per 100,000 (95 % CI: 513.7, 519.7), which increased significantly from 520.7 in 2016 to 636 in 2022 (p < 0.001).

Mortality trends

Between 2016 and 2022, a total of 24,455 deaths were recorded among hospitalized patients with E. coli-related diarrhea in Spain. The number of deaths increased significantly over the observation period, from 2643 in 2016 to 4676 in 2022, reflecting a growing burden of mortality associated with E. coli infections. Overall, mortality rates exhibited an increasing trend over time, particularly in older adults (p < 0.001). Fig. 2 shows mortality rates by age group. We also observed sex differences in mortality. Men had consistently fewer total deaths than women (11,394 vs. 13,061). However, mortality among men increased at a faster rate, particularly in the 60–69 and 70–79 age groups. Women consistently had higher mortality rates than men across most age groups, particularly among the elderly. The highest mortality rate was observed in 2020 and remained elevated in following years.

Geographical distribution

Fig. 3 shows the standardized hospitalization and mortality rates by region. The regions with the lowest standardized HR were Melilla and Andalucía (24 and 37 per 100,000 population, respectively). In contrast, the regions with the highest HR were La Rioja and Catalunya (127 and 129 per 100,000 population, respectively). Regarding mortality, the lowest in-hospital mortality rates were found in Catalunya and Cantabria (5.4 and 6.8 per 100,000 population, respectively). The regions with the highest mortality rates were Melilla, Castilla y León, and Andalucía (14, 13.2, and 12.7 per 100,000 population, respectively).

Multivariable logistic regression

Table 2 presents the results of the multivariable logistic regression analysis. We identified several significant predictors of mortality among hospitalized patients with E. coli-related diarrhea. Men had a similar risk of mortality compared to women, with an adjusted OR of 1.01 (95 % CI: 0.98, 1.04), indicating no significant sex-based difference in mortality risk. Older age was strongly associated with an increased risk of mortality. Patients aged ≥80 years had the highest odds of mortality, with an OR of 8.59 (95 % CI: 6.58, 11.23), compared to younger age groups (e.g., 18–59 years).

Several comorbidities were associated with an increased risk of mortality, including cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, and liver diseases. Malignancy (OR = 2.23 [95 % CI: 2.15, 2.30]) and sepsis (OR = 3.60 [95 % CI: 3.47, 3.74]) were the strongest comorbidity predictors.

Coinfections with certain pathogens were also associated with an increased risk of mortality, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa (OR = 1.71 [95 % CI: 1.61, 1.81]), Klebsiella spp. (OR = 1.14 [95 % CI: 1.10, 1.19]), and COVID-19 (OR = 2.35 [95 % CI: 2.21, 2.49]). Coinfection with Staphylococcus aureus was the strongest predictor of mortality (OR = 3.46 [95 % CI: 2.10, 5.69]).

Discussion

Our findings highlight significant trends in hospitalization rates, mortality, and risk factors, offering valuable insights into the epidemiology and clinical outcomes of E. coli infections in a large, nationally representative cohort.

Hospitalization trends

The overall hospitalization rate for E. coli-related diarrhea increased significantly over the study period, from 81.9 per 100,000 population in 2016 to 94.7 per 100,000 in 2022. This upward trend was particularly pronounced among older adults, particularly individuals aged ≥80 years, who presented the highest hospitalization rates (516.7 per 100,000). The elderly population also accounted for the majority of hospitalizations, reflecting the growing burden of E. coli infections in aging populations. This trend is consistent with global observations of increasing diarrheal disease burden among older adults, likely due to age-related declines in immune function and higher rates of comorbidities (Troeger et al., 2017; Anderson et al., 2019; Hebbelstrup Jensen et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2017; Quiros-Roldan et al., 2024).

Pediatric populations, particularly infants (<1 year), also had high hospitalization rates (227.15 per 100,000), though these rates declined over the observation period. This decline may reflect improvements in pediatric care, vaccination programs, or public health interventions targeting diarrheal diseases in children (Baye et al., 2021). However, the persistently high rates among infants underscore the need for maintained monitoring in this vulnerable population.

Mortality trends

Mortality among hospitalized patients with E. coli-related diarrhea also increased significantly over the study period, from2,643 deaths in 2016 to 4676 deaths in 2022. The elderly (≥80 years) accounted for the majority of deaths (13,163 deaths, 53.8 % of total mortality), with a mortality rate that increased over time. This aligns with the hospitalization trends and highlights the disproportionate burden of E. coli-related diarrhea mortality in aging populations (Troeger et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2017; Canizalez-Roman et al., 2016). Regarding sex differences, women had higher mortality rates than men across most age groups, particularly among the elderly. While initial crude rates suggested a higher mortality rate among women compared to men, this difference was not statistically significant in the adjusted logistic regression model (adjusted OR for men: 1.01, 95 % CI: 0.98–1.04). This sex disparity may reflect differences in underlying health conditions, healthcare access, or biological factors that influence susceptibility to severe outcomes rather than biological sex itself. We could not explain sex differences, although we are aware that our findings have clinical significance and should guide translational studies to interpret the pathological mechanisms or the causes of this disparity (Luo et al., 2019). Also, the geographical disparities in hospitalization and mortality rates could be influenced by regional differences in healthcare access, diagnostic procedures, or public health infrastructure, but these issues are out of the scope of this study.

The overall risk of mortality increased significantly over the study period, with the highest risk observed in 2020 (OR = 1.49). This peak in mortality may be attributed to the COVID-19 pandemic, which likely limited healthcare resources and exacerbated the severity of E. coli infections in vulnerable populations (Khavandegar et al., 2025). The persistence of elevated mortality risk in 2021 and 2022 suggests that the pandemic's impact on healthcare systems and patient outcomes may have extended beyond the initial surge. The coinfection with COVID-19 will be further discussed.

Risk factors for mortality

The logistic regression analysis identified several key predictors of mortality. Age was the strongest predictor. Patients aged ≥80 years had an 8-fold higher risk of mortality compared to infants. This highlights the vulnerability of elderly populations to severe outcomes from E. coli infections. Our findings were consistent with previous studies highlighting the impact of aging on immune function and the increased prevalence of comorbidities in older adults (Troeger et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2017; Khalil et al., 2018).

Comorbidities also played a significant role in mortality risk. Conditions such as malignancy (OR = 2.23), sepsis (OR = 3.60), and cardiovascular disease (OR = 1.64) were strongly associated with increased mortality. In contrast, we found that conditions like diabetes (OR = 0.88) and urinary infections (OR = 0.89) were associated with reduced risk. Again, we could not reasonably explain the protective effect of diabetes and urinary infections. We hypothesize that there might be some confounding features that were not included in the analyses. While these findings should be interpreted cautiously, potential explanations include enhanced medical monitoring and selection bias, as diabetic patients and those with recurrent urinary infections may receive more frequent healthcare contact, leading to earlier diagnosis and treatment of E. coli infections. So, if they were hospitalized sooner or received more aggressive supportive care, their mortality risk may have been artificially lowered. We are also aware that unmeasured factors, such as differential antibiotic use, milder pathogen strains, or socioeconomic disparities in care access, could influence outcomes. These associations do not imply a true biological protective effect and instead highlight the complexity of confounding in observational studies. Anyway, further research is needed to clarify the role of these conditions, including treatment patterns and pathogen virulence factors.

Coinfections with pathogens such as S. (OR = 3.46) and COVID-19 (OR = 2.35) were also significant predictors of mortality, highlighting the impact of secondary infections on clinical outcomes. Coinfection with other pathogens appears to aggravate diarrhea severity and the risk of poorer outcomes (Poolman and Anderson, 2018). The strong association between COVID-19 and mortality is particularly noteworthy, as it reflects the burden of the COVID-19 pandemic on patients with E. coli infections. Regarding COVID-19, SARS-CoV-2 may induce lymphocytes damage of the host, which may lead to the impairment of the immune system, resulting in microbial coinfections among those patients. A recent systematic review reported that Staph. aureus and E. coli were the most common bacterial coinfections during the COVID-19 pandemic in Romania (Vulcanescu et al., 2024).

Strengths and limitations

We are aware of several limitations. The study relied on administrative data, which may be subject to coding errors or misclassification. Our study analyzed cardiovascular disease as a composite variable, combining chronic heart failure and myocardial infarction due to data constraints. While this approach allowed us to assess the aggregate impact of cardiovascular diseases on mortality, it precluded evaluation of subtype-specific risks (e.g., heart failure vs. ischemic disease). Also, the lack of detailed clinical data limits our ability to explore specific factors contributing to severe outcomes. So, the use of an administrative database to identify DEC-related episodes is dependent on the quality of discharge report data. Reliance on ICD-10 coding may introduce misclassification bias due to variation in coding practices across institutions. Furthermore, while valuable for hospital management, this database lacks potentially important information, such as resource utilization during hospitalization (e.g., diagnostic tests, antibiotic treatments) and unrecorded clinical characteristics. Coding practices or underreporting may introduce bias. Additionally, the retrospective and observational design precludes causal inferences. The absence of individual patient-level data, pathogen-specific factors, and treatment regimens (unavailable in our original dataset) may further limit the reliability of our findings. Only patients with E. coli-related inflammatory enteritis were included in the study, and the burden of some enterotoxigenic strains of E. coli could not be evaluated. Additionally, the analysis did not account for potential confounders such as socioeconomic status or healthcare access, which may influence hospitalization and mortality outcomes among regions in Spain. These potential confounders could eventually explain the differences found when we analyzed the geographical distribution. Also, several comorbidities were found to be protective factors in E. coli-related diarrhea, suggesting that some confounding variables are distorting or masking the effects of analyzed comorbidities in mortality. Also, while our primary analyses of temporal trends used regression modeling to minimize multiple testing concerns, some subgroup analyses may be underpowered or susceptible to type I error due to unadjusted comparisons. While our analysis spanned 2016–2023 to assess long-term trends, we recognize that the COVID-19 pandemic may have transiently affected hospitalization practices, healthcare access, or pathogen circulation. These system-wide disruptions were beyond the scope of our trend analysis but warrant targeted investigation in future studies.

The strengths of our study include its nationwide scope, its large, nationally representative dataset, and the inclusion of detailed demographic and clinical variables. We also used a robust methodological approach. By analyzing this centralized administrative database, we captured comprehensive information to depict an overall picture and enhance the generalizability of our findings.

Implications for public health and clinical practice

The findings of this study have important implications for public health and clinical practice. The increasing burden of E. coli-related diarrhea hospitalizations and mortality, particularly among older adults, underscores the need for targeted interventions to prevent and manage these infections in aging populations. E. coli infections have significant implications due to their potential to cause a wide range of illnesses, from mild diarrhea to severe, life-threatening conditions. Effective public health strategies may include enhanced surveillance, vaccination programs, and public health campaigns to promote hygiene and food safety, as they are crucial for preventing outbreaks and managing the spread of E. coli. In clinical practice, prompt diagnosis, appropriate treatment, and careful monitoring of complications are essential for minimizing patient morbidity and mortality. Regarding surveillance, E. coli can cause large outbreaks, leading to significant morbidity and mortality. Public health agencies play a vital role in surveillance, identifying outbreaks, and implementing control measures to prevent further spread. Also, food safety emphasizes proper management to prevent foodborne transmission. Public health initiatives should focus on promoting safe food handling practices, including proper cooking temperatures, thorough washing of produce, and avoiding raw or unpasteurized products. Public health efforts include ensuring safe drinking water and promoting responsible recreational water use. From a clinical point of view, healthcare facilities must implement strict infection control measures, including hand hygiene, isolation precautions, and appropriate cleaning and disinfection protocols. Regarding antibiotic resistance, public health efforts should focus on promoting responsible antibiotic use to slow the development of resistance. Regarding comorbidities, the strong association between some conditions and mortality highlights the importance of integrated care for patients with E. coli infections, particularly those with underlying health conditions. We have identified vulnerable populations such as children, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals, who are at higher risk of severe E. coli infections. Public health strategies need to address the specific needs of these vulnerable populations.We should be also aware for coinfections, which significantly increase the risk of mortality, and consider early interventions to improve the outcomes. Clinicians should be aware of the various clinical presentations of E. coli infections, including diarrhea, and other extraintestinal infections, and must be vigilant for potential complications, since early recognition and management of complications are essential for improving patient outcomes (Mueller and Tainter, 2025; van den Beld et al., 2019). Also, regional disparities in hospitalization and mortality rates highlight the importance of addressing inequities in healthcare access and resource allocation.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the increasing burden of E. coli-related diarrhea hospitalizations and mortality in Spain, particularly among infants and older adults. Hospitalization rates increased significantly over the study period, particularly among the elderly and women. Infants and older adults accounted for the highest hospitalization burden. Sex disparities were evident, with men showing higher hospitalization rates in nearly all age groups. Other factors, such as age, some comorbidities, and coinfections were significant predictors of mortality. These risk factors highlight the need for interventions to reduce the impact of E. coli-related diarrhea in certain populations. Future research should explore sex disparities and the role of coinfections on E. coli-related outcomes.
