Marginal Contrast Saturation‐Based Intraoperative Scoring System Predicts Objective Response to DEB‐TACE: Development and Validation of a CBCT‐Derived Real‐Time Score
Authors: Zhongsong Gao, Bo Li, Bo Feng, Cheng Sun, Ying Zang, Xin Zhang, Yongmei Wang, Qi Xin, Kefeng Jia, Rong Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo develop and validate a simple intraoperative scoring system based on preoperative and cone‐beam CT (CBCT) features for real‐time prediction of objective response (OR) by mRECIST after DEB‐TACE in patients with HCC.Materials and MethodsIn this single‐center retrospective study, 274 patients (with a total of 386 HCC tumors) who underwent first‐session DEB‐TACE from 2022 to 2024 were included. CBCT was used to assess a 5‐grade marginal contrast saturation (MCS), capsule presence, vascular lake phenomenon (VLP), and other candidate imaging features. Tumor response was assessed on contrast‐enhanced CT using mRECIST. Analyses were performed on a tumor basis, with tumors assigned to development (n = 286) and validation (n = 100) sets by patient‐level stratified randomization (7:3). Variables associated with OR were entered into a multivariable logistic regression; β coefficients were converted into a point‐based score. Model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination and calibration using the AUROC and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test.ResultsMost patients were male (78.8%), with a mean age of 62.5 ± 9.4 years. The median tumor diameter was 3.75 cm (interquartile range [IQR], 2.50–6.50 cm). The overall objective response rate (ORR) was 67.9% (262/386). Introduction

Liver cancer remains a significant global health challenge, consistently ranking high in both incidence and cancer‐related mortality. Among primary hepatic malignancies, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most prevalent subtype, accounting for approximately 75%–85% of diagnosed cases [1]. Among locoregional options, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), the selective administration of chemotherapeutic agents into tumor‐feeding arteries under digital subtraction angiography, is recommended for intermediate‐stage (BCLC B) HCC according to BCLC guidelines [2]. Conventional TACE (cTACE) involves the injection of a chemotherapy–lipiodol emulsion followed by vessel occlusion with a gelatin sponge [3]. In contrast, drug‐eluting bead TACE (DEB‐TACE) utilizes beads saturated with the drug to permit slow intratumoral release, thereby reducing systemic peak concentrations while maintaining efficacy and improving tolerability [4, 5].

Unlike cTACE, where intraprocedural lipiodol deposition provides a visual surrogate of embolization adequacy, DEB‐TACE beads are radiolucent on X‐ray, leaving operators without a unified, objective real‐time standard to judge the degree of embolization. Studies have highlighted this gap and proposed imaging‐based solutions, such as marginal contrast saturation (MCS) [6], which provides a practical, rim‐focused, semiquantitative signal on cone‐beam CT (CBCT) that can be graded on‐table and is presumed to reflect peritumoral microsphere deposition. Other CBCT features, such as clear and complete margins [7] and the vascular lake phenomenon (VLP) [8], have also been shown to be associated with improved tumor response. These findings indicate that CBCT‐derived intraprocedural features can effectively predict therapeutic outcomes. Although several multi‐feature imaging models have been developed for post‐TACE response prediction, such as postoperative functional MRI metrics and preoperative CT radiomics [9, 10], these approaches require offline processing and are not designed for real‐time intraprocedural guidance. Moreover, most prior CBCT studies have evaluated these features as individual markers, and an easy‐to‐use, integrated point‐based tool for end‐of‐procedure response prediction after DEB‐TACE remains limited.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to develop and validate a scoring model based on preoperative and intraoperative imaging features for the real‐time prediction of tumor objective response, as defined by the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST), following DEB‐TACE in patients with HCC.

Materials and Methods

This was a single‐center retrospective study approved by the institutional review board (number: IRB2022‐039‐01). The requirement for informed consent was waived.

Patients

From January 2022 to December 2024, we reviewed 369 consecutive patients who underwent their first session of DEB‐TACE at our institution. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) HCC confirmed by histopathology or clinically diagnosed according to the Primary Liver Cancer Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines [11] and/or the diagnostic criteria of the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) [12]; (2) liver function classified as Child–Pugh class A or B; (3) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) score of 0–1; (4) availability of contrast‐enhanced abdominal CT or MRI within 4 weeks before DEB‐TACE and within 12 weeks after the procedure; and (5) at least one target lesion measurable according to mRECIST. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) incomplete clinical or imaging data; (2) additional lipiodol injection into the target lesion during the DEB‐TACE procedure, as this could confound the intraoperative assessment of MCS; and (3) poor image quality, defined as intraoperative CBCT images in which tumor margins or MCS could not be reliably assessed because of motion or other artifacts. Finally, 274 patients were included. Most were men (216/274, 78.8%), and the mean age was 62.5 ± 9.4 years. In total, 386 tumors were treated (median size, 3.75 cm; IQR, 2.50–6.50 cm). The flowchart of the study is shown in Figure S1.

Pretreatment Evaluation

Pretreatment evaluation included comprehensive medical and physical assessments, laboratory tests, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT). Baseline data included demographic characteristics, liver function parameters, tumor markers (including alpha‐fetoprotein [AFP]), and BCLC stage. Imaging features, including tumor size, tumor location, homogeneous intratumoral enhancement, hypervascularity, the presence of a capsule, and vascular invasion, were documented. Hypervascularity was defined as conspicuous tumor‐associated arterial vascularity on pretreatment contrast‐enhanced CT or MRI, characterized by marked arterial‐phase enhancement relative to the surrounding liver parenchyma and/or prominent intratumoral or peritumoral arterial structures.

DEB‐TACE Procedures

DEB‐TACE procedures were carried out in a dedicated angiographic suite under local anesthesia (2% lidocaine) with intravenous conscious sedation. Access was obtained via the common femoral artery, after which catheter‐based angiography of the celiac trunk and superior mesenteric artery was performed to map the hepatic arterial anatomy, identify tumor‐feeding vessels, and verify the patency of the portal vein.

When helpful, intraprocedural CBCT was acquired to assist superselective catheterization and to assess tumor vascularization. Target feeders were then catheterized with a microcatheter, and chemoembolization was performed using 100–300 μm or 300–500 μm drug‐eluting beads (DEBs; Boston Scientific, USA, or CalliSpheres, Hengrui Medicine, China) preloaded with epirubicin (40 mg per vial). The intended treatment endpoint was sluggish flow/near stasis in the superselective tumor‐supplying artery after DEB delivery (typically up to two vials, as clinically appropriate). If near stasis was not achieved after DEB administration, adjunctive bland embolization was used until the endpoint was reached.

Measurement of CBCT Features

All procedures were performed in a dedicated angiography suite equipped with a ceiling‐mounted angiographic C‐arm system with a 40 × 30 cm flat‐panel detector (Allura Xper FD20, Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). Unenhanced CBCT was acquired at the end of DEB‐TACE using the “Xper CT Abdomen Fast Low Dose” protocol. Acquisition parameters were 120 kV with an automatically adjusted tube current ranging from 50 to 325 mA. The C‐arm was positioned at the head end of the table and the liver centered in the isocenter of rotation. A total of 384 projections were acquired over a 240° angular range (−120° to +120°) during an approximately 4‐s rotation. The volumetric dataset was reconstructed using a filtered back‐projection algorithm, yielding an isotropic voxel size of 0.65 mm (matrix 297 × 384; field of view approximately 250 × 250 × 194 mm). Images were automatically transferred to a dedicated workstation for analysis. Immediately after DEB injection, CBCT was used to assess the MCS, defined as contrast stasis along the tumor rim. For analysis, MCS was summarized (per prior descriptions [6]) as a five‐grade metric in 25% increments on the axial slice with the largest tumor diameter: I (< 25%), II (25%–< 50%), III (50%–< 75%), IV (75%–< 100%), and V (100% circumferential saturation). For multifocal disease, each tumor's feeding artery was superselectively catheterized and embolized in sequence, and CBCT was performed after each embolization to assess the MCS. Examples of each category are shown in Figure S2. In addition, the angiographic endpoint was recorded using the subjective angiographic chemoembolization endpoint (SACE) score (I–IV) [13]. SACE assessments were performed by two interventional radiologists with more than 5 years of experience; in cases of disagreement, a senior radiologist with more than 10 years of experience made the final determination. The MCS was independently graded by two interventional radiologists. The VLP was noted as present/absent, with adjunctive embolization performed at the operator's discretion when the VLP occurred. The presence of a capsule and hypervascularity were noted.

Follow‐Up Imaging

All patients underwent contrast‐enhanced CT or MRI within 12 weeks after DEB‐TACE, in accordance with the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidelines [14]. The primary endpoint was assessed using the first post‐treatment follow‐up scan. Follow‐up tumor response was assessed on hepatic‐arterial phase CT using mRECIST [15] and categorized as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). The follow‐up images were independently reviewed by two radiologists who were blinded to the CBCT features of the treated HCC. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Objective response (OR) was defined as CR or PR. Objective response rate (ORR) was defined as the proportion of treated tumors achieving OR.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed on a tumor basis. To account for the intrapatient clustering of tumors, we performed patient‐level stratified random sampling to split the dataset into development and validation sets at a 7:3 ratio. All tumors from a single patient were allocated to the same set to preserve independence between datasets and avoid information leakage. Continuous variables are summarized as the mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables are summarized as n (%). t ‐tests or Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests were used for continuous variables, and χ2 or Fisher's exact tests were used for categorical variables, as appropriate. Interrater reliability for the MCS was assessed using a two‐way random‐effects, absolute‐agreement ICC. Values less than 0.5, between 0.5 and 0.75, between 0.75 and 0.9, and greater than 0.90 are indicative of poor, moderate, good, and excellent agreement, respectively [16]. Spearman's rank correlation was used to assess associations between intraoperative metrics (MCS and SACE) and mRECIST response. Spearman rank correlations were interpreted a priori as weak when the ρ was < 0.30, moderate when 0.30 ≤ ρ < 0.50, and strong when the ρ was ≥ 0.50. In the development set, variables associated with OR were first screened by univariable logistic regression. Covariates with p < 0.05 were entered into a multivariable logistic regression, with stepwise selection to derive the final model. Model discrimination was quantified by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Calibration was evaluated with calibration plots and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test. A parsimonious point‐based score was then derived from the multivariable model by scaling β‐coefficients. The optimal cutoff was determined by the Youden index to stratify nodules into “high‐response probability” and “low‐response probability” groups, and the corresponding sensitivity and specificity were reported. Robustness was examined in sensitivity analyses using disease control response (DCR) and complete response (CR) as alternative endpoints in the whole dataset. All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All tests were two‐sided, with the significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Patients and Tumor Baseline Characteristics

Among the 274 included patients, hepatitis B virus was the predominant etiology (61.3%), followed by alcohol‐related liver disease (16.4%) and hepatitis C (8.4%). All patients had preserved liver function (Child–Pugh A/B: 47.1%/52.9%), with a median model for end‐stage liver disease (MELD) of 8 (IQR 7–11). Baseline AFP concentration was < 400 ng/mL in 79.2% and ≥ 400 ng/mL in 20.8% of patients. In terms of BCLC stage, 44.1% were stage 0/A, 24.5% were stage B, and 31.4% were stage C. Correlation Analysis of Tumor Response and Intraoperative Score (MCS/SACE)

Among the 386 treated tumors, the ORR was 262/386 (67.9%), comprising 55 complete responses (CR, 14.2%) and 207 partial responses (PR, 53.6%); the remaining lesions showed stable disease (SD) in 68 (17.6%) patients and progressive disease (PD) in 56 (14.5%) patients. Interreader agreement for intraoperative MCS grading was good, with an ICC of 0.88 (95% CI, 0.60–0.97). The MCSs were distributed as follows: I, 51 (13.2%); II, 57 (14.8%); III, 76 (19.7%); IV, 169 (43.8%); and V, 33 (8.6%). The SACE grades were I, 29 (7.5%); II, 155 (40.2%); III, 119 (30.8%); and IV, 83 (21.5%). Examples of outcomes by baseline MCS: a lesion graded as MCS G3 exhibited a PR at the first follow‐up imaging, whereas another lesion graded as MCS G5 achieved a CR at the first follow‐up and sustained CR on serial assessments (Figures S3 and S4). Spearman correlation analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between MCS and OR, whereas SACE exhibited only a weak positive correlation with OR (Figure 1): MCS, ρ = 0.66, p < 0.001; and SACE, ρ = 0.21, p < 0.001.

Model Development and Validation

In the development set, multivariable logistic regression revealed four independent predictors of OR: tumor diameter, presence of a tumor capsule, occurrence of VLP, and MCS grade, as shown in Table 2. We transformed the regression coefficients into a pragmatic system to facilitate clinical use. The β coefficients were as follows: tumor diameter < 5 cm (β = 1.70), tumor capsule (β = 1.07), VLP (β = 2.28), and MCS (per‐grade β = 1.36). Points were assigned to approximate their relative effects: diameter < 5 cm (+2), presence of a capsule (+1), VLP (+2), and MCS graded as I (+1), II (+2), III (+3), IV (+4), or V (+5). The details are listed in Table 3. The total score ranges from 1 to 10, with higher scores indicating a greater likelihood of objective response. The scoring system demonstrated excellent discrimination in the development set, with an AUROC of 0.91 (95% CI, 0.87–0.94). Using the Youden index–derived cutoff of 5.5 points, the corresponding sensitivity and specificity were 0.77 (95% CI, 0.70–0.82) and 0.92 (95% CI, 0.84–0.96), respectively, and the calibration was good (Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test, p = 0.43). In the validation set, the model performance remained high, with an AUROC of 0.93 (95% CI, 0.86–0.99); at the same cutoff, the sensitivity and specificity were 0.79 (95% CI, 0.67–0.88) and 0.90 (95% CI, 0.74–0.98), respectively, and the calibration was likewise acceptable (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.46). In the development set, the ORR differed markedly between the groups (94.84% vs. 34.35%; p < 0.001). The validation set showed a similarly significant difference (94.83% vs. 35.72%; p < 0.001) (Figure 2).

Sensitivity Analyses

When the pooled dataset was used and the secondary endpoint was redefined as DCR, the point‐based scoring model maintained strong performance. Discrimination remained high (AUROC = 0.90; 95% CI, 0.85–0.94), with good calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow, p = 0.24). At the Youden index–derived cutoff, the sensitivity and specificity for DCR were 0.79 (95% CI, 0.75–0.84) and 0.89 (95% CI, 0.78–0.96), respectively. Consistent with this finding, the DCR differed significantly between the low‐ and high‐response strata (p < 0.001; Figure 3). When the endpoint was restricted to CR, the model performance was moderate (AUROC = 0.80; 95% CI, 0.74–0.85), whereas the calibration remained acceptable (Hosmer–Lemeshow, p = 0.48). At the same cutoff, the sensitivity and specificity for CR were 0.76 (95% CI, 0.63–0.87) and 0.74 (95% CI, 0.69–0.79), respectively, and the group differences in CR remained statistically significant (p < 0.001).

Discussion

The main findings of our study are as follows: we developed and validated a simple intraoperative scoring system—based on four imaging features (tumor size, capsule presence, VLP, and MCS)—that predicts objective response after DEB‐TACE.

Assessment by mRECIST after DEB‐TACE is prognostically meaningful: ORR is an independent predictor of survival and has been proposed as a potential surrogate endpoint for overall survival [17]. Building on this, several imaging‐based models have been developed to predict OR, such as postoperative functional MRI metrics [9] and preoperative CT radiomics [10], with reported discrimination frequently exceeding an AUC of 0.9. However, these approaches generally require offline processing and therefore offer limited utility for intraprocedural decision‐making. In this study, we integrated key preoperative and intraoperative features into a point‐based model that can be applied in real time. The four‐feature score was derived and validated with a hold‐out validation set, which demonstrated strong discrimination and good calibration. A persistent challenge is that DEB‐TACE lacks a unified, objective embolization endpoint. Some centers still rely on operator experience or SACE [18], yet reader‐to‐reader variation is moderate [13]. Compounding this, DEBs are radiolucent, so SACE primarily reflects contrast kinetics rather than the actual peritumoral microsphere distribution. Efforts have explored intraprocedural CBCT/DSA markers as indicators of impending objective response. Tumor enhancement on post‐TACE CBCT was significantly associated with a 1‐month MR–based tumor response [19], and intraoperative CBCT with clear and complete margins or complete contrast staining [20] similarly predicted early mRECIST outcomes [7], supporting the feasibility of on‐table assessment. MCS has been proposed as a semiquantitative CBCT sign that can be graded on the table [6] and is presumed to better mirror peritumoral microsphere deposition, thereby directly informing intraprocedural decisions. These markers have mostly been reported in single‐center association studies and have not been integrated into a simple, predictive model nor validated in an independent cohort. The MCS also showed reliable interrater agreement in the current study. In our study, the 95% CIs for MCS grades IV and V were relatively wide, likely reflecting the limited sample size. Mechanistically, MCS likely represents coronal enhancement: once intratumoral arterial inflow is effectively occluded, contrast is retained within the capsular arteries, draining veins, or adjacent liver parenchyma [21, 22]. In other words, when the tumor has been adequately saturated with DEBs, this peritumoral retention appears on CBCT as a continuous, high‐grade peripheral rim. Notably, higher MCS grades may indicate more effective embolization of the tumor edge—the biologically active interface and a frequent locus of residual/recurrence or portal venous inflow—thereby predicting a superior objective response.

With respect to VLP, multiple studies have reported an incidence of approximately 10%–30% [8, 23, 24], with VLP associated with a higher ORR. Larger tumor diameter, the presence of a capsule, and elevated AFP are associated with a VLP. The mechanistic hypothesis of VLP is that rapid postembolization pressure increases within fragile intratumoral microvasculature, leading to focal rupture and pooling [25]. This hypothesis, however, still lacks confirmation.

In addition to these intraprocedural markers, our model also retained two more tumor‐related predictors, namely, a smaller size and the presence of a capsule, which is consistent with previous TACE literature [26]. We observed that smaller tumors were more likely to achieve OR, in line with previous reports using DEB‐TACE and cTACE [8, 27]. Although very small lesions can be technically challenging to target and may have relatively greater portal than arterial perfusion, once they are adequately catheterized and embolized, they are more likely to be completely devascularized, which may explain their higher response rates. The presence of a capsule was another favorable feature. The radiological capsule correlates with a histologic rim of prominent sinusoids and/or peritumoral fibrosis that is continuous with Glisson's capsule fibrosis [28]. Compared with nonencapsulated tumors, encapsulated tumors respond better to TACE, whereas nonencapsulated lesions are thought to be more heavily supplied by the portal vein [29]. In this context, the presence of a capsule may mark tumors with a clearer arterial–parenchymal interface and a more confined vascular territory, making them more amenable to complete embolization—especially when combined with high‐grade MCS at the rim.

Clinically, this scoring system enables real‐time stratification of tumors according to their probability of achieving objective response at the end of a DEB‐TACE session. For lesions predicted to have a low probability of response, the score may prompt the operator to reassess the adequacy of embolization, actively search for residual intrahepatic or extrahepatic tumor‐feeding arteries, and perform additional embolization when technically feasible. Conversely, a favorable score may provide reassurance that an adequate embolization endpoint has been achieved. Importantly, this intraprocedural score is not intended to replace post‐treatment imaging. Rather, it serves as a complementary decision‐support tool: during the procedure, it may inform immediate procedural optimization, while postoperatively it may help tailor surveillance intensity and guide early consideration of combination therapy. Contrast‐enhanced CT or MRI remains essential for formal response assessment and definitive treatment planning.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged when the findings are interpreted. First, it is a single‐center, retrospective analysis without external validation and is therefore prone to selection and information bias; treatment decisions, imaging protocols, and periprocedural techniques reflect local practice patterns and may limit generalizability. Second, although we implemented independent double reading with adjudication, the assessment of MCS and several ancillary imaging features remains semisubjective. Third, our model was optimized to predict short‐term radiological response (mRECIST) rather than long‐term outcomes. Therefore, late recurrence and durability of response were not assessed, and while ORR/DCR are clinically meaningful, their relationship to long‐term endpoints and post‐TACE treatment sequencing warrants further validation. Finally, clinical and laboratory variables were not included in the score because the analysis was performed at the tumor level, whereas these variables are patient‐level and thus shared across multiple lesions within the same patient. Moreover, the score was designed as a simple imaging‐only intraprocedural tool for on‐table use. Future studies using patient‐level endpoints may evaluate whether adding clinical variables to CBCT features further improves performance.

Conclusion

We developed and internally validated a simple, imaging‐only, intraoperative scoring system for predicting short‐term objective response after DEB‐TACE in patients with HCC. The score enables rapid intraoperative judgment, aiding subsequent management.

Short‐headed arrows indicate residual enhancing tumor, accounting for partial response (PR).
