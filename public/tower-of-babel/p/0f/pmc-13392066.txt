Grafted magnetite Fe3O4-VTES nanoparticles based on glycidyl methacrylate and dimethylamino ethyl methacrylate: synthesis, characterization, adsorption and release of amoxicillin
Authors: Parisa Esmatian, Saeideh Ebrahimiasl, Sudabeh Davaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, magnetic iron oxide (Fe2O4) nanoparticles with an inverse spinel structure were synthesized via the co-precipitation method under both thermal and hydrothermal conditions using Fe (II) and Fe (III) chlorides as precursors. The effects of parameters such as temperature and solvent type were investigated. Sodium hydroxide, were used as alkaline agents to adjust the pH during synthesis. To prevent nanoparticle agglomeration and improve size distribution, surface modification was performed using vinyl triethoxy silane (VTES). The morphology and structure of the synthesized nanoparticles were characterized by scanning electron microscopy, transmission electron microscopy, and X-ray diffraction. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy confirmed the functional groups, while magnetic properties were analyzed by vibrating sample magnetometry. Subsequently, radical polymerization of glycidyl methacrylate (GMA) and 2-(dimethylamino) ethyl methacrylate (DMAEMA) was carried out on the modified Fe2O4 surface to form a magnetic polymer nanocomposite. The obtained nanocomposite was employed for amoxicillin drug loading under various drug-to-carrier ratios. The results indicate that the synthesized GMA/DMAEMA magnetic nanocomposites possess excellent drug-loading capability, appropriate magnetic response, and promising potential for application as an efficient targeted drug delivery system. The results of invitro release demonstrate that Poly(DMAEMA-GMA)-VTES-coated Fe2O4 nanoparticles exhibit a biphasic release profile with an initial burst followed by sustained, diffusion-controlled amoxicillin release, highlighting their suitability for prolonged drug delivery.

Introduction

Nanotechnology has emerged as one of the most rapidly evolving fields of modern science, offering the capability to design and fabricate materials with controlled structures at the nanometer scale1. Nanomaterials have unique physicochemical, magnetic, and optical properties, which have led to extensive applications in electronics, catalysis, and biotechnology2–4. In medicine and pharmaceutics, the development of nanostructured materials has enabled advanced drug delivery systems capable of improving therapeutic efficacy while minimizing side effects5–7. Functionalized nanosheets, hybrid nanocomposites, and green-synthesized nanoparticles have shown particular potential for enhancing drug adsorption, enabling controlled release, and achieving targeted delivery8,9.

Among various nanomaterials, magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs), particularly magnetite (Fe2O4), have attracted significant attention due to their superparamagnetic behavior, biocompatibility, and high surface-to-volume ratio10–12. These properties make them ideal candidates for targeted drug delivery, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)13, and hyperthermia treatment14. However, the inherent tendency of Fe2O4 nanoparticles to aggregate due to strong magnetic dipole–dipole interactions limits their stability and biomedical applicability15. Therefore, surface modification of MNPs is essential to enhance their dispersion stability, prevent oxidation, and introduce functional groups for further conjugation with polymers or bioactive molecules16–18. In particular, polymer coatings such as polyethylene glycol (PEG), chitosan, and poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) have been reported to enhance stability, biocompatibility, and drug loading efficiency19–21.

Polymer coating of magnetic nanoparticles provides an effective strategy to improve their colloidal stability and biocompatibility while enabling the incorporation of functional monomers for drug loading and controlled release22–24. Recent studies (2020–2025) have demonstrated that functionalized Fe2O4 nanocarriers exhibit improved drug loading capacity and controlled release behavior. For instance, Fe2O4@ZIF-8 nanocomposites provide high loading efficiency and pH-responsive drug release due to their porous structure, while PEG- and PNIPAM-modified systems improve biocompatibility and introduce stimuli-responsive behavior25–27. However, most reported systems rely on single-functional polymer coatings, which often limit the tunability of drug–carrier interactions and restrict multifunctionality. Therefore, multifunctional magnetic nanocarriers with combined structural and responsive properties are still required.

In this context, glycidyl methacrylate (GMA) and 2-(dimethylamino)ethyl methacrylate (DMAEMA) are widely used monomers for constructing functional polymer networks with hydrophilic, pH-sensitive, and reactive epoxy functionalities28–31. Their copolymerization on Fe2O4 nanoparticles can produce hybrid nanocomposites with tunable drug-loading and release properties32–34. The incorporation of vinyltriethoxysilane (VTES) provides stable covalent anchoring sites on the Fe2O4 surface through Fe–O–Si bonding, while the copolymer shell introduces epoxy and tertiary amine functionalities, enabling enhanced drug–polymer interactions and tunable physicochemical properties23–26.

Unlike previously reported single-polymer systems, the proposed nanocomposite integrates multiple functional roles, including improved drug loading via electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions, pH-responsive release behavior due to DMAEMA units, and structural stability with magnetic separability.

Amoxicillin, a broad-spectrum β-lactam antibiotic, is commonly used to treat bacterial infections35. However, its short biological half-life and limited bioavailability necessitate controlled delivery systems36–38. Incorporating amoxicillin into magnetic polymer nanocomposites can enhance therapeutic performance via site-specific delivery and reduced systemic toxicity.

Based on these considerations, the present study aims to synthesize Fe2O4 nanoparticles via the co-precipitation method, followed by surface modification and fabrication of GMA/DMAEMA-based magnetic nanocomposites for controlled drug delivery8,9,21. The materials were characterized using XRD22, FTIR23, SEM, TEM24,27, VSM38, and UV–Vis spectroscopy39. Drug release behavior was evaluated under physiological conditions.

Materials and methods

Materials

Ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl₃·6H₂O), ferrous chloride tetrahydrate (FeCl₂·4H₂O), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), triethylamine (TEA), vinyltriethoxysilane (VTES), glycidyl methacrylate (GMA), 2-(dimethylamino)ethyl methacrylate (DMAEMA), ammonium persulfate (APS) as the initiator, and 1,4-dioxane as the solvent were all purchased from Merck and used without further purification40,41.Amoxicillin was obtained from a pharmaceutical supplier and used as the model drug for loading experiments. Deionized water was used throughout all synthesis procedures42.

Synthesis of Fe2O4 magnetic nanoparticles

Fe2O4 nanoparticles were synthesized using the co-precipitation method. In a typical procedure, aqueous solutions of ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl₃·6H₂O, 0.2 M) and ferrous chloride tetrahydrate (FeCl₂·4H₂O, 0.1 M) were used in a molar ratio of 2:1. Sodium hydroxide solution (1.0 M) was used as the precipitating agent to adjust the pH to 10–11 during co-precipitation under a nitrogen atmosphere with magnetic stirring at 80 °C43. The alkaline precipitating agent (NaOH) was slowly added until the pH reached 10–11, leading to the formation of a black precipitate of Fe2O4 nanoparticles. The mixture was mechanically (1000 rpm) at 80 °C stirred for 1 h, then cooled to room temperature. The obtained nanoparticles were separated using a magnet, washed several times with deionized water and ethanol to remove unreacted ions, and dried under vacuum at 60 °C44,45.

Surface modification of magnetic nanoparticles

To prevent aggregation and introduce reactive functional groups, the Fe2O4 nanoparticles were modified with silane coupling agents. In this step, 0.5 g of Fe2O4 nanoparticles was dispersed in 50 mL of ethanol–water mixture (80:20 v/v) and sonicated for 30 min46. Subsequently, 2 mL of VTES was added dropwise, and the mixture was refluxed at 80 °C for 4 h under constant stirring. The resulting silane-modified Fe2O4 nanoparticles were magnetically separated, washed with ethanol, and dried under vacuum. These modified nanoparticles provided vinyl functional groups for subsequent polymer grafting47.

Synthesis of magnetic polymer nanocomposite

The polymerization of GMA and DMAEMA monomers on the surface of modified Fe2O4 nanoparticles was performed via radical in-situ polymerization. In a typical synthesis, 0.3 g of silane-modified Fe2O4 was dispersed in 30 mL of 1,4-dioxane and sonicated for 20 min. Then, predetermined amounts of GMA and DMAEMA monomers were added along with 0.05 g of APS as the initiator. The reaction mixture was purged with nitrogen to remove oxygen and heated to 70 °C for 4 h under continuous stirring. The resulting magnetic nanocomposite was collected by magnetic separation, repeatedly washed with ethanol and water, and dried at 60 °C under vacuum48,49.

Drug loading and release with amoxicillin

Drug loading

Amoxicillin was loaded onto Poly (DMAEMA-GMA)-VTES-coated Fe3O4 nanoparticles by dispersing 100 mg of the synthesized nanocomposite in 50 mL of an aqueous amoxicillin solution at different initial concentrations (0.5–2 mg/mL). The suspension was stirred at room temperature for 24 h to allow adsorption and entrapment of the drug. After incubation, the amoxicillin-loaded nanoparticles were magnetically separated, washed with distilled water to remove unbound drug, and dried at ambient temperature. The concentration of unbound amoxicillin in the supernatant was determined spectrophotometrically at 272 nm50.

Drug release

The in vitro release of amoxicillin from the loaded nanoparticles was studied in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) at 37 °C. A known amount of drug-loaded nanoparticles was dispersed in 50 mL of release medium and placed in a shaker at 100 rpm. At predetermined time intervals, 2 mL aliquots were withdrawn, and an equal volume of fresh PBS was added to maintain constant volume. The concentration of released amoxicillin was measured spectrophotometrically at 272 nm51,52. All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and results are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was evaluated where applicable.

Results and discussion

Structural formation and physicochemical properties of Fe2O4 nanoparticles

Magnetite nanoparticles were synthesized through a controlled co-precipitation route, where maintaining an optimal Fe2⁺/Fe3⁺ ratio and an oxygen-free alkaline medium proved essential for stable nucleation. Variations in pH affected both surface hydroxylation and particle charge, thereby influencing nucleation kinetics53.

Figure 1 Schematic illustration of the pH-dependent surface speciation of magnetite nanoparticles during co-precipitation synthesis. At acidic pH (pH < 7), extensive protonation of surface hydroxyl (≡Fe–OH) groups results in a positively charged surface dominated by ≡Fe–OH₂⁺ species. Near neutral pH, the surface approaches its point of zero charge, where protonated and deprotonated sites coexist. Under alkaline conditions (pH > 7), deprotonation of surface hydroxyl groups leads to the formation of negatively charged ≡Fe–O⁻ species. These pH-induced variations in surface charge and hydroxylation significantly influence electrostatic interactions, nucleation kinetics, colloidal stability, and growth mechanisms of magnetite nanoparticles.Fig. 1Effect of pH on Fe3O4 magnetite nanoparticles surface ion.

Effect of pH on Fe3O4 magnetite nanoparticles surface ion.

Magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles characteristics

FT-IR Spectroscopy

The FT-IR spectrum of Sample 1 displays several characteristic absorption bands, reflecting both the metal–oxygen lattice vibrations and surface functional groups. A strong band below 800 cm−1 is observed, with distinct peaks at 440, 580, and 620 cm−1, which are attributed to Fe–O stretching vibrations within the magnetite lattice. The band at 580 cm−1 is particularly assigned to the Fe–O–Fe stretching, indicative of the octahedral and tetrahedral coordination in Fe2O454.

A broad band centered at 3402 cm−1 corresponds to the –OH stretching vibrations of adsorbed water and surface hydroxyl groups, suggesting the presence of hydrogen-bonded –OH functionalities on the nanoparticle surface. Additionally, weaker bands observed around 1630–1650 cm−1 can be attributed to H–O–H bending vibrations of physisorbed water molecules. Minor peaks in the 1000–1200 cm−1 region may indicate Fe–OH bending or surface hydroxylation effects, which are consistent with pH-dependent surface charge modifications. Overall, the FT-IR spectrum confirms the formation of magnetite nanoparticles with surface hydroxyl groups, which play a crucial role in colloidal stability and surface reactivity (Fig. 2).Fig. 2FT-IR spectrum of magnetite Fe3O4 nanoparticles.

FT-IR spectrum of magnetite Fe3O4 nanoparticles.

Crystalline phase analysis

The crystalline structure of the synthesized magnetite nanoparticles was systematically investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) over a 2θ range of 20–100°. The diffraction pattern unequivocally confirms the formation of a single-phase Fe2O4 with an inverse spinel structure. The presence of sharp and well-defined diffraction peaks indicates the high crystallinity of the nanoparticles. As depicted in Fig. 3, the characteristic reflections corresponding to the (220), (311), (400), (422), (511), and (440) crystallographic planes are clearly observed, in excellent agreement with standard reference data for magnetite. Notably, no additional peaks attributable to secondary phases such as Fe (OH)₃ or Fe₂O₃ are detected, confirming the phase purity of the sample55.Fig. 3XRD spectrum of synthesized magnetite Fe3O4 nanoparticles.

XRD spectrum of synthesized magnetite Fe3O4 nanoparticles.

Among the reflections, the (311) plane exhibits the highest intensity and was employed to estimate the average crystallite size. Using the Scherrer equation, the crystallite size of the Fe2O4 nanoparticles was calculated to be approximately 15.15 nm, confirming their nanoscale dimensions and the effectiveness of the controlled co-precipitation synthesis method57–59.

Magnetic performance

The magnetic hysteresis loop of Fe2O4 nanoparticles recorded at 25 °C is shown in Fig. 4. The synthesized nanoparticles exhibit strong magnetic behavior with a fully reversible magnetization process. A saturation magnetization (Ms) of 68 emu/g is obtained under an applied magnetic field of 10,000 G, while the magnetization drops to nearly zero after removal of the field, indicating negligible remanent magnetization. The coercivity value is approximately 20 Oe, which, together with the low remanence, confirms the superparamagnetic nature of the Fe2O4 nanoparticles. This behavior is consistent with the nanoscale particle size (~ 15.15 nm) calculated from XRD data56. This reversible magnetic behavior is crucial for applications where rapid magnetic separation and zero residual magnetism are required.Fig. 4Magnetic remanence diagram of synthesized Fe3O4 magnetite nanoparticles.

Magnetic remanence diagram of synthesized Fe3O4 magnetite nanoparticles.

Morphology and particle size distribution

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and point-by-point analysis of the nanoparticles were performed to investigate the particle size and morphology using an electron microscope operated at an accelerating voltage below 30 kV under high-vacuum conditions (10⁻5 Torr) (Fig. 5). As can be seen from Fig. 5, the nanoparticles are uniformly distributed and exhibit a homogeneous plate-like morphology. The well-defined shapes and narrow size distribution indicate a successful synthesis process, consistent with the particle size range obtained from SEM and XRD analyses57. After careful examination of different regions of the sample, the size of the magnetic Fe2O4 nanoparticles was estimated to be in the approximate range of 8–40 nm. The particle sizes obtained from SEM are in good agreement with the XRD results and the crystallite size calculated using the Scherrer equation. Modification of Fe3O4 with VTES for polymerization with GMA and DMAEMA

Fe2O4 nanoparticles were initially hydroxylated to generate surface –OH groups, enabling subsequent silanization. Surface modification was performed using vinyltriethoxysilane (VTES) in an aqueous ammonia medium. In this process, hydrolysis of ethoxy groups followed by condensation with surface hydroxyl groups led to the formation of stable Fe–O–Si bonds, resulting in the covalent immobilization of vinyl functionalities on the Fe2O4 surface (VTES–Fe2O4).

The vinyl-functionalized nanoparticles were subsequently employed as surface-bound reactive sites for surface-initiated free-radical copolymerization of glycidyl methacrylate (GMA) and 2-(dimethylamino) ethyl methacrylate (DMAEMA), using ammonium persulfate (APS) as the radical initiator at 70 °C. The grafting-from process enabled the in-situ growth of poly (GMA-co-DMAEMA) chains directly from the nanoparticle surface, yielding a covalently anchored polymer shell60.

The resulting nanostructure incorporates epoxy functionalities from GMA and tertiary amine groups from DMAEMA, providing dual chemical reactivity. This surface-initiated polymerization approach effectively suppresses homopolymer formation in the bulk phase, as confirmed by the efficient removal of non-grafted species via magnetic separation and centrifugation61.

Overall, a well-defined VTES–Fe2O4–g–poly (GMA-co-DMAEMA) nanostructure was obtained, featuring a uniformly grafted and robust polymer layer strongly anchored to the magnetic core, thereby enhancing surface functionality and expanding its applicability in advanced nanomaterials and catalytic systems (Fig. 7).Fig. 7How GMA and DMAEMA polymers are attached to the VTES-Fe3O4 surface.

How GMA and DMAEMA polymers are attached to the VTES-Fe3O4 surface.

In the next step, surface-initiated free-radical copolymerization of glycidyl methacrylate (GMA) and 2-(dimethylamino) ethyl methacrylate (DMAEMA) was performed using ammonium persulfate (APS) as the radical initiator at 70 °C. This grafting process led to the formation of a functional polymer shell containing epoxy and tertiary amine moieties anchored onto the nanoparticle surface. The resulting polymer-grafted magnetic nanoparticles exhibit enhanced surface functionality, making them suitable for further chemical modification and advanced nanochemical applications.

Modified and grafted Fe3O4 nanoparticles characteristics

FT-IR spectroscopy

FT-IR spectroscopy was used to monitor and confirm the stepwise surface modification of Fe2O4 nanoparticles. The spectrum of VTES-functionalized Fe2O4 (Fig. 8b) shows a broad absorption band at around 3440 cm−1, which can be attributed to O–H and N–H/NH₂ stretching vibrations. The presence of a band at approximately 1603 cm−1 is assigned to C–N stretching vibrations, while the peaks observed at 1410 and 1276 cm−1 correspond to CH₂ bending modes. A strong absorption band at 1041 cm−1, characteristic of Si–O–Si and Si–O–Fe stretching vibrations, confirms the successful silanization and covalent attachment of VTES onto the Fe2O4 surface. In addition, the absorption bands at 603 and 443 cm−1 are associated with Fe–O vibrations of the magnetite core. Following polymer grafting, the FT-IR spectrum of Poly-(DMAEMA-co-GMA)-VTES-Fe2O4 (Fig. 8a) exhibits several new characteristic bands, indicating the formation of a polymer layer on the nanoparticle surface. The bands at 2922 and 2850 cm−1 are attributed to aliphatic C–H stretching vibrations of CH, CH₂, and CH₃ groups. The appearance of a pronounced absorption band at approximately 1650 cm−1 corresponds to the ester C = O stretching vibration originating from DMAEMA and GMA units. Additional bands at 1141 and 1048 cm−1 are assigned to C–N, Si–C, and Si–O stretching vibrations. Notably, the disappearance of characteristic vinyl C = C and –CH₂ = absorption bands confirms that free-radical polymerization proceeded from the surface-bound vinyl groups of VTES. These spectral changes clearly demonstrate the successful grafting of DMAEMA/GMA copolymer chains onto the Fe2O4 nanoparticle62.Fig. 8FT-IR spectrum (a) surface-modified Fe3O4- VTES nanoparticles, (b) Fe3O4- VTES nanoparticles.

FT-IR spectrum (a) surface-modified Fe3O4- VTES nanoparticles, (b) Fe3O4- VTES nanoparticles.

In vitro drug release of amoxicillin

To further understand the release mechanism, the experimental data were fitted to zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer–Peppas kinetic models. The best fit was observed for the Higuchi model, suggesting that diffusion through the polymer matrix is the dominant release mechanism. The in vitro release profile of amoxicillin from Poly (DMAEMA-GMA)-VTES-coated Fe3O4 nanoparticles exhibited a biphasic pattern (Fig. 10). An initial burst release of approximately 25% was observed within the first 3 h, attributed to surface-adsorbed drug. This was followed by a sustained release reaching 75–80% over 48–72 h, indicating diffusion-controlled release through the polymeric matrix67.

Similarly, polymer-coated Fe2O4 systems such as PEG- or PNIPAM-based nanocomposites provide enhanced biocompatibility and stimuli-responsive drug release profiles71. Compared with these systems, the present work introduces a dual-functional poly(GMA-co-DMAEMA) coating anchored via VTES, which simultaneously enhances chemical reactivity, drug–polymer interactions, and sustained release performance. Therefore, the proposed nanocarrier demonstrates competitive or improved performance relative to recently reported Fe2O4-based drug delivery systems.

Conclusion

The synthesis strategy employed in this work successfully produced magnetite nanoparticles with well-defined structural, magnetic and morphological characteristics suited for subsequent surface engineering. The co-precipitation route yielded phase-pure Fe2O4 with nanoscale crystallite dimensions and strong superparamagnetic behavior, while also providing a hydroxyl-rich surface that served as an effective platform for chemical functionalization. Systematic characterization confirmed that the pristine nanoparticles possessed the necessary structural integrity, dispersibility and responsiveness required for biomedical modification.

Surface engineering through sequential salinization and polymer grafting resulted in controlled growth of an organic shell without altering the crystalline phase of the magnetic core. Incorporation of VTES introduced reactive silane functionalities, followed by successful graft polymerization of DMAEMA and GMA, forming a stable polymeric matrix around the nanoparticles. Each modification step progressively reduced saturation magnetization, consistent with increased organic content, while preserving superparamagnetic behavior critical for magnetic manipulation and may be potentially applicable for magnetically assisted drug delivery systems; however, in vivo targeting was not experimentally evaluated in this study. Morphological analyses confirmed improved particle dispersion and enlargement consistent with shell formation.

The final polymer-coated magnetic nanocomposite demonstrated efficient loading of amoxicillin, with loading capacity strongly influenced by the polymer-to-drug ratio. FT-IR evidence confirmed the entrapment of the drug within the hybrid structure, and magnetic measurements showed that superparamagnetic behavior was maintained after encapsulation. Taken together, the results indicate that the engineered nanocomposite integrates structural stability, functional surface chemistry, magnetic responsiveness and drug-loading capability, making it a promising candidate for controlled drug delivery and other biomedical applications.

The biocompatibility and potential cytotoxicity of polymer-grafted Fe2O4 nanoparticles remain important considerations. Although Fe2O4 is generally regarded as biocompatible, surface functionalization may influence cellular response. Therefore, further in vitro cytotoxicity and hemocompatibility studies are recommended for biomedical translation.
