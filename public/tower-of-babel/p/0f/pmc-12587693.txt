Ascorbic acid attenuates immunosenescence and cognitive decline via MYH9-Mediated CD8⁺ T cell differentiation
Authors: Taotao Mi, Shanshan Yang, Nan Wang, Fengjiao Huo, Meili Zhao, Shuyao Lv, Tingting Su, Shengyu Feng, Hao Wang, Liuling Guo, Jian-Kang Zhu, Hailiang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground​​ Immune function decline (immunosenescence) accelerates systemic aging and adversely impacts cognitive function. Antioxidants may mitigate these effects; however, the role of ascorbic acid (AA), a key antioxidant, in counteracting immunosenescence and enhancing cognition remains inadequately explored.ResultsIn this study, AA administration (0.1 mg/g, tail vein, every 2 days for 30 days) significantly improved cognitive function in aged (16-month) C57BL/6 mice, without altering anxiety-like behavior (as assessed in the open field test). This was associated with elevated peripheral blood lymphocytes (T cells, B cells) and CD8⁺ T cells, alongside reduced myeloid cells (CD11b⁺). Single-cell RNA sequencing of PBMCs revealed AA reversed immunosenescent signatures—increasing T/B cell populations and decreasing neutrophils/macrophages—mimicking youthful immune profiles. In vitro, AA skewed hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) differentiation toward CD8⁺ T cells (increasing DN2 stage, suppressing myeloid CD11b⁺ cells) and enhanced splenic CD8⁺ T cell generation. Mechanistically, AA bound MYH9, activating cytoskeletal pathways. MYH9 inhibition (blebbistatin) reduced CD8⁺ T cells and increased CD11b⁺ cells—effects rescued by AA. Crucially, CD8⁺ T cell depletion abolished AA’s cognitive benefits, confirming their essential role.ConclusionsIn summary, AA mitigates immunosenescence and improves cognitive function by targeting MYH9 to regulate CD8⁺ T cell differentiation and function. These findings establish a mechanistic basis for AA as a potential therapeutic agent against age-related immune and cognitive decline.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12979-025-00538-4.Keywords: Ascorbic acid, Cognitive functions, Immunosenescence, CD8+ t cells

Background

With the accelerating global aging population, deciphering the biological mechanisms of senescence and their health implications has become a critical scientific imperative [1]. Aging drives immunosenescence—and concurrently contributes to cognitive deterioration [2, 3]. Projections indicate that dementia will affect approximately 153 million people worldwide by 2040 [4]. The profound impact of cognitive decline on independence and quality of life in the elderly, coupled with the current lack of disease-modifying therapies, underscores the urgency of exploring ​nutritional interventions​ to mitigate or reverse such decline [5–13].

AA, recognized for its ​potent antioxidant and immunomodulatory properties, plays a pivotal role in neuroprotection and cognitive regulation [14]. Studies confirm that AA influences the nervous system through multiple mechanisms: (i) Promoting neuronal differentiation, growth, and myelination [15]. (ii) Modulating synaptic transmission—inhibiting neurotransmitter-receptor interactions [16] and regulating neurotransmitter release/uptake [17]. (iii) Serving as an essential cofactor for neurotransmitter synthesis [18]. Furthermore, AA is a significant immunomodulator: it enhances immune cell activity [19, 20], ​​while immunosenescence acts as a core driver of systemic aging, directly threatening host health​​ [21]. Notably, moderate AA supplementation alleviates immunosenescence and improves immune function in the elderly​​ [22, 23], for instance, enhancing memory T cell function in aged mice​​ [24].

Although preliminary evidence suggests dual protective effects of AA on immunity and cognition, its mechanistic actions in the context of aging require deeper investigation​. ​​This study focuses on the impact of AA on immunosenescence and cognitive function in middle-aged mice, specifically examining its regulation of T cell differentiation and functionality​. Results demonstrate that ​​AA ameliorates features of immunosenescence and enhances cognitive performance by restoring CD8⁺T cell differentiation and function​. ​​These findings provide a foundational theoretical basis for designing nutritional strategies to bolster immune health and cognitive function in the aging population​.

Results

AA contributes to better cognitive function in aged mice and elevates the proportion of CD8+ T cells

To verify the effect of AA on the cognitive function of aged mice (C57BL/6), we set up a 16 M AA group (tail vein injection of 0.1 mg/g AA, every 2 days for 30 days) and a 16 M control group (tail vein injection of of PBS). Their cognitive functions were detected through behavioral tests. The open field test is a classic behavioral experiment used to evaluate the spontaneous activity and anxiety state of mice. As shown in Fig. 1A, assessment of locomotor activity in the open field test indicated no significant differences between the AA-treated group and the control group (Ctrl) regarding time spent in the center, total distance traveled, or average velocity within the central zone (all P > 0.05). These results suggest that AA treatment did not induce anxiety-like behaviors or alter general locomotor activity in the mice. The new object recognition experiment assesses the cognitive memory ability of mice through the behavioral method of the duration of exploration for familiar objects and new objects by mice. This is quantified by the cognitive index. According to Fig. 1B, the AA mice exhibited significantly better cognitive ability than the Ctrl mice (P < 0.01). The observed cognitive enhancement following intravenous AA administration is associated with an elevated ratio of immune T cells. And the AA group demonstrated a notable elevation in peripheral blood T cells, CD8+ T cells, and B cells, along with a significant reduction in myeloid cell numbers (CD11b+), while no significant changes were observed in CD4+ T cells (Fig. 1C-E). This suggests that the improvement in the cognitive function of mice is related to the proportion of immune cells. Subsequently, we conducted an analysis of gene expression in the hippocampus of aged mice (n = 2) that were administered intravenous AA or PBS using RNA-seq detection (Fig. S1). The findings from the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) indicated that AA had a significant impact on pathways associated with longevity regulating pathway (Fig. S1B), neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction (Fig. S1C), as well as various pathways related to synaptic function (Fig. S1D).

AA mitigates age-related decline in lymphocytes and increases in myeloid cells

In order to examine the impact of AA on the immune system, particularly in relation to immunosenescence during aging, single-cell sequencing was conducted on PBMCs obtained from young mice (8 weeks old, tail vein injection of PBS, n = 3), aged mice (16 months old, tail vein injection of PBS, n = 3), and aged C57BL/6 mice administered with AA (tail vein injection of 0.1 mg/g AA, every 2 days for 30 days, n = 3). The sequencing data was analyzed using CellRanger software. Dimensionality reduction through the application of Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) facilitated the identification of diverse immune cell lineages and subgroup classifications based on canonical marker gene expression.

Clustering analysis of PBMCs from aged mice and those treated with AA revealed that AA treatment resulted in an increase in the abundance of T cells (CD8b1, Igfbp4, Ctla4, Ccl5), and B cells (H2-Eb1), while the populations of Neutrophils (Il1b, Ngp), Macrophages (Cst3, Lyz2), and Erythroid cells (Hbb-bt, Hba-a1) decreased (Fig. 2A-D). Concurrently, we analyzed PBMC single-cell sequencing data from young and aged mice to investigate the potential correlation between alterations in immune cell populations induced by AA treatment in aged mice and the process of rejuvenation. The results demonstrated a decrease in the proportions of T cell (CD8b1, Igfbp4) types, coupled with an increase in Neutrophil (S100a8), Macrophage (Cst3, Lyz2, Ifit3), and Erythroid cells (Hba-a1, Hbb-bt) in aged mice (Fig. 2E, F). The findings strongly indicate that AA mitigates the rise in myeloid cells induced by senescence, while also enhancing the levels of lymphocytopenia cells during senescence.

By using the pseudo-time analysis of Monocle, we were able to map the developmental trajectory of PBMC cells and evaluate the expression of marker genes, revealing the key points that distinguish five different states during cell differentiation. As shown in Fig. S2A-C, there are two major branch nodes, with erythroid cells at the beginning of the pseudo-temporal sequence, B cells at the end, and T cells, macrophages, and neutrophils in the intermediate states. Branch 1 encompasses state 1 (pre-branch, consisting of erythroid cells with marker genes Hba-a1, Hba-a2, Hba-bt, and Hba-bs), states 3 and 4 (Cell fate 1, including Macrophage cells, and Neutrophils with marker genes S100a8 and S100a9), and state 5 (Cell fate 2, comprised of B cells with marker genes Igha and Lyz2) (Fig. S2D, E). Branch 2 includes state 1 (pre-branch with marker genes Il18r1 and Rgs1), state 2 (Cell fate 1, T cells with marker genes Bcl2, Arl4c, and Cd28), and states 3, 4, and 5 (Cell fate 2 with marker genes Inpp5d and Cd55) (Fig. S3). The KEGG pathway analysis of T cell (Ctla4, cluster 3; Igfbp4, cluster 8; Ccl5, cluster 11; CD8b1, cluster 13) and B cell (H2-Eb1, cluster 1) subgroups identified key pathways such as T cell receptor signaling and hematopoietic cell lineage. Additionally, Neutrophil (IL-1b, cluster 7; Ngp, cluster 10) and Macrophage (Lyz2, cluster 2; Cst3, cluster 9 pathways were found to involve IL-17, TNF signaling, and osteoclast differentiation (Fig. S4). Overall, the results of single-cell sequencing underscore the significant impact of AA in reversing immunosenescence.

AA enhances the in vitro differentiation of HSCs into lymphocytes, with a particular emphasis on CD8+ T cells

Given the observed promotion of lymphoid cell proliferation in vivo by AA, an in vitro differentiation system utilizing Lin−CD117+ HSCs from 8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice co-cultured with OP9-DL1 cells over a 12-day period was employed to assess the impact of AA on lymphocytes. The experiment included a control (Ctrl) group and an AA-treated group. The differentiation stages of T cells were identified through the assessment of CD44 and CD25 expression levels across different T cell phases (Fig. 3A, B). In comparison to the control group, the group treated with AA showed a significant increase in DN2 (CD44+ CD25+) stage cells and a notable decrease in DN3 (CD44− CD25+) stage cells (P < 0.01), while no significant changes were observed in DN1 (CD44+ CD25−) and DN4 (CD44− CD25−) stages (Fig. 3C). Additionally, there was a significant reduction in the proportion of myeloid CD11b+ cells (Fig. 3D). These findings suggest that AA inhibits the differentiation of hematopoietic stem cells into myeloid cells. Moreover, following a 28-day co-culture with OP9-DL1 cells, a model illustrating T cell maturation differentiation was established (Fig. 3A) and divided into Ctrl and AA-treated groups. The AA group exhibited a notable increase in the CD8+ T cell ratio compared to the Ctrl group (Fig. 3E, F), while there was no significant change in CD4+ T cell ratios (Fig. 3E, G). These results suggest that AA skews hematopoietic stem cell differentiation towards CD8+ T cells during T cell maturation and inhibits differentiation into myeloid CD11b+ cells. In summary, AA promotes the differentiation of HSCs into CD8+ T cells. Concurrently, the in vitro application of AA to spleen monocytes led to a notable increase in the CD8+ T cell population and a reduction in the number of B cells (Fig. S5).

Myh9 protein binding to AA promotes CD8+ T cell differentiation

In order to identify the specific proteins targeted by AA for modulation of immune cells, a MetPro metabolic protein interaction study was conducted (Fig. 4A). Utilizing nanoLC-MS/MS analysis of purified PBMC proteins, it was determined that 18 proteins exhibited significant upregulation, while 25 proteins showed significant downregulation in response to AA treatment (Fig. S6A). Further analysis using COG/KOG classification demonstrated that the upregulated proteins were primarily associated with post-translational modifications, protein turnover, chaperones, signal transduction, and cytoskeletal function (Fig. S6B).

The KEGG pathway analysis revealed that AA primarily enriched pathways related to the regulation of actin cytoskeleton (Fig. 4B). Among the proteins that potentially bind with AA, both Rhoa and Myh9, which are cytoskeletal proteins, showed significant increases (Fig. 4C). Additionally, Myh9 and Rhoa exhibited extensive interactions (Fig. 4D, E). Ion resonance measurements indicated that AA had an affinity of 7.16 mM with Rhoa and 628 µM with Myh9, suggesting a certain degree of affinity between AA and Myh9 (Fig. 4F). Though the affinity is not strong, to evaluate Myh9’s effect on CD8 + T cell proliferation, mouse splenocytes were treated with the Myh9 inhibitor blebbistatin at a concentration of 10 µM. Flow cytometric analysis was utilized to examine the proportions of CD8+ T cells and CD11b+ cells, revealing a statistically significant decrease in the CD8+ T cell ratio and a marked increase in the CD11b+ cell ratio. Furthermore, the blebbistatin + AA group exhibited a significant elevation in the CD8+ T cell ratio and a reduction in CD11b+ cells compared to the blebbistatin group (Fig. 4G-J).

The findings indicate that the suppression of Myh9 in spleen cells hinders the ratio of CD8+ T cells while increasing the proportions of myeloid CD11b+ cells. This inhibitory impact is alleviated by AA (Fig. 4G-J). The results suggest that Myh9 facilitates the expansion of CD8+ T cells, and AA modulates CD8+ T cells by interacting with Myh9.

Depleting CD8+ T cells compromises mice’s cognitive functions

Our results above showed that AA can promote the proportion of CD8+ T cells by binding to Myh9 protein, and that CD8 + T cells is correlated with cognition function. Therefore, 8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were treated with Rat IgG2b isotype as a control and anti-mouse CD8α antibody (aiming for a depletion of CD8+ T cells in peripheral system). The group receiving the anti-mouse CD8α antibody exhibited a significant decrease in peripheral CD8+ T cell levels compared to the control group, while levels of CD4+ T cells and NK cells remained unchanged (Fig. 5A-F). Behavioral assessments showed no significant differences in the duration spent in the central area between mice treated with anti-CD8α and those treated with IgG2b isotype (Fig. 5G, H). However, a notable decline in cognitive abilities was observed in the anti-CD8α treated mice (Fig. 5I). This indicates that the proportion of CD8+ T cells influences cognitive abilities in mice, suggesting that increasing CD8+ T cell counts could potentially elevate their cognitive levels.

Materials and methods

Mice

Female C57BL/6 mice at two age stages (8-week-old young adults and 8-month-old middle-aged adults) were sourced from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The middle-aged cohort was aged to target maturity at the Experimental Animal Center. All mice were housed in the SPF facility of Tongji University under controlled conditions: 12-hour light-dark cycle, ambient temperature 22 ± 1 °C, humidity 50 ± 10%, with ad libitum access to irradiated standard chow and autoclaved water. ​Enrichment devices​ (nesting materials, running wheels) were provided to promote species-typical behaviors.

Drug treatment

(+)-Sodium L-ascorbate (Sigma) was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.5 and administered to mice intravenously (i.v.) at a concentration of 0.1 mg/g every other day for one month. This dosage and frequency were converted from the intraperitoneal (i.p.) regimen reported by May et al. [25], with the intravenous dose adjusted based on standard pharmacokinetic principles to account for differences in bioavailability. Blebbistatin (MedChemExpress) was initially dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) from Sigma before being diluted with PBS at a pH of 7.5 for intravenous injection (i.v.) in C57BL/6 mice at a dosage of 0.1 mg/g every other day, and continued for one month. The Rat IgG2b isotype and Anti-mouse CD8α-InVivo antibodies (Selleckchem) were each diluted to a final volume of 1 ml using sterile PBS and injected intraperitoneally at a dosage of 200 µg per mouse.

The experimental mouse cohorts were categorized into various groups based on the route of administration and treatment conditions. Specifically, intravenous injection groups included 16-month (16 M) controls and 16 M AA-treated mice, as well as 8-week (8 W) controls. Another set of groups consisted of 8 W controls, 8 W AA, 8 W blebbistatin, and 8 W blebbistatin + AA. Lastly, 8 W Rat IgG2b isotype and 8 W Anti-mouse CD8α -InVivo antibody groups were also established.

Subsequently, mouse samples were gathered for analytical procedures including single-cell sequencing, RNA sequencing, and flow cytometry.

10 × Single-cell RNA sequencing

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from C57BL/6 mice (n = 3 per group: young, aged, and aged treated with AA) by collecting 1 ml of peripheral blood in EDTA-coated tubes, diluting it in an equal volume of RPMI 1640 medium, and layering it over lymphocyte separation medium in centrifuge tubes. After centrifugation at 400 g for 25 min, the intermediate white layer containing PBMCs was collected, centrifuged at 500 g for 10 min, and half of the supernatant was discarded. The cells were subsequently rinsed with 2 ml of RPMI 1640 medium and subjected to centrifugation at 500 g for 5 min. The resulting supernatant was removed, and the cell pellet was reconstituted in 500 µL of fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco). Cell viability was assessed by staining with 0.4% trypan blue (Merck Millipore) and examining under a microscope, with only cells demonstrating over 80% viability being utilized for library preparation.

The single-cell libraries were prepared utilizing the Chromium™ Controller and the Chromium™ Single Cell 3ʹ Reagent Kits v3 (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA). Single cells, reagents, and gel beads containing 10X Barcodes were enclosed within Gel Bead-In-EMulsions (GEMs), facilitating single-cell lysis and reverse transcription. Subsequent to GEM disruption, cDNA underwent amplification through PCR, with subsequent size selection (200–300 bp). The fragments were subjected to end-repair, A-tailing at the 3’ ends, and adapter ligation.

The adapter-ligated fragments underwent purification utilizing double-sided Solid Phase Reversible Immobilization (SPRI) beads. Following sample index PCR, a subsequent round of SPRI purification was carried out. The fragment distribution of the prepared library was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA), and its concentration was quantified via real-time quantitative PCR employing TaqMan probes. Subsequent sequencing was conducted on an Illumina platform.

Analysis of single-cell transcriptomics data

The analysis of raw sequencing data involves the utilization of the Cell Ranger Single Cell Software Suite (version 5.0.0) from the 10x Genomics website for the generation of a gene expression matrix. Subsequent downstream analysis is conducted using the R package Seurat (version 3.1.0) [26]. Standardization of the gene expression dataset includes the exclusion of cells with fewer than 200 genes or exceeding 90% of the highest gene count. Furthermore, cells are sorted based on mitochondrial read percentages in descending order, with the top 15% being excluded from the analysis. Gene expression values are normalized based on library size, log-transformed, and only genes with significant variance are selected for analysis. Principal component analysis is used for dimensionality reduction, followed by clustering using graph-based methods in the PCA space. UMAP is used to visualize these clusters in two dimensions. The FindConservedMarkers function within the Seurat software is utilized to identify marker genes for individual clusters based on specified criteria (logfc.threshold > 0.25 and minPct > 0.25). These clusters are then annotated with known cell types sourced from a cell marker database [27]. Additionally, the same Seurat function is employed to detect genes that exhibit differential expression in the samples, following specific criteria (logfc.threshold > 0.25, minPct > 0.25, and Padj ≤ 0.05). Next, pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed using the R package Monocle2 [28].

Isolation and purification of Lin−CD117+ HSCs

C57BL/6 mice are euthanized by cervical dislocation and then sterilized by immersion in 75% ethanol for a duration of 60 s. Subsequently, the femurs and tibias are extracted and placed in a dish containing pre-chilled PBS for purification. Any adherent muscle and connective tissues are excised from the bones, which are then submerged in pre-chilled PBS. The ends of the tibias are excised, and the marrow is flushed out using a 1 mL syringe until the bones appear white. Gentle pipetting is employed to disperse the bone marrow cells.

The resulting single-cell suspension in PBS is filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer to obtain bone marrow cells. The collected cells are centrifuged at 440×g for 5 min to remove the supernatant. Subsequently, the cells are resuspended in 5 mL of red blood cell lysis buffer (BD Biosciences) for 1–2 min, followed by the addition of 5 mL of precooled PBS to halt the lysis process. After centrifugation at 440×g for 5 min and discarding the supernatant, the cells are washed in 5 mL of precooled PBS and filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer.

A 10 µL aliquot of the cell filtrate is utilized for cell counting, while the remaining filtrate is subjected to centrifugation at 440×g for 5 min. Subsequently, the supernatant is discarded, and the cells are reconstituted in precooled PBS to achieve a concentration of 2.5 × 10^8/mL. Utilizing the Mouse Lineage Cell Depletion Kit (Miltenyi Biotech) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines, lineage cells are depleted through negative selection, followed by positive selection for CD117+ cells in order to isolate the desired Lin−CD117+ HSCs.

OP9-DL1 co-culture system

As previously demonstrated, co-cultivation utilizes OP9-DL1 cells to direct Lin−CD117+ HSCs towards T cell differentiation [29, 30]. Prior to initiating differentiation, OP9-DL1 cells in their logarithmic growth phase are seeded at a density of 3 × 10^4 cells per well in 12-well plates. Upon reaching approximately 80% confluence, 10^5 Lin−CD117+ HSCs are introduced into each well. The co-culture of Lin−CD117+ HSCs with OP9-DL1 cells occurs in alpha-minimum essential medium (Gibco) supplemented with 20% FBS, 5% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) (Gibco), and 5ng/mL of both recombinant mouse IL-7 (Peprotech) and rmFlt3-L (Peprotech). The process of inducing differentiation into T cells is evaluated at 12 and 28 days in control groups (without AA) and ascorbic acid-treated groups (at a concentration of 50 µM), with each group having n = 3 replicates.

Cell preparation and flow cytometry analysis

In the process of preparing mouse PBMCs for flow cytometry analysis, C57BL/6 mice are initially anesthetized and blood is collected via orbital bleeding into EDTA-containing tubes. Following this, 100 µl of blood is transferred into a 2 mL centrifuge tube and mixed with fluorescently labeled antibodies. The resulting mixture is then incubated at 4 °C in darkness for 20–25 min for staining. Subsequently, a three-fold volume of red blood cell lysis buffer is added, thoroughly mixed, and incubated in darkness at 4 °C for 30 min for cell lysis. Following centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min, the supernatant is decanted. Subsequently, the cells undergo two washes with 1 mL of PBS, followed by centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min and removal of the supernatant. The resulting pellet is then resuspended in 300 µl of PBS and filtered through a 200-mesh cell strainer.

To isolate spleen cells from mice for flow cytometry analysis, the spleen is collected post-euthanasia. The spleen is moistened with PBS, washed twice, and placed on a 70 μm cell strainer that has been pre-moistened with PBS, where it is then minced. A cell grinding rod was utilized to homogenize the spleen, followed by multiple rinses of PBS to isolate spleen cells. The resulting cell suspension underwent centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min, with subsequent removal of the supernatant and addition of 4 mL of red blood cell lysis buffer for 2–3 min. The reaction was terminated by the addition of 10 mL of PBS, followed by another round of centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min. And resuspend the cells in the culture medium. Spleen cells were then plated onto 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well and exposed to a specific concentration of AA (50 µM) for 24 h, with the control group receiving an equivalent volume of PBS. The obtained cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min, then the supernatant was removed and the cells were resuspended with 100 µl.

During the preparation of samples following co-culture of HSC cells with OP9-DL1 cells for flow cytometry analysis, it is observed that OP9-DL1, a stromal cell line originating from bone marrow, expresses Notch ligand Delta-like 1 to support the ex vivo differentiation and maturation of T cells. This differentiation process involves progression through distinct stages characterized by the expression of CD44 and CD25, resulting in the generation of CD44−CD25− (double negative) cells, transitioning to CD4+CD8+ (double positive) cells, and ultimately leading to the formation of CD4+/CD8+ single positive cells. Following the completion of co-culture, cells are subjected to centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min, after which the supernatant is discarded and the cells are washed 2–3 times with PBS. Subsequently, the cells undergo another centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min before being resuspended in an appropriate volume of PBS for the purpose.

Flow cytometry is utilized for the quantification of various cell populations, such as T cells (CD3+/CD4+/CD8+), B cells (CD45R+), NK cells (CD3−NK1.1+), CD11b+ cells, and developmental stages DN1 (CD44+CD25−), DN2 (CD44+CD25+), DN3 (CD44−CD25+), and DN4 (CD44−CD25−). After the prepared cell suspension was mixed with the target antibody, it was incubated on ice for 20 min. The antibody was provided by BD Biosciences and used at a concentration of 1:1000 as recommended. After the incubation, the mixture was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min. The cells were then resuspended in 300 µl of PBS, filtered through a 200-mesh filter, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Data analysis is carried out using FlowJo software (FlowJo ™ v10). Live lymphocytes and spleen cells were identified and gated based on their known locations using forward scatter area (FSC-A) and side scatter area (SSC-A) parameters. Doublets were excluded by applying a gate on forward scatter height (FSC-H) versus FSC-A. CD3+ T cells, CD45R/B220+ B cells, and CD11b+ cells were subsequently gated within the singlet population. CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells were further gated from the CD3+ T cell population, while NK1.1+ cells were gated from the CD3− T cell population. All flow cytometry data were collected following the MIFlowCyt reporting standards.

Metpro-II metabolite ascorbic acid interacts with PBMC protein

The Metabolic Protein Interaction (MetPro PMI) research methodology was developed to examine the interplay between proteomes and their associated metabolites. Following the isolation of total proteins from mouse PBMC, the proteins are quantified utilizing a BCA assay (Merck Millipore) and their structural integrity is evaluated. Subsequently, the protein solution is diluted to a concentration of 1 mg/mL. From a total of 600 µg of proteins, six aliquots are generated, with each set of three forming a distinct group.

The control group (#1, #2, #3) is administered distilled water, while the experimental group (#1, #2, #3) is supplemented with 33 nmol of AA and incubated at 25 °C for 10 min. Proteinase K (PK) (Sangon Biotech) is introduced to each group at a 1:100 ratio and incubated for an additional 5 min at 25 °C. Subsequently, the samples are subjected to a water bath at temperatures exceeding 95 °C to fully deactivate the PK. Following a 5-minute cooldown to room temperature, a solution containing 2% sodium deoxycholate (DOC) and 200 mM ammonium bicarbonate (ABC) (resulting in a final concentration of 1% DOC and 100mM ABC) at pH 8.5 is added. Subsequently, DL-dithiothreitol (DTT) is added to the mixture to achieve a final concentration of 10 mM, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. Iodoacetamide (IAA) is then introduced to reach a final concentration of 40 mM, and the samples are incubated in the absence of light at room temperature for 45 min. Trypsin is added at a ratio of 1:50 for an overnight incubation in a water bath set at 37 °C. The enzymatic reaction is terminated by the addition of 50% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) to a final concentration of 2%, resulting in precipitation. The pH of the solution is adjusted to below 3 using formic acid, leading to the precipitation of DOC as a white solid. Following centrifugation at 16,000 g for 10 min at room temperature, the supernatant is meticulously transferred to fresh Eppendorf tubes. The samples are subjected to two washes with 500 µL of 2% TFA, with subsequent centrifugation and consolidation of the supernatants from all washes. The C18 column is primed with a 0.5 mL acetonitrile wash, equilibrated twice with 0.5 mL of Buffer A, and peptides are loaded onto the column 2–3 times to ensure complete passage through the column. Peptides are washed twice with 0.5 mL of Buffer A, then eluted with 0.5 mL of Buffer B into a new tube. The peptides are subsequently desiccated under vacuum at 37 °C for approximately 5 h and subsequently reconstituted in 100 µL of 0.1% formic acid, yielding an estimated concentration of 1 µg/µL.

NanoLC-MS/MS analysis

Each sample undergoes separation using the EASY-nLC 1200 nanoUPLC system from Thermo Fisher Scientific, with 2 µL of total peptide content. Data acquisition is conducted with a Q-Exactive HFX mass spectrometer, also from Thermo Fisher Scientific, featuring a nano-electrospray ion source. The separation process employs a reversed-phase column (dimensions: 100 μm in diameter, 15 cm in length, Reprosil-Pur 120 C18-AQ, with a particle size of 1.9 μm). The mobile phases consist of a combination of acetonitrile, water, and formic acid, with mobile phase A containing a 0.1% formic acid solution in water (98% water, 2% acetonitrile) and mobile phase B containing a 0.1% formic acid solution in acetonitrile (80% acetonitrile, 20% water). After the column is equilibrated with 100% mobile phase A, the samples are loaded directly onto the column. The separation process is carried out at a flow rate of 300 nL/min using a gradient over a duration of 120 min. The gradient profile for mobile phase B is characterized by a series of stepwise increases in concentration over a 120-minute period. Specifically, the concentration remains at 5% for the first 2 min, then gradually increases to 22% over the next 98 min, followed by a more rapid increase to 45% over 16 min, and finally a rapid escalation to 95% over 2 min, maintaining this concentration for the final 2 min. Mass spectrometric analysis utilizes a data-dependent acquisition mode in positive ion mode for the duration of the 120-minute analysis.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

As previously stated [31], total RNA was extracted from four biological samples (two per group from both the AA-treated and control groups) for the purpose of constructing an RNA-seq library, which was subsequently sequenced and analyzed using the BGIseq500 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China). The initial sequencing reads underwent purification by removing those containing adaptors or poly-N sequences, as well as reads with a high proportion of low-quality bases. The resulting clean reads were saved in FASTQ format and subsequently aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.0.4) [32]. The clean reads were aligned against the reference coding gene set using Bowtie2 (v2.2.5) [33], and gene expression levels were quantified through RSEM (v1.2.12) [34]. Heatmaps depicting gene expression variations across various samples were created using pheatmap (v1.0.8). Differential expression analysis was carried out utilizing DESeq2 (v1.4.5) [35], which identified genes with statistically significant differences at a threshold of Q < 0.05. In order to clarify phenotypic variations, differentially expressed genes were annotated and subsequently analyzed for enrichment in Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways using the hypergeometric test implemented in Phyper. The statistical significance of terms and pathways was rigorously controlled through Bonferroni correction, with a threshold set at Q ≤ 0.05.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis of AA with Myh9 or Rhoa

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) enables the continuous monitoring of molecular interactions through the detection of signal shifts on a sensor chip. The strength of interaction between AA and the proteins Myh9 and Rhoa is evaluated using the SPR technique on the BIAcore 3000 instrument (GE Healthcare, Chicago, Illinois). Myh9 or Rhoa proteins are affixed to a CM5 sensor chip for immobilization, with 10 µL of Myh9 or Rhoa at a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL introduced into the flow cell at a flow rate of 5 µL/min. The successful immobilization of Myh9 or Rhoa onto the sensor chip was confirmed by observing an increase of approximately 5000 Response Units (RU). A control was implemented by omitting the injection of Myh9 or Rhoa into one of the flow cells. As for the experimental conditions, AA was diluted in a buffer solution containing 20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP at pH 7.2. Following the immobilization of proteins on the chip, AA at various dilutions was flowed across it at a rate of 30 µL/min for a duration of 3 min. After the injection of the samples, a flow buffer is circulated through the sensor for a duration of 3 min to aid in dissociation. Subsequently, the sensor surface is regenerated by the introduction of 20 µL of a 10 mM glycine-HCl solution (pH 2.25). Data analysis is conducted at a temperature of 25 °C utilizing BIAevaluation software version 4.1.1 (GE Healthcare).

Open field test (OFT)

The Open Field Test (OFT) is a commonly employed behavioral assay used to quantitatively assess spontaneous activity, exploratory behavior, and levels of anxiety or depression in animals. Prior to commencing the experiment, mice designated for testing are acclimated to the experimental environment by being placed in the testing room in advance. The experiment takes place in a behavior laboratory that is designed to provide a tranquil setting with soundproofing and controlled conditions of light, temperature, and humidity to ensure consistency. Mice are methodically removed from their housing cages, oriented towards the apparatus, and promptly placed at the central point of the experimental enclosure (dimensions: 50 × 50 × 30 cm). The behavioral analysis software is designed to monitor mouse activity within the testing arena for a consistent 15 min per session upon initiation. Analysis of the time spent by the mice in the central area allows for the inference of their anxiety levels. All batches of mice were tested simultaneously at the same time point. The recording duration was standardized at 15 min for all experimental groups and batches.

New object recognition (NOR)

The Novel Object Recognition (NOR) assay is utilized to assess the cognitive and memory functions of experimental animals by comparing their exploration times of familiar versus novel objects. To reduce stress and acclimatize the mice, they are handled daily before the commencement of the experiment. The experimental protocol consists of three sequential stages: an initial adaptation period lasting three days, during which mice are allowed to explore an empty apparatus for a duration of 10 min; a subsequent familiarization phase lasting 10 min, wherein two identical, odorless objects are securely positioned diagonally within the apparatus at a distance of 10 cm from the walls, and mice are introduced equidistantly to the objects for a 10-minute exploration; and finally, one of the identical objects is replaced, and the mice are positioned in the same manner as in the familiarization phase, with their movements monitored by camera and software for a period of 5 min. The Cognitive Index (D) is computed as a means of assessing the mice’s inclination towards the novel object, employing the equation: D = N/(N + F), where “N” represents the duration of exploration of the novel object, and “F” denotes the duration of exploration of the familiar object throughout the testing period. All batches of mice were tested simultaneously at the same time point.

Statistical analysis

The data were subjected to statistical analysis using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software Inc.). Results are reported as standard error of the mean (SEM) and were obtained from a minimum of three independent experiments. The unpaired t-test was employed for statistical comparison between the two groups, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The accelerated global aging trend has established cognitive impairment as a critical public health burden, with approximately 50% of individuals aged 60 years and older exhibiting varying degrees of cognitive decline [36]. Beyond classical neurodegenerative pathologies [37–39], ​immunosenescence—characterized by age-related immune dysfunction—has been confirmed as a core driver of cognitive deterioration [40]. This study demonstrates that AA supplementation selectively expands peripheral CD8⁺T-cell populations in middle-aged mice​ (p < 0.05), concomitant with improved memory performance.​​ ​Furthermore, the observed cognitive enhancement—such as in novel object recognition—was not attributable to changes in locomotor activity induced by AA. More critically, we identify a previously unrecognized mechanism wherein AA enhances T-cell receptor (TCR) signaling via modulating MYH9 activity, thereby lowering the antigen recognition threshold and promoting CD8⁺T-cell survival. While the cognitive benefits of AA remain contentious [41–44], our data reconcile this controversy by establishing intravenous delivery as a critical efficacy determinant – a factor overlooked in prior null-effect studies using oral administration. We recognize that intravenous injection is less feasible for routine human use due to practical constraints such as the need for clinical supervision and poor patient compliance. This route was chosen in our preclinical study to achieve precise and consistent systemic exposure throughout the extended treatment regimen, thereby circumventing the absorption variability and limited bioavailability associated with oral or intraperitoneal routes, which could obscure the interpretation of long-term therapeutic effects. This work extends beyond classical antioxidant paradigms by revealing AA’s immunomodulatory function through cytoskeletal remodeling, contrasting with prior emphasis on redox balance. The essential role of CD8⁺T cells in cognitive maintenance aligns with clinical evidence linking CD8⁺T-cell depletion to accelerated cognitive decline [45–47].

Functional inhibition of MYH9 (via blebbistatin) significantly impaired CD8⁺T-cell activation efficiency (p < 0.001), while AA supplementation (0.1 mg/g, 1 month) reversed this defect (p < 0.05 vs. blebbistatin-only). Research shows that blebbistatin disrupts TCR signaling and significantly impairs CD8⁺ T cell activation efficiency [48]. Our work shows that this rescue phenotype positions MYH9 as a tractable target for immunosenescence intervention, though the precise molecular interaction (e.g., AA-mediated phosphorylation vs. conformational stabilization) requires further validation.​​​.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although consistent trends were observed across experiments, the sample size—particularly in early exploratory phases such as the preliminary RNA-seq analysis—may have limited statistical robustness. We emphasize that future studies should include larger cohorts to strengthen these findings. Second, the intrinsic AA synthesis capability of C57BL/6 mice likely leads to an underestimation of the therapeutic efficacy that might be achieved in humans, who are dependent on dietary vitamin C [49]. Subsequent investigations using Gulo−/− models, which mimic human vitamin C auxotrophy, will be critical for validating translational relevance. Third, while intravenous administration was essential for ensuring precise bioavailability in this preclinical study, its clinical impracticality underscores the need to develop patient-compliant alternatives—such as liposome-based delivery systems [50]. Finally, cognitive assessment was restricted to a single behavioral domain (novel object recognition). Future work should incorporate complementary tests—such as the Morris water maze for spatial learning and attentional set-shifting tasks for executive function—to evaluate the full scope of cognitive enhancement. Fifth, it should be noted that only female mice were used in this study. This decision was based on the well-documented aggressive behavior observed in group-housed sexually mature and mated male C57BL/6 mice, which poses challenges for long-term behavioral and physiological assessments. Future studies should explicitly examine sex differences in the cognitive and immune-modulatory effects of AA.​ Sixth, although our data underscore the essential role of peripheral CD8⁺ T cells in mediating the cognitive benefits, the specific mechanisms by which these cells communicate with the brain remain unclear. Potential pathways may include cytokine signaling (e.g., IFN-γ), direct cellular interactions within the meninges, or modulation of systemic inflammation. Clarifying this brain-immune communication axis will be a central objective of our future research. Most importantly, although MYH9 was identified as a key mediator, the precise molecular mechanisms—such as whether AA binding induces conformational changes or modulates post-translational modifications like phosphorylation—remain to be elucidated. Structural validation through cryo-electron microscopy and functional studies using T cell-specific Myh9 knockout models will be essential to clarify these interactions. Addressing these questions systematically, alongside further validation in primate models and phase 0/I human trials, will help facilitate the clinical translation of this nutrient–immunity axis.

We propose a strategy for subsequent research. Single-cell multi-omics profiling​ of AA-reprogrammed CD8⁺T-cell subsets to decipher epigenetic (e.g., methylation) and transcriptomic drivers of immune rejuvenation. Age-stratified intervention trials​ using immune aging biomarkers (e.g., CD28⁻CD57⁺ T-cell ratios) to define optimal therapeutic windows. ​Combinatorial approaches​ integrating AA with. mTOR inhibitors (e.g., rapamycin) to amplify T-cell function.

Conclusion

Collectively, our study demonstrates that AA counteracts features of immunosenescence and alleviates cognitive deficits in aged mice by selectively promoting CD8⁺ T cell differentiation through targeting MYH9 activity. These findings propose a novel nutrient–immunity–cognition axis hinging on cytoskeletal regulation of T cell receptor signaling. Further validation in larger cohorts including both sexes, mechanistic elucidation of AA-MYH9 interactions, and translational studies using human-relevant models will be essential to evaluate the therapeutic potential of AA in attenuating age-related cognitive decline.
