Gastric Cancer-Associated Immunomodulatory Fibroblasts Persist Following Helicobacter pylori Eradication: The Need to Douse a Smoldering Fire
Authors: Jennifer M Noto, Richard M Peek
Published: 2026

Gastric cancer remains a significant global health concern, ranking as the fifth most common malignancy and fifth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide.1 Helicobacter pylori, the most prevalent bacterial pathogen, represents the strongest known risk factor for this malignancy. Although treatment of H pylori is an effective strategy to reduce the incidence and mortality of gastric cancer, eradication alone may not be sufficient in preventing gastric carcinogenesis among persons who harbor pre-existing premalignant lesions.2 The study by Fu and colleagues begins to address this critical issue by leveraging single-cell RNA sequencing to demonstrate that H pylori remodels the gastric mucosal niche and establishes a self-perpetuating inflammation-to-cancer transformation microenvironment that persists in a subset of patients with advanced atrophic gastritis following successful H pylori eradication.3

Several recent studies have similarly used innovative technologies such as single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics to define the gastric microenvironment within the context of H pylori infection and across different stages of the gastric carcinogenesis cascade. Each of those reports has emphasized the presence of vast cellular heterogeneity and lineages, which include immunomodulatory cancer-associated fibroblasts (iCAFs).4, 5, 6, 7, 8 However, the current study3 builds upon this existing literature by defining the gastric microenvironment following H pylori eradication therapy, as well as expanding upon the heterogeneity of iCAF subpopulations and their specific epithelial and immune cell interactions within the gastric mucosa.

For this work, Fu and colleagues3 constructed a single-cell atlas of gastric antral mucosa, derived from normal healthy patients, H pylori-infected patients with nonatrophic gastritis (NAG) or chronic atrophic gastritis (CAG), as well as H pylori-eradicated patients with CAG. The H pylori-eradicated CAG group was further stratified into 2 classes: Class 1 patients showed pathological improvement following H pylori eradication, whereas Class 2 patients demonstrated no histologic change. The authors then specifically focused their analysis on defining the heterogeneity of iCAFs as key stromal regulators of specific epithelial-immune cell interactions. The results demonstrate that H pylori infection alters the abundance of 3 iCAF subgroups, and the authors also identify a novel iCAF differentiation hierarchy whereby H pylori infection promotes differentiation of the restorative iCAF2 subtype into pathogenic and procarcinogenic CCN2+ iCAF1 and C3+ iCAF3 subpopulations. Importantly, H pylori-induced pathogenic iCAF reprogramming is dichotomous, as it reverts back to the pattern observed among healthy controls in the Class 1 eradication group but persists in the Class 2 eradication group.

In conjunction with iCAF reprogramming, the authors further demonstrate that H pylori infection alters T and B cell interactions with iCAF subtypes within the gastric mucosa. Specifically, H pylori and disease progression were linked to a pathogenic shift and progressive immunosuppression that was defined by exhausted CD4+ T cells and expanded T regulatory cell populations, as well as activation of germinal center B cells. In healthy controls, T and B cell immunoregulatory components interacted more strongly with the restorative iCAF2 subtype, whereas, among H pylori-infected patients with NAG, there was a pathogenic shift and increased interactions with carcinogenic iCAF1 subtypes. Again, these immunosuppressive phenotypes and pathogenic interactions were found to revert to those of healthy controls in the Class 1 eradication group but not in the Class 2 eradication group.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate for the first time that H pylori eradication per se is not sufficient to reverse the immunosuppressive and neoplastic properties of immune effectors and iCAFs within the antral gastric microenvironment. Further, these data highlight the need for additional interventions following H pylori eradication to interrupt and reverse the inflammation-to-cancer transformation phenotype and prevent progression to gastric cancer.

Limitations of this study include the relatively small cohort size and the lack of comprehensive longitudinal tracking of H pylori-induced gastric carcinogenesis in individual patients. Thus, prospective studies are needed to more precisely define the gastric microenvironment during active H pylori infection throughout all the stages of gastric carcinogenesis, as well as evaluate the effectiveness of H pylori eradication therapy on subsequent disease. Further, this study raises the intriguing possibility of targeting pathogenic iCAFs as a strategy to prevent gastric carcinogenesis in patients with advanced atrophic gastritis. However, interventional studies are first needed to demonstrate that pathogenic iCAFs and their sustained interactions with immunoregulatory components are reversible within the context of gastric carcinogenesis. Significant therapeutic advances have been made in: (1) targeting cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) via depletion of CAFs through precision chemotherapy; (2) reducing the tumor-promoting and immunosuppressive functions of CAFs through immunotherapy; and (3) reprogramming CAFs to a quiescent state. Although this has been attempted in several different models of carcinogenesis, it has yet to be explored within the context of gastric carcinogenesis, providing a fruitful avenue for future investigations.9,10
