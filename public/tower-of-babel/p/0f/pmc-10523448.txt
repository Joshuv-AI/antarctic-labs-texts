Enhancing impacts of mesoscale eddies on Southern Ocean temperature variability and extremes
Authors: Qingyou He, Weikang Zhan, Shuqun Cai, Yan Du, Zhiwu Chen, Shilin Tang, Haigang Zhan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceExtreme temperature events have severe impacts on marine organisms and ecosystems. While satellite observations have shown an increase in surface marine heatwaves associated with global warming, our understanding of such events in the subsurface ocean, where many organisms reside, remains limited. Here, we demonstrate that nearly half of the observed subsurface temperature extremes in the Southern Ocean are within ocean eddies. Moreover, we observe notable increases in temperature anomalies within eddies over the past decade, which promote the increase in subsurface ocean temperature variability and extremes. Our findings suggest that eddies may be an important driver of subsurface marine temperature extremes and heatwaves and their significance is expected to enhance with ongoing global warming.

The Southern Ocean, which connects all basins in the world ocean, strongly influences global ocean circulation, biogeochemical cycles, and climate change (1, 2). Prevailing westerly wind over this circumpolar ocean fuels the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC), one of the most powerful current systems in the global ocean (3). This robust eastward flow facilitates the zonal transport of water masses across ocean basins, while simultaneously inhibiting meridional exchanges due to the steeply sloping isopycnals shoaling to the south (1, 4). The intense baroclinic instability within the ACC creates a breeding ground for vigorous eddy activities (5, 6) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). These eddies not only modify the vertical flux of heat, nutrients, and carbon between the sea surface and deep ocean but also transport them laterally across the fronts of the ACC (7–9), playing important roles in balancing the global heat budget (10, 11) and regulating the global carbon cycling (12–14).

Over the past decades, the Southern Ocean absorbed over two-thirds of the anthropogenic heat in the climate system (15, 16) and experienced pronounced warming (17–19). This warming is accompanied by more frequent, more intense, and longer-lasting extremely high-temperature events (also known as marine heat waves for prolonged ones, e.g., >5 d) (20, 21), which increase the risk of severe and even irreversible impacts on marine organisms, ecosystems, and fisheries (22–24). As one of the most ubiquitous and energetic features in this ocean (6), mesoscale eddies are expected to be an important cause of these events. Recent model simulations demonstrate that heat flux convergence by eddies acts as a dominant driver of surface marine heat waves (25). Eddy-resolved models are also reported to have better performance in simulating marine heat waves compared to low-resolution ones (26). However, despite the remarkable achievements made by model simulations, observational evidence of the influence of eddies on extreme temperature events, especially for those below the ocean surface that are outreach of satellite observations, is very limited (27).

On the other hand, satellite observations have shown a rise in surface eddy kinetic energy over the past two decades (28, 29). However, the thermal consequences of increased eddy activity, especially in the context of ocean warming, are still poorly understood. Here, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of historically available hydrographic measurements from multiple observational platforms with concurrent satellite-based eddy observations to show a substantial increase in both mean and extreme temperature anomalies associated with eddies during the past decades.

Eddy-Associated Mean and Extreme Temperature Anomalies

More than 500,000 temperature and salinity (T-S) profile measurements that passed a rigorous quality control procedure were obtained in the Southern Ocean (south of 30°S) between 1993 and 2019, during which satellite eddy data are available (Methods). The distribution of these profile measurements spans the Southern Ocean, with more than 150 profiles within each 2°×2° grid box for most regions north of 60°S, indicating the feasibility of this analysis (Fig. 1A). For each profile data, we associated it with the nearest eddy on the sampling day and normalized the distance between them using the eddy’s radius (R). Since the size of an eddy varies from case to case, this normalization enables the comparison of eddies with different radii (Fig. 1B) (30). Approximately 20% of the profiles are found within eddy interiors (d < R), with similar proportions (around 10%) for both anticyclonic eddies (AEs) and cyclonic eddies (CEs) (Fig. 1C). The overall percentage of profiles within eddies is about 5% lower than previous findings suggesting that eddies cover approximately one-quarter of the ocean surface (31). This difference could be due to the lower eddy occurrence probability near Antarctica because of sea-ice coverage and/or smaller eddies in higher latitudes that are not well detected by satellites (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C).

We computed temperature anomalies as the difference between each profile and the closest matching point in climatological monthly products, from the World Ocean Atlas 2018 (WOA 2018), to remove seasonal cycle signals and mitigate aliasing issues caused by sparse historical sampling (19). The resulting temperature anomalies display a generally normal distribution (Fig. 1D). However, the temperature anomalies within AEs exhibit a positive skew, while those within CEs display a negative skew. Specifically, at the depth of 400 m, the mean temperature within AEs is 0.5 °C higher than the mean value of all profile measurements, whereas in CEs, it is 0.5 °C lower. Furthermore, these eddies play a remarkable role in extreme temperature anomalies. Although only 10% of the profile measurements are within AEs, they account for 30% of the records with extremely high-temperature anomalies at the 95th quantile (Fig. 1E). This contribution increases even further to 40% for anomalies at the 99th quantile. Moreover, the influence of eddies on temperature extremes enhances with depth (Fig. 1F). In the layer between 500 m and 1,000, nearly half of the temperature anomalies exceeding 2 °C are observed within AEs. A similar pattern is observed for CEs, which made a substantial contribution to low-temperature extremes (Fig. 1F).

Mean temperature anomalies within AEs (CEs) in each 2°×2° grid box are estimated by subtracting mean temperature anomalies outside eddies (referenced background fields, d > 2R) from those within eddy interiors (d < R). The results show apparent regional variability in the thermal imprints of eddies, with the most pronounced temperature anomalies centered along the ACC (Fig. 2 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). In the ACC region, the magnitudes of temperature anomalies within both AEs and CEs exceed 0.5 °C throughout the upper 1,000 m, with peak values reaching over 2 °C at depths ranging from 300 m to 700 m (Fig. 2 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Considering the relatively low climatological mean temperature at these depths, these temperature anomalies represent approximately 30% of the relative perturbations in local temperature and seawater heat content (SI Appendix, Fig. S4).

Both extremely high (95th percentile)-temperature anomalies within AEs and low (5th percentile)-temperature anomalies within CEs exhibit geographic structures similar to the mean temperature anomalies (Fig. 2 A–D and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). The magnitudes of these extreme temperature anomalies are generally twice as large as the mean anomalies, indicating approximately 60% of relative perturbations in local temperature in the ACC (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). In most regions of the Southern Ocean, nearly half of the measurements showing extremely high-temperature anomalies (>95th percentile) are observed in AEs (Fig. 2E). Similarly, nearly half of the measurements indicating extremely low-temperature anomalies (<5th percentile) are observed in CEs (Fig. 2F). This contribution of eddies to temperature extremes is observed throughout the depth range analyzed in this study, except for the near-surface where the contribution is generally weaker (<20%) (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). This weaker contribution at the near-surface is presumably influenced by temperature extremes caused by surface forcings (e.g., surface heating, wind, and precipitation) and/or submesoscale activities that are more prevalent in the near-surface mixed layer (23, 24, 32).

The positive temperature anomalies within AEs and negative temperature anomalies within CEs are widely explained by eddy pumping, that is, downwelling of warm near-surface water in AEs and upwelling of cold deep water in CEs (33, 34). The intensity of resultant temperature anomalies is closely related to the vertical gradient of large-scale temperature fields and thus primarily peaks near the thermocline (10, 35, 36). However, in the ACC region, the pronounced eddy-associated temperature anomalies are not accompanied by intense large-scale vertical temperature gradients (SI Appendix, Fig. S3, right column). Instead, they exhibit strong geographical coherence with the sharp horizontal temperature gradients across the ACC (SI Appendix, Fig. S3, third column). This feature suggests that the cross-fontal movement of eddies, with water trapped within their interiors from their formation sites (eddy trapping hypothesis), may play an important role in generating these temperature anomalies (8, 37).

An important difference between the two mechanisms is that the vertical pumping of water is a quasiadiabatic movement that barely results in temperature anomalies on isopycnal surfaces (17, 38). We therefore computed eddy-associated potential temperature anomalies (θ′′) on isopycnal surfaces to separate the signal of eddy pumping. The results show notable potential temperature anomalies (0.5 to 1 °C) between σ = 1026.2 to 1027.2 kg m−3 in the ACC (Fig. 2 I and J). This layer generally corresponds to the depth range 100 to 500 m, consistent with the apparent temperature anomalies observed in the depth coordinate (Fig. 2 G and H). When these potential temperature anomalies are interpolated into the depth coordinate (gray contours in Fig. 2 I and J), they turn out to account for more than 50% of the total temperature anomalies in the upper 500 m (Fig. 2 K and L and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). This high ratio between the potential temperature anomalies and the total temperature anomalies underscores the important contribution of eddy trapping to regulating the temperature anomalies. However, the ratio between them decreases with depth, indicating a weakening influence of eddy trapping and a growing dominance of eddy pumping at greater depths.

Influence on Regional Temperature Variability

Although the net influence of eddies on regional mean temperature is overall weak because the effects of AEs and CEs often cancel each other out (SI Appendix, Fig. S2) (5, 39), the opposite-signed temperature anomalies induced by the two types of eddies imply an amplification of local temperature variability. To support this notion, we estimated the range of temperature variation as a function of distance from the center of eddies, considering both polarities of eddies. The range of temperature variation is defined here as the difference between extremely high (95th percentile) and low (5th percentile) temperature anomalies (40, 41). In contrast to a moderate change at the near-surface, the range of temperature variation decreases notably from 3.5 °C at eddy centers to 1.6 °C at twice eddy radii at the depth of 400 m (Fig. 3A). Beyond twice the eddy radii, the range of temperature variation remains relatively stable. The pattern is consistent for both extremely high- and low-temperature anomalies (Fig. 3 E and I), indicating that the horizontal influence of these eddies is confined within twice eddy radii.

To quantify the contribution of eddies in amplifying regional temperature variability, we computed the difference in the range of temperature variation, within each 2° × 2° grid, before and after excluding profile measurements potentially influenced by eddies (within twice eddy radii, d < 2R). The results show that excluding these eddy-related profiles leads to a decrease in the range of temperature variation by approximately 1 °C at the subsurface layer of the ACC (Fig. 3 B–D). The patterns remain consistent when the exclusion criterion is set as d < 1.5R or d < 2.5R (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). This decrease in temperature variation suggests that eddies contribute to amplifying the variability of subsurface ocean temperature by approximately 30% in the ACC region (Fig. 3D). The ratio is similar as the variability of temperature is measured by the standard deviation (SD) of temperature anomaly measurements (corresponding to 15.9th to 84.1th percentile in a normal distribution) (42, 43), supporting the important role of eddies in amplifying subsurface ocean temperature variability (SI Appendix, Fig. S10).

The amplification of ocean temperature variability contributed by eddies is composed of an increase in high-temperature extremes and a decrease in low-temperature extremes, with the latter being generally more pronounced than the former (Fig. 3 E–L). The spatial distribution of this temperature response shows that the increase in high-temperature extremes is more prominent south of the ACC fronts, while the decrease in low-temperature extremes is more noticeable north of the fronts (Fig. 3 D and H). This asymmetric temperature response aligns with the poleward movement of AEs originating from warmer waters north of the fronts and equatorward movement of CEs from colder waters south of the fronts (8, 44). It suggests that the cross-frontal movement of eddies may be an important source of the extremely high-temperature events on the cold side of the ACC and extremely low-temperature events on the warm side. This eddy effect is nearly imperceptible at the near-surface layer (Fig. 3 F and J), indicating that the variability in surface temperature driven by eddies is comparable to the variability caused by other surface dynamics.

Recent Trends

The ACC region experienced the strongest warming rates in the Southern Ocean over the past decades (17, 45). To assess how eddies have responded to this change, we estimated the linear trends of eddy-associated mean and extreme temperature anomalies over a 10° latitude (~1,000 km) band centered at the ACC axis between 2006 and 2019 (Fig. 4 A and B). Data before 2006 are discarded because of poor sampling (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). The results reveal significant increasing trends in the temperature anomalies within AEs, with rates of 0.01 to 0.1 °C decade−1 in the upper 1,500 m [under the Mann–Kendall test at the 95% confidence level (46)] (Fig. 4C). The increasing trends are most pronounced in the upper 500 m, peaking at 0.11 ± 0.02 °C decade−1 centered at 370 m. Similarly, negative temperature anomalies within CEs also exhibit increasing trends, although with a shallower and weaker peak of 0.07 ± 0.02 °C decade−1 at 250 m. The SDs of temperature anomalies within both AEs and CEs have also increased, indicating a greater variability in eddy-associated temperature anomalies (Fig. 4G). These results suggest that the impact of eddies on upper ocean temperature has strengthened as the ocean has warmed. In contrast, the trends in the components of temperature anomalies dominated by eddy trapping show weakening anomalies for eddies of both polarities (Fig. 4D), suggesting that changes in cross-frontal eddy movements are not responsible for the increase of the eddy-associated temperature anomalies. Instead, they are likely a result of enhanced eddy pumping due to the increase in eddy intensity (28) and upper ocean stratification (47, 48).

Trends for eddy-associated extreme temperature anomalies align closely with those for the mean temperature anomalies, except for a generally greater increasing rate of up to 0.38 ± 0.07 °C decade−1 at 450 m (Fig. 4E). Although not statistically significant at many depths (at the 95% confidence level), the percentages of temperature extremes occurring in both AEs and CEs have increased (by approximately 2%) over the past decade (Fig. 4F). These observations indicate that eddies have made a greater contribution to both the occurrence and intensity of extreme temperature events in a warming ocean.

The opposite sign in the trends of mean (and extreme) temperature anomalies within AEs and CEs (Fig. 4 C and E) implies a strengthening in the trend of regional temperature variability contributed by eddy activities. As shown in Fig. 5 A and B, when the profile measurements potentially influenced by eddies (d < 2R) are excluded, the estimated trend for extremely low-temperature anomalies increases by 0.04 to 0.05 °C decade−1 between 500 m and 1,000 m. This suggests that eddies have acted to mitigate the warming of low-temperature extremes in the subsurface ACC. Conversely, the increasing trend of extremely high-temperature anomalies weakens by 0.01 to 0.03 °C decade−1 as the eddy-related profiles are excluded, indicating that eddies have contributed to promoting the increase of regional high-temperature extremes. Overall, when eddy signals (eddy-related profiles) are artificially removed, the trends for extremely high- and low-temperature anomalies between 500 m and 1,000 m are similar (Fig. 5A), indicating little change in the range of temperature variation (Fig. 5B). However, when eddy activities are counted (all profile measurements are considered), the difference between them becomes approximately 0.04 ± 0.01 °C decade−1 (Fig. 5C). This change suggests that eddies have contributed to most of the increasing trend of regional temperature variability at these depths (Fig. 5D). The importance of eddies in promoting the increase in subsurface temperature variability is further supported by the weakening of the increasing trend of the SD of temperature anomaly measurements when the eddy-related profiles are excluded (Fig. 5 E and F).

We also estimated the linear trend of regional mean temperature anomalies in the ACC band. The results show a warming rate of 0.15 ± 0.03 °C decade−1 at the near-surface with a decrease in the warming rate with depth to less than 0.05 ± 0.01 °C decade−1 below 1,000 m (Fig. 5G). The pattern is in agreement with previous studies (16–18), which have also observed faster warming above the main thermocline compared to below it, indicating an intensification of upper ocean stratification (47, 48). Interestingly, when the eddy-related profiles are excluded, the estimated trends show a systematic increase of 0.002 to 0.02 °C decade−1 in the upper 1,500 m. This difference suggests that eddies have acted to mitigate the warming of the upper ocean by approximately 10% (Fig. 5H). One possible explanation is that energized eddy activities (29) have increased the variation of ocean temperature (Fig. 5 A–F). This increased temperature variation implies enhanced oceanic heat mixing and thus down-gradient heat transport from the sea surface into deeper waters (49, 50). The facilitated heat mixing by eddies may, on the one hand, help mitigate the warming of the upper ocean, providing a mechanism for eddies to counteract the overall warming trend and on the other hand, contribute to the accumulation of heat in the deep ocean, offering an alternative explanation for the observed heat accumulation in the deep ocean (51).

Potential Implications

The warming of the Southern Ocean is not spatially homogeneous (16, 52). It is characterized by greater temperature increase north of the ACC compared to the southern regions (18), indicative of an intensification of the ACC fronts (3). The intensified ACC fronts are accompanied by increased instability and thus higher eddy kinetic energy as revealed by satellite observations (28, 29). This increase in eddy activity, combined with enhanced upper ocean stratification (47, 48), results in greater eddy-induced temperature anomalies (Fig. 4). These temperature anomalies contribute to the overall increase in ocean temperature variability (Fig. 5 C–F) and also the enhancement of extreme temperature events (Fig. 5 A and B). In return, the increased temperature variability contributed by eddies implies a strengthening of oceanic heat mixing facilitated by eddies, which may promote the down-gradient transport of heat from the sea surface into deep waters, thus mitigating the warming of the upper ocean (Fig. 5 G and H) and, presumably, promoting the warming of deep ocean (51). Our analysis demonstrated pronounced thermal responses of eddies to the warming of the Southern Ocean and their potential influence on both regional mean and extreme temperature trends. These eddy effects are expected to persist and become even more pronounced as global warming continues.

Prolonged extremely high-temperature events (known as marine heat waves) are of particular concern due to their severe impacts on marine organisms and ecosystems (22, 24). Satellite observations have shown an increase in the frequency and severity of surface marine heat waves in the Southern Ocean as it warms (20, 21). However, understanding subsurface marine heat waves is challenging due to limited hydrographic measurements and a lack of continuous long-term records (23). By combining more than 500,000 historical available hydrographic measurements and satellite eddy observations, we demonstrated that AEs (CEs) are associated with nearly half of the subsurface high (low)-temperature extremes detected (Figs. 1 E and F and 2 E and F). Since eddies have relatively long lifetimes (from days to months) and a remarkable ability to maintain distinct water properties within their interiors (37, 53), these extremes may have similar durations and thus are more likely to be marine heat waves (cold spells) rather than transient perturbations. This implies that eddies may be an important driver of subsurface marine heat waves and cold spells. A statistical analysis in the western Tasman Sea has also shown that more than half of the Argo profile observations during surface marine heat waves are located in AEs (27), though the consistency between surface and subsurface marine heat waves is to be further quantified. In addition, we demonstrated an increase in the contribution of eddies to enlarging subsurface temperature extremes with the warming of the ocean (Figs. 4 E and F and 5 A and B), consistent with the intensification of marine heat waves observed in surface waters. These findings may have important implications for the understanding of the mechanisms behind marine heat waves.

Climate models play a crucial role in providing useful scenarios for future climate change (54). However, most current climate models have limitations in explicitly resolving ocean eddies and instead rely on parameterizations to represent their effects. This deficiency has been identified as an important reason for the discrepancies between model outputs and available observations, particularly in the Southern Ocean (55, 56). In this study, we demonstrated the important roles of eddies in agitating ocean temperature and that the eddy effects are enhancing with the warming of the ocean. These findings highlight the importance of accurately representing eddies in climate models to produce more realistic temperature distribution and to effectively capture the occurrence and evolution of extreme temperature events. Furthermore, our analysis suggests that eddies contribute to mitigating the warming of the upper ocean, which might have far-reaching consequences from the melt of Antarctic Sea ice to the rise in global sea level (54, 57). Incorporating these findings into model development and management strategies may help improve our ability to simulate and project future climate scenarios.

Methods

Observational Products.

The T-S profile data used in this study are obtained from the World Ocean Database (WOD), which collects historical available in situ hydrographic measurements from multiple platforms, such as ocean station, ship-based conductivity-temperature-depth, expendable bathythermograph, moored buoy, Argo float, and Glider over the global ocean (58). Here, we isolated all available (900,000) T-S profiles with quality control flags marked as “0” (accepted) in the Southern Ocean (south of 30°S) from the database between January 1993 and December 2019, during which satellite-based eddy observations are available.

The altimetric Mesoscale Eddy Trajectory Atlas product (META3.1exp, DT allsat) is obtained from Archiving, Validation and Interpretation of Satellite Oceanographic data (AVISO). Eddies in this product are automatically identified and tracked from daily ADT (absolute dynamic topography) dataset, based on overlapping closed contours of sea surface height (SSH) fields in agreement with a series of defined criteria (e.g., shape error ≤ 70%, only one extremum, 5 ≤ number of pixels ≤ 1,000 in a contour) (59). The product provides time series of the position, amplitude, radius, rotational speed, and associated edge contours of each identified and tracked eddies between January 1993 and March 2020. Eddy radius is estimated as the radius of the best-fit circle corresponding to the contour of maximum circum-average speed (defined as eddy edge). Eddy amplitude is defined as absolute SSH difference between the extremum of SSH within the eddy and the SSH around the effective contour defining the eddy edge. We estimated the probability of eddy occurrence in this study at each 1/4°×1/4° grid as the percentage of days when the grid cell was occupied by any eddies between 1993 and 2019 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Climatological monthly mean seawater temperature products with a spatial resolution of 1/4° from the WOA 2018 are used as a reference for large-scale seasonal temperature variability. Time-mean ADT products with a spatial resolution of 1/4° from the Copernicus Marine Environment and Monitoring Service (CMEMS) are used to outline the location of the ACC.

Estimation of Eddy-Associated Variability.

Although the WOD has conducted an initial quality control for the profile data, a more rigorous quality-control procedure is implemented to remove profiles with unreasonable observations and coarse vertical resolutions, which may introduce errors (especially near the main thermocline) when interpolating data to high-resolution grids (ranging from 10 m to 1,500 m at an interval of 5 m) subsequently. This process includes discarding profiles without temperature or salinity observations at depths shallower than 20 m or deeper than 100 m, with less than 10 unique observational values in the upper 200 m and with sampling intervals greater than 15 m between 0 and 100 m and greater than 25 m between 100 and 200 m (60). More than 500,000 T-S profile measurements remained after this filtering. These profiles are scattered across the Southern Ocean, with more than 150 profiles within each 2°×2° grid for most of the regions north of 60°S (Fig. 1A), supporting the representativeness of this analysis.

For each profile, we isolated all synchronous eddies identified by altimetry data and associated it with the nearest one. The distance of the profile to the associated eddy is normalized by the radius of the eddy (R) to favor the statistic of eddies of different sizes (Fig. 1B). The association of eddies to profiles ensures that each profile is assigned to a unique eddy and avoids the situation that a profile is detected in one eddy and at the periphery of another when searching profiles around eddies (36). Before computing eddy-associated temperature anomalies, each profile measurement is subtracted by the corresponding climatological monthly mean value at the closest matching point, from the WOA 2018 products, to remove seasonal cycle signals and avoid aliasing due to sparse historical sampling (19). Then, the mean temperature anomalies within eddies (TAE′′ for AEs and TCE′′ for CEs), in each 2°×2° grid (x, y) and depth level (z), are estimated as the difference in mean temperature anomalies between profile measurements within and outside eddies:[1]TAE′′x,y,z=TAE′x,y,z−TBG′x,y,z,[2]TCE′′x,y,z=TCE′x,y,z−TBG′x,y,z,

where TAE′, TCE′, and TBG′ are profile measurements of temperature anomalies (T′, relative to climatological mean states) within AEs (d < R), within CEs (d < R) and of background fields that are not influenced by eddies (BG, d > 2R). The overbar represents mean values. The definition of profiles outside twice eddy radii as the referenced background fields here aims to diminish the possible influence of twisted eddy shapes, as well as the complex submesoscale processes at eddy edges (61, 62). Our analysis in this study also demonstrates that the horizontal influence of eddies extends to twice eddy radii (Fig. 3A).

Extremely high (95th percentile) and low (5th percentile)-temperature anomalies within AEs and CEs are estimated as:[3]T95th_AE′′x,y,z=quantile(TAE′x,y,z-TBG′x,y,z,0.95), [4]T5th_CE′′x,y,z=quantile(TCE′x,y,z-TBG′x,y,z,0.05).

The contribution of eddies to extreme temperature events is estimated as the percentage of profiles with temperature anomalies greater (lower) than 95th (5th) percentile temperature anomalies that are observed in AEs (CEs):[5]P95th_AEx,y,z=N(TAE′x,y,z>T95th_All′x,y,z)N(TAll′x,y,z>T95th_All′x,y,z)×100%, [6]P5th_CEx,y,z=N(TCE′x,y,z<T5th_All′x,y,z)N(TAll′x,y,z<T5th_All′x,y,z)×100%,

where T95th_All′x,y,z=quantile(TAll′x,y,z,0.95) and T5th_All′x,y,z=quantile(TAll′x,y,z,0.05) are the 95th and 5th percentile temperature anomalies of all profile measurements (TAll′), respectively. N is the number of profile measurements that fulfills the conditions in the parentheses.

The range of temperature variation in this study is estimated as the difference between extremely high- and low-temperature anomalies (T95th-5th′=T95th′-T5th′). Assuming the profile measurements are randomly distributed in the ocean, the influence of eddies on regional temperature variability can be estimated by computing the difference in the range of temperature variation before and after excluding the profile measurements that are potentially influenced by eddies, i.e., within twice eddy radii (d < 2R):[7]ΔT95th-5th′x,y,z=T95th-5th_All′x,y,z-T95th-5th_BG′x,y,z.

A similar estimation on the influence of eddies on regional temperature variability is proceeded by estimating the difference in the SD of temperature anomaly measurements (corresponding to 15.9th to 84.1th percentile in a normal distribution) (42, 43) before and after excluding the eddy-related profiles (d < 2R). The same method is used to estimate the influence of eddies on regional mean and extremely high (low)-temperature anomalies.

To estimate eddy-associated potential temperature anomalies (θ′′) in a potential density (σ) coordinate, we first computed θ(z) and σ(z) from vertically gridded temperature (T(z)) and salinity [S(z)] profile measurements using seawater function. Then, θ(σ) and z(σ) are derived by interpolating θ(z) and z to gridded σ values ranging from 1,025 kg m−3 to 1,028 kg m−3 at an interval of 0.1 kg m−3, respectively. This process removes the component of temperature anomalies seen in a depth coordinate that is due purely to the uplift or depression of isopycnals (typically dominated by eddy pumping) (38). The remainders are thus the component of temperature anomalies dominated by cross-frontal eddy movement. The estimation of mean potential temperature anomalies within eddies on isopycnal surfaces is similar to the estimation of eddy-associated temperature anomalies in the depth coordinate:[8]θAE′′x,y,σ=θAE′x,y,σ-θBG′x,y,σ, [9]θCE′′x,y,σ=θCE′x,y,σ-θBG′x,y,σ.

The contribution of this component to the total temperature anomalies is estimated as:[10]PAEtrapx,y,z=θAE′′x,y,zTAE′′x,y,z×100%, [11]PCEtrapx,y,z=θCE′′x,y,zTCE′′x,y,z×100%,

where θ′x,y,z is estimated by interpolating the θ′x,y,σ into the depth coordinate using the information of zx,y,σ, respectively.

Trends, Significance, and Uncertainties.

For the trend plots, the statistic field is defined as a 10° latitude (~1,000 km) band centered at the ACC (red patch in the subplot in Fig. 4G). Although we have collected as many profile measurements as possible from multiple platforms, the data of about 200 profiles within each 2°×2° grid is relatively sparse considering the long observational period of nearly 20 y (Fig. 1A). We thus zoomed in on the statistic box and focused mainly on the ACC region where the thermal imprints of eddies are most apparent. We computed monthly series of mean and extreme temperature anomalies within eddies in the ACC band between 2006 and 2019 (Fig. 4 A and B). Data before 2006 are discarded because of poor sampling (<500 available profiles and <50 profiles in CEs (or AEs) per month, SI Appendix, Fig. S11). Before applying a linear least squares regression, the monthly anomalies are smoothed by a 12-mo running median filter to eliminate seasonal variations. The significance of the derived trends is detected using the nonparametric Mann–Kendall statistical test (46). The uncertainties of the estimates are assessed by the 95% CIs.

It is necessary to note that similar to the comparison of profile measurements within and outside eddies to isolate temperature (and other properties) anomalies induced by eddies (5, 63, 64), the comparison of regional mean and extreme temperature anomaly trends before and after excluding eddy-related profile measurements (Fig. 5) only reflects the contribution of the “presence of eddies” in an Eulerian framework. This is because the artificial removing of eddy-related profiles does not fully account for the heat flux resulting from eddy-background interactions such as eddy leakage, diffusion, and dissipation (65–67). These processes may mitigate the differences in water properties between eddies and the referenced background fields, leading to an underestimation of the effects of eddies. Quantifying the specific contribution of these eddy–background interactions is challenging with the currently available in situ observations. Direct comparisons between eddy-resolved models and parameterized models pose difficulties due to differences in the parameterization schemes employed. Continued research and advancements in observational techniques and modeling approaches are necessary to improve our understanding of the complex interactions between eddies and the ocean.
