Aortic isthmus flow reversal in fetal coarctation of the aorta and the associated factors
Authors: Guihong Chen, Yongfeng Han, Congxin Sun, Wei Xiang, Wei Zhao, Pei Zhou, Tianxiao Yu, Zhuo Chen, Bulang Gao, Shuping Ge, Jie Mi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe aim of this study was to investigate the presence of aortic isthmus flow reversal and its associated factors in fetuses with positive and false‐positive coarctation of the aorta (CoA) compared with normal controls.Material and MethodsPregnant women with fetuses suspected of CoA and normal control were enrolled, and these women experienced prenatal ultrasound scan and followed up for 6 months after birth to confirm the presence of CoA. All the ultrasound parameters were analyzed.ResultsA total of 134 pregnant women were enrolled, with 43 CoA‐positive fetuses and 91 CoA false‐positive fetuses, and 334 matched pregnant women were enrolled in the control group. Aortic isthmus flow reversal occurred in 28 (65.1%) fetuses in the CoA‐positive group, significantly (p < 0.05) more than in the false‐positive (37 or 40.7%) or control group (64 or 19.2%). Aortic isthmus flow reversal was mostly in the full systole (n = 17 or 60.7%) or late systole and early‐middle diastole (n = 10 or 35.7%) in the CoA‐positive fetuses (n = 27 or 96.4%), significantly (p < 0.001) different from that in the false‐positive or control group. The aortic isthmus flow reversal peak systolic velocity (PSV), flow volume, and ratio of reversed flow/forward flow were significantly (p < 0.05) increased in the CoA‐positive and false‐positive groups than in the control group. The aortic isthmus flow reversal incidence was significantly (p < 0.05) correlated with the middle cerebral artery (MCA) PSV in the total three groups or in the false‐positive group but was significantly (p < 0001) negatively correlated with the MCA resistance index (RI) in the CoA‐positive group. The incidence of the aortic isthmus flow reversal was significantly (p < 0.05) positively correlated with the umbilical artery (UA) RI in the false‐positive group and with the UA RI in the total three groups. Independently associated factors for aortic isthmus flow reversal were isthmic flow volume/CCO (combined cardiac output) in the CoA‐positive group.ConclusionsReversal of flow in the aortic isthmus is much more common in true‐positive cases of CoA as compared to controls, and isthmic flow reversal in the full systolic phase only suggests presence of CoA. The aortic isthmic reversed flow volume accounts for over half of the isthmic forward flow volume in the CoA‐positive fetuses than in the normal controls.

Key message

Flow reversal in the aortic isthmus is much more common and is over half of the isthmic forward flow in true CoA‐positive cases as compared to controls, and isthmic flow reversal in the full systolic phase only suggests CoA presence.

INTRODUCTION

In normal fetal cardio‐circulation, the right heart is dominant in Doppler studies, with the right ventricle supplying the systemic and placental blood circulation while the left ventricle essentially providing blood flow to the cerebral vascular network. 1 , 2 Compared with the neonates, the fetal circulation is unique because only a small proportion of the total heart output is directed toward the lungs, leading to limited venous return from the lungs to the left heart, which accounts for the right ventricular dominance in the fetus. 3 Moreover, there are two fetal arches, including the smaller aortic arch and the larger ductal arch, and insertion of the ductal arch into the descending aorta may cause coarctation of the aorta (CoA). The aortic isthmus is the only arterial connection between the right and left ventricles as a portion of the aorta located between the left subclavian artery origin and the junction point of the ductus arteriosus (DA) with the descending aorta, partially separating the fetal blood flow from the left and right ventricles. The isthmus blood flow represents the output flow of the two ventricles and the difference between placental and cerebral blood flow resistance. Therefore, the isthmic blood flow pattern reflects the balance between both ventricle outputs and differences in the resistance of both vascular systems. Because the effects of the outputs from the two fetal arterial arches are opposite on the isthmic flow, with a forward effect from the aortic arch but a retrograde effect from the pulmonary‐DA arch, changes in the relevant flow volume may alter the common flow pattern and direction in the isthmus on ultrasound examination. 2 , 4 The isthmic flow pattern may reflect progression of fetal hemodynamic deterioration in fetuses. Reversed blood flow through the foramen ovale and retrograde flow in the aortic arch have been revealed as sensitive predictors of severe left‐sided structural cardiac diseases, 5 , 6 and direct imaging observation of the fetal aortic arch may be the most reliable approach to predict postnatal CoA. 7 , 8 CoA is one of the commonest congenital cardiac diseases in neonates and children, possibly requiring immediate management and even surgery after birth. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Antenatal diagnosis of CoA lacks sensitivity and specificity, and fewer than one‐third of cases were detected through antenatal ultrasound examination. 14 , 15 False diagnosis of CoA may lead to parental anxiety and change of delivery plans for antenatal care, and unfortunately, a high false‐positive rate exists for prenatal diagnosis of fetal CoA using standard parameters. 15 , 16 Reversal of blood flow in the aortic arch, especially in the aortic isthmus, is not completely understood, especially in fetuses with CoA. Nonetheless, the degree and cardiac phases of aortic isthmus flow reversal and its associated factors are currently unknown and might be valuable for antenatal prediction of postnatal CoA, and this study was consequently conducted to investigate aortic isthmic flow reversal and its associated factors in CoA‐positive and false‐positive fetuses in comparison with normal controls.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Subjects

This one‐center study was performed between June 2021 and June 2023. The inclusion criteria were pregnant women with suspected fetal CoA but no comorbidities or other serious intracardiac and extracardiac malformations, normal menstruation, gestational age 20–40 weeks, antenatal ultrasound and physical examinations, delivery with a single fetus in our hospital, and 6‐month follow‐up after birth for confirmation of CoA in the baby by ultrasound, computed tomographic angiography, surgery, or autopsies on induced fetuses. Fetuses which were referred for suspected CoA but were confirmed to have no CoA during further check‐ups of ultrasound before and after birth were enrolled into the false‐positive group. For suspected fetal CoA, multiple ultrasound check‐ups were performed for confirmation. For normal fetuses, the inclusion criteria were those with no abnormalities through ultrasound examination and being healthy after birth in the same study period. For fetuses suspected of CoA, the inclusion criteria included the ratio of DA inner diameter to aortic isthmus inner diameter >1.3 and narrowed diameter of the aortic isthmus (Z‐score < −2) with or without aortic arch dysplasia (transverse arch diameter <1/2 of the abdominal aortic diameter). The exclusion criteria were pregnant women with baseline and hereditary diseases, twin pregnancy, abnormal non‐invasive prenatal test results, pregnancy complications, irregular menstrual cycles, unknown last menstrual cycle, fetuses with concomitant severe intracardiac abnormalities or aortic branch variation, or incomplete antenatal and postpartum follow‐up data. Because a high false‐positive rate existed for prenatal diagnosis of fetal CoA using standard parameters 15 , 16 and an unacceptably large proportion of fetuses which did not eventually require neonatal surgery underwent multiple ultrasound scans during the pregnancy, especially in the late gestation, pregnant women with the gestational age 20–40 weeks were included.

Instruments and methods

The GE Voluson E8 and E10 Color Doppler ultrasound diagnostic instruments (Austria) were applied, with 2D transabdominal probes (model C1‐6‐D, frequency 2‐5 MHz, and power <100 mW/cm2). Routine ultrasound scan and fetal echocardiography were carried out to assess the fetal growth, weight, condition, and possible intra‐ and extracardiac abnormalities. Measurement of the ultrasound images was performed by two experienced ultrasound physicians with over 10 years of experience, and if disagreement arose, a third senior researcher was involved to reach a consensus.

Echocardiographic parameters

The following ultrasound parameters were measured (Figure 1), including the peak systolic velocity (PSV) and velocity time integral (VTI), pulsatility index (PI) and resistance index (RI), left and right ventricular dimension at the end diastole, mitral valve E‐ and A‐peak flow velocity (MV‐E and MV‐A), tricuspid valve E and A peak (TV‐E and TV‐A) in four‐chamber view, aortic diameter at the aortic annulus at the outflow tract section, transverse arch diameter and aortic isthmus at the three vessel trachea view (3VTV), aortic arch diameter just before the brachiocephalic artery origin, aortic isthmus diameter and descending aorta (DAO) diameter at the sagittal view (SV), DA diameter, pulmonary artery (PA), main pulmonary artery (MPA) at the outflow tract section, flow parameters (velocity, peak systolic velocity or PSV, velocity time integral or VTI, flow volume), left cardiac output (LCO), right cardiac output (RCO) or PA flow volume, and combined cardiac output (CCO = LCO + RCO). The flow volume of each valve orifice or artery was computed in line with the formula Q = (D/2)2 × 3.14 × VTI × HR (immediate heart rate). For the isthmus blood flow spectrum, the manual envelope mode was selected to obtain the respective PSV and VTI of the total, systolic, diastolic and reverse flow, and the reversed flow phase was divided into six categories (Figure 2): none, late systole plus early (and mid)‐diastole, full systole, full systole and full diastole, late systole and full diastole, and early (and middle) diastole.

According to the guidelines and recommendations for performance of the fetal echocardiogram, 17 the ascending aortic inner diameter was measured 0.5–1 cm above the systolic aortic valve in the left ventricular outflow tract section, the PA inner diameter was measured at the pulmonary valve annulus (with the maximal diameter between the inner edges of the pulmonary valve attachment points during systole when the pulmonary valve was opened in the right ventricular outflow tract section or the short axis section of the great artery), the MPA inner diameter was measured at the systolic pulmonary valve in the right ventricular outflow tract section or the short axis section of the great artery, and the descending aortic inner diameter was measured at the descending aorta after confluence of the aortic isthmus and the DA.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted with the JMP software (10.01.2, SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Continuous measurement data meeting the normal distribution were presented in the mean and standard deviation and tested with the t‐test or in the median and interquartile range if not meeting the normal distribution and tested with the Mann–Whitney U‐test. Categorical data were presented in frequencies and percentages and tested with Chi‐square test. The case–control matching function was used to match the age and gestational age of the pregnant women between different groups. Associated factors for the presence of aortic isthmic flow reversal were detected with the univariate logistic regression analysis, and significant parameters in the univariate analysis after elimination of confounding factors were used for multivariate logistic regression analysis for independent factors of isthmic flow reversal. The statistically significant p level was set at <0.05.

The age and gestational age of the pregnant women and the fetal biological parameters were matched (p > 0.05) between the CoA‐positive and false‐positive groups (Table 1). In the control normal group, 334 pregnant women with matched age, gestational age, and fetal biological parameters were enrolled, with normal fetuses confirmed after birth.

Aortic isthmus flow reversal occurred in 28 (65.1%) fetuses in the CoA‐positive group, 37 (40.7%) in the false‐positive group, and 64 (19.2%) in the control group, with a significantly (p < 0.05) greater incidence in the positive and false‐positive group than in the control (Table 1). Most aortic isthmus flow reversal took place in the late systole and early‐middle diastole in the control (n = 56 or 87.5%), in the full systole (n = 17 or 60.7%), or the late systole and early‐middle diastole (n = 10 or 35.7%) in the CoA‐positive fetuses (n = 27 or 96.4%), and in the late systole and early‐middle diastole in the false‐positive fetuses (n = 29 or 78.4%), with a significant (p < 0.05) difference between any two groups. No CoA‐positive fetuses exhibited isthmic flow reversal in the late systole + full diastole or early diastole, whereas no normal or false‐positive fetuses exhibited isthmic flow reversal in the full systole. The aortic isthmus flow reversal PSV, VTI, flow volume, and ratio of reversed flow/forward flow were significantly (p < 0.05) increased in the CoA‐positive and false‐positive groups than in the control, and the aortic isthmus flow reversal flow volume was significantly greater in the false positive than in the CoA‐positive group (Table 1). The isthmic reversed flow volume accounted for over half of the isthmic forward flow volume in both the CoA‐positive and false‐positive fetuses than in the control.

The MCA PSV and RI decreased significantly (p < 0.05) in CoA‐positive fetuses than in the control or false‐positive group (Table 1). The umbilical artery (UA) PSV was significantly (p < 0.05) decreased while the RI was significantly (p < 0.05) increased in the CoA‐positive fetuses than in the control or the false‐positive group (Table 1). Compared with the normal control, the CoA‐positive fetuses showed significant (p < 0.05) decreases in the left ventricular dimension, aortic parameters (diameter and flow velocity), LCO, transverse aortic arch diameter, arch diameter, isthmus diameter, isthmus flow volume, and its ratio to the LCO or CCO (Tables 2, 3, 4). A significant (p < 0.05) increase was shown in the right/left ventricle ratio, DA diameter, and flow volume in the CoA‐positive fetuses compared with those in the normal control. The prevalence of aortic isthmic flow reversal was significantly (p < 0.001) negatively correlated with the MCA PSV (r = 0.49) and VTI (r = 0.50), RV/LV ratio (r = 0.36), and DAO flow volume (r = 0.41) in the total patients. No other significant (r < 0.03 and p > 0.05) correlation existed in the incidence of aortic isthmic flow reversal with the data in the total three groups, CoA‐positive or false‐positive group. In the total patients, the aortic isthmic flow was in a significant positive weak correlation with MCA PSV (r = 0.55, p < 0.0001) but in a significant negative weak correlation with UA RI (r = 0.46, p < 0.001). No other correlation existed in the aortic isthmic flow with the data of MCA or UA in the CoA‐positive or false‐positive group.

DISCUSSION

In this study investigating the presence of aortic isthmus flow reversal and its associated factors in CoA‐positive and false‐positive fetuses and normal controls, significant differences were detected in the ultrasound parameters between the normal control and the CoA‐positive or false‐positive fetuses, and significantly more isthmus flow reversal occurred in CoA‐positive and false‐positive fetuses than in the control. Right/left ventricular or atrial ratio, DAO flow volume, and isthmic flow volume/combined cardiac output ratio may significantly independently influence the presence of aortic isthmus flow reversal.

In normal fetuses, a brief end‐systolic notching has been reported in the flow velocity waveform at the isthmus level after 20 weeks of pregnancy, 18 and this velocity deceleration wave rises with advancement of gestation until a brief flow reversal at approximately 30 weeks of gestation. 18 , 19 The bidirectional flow pattern at the level of fetal aortic isthmus happens at two different times in the same cardiac cycle, with the forward flow appearing firstly over 2/3 of the systolic phase and the reversed flow wave appearing very late in the same cycle. The right ventricular output has a significant influence on the isthmus flow pattern in the late systolic phase. 19 , 20 , 21 These bidirectional flow pattern can only be observed on the sagittal view of the aortic isthmus between the left subclavian artery origin and the junction point of the DA with the descending aorta. This late systolic flow reversal in the fetal aortic isthmus can be explained by two physiological events: either a fall in cerebrovascular resistance or dominance of the right ventricle, which occur during the third trimester of gestation. 19 Decreased cerebrovascular resistance will drive blood flow to reverse via the isthmus during the systolic phase, but increased cerebrovascular resistance and fetal hypoxemia will cause cerebral vasodilation to promote an increase of blood flow from the left ventricle to the brain, 1 thus being able to eliminate any systolic reversal effect on the downstream isthmic flow. In fetuses, increased cerebrovascular flow caused by decreased cerebrovascular resistance is always associated with an isolated increase in the preload and stroke volume of the right ventricle because raised cerebral venous return is drained entirely through the superior vena cava into the right ventricle. A major part of the increased output of the right ventricle is pumped via the DA connecting the distal end of the isthmus and thus, exerts a systolic reversal effect on the isthmic flow.

The most likely explanation for the isthmic retrograde flow in the late systolic phase is probably related to the varied compliance of the fetal pulmonary arterial network. 19 In the fetal branch pulmonary arteries, a typical flow pattern is described as characterized by a quick systolic upstroke, a short acceleration time, and a late‐systolic flow reversal. 22 This end‐systolic flow reversal is attributed to the low compliance of the branch pulmonary arteries and the high peripheral pulmonary vascular resistance during the fetal period. 19 , 22 The greater the right ventricular output, the more significant the systolic flow reversal from the pulmonary arteries to the DA and the aortic isthmus. Thus, dropped left ventricular output and/or elevated right ventricular output may potentially decrease the systolic forward isthmic flow and cause flow reversal, depending on the extent of forward flow reduction across the aortic arch. The peripheral vascular resistance also has some potential effects on the systemic venous return and ventricular output through altering the ventricular preloads, especially for the supradiaphragmatic circulation, whose venous drain is almost exclusively via the superior vena cava. A cerebral arteriovenous fistula has been reported to cause excessive blood drainage through the superior vena cava and subsequently, systolic retrograde flow in the isthmus, 20 and cerebral arterial vasodilation like in the placental circulatory insufficiency may also cause systolic flow reversal in the isthmus.

In our study, a significantly greater incidence of aortic isthmus flow reversal occurred in the CoA‐positive fetuses than in the false positive or normal fetuses, and most aortic isthmus flow reversal was present in the full systolic phase in the CoA‐positive fetuses but in the late systole and early‐middle diastole in the false positive and normal fetuses. Most isthmus flow reversal (60.7%) took place in the full systolic phase only in the CoA‐positive fetuses, whereas no aortic isthmus flow reversal was present in the full systolic phase only in the false positive or normal fetuses, which can be used as a marker to diagnose CoA.

In our study, the left ventricular diameter was significantly decreased while the right/left ventricular diameter ratio was significantly increased in CoA‐positive and false‐positive fetuses compared with the normal controls. Moreover, the MCA RI was significantly decreased in CoA‐positive fetuses compared with the false positive or normal fetuses. Based on the above analysis, these factors in the CoA‐positive and false‐positive fetuses will cause significantly increased incidences of aortic isthmus flow reversal in the CoA‐positive and false‐positive fetuses compared with the normal controls, and the relevant aortic isthmus flow reversal parameters were also significantly raised in the CoA‐positive and false‐positive fetuses as shown in our study. Our study also revealed a significantly reduced diameter of the aorta at the annulus, aortic arch, and aortic isthmus but increased diameter of DA in CoA‐positive fetuses compared with the normal controls or the false‐positive fetuses. Reduced aortic, arch, and isthmic diameter will significantly decrease the blood flow derived from the left ventricle through the aortic isthmus, whereas increased DA diameter will promote blood flow from the right ventricle to the isthmus and descending aorta, consequently contributing to aortic isthmus flow reversal in CoA‐positive fetuses based on the above analysis.

The blood flow pattern in the aortic isthmus has been suggested as an indicator of deterioration of fetal hemodynamics, especially for the prediction of short‐term adverse perinatal and long‐term neurodevelopmental outcomes. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 In normal fetuses, placental resistance is smaller than that in the upper parts of the fetal circulatory system, and the movement of blood flow in the isthmus is antegrade to the DAO. Increases in the placental resistance in fetuses with placental insufficiency reverse the flow direction through the isthmus and precede hemodynamic decompensation, indicating a pathology. 23 , 26 , 27 Nonetheless, in normal fetal conditions, the aortic isthmus may show a short diastolic reversal flow velocity waveform during the last trimester of pregnancy, which may represent a normal physiological increase in the fetal systemic and placental vascular resistances as long as the fetal net isthmic blood flow is antegrade. 18 , 28 Nonetheless, net retrograde blood flow in the isthmus may suggest a relative decrease of oxygen content in the blood ejected from the left ventricle, especially the blood sent to the cerebral and coronary circulation. 29 In our study, the ratio of the aortic isthmus flow reversal flow volume to the antegrade flow was significantly increased in both the CoA‐positive and false‐positive groups compared with the normal controls, but the ratios remained less than 1 in all three groups, indicating a net antegrade flow in the isthmus.

Our study has revealed that the UA PSV was significantly (p < 0.05) decreased while the UA RI was significantly (p < 0.05) increased in the CoA‐positive fetuses than in the normal control or the false‐positive group, and the incidence of the aortic isthmus flow reversal was significantly (p < 0.05) positively correlated with the UA RI in the CoA false‐positive group and with the UA RI in the total three groups. The UA RI was a significant factor to promote aortic isthmic flow reversal in the false‐positive group, and the UA RI was a significant factor for isthmic flow reversal in the normal control. The UA parameters were not risk factors for isthmic flow reversal in the CoA‐positive group. In multivariate analysis, none of the UA parameters were revealed to be significant independent risk factors for the presence of aortic isthmic flow reversal.

In the true and false CoA‐positive fetuses, the isthmic PSV and flow velocity and volume in both the systole and diastole were all significantly decreased compared with the normal controls. Decreased flow velocity in the aortic isthmus has also been observed in the fetuses with intrauterine growth restriction, 30 which may imply a response to placental insufficiency as well as an association with increased afterload of the fetal heart, causing a rise in ventricular pressure and wall tension and an increased need for oxygen by the myocardium.

The MCA PSV and RI were found to be significantly (p < 0.05) positively correlated with the gestational age in our study, and the aortic isthmus flow reversal incidence was significantly (p < 0.001) negatively correlated with the MCA PSV in the total three groups or in the false‐positive group but significantly (p < 0001) negatively correlated with the MCA RI in the CoA‐positive group. With increase of the gestational age, the MCA RI firstly rises and then decreases, and in correspondence, the incidence of aortic isthmus flow reversal may firstly decrease and then increase.

Although many associated factors for isthmic flow reversal were revealed in the univariate analysis, significant independent associated factors were revealed to be right/left ventricular or atrial ratio, isthmic flow volume/CCO, and DAO flow volume in the false‐positive group, isthmic flow volume/CCO in the CoA‐positive group, and right/left atrium size, isthmic flow volume/CCO, and DAO flow volume in the normal control. The effects of all these factors to independently affect the presence of aortic isthmic flow reversal may be related to the increase or decrease of the right or left ventricular output.

Some limitations existed in this study, including one‐center study design, a small cohort of individuals, Chinese participants enrolled only, and no randomization, which may all produce a publication bias. Future randomized, controlled, multi‐center studies will have to be conducted involving multiple races and ethnicities for better outcomes.

CONCLUSION

To sum up, reversal of flow in the aortic isthmus is much more common in true‐positive cases of CoA as compared to controls. Most aortic isthmus flow reversal takes place in the full systole or the late systole and early‐middle diastole rather than in the late systole and full diastole or early diastole in the CoA‐positive fetuses, and isthmic flow reversal in the full systolic phase only suggests CoA presence. The aortic isthmic reversed flow volume accounts for over half of the isthmic forward flow volume in the CoA‐positive fetuses than in the normal controls. CoA‐negative fetuses may exhibit isthmic flow reversal in the late systole and full diastole or early diastole. These unique characteristics can be used to differentiate CoA‐positive cases from CoA‐negative cases.
