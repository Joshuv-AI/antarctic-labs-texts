Dental age estimation in children and adolescents with amelogenesis imperfecta
Authors: Selin Saygili, Sedef Ayse Tasyapan, Roberto Cameriere, Hulya Cakir Karabas, Mine Koruyucu, Yelda Kasimoglu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConsidering the difficulties presented by changed tooth growth patterns in amelogenesis imperfecta (AI), which might influence clinical decisions and treatment planning, the purpose of this study is to assess the accuracy of dental age assessment in Turkish children with AI in comparison to controls.MethodsA total of 416 Turkish children, ages 5 to 13.99, had their panoramic images examined in this retrospective study (104 with AI and 312 controls, 1:3 ratio). The London Atlas method and Cameriere’s European formula were used to estimate dental age. To improve accuracy, a new regression equation was created, and the outcomes were compared using statistical analysis.ResultsChronologic age and Cameriere’s European formula did not differ statistically significantly in either group (p = 0.226). However, there were statistically significant variations (p < 0.001) in the dental age estimates from the London Atlas approach, which were overstated by roughly 0.39–0.69 years in both groups. Using Cameriere’s formula, the new regression equation described 90.8% of the deviance in children with AI, while the London Atlas technique explained 83.0%.ConclusionsAccurate dental age estimation is crucial for children with AI, and the findings emphasize the superiority of Cameriere’s European formula over the London Atlas method, reinforcing the need for reliable techniques tailored to this unique population.Supplementary Information The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06797-3.

Introduction

Dental tissues, particularly enamel and dentin, provide valuable indicators of age due to their progressive and relatively predictable development patterns [1]. Throughout childhood and adolescence, changes in dental morphology and mineralization offer a reliable basis for estimating an individual’s chronological age [2]. These characteristics make dental development an essential focus in both clinical and forensic age estimation practices [3].

Although developmental enamel defects may be caused by systemic and genetic factors that can influence individual growth and, consequently, dental age, very few studies have evaluated the relationship between these defects and tooth development, one of which is the study by Tunç et al. [4]. They hypothesized that tooth development may be slower in hypomineralized enamel and that root growth may also be affected by this condition.

Amelogenesis imperfecta (AI), which is one of the developmental defects of enamel, is also known as enamel hypoplasia/hypomineralization.5 [5], AI is a rare genetic disease that affects the structure, quantity, and composition of enamel in both primary and permanent teeth [6]. Dental anomalies and nephrocalcinosis have also been reported to be associated with AI [7]. These dental anomalies and nephrocalcinosis can affect tooth development, and there are limited studies examining tooth development in individuals with AI [8, 9].

Research on tooth development in children with amelogenesis imperfecta — a developmental enamel defect characterized by genetically driven hypomineralization — remains scarce. Findings from Seow [9] suggested that dental development may be accelerated in children affected by this condition. In contrast, Kirzioglu et al. [10] compared children with and without amelogenesis imperfecta using three different dental age estimation methods and concluded that the condition had no significant impact on tooth development or dental age. Similarly, Gabardo et al. [11]employing both the Demirjian and Willems methods, found no notable difference in dental maturation between affected and unaffected children.

Considering the impact of conditions such as AI on tooth development, understanding its influence on dental age assessment becomes critical for pediatric dentists, forensic dentists, and orthodontists. Delays in tooth development can be associated with growth retardation and learning difficulties, making dental age estimation valuable not only for forensic purposes in children with uncertain birth records, but also for guiding appropriate dental treatment planning through accurate assessment of maturation in growing individuals [12]. Given the complexities involved, especially in individuals affected by conditions such as AI, the necessity of employing reliable methods is paramount. There are two primary approaches for determining dental age in children and adolescents: evaluating the eruption of teeth in the oral cavity and assessing the development and mineralization of crowns and roots through dental radiographs [13].

One of the widely used methods for dental age estimation is the London Atlas, introduced by AlQahtani et al. in 2010 [14]. This atlas provides schematic illustrations of dentition development at various ages, allowing practitioners to estimate age by matching a patient’s panoramic radiograph with the closest reference image. Another commonly used method is the European formula developed by Cameriere et al., [15] which calculates dental age based on the measurement of open apices in developing teeth. This approach has been tested across multiple populations and has shown high accuracy and reliability [16, 17].

Only a small number of studies have examined the potential impact of AI on the dental development of affected children in comparison with those without AI [9, 18]. This study aimed to evaluate the applicability of two different methods for estimating dental ages in children with AI in comparison to controls. Additionally, we aim to compare the dental age estimates with the chronological age in both children with AI and controls. Based on a literature review, the following hypotheses are proposed: (1) There would be no statistically significant difference in dental age and chronological age between children with AI and controls. (2) There would be no significant difference between dental ages determined using the Cameriere’s European formula and the London Atlas.

Materials and methods

This study received ethical approval from Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Istanbul University (Approval No. 2021/76). All procedures adhered to the committee’s applicable guidelines and standards and were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This retrospective study contains no identifying images or personal or clinical details that compromise anonymity. All radiographs were obtained as part of routine clinical care, for which informed consent had already been obtained from the participants’ parents or legal guardians at the time of image acquisition.

According to certain criteria, panoramic radiographs were included in this retrospective, double-blind, case-control study.

104 children with AI, 53 boys and 51 girls, ages 5 to 13.99, who presented to the Department of Pedodontics, Faculty of Dentistry, Istanbul University, were involved in the study. 159 boys and 153 girls made up the control group, which was chosen at random from among healthy people who had visited the clinic and given their consent. For a variety of clinical purposes, the control group’s panoramic radiographs were acquired.

After being anonymised, the researchers received the gathered panoramic radiographs and were only given the child’s gender. The age of the subjects or any other clinical information was unknown to the researchers when they performed their analysis.

Chronological age

The chronological age of the individuals was determined using a formula in Microsoft Excel, which subtracted the recorded date of birth (A) from the date that the panoramic radiograph was taken (B), and then the result was divided by 365. This calculation was performed in the decimal system to obtain the age in years.

London atlas method

In the London Atlas method, the development and eruption stage of the teeth were evaluated for each gender shown in the colored diagrams on the software [19]. As a result of the selections made for each tooth, the dental age was determined.

Cameriere’s European formula

The apex of the left seven permanent mandibular teeth (excluding the third molar) were evaluated using Cameriere’s European formula [20]. The number of teeth with closed apex (N0) was noted. In open apex teeth, the distance between the interior of the open apex was measured in single-rooted teeth (Ai, i = 1,., 5), and in multi-rooted teeth, the measurements for each root were summed (Ai, i = 6, 7). To evaluate the effect of possible differences between the magnification and angulation of X-rays, the normalized scores of each tooth (Xi = Ai/Li) were calculated by calculating the tooth length (L) with distance (A). The sum of the normalized scores of the teeth with open apex (s = X1 + X2+.+X7) was obtained. After these components were obtained, the calculation was made using the Cameriere’s dental age determination regression model shown below. In the gender (g) part of the formula, the number “1” for boys and “0” for girls was entered.

Calibration

Radiographs were evaluated by two specialists. The reliability of evaluations between different examiners was determined by having two researchers independently review all the radiographs. An intra- and inter-examiner reliability assessment was performed on a sample of 20 panoramic radiographs randomly selected from control group and 20 individuals with AI. These radiographs were reviewed a second time 2 weeks later by the same researchers. There were no significant differences between the measurements taken by different observers (inter-observer) and by the same observer at different times (intra-observer) for the paired sets of measurements on the re-examined panoramic radiographs. All measurements showed Cronbach’s Alpha values exceeding 0.9, indicating strong inter- and intra-examiner consistency.

Statistical analysis

Data for this study were collected through clinical research and entered Microsoft Excel for preliminary processing. After thorough editing and cleaning, the data were analyzed using various statistical methods. Frequency analysis was used for distribution assessment, intraclass correlation (ICC) for reliability checks, the Mann-Whitney U Test for comparing two independent samples, and Friedman (Wilcoxon) tests for multiple related samples. All analyses were conducted using the IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 software package. Additionally, a new regression equation was developed to further analyze the data.

Discussion

Due to environmental factors impacting skeletal age variability, methods that focus on dental development for age estimation are deemed to be more dependable [10]. There are clinical, morphologic, and radiologic methods employed to determine dental age, with radiologic evaluation of developing teeth being a common approach for assessing the dental age of children [21].

In this study, digital panoramic radiographs were used to assess age, following the approach of Bosmans et al., [22] in contrast to the original work by Kvaal et al., [23] which relied on conventional intraoral periapical radiographs to establish the regression formulas. It is well recognized that images of anterior teeth in panoramic radiographs are prone to inherent distortion compared to conventional intraoral periapical radiographs. This distortion, resulting from the projection geometry of panoramic imaging, represents a potential limitation in the use of digital panoramic radiographs for accurate assessment. Nevertheless, this method also presents the unique advantage of allowing for the evaluation of all teeth and the alveolar bone in both jaws, with all necessary measurements being obtainable from a single radiograph. This highlights the importance of using radiographic techniques that are both reproducible and accurate.

This study focused on two commonly used methods for dental age estimation: the London Atlas and Cameriere’s European formula. While both are widely accepted and clinically relevant, the exclusion of additional estimation methods may limit the breadth of comparison. Including a third method in future studies could provide a broader perspective and improve the generalizability of findings, particularly in populations with dental anomalies such as AI.

In 2006, Cameriere et al. [20] introduced a linear regression model for estimating dental age that used comparable measurements. The London Atlas method created by Alqahtani et al. in 2010 employs both an atlas and a software application to deduce chronologic age by evaluating the developmental and eruption stages of all upper and lower teeth on the right side [14].

As with many retrospective studies, our design limited the ability to control for potential confounding variables such as nutritional status, systemic health conditions, or environmental influences that may affect dental development. These unmeasured factors could have influenced the findings, and future prospective studies with broader data collection are recommended to address this limitation.

Another limitation is the absence of an external validation cohort to test the generalizability of our regression models. While our findings demonstrated strong internal consistency, further research using independent datasets is necessary to confirm the applicability of these methods across different populations and clinical settings.

Teeth, with their unique structural characteristics, play a crucial role in identification processes. AI is of particular significance in dentistry due to its pathologic impact on enamel and associated clinical features [24]. In addition to enamel defects, children with AI may exhibit other anomalies, such as issues with tooth eruption and pulpal calcifications [25]. Early detection and treatment of nephrocalcinosis, characterized by calcium deposits in the kidneys and potentially occurring in children with AI, can be facilitated by monitoring signs of enamel defects, tooth eruption issues, and pulpal calcifications [10]. There is a scarcity of research on tooth development in children with enamel defects, and there is no agreed-upon view on this matter [26].

During this study, which compared children with AI and controls in terms of dental age using Cameriere’s European formula, no differences were detected between the two groups. Despite the lack of observable differences, we believe that factors potentially leading to tooth eruption issues, such as impacted teeth and the premature removal of irreparable primary teeth, could locally influence tooth development.

Our findings revealed that there were no significant differences in dental age estimation between children with AI and the control group when using Cameriere’s formula. This method proved to be robust and closely aligned with the chronological age, even in cases affected by enamel defects. However, the London Atlas method showed a statistically significant discrepancy in children with AI, underscoring the challenges dental anomalies pose to accurate age estimation outcomes.

Children with AI frequently undergo early extraction of deciduous or even permanent teeth due to enamel defects, which can disrupt the expected eruption timeline. Since the London Atlas method relies on eruption and morphological staging, such disruptions may reduce its accuracy in this population. In contrast, Camerieres method evaluates root development — a process that continues independently of eruption — making it more reliable in cases where the eruption sequence is altered. This distinction highlights one of the key advantages of dental development-based methods over morphology-based approaches in patients with structural dental anomalies.

This disparity in results between children with AI and controls suggests that the choice of method for age estimation can have a significant impact on accuracy, depending on the health status of the subjects. The higher accuracy in controls using the London Atlas method could be attributed to the standardized developmental patterns in this group, whereas the varied developmental stages in children with AI might be better captured using Cameriere’s formula. One possible explanation for this reduced accuracy is that the London Atlas is morphology-based and relies on visual staging of tooth development. In AI cases, enamel anomalies such as hypoplasia or hypomineralization may obscure key morphological features, leading to errors in age estimation. These findings highlight the need for tailored approaches in age estimation practices, especially when dealing with pathologic conditions such as AI.

The variation in the percentage of deviation explained by the regression equations across different groups is noteworthy. These R² values reflect not only differences in accuracy but also in predictive consistency between methods. Cameriere’s formula showed greater predictive reliability in the AI group (R² = 0.908), while the London Atlas demonstrated slightly higher explanatory power in the control group (R² = 0.913), highlighting method-specific performance variability across populations. Specifically, for children with AI, the London Atlas explained 83.0% of the deviation (R² = 0.83) whereas Cameriere’s European formula accounted for 90.8% (R² = 0.908). among controls, the London Atlas method explained 91.3% (R² = 0.913), while Cameriere’s formula explained slightly less, at 86.9% (R² = 0.869).

Anticipating potential differences in dental development among children with AI, the study applied two widely used dental age estimation methods -Cameriere’s European formula [20] and London Atlas [14]- and compared to results with those of healthy controls. The regression models developed in this study enable the calculation of dental age, similar to the methods used in the research by Diz et al. [27] This approach allows for the estimation of the chronologic age of Turkish children.

Studies conducted in Turkey shown that the Cameriere’s European formula provides reliable age estimations in the Turkish population. While the Demirjian method, when used its original French-Canadian reference tables, may produce less accurate results, its performance improves significantly when adapted with population-specific standards [28, 29]. Gelbrich et al. [30] found that combining the Willems and London Atlas methods provided more accurate results than using a single method for dental age estimation. Koç et al., [31] in their 2021 study in Turkey, reported that the most reliable methods for age estimation were the Willems method (average estimate error of 0.80), the London Atlas method (average estimate error of 0.83), and the Nolla method (average estimate error of 0.89). The authors indicated that the accuracy of Cameriere’s European formula in estimating the dental age of children with AI closely aligned with their chronological age, without significant statistical variance. However, a statistical difference was observed between the chronological age and the ages estimated using the London Atlas.

The variations in study outcomes may stem from factors such as geographic location, ethnic diversity, climatic alterations due to global warming, evolving habits influenced by technological advancements, nutritional conditions, as well as the sample size and statistical methods employed in these studies. Reflecting on these aspects in the context of recent research, our findings revealed that although Cameriere’s European formula aligned closely with chronologic age, indicating no statistical difference, there was a notable statistical discrepancy when using the London Atlas to estimate chronological age. This underscores the reliability of Cameriere’s European formula in age estimation and suggests its preferential use in similar contexts.

The study further emphasizes the necessity of tailoring dental age estimation methods to specific populations. Factors such as geographic and ethnic diversity, nutritional conditions, and evolving habits contribute to variability in dental development, highlighting the importance of using population-specific approaches.

Crucially, Cameriere’s European formula [20], along with other prevalent techniques in the field [14, 32, 33], exhibits increased precision for younger individuals but a noticeable increase in variability after 12 years of age. Specifically, there is a notable reduction in both the accuracy and precision of age estimation methods as they apply to the later years of adolescence. Many studies exclude individuals aged over 15 years [16, 34]. In this scenario, the London Atlas stands out with its exceptional accuracy and consistency. It includes the third molar in its analysis and offers annual age representations up to 23 years. This makes it particularly valuable for age estimation in older adolescents. Additionally, the London Atlas is beneficial in cases involving younger individuals where gender cannot be determined, providing both gender-specific and gender-neutral dental charts.

The London Atlas is a useful resource for determining an adolescent’s age, but it has drawbacks. The fact that each category is assigned a set midpoint age, eliminating a range of ages, is a major disadvantage that limits the flexibility of age estimate. Even though the atlas offers thorough, tooth-specific images, there may be notable differences between an individual’s teeth and these standardized depictions due to the inherent heterogeneity in dental growth. It frequently happens that some teeth develop faster or later than the average shown in the atlas. Furthermore, there are no explicit rules in the atlas for properly evaluating people who exhibit such developmental anomalies. The lack of standardized scoring criteria increases the possibility of inter-rater variability by introducing discrepancies, especially among researchers with varying degrees of experience and skill. This study shows that the London Atlas considerably increases the accuracy of age estimate when compared to alternative dental charts, despite these drawbacks. It also provides a more accessible and useful substitute for other, more complicated techniques.

Our study highlights the effectiveness and reliability of Cameriere’s European formulain comparison to the morphology-based London Atlas method. While dental panoramic radiograph have certain limitations, their ability to provide comprehensive assessments of all teeth and alveolar bone from a single radiograph makes them invaluable in dental age estimation outcomes.

When comparing the development of teeth affected by AI, it was found that AI’s impact on the enamel structure had no significant effect on determining dental age. The use of Cameriere’s European formula may help reduce discrepancy in dental age assessments. Further research is warranted to investigate on different defects that may influence the tooth’s structure and shape.

Furthermore, while chronological age in this study was calculated by dividing the total number of days by 365, which is standard in many age estimation studies, this method does not account for leap years. Using 365.25 days as the denominator may improve precision, particularly in studies involving narrow age ranges or requiring fine-grained temporal accuracy. Future research aiming for higher precision may benefit from adopting this refinement in age calculation methodology.

Given the increased variability in dental age estimation methods during adolescence, integrating tools like the London Atlas for older age groups and Cameriere’s formula for younger individuals may offer the best accuracy. Furthermore, future research should explore the effects of other dental and systemic conditions on tooth development to refine and validate these methods further.

Although our findings showed no significant differences in dental age estimation using Cameriere’s method, the London Atlas method resulted in overestimations in children with AI. This may not necessarily indicate a delay in dental maturation, but rather the limitations of morphology-based staging in the presence of enamel defects. Since the London Atlas relies on visual assessment of crown and root development stages, enamel anomalies such as hypoplasia or hypomineralization may obscure morphological landmarks and affect staging accuracy. In contrast, Cameriere’s method evaluates root development and apical closure, which may be less influenced by enamel quality. These findings highlight the importance of selecting appropriate methods for individuals with enamel anomalies, particularly in legal or clinical contexts where accurate age estimation is crucial.

Given that distinct populations exhibit unique developmental traits, it is advisable to tailor any dental age estimation methods to specific groups through further research using more extensive and consistent samples.

Conclusions

This study highlights the reliability of Cameriere’s European formula in dental age estimation for Turkish children, including those with AI. Even in the presence of enamel defects, Cameriere’s technique closely matched chronological age, in contrast to the London Atlas, which displayed notable differences in situations involving AI. These results highlight the necessity of population-specific age estimation techniques as well as more study on the effects of systemic diseases and dental anomalies to improve existing techniques.
