Cinacalcet and Parathyroidectomy Outcomes in Symptomatic and Asymptomatic Primary Hyperparathyroidism: A Retrospective Observational Study
Authors: Aditya Dutta, Praphulla Koirala, Nupoor Vaghasia, Kajal Allawadi, Ambrish Mithal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is a common and symptomatic endocrine disorder in India. There has been a notable rise in asymptomatic PHPT (urban India), creating a dilemma in optimal treatment approach—surgery or medical management. Cinacalcet is a calcimimetic used in managing PHPT, but data regarding its use from India is limited. This retrospective study aimed to assess the prevalence of asymptomatic PHPT, compare symptomatic versus asymptomatic PHPT and evaluate the real-world outcomes among patients managed with parathyroidectomy (PTX) or cinacalcet.Methods:A retrospective analysis was conducted on 90 PHPT patients seen between January 2020 and July 2024 at a tertiary care centre in New Delhi, India. Clinical, biochemical and imaging data were collected, and real-world outcomes were assessed for PTX and cinacalcet. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS.Results:Of the 90 patients, 64.4% were symptomatic and 35.6% were asymptomatic. Symptomatic patients had higher parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels and more frequent MIBI localization. PTX was curative and led to significantly greater reductions in calcium (P = 0.006) and PTH (P = 0.009). Conversely, cinacalcet use was associated with normalization of calcium (P = 0.001) and lowering of PTH (P = 0.001) in both symptomatic and asymptomatic patients, with minimal side effects.Conclusion:A significant proportion of cases were asymptomatic PHPT, with biochemical and radiological profiles closely resembling those of symptomatic cases. While PTX provided definitive cure, cinacalcet led to short-term normalization of calcium and reduction of PTH. Cinacalcet serves as an alternative for patients who refuse surgery, are not candidates for surgery, or prefer medical management.

INTRODUCTION

Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is a common endocrine disorder seen in both outpatient and inpatient settings. Indian studies have described its presentation in symptomatic[12345678] and asymptomatic PHPT[9101112] forms. While Western populations transitioned to predominantly asymptomatic PHPT by the 1970s,[1314] Indian reports until 2023 indicated a symptomatic predominance.[8] Recent data, however, suggest a gradual shift toward asymptomatic disease in India.[151617]

Studies from West,[9] North,[10] and Southern India,[1112] report asymptomatic PHPT prevalence rates of 17.7%, 38% and 38.9%–41%, respectively. The Indian PHPT registry documented an increase in asymptomatic PHPT, with 10% of 554 cases in 2021 and only 5% in 2018.[718] This trend likely reflects widespread availability of automated biochemical assays and the growing use of osteoporosis screening.

The growing number of asymptomatic PHPT poses a therapeutic dilemma between surgical and medical management. Parathyroidectomy (PTX) remains the treatment of choice in most symptomatic and selected asymptomatic PHPT patients.[19] Medical therapy with calcimimetic (cinacalcet) and/or antiresorptive (alendronate/denosumab) is considered when surgery is contraindicated or declined. Cinacalcet has been shown to reduce hypercalcaemia and parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels while increasing serum phosphate (P), with a sustained benefit over 5.5 years.[20] It has shown efficacy across a broad disease spectrum, including post-PTX failure and asymptomatic PHPT.[21] Data on cinacalcet use in Indian cases with PHPT is scarce.

This retrospective study describes the clinical, biochemical and imaging profile of symptomatic and asymptomatic PHPT with emphasis on the real-world outcomes among patients managed with PTX or cinacalcet.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Subjects and data collection

A total of 157 consecutive patients of PHPT were seen between 01 January 2020 and 31 July 2024 in our department. Of these, 90 patients with available data were included in the analysis (and 67 were excluded due to incomplete baseline or follow-up data). Patients’ treatment decisions were based on current clinical guidelines as well as individual patient preferences. Cinacalcet was prescribed in following clinical scenarios: (1) patient refusal of PTX despite counselling, (2) PTX contraindication due to comorbidity and (3) asymptomatic PHPT not meeting guideline criteria for PTX.[19] Symptomatic PHPT was defined as the presence of one or more of the following: muscular pains, bone pains, fracture, proximal muscular weakness, nephrolithiasis, abdominal pain, constipation, pancreatitis, psychiatric symptoms, or polyuria. Asymptomatic PHPT was defined as absence of aforementioned classical symptoms, and incidental detection of PHPT during biochemical or osteoporosis screening. Nephrolithiasis or osteoporosis alone in the absence of overt clinical symptoms was classified as asymptomatic PHPT.[19]

The following parameters of interest were recorded: age, sex, menopausal status, presentation (symptomatic or asymptomatic), serum calcium (Ca), P, Alkaline phosphatase (ALP), 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25OHD), PTH, creatinine (Cr), urine calcium/creatinine ratio (UCCR), fracture(s), nephrolithiasis, bone mineral density (BMD) and T-score. Albumin-corrected calcium was calculated where albumin was available. Lesion localization was assessed using ultrasound (USG), Tc-99 Sestamibi scan (MIBI) and other imaging modalities. Additionally, lesion site, treatment (PTX or cinacalcet) and post-treatment Ca and PTH were documented (6-weeks for PTX and most recent for cinacalcet). Vitamin D was corrected in all insufficient/deficient patients; however, post-correction PTH was not measured routinely. All above tests were performed across different clinical laboratories; hence, uniform details were not available.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 26 was utilized for analysis. Missing data per variable were recorded, and a complete-case analysis approach was used. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. They were found to be non-normally distributed (P < 0.05). These variables were represented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Sub/Intra-group analysis of continuous variables was performed using the Mann–Whitney U test and the Wilcoxon-Signed Ranks test, respectively. Categorical variables were represented as number and percentage. Subgroup analysis of categorical variables was performed using the Chi-square test and the McNemar test. Comparisons between PTX and cinacalcet subgroups were unadjusted and should be interpreted as exploratory.

Ethical aspects

The institutional ethics committee of Max Superspeciality Hospital, Saket, New Delhi approved the study vide letter no BHR/RS/MSSH/DDF/SKT-2/IEC/ENDO/25-20 dated 23/09/2025. Due to retrospective nature of data, informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. Study followed principles laid down in declaration of Helsinki 1964 and its subsequent amendments.

RESULTS

Baseline

The cohort comprised 157 patients of PHPT, out of which 94 (59.8%) were symptomatic and 63 (40.1%) were asymptomatic. Baseline data of 90 patients were analysed [Table 1]. Of these, 58 (64.4%) were symptomatic and 32 (35.6%) were asymptomatic. The frequency of complaints in the symptomatic subgroup was as follows: muscular pains (100%), bone pains (82.2%), proximal muscular weakness (37.8%), nephrolithiasis (40%), abdominal pain (57.8%), constipation (44.4%), pancreatitis (13.3%), psychiatric (31.1%) and polyuria (13.3%). The median age was 57 years (48–67), with a female preponderance (n = 66, 73.3%). Among women, 46 (69.7%) were post-menopausal. Labs were as follows: Ca 11.4 mg/dl (11.05–12), P 2.75 mg/dl (2.4–3.2), ALP 135 IU/L (99.8–198.5), 25OHD 27.2 ng/ml (19.5–39.1), PTH 176 pg/ml (123.5–280.2), Cr 0.7 mg/dl (0.6–0.86) and UCCR 280 mg/g (124.4–404.9). Patient comorbidities included: prior/existing fractures in 12 (13.3%), nephrolithiasis in 25 (32.9%), osteopenia in 12 (16.9%) and osteoporosis in 44 (61.9%). USG and MIBI localized lesions in 70 (77.8%) and 63 (71.6%) patients, respectively. USG and MIBI were concurrent in 65.6% (59/90) patients. Details of lesion size, site and type are provided in Table 1. Additional imaging modalities used included 4D-CT (n = 14, 15.5%) and F-18 Choline PET/CT (n = 17, 18.9%). Forty-five patients underwent PTX, while the remaining 45 were managed with cinacalcet.

Symptomatic versus asymptomatic PHPT

Symptomatic patients had higher PTH levels (201.7 vs 143 pg/mL, P = 0.001), Cr (0.77 vs 0.66 mg/dL, P = 0.022) and more frequent MIBI localization (80.7% vs 54.8%, P = 0.01), whereas asymptomatic patients had lower distal radius BMD (0.533 vs 0.663 g/cm2; P = 0.024). In the symptomatic group, USG and MIBI localization rates were comparable (77.2% and 80.7%, P = 0.815). In the asymptomatic group, USG localized significantly more lesions (borderline) than MIBI (77.4% vs 54.8%; P = 0.065, two-sided; P = 0.033, one-sided). No statistically significant differences were found between the two groups for other clinical (age, osteopenia, osteoporosis, nephrolithiasis, cinacalcet use, PTX), biochemical (Ca, P, ALP, 25OHD, UCCR) and imaging (T-score, BMD, USG localization, other imaging and size) parameters [Table 2].

Real-world outcomes: PTX and cinacalcet

Patients who underwent PTX were younger (51 vs 64 years, P = 0.005), had higher PTH levels (200 vs 165 pg/mL, P = 0.031) and more frequent MIBI localization (37 vs 26, P = 0.009) as compared to the cinacalcet subgroup [Table 3]. Although PTX subgroup was frequently symptomatic (71.1% vs 57.8%, P = 0.186), there were no significant differences between the subgroups in osteoporosis, USG localization, nephrolithiasis, lesion size, Ca, or Cr levels. On follow-up at 6 weeks, Ca (9.3 vs 9.9 mg/dL, P = 0.006) and PTH (38.5 vs 107 pg/mL, P < 0.001) were significantly lower in the PTX subgroup [Table 3].

Cinacalcet usage

Among patients treated with cinacalcet, 26 (57.8%) were symptomatic and 19 (42.2%) were asymptomatic. Baseline biochemistry and clinical features are summarized in Table 3. Symptomatic patients had higher PTH levels (201.7 vs 143 pg/mL, P = 0.096) and more frequent MIBI localization (73.1% vs 36.8%, P = 0.009). No other significant clinical or biochemical differences were found between symptomatic and asymptomatic patients treated with cinacalcet [Table 4]. The median dose of cinacalcet used was 30 mg (30–60), and the median duration of follow-up was 8 months (3–14 months). There was a significant normalization of Ca (11.5 vs 9.9, P = 0.001) and reduction in PTH (165 vs 107, P = 0.001) in 27/30 (90%) patients where follow-up data was available. No significant differences were seen in the reduction of Ca & PTH between symptomatic or asymptomatic PHPT patients. The most common side-effect was nausea (8/45; 17.8%), with none other reported. There were no instances of breakthrough hypercalcaemia or discontinuation of cinacalcet during the follow-up period.

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective study of 90 PHPT patients from a single-centre in North India, asymptomatic PHPT was frequent, with its biochemical and radiological profile showing minimal differences when compared to symptomatic PHPT. While PTX provided definitive cure, cinacalcet was associated with modest reductions in Ca and PTH levels. Cinacalcet remains a non-curative treatment option for both symptomatic and asymptomatic PHPT.

There seems to be a public-private divide in presentation of PHPT. While studies from public-sector hospitals still largely report a symptomatic profile,[12345678] some studies from private tertiary care hospitals in West,[9] North,[10] and Southern India,[1112] have shown a prevalence of 17.7%, 38% and 38.9%–41% of asymptomatic PHPT, respectively [Figure 1]. Delays in diagnosis, vitamin D deficiency, limited access to healthcare and laboratory testing are contributory factors to the higher prevalence of symptomatic PHPT in public-rural settings. In contrast, greater access to biochemical testing (e.g., auto-analysers) and increased urban osteoporosis screening could explain the rising incidence of asymptomatic cases in private-urban settings. Developing countries such as Brazil,[22] Turkey,[23] and other Asian nations,[151617] have reported a higher prevalence of asymptomatic PHPT in the last decade.

Our results reveal a significant proportion of asymptomatic PHPT in the screened (40.1%) and the analysed (35.6%) cohort. Compared to asymptomatic patients, those with symptomatic PHPT had higher PTH levels (P = 0.001), Cr (P = 0.022) and MIBI localization (P = 0.01). Paradoxically, the asymptomatic subgroup had a lower BMD/T-score at lumbar spine (non-significant), and lower BMD at distal radius (significant). The latter finding is consistent with the natural history of PHPT.[19] No other clinical, biochemical or imaging features [Table 2] could differentiate asymptomatic PHPT from symptomatic PHPT. These findings contrast with a previous report suggesting lower Ca, PTH, adenoma size in asymptomatic PHPT.[7] The asymptomatic group had borderline higher adenoma localization with USG when compared to MIBI (77.4% vs 54.8%; P = 0.065). This is consistent with a previous report,[24] and may reflect operator dependence and lesser adenoma activity in this subgroup. Reduction in the clinico-biochemical differentiating factors between asymptomatic and symptomatic PHPT has been previously observed in developing nations.[1225] This may be attributed to improvements in nutrition and vitamin D levels, particularly in the urban populations.

PTX offers a robust biochemical cure and leads to an increase in BMD in patients of PHPT.[26] It is recommended for symptomatic PHPT, unless precluded by severe comorbidity. For asymptomatic cases, PTX is guided by expert recommendations.[19] On expected lines, our results indicated that patients who underwent PTX were more likely to be symptomatic (71.1%; P = 0.186), younger (P = 0.005), had higher PTH levels (P = 0.031) and greater MIBI localization (P = 0.009). However, there were no significant differences in Ca, Cr, UCCR, nephrolithiasis, osteoporosis and USG localization when compared to patients treated with cinacalcet. Post-PTX, Ca and PTH normalization was observed in all 45 patients [Table 3].

Regardless of the recommendations, patient treatment preferences can often take precedence, despite counselling for or against specific options. As a result, medical management becomes a practical treatment for patients who refuse surgery, even if symptomatic, or when surgery is not indicated/contraindicated. Cinacalcet (a calcimimetic) has previously been shown to lower Ca and PTH over long durations,[20] across varying disease severities,[21] in elderly patients,[27] and as bridging therapy for PTX.[28] It does not significantly improve BMD. For the latter, cinacalcet has been used in combination with alendronate,[29] and denosumab,[30] to achieve normocalcaemia while preserving/increasing BMD. In our study, cinacalcet was used to manage both asymptomatic and symptomatic PHPT. Its usage was associated with normalization of Ca (P = 0.001) and reduction in PTH (P = 0.001), irrespective of presentation (27/30 patients; 90%). Furthermore, it was well tolerated without any discontinuations. These findings are consistent with control rather than cure.

Strengths of this study include a single-centre design with endocrinologist-based patient care, data derived from patients with both symptomatic and asymptomatic PHPT and robust hypercalcaemia management. Notably, this is the first study from India to analyse and demonstrate the efficacy of cinacalcet in managing PHPT. Limitations of this study include its retrospective nature, small-sized cohort, short-term follow-up (8 months), selection bias, missing data and lack of long-term analysis on the effects of cinacalcet or PTX on P, UCCR, bone turnover markers and BMD. As PTX and cinacalcet subgroups were not matched at baseline, the comparative results likely have confounding. Therefore, drawing equivalence conclusions from this dataset is unlikely.

CONCLUSION

A decade after our previous publication,[10] this study reaffirms that the prevalence of asymptomatic PHPT in India is on the rise. The biochemical and radiological markers of asymptomatic PHPT are increasingly indistinguishable from its symptomatic counterpart. While asymptomatic patients may or may not require PTX, our study highlights discrepancies between clinical recommendations and actual practice. Treatment choice is often influenced by patient preferences, which may explain the increasing use of cinacalcet in managing PHPT. Cinacalcet could achieve short-term biochemical control (normalization of Ca and reduction of PTH) across the disease spectrum; however, it was not a curative treatment.
