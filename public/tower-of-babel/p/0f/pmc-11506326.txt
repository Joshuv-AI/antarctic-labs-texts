Pathologic Outcomes and Survival in Patients with Rectal Cancer and Increased Body Mass Index
Authors: Sameh Hany Emile, Giovanna Dasilva, Nir Horesh, Zoe Garoufalia, Rachel Gefen, Peige Zhou, Mariana Berho, Steven D. Wexner
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe assessed the association between increased body mass index (BMI) and rectal cancer outcomes.MethodsWe included patients who underwent surgery for stage I-III rectal adenocarcinoma who were divided according to BMI at diagnosis: ideal BMI (18.5–24.9 kg/m2) and increased BMI (≥25 kg/m2). Groups were compared using univariate association analyses relative to baseline characteristics, pathologic outcomes, overall survival (OS), and disease-free survival (DFS). Main outcome measures involved circumferential resection margin (CRM), pathologic TNM stage, total mesorectal incision (TME) grade, OS, and DFS.Results243 patients (64.6% male; median age 59 years) with a median BMI of 26.3 kg/m2 were included. 62.1% had BMI ≥25 kg/m2. Increased BMI patients had similar proportions of males (66.9% vs. 60.9%; p = 0.407) and comorbidities (ASA III: 47% vs. 37.4%; p = 0.24) to ideal BMI patients. There were no significant differences in cN1-2 stage (p = 0.279) or positive CRM (p = 0.062) rates. The groups had similar complete/near-complete TME, pathologic TN stage, and survival rates. Pathologic and survival outcomes were also similar with a BMI cutoff of 30.ConclusionsThere was a trend toward more nodal involvement in preoperative assessment and less CRM involvement in the final pathology of patients with increased BMI. Complete/near-complete TME and survival rates were comparable between the groups.

Introduction

Obesity has been shown to impact colorectal cancer (CRC) in several aspects. Obesity was identified as a risk factor for CRC, and although the reason is still unknown, it is likely due to hyperinsulinemia, typically associated with obesity since insulin is an established biochemical mediator between obesity and CRC [1]. A recent umbrella meta-analysis that assessed the association between obesity and 36 types of cancer found a strong link between obesity and CRC [2].

Obesity is also an important factor that can lead to technical difficulties in CRC treatment and have a negative impact on patients’ survival. Increased body mass index (BMI) was reported to be associated with higher conversion of minimally invasive colorectal procedures to open surgery and worse short-term outcomes [3]. Furthermore, obesity has a significant effect on long-term survival. A meta-analysis of 29 studies with 51, 303 CRC patients found lower overall survival (OS) of patients with obesity than ideal weight patients. The increased hazards of mortality were more pronounced when BMI was assessed before CRC diagnosis [4].

Surgery for rectal cancer is more challenging compared to colon cancer. This is due to anatomic considerations, including the narrow operative field in the bony pelvis and the proximity to the autonomic nerves, sphincter muscles, and other structures [5]. Indeed, obesity represents a particular challenge for surgical resection of rectal cancer. Experts advocate the use of certain platforms, such as the transanal total mesorectal excision (TaTME), for the management of rectal cancer in patients with obesity in an attempt to facilitate the technically demanding pelvic dissection in these patients [6].

Prior studies have demonstrated the challenges of rectal cancer surgery in patients with elevated BMI [7–9]. The association between increased BMI and pathologic characteristics and outcomes of rectal cancer is not clear. We hypothesized that patients with rectal cancer who have increased BMI may have different pathologic profiles from patients with ideal BMI and these differences may affect OS. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the impact of increased BMI on the clinicopathologic characteristics and pathologic outcomes of rectal cancer, compared to patients with ideal BMI.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a retrospective review of a prospective database of patients with rectal cancer who were treated in the Department of Colorectal Surgery at Cleveland Clinic Florida between 2016 and 2020.

Selection Criteria

Adult patients of either sex who were surgically treated for rectal cancer using any surgical approach were consecutively included. Patients who underwent non-operative watch and watch management and patients without BMI data were excluded from the study. Based on the WHO classification of weight categories, where ideal BMI ranges between 18.5 and 24.9 kg/m2, patients were classified according to their baseline BMI into two groups: ideal BMI and increased BMI (BMI ≥25 kg/m2). The two groups were compared for clinicopathologic characteristics and outcomes. A secondary analysis using a cutoff of 30 was conducted to compare the pathologic and survival outcomes of patients with obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2) and patients without obesity (BMI <30 kg/m2).

Management of Rectal Cancer

Evaluation included digital rectal examination and full colonoscopy with biopsies taken from suspicious lesions. Pathologic examination of biopsies from the lesions confirmed the diagnosis of rectal adenocarcinoma. Rectal cancer was classified as low, mid, and high according to the tumor distance from the anal verge (<5 cm, 5–10 cm, >10 cm). The assessment of the extent and stage of rectal cancer was done using MRI with rectal cancer protocol using 3T or 1.5T MRI systems and T2-weighted, small FOV, oblique-axial, sagittal, and coronal images without fat saturation.

Patients with locally advanced rectal cancers, defined as T3-4 and/or N1-2, were treated first with neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy or total neoadjuvant chemotherapy before surgical treatment that involved restorative low anterior resection or non-restorative abdominoperineal resection performed via an open, laparoscopic, or transanal approach (TaTME). Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy comprised a long-course standard radiotherapy (54 Gy) and 6–8 cycles of concurrent 5 Fluorouracil or Capecitabine. Total neoadjuvant chemotherapy consisted of a modified 6–8 cycles of FOLFOX6 or 5–6 cycles of CAPOX, induction chemotherapy followed by long-course chemoradiation therapy was given.

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was a composite of pathologic parameters that included mesorectal grading, surgical margin status, and harvested and positive lymph nodes. Secondary outcomes included OS and disease recurrence.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using EZR™ (version 1.55) [10] R software (version 4.1.2), and SPSS™ version 23 (IBM crop). Continuous data were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR) and categorical data were expressed as numbers and absolute percentages. Mann-Whitney test was used to analyze continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test or χ2 test was to process categorical data. Univariate and multivariate analysis of the predictors of CRM involvement was conducted. Kaplan-Meier statistics and log-rank test were used to estimate differences in survival between the two groups. Missing data were addressed using complete case analysis. p values <0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Entire Cohort Description

After screening 264 records, 20 patients who were treated with a watch-and-wait strategy and 1 patient who did not have data on BMI were excluded and therefore 243 patients (64.6% male) with a median age of 59 (IQR, 50–68) years and a median BMI of 26.3 (IQR, 23.2–29.4) kg/m2 were included. Ninety-three (38.3%) patients had ideal BMI <25 kg/m2, whereas 151 (61.7%) had BMI ≥25 kg/m2 (101 overweight, 36 class I obesity, and 14 class II-III obesity). 45.7% of patients had a low-lying tumor and 44% had mid-rectal tumors. 68.2% of tumors were of clinical T3-4 stage and 46.4% had clinical evidence of nodal involvement.

Adenocarcinomas accounted for 95.5% of cases whereas 1.8% and 2.7% were mucinous or signet-ring cell carcinomas, respectively. Most (89.2%) of adenocarcinomas were of low grade. Neoadjuvant therapy was given to 70.4% of patients. The majority (81.1%) of patients had a sphincter-saving low anterior resection and 81.4% of procedures were performed by laparoscopy. The median follow-up was 23 (IQR: 10, 40.5) months. Less than 10% of patients had missing data (Table 1).

Clinical and Treatment Characteristics of Patients according to BMI Class

Patients with increased BMI had comparable age, a similar proportion of male patients (66.9% vs. 60.9%, p = 0.407), and comorbidities (ASA III: 47% vs. 37.4%, p = 0.24) compared to patients with ideal BMI. Preoperative MRI revealed that the proportion of cT3-4 tumors (68.7% vs. 67.4%, p = 0.487) and nodal involvement (cN1-2) (57.1% vs. 47.8%, p = 0.279) was similar between the two BMI groups. Patients with increased or ideal BMI also had similar incidences of involved/threatened mesorectal fascia (34.8% vs. 37.2%, p = 0.775) and extramural venous invasion in MRI (10% vs. 17.9%, p = 0.469) (Table 1).

There were no significant differences between the two groups in the percentage of patients who received neoadjuvant therapy (69.5% vs. 71.7%, p = 0.928) and underwent laparoscopic colectomy (82.1% vs. 80.2%, p = 0.735). There were no significant differences between the two groups in surgical treatment (LAR: 83.4% vs. 77.2%, p = 0.241 and TaTME: 58.9% vs. 48.9%, p = 0.144).

Pathologic and Oncologic Outcomes of Patients according to BMI Class

Pathology of patients with increased BMI included more mucinous carcinomas (2.9% vs. 0, p = 0.09) and fewer signet-ring cell carcinomas (1.4% vs. 5%, p = 0.09) compared to patients with ideal BMI; however, these differences did not attain statistical significance. The pathologic T and N stages and lymphovascular invasion were comparable between the two groups (Table 2).

Both groups had similar rates of complete/near-complete TME (81.5% vs. 82.8%, p = 0.803). Patients with increased BMI had statistically similar rates of involved CRM (2.6% vs. 8.7%, p = 0.062) to patients with ideal BMI. Subgroup Analyses

As shown in the Supplementary Table (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000541085), patients with increased and ideal BMI had similar rates of CRM involvement, incomplete TME, local recurrence, and distant metastases, regardless of the disease stage and surgical approach. However, stage III patients with increased BMI had significantly lower rates of incomplete TME (9.1% vs. 29.3%, p = 0.014) than patients with ideal BMI.

Impact of Neoadjuvant Therapy on the Association between BMI and Outcomes

Patients with increased and ideal BMI had similar rates of CRM involvement whether they received neoadjuvant therapy (3.8% vs. 9.1%, p = 0.187) or not (0 vs. 7.7%, p = 0.131). In addition, regardless of the receipt of neoadjuvant therapy, both groups had similar rates of incomplete TME (neoadjuvant: 16.2% vs. 19.7%, p = 0.164, no neoadjuvant: 20% vs. 15.4%, p = 0.117), local recurrence (neoadjuvant: 5.9% vs. 7.9%, p = 0.408, no neoadjuvant: 5.1% vs. 11.5%, p = 0.382), and distant metastases (neoadjuvant: 22.5% vs. 25.4%, p = 0.709, no neoadjuvant: 10.3% vs. 26.9%, p = 0.100). Patients with increased and ideal BMI had similar rates of pT0 stage (neoadjuvant: 24.8% vs. 30.3%, p = 0.246, no neoadjuvant: 15.6% vs. 8%, p = 0.939) and pN0 tumors (neoadjuvant: 68.6% vs. 72.3%, p = 0.865, no neoadjuvant: 66.7% vs. 64%, p = 0.576).

Discussion

The present study investigated the association between increased BMI and the pathologic outcomes and survival of patients with rectal cancer. Despite lack of statistical significance, patients with increased BMI were more likely to be diagnosed with mucinous rectal carcinomas and nodal involvement on clinical assessment, and less likely to have CRM involvement. Increased BMI was noted in approximately 62% of the cohort studied. This observation is consistent with the rising epidemic of obesity in the US population. There has been a remarkable increase in the prevalence of obesity in the USA from 30.5% to 41.9% and severe obesity from 4.7% to 9.2% from 1999 to 2000 through 2017–March 2020 [11]. Patients with overweight or obesity were more commonly male and had more medical comorbidities which are expected given the negative impact of obesity on the metabolic status and overall health [12].

Increased BMI was associated with more lymph node involvement on clinical assessment, particularly advanced nodal disease, cN2 stage, which was almost twice that in patients with ideal BMI. The explanation of this interesting finding is not clear; however, it supports the conclusions of a former meta-analysis [13] that reported obesity increases the odds of lymph node metastases by 20%. There is cumulative evidence that obesity may potentiate the metastatic behavior of cancer. The characteristics of obese adipose tissue in cancers close to adipose tissue, such as breast cancer, mirror the tumor microenvironment and therefore provide an ideal microenvironment to enhance tumor progression and metastasis [14]. Similarly, it was observed that intra-abdominal cancers, including CRC, preferentially spread to the omentum, which is largely composed of adipocytes which may contribute significantly to the metastatic process [15]. On another hand, the pathologic N stage in patients with ideal or increased BMI was comparable implying a similar downstaging effect of neoadjuvant therapy that was given to most patients.

Another interesting finding was that patients with increased BMI were less likely to have involvement of the CRM. This observation may imply that obesity constitutes some type of a barrier against infiltration of the circumferential margin of the tumor. While there is no direct evidence to support this theory, it may be plausible to assume that rectal cancer would advance more slowly through a bulky, fatty mesorectum that acts as a large fatty cushion, radially displacing the mesorectal fascia [16].

Patients with increased BMI, albeit having a greater nodal involvement implying more advanced stage, had similar OS and disease-free survival to patients with ideal BMI. This finding was concordant with a study [17] on the impact of BMI on the surgical and oncologic outcomes of laparoscopic low anterior resection for locally advanced rectal cancer. The study found comparable oncologic outcome in terms of OS, local recurrence, and distant metastasis between patients with BMI less or greater than 25 kg/m2. The use of sphincter-saving surgery was also similar among the two groups, consistent with our findings and affirming that sphincter-saving rectal cancer surgery is feasible in patients with obesity.

The findings of the present study can have several clinical implications. Knowing that rectal cancer patients with increased BMI are at an increased of nodal involvement should increase caution when interpreting the clinical assessment of nodal status with MRI, especially given the recent evidence which showed limited accuracy of the tools used for clinical assessment of nodal involvement of rectal cancer [18]. Furthermore, since patients with increased BMI may have less chance of CRM involvement, this observation may affect the surgical management of these patients.

Although some of the differences in outcomes highlighted in our discussion did not reach statistical significance, they might be relevant to clinical practice. For example, the proportion of patients with increased BMI who had a clinical N2 stage was almost twice that of patients with ideal BMI, yet the p value >0.05. It has been noted that statistical significance should not be the only drive for interpretation of an outcome because it does not provide clinical insight into certain factors as treatment effect size and magnitude of change. That is why we examined the research outcomes primarily for their clinical significance, rather than statistical significance alone [19, 20]. The findings in this study differ from prior data [7, 8], possibly due to improved techniques in patients with elevated BMI, increased use of neoadjuvant therapy, or other factors.

The present study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. The single-center, retrospective design of the study may limit its external validity and have some risk of selection bias when choosing the surgical approach and procedure for patients with increased BMI. The limited sample size of the study is another important limitation. A large, multicenter cohort study is needed to confirm the initial findings of our study and detect subtle differences between the groups that were otherwise not detected in our analysis. Certain outcomes, such as conversion from laparoscopic to open surgery and operation time, were not available in the dataset used in the study. Nonetheless, this limitation may not be pertinent to the objective of the study which was the pathologic outcomes and survival.

Conclusions

There was a trend toward more nodal involvement on preoperative assessment and less CRM involvement in final pathology in patients with increased BMI. The rates of complete/near-complete TME and survival were comparable to those in patients with ideal BMI.
