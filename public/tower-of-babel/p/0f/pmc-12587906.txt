Development and psychometric properties of the caregiver sense of competence questionnaire for caregivers of people with multiple sclerosis (CareSoC-MS): a cross-sectional study
Authors: Andrea Tacchino, Jessica Podda, Anna Verri, Mario Alberto Battaglia, Giampaolo Brichetto, Michela Ponzio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesMultiple sclerosis (MS) competence is crucial for caregivers of people with MS (PwMS). Having validated MS-specific tools to measure caregivers’ Sense of Competence (SoC) is fundamental to understand if they are coping with their role and/or need support and training. The aim of the study was to develop and validate the Caregiver SoC questionnaire for caregivers of PwMS (CareSoC-MS), a self-report questionnaire, to assess SoC in MS caregivers.DesignCross-sectional study.SettingItaly.Participants191 MS caregivers (sex: 48.7% females; age: 58.6±12.6 years; education: 62.8% high-school/university) self-administered the 27-item CareSoC-MS questionnaire. Psychometric evaluation consisted of analyses of structural validity and reliability, construct validity and discriminant validity. The final version of the CareSoC-MS includes 14 items covering 4 relevant SoC domains: L1-Psychological well-being, L2-Impact on personal social life, L3-Efficacy of the provided care and L4-Satisfaction with one’s own performance as a caregiver.ResultsThe 14-item CareSoC-MS is a psychometrically valid questionnaire with no ceiling or floor effects. Test–retest reliability analysis showed significant intraclass correlation coefficients for L1 (0.80), L2 (0.96), L3 (0.88) and L4 (0.61). L1 and L2 were correlated with self-reported measures of burden, anxiety and depression. Care recipient disability was correlated with L1 and L2, caregiver sex with L1, and caregiver age with L3.ConclusionsThe CareSoC-MS is a valid self-report questionnaire that can be used in clinical practice and research. CareSoC-MS could inform healthcare professionals about how informal caregivers feel capable to face the caregiving requests and, in turn, could help prevent or postpone caregiver burden.Keywords: Multiple sclerosis, Caregivers, Caregiver Burden, Depression & mood disorders, Patient Reported Outcome Measures

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic inflammatory neurodegenerative disease with autoimmune demyelinating lesions of the central nervous system and is one of the most common causes of neurological disability in young adults.1 MS progressively worsens over time, and people with MS (PwMS) can experience a wide array of symptoms, including gait, cognitive, visual, fine motor, mood and fatigue difficulties. Due to the impact of MS on daily activities, approximately 30% of PwMS require assistance, and 80% of care is provided by informal caregivers, primarily partners or other family members.2

Caregiving is generally unplanned, and most informal caregivers of PwMS gradually adopt their roles as time passes and the disease progresses.3 They provide various forms of support, such as personal care, drug administration, practical help (eg, obtaining information) and emotional support, which enable PwMS to remain functional within their own homes, adhere to rehabilitation and pharmacological treatments, and participate in daily life and social activities.4 For these reasons, caregiving may be a demanding daily task that often lasts for years, with negative physical, emotional, social and financial consequences. This can increase the caregiver’s risk of both physiological and mental illness and may lead to neglect of their own health.5

Care is often provided haphazardly due to a lack of or insufficient knowledge of predisposing factors, basic skills for increasing caregiving capacity, strategies for preventing complications and importance of rehabilitation. Consequently, a lack of competence could impact the quality of care. For example, as demonstrated in dementia, if a caregiver responds negatively to requests for assistance, the likelihood of becoming ill increases, as well as the risk that the care recipient will move to a residential facility.6,8

Caregiver factors such as personality and coping style influence whether and how he/she will adapt to the role requests.9 In this wake, the long course of MS may allow caregivers to evolve the sense of competence (SoC)—how one feels about her/his ability to provide what is necessary in their caregiver role—and to prepare and adapt to new roles as the disease evolves.8 Recent studies are beginning to explore the positive aspects of caregiving and report SoC as one of the main protective factors against caregiving stress.10 11 Furthermore, a higher SoC has been linked to lower levels of burden and a reduced risk of the care recipient entering residential care.10 12 For these reasons, informing caregivers about their care, training them to provide care and teaching coping strategies can be of significant clinical relevance, improving SoC and reducing caregiver burden.

In this context, having validated tools to measure caregivers’ SoC is important to better understand whether they are effectively coping with the challenges of their role and whether they require support and training. This also helps to prevent the negative effects of caregiving and maximise the positive aspects, such as the discovery and recognition of one’s potential. To date, the most common tools used to measure SoC are questionnaires (eg, the SoC Questionnaire) and were developed and validated primarily for informal caregivers of patients with dementia and stroke survivors.8 13 In addition, the disadvantage of current questionnaires is their unsuitability for clinical practice; they are too long for history taking. A short scale would facilitate efficient and structured caregiver attention.

Here, we aim at designing, developing and validating a new self-administered tool, that is, the Caregiver SoC questionnaire for caregivers of PwMS (CareSoC-MS). Indeed, a better understanding of the determinants of SoC in relation to caregiving challenges can contribute to the development of interventions that improve caregivers’ perceived capacity and prevent high levels of caregiving burden. Furthermore, this can help identify appropriate targeted support interventions that improve health outcomes and well-being of family caregivers, thus enhancing their quality of life.

Methods

This is a cross-sectional study consisting of two phases14: (1) the design and development of the CareSoC-MS and (2) the psychometric evaluation of the questionnaire.

Stakeholder involvement

A multidisciplinary panel of experts in neurological care for MS and six caregivers of PwMS followed as outpatients at the Italian MS Society (AISM) Rehabilitation Service of Genoa were invited to participate collaboratively in the design and development of the preliminary version of the CareSoC-MS. Multiple expertise and different perspectives would ensure the widest possible representation of relevant aspects of MS.

The multidisciplinary panel of experts consisted of a physiatrist, a neuropsychologist, a social worker, a nurse and a physical therapist working at the AISM Rehabilitation Service of Genoa. Caregivers (3 females and 3 males) were involved if they respected the definition of Buchanan et al15 to provide one or more of the following care tasks: personal care, homemaking, assistance with daily activities, mobility and leisure activities.

CareSoC-MS design and development

The design and development phases were carried out at the Italian MS Foundation (FISM) in Genoa (Italy) and followed a threefold process: (1) item generation, (2) item selection and (3) item reduction.

Item generation and selection

Based on a comprehensive review of the scientific literature and the guidelines for caregivers of PwMS defined by the MSIF (ie, the umbrella network of MS organisations) (https://www.msif.org/), the research team and the multidisciplinary panel of experts jointly identified six relevant domains: psychological well-being, impact on personal social life, efficacy of care provided, satisfaction with one’s own performance as a caregiver, finances and the relationship between the caregiver and the impaired person.

An initial pool of 98 items was generated by the multidisciplinary panel of experts based on a critical review of the literature on the experiences of informal caregivers of PwMS and a selection from existing tools such as the SoC Questionnaire8 originally developed and validated for caregivers of demented persons, the Revised Scale for Caregiving Self-Efficacy16 17 and the Adult Carer Quality of Life questionnaire.18 The SoC Questionnaire items were translated from English to Italian and back-translated from Italian to English because the validation in Italian was lacking. We translated this questionnaire using a forward-backward procedure as proposed by Beaton et al.19 First, the direct translation into Italian was performed independently by two translators; due to the simplicity of the words and the content of the questions in the English version, the two translators did not report any difficulties at this stage. The synthesis of the two translations was then back-translated into the original language independently by two other translators, completely blinded to the original version, for a validity check; it ensured that the translated version reflected the same content of the items as the original version. The four translators, together with a statistician (MP), two psychologists (AT and JP) and two Italian/English language professionals, composed an expert committee that identified and mitigated potential semantic and idiomatic issues. Due to the low difficulty level of almost all items and the small cultural gap between the original and target versions, the decision-making process to resolve grammatical/meaningful and colloquial differences was carried out smoothly and a consensus was quickly reached on the final version to be used.

The response format used was a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (completely agree) to 5 (completely disagree). Higher scores indicate a better SoC.

Item reduction

Although the panel of experts was aware of the overlap in meaning and content of most items, due to the use of tools measuring similar constructs, they decided to present the entire set of 98 items to the 6 caregivers, to capture the most appropriate nuance for MS.

The six caregivers participated in two rounds of interviews during which they completed the pool of 98 items. Adopting the ‘think aloud’ method, caregivers commented on whether and to what extent the items and answer options fulfilled the criteria for appropriateness, accuracy and relevance. The comments were collected, transcribed and analysed thematically to identify common themes, item issues and wording concerns.

Finally, the feedback was presented to and revised by the expert panel. Modifications to the questionnaire were made between the first and the second rounds. Based on full agreement on relevance, the items were maintained; those deemed unclear (ie, readability criterion) were adapted by the experts. Following this process, 71 items were removed and 10 were revised for clarity. The second round of interviews resulted in only minor changes to the questionnaire. A debriefing meeting held with the six caregivers led to the drafting of the preliminary version of the 27-item CareSoC-MS.

Statistical analysis

The psychometric evaluation phase consisted of analyses of structural validity, reliability, construct validity and discriminant validity.

Structural validity

Structural validity was evaluated with exploratory factor analysis (EFA).20 Factors were extracted through principal component factor analysis with a varimax orthogonal rotation method based on the assumption of independence of factors. Bartlett’s test of sphericity (BTS) and Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test were conducted to confirm the suitability of the data. BTS with p<0.05 and a KMO value of 0.60 were considered suitable when performing the EFA.21 22 Kaiser’s criterion for factors with an eigenvalue ≥1 was used as the criterion for components extraction. Factor loadings higher than 0.40 were considered significant.

Items with high loading on more than one factor or items with low loading on all factors (ie, <0.40) were eliminated, because they determined high instability and poor contribution to the constructs measured by the scale.23

Model fit was tested with the ratio between χ² and df (good if ≤3) and the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) (good if ≤0.08).24 We reported these statistics using the Satorra-Bentler (SB) adjustment (χ2/dfSB and RMSEASB) given the non-normal distribution of the scale items25; to improve the model fit, we considered the covariance of the error terms.

In addition, we examined floor and ceiling effects, defined as the percentages of respondents who had the lowest (ie, 27 for the preliminary version) and highest (ie, 135 for the preliminary version) scores, respectively. Floor and ceiling effects were considered present if >15% of participants achieved either the lowest or highest scores26 on the CareSoC-MS.

Reliability

Reliability was evaluated in terms of internal consistency, homogeneity and test–retest evaluation.

Internal consistency was estimated for each dimensional scale through the Cronbach’s α coefficient and homogeneity using item-total correlation. An α coefficient of 0.70 was determined as an acceptable threshold for internal consistency; 0.80 and above are preferred for psychometric quality of the scales.23

If a factor had only two items, using Cronbach’s alpha as an estimate of reliability was not ideal. In this case, we have reported the Pearson’s correlation coefficient between the two items as the most appropriate indicator of their relationship; r≥0.70 is generally considered the minimum acceptable threshold for two-item reliability.27

An item-total correlation ≥0.30 was used as a criterion for satisfactory homogeneity.23 28

Test–retest correlations are determined by calculating intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC, two-way analysis of variance random effect model for agreement). Coefficients were established on questionnaires completed approximately 2 weeks after the first completion. An ICC value of 0.70 is recommended as the minimum standard for test–retest reliability.23

Construct validity

Construct validity was assessed by comparing the CareSoC-MS with related constructs such as self-perceived burden (ie, the extent to which caregivers perceive that caregiving has hurt their emotional, social, financial, physical and spiritual functioning) and mood (ie, depression and anxiety); it was based on literature showing an association between SoC and these concepts.29 30 The Spearman’s r correlation analysis was performed for construct validity; correlations were considered low at <0.30; moderate at 0.30–0.59 and high at ≥0.60.31 The a priori hypotheses are as follows: (1) moderate-to-high negative correlation between caregivers’ SoC and self-perceived burden, as measured by the Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI)32; indeed, caregiver burden due to caregiving has been found to decrease caregiver SoC, particularly with respect to caregiving capacity8; (2) moderate-to-high negative correlation between caregivers’ SoC and mood, as measured by the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) that provides information on anxiety (HADS-a) and depression (HADS-d); indeed, mood and SoC can influence each other.33

Discriminant validity

Known-groups analysis was used to evaluate whether a tool can discriminate between predefined groups of caregivers who are expected to achieve different scores on the measure of interest. It was assessed by comparing the median scores of known groups using the Kruskal-Wallis test for multiple groups or the Mann-Whitney U test when comparing two groups. In particular, associations between CareSoC-MS and characteristics such as disability level, measured with the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) and disease duration of the care recipient, and caregiver age and sex were assessed. The variable related to the disability severity was stratified into three levels: mild (EDSS 0–3), moderate (EDSS 4–6.5) and severe (EDSS ≥7) disability.

Recruitment, data collection, measures and data analysis

Informal caregivers were recruited through a national network of psychologists working on MS (Rete Psicologi AISM) created by AISM and its Foundation (FISM). Caregivers were included if they respected the definition of Buchanan et al.15 Caregivers were invited to take part in the study in person or by telephone, if necessary (May–December 2021).

To establish the psychometric properties of the questionnaire, the minimum sample size was estimated based on 5–20 respondents per item as recommended by Rattray and Jones.34 As the number of items of the preliminary version is 27, a minimum of 135 caregivers (cases/item ratio=5) was required to evaluate the psychometric properties of the questionnaire.

Enrolled caregivers completed the self-administered CareSoC-MS along with a questionnaire collecting personal demographic variables (age, gender and education level) and disease-specific information (disease duration and disability level according to the self-EDSS). They were also asked to self-administer the ZBI35 and the HADS36 to assess caregiver burden and mood, respectively.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise demographic and clinical information. STATA Statistical Software, release V.17 (StataCorp), was used for the analysis.

Results

200 caregivers were enrolled in the study; 9 (4.5%) were excluded because they returned incomplete questionnaires. Thus, the analysis was restricted to 191 caregivers with a higher-than-required cases/item ratio (approximately 7). The excluded participants did not differ significantly from the analysed sample in terms of sex (p=0.744), age (p=0.746) and education level (p=0.660).

Characteristics of the sample

The mean age of caregivers was 58.6 (±12.6) years, 51.3% (n=98) were men, and 62.8% had a medium-high educational level (high school or university). Care recipients were the 60.2% women, with a mean age of 58.1 (±11.8) years; the 44.5% of them were severely disabled (EDSS ≥7).

Structural validity and reliability

EFA was carried out on the 27 items. BTS with p<0.001 and KMO of 0.853 confirmed the suitability of the data supporting the use of the principal component factor analysis. The analysis, after orthogonal rotation, identified six components with eigenvalue >1.

Three items (9, 15 and 22) with loading <0.40, and seven items (4, 5, 6, 8, 11, 21 and 23) with cross-loading greater than 0.40 were deleted. Further analysis showed that the remaining 17 items yielded a 6-factor solution, which cumulatively accounted for 65.3% of the total variance. The six factors, corresponding to six subscales, were labelled as follows: L1 (‘Psychological well-being’), L2 (‘Impact on personal social life’), L3 (‘Efficacy of the provided care’), L4 (‘Satisfaction with one’s own performance as a caregiver’), L5 (‘Finances’) and L6 (‘Relationship between the caregiver and the impaired person’).

Internal consistency analysis of the total score and subscales revealed a Cronbach’s α coefficient at least acceptable for L1, L2 and L3 (0.83, 0.83, 0.70), and a Pearson’s coefficient with a good internal consistency (0.71) for L4; three out of four above the preferred threshold for scales psychometric quality. Cronbach’s α coefficient was not acceptable for L5 (0.55) and L6 (alpha not available because it was represented by only one item). Thus, the L5 and L6 subscales (items 7, 24 and 25) were omitted from further analyses, and the final version of the CareSoC-MS consisted of 14 items (online supplemental material 1). The internal consistency of the final version was 0.81, and the total variance explained by the four factors was 67.9%.

Cronbach’s alpha for the 14 items ranged from 0.779 to 0.809. Similarly, the item-total correlation data showed that all items were significantly correlated with the total, with values ranging from 0.371 to 0.713. These values are consistent with published satisfactory thresholds for scale reliability. No floor or ceiling effects were found in CareSoC-MS (0.0% scored 14 and 0.5% scored 70, respectively).

Discussion

The results showed that the CareSoC-MS questionnaire is a reliable and valid instrument to assess the SoC experienced by informal caregivers of PwMS. To our knowledge, this is the first study validating a tool to measure SoC in informal caregivers of PwMS.

Psychometric evaluation indicates that the 14-item CareSoC-MS questionnaire was valid with no ceiling or floor effects.

Construct validity was statistically supported by principal components analysis, which revealed a scale structure able to identify six factors relevant to caregivers of PwMS, reduced to four after internal consistency analysis: (1) psychological well-being, (2) impact on personal social life, (3) efficacy of the provided care and (4) satisfaction with one’s own performance as a caregiver. The internal consistency of the final version was good, as indicated by the mean Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.81 (acceptable to good considering the four subscales). However, it is important to note that the test–retest reliability of the two-item factor was below the commonly accepted threshold (ICC=0.61), which may suggest limited temporal stability and should be considered when interpreting the consistency of this construct over time.

The SoC of informal caregivers of PwMS was highly correlated with self-perceived burden and moderately correlated with mood.

Self-perceived burden

Burden was reported by 67.5% of our sample; as expected, higher levels of self-perceived burden were associated with lower perceived SoC. This finding is consistent with previous literature suggesting that caregiver burden partially mediates the impact of caregiver competence and health-related quality of life in the elderly.37 Moreover, van der Lee et al29 reported that caregiver SoC was an important factor in caregiver burden over time; caregivers who felt less competent reported greater burden over time.

All CareSoC-MS factors correlated with self-perceived burden; psychological well-being and impact on personal social life were the most correlated. Recent findings show that caregiver burden is a multidimensional reaction to a range of factors associated with daily caregiving for a person with MS, including physical, emotional and especially psychological and social stressors.38 39

Mood

Nearly 51.3% of our sample had clinically significant anxiety symptoms, and approximately 25.7% had symptoms suggestive of depression. Higher levels of both anxiety and depression significantly correlated with a lower SoC. This is compatible with previous studies reporting that some caregivers may experience depressive symptoms when exposed to chronic stress, and others may experience worsened health.30

Psychological well-being and the impact on personal social life factors were the most correlated with SoC. It would reflect findings reporting that social life of spouses of PwMS was limited by caregiving activities.40

Disability of care recipient and sex and age of caregivers

As expected, caregivers of PwMS with moderate or severe disability reported a reduced SoC, as a consequence of new and more demanding challenges due to the evolution of MS. This finding can be explained in light of existing literature on caregiver burden showing that it was higher in those assisting PwMS with more severe disability38 and worse overall health.41

Caregivers were significantly different by sex only for the psychological well-being factor, with females reporting a lower level of SoC. This is in line with previous research showing that, compared with males, being a female caregiver living with a person with a neurological disease predicted a lower SoC.8 As the literature shows, women caregivers usually experience higher levels of physical distress and exhaustion/tiredness, and the majority of caring responsibilities taken by women suggests a link with gender role expectations.42 Culturally, women’s expected roles are still marked with obligations to provide care, and prioritising the care provision over their own well-being is often normalised. Also, an increase in depression, anxiety and distress in spouses during the caregiving process has been observed. Moreover, anxiety could be linked to fears concerning disease evolution43; indeed, caregivers who are unsure of what to expect in terms of disease prognosis or where to access formal support, in other words, who lack the necessary knowledge to provide adequate care or plan for the future, may feel overwhelmed.

Strengths and limitations

A possible limitation is that, although it is generally recommended to conduct EFA and the confirmatory factor analysis on separate samples, the same dataset was used for both analyses due to the limited size of our sample. Moreover, the discriminant validity of CareSoC-MS was not investigated considering other clinical aspects relevant to PwMS, such as cognitive disorders and motor impairments, evaluated through performance tests and self-reported outcomes. Also, we did not include younger caregivers, who may have experienced and reacted differently to the same circumstances than our sample. Other potentially important aspects, such as the duration of caregiving or the time at which they begin ‘acting’ as caregivers, should be taken into account. Well before a significant worsening in disability, ‘caregiver-to-be’ may already need to support the person with MS in their daily activities. In addition, the caregiver’s attitude towards the care recipient was not considered.

Although other domains relevant to MS caregivers (eg, physical burden and role conflict) could be considered, the CareSoC-MS still provides a comprehensive view of the caregiver’s SoC.

The direct engagement of MS caregivers and the multidisciplinary panel of experts from the early stages of designing and developing the scale is one of the instrument’s strengths.

Conclusions

The CareSoC-MS is a reliable and valid tool to assess SoC among caregivers of PwMS. Comparison between groups indicated that it is sensitive in capturing differences due to the disability level of the care recipient. As expected, caregivers of patients with higher EDSS showed worse scores.

Through the CareSoC-MS, healthcare professionals could relieve how informal caregivers feel capable to face the caregiving requests and could make them aware of the need to take good care of themselves from the start, as caregiving is demanding to avoid negative influence on their quality of life. This, in turn, could help prevent or postpone caregiver burden, which would clearly be beneficial for all concerned. Future research could further explore the availability of health and social resources for caregivers, as well as their characteristics and context-specific unmet needs of caregivers. In addition, more longitudinal studies are needed to explore how SoC influences caregiver burden as MS progresses. With these insights, clinical practice may be able to intervene on these predictors to prevent high levels of burden in family caregivers.
