Retro-odontoid pseudotumor and subaxial spinal osteophytes: instability as the etiology and stabilization as the treatment. Illustrative cases
Authors: Atul Goel, Aimee Goel, Abhidha Shah, Apurva Prasad, Ravikiran Vutha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDRetro-odontoid pseudotumors (ROPs) and subaxial cervical osteophytes are generally considered distinct causes of ventral neural compression. The authors hypothesize that both represent secondary changes related to underlying cervical spinal instability rather than primary pathological entities.OBSERVATIONSBetween January 2022 and June 2025, 5 patients presented with coexisting ROP and multilevel subaxial osteophytes causing significant ventral dural compression. All patients underwent posterior stabilization of the atlantoaxial and involved subaxial segments aimed at achieving arthrodesis. No direct decompression, including resection of pseudotumor or osteophytes, was performed. All patients experienced immediate and sustained neurological improvement postoperatively. Follow-up imaging (≥ 6 months) demonstrated solid stabilization and regression of both ROP and osteophytes. No patient required additional decompressive surgery.LESSONSROP and subaxial osteophytes may represent reversible, secondary manifestations of cervical instability. Stabilization alone can result in both clinical recovery and radiographic resolution, potentially obviating the need for direct decompressive procedures. Recognition of instability as the primary pathology has important implications for surgical strategy. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26416

Our earlier reports have proposed that the fundamental nodal point in the genesis of both retro-odontoid pseudotumor (ROP) and subaxial spinal osteophytes is underlying spinal instability, and that stabilization of the involved segments constitutes the appropriate treatment.1–6 A review of the available literature did not reveal any prior report that systematically discusses or evaluates the similarities in the pathogenesis and management of both ROP and subaxial osteophytes.

We have included both entities within the spectrum of degenerative spinal disorders. Despite their apparent compressive effect on neural structures, our observations suggest that these lesions are secondary manifestations of chronic spinal instability, serve a protective or adaptive role, and are potentially or demonstrably reversible following spinal segmental stabilization. In this report, we analyze the clinical and radiological outcomes following craniovertebral and subaxial spinal stabilization in patients presenting with both ROP and multilevel subaxial osteophytes.

Illustrative Cases

During the period from January 2022 to June 2025, 5 patients were identified in whom both ROP and multisegmental subaxial cervical osteophytes were present. Cases with retroodontoid bulge and without any significant soft tissue component were not included in the study.

Surgical Technique

All patients were operated on in the prone position under cervical traction. Traction was applied after induction of anesthesia and allowed a “floating” head posture. The primary purpose of traction was to maintain a stable head position and to prevent pressure on the face and eyes from the headrest, rather than to achieve any kind of distraction or reduction of instability. Atlantoaxial fixation was performed using the technique previously described by us.7,8 Subaxial cervical stabilization was carried out using Camille’s transarticular fixation technique.9,10 The spinous processes were sectioned at their bases, morselized, and used as autologous bone graft. The posterior elements of the atlas, axis, and laminae of the involved subaxial cervical segments were decorticated to create an optimal bed for bony fusion. No bone, soft tissue, osteophyte, or ROP resection was done for the purpose of decompression.

Results

All patients experienced significant relief from their presenting symptoms in the immediate postoperative period. The duration of follow-up ranged from 9 to 28 months. At a minimum follow-up of 6 months, clinical improvement was sustained and continued to progress. Although radicular symptoms were relatively less prominent, both radiculopathy and myelopathy showed improvement. The neurological status at the time of last follow-up is detailed in Table 2. Postoperative MRI demonstrated varying degrees of reduction in the size of both the ROP and the subaxial osteophytes at the final follow-up in all cases (Fig. 1). Discussion

Observations

The unique human standing posture has implications for the human spine. While extension of the spine is an active muscular movement that facilitates standing and sitting posture, flexion is essentially a passive movement. A large majority of the muscle bulk that works on the spinal column is located in the nape of the neck or back of the spine, and its fulcrum of activity is focused on the facets and facet articulation. Only a minority of muscles work around the intervertebral disc or the vertebral body. Acute or long-standing weakness of the muscles of the nape of the neck and the subaxial spine can be due to injury, misuse or disuse, and lack of their proper care by appropriate and full use. Owing to their focused activity, the impact of weakness of the muscles is primarily at the facet articulation, which becomes unstable; joint space is reduced, and there is listhesis of the inferior facet of the rostral vertebra over the superior facet of the caudal vertebra or there is vertical spinal instability.5,11 The net effect of instability is “telescoping” of the spinal segments. Unlike the flat orientation of facets of atlantoaxial joint wherein the dislocation is horizontal, due to oblique orientation of the facets in the cervical and dorsal spine and a more vertical orientation in the lumbar spine, the dislocation is not horizontal, but is vertical or oblique when observed from a profile view. Because of the structural difference, our studies identify facet instability at the craniovertebral junction as being of central or axial type and facet instability of the subaxial spine as vertical facet instability.11,12 Given the oblique profile and lateral location of the facets, some distance away from neural structures, abnormalities of face alignment or instability of the facet joint may not be clearly seen even on modern computer-based dynamic images. Both central or axial atlantoaxial instability and vertical spinal instability are manifested by several secondary alterations that have a protective role and are potentially or manifestly reversible after stabilization.

Retro-odontoid bone or cartilaginous masses have been described by various terms, including pseudotumor, inflammatory granulation tissue, degenerative fibrocartilaginous tissue, and cystic degeneration.1–4,13–16 These lesions may at times produce severe compression of the cervicomedullary junction. Owing to their tumor-like appearance on imaging and the frequent association with significant neurological deficits, such lesions have been historically regarded as primary pathological entities and managed accordingly. Consequently, surgical strategies focused on direct excision of the mass to relieve neural compression, and multiple approaches—anterior transoral, posterior, lateral, and anterolateral—were advocated for resection. In 2004, Goel et al. proposed that retro-odontoid tissue is not a primary lesion but rather a secondary manifestation of chronic or long-standing atlantoaxial instability, and that atlantoaxial stabilization alone constitutes definitive treatment, obviating the need for direct resection of ROP or any kind of bone or soft tissue decompression.1 ROP is more often a component of degenerative spinal disease and is encountered in older patients. Subsequent observations have demonstrated that retro-odontoid cysts and pannus formation related to rheumatoid affection of craniovertebral junction similarly represent secondary adaptive responses to atlantoaxial instability.2,3 More often, atlantoaxial instability is of central or axial variety and may not be identified by conventional and validated radiological parameters.13,17 Importantly, these studies showed that stabilization of the atlantoaxial joint could result in gratifying clinical recovery and immediate or early postoperative regression of the retro-odontoid pathology.

From this perspective, ROP is best understood as a reactive and potentially protective phenomenon arising from chronic instability at the atlantoaxial joint. The lesion is not only nonneoplastic but also potentially reversible once physiological stability is restored. Several later reports have corroborated this concept by documenting spontaneous resolution of retro-odontoid tissue following atlantoaxial fixation alone.18–20

On similar grounds, we observed that the formation of osteophytes, buckling of the posterior longitudinal ligament and ligamentum flavum, reduction of disc space height, abnormal bone formation with fusion, and the consequent narrowing of the spinal canal and neural foramina are all sequelae of chronic vertical spinal instability. This instability is related to weakness of the spinal extensor musculature located in the posterior spinal column and nape of the neck.5 Based on our clinical experience, we propose that stabilization—and not decompression—of the unstable spinal segments constitutes the definitive treatment. The rapidity and durability of clinical recovery, as well as the potential need for delayed decompressive surgery following this surgical strategy, have been discussed in our previous reports. Our related publications further document the fate and regression of osteophytes following treatment with spinal fixation alone.6 The exact time duration for regression of both ROP and subaxial osteophytes is variable. Based on our earlier studies, we estimate that radiological regression in their size typically becomes apparent over approximately 3–6 months. Early postoperative clinical recovery was observed after segmental spinal fixation despite the fact that the ROP and osteophytes remained essentially unchanged in size, and no direct decompression of the spinal canal or neural foramina was performed. Although stabilization of the atlantoaxial joint for ROP is now an accepted treatment modality, fusion alone, without decompression, for subaxial spinal degeneration remains to be validated by other groups. Despite our positive clinical experience and multiple clinical reports on the subject over the past 15 years, we believe that further studies are necessary, particularly in cases of radiculopathy, rapidly progressive myelopathy, and central cord syndrome.

Lessons

Muscular weakness, particularly in older patients, is a generalized process that commonly involves multiple spinal segments. Contrary to prevailing opinion, atlantoaxial instability is frequently associated with concomitant subaxial cervical spinal instability. The spectrum of cervical degenerative changes represents the end result of a cascade of secondary spinal alterations driven by underlying instability.

Given that ROP and osteophyte formation are components of this secondary degenerative complex—and are themselves consequences of segmental spinal instability—stabilization of the unstable spinal segments provides a rational basis for the surgical management of both conditions.
