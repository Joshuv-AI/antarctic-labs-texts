The ClockΔ19 mutation promotes osteoarthritis via impairing SIRT3-mediated mitochondrial homeostasis in mice
Authors: Qijing Zhang, Yanhong Liu, Yuwen Chen, Yanqing Zhou, Zuoqin Yan, Ruizhe Qian, Chao Lu, Bingxuan Hua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic degenerative joint disease characterized by cartilage breakdown and disrupted joint homeostasis. Mitochondrial dysfunction in chondrocytes contributes to cartilage degeneration, but the mechanisms linking circadian dysregulation to mitochondrial homeostasis in OA remain incompletely understood. ClockΔ19 mice are a well-established model of impaired CLOCK-dependent circadian transcriptional regulation and exhibit metabolic and mitochondrial abnormalities.MethodsTwo Gene Expression Omnibus datasets of human OA cartilage were integrated for bioinformatics analysis. OA severity was assessed in ClockΔ19 mice and wild-type littermates subjected to destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM). Primary chondrocytes were treated with interleukin-1β to mimic the inflammatory microenvironment of OA. Mitochondrial membrane potential, reactive oxygen species production, and NAD⁺/NADH ratio were evaluated by JC-1 staining, DHE staining, and biochemical assay, respectively. Protein expression and relative SOD2 K68 acetylation were examined by western blotting, immunoprecipitation, and immunostaining. CLOCK binding to the Sirt3 promoter was analyzed by chromatin immunoprecipitation. The role of SIRT3 was further examined by lentiviral overexpression in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes.ResultsIntegrated analysis of public datasets revealed circadian rhythm- related transcriptional alterations in human OA cartilage. ClockΔ19 mice developed more severe cartilage degeneration after DMM surgery than wild-type controls. In vitro and in vivo, ClockΔ19 mutation was associated with reduced SIRT3 expression and increased relative SOD2 K68 acetylation or Ac-SOD2 K68 immunoreactivity. In ClockΔ19 chondrocytes, these changes were accompanied by elevated oxidative stress, decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, and a reduced NAD⁺/NADH ratio under interleukin-1β stimulation. Mechanistically, CLOCK occupied the Sirt3 promoter, and ClockΔ19 chondrocytes showed reduced Sirt3 expression. Moreover, SIRT3 overexpression alleviated mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes.ConclusionCLOCK contributes to mitochondrial homeostasis in chondrocytes by regulating Sirt3 transcription and modulating the SIRT3–SOD2 K68 acetylation pathway. Disruption of this regulatory pathway may represent a mechanism linking circadian dysfunction to OA progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13018-026-07050-5.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic progressive joint degenerative disease that represents a leading cause of disability and pain worldwide [1]. OA lesions affect various parts of the joint, including articular cartilage, subchondral bone, ligaments, joint capsules, and synovium [2]. Its main pathological features are gradual loss of articular cartilage, subchondral osteosclerosis, and osteophyte formation [3]. However, there is currently no effective treatment to repair damaged cartilage, and patients with advanced OA usually rely on joint replacement surgery. Therefore, it is crucial to better understand the molecular mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of OA and to develop new therapeutic targets for the treatment of this disease.

CLOCK (Circadian Locomotor Output Cycles Kaput) is a core transcription factor in the mammalian circadian rhythm system and plays a central role in maintaining endogenous rhythmic oscillations [4]. The CLOCK protein forms a heterodimer with its partner, BMAL1 (Brain and Muscle ARNT-Like 1), and then the heterodimer binds to E-box cis-elements to activate the transcription of downstream clock genes such as Per (Period) or Cry (Cryptochrome), thereby establishing a canonical transcription–translation feedback loop (TTFL) that maintains approximately 24-hour biological rhythms [5]. Beyond its central role in the brain’s circadian pacemaker, CLOCK is broadly expressed in peripheral tissues, including the liver, heart, skeletal muscle, and cartilage [6–8], where it regulates metabolism, oxidative stress, cell proliferation, and inflammation [9–12]. Dysregulation or loss of CLOCK function has been linked to metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and musculoskeletal pathologies [13–17]. Our previous work showed that Clock mutation accelerates OA progression by promoting NF-κB activation, characterized by enhanced nuclear translocation, reduced NF-κB p65 acetylation at Lys310, and increased NF-κB p65 phosphorylation at Ser276 [17]. These findings suggest a link between CLOCK dysfunction and inflammatory transcriptional regulation in articular cartilage. Consistently, analysis of human transcriptomic datasets revealed enrichment of circadian rhythm-related pathways in OA cartilage, suggesting that circadian dysregulation may contribute to disease pathology. Among known variants, the ClockΔ19 mutation-characterized by deletion of the exon-19-encoded region (amino acids 514–564)-is the first identified CLOCK mutation in mammals and disrupts circadian and metabolic regulation [18]. Because this region is required for the transcriptional and histone acetyltransferase activities of CLOCK [19], ClockΔ19 mice provide a valuable model to investigate how impaired CLOCK-dependent transcriptional activity within the CLOCK–BMAL1 transcriptional machinery affects downstream biological functions.

Previous studies have shown that the loss of BMAL1 in the liver reduces Sirtuin 3 (SIRT3) activity, increases global mitochondrial protein acetylation, and impairs oxidative enzyme function [20]. However, the relationship between CLOCK and SIRT3 in chondrocytes has not been clearly established. Given the critical role of CLOCK-dependent circadian regulation in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis [21], we hypothesized that CLOCK may contribute to mitochondrial function through SIRT3-dependent mechanisms. To test this possibility, we focused on SIRT3 as a downstream effector of CLOCK in chondrocytes, serving as a potential link between circadian regulation and mitochondrial function.

SIRT3 is a class III NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase protein in mitochondria that plays a pivotal role in energy metabolism [22–27], oxidative stress defense [28–32], and inhibition of apoptosis [33–35]. SOD2, a key mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme, is an important downstream target of SIRT3-mediated deacetylation. Specifically, SOD2 acetylation at lysine 68 (K68), which is regulated by SIRT3, has been associated with reduced SOD2 antioxidant activity and increased mitochondrial oxidative stress [28, 29]. Loss of SIRT3 results in mitochondrial dysfunction, accelerated cellular senescence, and enhanced extracellular matrix degradation in osteoarthritic chondrocytes [36–38]. Accumulating evidence indicates that SIRT3 expression is under the regulation of circadian clock genes [20, 39, 40]. Further promoter analysis revealed potential CLOCK binding sites within the Sirt3 promoter region, suggesting that CLOCK may regulate Sirt3 transcription in chondrocytes.

In this study, we investigated whether circadian dysfunction contributes to OA progression through impaired mitochondrial homeostasis in chondrocytes. Using ClockΔ19 mice, primary chondrocytes, and integrated bioinformatics analysis, we examined the effects of ClockΔ19-associated CLOCK dysfunction on mitochondrial oxidative stress and the SIRT3–SOD2 K68 acetylation pathway. We further explored whether CLOCK directly regulates Sirt3 transcription and whether restoration of SIRT3 could rescue mitochondrial dysfunction in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis and screening

Human OA and normal cartilage transcriptomic datasets GSE114007 and GSE285234 were downloaded from the GEO database [41]. GSE114007 contains 18 normal cartilage samples and 20 OA cartilage samples, while GSE285234 contains 6 normal cartilage samples and 6 OA cartilage samples. Raw count data were processed using R software. For the integrated analysis, genes shared by both datasets were retained. Lowly expressed genes were filtered by retaining genes with counts ≥ 10 in at least two samples. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, with dataset origin specified as batch and disease status specified as condition in the design model to account for dataset-specific effects. The OA-associated effect was extracted using the contrast of OA versus normal cartilage. DEGs were defined as genes with adjusted P value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 1. PCA was performed using DESeq2 variance-stabilized expression values, and batch-effect correction was applied only for visualization. GSE114007 and GSE285234 were also analyzed separately using DESeq2 as dataset-specific sensitivity analyses. The expression levels of CLOCK, BMAL1, and SIRT3 were evaluated using DESeq2-normalized counts. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler package. All genes tested in the integrated DESeq2 analysis were used as the background gene set. Significant enriched terms were defined as terms with adjusted P value < 0.05. Selected enrichment results were visualized using the MicroBioinformatics platform [42].

Animals

The ClockΔ19 mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory and subsequently bred at the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University (GemPharmatech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Age-matched C57BL/6J mice were maintained under the same conditions and served as wild-type controls. Male mice aged 8–12 weeks were primarily used in this study. All animals were fed a standard chow diet and housed in a specific pathogen-free facility under controlled environmental conditions with either a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle (lights on at 8:00 a.m., off at 8:00 p.m.) or constant darkness. All experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Institutes of Health, USA) and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Medical College, Fudan University.

A surgical model of osteoarthritis was established by destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) in 8-week-old male mice. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and the fur around the right knee was shaved and sterilized. A medial parapatellar incision was made from the distal patella to the proximal tibia to expose the joint capsule. After opening the capsule and gently displacing the patellar tendon laterally, the medial meniscotibial ligament was transected to destabilize the medial meniscus. Sham-operated mice underwent the same surgical exposure without transection of the ligament. All animals received penicillin prophylaxis to prevent infection and were housed under standard conditions with free access to food and water. Five mice were included in each group. Knee joint samples were collected 8 weeks after surgery for further analysis.

Isolation and culture of primary murine chondrocytes

Primary articular chondrocytes were isolated from the knee joint cartilage of 5-day-old C57BL/6J mice according to a standard collagenase II digestion protocol. Neonatal cartilage was selected to ensure higher cell viability and purity because of minimal matrix calcification at this developmental stage. Cartilage tissues for primary chondrocyte isolation were collected within the same defined Zeitgeber time window (ZT12–ZT16) to minimize circadian variation across groups, followed by overnight digestion and standardized cell processing. Primary chondrocytes were cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. The medium was changed daily. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2, and all subsequent experiments were performed using passage 1 (P1) chondrocytes. Primary chondrocytes were cultured under standard conditions and were not subjected to dexamethasone- or serum shock-based synchronization before RNA/protein collection or functional assays. For inflammatory stimulation, chondrocytes were treated with murine interleukin-1β (IL-1β, 10 ng/mL; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA), reconstituted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Beyotime, Shanghai, China), for 24 h. For gene overexpression, cells were infected with recombinant lentivirus (WZ Biosciences, China) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 25 for 48 h, followed by the indicated experimental procedures.

Histological processing, OARSI scoring, and synovitis scoring

Knee joint samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) for 48 h and decalcified in 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA; Sigma-Aldrich). After decalcification, specimens were dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned sagittally at 6 μm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol series, and stained with hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) and Safranin O-Fast Green to evaluate cartilage structure and matrix integrity. Cartilage degeneration was evaluated using the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) histopathology scoring system (Glasson et al., 2010). Four joint quadrants-the medial femoral condyle, medial tibial plateau, lateral femoral condyle, and lateral tibial plateau-were graded on a semi-quantitative scale from 0 to 6 as follows: 0 = normal cartilage; 0.5 = loss of Safranin O staining without structural alterations; 1 = superficial fibrillation without loss of cartilage; 2 = vertical clefts extending below the superficial layer; 3 = vertical clefts or erosion extending into the mid-zone (1–25% of quadrant width); 4 = loss extending into the deep zone/calcified cartilage (25–50% of quadrant width); 5 = loss involving 50–75% of the quadrant width; 6 = loss involving > 75% of the quadrant, reaching the calcified cartilage. For each joint, the highest score observed among all sections and quadrants was recorded as the osteoarthritis severity grade. Synovial inflammation was further evaluated on H&E-stained sections using a semiquantitative synovitis scoring system [43]. Synovial lining layer hyperplasia, sublining cell density, and sublining fibrosis were independently scored in predefined joint regions, and the summed score was used as the synovitis score. Scoring was performed in a blinded manner.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with 1% PMSF and 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (PIC). Protein lysates were separated by 10% or 12.5% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection reagent (Bio-Rad, USA) and imaged using a gel documentation system (Tanon, China).

Primary antibodies used included SIRT3 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, #5490), SOD2 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, #13141), and acetyl-SOD2 K68 (1:1000, Affinity Biosciences, USA, #AF3751). β-Actin (1:10000, Affinity Biosciences, USA, #T0023) served as the loading control. Band intensity was quantified using Fiji software. For relative SOD2 K68 acetylation analysis, the Ac-SOD2 K68 signal was normalized to the corresponding total SOD2 signal.

Immunoprecipitation

For immunoprecipitation-based validation of relative SOD2 K68 acetylation, primary chondrocytes were lysed in immunoprecipitation lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Cell lysates were incubated with anti-SOD2 antibody overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Protein A/G magnetic beads (MedChemExpress, USA, #HY-K0202). The immunoprecipitates were washed, eluted, and subjected to western blotting. Ac-SOD2 K68 and SOD2 levels in the immunoprecipitated samples were detected by immunoblotting, and the IP-Ac-SOD2 K68 signal was normalized to the corresponding IP-SOD2 signal.

NAD+/NADH assay

NAD+/NADH levels were determined using the NAD+/NADH assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, #S0176S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Primary mouse chondrocytes were seeded into six-well plates (1.0 × 10⁶ cells/well), and 200µL of extraction buffer was added. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a multi-plate reader (BioTek).

Assessment of mitochondrial function

Mitochondrial membrane potential and intracellular ROS levels were assessed using JC-1 (#C2003S) and dihydroethidium (DHE; #S0064S) probes (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), respectively. Chondrocytes were incubated with JC-1 working solution at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark, followed by washing with buffer according to the manufacturer’s instructions. JC-1 red and green fluorescence signals were imaged using a laser scanning confocal microscope under fixed settings, and mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated based on the red/green fluorescence ratio. For ROS detection, cells were incubated with DHE at 37 ℃ for 30 min in the dark and washed gently with PBS. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope under identical exposure conditions from three to five randomly selected fields per sample.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the manufacturer’s protocol (TOYOBO, Japan). Transcript levels were quantified with SYBR Green-based real-time PCR using the LightCycler system (Roche, Switzerland) and Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (YEASEN, China). Relative mRNA expression was normalized to Actin and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primers used are shown below.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation and ChIP-qPCR assay

Cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 8 min and quenched with 0.125 M glycine for 10 min. After washing, cells were resuspended in low-salt lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES/KOH, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.05% SDS, 10 mM EDTA, and protease inhibitors). Chromatin was sheared into 200–400 bp fragments using a Q800R2 DNA shearing sonicator (Qsonica, Newtown, CT, USA) for 15 min with 30-s on/off cycles. The fragmented chromatin was immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated antibodies, followed by incubation with Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA; catalog no. sc-2003) for 5 h at 4 °C. Beads were sequentially washed twice with low-salt buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 250 mM LiCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40, and 0.5% sodium deoxycholate). DNA–protein complexes were eluted in elution buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS), treated with Proteinase K (Yeasen, catalog no. 10401ES60) and RNase A (Thermo Fisher, catalog no. EN0531), and incubated at 65 °C for 4 h to reverse cross-linking. The primers were as follows:

ChIP-qPCR, Primer1, F, 5′- ACTCTGCTGTAAGAAGGCCG-3′;

ChIP-qPCR, Primer1, R, 5′- GTTACCTTGACGACCGCAAC-3′.

ChIP-qPCR, Primer2, F, 5′- TCCAGGCCAGTCAGAGCTAT-3′;

ChIP-qPCR, Primer2, R, 5′- ACTTTTAACGTGTGTGCGCC-3′.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired t-tests, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

OA-associated transcriptional changes were then assessed using a batch-adjusted DESeq2 model. Using adjusted P value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 as the cutoff, the integrated analysis identified 1,019 DEGs between OA and normal cartilage samples, including 624 upregulated and 395 downregulated genes in OA (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Table S1). Enrichment analysis was performed separately for upregulated and downregulated DEGs. Upregulated DEGs were mainly enriched in extracellular matrix organization, cytokine production, inflammatory response, ECM-receptor interaction, PI3K-Akt signaling, and focal adhesion, whereas downregulated DEGs were enriched in circadian regulation of gene expression, circadian rhythm, reactive oxygen species metabolic process, FoxO signaling, and HIF-1 signaling pathways (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Table S2).

To further evaluate dataset-specific patterns, GSE114007 and GSE285234 were analyzed separately using the same DEG criteria. GSE114007 identified 2,167 DEGs, including 1,240 upregulated and 927 downregulated genes in OA, whereas GSE285234 identified 4 DEGs, including 3 upregulated and 1 downregulated gene in OA (Figure S1 and Supplementary Tables S3, S4). These independent analyses revealed dataset-specific differences in DEG numbers and were presented as sensitivity analyses.

Given the enrichment of circadian rhythm-related pathways, we next examined CLOCK, BMAL1, and SIRT3 expression in these datasets. BMAL1 was significantly decreased in OA cartilage in the integrated analysis and in GSE114007, whereas CLOCK and SIRT3 did not show significant differences at the mRNA level in these public transcriptomic datasets (Figure S2). Together with previous evidence that chondrocyte-specific Bmal1 deletion can lead to cartilage degeneration [44], these findings support the relevance of circadian transcriptional dysregulation in OA cartilage. Importantly, unchanged CLOCK mRNA expression does not necessarily indicate preserved CLOCK function, because CLOCK-dependent effects may depend on transcriptional activity rather than transcript abundance alone.

ClockΔ19 mutation aggravates osteoarthritic pathology in vivo

Based on these circadian rhythm-related transcriptional alterations, we next focused on the potential functional role of CLOCK in OA pathogenesis. Given previous evidence linking CLOCK to mitochondrial homeostasis and stress responses in peripheral cells [10], we investigated whether impaired CLOCK function affects OA progression in vivo. To this end, we established a destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) model in 8-week-old wild-type (WT) and ClockΔ19 mice. Safranin O-Fast Green staining showed that DMM surgery induced cartilage degeneration in both genotypes, while ClockΔ19 mice exhibited more severe pathological changes than WT mice, including greater loss of proteoglycan staining, surface fibrillation, and structural disruption of the articular cartilage (Fig. 2A). Consistently, OARSI scores were significantly increased after DMM surgery and were higher in ClockΔ19 mice than in WT mice subjected to the same procedure (Fig. 2B). To provide additional histological assessment beyond cartilage degeneration, synovial changes were further examined by H&E staining. Compared with WT mice, ClockΔ19 mice displayed more pronounced synovial thickening and histological abnormalities after DMM surgery (Fig. 2C). Semiquantitative analysis confirmed that synovitis scores were increased after DMM and were higher in ClockΔ19 mice than in WT controls (Fig. 2D). Immunohistochemical analysis further showed decreased COL2A1 staining and increased MMP13 staining in articular cartilage after DMM surgery, with these changes being more prominent in ClockΔ19 mice than in WT mice (Fig. 2E–G). These findings indicate that ClockΔ19 mutation is associated with aggravated DMM-induced cartilage degeneration, enhanced synovial inflammation, and a shift toward a catabolic cartilage phenotype in vivo. 3.ClockΔ19 mutation reduces SIRT3 expression and disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis

ClockΔ19 mutation reduces SIRT3 expression and disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis

Previous studies have shown that the loss of BMAL1 in the liver reduces SIRT3 activity and impairs mitochondrial oxidative function [20]. Since CLOCK, together with BMAL1, plays an important role in circadian transcriptional regulation and mitochondrial homeostasis [21], we next examined whether SIRT3 might be involved in the effects of ClockΔ19 mutation in chondrocytes. To mimic the inflammatory microenvironment characteristic of osteoarthritis, primary chondrocytes were treated with interleukin-1β (IL-1β, 10 ng/mL), an established in vitro model of cartilage inflammation [45]. Western blot analysis showed that SIRT3 protein levels were reduced in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes compared with WT controls and were further decreased after IL-1β stimulation (Fig. 3A, B). The Ac-SOD2 K68 signal was analyzed within the molecular weight range corresponding to SOD2/MnSOD. Based on this analysis, IL-1β stimulation did not significantly change the Ac-SOD2 K68/SOD2 ratio in WT chondrocytes, whereas it further increased this ratio in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes. Under IL-1β stimulation, ClockΔ19 chondrocytes also exhibited a higher Ac-SOD2 K68/SOD2 ratio than IL-1β-treated WT chondrocytes, suggesting that ClockΔ19 mutation enhances the susceptibility of chondrocytes to inflammation-associated relative SOD2 K68 acetylation (Fig. 3A, C). To further validate this finding, SOD2 immunoprecipitation followed by Ac-SOD2 K68 immunoblotting was performed. Consistently, the IP-Ac-SOD2 K68/IP-SOD2 ratio was increased in IL-1β-treated ClockΔ19 chondrocytes compared with IL-1β-treated WT chondrocytes, further supporting enhanced relative SOD2 K68 acetylation in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes under inflammatory stress (Figure S3A, B). Functionally, ROS fluorescence intensity was substantially elevated in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes relative to WT and was further increased under IL-1β treatment (Figure S3C, D). JC-1 staining revealed a significant decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential, reflected by a lower JC-1 aggregate-to-monomer ratio in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes compared with WT, which was further aggravated by IL-1β exposure (Figure S3E, F). In addition, the intracellular NAD+/NADH ratio was reduced in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes under IL-1β stimulation (Fig. 3H). To determine whether these alterations were also present in vivo, we further examined articular cartilage from DMM-operated WT and ClockΔ19 mice. Immunostaining showed reduced SIRT3 expression and increased Ac-SOD2 K68 immunoreactivity in the articular cartilage of ClockΔ19 mice compared with WT controls after DMM surgery (Fig. 3D-G). Together, these findings suggest that ClockΔ19 mutation is associated with reduced SIRT3 expression, greater inflammation-associated relative SOD2 K68 acetylation, oxidative stress, and impaired mitochondrial homeostasis in chondrocytes and articular cartilage.

4.CLOCK binds to the Sirt3 promoter and regulates Sirt3 expression

CLOCK binds to the Sirt3 promoter and regulates Sirt3 expression

Because CLOCK acts as a transcriptional regulator, we investigated whether it regulates Sirt3 at the mRNA level. qPCR analysis showed that Sirt3 transcript levels were significantly lower in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes compared with wild-type controls (Fig. 4A), consistent with reduced SIRT3 protein expression. Analysis using the JASPAR database revealed a predicted CLOCK-binding motif containing an E-box-like sequence (Fig. 4B), and two potential CLOCK binding sites were predicted within the promoter region of the mouse Sirt3 gene (Fig. 4C, D). To determine whether CLOCK occupies these regions (Site 1 at approximately − 1969 bp and Site 2 at approximately − 653 bp relative to the Sirt3 transcription start site), we performed ChIP-qPCR assays. The results demonstrated that the enrichment of CLOCK at the Sirt3 promoter was significantly lower in chondrocytes from ClockΔ19 mice compared with wild-type controls (Fig. 4E), indicating impaired CLOCK occupancy at the Sirt3 promoter in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes. Together, these findings support that CLOCK binds to the Sirt3 promoter and contributes to the maintenance of Sirt3 transcription in chondrocytes. Reduced CLOCK occupancy in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes may therefore contribute to reduced Sirt3 expression and impaired mitochondrial homeostasis.

5.SIRT3 overexpression alleviates mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes

5.SIRT3 overexpression alleviates mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes

SIRT3 overexpression alleviates mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes

To determine whether restoration of SIRT3 expression could alleviate the mitochondrial abnormalities in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes, we overexpressed SIRT3 in primary chondrocytes isolated from ClockΔ19 mice and examined its effects under PBS or IL-1β treatment. Western blot analysis confirmed efficient SIRT3 overexpression under both basal and IL-1β-stimulated conditions (Fig. 5A, B). IL-1β markedly increased the Ac-SOD2 K68/SOD2 ratio in empty vector-transduced ClockΔ19 chondrocytes, whereas SIRT3 overexpression significantly reduced this ratio under IL-1β stimulation, suggesting partial attenuation of IL-1β-induced relative SOD2 K68 acetylation (Fig. 5A, C). To assess whether SIRT3 overexpression also affected matrix metabolic markers, we further examined the expression of anabolic and catabolic genes in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes. qPCR analysis showed that, under IL-1β stimulation, SIRT3 overexpression partially reversed the IL-1β-induced increase in Mmp13 expression and decrease in Col2a1 expression (Fig. 5D, E). Consistently, DHE staining showed that IL-1β increased ROS levels in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes, whereas SIRT3 overexpression reduced ROS accumulation under both basal and IL-1β-stimulated conditions (Fig. 5F, G). JC-1 staining further showed that IL-1β decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, as indicated by a reduced red/green fluorescence ratio, while SIRT3 overexpression increased the red/green fluorescence ratio under both basal and IL-1β-stimulated conditions and partially restored mitochondrial membrane potential in IL-1β-treated ClockΔ19 chondrocytes (Fig. 5H, I). Together, these findings indicate that SIRT3 overexpression attenuates oxidative stress, improves mitochondrial dysfunction, and partially restores matrix metabolic homeostasis in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes.

Discussion

This study expands our understanding of how the circadian regulator CLOCK influences mitochondrial homeostasis in chondrocytes. Our data show that the ClockΔ19 mutation aggravates DMM-induced OA pathology and is associated with impaired mitochondrial function and enhanced oxidative stress. Mechanistically, CLOCK occupies the Sirt3 promoter and contributes to the maintenance of Sirt3 transcription, thereby maintaining SIRT3 expression and regulating SOD2 K68 acetylation. In ClockΔ19 chondrocytes, this regulatory pathway is disrupted, leading to reduced SIRT3 expression, increased relative SOD2 K68 acetylation under inflammatory stress, and excessive mitochondrial oxidative stress (Fig. 6). Moreover, restoration of SIRT3 expression alleviated mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes, supporting a functional link between CLOCK, SIRT3, and mitochondrial redox homeostasis. Together, these findings support a model in which CLOCK contributes to SIRT3-dependent mitochondrial homeostasis in chondrocytes.

Previous studies have mainly focused on the regulation of core clock genes or on the role of the CLOCK-BMAL1 complex in metabolic control [5, 46]. However, how circadian factors regulate mitochondrial function in chondrocytes has remained largely unclear. Our previous work identified a post-transcriptional mechanism in which CLOCK interacts with PUF60 to regulate Drp1 mRNA stability and mitochondrial homeostasis, linking circadian signaling to mitochondrial fission control [21]. In contrast, the present study supports a transcriptional mechanism in which CLOCK binds to the Sirt3 promoter and contributes to Sirt3 expression, thereby providing a molecular basis for circadian regulation of mitochondrial metabolism. Taken together, these findings suggest that CLOCK coordinates mitochondrial homeostasis through distinct yet complementary regulatory layers, including post-transcriptional control of mitochondrial dynamics via the PUF60-DRP1 axis and transcriptional regulation of mitochondrial redox balance via SIRT3.

Among the mitochondrial sirtuins, SIRT4 and SIRT5 are also localized within mitochondria together with SIRT3 [47]. However, SIRT3 is the major mitochondrial deacetylase most directly linked to SOD2 deacetylation and antioxidant defense [28, 29, 47]. Therefore, the present study focused on the CLOCK–SIRT3–SOD2 K68 pathway. The potential involvement of SIRT4 and SIRT5 in circadian regulation of chondrocyte mitochondrial homeostasis remains to be investigated in future studies. Given the NAD⁺-dependent nature of SIRT3 activity [38], the decreased NAD⁺/NADH ratio observed in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes may reflect disturbed cellular redox homeostasis associated with mitochondrial dysfunction. Because mitochondrial metabolism, circadian rhythmicity, and NAD⁺-dependent mitochondrial homeostasis are closely interconnected [20], a reduced NAD⁺/NADH ratio may create a metabolic environment unfavorable for SIRT3-dependent deacetylation. However, several mechanistic questions remain unresolved. Although our data support a link between CLOCK, SIRT3, and SOD2 K68 acetylation, direct SIRT3 enzymatic activity was not measured. In addition, the present study does not establish that CLOCK directly regulates NAD⁺ metabolic enzymes, NAD⁺ biosynthesis, or the NAD⁺/NADH ratio at the transcriptional level in chondrocytes. Future studies assessing NAD⁺ metabolic enzymes, SIRT3 enzymatic activity, and direct CLOCK transcriptional targets will be needed to clarify this mechanism.

This study has several limitations. First, we used ClockΔ19 mice, a global mutant model, rather than cartilage-specific or inducible Clock genetic models. Because the ClockΔ19 allele differs from complete Clock deletion in both genetic nature and circadian phenotype [48], the observed OA-related changes should be interpreted with caution. These phenotypes cannot be attributed exclusively to chondrocyte-autonomous CLOCK dysfunction, as systemic circadian, neuroendocrine, metabolic, or inflammatory effects may also contribute. In addition, ClockΔ19 does not directly correspond to a common human OA-associated CLOCK variant. Nevertheless, human CLOCK polymorphisms have been associated with circadian and metabolic traits relevant to OA susceptibility [49], and recent genetic and transcriptomic studies have implicated circadian clock pathways and core clock components, including CLOCK, ARNTL/BMAL1, and NR1D1, in OA-related biology [50, 51]. Thus, ClockΔ19 should be interpreted as a functional model of impaired CLOCK-dependent transcriptional regulation rather than as a direct equivalent of human OA-associated CLOCK variants. Moreover, DMM surgery was performed in 8-week-old mice, whereas mice older than 12 weeks may better represent skeletally mature adult animals. Future studies using cartilage-specific or inducible Clock models, preferably combined with DMM surgery in skeletally mature mice, will be needed to define the tissue-specific role of CLOCK in OA progression.

Second, CLOCK normally functions together with BMAL1 within the core circadian transcriptional machinery [5]. Because the exon-19-encoded region is required for normal CLOCK transcriptional and histone acetyltransferase activity [19], ClockΔ19 may impair CLOCK-dependent transcriptional output within the CLOCK–BMAL1 transcriptional machinery. Therefore, BMAL1-dependent transcriptional regulation may also be affected in this model, and we cannot exclude the contribution of altered BMAL1-dependent regulation to the mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress phenotypes observed in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes. Future studies using BMAL1 gain- and loss-of-function approaches and CLOCK–BMAL1 transcriptional assays will be needed to clarify this issue.

Third, the in vivo evidence for oxidative stress-related changes remains indirect. Although ROS levels were increased in ClockΔ19 chondrocytes in vitro, ROS or oxidative damage markers were not directly measured in cartilage tissue in vivo. Ac-SOD2 K68 immunostaining provided supportive evidence of altered SIRT3–SOD2-related mitochondrial regulation, but it should not be interpreted as a direct measurement of ROS or oxidative damage. Future studies using additional in vivo oxidative damage markers, such as 4-HNE, are needed. Moreover, the rescue effect of SIRT3 was examined only in vitro, and in vivo SIRT3 restoration will be required to further validate this mechanism.

Finally, primary chondrocytes were not subjected to dexamethasone- or serum shock-based synchronization before downstream analyses. Although cartilage tissues were collected within the same Zeitgeber time window and processed under standardized conditions, this design does not allow rigorous evaluation of phase-dependent effects. In addition, although synovitis was assessed, subchondral bone changes were not evaluated. Future studies incorporating time-course sampling, cartilage-specific models, and more comprehensive whole-joint analyses will be needed to define the tissue-specific and temporal role of the CLOCK–SIRT3–SOD2 K68 pathway in OA.

In summary, our findings support a model in which CLOCK contributes to Sirt3 transcriptional regulation and thereby helps maintain mitochondrial integrity and redox balance in chondrocytes. These results provide new insight into how circadian dysregulation may contribute to OA pathogenesis.

Conclusions

ClockΔ19 mutation is associated with impaired SIRT3–SOD2 K68 regulation, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in chondrocytes. Restoration of SIRT3 expression partially re-establishes mitochondrial homeostasis, highlighting the CLOCK–SIRT3–SOD2 K68 pathway as an important regulator of cartilage homeostasis and a potential mechanism contributing to osteoarthritis progression.
