Effectiveness Spatiotemporal of Pressure Unloading and Gas Extraction in Remote Upper Protected Seam Mining
Authors: Zujin Bai, Hongbo Kou, Qian Ma, Jun Deng, Xiang-hong Li, Xin Yi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere gas hazards are increasing in deep mining areas, and there exists an enormous traditional challenge for protected seam mining. In this work, we conducted a multifaceted investigation into the spatiotemporal effectiveness exerted by pressure unloading from a distant safeguarded stratum in concert with the application of gas extraction methodologies. The stress and displacement evolution in the protected coal seam (PCS) are analyzed by applying Flac3D, and then positives are verified by the measurement of coal seam deformation and investigation of the unloading boundary. The results show that diminution coefficient of 0.62, and record an expansive deformation rate of 5.83%. The opportune temporal window for effective gas drainage is discerned, with its time window spans from 58 to 67 days as the expanse between 350 and 400 m progresses at the working face. Continuous and effective gas extraction occurs when transverse cracks in the PCS reach an optimal state of pressure relief. The final cumulative gas extraction was 320,300 m3 with an extraction efficiency of 34.3%. A residual gas volume of 3.68 m3/t and a residual gas pressure of 0.5 MPa reflect the efficient gas extraction.

Introduction

Coal accounts for nearly one-third of the primary energy consumption in the world. The situation of rich in coal, poor in oil, and low in gas makes the energy center of gravity always in the direction of coal China.1,2 Coal bed methane (CBM) is a byproduct of the coal generation process with the advantage of a clean energy source.3 It is also the dominant factor in gas explosions and coal or gas protrusion accidents, causing tremendous hidden danger to coal mining.4 Although the number of major and especially major accidents coal mining industry is relatively small in China, accounting for one-third of the total number of accidents. The proportion of fatalities is as high as 65%, which shows that the death rate in the coal mining industry is extremely high in the event of an accident.5 By employing unloading gas extraction technology and achieving effective coal and gas comining under coal seam group circumstances, protective seam mining reduces the risk of coal seam protrusion and, therefore, gas mishaps.6

The purpose of protective seam mining is to enhance the permeability of the protected coal seam (PCS) for efficient gas extraction.7 The investigation of the interaction between an unloaded coal body and axial stress plays a crucial for determining the penetration characteristics under triaxial stress conditions within PCS.8 There are many reports about distribution characteristics of gas permeability under orthogonal repeated mining conditions through numerical simulation combined with on-site experiments.9 The examination of expansion and deformation rates, as well as permeability changes within the coal seams following the extraction of the lower protective seam, which played a vital aspect in PCS.10 The growth of the coal seam fractures revealed a distinct butterfly grid pattern, and mining the upper protective seam considerably improved the coal seam’s permeability.11 The gas seepage characteristics of axially unloaded coal bodies under different surrounding and gas pressure conditions were investigated by using an experimental system for mechanical seepage analysis of coal rocks.12 Mining a soft rock protection seam is considered a viable method where the coal seam cannot be mined for protection, Cheng et al. determined the optimum seam spacing of 59 m for setting a soft rock protection seam.13 To quantitatively characterize the permeability distribution inside the underlying PCS along the strike direction at the limit of the mine holes, it is split into four discrete zones. Remarkably, the analysis reveals that the actual area exhibiting heightened permeability surpasses the total area of the mine openings within the protective seam.14 The unloading impact of the initial coal seam mining holds significant importance in the context of gas extraction. Furthermore, there are also some works that use numerical simulations to study the evolution patterns of plastic zones, stress distribution, and permeability within coal seams during mining, with the ultimate goal of determining unloading effects.15 The behavior of the overloaded rock, the redistribution of stresses, and the deformation characteristics of the coal seams located below the mining void area were characterized using 3DEC software. The effective unloading zone expands as the mining depth increases, providing incremental protection in a layered fashion for the sharply inclined extra-thick coal seams.16 After the protective seam is mined, three distinct zones of the overlaying rock formation exhibit dynamic evolutionary patterns that are studied using an integrated method based on seepage mechanics and damage mechanics principles. In addition, the concept of equivalent layer spacing permeability increase coefficient is introduced as a new parameter to describe these evolutionary phenomena.17 Three distinct zones may be seen in the pattern of stress distribution that follows the mining of the lower protective seam: the initial stress zone, the pressure release zone, and the stress concentration zone.18 A stress path-dependent permeability law has been developed, and a corresponding algorithm has been devised and integrated into the FLAC3D software. This novel permeability law was employed for the first time to simulate the unloading and penetration processes of the protective seam, along with the flow dynamics of coal seam methane during the mining operations of the upper longwall within the protective seam.19 Li et al. seek to ascertain if the gas in the PCS can be released successfully and to find a workable solution to the issue of the coal roof and floor covering rock being destroyed when the protective seam is mined.20 The findings demonstrate that there are increasing, steady, and decaying phases in the flow of pressure relief gas extracted from PCS by surface drilling.21 Based on the characteristics of the distribution of gas permeability coefficient under repeated mining settings, a precise gas extraction technique is created. Both the total increased stress belt of the coal pillar damaged zone and the total lowered stress belt of the other zones in the vertical direction have a significant impact on methane migration.22 The protective coal layer’s protected scope is determined by Fang et al. using varying dip angles.23 Zhang et al. classified the rock mass above the protected coal seam into five zones that are separated horizontally: premining, compression, expansion, recovery, and recompacted.24

The aforementioned academics have investigated how the mining of the protective seam alters the permeability and stress laws in the PCS. However, few studies have been reported on the spatiotemporal effects of protective seams over long distances. So, a case of a long-distance upper PCS (average layer spacing 71 m) is specially selected from Henan province, China in this work. The spatiotemporal evolution law of the internal stress in the PCS was analyzed by employing Flac3D during the mining of the protective seam. Lastly, the CBM extraction scenario was used to examine and validate the PCS’s permeability, expansion, and deformation rates.

Methods

Project Background

Pingdingshan Tian’an Coal Industry Co., Ltd., is located in the middle-western part of Pingdingshan Coal Field in Henan Province, China. The W8 coal seam has an average thickness of 2.2 m, while the D5–6 coal seams are spaced 71 m apart. The D seam is a well-known coal seam with a maximum gas pressure of 2.1 MPa and a gas concentration of up to 7.93 m3·t–1. W8 is a nonprotruding seam located at a shallow depth of −650 m with a strike direction length of 2300 m and a dip length of 220 m, with surface elevations ranging from 160 to 260 m. The coal has a bulk weight of 1.5 kg·m–3 and a coefficient of firmness between 1.0 and 1.5.

The working face adopts a “one side and four lanes” arrangement, and the lengths of the machine and wind roadways in the W8 coal seam are 2300 and 2319 m, respectively. Furthermore, the 2472 m-long high extraction highway for the machine roadway is positioned in the rock strata, 18 m above the W8 coal seam, and is staggered 30 m outward. The inner segment of the air highway’s high extraction roadway is positioned in the rock strata, 18 m above the top plate of the W8 coal seam, and is staggered by 20 m within. The working face has a total length of 609 m. The layout of the working face is shown in Figure 1.

The uppermost layer of rock will decompress if the bottom protecting seam is mined. The cloud plot of the stress field of the overlying rock layer in the direction of the strike direction is taken to study the trend of stress change in order to invest the pattern of stress field changes in the mining operation. Stress maps of the overlying rock layers were selected at 20, 50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 m advancement of the working face, as displayed in Figure 3. The stresses in the protective seeam hardly affected by mining, and still in the initial state of stress as the face advanced up to 20 m. As the face continues to advance, the stresses in the protective seam begin to decrease for especially at 50 m, indicating that the pressure is beginning to be released. As the face continued to advance to 100, 150, 200 m, and finally 250 m, the stresses within the PCS continued to decrease. The unloading area within the PCS shows a gradual expansion trend.

Mechanism of the Spatiotemporal Effect of Pressure Unloading

The formation of an internal stress field in a coal rock body is the basic reason for the creation and development of internal cracks. The overlying rock layer experiences stress variations attributable to self-weight and ground stress during the mining of protective seams, giving rise to localized stress concentration and reduction. The main manifestation of this is a decrease in stress in the overlying rock seam and an increase in stress on both sides of the extraction zone from Figure 3. Horizontal stress is symmetrically distributed, and the overlying rock layer depressurization from Figure 7. As stress accumulates, the coal seam experiences compression, leading to the closure of pre-existing fissures. This results in a diminished permeability coefficient, establishing a gas flow state characterized by reduced permeability and flow rates. Subsequently, stress changes within the rock formation affect changes in displacement. In the proximity of the fissure zone and subsidence area, the protective seam undergoes tensile deformation due to horizontal stress influence. Simultaneously, the upper and lower plates of the coal seam experience expansion deformation caused by vertical stress impact, resulting in differential displacements.

The expansion and deformation of coal seam play a pivotal role in widening the primary fissures within the PCS and augmenting secondary fissure development. It creates channels for the free gas to escape, greatly increasing the coal seam’s permeability. This enhanced permeability, in conjunction with gas flow, provides the necessary conditions for gas desorption.28 Specifically, significant advancement of the work face is needed to cause changes in the stress conditions within the PCS when the protective seam is located far from the PCS. The displacement of the PCS gradually diminishes with the progression of the working face, generating a discernible spatial and temporal effect on the depressurization process of the PCS. Leveraging the stress concentration and depressurization effects within the protective seam in a judicious manner ensures coal seam permeability and gas flow stability. It approach facilitates decompression gas extraction, effectively mitigating the risk of coal seam protrusion.

Unloading Wwindow Period Determination

On-site assessments were conducted to explore the gas extraction effectiveness of unloading drill holes within the D5–6 coal seam. Only the low amounts of gas from drill holes 1–1# and 2–2# were extracted once the mining face had advanced by 20 m. The observation indicated that the PCS had experienced minimal impact from mining, and the fissures had not yet fully developed at extended distances. The stress conditions within the PCS change significantly as the working face advances. Coal seam expanded and deformed, resulting in mining-induced cracks, and the gas transitioned to a state of increased permeability and flow, when the mining face advanced to 86.2–87.2 m in the W8 seam. As a result, mining of high concentrations of gas began in the depressurized zone of the D5–6 seam. Lateral development of fractures in the PCS led to the extraction of high concentrations of natural gas in drill holes 7–1# and 10–2# when the workforce was advanced to 200 m. Nevertheless, after the mining face progressed 400m, the transverse tensile fissures within the vicinity of drill holes 1–1# and 2–2# closed, resulting in a decline in the extracted gas concentration until no further gas could be extracted. Most of the drill holes exhibited stable gas concentrations within the range of 70 to 100% for a certain period. The field results of the practice are consistent with the numerical simulations.

The window period for unloading gas extraction extends until the mining face advances approximately 350 to 400 m from Table 2. Taking into account the speed of the advancing working face, the duration of this window period is calculated to be between 58 and 67 days. This extended window period proves highly advantageous for unloading gas extraction. This window period must remain continuous and uninterrupted to enhance the efficiency of unloading gas extraction.

Examination of the Effectiveness and Scope of Protection

Effectiveness of Unloading Gas Extraction

A total of 108 sets of 324 D5–6 relief gas drainage holes were drilled in the high extraction lanes of the machine tunnel. These drill holes operate at a negative pressure of 8 kPa for extraction. The data related to pressure relief gas extraction is systematically collected and recorded every month. The monthly average flow rate in the main roadway ranged from 18.74 to 28.79 m3/min during mining at the W8-32010 face. And the average concentration ranged from 19.68 to 36.14%. The final cumulative gas extraction amounted to 5,534,062.29 m3.

W8-32010 machine roadway highway to the Group D coal seam construction of a total of three pressure measurement drill holes. Gas pressures of 2.1, 0.85, and 0.85 MPa were measured at 1250, 1950, and 2150 m respectively. The measured maximum gas content of 3.12 m3/t, the backward thrust of the maximum gas content of 7.73 m3/t (gas pressure of 2.1 MPa), and the maximum gas content of 1.06 million m3, the gas reserve of Group D coal seam is 1.06 million m3. The Group D drill holes exceeded the time window of extraction, and the extraction is over. With a gas extraction rate of 34.3%, a residual gas content of 3.68 m3/t, a residual gas pressure of 0.5 MPa, and an average monthly concentration ranging from 19.68 to 36.14%, the original gas content of the PCS is 6.643 m3/t, with a corresponding gas reserve of 934,000 m3 within the unloading range. The substantial increase in gas extraction indicates that the permeability of the coal body has been substantially improved to protect the seams from being mined.

Conclusions

This study investigated the protective impact of a long-distance upper PCS using a mix of field testing and numerical simulation calculations. Following that, the laws of protection scope, variable features of expansion and deformation coefficient, and distribution law and displacement of stress were discovered. Lastly, the unloading gas spatiotemporal distribution law was used to propose the ideal time frame for unpressurized gas extraction. The following is a summary of the comprehensive conclusions.

As the mining face advances, the stresses within the PCS continue to decrease obtained from sophisticated numerical simulations using FLAC3D software. This is accompanied by a concomitant increment in displacement, culminating in an expansion of the decompression zone. Notably, an inverse correlation exists between the progression of the mining face and stress levels within the seam, contrasted with a direct correlation to seam displacement. The calculated unloading coefficient of 0.62 and expansion deformation of 5.83% reflect significant unloading and permeability effects within the coal seam.

Furthermore, the optimal time window for pressurized gas unloading during extraction persisted as the face advanced from 350 to 400 m. This period it was ranged from 58 to 67 days according the advancement velocity of the working face.
