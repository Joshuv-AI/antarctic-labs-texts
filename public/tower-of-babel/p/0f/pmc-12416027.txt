Morphological and Phylogenetic Characterization of Four Additional Xylaria-associated Species: Three New Species and One Newly Recognized Species in Korea
Authors: Dae Ho Kim, Young-Nam Kawg, Hyung So Kim, Sang-Kuk Han, Chang-Sun Kim, Jong Kyu Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe genus Xylaria Hill ex Schrank is an ecologically significant group of wood-decaying fungi that contribute to nutrient cycling and ecosystem stability in forests worldwide. Despite a recent global increase in the descriptions of new species, Korean Xylaria species have rarely been reexamined using modern taxonomic frameworks. In this study, dried specimens preserved at the Korea National Arboretum were re-identified through integrative morphological and molecular analyses using four genetic markers (ITS, ACT, TUB2, and RPB2). Consequently, one previously unrecorded species (Xylaria frustulosa) and three novel species (Heteroxylaria aciculiformis, Xylaria gwangneungensis, and Xylaria pratimongolica) were discovered. Morphological observations revealed that H. aciculiformis is characterized by its slender, needlelike, unbranched stromata with fine longitudinal striations and semi-exposed perithecia; X. gwangneungensis has distinctive capitate stromata with a short stipe and a solitary perithecium; and X. pratimongolica exhibits an erect, oriental brush-like stroma partially developed underground. Phylogenetic analyses supported the unique status of these taxa, as they formed distinct and well-supported clades separate from known species within Xylariaceae. These findings further elucidate the diversity of the Xylariaceae family and demonstrate the value of combining morphological and molecular data to resolve cryptic species within this genus.Keywords: Xylaria, Heteroxylaria, fungi, taxonomy

Introduction

The genus Xylaria Hill ex Schrank, commonly referred to as wood-decaying fungi, is widely distributed across temperate, tropical, and subtropical regions [1]. Members of this genus primarily colonize dead wood but are also found on various substrates, including fallen fruits and seeds, leaf litter, petioles, ant nests, and soil [2]. Xylaria species contribute significantly to forest ecosystems by decomposing recalcitrant plant materials, such as lignin and cellulose, thereby facilitating nutrient cycling, improving soil fertility, and supporting ecological stability [3]. They also produce a wide range of secondary metabolites with notable biological activities, including antimicrobial, antifungal, and cytotoxic effects, underscoring their potential application in biotechnology and natural product development [4].

The stromata of Xylaria species exhibit considerable variation in size, shape, and coloration, depending on their developmental stage. Despite this variation, the stromata are typically characterized by erect, woody, and leathery structures in which perithecia are embedded. This feature is regarded as one of the most important morphological characteristics for identifying the members of a genus [5,6]. Nevertheless, species-level identification remains difficult because of high morphological similarity among taxa and the presence of cryptic species that cannot be distinguished based on morphology alone [7,8].

To address these challenges, recent studies have adopted integrative taxonomic approaches that combine morphological and molecular data. These methods have led to the discovery of numerous new species and have significantly advanced our understanding of phylogenetic relationships within the genus [9–12]. As of September 2024, more than 660 species of Xylaria were recorded in the Index Fungorum (https://www.indexfungorum.org/). The number of species has increased rapidly recently, with more than 110 new species being described between 2020 and 2024. For example, Ju and Hsieh [11] and Ju et al. [12] described 19 new species in 2023, and Li et al. [8] reported an additional 40 new species in 2024. In addition to the increase in species descriptions, several new genera such as Heteroxylaria [13] and Neoxylaria [14] have also been proposed.

In South Korea, 11 Xylaria species have been documented to date. These include X. carpophila, X. cubensis, X. filiformis, X. hypoxylon, X. longipes, X. oxyacanthae, X. persicaria, X. polymorpha, X. ripicola, X. rogersii, and X. tentaculata [15–18]. However, most of these species were reported before the 2000s and have not been reassessed using recent taxonomic frameworks. Therefore, re-identification of Xylaria specimens collected in Korea based on updated morphological and molecular criteria is required. This study aimed to reevaluate Xylaria specimens preserved in the Korea National Arboretum (KNA). During the re-identification process, several species that had not been previously described in Korea were identified. Additionally, some specimens exhibited morphological characteristics that differed from those of previously reported taxa. The findings of this study provide new insights into the diversity of Xylaria and related genera.

Morphological observations

Photographs captured at the time of specimen collection were used to document the ecological context. To investigate the morphological characteristics of the Xylaria specimens, both the macroscopic and microscopic features were examined and photographed using a stereomicroscope (Leica Z16 APO, Wetzlar, Germany) and an optical microscope (Leica DM 6000 FS, Wetzlar, Germany). The specimens were carefully cut into thin sections and mounted in distilled water, Melzer’s reagent, and 1% SDS solution for microscopic observation.

DNA extraction and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification

Genomic DNA was extracted from the dried specimens using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD). Molecular identification was conducted by amplifying four gene regions: the internal transcribed spacer (ITS), alpha-actin (ACT), beta-tubulin (TUB2), and RNA polymerase II second largest subunit (RPB2). For PCR amplification, different primer combinations were used for each gene region. The ITS region was amplified using the primer pair ITS5 and ITS4 [19] or ITS5 and LR3 [20]. The ACT region was amplified using ACT-512F and ACT-783R [21]. The TUB2 region was amplified using either T1 or T22 [22] or bt2a and bt2b [23]. For the RPB2 region, amplification was performed using either fRPB2-5F and fRPB2-7cR [24] or 980F and fRPB2-7cR [25].

PCR reactions were prepared using AccuPower® Taq PCR PreMix (Bioneer, Daejeon, South Korea) or AccuPower® Gold HotStart Taq PCR PreMix & Master Mix (Bioneer Inc., Daejeon, South Korea). A typical 20 μL reaction contained 1 μL of each primer, 1 μL of DNA template, and nuclease-free water added to reach the final volume, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The PCR PreMix tubes contained Taq DNA polymerase, dNTPs, Tris–HCl, KCl, MgCl2, stabilizer, and a tracking dye. PCR amplification was performed under the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94 °C for 20 s, 56 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, and a final extension step at 72 °C for 3 min. Reactions were performed at 4 °C.

Sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

The PCR products were purified and sequenced by Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, South Korea). Raw sequences were edited and assembled using Geneious Prime®, and sequence similarity was assessed through BLASTn searches (v. 2.13.0) against the GenBank database.

Sequence alignment was performed using MAFFT version 7 (https://mafft.cbrc.jp/). Alignments were manually refined before phylogenetic analyses. Maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) analyses were performed using the GTR + I + G model, which was selected as the best-fitting nucleotide substitution model using JModelTest v. 2.1.10 [26]. For BI, the analysis was run for 1,000,000 generations, with the initial 250,000 generations discarded as burn-ins. The resulting consensus trees were visualized using FigTree v. 1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/). Biscogniauxia arima YMJ122 [27] was used as the outgroup.

Results

Phylogenetic analysis

To re-identify Xylaria specimens in KH, nucleotide sequences from four genetic loci: ITS, ACT, TUB2, and RPB2 were analyzed. BLAST searches against the National Center for Biotechnology Information GenBank database were conducted for all sequences, and similarities to existing taxa were evaluated.

Specimen KA21-1304 exhibited high sequence identity with X. frustulosa at all loci. In the ITS region, it showed 99.44% identity (505 of 513 bp) with X. frustulosa SGSF173 (GenBank accession no. MT146476). The ACT gene showed 97.42% identity (302/310 bp) to X. frustulosa 771 (GQ449237), TUB2 showed 97.50% identity (1483/1521 bp) with X. frustulosa 92092010 (GQ495944), and RPB2 showed 97.67% identity (1131/1158 bp) with X. frustulosa 92092010 (GQ844838). These results confirmed that KA21-1304 belongs to a genetic lineage that is highly consistent with X. frustulosa.

Specimens KA21-0529 and KA23-0243 exhibited the highest sequence similarity to the unidentified specimen, Xylaria sp. 301, across all four loci. In the ITS region, both isolates showed 99.65% identity (575/577 bp) with LC462020. The ACT gene sequences showed 99.61% (258/259 bp) and 100% (259/259 bp) identity with LC464982; TUB2 showed 99.65% (1153/1157 bp) and 100% (370/370 bp) identity with LC464983; and RPB2 showed 99.74% (1172/1175 bp) and 99.91% (1122/1123 bp) identity with LC467015. Among the described species, the highest similarity was observed with Xylaria rohrensis (YMJ2139, HAST145767); however, the sequence identities were notably lower across all loci (92.8–92.97% for ITS, 94.33–94.67% for ACT, 89.12–87.97% for TUB2, and 91.42–92.03% for RPB2) indicating substantial genetic divergence from known species.

The group identified as Xylaria gwangneungensis (KA19-0024, KA21-0065, KA23-0029, KA24-0016, KA24-0041, and KA24-0359) showed the highest similarity to unidentified endophytic Xylaria strains. The ITS sequences matched Xylaria sp. KoRLI047341 (MN341824) with 99.11–99.64% identity (555–558 out of 560 bp). The ACT sequences showed 94.12–94.49% identity (256–257/272 bp) to Xylaria sp. genotype 219 FL0359 (KU684048), and TUB2 matched Xylaria sp. genotype 210 FL0043 (KU684137) with 93.99–94.54% identity (1016–1022/1081 bp). Notably, KA24-0016 displayed a lower similarity (90.91%, 300/330 bp) to TUB2. RPB2 sequences showed 95.73–96.22% identity (987–992/1031 bp) with Xylaria genotype 575 NC0985 (KU684272). Comparisons with described species revealed consistently lower similarities: ITS showed 88.58–88.75% identity with Xylaria arbuscula RHP 21 (MT215513), ACT and RPB2 displayed 91.55–92.93% and 94.87–95.92% identity with Xylaria finismundoensis BHI-F502, and TUB2 matched Xylaria coccophora 786 (GQ502705) showed 85.89–89.59% identity.

Specimens KA23-0382 and KA23-0383, collectively designated Xylaria pratimongolica, demonstrated low similarity with known Xylaria species. ITS sequences showed 91.02% identity (527/579 bp) to Podosordaria tulasnei CBS 128.80 (KT281902) and Xylaria vaporaria CBS 386.35 (MH855714). ACT sequences matched Xylaria genotype 317 FL0491 (KU684052) with 81.27% and 81.91% identity (230/283 and 231/282 bp) and Xylaria ischnostroma SWUF18-22.1 (OQ845427) with 79.87% and 79.80% identity (238/298 and 237/297 bp), respectively. TUB2 showed 88.10% identity (918/1042 bp) with genotype 317 FL0491 and 85.17% (1108/1301 bp) with X. fimbriata 491 (GQ502705). The RPB2 sequences exhibited 87.50% identity (924/1056 bp) with Podosordaria sp. CNF 2/11073 (OQ877111), and 87.10–87.27% (1033–1035/1186 bp) to X. fimbriata 491.

A ML phylogenetic tree was constructed using the concatenated sequences from all four loci (Figure 1). Specimen KA21-1304 was placed within the PO clade and clustered with the reference sequence of X. frustulosa, thus confirming its identification. Specimens KA21-0529 and KA23-0243 were placed within the HH clade, X. gwangneungensis within the HY clade, and X. pratimongolica (KA23-0382 and KA23-0383) specimens were grouped within the TE clade. However, in all three cases, the specimens formed distinct, well-supported subclades that were separate from the reference species included in the phylogenetic tree. Hence, the phylogenetic positions of these three groups indicate that they are likely novel species.

Discussion

In this study, four species within the Xylariaceae family were investigated using a combination of distinctive morphological characteristics and robust multi-gene phylogenetic analyses. Among them, H. aciculiformis, X. gwangneungensis, and X. pratimongolica are novel and identified for the first time, while X. frustulosa is newly recorded in Korea. Each species provides new insights into the taxonomic diversity within the family.

Heteroxylaria aciculiformis is easily distinguished by its slender, unbranched stroma with an acicular sterile apex, fine longitudinal striations in the fertile region, and semi-exposed perithecial mounds. This species is morphologically similar to X. noduliformis, X. diminuta, and X. hedyosmicola; however, X. noduliformis and X. diminuta typically bear curved stromata with perithecial mounds half- to fully exposed and smaller ascospores (<13 μm). In addition, X. hedyosmicola differs by having a conspicuously long filiform sterile apex at the stromatal tip and by producing ascospores of comparable size that are surrounded by a hyaline sheath. Although phylogenetically similar to H. rohrensis and H. reevesiae, they differ morphologically. H. aciculiformis has small, needlelike stromata with slightly protruding perithecial mounds and larger ascospores (13–15 × 6.8–8 μm), whereas H. rohrensis possesses larger stromata (4–6 cm) with inconspicuous perithecial mounds and small ascospores (10–12 × 6–7 μm), and H. reevesiae shows inconspicuous perithecial mounds and ascospores (9–11 × 4–6 μm) [9,13,28]. Phylogenetic analyses based on the ITS, ACT, TUB2, and RPB2 gene regions confirmed their close relationship with these taxa but supported their status as distinct species due to morphological differences. Interestingly, this species was found not only on fallen fruits but also on the petioles of fallen leaves, suggesting a broader substrate range than previously known for Heteroxylaria species [13].

Xylaria gwangneungensis is characterized by a unique capitate stroma with a short stipe and globose apex bearing a single perithecium. Phylogenetically, it is closely related to X. coccophora, X. oligotoma, and X. venustula within the HY clade; however, while these related species have a pointed apex, X. gwangneungensis possesses a capitate stroma with a solitary perithecium [9]. Although identical ITS sequences have been documented as lichen-associated endophytes, repeated collections from the decaying wood of Quercus serrata and other hardwoods confirmed its saprobic role, suggesting that this species may have both endophytic and saprobic phases.

Xylaria frustulosa, previously misclassified into various related genera owing to its unusual flattened to pulvinate stroma, was confirmed as a member of Xylaria by its typical unitunicate asci with an amyloid apical apparatus and pigmented ascospores [29,30]. Phylogenetic placement within the PO clade further supports this identification. Although Van Der Gucht [31] described a short germ slit, this feature was not observed here, which is consistent with the observations of Fournier et al. [32]. The ascospores were small, pale olive brown, and matched previously reported dimensions and coloration. This specimen was collected from decayed wood in an inland forest, extending its known ecological range in Korea.

Xylaria pratimongolica was discovered in the inland grasslands of Mongolia, a region where the diversity and distribution of macrofungi remain insufficiently studied despite its unique biogeographic context [33]. This species is morphologically similar to X. ripicola in having an erect stroma with a slender sterile apex and immersed perithecia but differs in its occasional branching into two or three segments and its white fertile part, whereas X. ripicola remains unbranched with a black fertile region [17]. Multi-gene phylogenetic analysis placed X. pratimongolica near X. fimbriata within the PO clade but with lower sequence similarity (85.6–85.8%), supporting its status as a distinct species. The discovery of this species highlights its potential for uncovering novel fungal taxa in Mongolian grassland ecosystems.

In conclusion, this study elucidates the diversity and taxonomy of Xylariaceae by combining detailed morphological observations and molecular phylogenetic data. Particularly, it underscores how molecular tools continue to complement traditional morphological analyses, leading to the description of new taxa and records. Continued integrative research, including broader sampling and ecological studies, is essential to resolve the taxonomy and clarify the ecological roles of this important fungal family.
