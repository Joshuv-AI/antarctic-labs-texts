Exploring XDH expression as a prognostic marker for urothelial carcinoma
Authors: Meng-Yun Hung, Chien-Feng Li, Hsiang-Ying Lee, Hung-Lung Ke, Yow-Ling Shiue, Hsin-Chih Yeh, Wen-Jeng Wu, Yu-Ching Wei, Wei-Ming Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeXanthine dehydrogenase (XDH) serves as a key enzyme in purine metabolism and plays a significant role in carcinogenesis. However, the clinical significance of XDH in urothelial carcinoma (UC) progression remains unclear. Using a transcriptomic UC database (GSE32894), we found that among the genes related to XDH activity (GO:0004854), XDH was significantly downregulated during UC progression. Therefore, we aimed to assess the prognostic value of XDH immunoexpression in urinary bladder UC (UBUC) and upper tract UC (UTUC) patients.MethodsImmunohistochemical staining for XDH was performed on 340 UTUC and 295 UBUC specimens. The association of XDH expression with clinicopathological features and patients’ outcomes, including disease-specific survival (DSS) and metastasis-free survival (MFS), was analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test, Kaplan–Meier analysis, and multivariate Cox proportional hazards model to identify independent prognosticators.ResultsLow XDH expression was significantly associated with high tumor stage, nodal metastasis, high tumor grade, perineural invasion, vascular invasion, and a high mitotic rate (all P < 0.01). Kaplan–Meier survival curves showed that low XDH expression was associated with poor DSS and MFS (both p < 0.001). After adjusting for the standard pathological parameters, XDH expression status was an independent prognostic factor for DSS and MFS in UBUC and UTUC (all P < 0.01) in the multivariate analysis model. To associate XDH with potential functions in UC, gene co-expression examination was performed using the GO classification system. XDH upregulation was linked to pyroptosis and desmosomes.ConclusionLow XDH expression correlates with aggressive pathological features and poor oncological outcomes, indicating its prognostic and therapeutic significances in patients with UC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at10.1007/s12672-026-05270-z.Keywords: Xanthine dehydrogenase, Upper urinary tract cancer, Bladder cancer, Urothelial carcinoma, Survival

Introduction

Urothelial carcinoma (UC) is prevalent in industrialized countries worldwide [1, 2]. Various carcinogens, including cigarette smoke, industrially hazardous poisons, and genetic variants are involved in UC progression [1, 2]. UC occurs in the upper (renal pelvis, ureters) and lower (bladder and urethra) urinary tracts. In 90–95% of cases, UC resides in the bladder; the disease is called urinary bladder urothelial carcinoma (UBUC) [3, 4]. Upper urinary tract urothelial cancer (UTUC) accounts for approximately 5–10% of UC cases in Western countries but has an unusual prevalence in Taiwan [5, 6]. According to the GLOBOCAN database, UBUC ranks as the 10th most common cancer, accounting for 3% of 19.3 million new cancer cases and 2.1% of 9.9 million deaths in 2020 [7, 8].

UC represents a clinical challenge because of its highly heterogeneous characteristics [9, 10]. Although radical surgery is usually performed, disease progression and tumor metastasis occur frequently [2, 4, 5]. Currently, there are limited clinicopathological factors that can effectively predict UC [2–5]. It is necessary to discover new biomarkers for better risk stratification in UC patients. Purine metabolism is important for cell proliferation, which is a cancer hallmark [11, 12]. Xanthine dehydrogenase (XDH) acts as a key enzyme in purine degradation, serving as a rate-limiting factor in this process [13–15]. Therefore, we performed data mining on a published transcription profiling database of UC (GSE31684), focusing on genes related to XDH activity (GO:0004871). Our findings indicate that XDH exhibited the most pronounced downregulation in the- high stage (pT2-T4) compared with the low-stage (pTa-T1) UBUCs. This suggests that the XDH gene might function as a tumor suppressor in UCs.

The human XDH gene, also known as XOR, is mapped to human chromosome 2p23.1, and encodes a protein, xanthine dehydrogenase. It metabolizes hypoxanthine/xanthine to uric acid [13–15]. XDH dysregulation may induce DNA damage and carcinogenesis through the production of abundant reactive oxygen or nitrogen [16]. XDH-derived oxidative stress controls several intracellular signals such as hypoxia-inducible factor-α and nuclear factor-kappa B [16, 17]. XDH downregulation has been observed in liver, breast, and gastric cancers [18–22]. However, the role of XDH in UC is not well-understood. Herein, we aimed to explore the prognostic role of XDH in UBUC and UTUC patients.

Materials and methods

GEO microarray dataset analysis (GSE31684)

To explore the clinical relevance of XDH mRNA levels in urothelial carcinoma (UC), data mining was initially performed on a dataset (GSE31684) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), with analysis of 93 UBUCs using Affymetrix U133 Plus 2.0 arrays [23]. Raw files were imported into the Nexus Expression 3 software (BioDiscovery) to computerize the expression levels, as described earlier [24](Chan et al., 2019). To identify the differentially expressed genes of xanthine dehydrogenase activity (GO:0004871) related to UC invasion, we compared invasive UC with non-invasive UC. Significant genes were selected for further evaluation.

Patients and clinicopathological information

In total 635 consecutive patients with UTUC (n = 340) or UBUC (n = 295) who underwent surgery with curative intent (1998–2004) were recruited. The clinical and pathological data of each patient were retrospectively reviewed from the medical charts, including age, sex, tumor location, tumor number, and tumor stage (pT), histological grade, perineural invasion, vascular invasion, lymph node status, and mitotic rate. None of the patients had received neoadjuvant radiotherapy or chemotherapy before surgery. The patients underwent standard postoperative follow-up protocols according to the guidelines. All samples were assessed by two pathologists. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chi Mei Medical Center (Approval No. 10501-005). All procedures involving human data were conducted in accordance with institutional ethical standards and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient-informed consent was obtained in accordance with the procedures approved by the Institutional Review Board.

Immunohistochemistry and scoring

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded samples underwent standard immunohistochemical procedures. Following antigen retrieval, sections were treated with a primary antibody against XDH (rabbit monoclonal, ab80158, diluted at 1:100, Abcam) for 1 h. The DAKO ChemMate EnVision Kit (K5001, Carpinteria, CA, USA) was used to detect the primary antibodies. We defined positive immunoreactivity as the detection of a brown chromogen in the cytoplasms of UCs. A section incubated without anti-XDH antibody served as a negative control. Two pathologists estimated the XDH immunoreactivity. The labeling index was recorded as 0–24% (1+), 25–49% (2+), 50–74% (3+), and 75–100% (4+) of the UC cells that displayed strong cytoplasmic staining. High XDH expression was defined as samples with 3 + and 4 + immunoexpression, while those with 1+ to 2+ immunoexpression were regarded as having low expression.

Functional annotation of the cancer genome atlas data

To associate XDH with potential functions in UC, we appraised the co-expressed genes of XDH mRNA levels in the bladder cancer (BLCA) dataset (n = 411) from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database via the cBioPortal platform (http://cbioportal.org). Subsequently, we assessed the top 200 transcripts with either negative or positive correlations with XDH using the Gene Ontology (GO) classification system (http://geneontology.org/). This analysis categorized transcripts based on their cellular components, biological processes, or molecular functions, and they were ranked by fold enrichment for functional annotation. To visualize the representative GO terms, an R script utilizing the ggplot2 package was employed.

Statistical analysis

SPSS software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was utilized for all statistical analyses. Pearson’s chi-square test was employed to examine the relationship between XDH expression status and various clinical and pathological features. We used Kaplan–Meier survival analysis to evaluate the survival impact of XDH expression status on patient outcomes, including metastasis-free survival (MFS) and disease-specific survival (DSS). These outcomes were measured from the time of curative surgery to the occurrence of metastasis and cancer-related death. Significant variables identified in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis using the Cox regression model to identify independent prognostic factors. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

XDH was a significantly downregulated transcript related to UC invasiveness

From the transcriptomic profiling of the GSE31684 dataset, we recognized two probes covering two genes related to xanthine dehydrogenase activity (GO:0004854) (Table 1; Fig. 1). To assess the prognostic significance of XDH and AOX1, a preliminary survey was conducted using GEPIA. We found that only XDH expression status was significantly associated with overall survival in bladder cancer (Supplementary Fig. 1). Therefore, XDH was selected for further evaluation in our UC cohort by immunostaining. XHD expression gradually decreased from non-muscle invasive to muscle-invasive UCs (Fig. 2).

The UBUC cohort included 295 patients (216 men and 79 women). Of these, 172 patients had non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC, Ta, and T1), while 123 patients had muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC, T2-4). The majority of patients (81.0%) presented with high-grade tumors. There were 29 patients had nodal metastatic bladder cancer at the time of diagnosis. Vascular invasion was detected in 49 tumors, while perineural invasion was observed in 20 tumors. High mitotic activity was observed in 156 tumors. Similar to UTUC, low XDH expression was notably associated with advanced tumor stage, nodal metastasis, high tumor grade, vascular invasion, perineural invasion, and increased mitotic activity (all P < 0.01).

Prognostic value of XDH immunoexpression

In the UTUC cohort, 61 patients died of UTUC, while 70 developed subsequent metastatic tumors after surgery. In univariate analysis, low XDH expression correlated with unfavorable DSS (p < 0.0001) and MFS (p < 0.0001) in UTUC patients, together with high tumor stage, high histological grade, multifocality, nodal metastasis, vascular invasion, perineural invasion, and high mitotic rate (Table3 ; Fig. 3). A multivariate analysis revealed that XDH expression served as an independent prognostic indicator for both DSS (p = 0.002) and MFS (p = 0.002) in the UTUC group (Table 3).

Kaplan−Meier survival curves show that low XDH expression is associated with significantly poor disease-specific survival and metastasis-free survival of patients with UTUC (A and B, respectively) and UBUC (C and D, respectively)

In the UBUC cohort, we found 52 patients who passed away from UBUC and 76 who later developed metastatic tumors. Low XDH expression in UBUC was linked to poor DSS (p < 0.0001) and MFS (p < 0.0001) (Table 4 ; Fig. 3). Additionally, together with nodal metastasis and perineural invasion, XDH expression was an independent prognostic factor of tumor metastasis (p < 0.001) and cancer-related death (p = 0.007) in UBUC (Table 4).

XDH upregulation is linked to pyroptosis and desmosomes

We conducted gene co-expression analysis to explore the potential functions of XDH in UC. We analyzed the BLCA dataset (n = 411) from the TCGA database, focusing on the top 200 transcripts showing either positive or negative correlation with XDH (Supplementary Tables 1 and Table 2). These genes were then subjected to functional annotation using the GO classification system. Regarding biological processes (Fig. 4A), the top terms correlated with XDH upregulation included desmosome organization (GO:0002934, fold enrichment:30.59) and pyroptosis (GO:0070269, fold enrichment:18.54). In terms of molecular functions (Fig. 4B), the top terms positively correlated with XDH comprised cysteine-type endopeptidase activity involved in the apoptotic signaling pathway (GO:0097199, fold enrichment:37.07) and caspase recruitment domain (CARD) domain binding (GO:0050700, fold enrichment:25.49). With respect to cellular components (Fig. 4C), the first and second most prominent terms positively correlated with XDH were absent in the melanoma 2 (AIM2) inflammasome complex (GO:0097169, fold enrichment:50.98) and desmosomes (GO:0030057, fold enrichment:27.45), respectively. Pyroptosis induction is a potential type of cancer treatment [25]. Desmosomes are important in tumor suppression [26]. Accordingly, these observations revealed that high XDH expression and its co-upregulated genes were significant correlation with pyroptosis and desmosomes, indicating a potentially critical role for XDH in the suppression of UC.

Discussion

Purines are essential components of nucleotides that provide energy for cell survival and proliferation; thus, dysregulation of purine metabolism is associated with cancer progression.11, 12 XDH is a critical enzyme in purine metabolism.11,12 However, the effect of XDH expression on UC progression is poorly characterized. Herein, focusing on the genes of activity, we discovered that XDH was the most downregulated gene in UC progression. Subsequently, its clinical significance has been validated in a large cohort. We demonstrated that low XDH expression was significantly associated with aggressive pathological features and poor DSS, as well as MFS in UTUC and UBUC. UC patients with low XDH expression had a 2.5 to 5.2-fold increased likelihood of metastatic tumor development and a 2.7-fold increased likelihood of cancer-related death.

XDH catalyzes the oxidation of hypoxanthine to xanthine and is involved in the innate immune system and inflammatory cascades [16, 27, 28]. XDH expression is increased by inflammatory cytokines, growth factors, and irritative stimuli [16]. XDH activation can generate plentiful reactive nitrogen and oxygen species, which may cause endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, cytotoxicity cell proliferation, and mutagenesis [16, 27, 29, 30]. In human tissues, low XDH activity levels are common, with the highest levels observed in the liver, intestine, and breast. The role of XDH in oncogenesis has been studied in several neoplasms. XDH is significantly decreased in liver, gastric, and breast carcinomas compared to adjacent non-cancerous tissue [18–22]. In liver cancer, low XDH mRNA abundance correlates with high tumor stage and shorter disease-free survival [18]. Knocking down XDH expression accelerates cell migration and invasion. It also upregulates mesenchymal marker genes expression [18]. Moreover, loss of XDH potentiates the propagation of liver cancer stem cells and chemoresistance [18, 19]. In gastric cancer, decreased XDH expression was associated with advanced stage, large tumor size, lymph node metastasis, cellular aneuploidy, high mitotic index, and high cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) expression [20]. In breast cancer, the downregulation of XDH is associated with some unfavorable features, including large tumor size, positive axillary lymph nodes, high histological grade, and high COX-2 expression [21]. In multivariate analysis, XDH expression was an independent predictor of distant disease-free survival. An in vitro study showed that overexpression of XDH inhibited migratory activity mediated by a reduction in COX-2 and MMP protein levels [22].

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the prognostic value of XDH expression in UTUC and UBUC cohorts and demonstrate that low XDH expression is linked to aggressive cancer features and poor clinical outcomes. Our pilot findings are clinically valuable. Approximately 75% of bladder cancer cases are diagnosed with NMIBC, which show a better overall survival rate [2, 3]. However, the bladder recurrence rate after primary TURBT is 50–70%, and 10–20% of tumors progress to MIBC, resulting in unfavorable outcomes [2–4]. Identifying patients with high progression risk is essential. We observed a significant correlation between low XDH expression and advanced tumor stage as well as histological grade, indicating an increased risk of progression. If a tumor has low XDH expression in the TURBT specimens, re-staging TURBT or early radical surgery may be considered. Radical cystectomy is the standard treatment choice for localized MIBC. In a selected patient population, trimodality bladder-preserving treatment may have comparable clinical outcomes [2, 4]. This approach necessitates strong collaboration across multiple disciplines and a high level of adherence from patients. Our observations showed that patients with bladder cancer with low XDH expression had a high probability of nodal and distant metastasis. Therefore, they may be candidates for radical cystectomy with extended lymph node dissection and not for trimodality therapy. For high risk UTUC, radical nephroureterectomy is the standard therapy, while kidney-sparing surgery is suggested to be a low-risk (low tumor grade/stage) disease without compromising oncological outcomes [5, 31]. Our results showed that low XDH expression in UTUC correlated with aggressive pathological features, such as a high grade, stage, perineural invasion, and vascular invasion. Therefore, combining ureteroscopic biopsy grade, XDH expression in biopsy specimens, and imaging findings could aid in the decision-making process regarding radical nephroureterectomy versus kidney-sparing therapy. Moreover, radical nephroureterectomy with lymph node dissection or template-based lymph node dissection should be performed in low XDH expression UTUC patients.

Recent advances in liquid biopsy have transformed UC management by enabling non-invasive assessment of tumor burden, molecular heterogeneity, and minimal residual disease through urine- and blood-based circulating tumor DNA analyses, alongside molecular subtyping and multi-omic profiling for personalized care [32]. Urine, as a biologically enriched medium and natural ultracentrifuge of blood, serves as a key source of tumor-derived analytes [33]. However, clinical implementation remains limited by challenges in standardization, pre-analytical variability, and prospective validation [32, 33]. In this context, tissue-based biomarkers such as XDH provide complementary, stable prognostic stratification, whereas liquid biopsy enables dynamic disease monitoring. Their integration supports a multi-layered precision oncology framework that combines baseline tumor biology with real-time molecular surveillance for improved risk stratification.

The biological regulation of XDH in UC progression is unclear; thus, we performed a bioinformatic analysis to discover the possible mechanism. Initially regarded as caspase-1-dependent apoptosis, pyroptosis was implicated in cell death in Salmonella-infected macrophages [34]. Nevertheless, pyroptosis is characterized by cell swelling, membrane perforation, and release of cellular contents, which makes it considerably distinct from apoptosis, and is described as a form of programmed inflammatory cell death [35]. Interestingly, we observed that many genes that positively correlated with XDH were involved in this process through bioinformatic analysis (Fig. 4). The initiation of canonical caspase-1 (Spearman’s correlation:0.46) (Supplementary Fig. 2A)-dependent pyroptosis is the activation of the inflammasome, which comprise various pattern recognition receptors, including AIM2 (Spearman’s correlation:0.466) (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Activated by double-stranded DNA, AIM2 initiates the formation of an inflammasome complex with the apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a procaspase-1 and CARD [36]. Subsequently, active caspase-1 can trigger the development of inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-1β, and cleave gasdermin D (GSDMD) into its N-terminal and C-terminal [37, 38]. Later, the N-terminal domain of GSDMD can aggregate to create pores in the cell membrane, leading to cell membrane rupture and the release of cellular contents. However, we found that GSDMC was the third most significant gene co-upregulated with XDH in our analysis (Spearman’s correlation:0.551) (Supplementary Fig. 2C). A recent study demonstrated that activated STAT3 (pSTAT3) can promote nuclear PD-L1 (nPD-L1) translocation and transcriptionally activate GSDMC gene expression [39]. Subsequently, caspase-8 activated by tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) can cleave GSDMC into the N-terminus, leading to pore formation similar to that of other gasdermins. Additionally, bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) has been shown to activate human caspase-4 (Spearman’s correlation:0.393) (Supplementary Fig. 2D) and caspase-5 to drive pyroptosis independent of the inflammasome in a non-canonical manner [40]. Collectively, the correlation between XDH mRNA levels and its co-upregulated genes and pyroptosis in UC development suppression deserves further investigation.

Despite the promising prognostic value of XDH immunoexpression, some limitations should be mentioned. First, given the retrospective design, inherent selection biases cannot be excluded, and prospective, multi-institutional studies across diverse populations are required to validate the generalizability and clinical applicability of XDH as a prognostic biomarker. Second, although semi-quantitative immunohistochemical assessment was independently performed by experienced pathologists, the lack of standardized scoring thresholds may limit inter-institutional reproducibility. Future incorporation of digital pathology platforms and automated image analysis algorithms may enable more objective and scalable assessment. Third, while our bioinformatic analyses suggest potential involvement of XDH in pyroptosis-related signaling and desmosomal integrity, these findings are hypothesis-generating and do not establish causality; further functional validation through in vitro and in vivo studies is required. Finally, the applicability of XDH assessment in limited biopsy specimens, such as transurethral resection or ureteroscopic samples, remains uncertain. Given its potential relevance for pre-operative decision-making, this issue warrants dedicated prospective investigation. Despite these limitations, the inclusion of extensive UTUC and UBUC cohorts with complete pathological characteristics are a significant strength of our study, enhancing the credibility of our findings.

Conclusions

XDH expression has emerged as a prognostic marker for UC. It is linked to characteristics indicative of clinically aggressive UC, including advanced tumor stage, high histological grade, nodal metastasis, vascular invasion, perineural invasion, and high mitotic activity. These findings support its utility in identifying biologically aggressive disease across both UTUC and UBUC.

From a translational perspective, assessment of XDH expression could aid in risk stratification and may help inform clinical decision-making, particularly in identifying patients who might benefit from closer surveillance or more aggressive treatment strategies. In addition, given the role of XDH in purine metabolism, its dysregulation may reflect underlying biological processes relevant to therapeutic response. However, the therapeutic relevance of targeting XDH remains exploratory, and further functional studies as well as prospective clinical validation are required to determine whether modulation of XDH-related pathways can be translated into effective treatment approaches.
