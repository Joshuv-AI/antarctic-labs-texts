Explainable machine learning predicts mortality in critically ill patients with nonvariceal upper gastrointestinal bleeding: a MIMIC-IV study with external validation
Authors: Jialin Lu, Chuting Yu, Hangbang Li, Qiuxin Li, Ye Gao, Wei Wang, Luowei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-variceal upper gastrointestinal bleeding (NVUGIB) poses significant mortality risk in critically ill patients, necessitating accurate early prognostic assessment for timely interventions. Recent advances in machine learning have demonstrated potential to significantly improve predictive performance compared to conventional clinical scores. Accordingly, this study aims to establish a machine learning model named NVUPreM to predict 30-day NVUGIB mortality and validate its superiority over traditional scoring systems.MethodsThis retrospective study derived data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (n = 2912) and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (n = 7,742) for model development and external validation, respectively. Predictors were selected via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression to minimize multicollinearity. Thirty-six machine learning algorithms were evaluated using tenfold cross-validation. The optimal model (NVUPreM) was compared against eight clinical scoring systems (AIMS65, Charlson, GBS, GCS, Admission-Rockall, SAPS II, SOFA) using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration, decision curve analysis, and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) for interpretability.ResultsAmong 2,329 eligible NVUGIB patients, 489 (21.0%) died within 30 days. The NVUPreM model demonstrated good discrimination (AUC = 0.876, [95% CI 0.846–0.907]) and sensitivity (0.86), showing consistently strong predictive performance among all models. In internal validation, the NVUPreM model outperformed all clinical scores as evidenced by superior AUCs (AIMS65: AUC = 0.693; Charlson: AUC = 0.636; GBS: AUC = 0.575; GCS: AUC = 0.707; NVUPreM: AUC = 0.876; Admission-Rockall: AUC = 0.633; SAPS II: AUC = 0.777; SOFA: AUC = 0.665), decision curve analysis and calibration curve. External validation in eICU confirmed the robustness of the NVUPreM model in terms of discrimination (AUC = 0.82, [95% CI 0.803–0.837]), calibration, and clinical application. The interpretability analysis illustrated the relative importance of individual predictors and their directional associations with 30-day mortality risk.ConclusionThe NVUPreM model significantly outperforms existing clinical scores in predicting 30-day NVUGIB mortality, offering both accuracy and interpretability, which could assist clinicians in early high-risk patient identification and personalized intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04709-5.

Introduction

Non-variceal upper gastrointestinal bleeding (NVUGIB) is a common and life-threatening condition, with a global annual incidence estimated at 48–160 cases per 100,000 population [1–4]. Accounting for the vast majority (80–90%) of upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB) episodes [5–7]. NVUGIB remains a leading cause of hospitalization [8], particularly among critically ill patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs). Although advancements in medical and endoscopic therapies have reduced mortality over time [9, 10], the risk of death remains significant. Consequently, accurate identification of high-risk patients is crucial for optimizing ICU management of NVUGIB.

The heterogeneity in NVUGIB outcomes necessitates prompt risk stratification to prioritize intervention and intensive monitoring for high-risk individuals. Several clinical risk scores, such as the Rockall score, AIMS65, and the Glasgow-Blatchford Score (GBS), have been developed for this purpose [11], with subsequent large prospective studies validating specialized scores like the Italian PNED system for mortality prediction [12–14]. These tools utilize clinical and laboratory data, sometimes incorporating endoscopic findings, to predict rebleeding and mortality risk [11].

Machine learning (ML), leveraging computational power and algorithmic innovation, offers a transformative approach for clinical prediction [15]. ML algorithms analyze complex data patterns, continuously improving their accuracy in classification and outcome prediction, thereby supporting enhanced clinical decision-making [16–18]. Recent evidence suggests ML models may outperform traditional clinical scores in prognosticating UGIB outcomes [18]. However, current ML applications to NVUGIB prediction face limitations, including small sample sizes, inadequate validation, and insufficient direct comparison with established clinical scores [18, 19].

The Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, a comprehensive, freely accessible repository of ICU patient data, provides an ideal platform for developing robust ML models for critically ill NVUGIB patients. Yet, conclusive evidence demonstrating the superior predictive performance of ML algorithms specifically for 30-day mortality in this high-risk cohort remains lacking.

Therefore, this study aimed to: (1) develop, validate, and identify the optimal ML model for predicting 30-day mortality in critically ill NVUGIB patients using the MIMIC-IV database; and (2) systematically compare the performance of the best-performing ML model against traditional clinical risk scoring systems within the same patient cohort.

Methods

Data sources

This retrospective study utilized data from two critical care databases: MIMIC-IV (v2.2) database [20], containing de-identified electronic health records from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (2008–2019), and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (v1.2) [21], a multi-center de-identified database encompassing over 200,000 ICU admissions across 208 US hospitals (2014–2015). Both databases provide detailed clinical information pertinent to ICU care, including laboratory values, medications, vital signs, diagnoses, procedures, and outcomes. The study received ethical approval (Human Research Participants Protection Certification No. 68594036), and all database access requirements were fulfilled.

Participants

Patients diagnosed with NVUGIB according to International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 9th (5789, 53021, 5307, 53140, 53541, 53511, 53783, 53082, 53551, 53100, 53440, 53501, 53531) and 10th (K254, K2211, K922, K31811, K226, K2101, K2971, K284, K2921, K2961, K250, K2901). Revision codes were eligible for inclusion from both the eICU and MIMIC-IV databases. Patients who met the following criteria were excluded from the study: (1) patients aged < 18 years old upon initial hospitalization; (2) patients experiencing multiple NVUGIB-related ICU admissions, for whom only the first admission data were extracted.

Data collection

Data extraction employed PostgreSQL (v13.7.2) and Navicat Premium (v16) via Structured Query Language (SQL) queries. Specifically, urine output was defined as the cumulative urine volume recorded within the first 24 h after ICU admission, calculated by summing all discrete measurements during this period (unit: mL). To minimize bias from missing data, all variables with missing values exceeding 40% of observations were excluded first. Subsequently, we excluded participants demonstrating missing data for more than 20% of the remaining variables. Then the random forest algorithm, trained on the subset of variables that were complete, was employed for imputing missing data. This process was efficiently implemented using the “mice” package in R software, which provides a comprehensive and flexible framework for multiple imputation (MI) [22, 23]. To rigorously avoid data leakage, all imputation was performed fold-wise within the cross-validation framework. The imputation was run for m = 10 imputations and maxiter = 5 iterations, which ensured stable convergence. The outcome variable (30-day mortality) was excluded from the imputation models to prevent bias.

Clinical risk scoring systems

Pre-endoscopic clinical risk scores, including the GBS, Admission-Rockall score, AIMS65, Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Charlson Comorbidity Index, and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) were compared with ML models, as only pre-endoscopic variables are typically available to clinicians for initial risk assessment [18].

Statistical analysis

Initial analysis revealed severe right-skewness in continuous variables. Logarithmic transformation was applied to mitigate outlier effects and approximate normality. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data, or median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed variables, with normality determined by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (%) and compared using χ² test or Fisher’s exact test (nominal) or rank-sum test (ordinal).

Feature selection was performed using L1-penalized least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression (glmnet package) [24, 25] to mitigate multicollinearity and prevent overfitting. The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was determined via 10-fold cross-validation, with features selected based on the minimum lamda (λmin) to balance model parsimony and predictive accuracy.

After feature selection, a diverse array of ML algorithms was employed for model construction, which included various implementations of Generalized Linear Models (GLM), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), regularization methods, Probabilistic Discriminant Analysis (PDA), Random Forest, Decision Trees, multiple kernel-based methods, neural networks, recursive partitioning trees, conditional inference trees, Support Vector Machine (SVM), and other machine learning algorithms. To prevent overfitting and assess generalizability, all models were trained and validated using a 10-fold cross-validation strategy repeated 10 times (9 folds training, 1 fold validation per iteration). In addition, a grid search was conducted using the training dataset to select optimal hyperparameters for each model [see Additional file 1].

We comprehensively evaluated model performance by assessing discrimination, calibration, and statistical robustness. Discrimination was primarily measured by the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) with its 95% confidence interval, calculated using DeLong’s test. Calibration was assessed using the Brier score (for overall accuracy), the calibration intercept (indicating over- or under-prediction), and the calibration slope (where a value of 1 indicates perfect calibration). Additionally, secondary metrics including accuracy, precision, recall (sensitivity), F1 score, specificity, negative predictive value (NPV), and positive predictive value (PPV) were examined. The optimal model was selected by prioritizing high sensitivity alongside a superior AUC-ROC. To ensure robustness and mitigate overfitting, we calculated the Events Per Variable, defined as the number of outcome events (deaths) divided by the number of predictors in the final model.

To evaluate the model, we first analyzed predictor importance scores derived from the varImp function. Importance scores were extracted and ranked by their importance scores to identify the most influential predictors in the model. For richer insight into the model’s decision mechanisms, interpretability was comprehensively evaluated using SHAP analysis. SHAP values were estimated via the kernelshap method and subsequently visualized with shapviz. The resultant SHAP matrices were then integrated into a unified analytical framework, enabling both detailed case-specific interpretation and aggregate feature importance assessment through absolute value aggregation.

Following model development, a comparison was made between the top-performing model and current clinical risk scoring systems. The evaluation focused on discrimination (AUC), calibration (visual assessment of predicted vs. observed risk plots), and clinical utility using decision curve analysis (DCA). DCA was conducted over the complete theoretical threshold range from 0 to 1 (with a step size of 0.005) to provide a comprehensive view of model performance. The net benefit was calculated and presented in its raw, unstandardized form, which directly quantifies the net reduction in interventions per patient without a negative outcome compared to strategies of “treat all” or “treat none”.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A cohort of 10,654 critically ill patients with NVUGIB was recruited in this study. The MIMIC-IV cohort (n = 2912) was randomly divided into a training set (n = 2329) and an internal validation set (n = 583). For external validation, we utilized an independent cohort of 7742 patients from the eICU database to assess model generalizability (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics and key predictive variables in the derivation and validation cohorts

Predictor selection

LASSO regression analysis of 83 admission variables identified 29 significant predictors of critical illness (Fig. 2a-b). Key predictors included the demographic factor of age, as well as extremes of physiological and laboratory parameters, including maximum and minimum values of systolic blood pressure (SBP), respiratory rate, temperature, oxygen saturation (SpO2), white blood cell count (WBC), partial thromboplastin time (PTT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST). Additional predictors included minimum values of platelets, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), prothrombin time (PT), absolute lymphocyte count, and total bilirubin, along with maximum values of absolute monocyte count and anion gap. The model also incorporated clinical metrics such as urine output and the individual components of the Glasgow Coma Scale (eye, verbal, and motor responses), as well as clinically relevant comorbidities including cerebrovascular disease, peptic ulcer disease, and metastatic solid tumors.

Model performance comparisons

Among the 36 evaluated ML algorithms, model selection was based on a joint consideration of discrimination (AUC) and sensitivity, rather than optimization of a single performance metric. Although the spls model did not yield the numerically highest AUC or sensitivity alone, it demonstrated consistently strong discriminative performance together with a favorable sensitivity profile, making it the most balanced model for clinical risk stratification. Model performance metrics

NPV negative predictive value, PPV positive predictive value

Figure 2c presents a boxplot of the average AUC across 36 ML models, derived from ten-fold cross-validation. Notable performance variations were observed across models and the spls model exhibited higher median AUC values with compact IQRs, indicating consistent and robust predictive performance. The ROC curves shown in Fig. 2d illustrate the discriminative performance of all models. The spls model achieved an AUC of 0.876 (95% CI 0.846–0.907), which was comparable to other top-performing models such as svmLinear (AUC = 0.871) and glmboost (AUC = 0.862). To formally assess statistical differences in discrimination, pairwise comparisons were conducted using DeLong’s test with the spls model as the reference. These analyses indicated that the difference in AUC between spls and svmLinear was not statistically significant, whereas the performance of several lower-performing models differed significantly from spls (Additional file 4).

Beyond discrimination, the spls model maintained a favorable balance across clinically relevant metrics, including sensitivity (0.86), accuracy (0.80), and F1-score (0.61). Owing to this combination of high sensitivity and robust overall performance, the spls model was designated as the Non-Variceal Upper Gastrointestinal Hemorrhage Mortality Prediction (NVUPreM) model. Detailed AUC estimates with 95% CIs are provided in Additional file 3 and 4, and comprehensive calibration assessments (Brier score, calibration intercept, and slope) are reported in Additional file 5 and 6.

The feature importance rankings for the NVUPreM model are illustrated in Fig. 3a. Among the features contributing to death in critically ill patients with NVUGIB, GCS-Verbal, GCS-Motor, GCS-Eyes, maximum PTT, minimum PTT, and minimum PT were the most influential.

In addition, SHAP analysis was performed to identify key variables contributing to the predictive performance of the model in the testing cohort (Fig. 3b). The SHAP beeswarm plot illustrates the direction and magnitude of each variable’s contribution to mortality risk. Lower values of the GCS components (motor, eye, and verbal responses) were associated with increased mortality risk, as reflected by positive SHAP values. Similarly, decreased levels of urine output were linked to higher predicted mortality. Abnormal coagulation parameters, including prolonged PT and PTT, also contributed positively to mortality risk. These findings enhance the interpretability of the model by clarifying how individual predictors influence outcome predictions.

In a supplementary analysis, the three individual GCS components were replaced by the total GCS score (3–15). The model demonstrated comparable discriminative performance, with stable AUC values, as detailed in Additional file 7.

Comparison with clinical risk scoring systems

Internal validation

In the internal validation cohort (n = 583), we compared the discriminative performance of the NVUPreM model with established clinical risk scoring systems. Model performance was primarily assessed using AUC, DCA, and calibration curves. As shown in Fig. 4b, NVUPreM (AUC = 0.876, [95% CI 0.846–0.907]) performed better than SAPS II (AUC = 0.777, [95% CI 0.732–0.821]), GCS (AUC = 0.707, [95% CI 0.653–0.76]), AIMS65 (AUC = 0.693, [95% CI 0.646–0.74]), Charlson (AUC = 0.636, [95% CI 0.585–0.687]), and Admission-Rockall score (AUC = 0.633, [95% CI 0.584–0.683]). DCA further confirmed the superior clinical utility of NVUPreM, demonstrating the highest net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities compared to other scores (Fig. 4c). Calibration analysis showed good agreement between predicted and observed mortality risk, indicating that the NVUPreM model was well calibrated in the internal validation cohort (Fig. 4a).

External validation

In the independent eICU cohort (n = 7742), the performance of NVUPreM was externally validated against established clinical risk scoring systems using discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility analyses. ROC curve analysis revealed that NVUPreM achieved the highest discriminative performance with an AUC of 0.82 (95% CI 0.803–0.837), significantly outperforming SOFA (AUC = 0.806), SAPS II (AUC = 0.797), AIMS65 (AUC = 0.762), GCS (AUC = 0.758), GBS (AUC = 0.712), Admission-Rockall (AUC = 0.614), and Charlson (AUC = 0.562), where non-overlapping confidence intervals between NVUPreM and lower-performing scores (e.g., Charlson, Admission-Rockall) indicated statistical significance (Fig. 5b). DCA highlighted the superior clinical utility of the NVUPreM model, as it provided the highest standardized net benefit across a broad range of high-risk thresholds (0.2-1.0), surpassing all comparator systems and consistently outperforming the “All” and “None” intervention strategies (Fig. 5c). Calibration curves further validated NVUPreM’s reliability, showing close alignment between predicted and observed probabilities, particularly at higher risk thresholds, in contrast to deviations observed in traditional scores (Fig. 5a). These results robustly validate NVUPreM as a precise and practical tool for risk stratification in critically ill NVUGIB patients.

Discussion

We systematically developed and validated 36 ML models to identify an optimal predictive framework (NVUPreM) for 30-day mortality among critically ill patients with NVUGIB using the MIMIC-IV database. Compared with prior ML-based studies predicting mortality in UGIB patients [18], our work addresses three key limitations: small sample sizes, limited validation, and a narrow scope of score comparisons. By leveraging two large-scale, multicenter, real-world critical care databases (MIMIC-IV and eICU), and conducting comprehensive external validation in an independent cohort, we substantially strengthened the robustness and generalizability of the proposed model.

Unlike previous studies that evaluated only a small number of ML algorithms [18, 26], we innovatively implemented a broad spectrum of 36 models using a unified pipeline. This rigorous approach identified the spls model as optimal, forming the basis of the NVUPreM framework. This mitigates model selection bias and provides a valuable reference for future ICU algorithm selection.

Furthermore, our comparative strategy against clinical scoring systems offers novel insights. Current limitations of traditional scores are well-recognized: the Rockall score relies on post-endoscopic findings, limiting its pre-endoscopy utility; AIMS65 lacks sufficient accuracy in predicting the need for endoscopic intervention [27]. In contrast, the GBS outperforms both the Rockall score and AIMS65 in predicting transfusion needs, endoscopic hemostasis, or mortality risk, making it widely utilized for guiding disposition decisions. While existing studies typically compare ML models with three major scoring systems (GBS, AIMS65 and Rockall score) [17, 18], our analysis extended the comparison to seven clinical metrics (SAPS II, SOFA, Charlson, GCS), covering disease severity, organ function, and comorbidity assessment. Across all evaluated dimensions, NVUPreM demonstrated superior performance. Unlike traditional scores reliant on limited variables, the NVUPreM model integrates 30 clinically relevant predictors capturing neurological function (GCS subscales), coagulation abnormalities (PT and PTT extremes), metabolic derangements (anion gap), oncologic burden (metastatic cancer), and insurance status. It should be emphasized that insurance status functions as a surrogate marker of social vulnerability rather than a causal determinant of mortality. Notably, the top-ranked importance of GCS-Verbal, GCS-Motor and GCS-Eyes underscores the pivotal role of consciousness and motor response in ICU prognosis, consistent with findings by Deng et al. [17]. Additionally, the strong predictive power of coagulopathy (e.g., prolonged PTT) reflects the pathological cycle of bleeding and coagulopathy in critically ill patients, further validating the biological plausibility of the model. In contrast, traditional scores like AIMS65 (AUC = 0.693) and Admission-Rockall (AUC = 0.633) fail to incorporate dynamic physiological parameters or neurological status, limiting their utility in rapidly deteriorating ICU patients.

Moreover, to tackle the “black box” nature of complex ML models hindering clinical trust, we employed SHAP analysis. Clinician adoption of ML tools is often hindered by insufficient transparency in the derivation of predictions, raising concerns about trust and accountability. The SHAP approach provides interpretable insights into individual predictions, bridging the gap between algorithmic outputs and actionable clinical understanding, thereby enhancing real-world applicability.

It should be emphasized that while urine output was identified as an important predictor in our ICU-based cohort, it primarily reflects early organ perfusion and disease severity rather than a procedural requirement. In the MIMIC-IV and eICU databases, urine output is routinely measured in ICU patients, often via indwelling Foley catheters, but this does not imply that Foley catheter placement is warranted solely for risk prediction in non-ICU settings. Clinicians in emergency or general ward settings should continue to follow standard care protocols, and the use of NVUPreM should complement—not dictate—clinical judgment.

Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the model was developed and validated exclusively using data from U.S. intensive care units, which may limit generalizability to other healthcare systems. Second, model calibration may vary across institutions, underscoring the need for local recalibration prior to clinical deployment.

Despite these limitations, we still believe that NVUPreM holds strong potential to assist clinicians in early identification of high-risk patients and support clinical decision-making. Before routine clinical implementation, prospective validation in well-designed clinical trials is necessary to confirm its effectiveness and reliability in real-world settings. Ultimately, NVUPreM may serve as a complementary tool alongside established clinical scoring systems, enhancing—but not replacing—clinical judgment in the management of critically ill patients.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed and validated the NVUPreM for prediction of 30-day mortality in critically ill NVUGIB patients. Its clinical utility lies in the exclusive use of dynamic physiological parameters available at admission, including GCS components, routine laboratory tests (e.g., platelets, PTT, BUN), and vital signs. Demonstrating superior discriminative performance over conventional clinical scores, the NVUPreM model shows potential for precise risk stratification for ICU patients, which could aid in the prompt implementation of corresponding treatment plans to reduce mortality. Future prospective studies are needed to validate its real-world utility and explore integration into clinical decision-support systems to optimize NVUGIB management in critical care.
