Outcomes following a ‘positive’ response to diagnostic intra-articular hip injection for pre-arthritic hip pain based on the level of percent pain improvement
Authors: Andrea B McCullough, Niyathi Prasad, Julia Cave Arbanas, Abby L Cheng, Heidi Prather, Devyani Hunt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiagnostic hip injections are used to confirm an intra-articular source of hip pain in adolescents and young adults in order to guide management. There is currently no data-driven consensus regarding what cutoff metric should be used to constitute a ‘positive’ response to injection. This study compared using either 50% or 75% immediate pain improvement to define a positive response to fluoroscopically-guided diagnostic hip injection to aid in management decision-making. Rates of surgical management and likelihood of satisfaction with care at 12+ months were then compared between the two groups. Amongst 56 hips, (mean age 18.5 (SD ± 5.2) years, 89% (50/56) female) there was no difference in the rate of progression to surgery amongst responders when using either a ≥50% or ≥75% cutoff of pain improvement (71% (29/41) versus 74% (20/27), between-group difference 3% [95% CI, −18% to 25%], P = .76). Amongst those undergoing surgery, there was no difference in patient-reported satisfaction, at 12+ months based on a ≥50% versus ≥75% pain improvement cutoff (74% (14/19) versus 69% (9/13) reporting ‘satisfied’ or ‘very satisfied’ on 5-point Likert Scale, between-group difference 4% [−27% to 36%], P = 0.78). Our findings suggest that a ≥50% cutoff could be considered a ‘positive’ response in a clinical setting and used to guide management decisions.

Introduction

Pre-arthritic hip disorders are intra-articular hip abnormalities that cause pain, dysfunction, and may predispose individuals to developing early hip osteoarthritis. These disorders typically occur in a younger population and include diagnoses such as femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) and acetabular dysplasia with or without associated labral tears and cartilage injury [1].

Previously, the diagnosis of a pre-arthritic hip disorder as the aetiology of pain relied heavily on radiographic imaging [2]. However, it has since been established that individuals may have radiographic anomalies, such as hip labral tears and FAI or acetabular dysplasia, without being symptomatic, indicating that these anomalies may not always be the source of pain [3, 4]. Current practice utilizes diagnostic intra-articular hip injections to confirm that the aetiology of pain originates from the hip joint before considering more invasive treatments [5].

In pre-existing literature, a variety of descriptive and numeric cutoffs have been put forth to define a ‘positive’ response to diagnostic hip injection, indicating the patient’s pain generator is largely due to an intra-articular source. Examples of descriptive or categorical cutoffs used in previous studies include: ‘pronounced temporary improvement in symptoms’, ‘any decline’ in pain post-injection, or ‘partial or complete relief’ [6, 7]. Other studies more commonly have utilized either 50%, or 75% or greater cutoffs, indicating a lack of consensus regarding what should be considered a ‘positive’ response to diagnostic injection [8–12].

Given that a patient’s response to a diagnostic hip injection is intended to guide management recommendations, a clearer definition of what constitutes a ‘positive’ response is important to help guide clinicians in the management and counselling of patients. The primary purpose of this study was to compare rates of surgery and subsequent satisfaction at 12+ months when defining a positive response as either ≥50% or ≥ 75% pain improvement post-injection. A secondary purpose of our study was to compare rates of satisfaction based on progression to surgery versus continued conservative management.

Materials and methods

This was a single-site prospective cohort study conducted within the orthopaedic department of a tertiary care academic medical centre in the USA. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained prior to participant recruitment. Participants provided written informed consent prior to participating. Patients were enrolled from 3 June 2015 to 31 July 2019. Follow-up data was obtained from 3 August 2016 to 22 June 2021.

Participants

Patients were eligible for the study if they were between the ages of 12 and 45 years old; tested positive for a minimum of one of four provocative hip tests (listed below); reported pain in the anterior groyne, lateral hip, and/or posterior pelvis; had a Tönnis grade of zero or one on hip radiographs, and were scheduled for a fluoroscopically-guided diagnostic intra-articular hip injection as part of clinical diagnosis and management of their pain. Patients were excluded if they had a history of hip surgery, tumour, or infection of the hip or pelvis; history of inflammatory arthropathy; were pregnant; had pain that radiated posteriorly beyond the gluteal fold; any major bony hip deformity (slipped capital femoral epiphysis, Legg-Calve-Perthes disease, acetabular dysplasia with lateral centre edge angle less than 20); had an allergy to local anaesthetic or contrast dye; or were unable to provide consent.

Provocative tests

Immediately prior to the injection, a board-certified sports medicine physiatrist performed four provocative hip tests and asked the patient to report a numeric pain score (NPS) with each test, with zero indicating no pain and 10 indicating the worst possible pain [13]. Participants were also asked to report the location of pain provocation, whether in the anterior groyne, lateral hip, and/or posterior pelvis. Provocative tests included end-range hip flexion, log roll, flexion adduction internal rotation (FADIR), and flexion abduction external rotation (FABER) [14, 15].

Intra-articular hip injection procedure

All injections were performed under fluoroscopic guidance by the physiatrist who performed the provocative tests. After informed consent for the procedure, the patient was positioned supine on the fluoroscopy table. The intertrochanteric line was identified, and the site to be injected was marked. Using sterile technique, a 25-gauge needle was used to infiltrate 0–2 ml of 1% lidocaine subcutaneously. Then, either a 22 or 25-gauge spinal needle was advanced under fluoroscopic guidance. Confirmation of intra-articular placement was made with infusion of 0.5–3 ml of Omnipaque contrast. This was followed by infusion of 2 ml of 1% lidocaine and 2 ml of 0.25% bupivacaine or 0.2% ropivacaine [16]. The area was then cleansed with sterile water, and any bleeding was controlled with local pressure.

Post-injection

Hip provocative tests were re-assessed between five and 30 min post-procedure. With each test, participants were asked to report an NPS and the percent pain improvement compared to pre-injection. After the exam, participants were asked to report their overall post-injection NPS and percent of pain improvement.

Follow-up

At a minimum of 12 months after the diagnostic injection, participants’ electronic medical records were reviewed to determine whether they had undergone surgical intervention for their pre-arthritic hip disorder (e.g. hip arthroscopy and/or periacetabular osteotomy). Conservative management included an accepted multidisciplinary approach, including focused physical therapy, medications, movement pattern training, and activity modification [5]. Additionally, participants were emailed and called to complete follow-up questionnaires, which inquired about whether they had undergone surgery, as well as their satisfaction with their hip. Satisfaction was assessed using the following question: ‘How satisfied are you with the outcome of your hip treatment?’ with responses reported on a 5-point Likert scale with possible answer options of ‘Very satisfied’, ‘Satisfied’, ‘Neither satisfied or dissatisfied’, ‘Dissatisfied’, and ‘Very dissatisfied’. Participants were considered to be satisfied with their outcomes if they chose either ‘Very satisfied’ or ‘Satisfied’ [17].

Statistical analysis

Participants were grouped into responders and non-responders to the diagnostic injection. Participants in the ‘responder’ group included those reporting ≥50% of overall pain improvement. The analysis was then repeated with a ‘responder’ definition of ≥75% of overall pain improvement. Means, standard deviations, and confidence intervals were calculated for participants’ demographic characteristics, hip range of motion, and pain scores. Using two-tailed t-tests, between-group differences were assessed for numeric and percent pain improvement with individual provocative tests and overall pain. For the two primary outcomes, two-proportion z-tests were used to compare: (i) rates of surgery between responders defined with a 50% versus 75% cutoff, and (ii) rates of satisfaction with results of treatment between responders (defined with a 50% cutoff) who did and did not undergo surgery. P-value was set at .05.

Amongst all participants, the median follow-up time was 24 months via medical record review. About 73% (41/56) of participants completed the 12-month follow-up questionnaire. Discussion

In young adult and adolescent patients with hip pain and pre-arthritic hip disorders, diagnostic intra-articular hip injections are often used to help confirm an intra-articular source of hip pain. Many clinicians utilize this response alongside history, physical exam, and imaging to guide management, especially when considering whether to pursue surgical intervention. The group with ≥50% improvement had greater improvement in pain scores with provocative physical exam manoeuvres than non-responders (<50% improvement), which was statistically significant. In this study, there was no difference in the rate of surgery amongst responders whether using a 50% or 75% cutoff (71% versus 74% proceeded with surgery, respectively), and amongst those undergoing surgery, there was no difference in patient-reported satisfaction at 12+ month follow up whether using a 50 or 75% cutoff (74% versus 69% satisfied after surgery, respectively). This suggests that a ≥50% improvement after diagnostic intra-articular hip injection could be considered a ‘positive’ response in a clinical setting and used to guide management decisions.

In a recent systematic review, Martins et al. found six studies evaluating surgical outcomes based on response to diagnostic hip injection [18]. There was considerable heterogeneity defining what constitutes a ‘positive response’; 3 studies used 50% pain improvement as their cutoff to define a ‘positive response’ with others considering any pain improvement as a ‘positive response’ [18]. The authors were concerned that including participants who had any amount of improvement could result in including ‘false responders’ in the responder group. Overall, they found that 5/6 studies found a statistically higher likelihood of satisfactory surgical outcomes for those with higher improvement with diagnostic injection. Our findings suggest that there is no significant difference in satisfaction with surgical outcomes between those with a ≥75% versus ≥50% cutoff. We did not have a sufficient number of non-responders (<50%) who underwent surgery to draw meaningful comparisons there. Non-responders who did not undergo surgery proceeded with evaluation and treatment for other extra-articular causes of hip pain.

A secondary purpose of our study was to compare rates of satisfaction based on progression to surgery versus continued conservative management. Using a ≥50% improvement cutoff, responders who did not undergo surgical management had no difference in levels of satisfaction with the results of their care compared to those who underwent surgery (74% versus 75%, respectively). This suggests it is possible to attain high levels of satisfaction with conservative management even in the setting of a confirmed intra-articular source of pain. This is notable especially considering that a trial of conservative management is typically done prior to proceeding to diagnostic injection. This can be useful information when counselling patients on their management options.

This study has limitations. This study was performed at a single institution, and management decisions regarding proceeding to surgery versus continuing conservative management is, in part, influenced by the practice patterns of the treating physicians. Second, due to the limited sample size, subgroup analyses were limited, and the conclusiveness of our findings. Most notably, the number of non-responders that underwent surgery was too small to draw any meaningful comparisons on the outcomes of surgery between responder and non-responder groups. Third, while overall satisfaction is a crucially important patient-reported outcome, especially in the context of surgical intervention, additional investigation would benefit from assessment of more detailed patient-reported outcomes that shed light on subdomains of hip function on its impact on quality of life.

In conclusion, in young adult and adolescent patients undergoing diagnostic hip injection, ≥50% pain improvement could be considered a ‘positive’ response in a clinical setting and could be used to guide management decisions. Additionally, symptom satisfaction at least 1 year later was reported by a majority of patients who responded to a diagnostic injection, regardless of whether the patients pursued surgery or continued conservative management. Therefore, confirmation of intra-articular hip pain may not necessarily suggest that surgery is required to achieve satisfactory symptomatic improvement.
