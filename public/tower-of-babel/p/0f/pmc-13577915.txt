An iPSC-derived neural progenitor cell therapy for subacute spinal cord injury: a phase 1 trial with long-term follow-up
Authors: Keiko Sugai, Osahiko Tsuji, Kanehiro Fujiyoshi, Masahiro Ozaki, Takayuki Abe, Tsuneo Kohzuki, Hiroki Okawara, Tomonori Sawada, Miho Sumida, Ema Yoshioka, Tomoko Shofuda, Shioko Kawashima, Ryo Yamaguchi, Munehisa Shinozaki, Naoko Takasu, Narihito Nagoshi, Toshiki Okubo, Jun Kohyama, Takeshi Maeda, Yoshiyuki Yato, Yonehiro Kanemura, Shinya Yamanaka, Masaya Nakamura, Hideyuki Okano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal cord injury (SCI) causes irreversible motor and sensory deficits, and no therapy currently restores the damaged neural circuitry. Previous work has shown that transplantation of induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived neural stem/progenitor cells (NS/PCs) restored motor function in preclinical models of subacute SCI; however, the safety of this approach in humans remains unknown. Here we report results from a first-in-human, open-label study of iPSC-NS/PC transplantation in four patients with subacute cervical complete SCI. The primary safety end point was achieved, with no tumor formation or graft-related adverse events observed during 2–4 years of follow-up and stable graft sites on imaging. Exploratory efficacy was assessed as a secondary end point. Median improvement in the International Standards for Neurological Classification of Spinal Cord Injury motor score from baseline (2 weeks after injury) to week 52 was 13 points (range 10–40), with two patients improving in American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale (grade A → C and A → D). These gains were numerically greater than spontaneous recovery observed in a registry-based cohort. This study provides clinical evidence that transplantation of human iPSC-NS/PCs into the injured spinal cord is feasible and safe under short-term immunosuppression, with findings supporting further clinical evaluation. The trial is registered at UMIN000035074, UMIN000050104 and jRCTa031190228.Subject terms: Spinal cord diseases, Translational research, Neural stem cells, Stem-cell research, Induced pluripotent stem cells

Main

Spinal cord injury (SCI) leads to irreversible disruption of neural connectivity, causing paralysis and lifelong disability. The human spinal cord has limited intrinsic regenerative capacity, and restoration of lost neural circuits remains a major unmet clinical challenge in the field of neuroscience and regenerative medicine. Globally, more than 20 million people live with SCI-related disability, and this number is expected to increase as populations grow and age1. Although advances in acute management have improved survival, current standard care, including surgical decompression, stabilization and rehabilitation, cannot reconstruct damaged neural circuits, and no approved therapy has yet been shown to restore substantial neurological function.

Recent advances in stem cell biology have enabled the generation of human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived neural stem/progenitor cells (iPSC-NS/PCs). In our preclinical studies, transplanted human iPSC-NS/PCs promoted functional motor recovery in rodent2–5 and nonhuman primate6 models of subacute SCI. Following transplantation, the iPSC-NS/PCs predominantly differentiated into neurons, including glutamatergic neurons and a subset of GABA-utilizing (GABAergic) neurons, as well as astrocytes and oligodendrocyte-lineage cells, contributing to remyelination and attenuation of spinal atrophy. Chemogenetic manipulation using designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs), together with in vivo activity imaging, demonstrated that graft-derived neurons exhibited activity coupled to host neural circuits, and that enhancing graft neuronal activity augmented synaptic interactions with host circuits and improved functional recovery, supporting a functional contribution of graft-derived neurons to host circuitry4,5. Graft-derived neurons also promoted the regrowth of host axons, including serotonergic descending fibers. Furthermore, transplanted cells secreted neurotrophic and angiogenic factors, including hepatocyte growth factor, brain-derived neurotrophic factor and vascular endothelial growth factor, thereby supporting neuronal survival, axonal growth and tissue repair2.

Given safety concerns associated with pluripotent stem cell-derived products, particularly the risk of abnormal proliferation, extensive preclinical investigations have been performed7,8. These included purification strategies to eliminate tumorigenic cells3, and stringent quality control criteria to exclude genomic instability or tumorigenic potential9. Together, these studies provided a rationale for clinical translation.

Here we report a first-in-human clinical study of iPSC-NS/PC transplantation in patients with subacute complete SCI (schematic overview in Fig. 1a), designed to assess safety and explore neurological outcomes following transplantation of iPSC-NS/PCs in the injured spinal cord.

Results

Study participants and study design

In this study, seven individuals with subacute traumatic SCI provided informed consent and underwent screening between December 2021 and November 2023, of whom four met the eligibility criteria and were enrolled in the study. The enrolled participants underwent transplantation of human iPSC-NS/PCs (Fig. 1b). All enrolled participants were male, aged 26–66 years and had cervical SCI with a neurological level of injury (NLI) between C4 and C6. Following spinal stabilization surgery at acute-care hospitals, candidates were transferred to Keio University Hospital for baseline evaluation. Eligibility criteria included traumatic SCI with an NLI between C3/4 and T10, American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale (AIS) grade A, neurological stability before transplantation, and absence of contraindications to immunosuppression. Key exclusion criteria included previous central nervous system (CNS) disease, active infection, severe respiratory failure, major organ dysfunction or other comorbidities that could interfere with surgery, immunosuppression or reliable neurological assessment.

Baseline was defined as the later of 14 days after injury or the date of informed consent.

AIS A was defined as the absence of voluntary anal contraction and deep anal pressure, together with no sensory perception in the S4–S5 region, as defined in the International Standards for Neurological Classification of Spinal Cord Injury (ISNCSCI)10. AIS A status was reconfirmed on the day of transplantation.

This phase 1 study was conducted as a 52-week open-label, single-arm, first-in-human interventional clinical study designed primarily to evaluate the safety of intramedullary transplantation of human iPSC-NS/PCs, followed by a planned long-term observational follow-up study (Fig. 2a). The primary end point was safety, assessed through systematic recording of all adverse events (AEs) occurring during the 52-week study period, together with evaluation of their severity and causal relationship to the transplanted cells, the surgical procedure or immunosuppressive therapy. Secondary exploratory outcomes were assessed using validated clinical scales for neurological, functional and pain measures. Imaging evaluations, conducted as part of the safety assessment, included serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) up to 52 weeks and whole-body [18F]fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18F-FDG PET) performed at week 24. After the first 52 weeks, neurological examinations, MRI assessments, and monitoring for late AEs were performed at 6-month intervals up to 3 years after transplantation and annually thereafter. Follow-up durations after transplantation were 2–4 years (patient 1, 4 years; patient 2, 3 years; patient 3, 2.5 years; patient 4, 2 years).

Generation of iPSC-NS/PCs

The integration-free human iPSC line YZWJs513, established under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) conditions by the Center for iPS Cell Research and Application (Kyoto University), served as the starting material. This line was derived from umbilical-cord blood cells of a healthy donor homozygous for the most common human leukocyte antigen (HLA) haplotypes in the Japanese population (HLA-A*24:02, HLA-B*52:01, HLA-DRB1*15:02, HLA-C*12:02, HLA-DQB1*06:01, HLA-DPB1*09:01)11. iPSCs were differentiated into NS/PCs at Osaka National Hospital using a previously validated protocol for embryoid bodies derived from (EB-NS/PCs)7. The resulting cell product met predefined quality standards9 (Extended Data Table 1 and Extended Data Fig. 1).

Transplantation of iPSC-NS/PCs

All participants received a single intramedullary transplantation of iPSC-NS/PCs into the lesion epicenter, with 2 × 106 iPSC-NS/PCs administered per participant (Fig. 2c). Perioperative immunosuppression was initiated the day before transplantation, maintained for 6 months, and discontinued by 9 months after transplantation. Standard rehabilitation was provided within the framework of the Japanese national health insurance system. Further details of the interventional study protocol and in vivo preclinical validation have been described previously9.

Primary outcome: safety

All four enrolled participants completed the planned 52-week interventional study period and were included in the safety analysis and exploratory efficacy analyses. Across all participants, a total of 84 AEs were recorded, with recurrent events of the same type counted separately (Table 2).

Summary of adverse events

One participant developed transient hyperkalemia with a serum potassium concentration of 6 mEq l−1, meeting the definition of a serious AE. The finding was based on a single laboratory measurement, was asymptomatic, resolved spontaneously, and was considered most consistent with a blood-sampling artifact rather than a true clinical abnormality. No serious AEs attributable to the transplanted cells were observed.

Two moderate AEs related to the surgical procedure were reported: increased upper airway secretion and cerebrospinal fluid leakage. A total of 22 mild or moderate AEs were considered related to the immunosuppressant tacrolimus. Among these, hypomagnesemia (considered probably related) and urinary tract infection (considered possibly related) were the most frequent, occurring in all participants. Urinary tract infection was considered possibly related because, although common after SCI, an effect of tacrolimus could not be excluded.

MRI and 18F-FDG PET imaging during the 52-week interventional study period showed no radiological evidence suggestive of abnormal cell proliferation (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 2). At 52 weeks after transplantation, T2-weighted MRI demonstrated a more localized T2-hyperintense area at the injury site compared to the acute phase, consistent with evolving post-traumatic changes. PET images at 24 weeks showed only physiological uptake in the salivary glands and around postoperative bone structures, with no abnormal uptake in the spinal cord.

Beyond the 52-week interventional study period, long-term observational follow-up was continued. During long-term follow-up, across all participants, no late-onset serious AEs, progressive neurological deterioration, or radiological findings suggestive of tumor formation, abnormal cell proliferation, or other graft-related radiological abnormalities were identified (Extended Data Figs. 3a–6a). On serial long-term MRI, some patients demonstrated signal changes suggestive of chronic myelomalacia at the injury site. These findings were considered compatible with the natural course of post-traumatic SCI and showed no features suggestive of tumor formation or abnormal cell proliferation.

Exploratory efficacy outcomes

All participants showed improvement in neurological and functional outcomes from baseline during the interventional study period. The ISNCSCI total motor score (range 0–100), which quantifies motor function, and the Spinal Cord Independence Measure III (SCIM III, range 0–100)12, which assesses independence in activities of daily living, both increased from baseline in all participants. Total motor score increased most rapidly during the first 12 weeks after transplantation, followed by more gradual improvement that continued in most participants up to week 52. The median change in total motor score was 7 points at week 24 and 13 points at week 52 after injury. Sensory function, assessed by ISNCSCI light touch and pinprick scores, improved in all participants during the interventional study period up to 52 weeks after transplantation.

Improvement in AIS grade was noted in two participants, one from A to C and another from A to D, consistent with partial recovery of sensory function and voluntary motor activity below the injury level. By 52 weeks after transplantation, two of the four participants (50%) had reached an AIS grade of C or higher. A summary of neurological and functional outcomes is provided in Table 3.

Comparison of motor recovery with historical cohort

For contextual reference, motor recovery was examined using a historical cohort from the Japan Single-Center Study for Spinal Cord Injury Database (JSSCI-DB)13,14 at the Spinal Injuries Center (Fukuoka, Japan), which included 986 patients hospitalized between 2013 and 2024. Patients meeting the eligibility criteria of the present study were identified for comparison from the registry cohort, resulting in a final cohort of 52 patients (Supplementary Fig. 1). The mean age was 57.9 ± 19.0 years, 86.5% were male (45 out of 52), and the baseline total motor score at 2 weeks after injury had a mean of 12.9 ± 10.9 and a median of 10.0.

In this cohort, changes in total motor score from baseline (2 weeks after injury) showed median increases of 5 and 7 points at weeks 24 and 52, respectively, in the available-case analysis, and 4 and 4 points in the last-observation-carried-forward (LOCF) analysis. In comparison, the transplantation group showed median improvements were 7 and 13 points at the corresponding time points.

At 52 weeks, 3 out of 25 patients (12.0%) in the available-case analysis and 8 out of 52 patients (15.4%) in the LOCF analysis achieved an AIS grade of C or higher, compared to 50.0% (2 out of 4) observed in the transplantation group.

These findings provide contextual reference for interpreting neurological recovery in the present study and suggest numerically greater improvement in the transplantation group. However, given the small sample size and residual differences in patient selection, these comparisons should be interpreted with caution. Detailed numerical results are provided in Supplementary Tables 3–6.

Pain and spasticity assessments

Pain was assessed using the PainDETECT questionnaire15 and the Neuropathic Pain Symptom Inventory (NPSI)16. During the interventional study period, all participants reported some degree of pain during follow-up. None experienced persistent pain with a numerical rating scale (NRS) score of 5 or higher, and all symptoms were manageable with oral medication. At week 52, three participants exhibited mild ankle spasticity graded as 1 or 1+ on the Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS)17. None of the participants exhibited grade 2 or higher spasticity, and no invasive interventions were required.

PainDETECT scores during long-term follow-up showed no consistent increase across participants. No participant developed persistent moderate-to-severe neuropathic pain, and symptoms remain manageable with conservative treatment.

A summary of pain and spasticity outcomes during the interventional study is provided in Table 3, and individual long-term pain trajectories are shown in Extended Data Figs. 3d–6d.

Immunological assessments

To evaluate immunological compatibility between donor cells and recipients, HLA typing and mixed lymphocyte reaction (MLR) assays were performed before transplantation.

HLA analysis revealed that patient 1 and patient 4 shared at least one allele with the donor at all six major loci, whereas patient 2 had no matches and patient 3 had one match (Table 1). The degree of HLA matching was not used as an exclusion criterion, and no clear association was observed between the degree of HLA matching and neurological recovery.

In the MLR assay, stimulation-index values for patients 1–3 were below 1, indicating minimal lymphocyte activation in response to the donor cells. In contrast, patient 4 showed unexpectedly low stimulation-index values even in the positive control (Extended Data Table 3).

Discussion

This study provides first-in-human evidence that transplantation of human iPSC-NS/PC into the injured human spinal cord is clinically feasible and can be performed with an acceptable safety profile at least in the medium term. Across all four participants, no tumor formation or graft-related AEs were observed during the 52-week interventional period and up to 4 years of follow-up. These findings support the safety profile previously established in preclinical studies and provide a noteworthy foundation for the clinical application of iPSC-based therapies in the CNS.

Although the clinical and safety findings are encouraging, important mechanistic questions remain. For example, in the present study, graft survival, differentiation and functional integration could not be directly demonstrated. This is an inherent limitation of human CNS transplantation studies, in which in vivo histological confirmation is not feasible. Immunological assessment showed minimal lymphocyte activation in the MLR assays, consistent with low immunogenicity, as supported by in vitro studies18,19 and a nonhuman primate study demonstrating survival of allogeneic iPSC-derived neural cells in the CNS20. In addition, a previous study of allogeneic neural progenitor cell transplantation in patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis confirmed graft survival, including in participants where immunosuppression was discontinued at 4 and 9 months after transplantation21. Similarly, in allogeneic iPSC-derived neuronal transplantation for Parkinson’s disease, indirect evidence of graft survival has been reported under an immunosuppressive regimen comparable in agent and dosing to that used in the present study22. Together with the absence of delayed neurological deterioration or AEs after discontinuation of immunosuppression, these findings are consistent with possible graft persistence in this context, although this could not be directly demonstrated in the present study. The development of methods to directly evaluate graft survival and function in the human spinal cord will be important in future studies.

Although HLA matching is generally considered advantageous in allogeneic organ transplantation, its necessity in iPSC-derived neural transplantation in the CNS remains unclear23. In this study, no clear association was observed between the degree of HLA matching and clinical outcomes, and no immune-related AEs or signs of rejection were identified. These findings suggest that, under transient immunosuppression, transplantation may be clinically feasible even with limited HLA matching iPSC-derived neural transplantation in the CNS.

Neurological and functional improvements were observed in all participants; however, given the study design and small sample size, these changes cannot be attributed to the intervention. Comparisons with registry data provide clinical context but cannot establish causality. Rehabilitation intensity was limited to the standard range provided within the Japanese national healthcare system24 and was not unusually intensive in the present study. By contrast, recovery after SCI is highly variable, and the effects of spontaneous recovery cannot be excluded. Notably, variability in recovery patterns was observed across participants, with greater improvements in those with relatively preserved spinal cord structure (patient 1 and patient 3). This observation is consistent with preclinical findings suggesting that residual neural networks may contribute to recovery after cell transplantation25, but is also compatible with patterns of spontaneous recovery. While definitive conclusions regarding causality cannot be drawn, this pattern may provide useful insight into factors influencing recovery in this setting. If recovery is influenced by residual neural networks, cell transplantation strategies may need to be tailored according to lesion severity. In individuals with more extensive lesions and minimal residual neural networks, emergent surgical reduction and stabilization to minimize secondary damage, followed by combinatorial approaches integrating cell therapy with advanced rehabilitation strategies or neurotrophic modulation26,27, may be necessary to achieve meaningful recovery.

Over the past two decades, various cell transplantation approaches for SCI have demonstrated feasibility and safety in human clinical studies28–32, but consistent therapeutic efficacy has not been established. The present study should be interpreted within this context, where the distinguishing feature lies in demonstrating that a quality-controlled iPSC-derived cell product can be delivered safely into the human spinal cord under standardized conditions. The advantages of iPSC-derived therapies reside not in early clinical efficacy, but in their ethical acceptability, stable supply and scalability of manufacturing. These features enable reduced donor dependency and improved product consistency, facilitating the development of reproducible cell-based therapeutics. In this context, the present study provides a practical foundation for future controlled trials. At the same time, this approach requires invasive intramedullary delivery and transient immunosuppression, and its clinical development will ultimately depend on demonstration of both efficacy and an acceptable risk–benefit profile in appropriately controlled studies. These risks were clearly explained to all participants, and written informed consent was obtained. This establishes a clinically applicable framework for delivering quality-controlled iPSC-NS/PCs into the human spinal cord.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a small, open-label safety study without a placebo control and was not designed to permit causal inference regarding efficacy. Historical registry data were therefore used solely as contextual reference. To enhance comparability, baseline neurological assessment was defined at 2 weeks after injury in both cohorts, and subsequent changes were evaluated from this time point. The registry cohort was aligned with the inclusion criteria with respect to injury etiology, severity, neurological level and age, and patients meeting major exclusion criteria, including those with severe comorbidities or neurological or psychiatric conditions, were also excluded. Nevertheless, these cohorts do not constitute completely matched controls, and residual differences in patient characteristics and injury pathology cannot be fully eliminated, including unmeasured factors such as laboratory abnormalities or detailed MRI findings that could not be excluded in the registry-based cohort. Future studies with appropriate controls will be required for the most robust assessment of causal relationships. In addition, further enhancement of well-curated longitudinal registry data may strengthen comparative evaluation in settings in which controlled study designs are difficult to implement.

Second, although long-term observational follow-up is ongoing, the maximum duration available at the time of manuscript preparation is approximately 2–4 years. These data further support the safety profile observed during the 52-week interventional period, and no imaging or clinical findings suggestive of tumor formation, graft overgrowth or unexpected neurological deterioration have been identified to date. Continued long-term surveillance will be needed to further define the long-term safety profile of this iPSC-derived cell product, including the risk of delayed tumorigenesis, graft overgrowth and chronic immune-mediated adverse effects.

Third, direct evidence of graft survival, differentiation and functional integration could not be obtained in the present study. In addition, advanced diffusion imaging and tractography33 were not feasible because of metallic artifacts arising from spinal instrumentation. Thus, graft persistence and its mechanistic contribution to recovery remain unresolved, underscoring the need for methodological advances that will enable direct evaluation of graft fate and biological activity in future clinical studies.

Fourth, the optimal duration and regimen of immunosuppression remain uncertain. In the absence of direct evidence of graft survival, it cannot be determined whether discontinuation of tacrolimus may have affected graft persistence. Although no adverse clinical or radiological findings were observed after withdrawal during follow-up, further studies are required to establish appropriate immunosuppression strategies.

Fifth, the study population was restricted to Japanese male participants, which may limit generalizability. In addition, the open-label design introduces the potential for observer bias and expectancy effects, particularly for clinically assessed outcomes.

In conclusion, this first-in-human clinical study of four participants with subacute complete SCI demonstrated that transplantation of human iPSC-NS/PCs can be performed safely under transient immunosuppression, with no tumor formation, graft-related AEs, or unexpected neurological deterioration observed during follow-up extending up to approximately 2–4 years. Although this study was not designed or powered to establish therapeutic efficacy, it provides a necessary foundation for subsequent controlled trials aimed at determining whether iPSC-NS/PC transplantation can confer durable and clinically meaningful functional outcomes. These findings support the clinical translation of iPSC-derived cell therapies for SCI.

Methods

Study approval, registration and safety oversight

This was a single-arm, open-label, phase 1 clinical study designed primarily to evaluate the safety of transplantation of human iPSC-NS/PCs in patients with subacute complete traumatic SCI. Exploratory efficacy outcomes, including neurological and functional recovery, were evaluated as secondary end points.

The study protocol and any subsequent amendments were reviewed to ensure compliance with the Act on the Safety of Regenerative Medicine and approved by the Certified Committee for Regenerative Medicine at Keio University (R2016001) and the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare. The study was registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000035074) and in the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCTa031190228). The long-term observational follow-up was approved by the Ethics Committee of Keio University (approval number 20221199) and registered in the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN000050104). Transplantation was performed at Keio University Hospital (Tokyo, Japan), and subsequent rehabilitation was conducted at Murayama Medical Center (Tokyo, Japan). The subsequent observational study beyond 52 weeks was performed at Keio University Hospital. There was an investigator initiated clinical study and conducted without industry sponsorship. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this trial.

After transplantation of the first participant, the absence of safety concerns was reviewed at 3 months by an independent data-monitoring committee established for this clinical study, after which transplantation of subsequent participants was initiated.

Participants

Participants with traumatic SCI classified as AIS grade A were eligible. Key inclusion criteria were age 18 years or older, and an NLI between C3/4 and T10. Transplantation was scheduled between 14 and 28 days after injury, and AIS grade A status was reconfirmed on the morning of transplantation. Exclusion criteria included multiple-site SCI, transection injury, or dural rupture on MRI, a history of SCI, or other spinal cord or subarachnoid disease, as well as pre-existing neurological disorders that could independently cause motor impairment. Participants were also excluded if they had clinically significant abnormalities in laboratory test results, severe respiratory failure, major organ damage or other conditions that would preclude rehabilitation, safe administration of immunosuppressants or reliable neurological assessment. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are described in the study protocol (Supplementary Information) and have been described previously9. Written informed consent was obtained after explanation of study risks, including those associated with first-in-human iPSC-NS/PC transplantation, as well as alternative treatment options.

Sex was recorded based on participant self-report. Sex was not considered in the study design because this phase 1 study enrolled all eligible participants who met the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria during the study period. Participants did not receive financial compensation for participation. Medical costs related to study-specific procedures and follow-up assessments were covered by the study.

Preparation of human iPSC-NS/PCs

The integration-free human iPSC line YZWJs513 was established under GMP conditions through the iPS Cell Stock Project organized by the Center for iPS Cell Research and Application (Kyoto University). It was created using umbilical-cord blood cells obtained from a healthy volunteer homozygous for most common HLA types in the Japanese population: HLA-A*24:02, HLA-B*52:01, HLA-DRB1*15:02, HLA-C*12:02, HLA-DQB1*06:01 and HLA-DPB1*09:0111. The iPSCs were then differentiated into NS/PCs at Osaka National Hospital using a previously reported protocol for EB-NS/PCs7. In brief, the iPSCs were induced into NS/PCs using serum-free culture of embryoid body-like aggregates with the quick reaggregation (SFEBq) protocol for 14 days, and then expanded using the neurosphere culture technique34,35. The cells from fourth passage were designated as the final cell product.

This final product, designated ONH-iPSNPC-003, met the predefined quality standards. These included characteristics of NS/PCs such as morphology, phenotypic marker expression and terminal neural differentiation capacity, as well as purity (absence of undifferentiated cells), chromosomal normality and absence of genomic mutations associated with reported tumorigenesis risks (Extended Data Table 1 and Extended Data Fig. 1). Quality control testing was performed in compliance with GMP according to validated standard operating procedures established through multiple (three or more) preclinical validation studies, and all results were reviewed by an independent quality assurance unit. The cells were cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen, shipped to Keio University and stored until a potential candidate was identified. At 4 days before transplantation was scheduled to take place, the cells were thawed and cultured to allow them to recover.

Cells were then treated with [N-(3,5-difluorophenacetyl)-l-alanyl]-S-phenylglycine t-butyl ester (DAPT), a γ-secretase inhibitor that is used to suppress Notch signaling3. This treatment was continued for 24 h. Before transplantation, all culture supernatant was carefully removed to ensure that no residual DAPT or culture medium remained. The iPSC-NS/PCs were then resuspended in artificial cerebrospinal fluid for injection.

Confirmation of the long-term stability of the iPSC-NS/PCs

NS/PCs derived from the same iPSC line as those used in the clinical study, but manufactured as a separate batch one year earlier using the same protocol and meeting the same quality standards, were used to evaluate long-term stability. Four predefined parameters were assessed immediately after cryopreservation, at 3 and 6 months, and subsequently at 6-month intervals up to 5 years. The assessments included viable cell count and viability assays; an ATP assay to measure the cell proliferation rate; flow cytometry assays to evaluate the expression of cell surface markers such as PSA-NCAM and GD2 and confirm cell identity; and immunostaining for βIII-tubulin and GFAP to assess differentiation potential and potency. The results are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Surgical procedure

Before surgery, preoperative MRI was used to identify the rostrocaudal level of the SCI epicenter and to measure the anteroposterior diameter of the spinal cord at that level. The depth corresponding to the injury epicenter was calculated as half of the anteroposterior diameter.

Under general anesthesia, participants were placed prone, and the spinal cord was surgically exposed. Intraoperative ultrasonography was used to identify the injury epicenter and to confirm that its depth and location were consistent with the preoperative MRI findings. A microsyringe was preloaded with the cell suspension containing 2.0 × 106 iPSC-NS/PCs, and the needle depth was preset using a depth stopper according to the preoperatively calculated distance. The needle was manually advanced toward the injury epicenter while avoiding blood vessels. Cell injection was performed slowly over at least 60 s, followed by a 3-min dwell time with the needle left in place before withdrawal. The absence of cell leakage was confirmed, after which the surgical field was irrigated and the wound was closed in a standard manner.

Immunosuppression

Perioperative immunosuppression was provided using tacrolimus. Tacrolimus administration was initiated 1 day before transplantation via intravenous infusion and maintained at a target trough level of 5–10 ng ml−1. Once oral intake became feasible, tacrolimus was switched to oral administration and maintained within the same target trough range for 6 months. Tacrolimus was then gradually tapered and discontinued by 9 months after transplantation. Blood trough levels were regularly monitored to ensure adherence to the target range and to minimize immunosuppression-related AEs.

Mixed lymphocyte reaction assay

An MLR assay was performed to assess donor–recipient immunological compatibility. Peripheral blood (20 ml) was collected from each participant before initiation of the immunosuppressive therapy as the responder cell source. iPSC-NS/PCs used for transplantation were prepared as stimulator cells. At least 3.0 × 106 cells were collected, suspended in culture medium without air exposure, and stored at 4 °C until use. Peripheral blood (10 ml) obtained from an unrelated healthy volunteer, recruited under a separately approved ethical protocol with written informed consent, was used as an allogeneic control. The MLR assay was conducted by an external clinical laboratory (SRL) according to their standardized procedures.

Rehabilitation

In-hospital rehabilitation was provided within the usual therapeutic range, as defined by the Japanese national health insurance system24 during the 52-week interventional study period. That is, for the first 90 days after injury, rehabilitation was provided for up to 3 h per day, and up to 2 h per day thereafter. Rehabilitation included physical therapy, occupational therapy and dysphagia rehabilitation, all of which were tailored to each participant’s neurological status. No advanced interventions, such as electrical stimulation or robotic-assisted therapy were employed.

Outcome measures

During the 52-week interventional study period, safety was the primary outcome and was assessed through systematic monitoring of all AEs. Exploratory efficacy outcomes were evaluated using validated neurological, functional, and patient-reported outcome measures.

Neurological function was assessed using the ISNCSCI10, including motor and sensory scores and neurological severity was additionally categorized using the modified Frankel classification36,37. Functional independence was evaluated using SCIM III12. Neuropathic pain was assessed using the PainDETECT questionnaire15 and the NPSI16 and spasticity was evaluated using the MAS17. These outcome measures were assessed at 1, 3, 5 and 8 weeks after transplantation and subsequently at 4-week intervals until 52 weeks after transplantation.

In the subsequent long-term observational follow-up, neurological and functional outcomes were assessed using ISNCSCI motor and sensory scores and SCIM III, and pain and spasticity were evaluated using PainDETECT and MAS. These assessments were performed at 6-month intervals for the first 3 years after transplantation and annually thereafter.

MRI acquisition and evaluation

MRI of the cervical spine was performed at baseline before cell transplantation, at predefined time points during follow-up, including at 3, 12, 24 and 52 weeks after cell transplantation during the interventional study. During long-term observational follow-up, MRI was performed at 6-month intervals for the first 3 years and annually thereafter.

MRI was performed using either a 1.5T MRI scanner (Signa HDxt and Signa Artist, GE Healthcare) or a 3T MRI scanner (Discovery MR750 and Signa Pioneer, GE Healthcare), with a standard cervical spine coil. The imaging protocol included sagittal and axial T1-weighted and T2-weighted sequences. Sagittal images were acquired with slices oriented parallel to the long axis of the cervical spinal cord, and axial images were obtained at cervical levels C1 through C7 with slices oriented parallel to the intervertebral disks. For T1-weighted imaging, repetition time (TR) ranged from approximately 350 to 660 ms and echo time (TE) from 6 to 13 ms. For T2-weighted imaging, TR ranged from approximately 3,100 to 5,000 ms and TE from 80 to 130 ms. The field of view ranged from approximately 230 to 400 mm for sagittal images and 160 to 200 mm for axial images. The flip angle was 90° for all sequences. Slice thickness ranged from 3 to 4 mm for sagittal images and from 4 to 5 mm for axial images.

Contrast-enhanced MRI was not included in the imaging protocol, as contrast enhancement is known to be variable and nonspecific in intramedullary spinal cord tumors, including gliomas, and may also be observed in non-neoplastic conditions such as SCI itself. Accordingly, tumor surveillance was based on serial noncontrast MRI to evaluate temporal changes in spinal cord morphology and signal characteristics around the transplantation site.

MRI evaluation for tumor formation was based on established clinical and radiological features reported for intramedullary spinal cord tumors and related pathological processes38–41. Intramedullary tumors are generally recognized on MRI as either mass-forming lesions that cause focal spinal cord compression or infiltrative lesions that diffusely involve the spinal cord parenchyma. Accordingly, MRI surveillance focused on identifying both patterns. For mass-forming lesions, tumor formation was defined as the appearance of a newly developed, well-demarcated lesion that was not present on preceding MRI examinations. For infiltrative lesions, particular attention was paid to progressive or heterogeneous signal abnormalities on T1- and T2-weighted images, lesion expansion, ill-defined lesion margins, craniocaudal spinal cord edema, inflammatory signal changes adjacent to the transplantation site and spinal cord swelling, including features described for gliomas, which represent a major category of infiltrative spinal cord tumors40.

All MRI scans were reviewed descriptively by experienced spine surgeons and neuroradiologists, focusing on potential tumor formation and other graft-related pathological changes following iPSC-NS/PC transplantation.

PET acquisition and evaluation

18F-FDG PET imaging of the whole body was performed 24 weeks after cell transplantation to evaluate metabolic activity. Patients were instructed to fast for at least 6 h before tracer injection. A dose of 4.0 MBq kg−1 FDG was administered intravenously, followed by a 60-min uptake period while the patient was in a resting position. Imaging was performed using a PET–CT scanner (Biograph Vision 600, Siemens Medical Solutions), with acquisition covering the entire body from the top of the head to the soles of the feet. A low-dose CT scan was acquired simultaneously for attenuation correction and anatomical localization.

Axial PET images of the cervical spine were reconstructed using standard algorithms. To measure the maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax), volumes of interest (VOIs) were manually defined to encompass the entire spinal cord cross-section at the axial slice corresponding to the level of transplantation. The SUVmax within each VOI was calculated using dedicated image analysis software (syngo.via, Siemens Healthineers). The SUVmax values were compared to reported physiological uptake levels of the spinal cord in healthy individuals to assess abnormal metabolic activity, with cervical spinal cord SUVmax values ranging from approximately 1.8 to 2.5, as reported in previous studies42,43.

Adverse event assessment

All reported AEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA), v.27.1, to ensure standardized classification across categories. The severity of AEs was graded in accordance with the Japanese governmental criteria for classification of adverse drug reactions, as defined by a notification of the Pharmaceutical Safety Bureau of the Ministry of Health and Welfare (notification no. 80, 1992).

For symptoms considered intrinsic to the underlying SCI, reflecting pre-existing neurological or autonomic dysfunction rather than effects of the intervention, severity grading was performed using the same three-grade classification framework based on a study-specific AE grading reference sheet. This reference sheet was predefined before trial initiation through consultation with an independent data-monitoring committee established for this clinical study, to ensure consistent and appropriate grading in the context of SCI.

According to these criteria, AEs were classified as grade 1 (mild adverse reactions), grade 2 (moderate adverse reactions that were neither mild nor severe) or grade 3 (severe adverse reactions), defined as events that, depending on patient characteristics or clinical circumstances at onset, could potentially result in death or permanent functional impairment that interferes with activities of daily living.

Statistical analysis

All patients who received study treatment were included in the safety and efficacy analysis populations. Continuous variables were summarized descriptively, and categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Safety data were summarized descriptively, with point estimates of proportions calculated without confidence intervals. Given the small sample size of this study, no formal hypothesis testing or confidence interval estimation was performed for efficacy end points. Longitudinal changes in motor scores through 52 weeks were summarized descriptively. All enrolled participants were male; therefore, no sex-based analyses were performed. All analyses were performed using SAS v.9.4 (SAS Institute) and R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Historical cohort analysis

An exploratory comparison was conducted using data from the JSSCI-DB13,14, a registry of patients with traumatic SCI at the Spinal Injuries Center (Fukuoka, Japan), which included 986 patients registered between 2013 and 2024. From this population, patients classified as AIS grade A at 2 weeks after injury with neurological injury levels between C4 and C8 were identified as a historical cohort for comparison with the present clinical study. Additional eligibility criteria were applied using available registry variables to approximate the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the present study, including exclusion of patients with severe respiratory impairment and those with major comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, cerebrovascular disease, renal dysfunction, or psychiatric disorders. Patients without available total motor score data beyond 4 weeks post-injury were excluded. The final analysis set comprised 52 patients.

Total motor score at 2 weeks after injury was defined as baseline, and changes from this baseline were evaluated over time using available-case and LOCF approaches. When measurements at a given time point were missing, values at hospital discharge were used if temporally closest. Neurological outcomes were summarized descriptively as changes in total motor score over time and visualized using longitudinal box-and-whisker plots through 52 weeks after injury. AIS grade conversion to C or higher at 52 weeks was also evaluated. No formal statistical testing was performed.

Information on MRI-based exclusion criteria (for example, multilevel SCI, complete transection or dural injury) and laboratory-based exclusion criteria were not available in the registry and could not be applied. Therefore, residual differences in patient selection between the registry cohort and the present study cannot be excluded.
