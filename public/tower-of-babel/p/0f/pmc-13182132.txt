Prehospital treatment modalities for acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: a scoping review
Authors: Johanne Thorngaard Kristensen, Asger Bülow, Arne Sylvester Rønde Jensen, Martin Faurholdt Gude
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) is a frequent reason for emergency calls and carries substantial short- and long-term mortality. Prehospital management is widely used but variably supported by evidence.Study objectiveTo systematically identify and map prehospital interventions for AECOPD and describe the breadth and characteristics of the existing evidence, with the aim of informing future research priorities and prehospital care planning.MethodsPubMed and Embase were searched through January 2025 following PRISMA-ScR standards. Two reviewers independently screened and charted studies, with third-reviewer consensus. Eligible studies addressed prehospital interventions for confirmed or suspected AECOPD. No formal quality appraisal was performed. The protocol was preregistered on OSF (10.17605/OSF.IO/CZNG8).ResultsAmong 2,591 records, 34 studies were included (28 primary and 6 secondary). Most primary evidence was observational, and only about two-thirds of studies enrolled confirmed AECOPD populations. Controlled oxygen therapy (n = 8) was associated with lower observed mortality and fewer adverse physiological outcomes compared with liberal oxygen delivery, informed by one cluster randomized trial and several observational studies. CPAP (n = 7) and NIV (n = 7) consistently improved short-term physiological parameters. Randomized evidence for patient-centered outcomes remains limited, as all CPAP/NIV trials were small, enrolled mixed respiratory failure populations, or both. Evidence for dexamethasone, air-driven nebulizers, point-of-care ultrasound, and hyperoxemia harms was sparse and largely observational.ConclusionControlled oxygen therapy has the largest prehospital evidence base, and titrated oxygen targeting SpO₂ 88–92% is associated with more favorable physiological profiles and lower observed mortality than liberal oxygen delivery, although certainty is limited without formal quality appraisal. CPAP and NIV improve short-term physiology, but effects on patient-centered outcomes remain uncertain. Evidence for other interventions is minimal, and substantial knowledge gaps remain.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-026-01571-2.

Introduction

Chronic respiratory diseases affected about 544.9 million people globally by 2017, with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) accounting for approximately 55% of cases [1]. COPD caused an estimated 3.2 million deaths worldwide in 2015, a 12% increase since 1990, making it the third leading cause of death globally [2, 3]. Defined by irreversible, reduced expiratory airflow due to alveolar damage and loss of elastic tissue, COPD remains a major global health burden [1, 4, 5].

Acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD), defined as sudden worsening of respiratory symptoms requiring additional therapy, accelerate disease progression, impair quality of life, and carry high short- and long-term mortality, with 30-day and 1-year death rates around 10% and 30%, respectively [6–10]. In the U.S., annual COPD treatment costs exceed $32 billion, with exacerbations accounting for more than half of expenditures [9, 11]. About two-thirds of patients presenting to emergency departments (EDs) with COPD arrive by ambulance [10, 12].

Although specific prehospital interventions and ED management strategies have been reviewed, a comprehensive assessment of prehospital AECOPD care remains limited. This scoping review maps and summarizes existing literature on prehospital management of AECOPD, highlighting current practices, evidence strength, and knowledge gaps to inform future care strategies.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR guidelines [12]. The protocol, based on the methodological framework by Arksey and O’Malley, was preregistered with the Open Science Framework (OSF) on December 11, 2024 (10.17605/OSF.IO/CZNG8) before literature screening to ensure transparency and adherence to predefined methods [13, 14].

Information sources, search and selection of sources of evidence

A search strategy combining keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) was developed with support from an Aarhus University Library information specialist and refined through team discussion. No date limits were applied, but only English-language studies were included. PubMed and Embase were last searched on January 27, 2025; detailed search strings are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Search results were managed in Covidence software [16]. Screening occurred in three phases: a pilot round on a small article sample to ensure consistent application of criteria, followed by title/abstract and full-text screening. The first 60 records were screened jointly by all three reviewers (JTK, AB, MFG) to ensure consistency, after which two reviewers (JTK, AB) screened independently, with discrepancies resolved by the third (MFG).

Data charting and synthesis

After reviewer consensus on key variables, one reviewer (JTK) extracted data on study characteristics, interventions, outcomes, and findings, exported to Excel for synthesis. No formal quality or risk-of-bias assessment was performed, consistent with scoping review methodology. Data were summarized descriptively in tables and figures for clarity. Heterogeneity in study populations, designs, and outcomes was described narratively rather than quantitatively analyzed, consistent with scoping review methodology.

Results

The initial search yielded 2,591 records. After removing duplicates, 2,426 titles and abstracts were screened, with 2,339 excluded. Of 87 full texts assessed, 12 were not retrievable and 5 were excluded due to non-English language, leaving 70 records for review. After applying eligibility criteria, 34 studies were included in the final analysis, comprising 28 primary studies and 6 secondary evidence sources (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the primary studies

The 28 primary studies originated from Europe (n = 12, 45%) [17–28], Australia or New Zealand (n = 7, 24%) [29–35], North America (n = 6, 21%) [36–41], and Asia (n = 3, 10%) [42–44], with over half (n = 16) published in or before 2015 [18, 19, 23–25, 28–31, 34–38, 40, 41]. The majority of studies (n = 18, 66%) enrolled patients with confirmed COPD or AECOPD [17–19, 21–24, 26, 27, 29, 31, 32, 34, 35, 40, 42–44]. The remainder (n = 10, 34%) included mixed or suspected COPD or broader non-traumatic respiratory failure cohorts, limiting COPD-specific interpretation [20, 25, 28, 30, 33, 36–39, 41]. Controlled oxygen therapy

Interpretation of intervention-specific findings must consider that not all studies enrolled confirmed AECOPD populations, and several included mixed respiratory failure cohorts. Observational studies consistently show that high-flow oxygen in AECOPD is associated with worse outcomes than titrated delivery, including higher rates of respiratory acidosis, hypercapnia, ICU admission, and in-hospital mortality [17–19, 29, 31]. In a Danish prehospital cohort, mortality was 19.6% with high-flow oxygen versus 4.6% with titrated oxygen [17], while a UK hospital audit reported 14% versus 2% [18]. Other audits linked high-flow oxygen to increased risk of hypercapnia, ICU admission, and poor composite outcomes such as death, ventilation, or respiratory failure [19, 29, 31]. Risks were greater in patients with longer transports (> 30 min) [19], and those with severe underlying disease (e.g., previous respiratory failure, assisted ventilation, or home oxygen or nebulizer use) [19, 31], whereas higher baseline Glasgow Coma Score and oxygen saturation were protective [29].

The strongest evidence comes from the cluster randomized controlled trial (RCT) by Austin et al., which included a relatively small sample of 214 patients with confirmed COPD [35]. Titrated oxygen targeting SpO₂ 88–92% reduced mortality from 9% with high-flow to 2% — an absolute difference of 7% points (NNT ≈ 14; p = 0.04). In patients without confirmed COPD, mortality fell from 9% to 6% (NNT ≈ 33), though no p-value was reported.

In summary, controlled oxygen therapy was generally consistently associated with lower rates of hypercapnia, acidosis, ICU admission, and mortality compared with high-flow oxygen across one cluster randomized trial and multiple observational studies. The available literature suggests benefit of titrated oxygen targeting SpO₂ 88–92% in prehospital AECOPD care, noting that we did not perform formal risk-of-bias appraisal in this scoping review.

Hyperoxemia

Across emergency medical services (EMS) and ED studies in AECOPD, “hyperoxemia” is defined relative to COPD oxygen targets (88–92%) rather than normal physiology, with thresholds ranging from PaO₂ >65 mmHg (~ SpO₂ >92%) to PaO₂ >100 mmHg on the first ED blood gas, usually after prehospital oxygen [51, 52].

In New Zealand, a retrospective study of 680 ambulance presentations with suspected AECOPD included 254 patients with ED blood gases obtained within four hours of arrival [34]. 24% had hyperoxemia (PaO₂ > 100 mmHg). It was associated with higher odds of hypercapnic respiratory failure (adjusted OR 2.9, 95% CI 1.6–5.3, p < 0.001) and assisted ventilation (adjusted OR 5.27, 95% CI 2.7–10.4, p < 0.001), but not with mortality (adjusted OR 1.74, 95% CI 0.48–6.25, p = 0.41).

In Singapore, a prospective study of 326 confirmed AECOPD cases defined hyperoxemia as PaO₂ > 65 mmHg (~ SpO₂ > 92%) on the first ED blood gas [42]. An initial ED SpO₂ >95% predicted hyperoxemia on blood gas (adjusted OR 2.62, 95% CI 1.61–4.33, p < 0.001), as did prehospital use of a non-rebreather mask (adjusted OR 2.01, p = 0.04) and ED nasal cannula use (adjusted OR 1.69, p = 0.03). One-year follow-up showed no significant associations with mortality (hazard ratio 1.57, p = 0.196) or rehospitalization, though three-month mortality was borderline higher in the hyperoxemic group (10.9% vs. 4.3%, p = 0.05) [43].

A Danish audit of 405 confirmed AECOPD cases compared SpO₂ strata among patients receiving high-concentration oxygen on arrival and found no significant associations with length of stay, ventilatory support, or in-hospital mortality [23].

In summary, hyperoxemia was common among prehospital AECOPD patients but showed inconsistent associations with adverse outcomes. Across three studies, over-target oxygenation was associated with greater risk of hypercapnia and assisted ventilation, while effects on mortality, hospital stay, and readmission were not statistically significant.

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP)

In prehospital AECOPD populations, continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) consistently improves short-term physiology, but effects on clinical outcomes remain uncertain. Several included CPAP studies enrolled mixed acute respiratory failure populations in which COPD comprised only a subset.

Observational cohorts from the U.S. and Germany (n = 74, 340, and 22) reported similar improve­ments: oxygen saturation increased from 80 to 85% to 94–97%, respiratory rate decreased from 27 to 35 to 18–28 breaths/min, and heart rate declined modestly [21, 36, 39]. Changes in Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), blood pressure, and end-tidal CO₂ were inconsistent [36]. Hard outcomes varied: one study suggested a 77% mortality reduction with CPAP (OR 0.23, p = 0.005), though the effect was overstated by the odds ratio given a control mortality of 24% [39]. Conversely, in before–after comparisons, mortality was higher in the CPAP period (18.8% vs. 14.9%, p < 0.001) [21] and in another before-after study (5.9% vs. 2.2%, p = 0.13) [37]. Intubation rates were generally unchanged [37, 40]. Increased downstream ED use of non-invasive ventilation (NIV) was also noted (20% vs. 13.4%, p < 0.001) [40]. Additionally, in an Italian out-of-hospital helmet CPAP cohort, Garuti et al. reported lower in-hospital mortality compared with non-contemporaneous historical controls (prehospital CPAP 2.9%, hospital CPAP 13%, overall CPAP 8.6% vs. 24% in controls), although the components of standard care in the control group were not fully specified. The mixed acute respiratory failure population and before–after design further limit causal inference [28].

Randomized evidence is scarce. The largest RCT of prehospital CPAP, an open-label study from Australia (n = 708, 34% COPD), found significant improvement in median dyspnea scores (–2 points, p < 0.001) and median respiratory rate (–4 breaths/minute, p < 0.001), but no effect on hospital length of stay or 30-day mortality [33]. By contrast, a smaller Canadian randomized trial (n = 71, 34% COPD) reported marked benefits: intubation decreased from 50% to 20% (adjusted OR 0.16, 95% CI 0.04–0.70), and mortality from 35% to 14% (OR 0.30, 95% CI 0.09–0.99), with numbers needed to treat of 3 and 5, respectively. Hospital and ICU length of stay were unchanged [41].

In summary, CPAP reliably improves physiological parameters, but evidence for patient-centered outcomes in confirmed AECOPD remains inconsistent, supported by one RCT and multiple observational studies. Because many studies included mixed respiratory failure populations, COPD-specific inference is limited.

Non-invasive ventilation (NIV)

Prehospital NIV in populations including AECOPD consistently improves short-term physiology and appears safe. Evidence for more clinically significant outcomes remains weak.

We identified three cohorts: a German prospective cohort (n = 103; 54% COPD) [20], a German multicenter prospective cohort (n = 545; AECOPD/acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema) [26], and an Australian retrospective cohort (n = 264; 45% COPD) [30]. Compared with standard medical care, prehospital NIV reduced respiratory rate (range 18–30 to 17–24 breaths per minute) [26], increased oxygen saturation (range 92–95% to 96–98% SpO₂) [20, 30], and lowered dyspnea scores (median 7 to 2 points) [20]. Effects on heart rate and blood pressure were inconsistent [20, 26, 30]. In matched comparisons with endotracheal intubation, the multicenter cohort found that prehospital NIV was associated with a shorter ICU length of stay and a shorter duration of mechanical ventilation, with improved oxygenation on ED arrival and no reduction in in-hospital mortality [30]. NIV was also associated with longer on-scene and transport times (38 vs. 20 min, p < 0.001) without reported adverse events [20], and NIV failure was mainly associated with a low initial GCS (< 15) [38].

A Spanish prospective cohort (n = 317; 48% confirmed AECOPD) compared prehospital to in-hospital NIV initiation [22]. Prehospital NIV was associated with markedly fewer hospital readmissions (14.2% vs. 42.7%, p < 0.001) and ED revisits (17.9% vs. 48.3%, p < 0.001), with non-significant trends toward shorter hospital length of stay (8 vs. 8.8 days, p = 0.07), lower in-hospital mortality (12% vs. 19.4%, p = 0.08), and fewer ICU admissions (9.9% vs. 11.3%, p = 0.73) [22].

Randomized evidence is limited and underpowered for hard outcomes. A German open-label trial (n = 36; AECOPD) reported improved respiratory rate (p = 0.001), improved dyspnea (p < 0.001), and a shorter ICU stay (mean 59 vs. 185 h, p = 0.02) for prehospital NIV, with no differences in endotracheal intubation, mortality, or total hospital length of stay; exact prehospital-to-arrival values for respiratory rate and dyspnea were not reported [25]. A pilot randomized trial (n = 51) likewise showed faster physiological improvement and a shorter ICU stay with prehospital NIV compared with standard care, as described in the original study, without significant differences in endotracheal intubation, invasive ventilation hours, or survival [24].

Overall, prehospital NIV reliably improves physiological parameters and is generally safe, but long-term benefits and hard outcomes in confirmed AECOPD remain uncertain.

Characteristics of secondary evidence

Six secondary evidence sources were identified: three originated from North America [45–47], two from Australia and New Zealand [48, 49], and one from Europe [50]. Their scopes spanned controlled oxygen therapy [46, 48, 49], non-invasive ventilatory support with CPAP/NIV [45, 50], and combined oxygen/CPAP guidance for prehospital respiratory distress [47]. Methodologically, one was PRISMA-based [45], two were systematic reviews [48, 50], one was an evidence-based practice guideline [47], one a narrative/overview [46], and one a multi-year audit [49].

Primary-study overlap was substantial. The cluster-randomized trial by Austin et al. [35] appeared in four secondary sources [46–49], while several other trials recurred across two to three sources (see Table 4; Fig. 3). This overlap may have amplified certain signals (notably for controlled oxygen).

Across sources, conclusions were consistent: controlled oxygen targeting SpO₂ 88–92% was favored over high-flow oxygen for AECOPD, with lower risks of hypercapnia, acidosis, and mortality [46–49]. For CPAP/NIV, reviews found physiologic improvement and general safety, but inconsistent effects on hard outcomes (intubation, mortality) and marked heterogeneity in designs, populations, and implementation [45, 50]. Collectively, the reviews highlight evidence gaps—particularly COPD-only randomized trials—and recommend larger, well-designed studies to clarify patient selection, protocols, and outcome benefits.

Battery-powered air nebulizers

Evidence is limited to a single audit (pre-/post observational design), and overall certainty is very low. In the Dutch study by Heys et al., 200 prehospital AECOPD patients before and 200 after the introduction of air-driven nebulizers were compared [32]. Subsequent to the change from oxygen-driven to air-driven nebulizers, use of air nebulizers increased from 0% to ~ 56% (p < 0.001). The delivered oxygen fraction dropped from 100% to 71.5% (p < 0.001), and median SpO₂ decreased from 96% to 94% (p < 0.001). The proportion of patients arriving in the 88–92% SpO2 target range rose from 16.5% to 24%, although the adjusted odds of target attainment (OR 1.60, 95% CI 0.97–2.62) did not reach statistically significant [32].

Lower inspired oxygen fractions may help limit oxygen-induced hypercapnia, but Heys et al. reported that patients with low baseline saturations risked hypoxemia [32]. They emphasized the need for careful titration and close monitoring. In summary, battery-powered nebulizers reduce oxygen delivery during bronchodilation, yet evidence is limited to a single study and no clear clinical benefit has been established.

Intravenous dexamethasone

Evidence for prehospital dexamethasone in AECOPD is limited and stems from a single retrospective cohort [44]. In Thailand, a study of 93 confirmed COPD patients found that prehospital on-scene intravenous dexamethasone (8 mg) was associated with similar hospital admission rates (80.8% vs. 85.4%, p = 0.561) and a non-significant reduction in median ED length of stay (232 vs. 296 min, p = 0.106). Interpretation is limited by missing data on symptom onset, timing of administration, and transport duration, which may dilute any measurable effect of a single corticosteroid dose [44].

In summary, current evidence does not demonstrate a clear benefit of prehospital dexamethasone in AECOPD, and overall certainty remains low.

Point-of-care ultrasound

Evidence is limited to a single small case-control study. Zanatta et al. evaluated prehospital lung ultrasound in 18 patients with suspected AECOPD and found that, compared with standard care, ultrasound-integrated assessment was associated with lower furosemide use (3.3 vs. 15.3 mg, p = 0.036), greater CPAP use (p = 0.011), and lower PCO₂ on ED arrival (42.6 vs. 52.2 mmHg, p = 0.049) [27]. Use of methylprednisolone and ED length of stay did not differ significantly. The authors reported high diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity 100%, specificity 94.4%) for detecting interstitial/alveolar fluid (B-lines) [27]. In this study, ultrasound was primarily used to support differential diagnosis, particularly to distinguish COPD exacerbation from cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, and findings influenced treatment choices and transport decisions in 42% of cases.

In summary, prehospital lung ultrasound may assist diagnostic clarification and early management decisions in suspected AECOPD, but evidence is confined to one small study and no demonstrated improvement in patient-centered outcome has been shown.

Discussion

A key feature of the evidence base is substantial clinical heterogeneity. Only about two-thirds of primary studies enrolled patients with confirmed AECOPD, while the remainder included suspected COPD or mixed acute respiratory failure populations, often with cardiogenic pulmonary edema. This limits the degree to which COPD-specific conclusions can be drawn and underscores that many findings reflect broader respiratory failure management rather than AECOPD alone.

Controlled oxygen therapy

Controlled oxygen therapy has been associated with lower observed mortality and improved physiological outcomes compared with high-flow oxygen, particularly during longer transports, although interpretation is limited by study design and lack of formal quality appraisal. Interpretation of existing evidence must consider potential confounding by indication, as patients receiving higher oxygen flows are often more severely ill. However, the cluster randomized trial by Austin et al. supports a strategy of titrated oxygen targeting SpO₂ 88–92%, which is consistent with concern that excessive oxygen may contribute to adverse physiological effects [35]. Consistent with guideline summaries (GOLD 2024), ventilatory support with NIV is primarily relevant in hypercapnic respiratory failure, while oxygen is titrated to avoid over-oxygenation [8].

Across all secondary evidence sources, findings were concordant: controlled oxygen targeting SpO₂ 88–92% was favored over high-flow oxygen for AECOPD, with lower risks of hypercapnia, acidosis, and mortality [46–49]. The cluster-randomized trial by Austin et al. [35] represents the largest randomized evidence base, reporting a mortality reduction, including among patients without confirmed COPD. Although the mechanism in non-COPD subgroups remains uncertain, this trial and multiple observational studies collectively suggest that titrated oxygen delivery is a reasonable prehospital strategy, while acknowledging limited certainty without formal quality appraisal. The study design in Austin et al. included volunteer EMS providers, which may have contributed to an additional cluster effect related to training and behavior rather than physiology alone. A large and well-designed randomized controlled trial in confirmed AECOPD, the ongoing Danish STOP-COPD trial, will provide more definitive evidence [53].

Hyperoxemia

Hyperoxemia is frequently observed in ambulance-treated AECOPD, but its prognostic significance remains uncertain. Reported associations likely reflect variation in thresholds used to define hyperoxemia, timing of blood gas sampling, and patient characteristics such as disease severity, diagnostic certainty, and oxygen delivery methods. The key question is whether oxygenation above the recommended target range independently predicts worse outcomes.

Confounding by indication must be considered, since patients with more severe illness are more likely to receive high-concentration oxygen and thus face a greater risk of overshooting the target. However, high arterial oxygen levels are also seen in less severe cases, where they may reflect preserved lung function rather than oxygen-induced harm, complicating causal interpretation. Observational data indicate that SpO₂ values above 95% and the use of non-rebreather masks or nasal cannulas are associated with hyperoxemia, illustrating how easily oxygen delivery can exceed the intended range in prehospital care [42].

No uniform threshold defines when hyperoxemia becomes harmful, complicating titration in dynamic prehospital settings. Apart from the Austin et al. randomized trial targeting SpO₂ 88–92%, which indirectly informs the risk of over-oxygenation, no randomized evidence specifically addresses this issue. Consequently, causal inference remains limited, and reverse causation cannot be excluded. Variation in definitions, measurement timing, and oxygen administration practices across studies further limits comparability. Although several large observational cohorts have examined this topic, the overall strength of evidence remains low to moderate, with substantial risk of bias. The current evidence does not allow clear separation between the effects of oxygen induced hyperoxemia and the clinical trajectory associated with severe illness. Importantly, trials comparing titrated versus high-flow oxygen evaluate oxygen delivery strategies rather than the independent effects of hyperoxemia itself.

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP)

CPAP provides consistent physiological improvement in prehospital AECOPD, but effects on mortality, intubation, and hospital outcomes remain uncertain. However, most studies enrolled mixed acute respiratory failure populations rather than confirmed AECOPD alone. Variability in disease severity, timing of initiation, and provider expertise likely contributes to inconsistent findings. Selection bias is also possible, as patients receiving prehospital CPAP may represent a higher-risk subset than those managed with standard care.

Observational evidence is mixed. Some cohorts reported improved oxygenation and respiratory rate without mortality benefit, while others found neutral or adverse trends. The study by Cheskes et al. exemplifies this gap, showing no reduction in intubation or death despite improved physiological parameters. The two randomized trials likewise produced divergent results and had limited generalizability, as only about one-third of participants had confirmed AECOPD, and treatment protocols differed in device type, pressure settings, and provider experience. The apparent mortality reduction reported by Garuti et al. could reflect selection bias and residual confounding, as the study was nonrandomized and used noncontemporaneous controls [28].

Secondary evidence also diverges. Some systematic and narrative reviews found no consistent reduction in intubation or mortality, whereas others reported potential benefit in selected populations, though all agree that CPAP is safe and well tolerated in prehospital use. Both reviews highlighted the absence of major adverse events and noted that treatment discontinuations were mainly due to patient discomfort or logistics.

Overall, while the clinical efficacy of CPAP in AECOPD remains uncertain, the intervention appears safe when delivered by trained EMS providers with appropriate equipment and monitoring. The available evidence therefore supports CPAP primarily as a stabilizing measure rather than a proven outcome altering intervention.

Non-invasive ventilation (NIV)

Prehospital NIV appears safe and consistently improves short-term physiology in AECOPD. However, many included studies enrolled mixed respiratory failure populations rather than confirmed AECOPD alone. Although on-scene treatment time is prolonged, outcomes remain favorable and no excess harm has been reported. The benefits may be overestimated, as surrogate measures such as oxygenation or respiratory rate were often emphasized over clinically meaningful endpoints like mortality, intubation, or ICU stay, which were usually secondary and underpowered. NIV failure has been associated with low initial Glasgow Coma Scale scores [38], underscoring the importance of timely initiation before deterioration reduces cooperation and airway protection.

Evidence for hard outcomes remains weak and interpretation limited by bias. Cohort studies suggest possible reductions in ICU stay, ventilation duration, and readmissions, but findings for mortality and total hospital stay are inconsistent. Most cohorts lacked adequate confounding adjustment, limiting certainty for COPD-specific effects. Randomized trials were small, heterogeneous in protocol, and enrolled few confirmed COPD patients, leaving them underpowered to detect differences in mortality or intubation rates. Consequently, it remains unclear whether short-term physiological improvements translate into meaningful clinical benefits.

Secondary evidence likewise finds current data insufficient for firm recommendations. Both reviews cite limited sample sizes, variable inclusion criteria, and protocol heterogeneity, but agree that prehospital NIV appears safe when delivered by trained providers.

Overall, prehospital NIV appears feasible and is associated with short-term physiological improvements, but evidence for patient-centered outcomes remains limited. Evidence supporting NIV for acute hypercapnic AECOPD is strong in the in-hospital setting (e.g., Cochrane and recent high-intensity NIV trials such as HAPPEN), but this evidence does not necessarily generalize to prehospital initiation, where context, monitoring, and resources differ [54, 55]. Current prehospital data therefore suggest physiological benefit but remain insufficient to establish downstream clinical impact.

Adjunctive therapies

Evidence for adjunctive therapies in prehospital AECOPD is sparse and almost entirely observational, leaving findings highly susceptible to short-term bias and confounding. Battery-powered air-driven nebulizers have been evaluated in only one observational study, which suggested a modest, non-significant improvement in attainment of target oxygen saturation ranges by lowering inspired oxygen fraction during bronchodilator delivery. Clinical relevance remains uncertain.

Prehospital dexamethasone has likewise been evaluated in a single observational cohort, which found no significant effect on ED length of stay or hospital admission rates. Interpretation is limited by missing data on symptom onset and transport duration, both of which could influence the measurable effect of a single corticosteroid dose. Potential time-dependent confounding may also have occurred, as patients with longer transport times had more opportunity to respond, while confounding by indication remains likely since prehospital administration was at the discretion of EMS providers and those treated may have had more severe disease than those who were not.

Point-of-care lung ultrasound was assessed in a single small case-control study, where it functioned mainly as a diagnostic adjunct to differentiate COPD exacerbation from cardiogenic pulmonary oedema through detection of B-lines. Ultrasound findings appeared to influence decisions on diuretic therapy, selection of respiratory support (e.g., CPAP), and possibly hospital destination. However, it showed no effect on broader clinical outcomes. Interpretation is limited by the small sample size and risk of confounding by indication, as clinicians may have used ultrasound more often in diagnostically uncertain cases. Moreover, because the same clinicians performed the scans and made treatment decisions, observer and measurement bias cannot be excluded.

No secondary evidence has specifically addressed these interventions, underscoring a persistent evidence gap.

Overall, evidence strength is very low across all three modalities, constrained by small sample sizes, single-study origins, and the absence of randomized data. These findings highlight that several interventions commonly used in prehospital AECOPD care continue to rely on minimal empirical support.

Future research should prioritize adequately powered randomized trials in confirmed AECOPD populations, with clear diagnostic criteria and standardized EMS treatment protocols. Key areas include optimal timing and patient selection for non-invasive ventilatory support and direct comparison of prehospital respiratory support strategies. Recent in-hospital trial evidence showing HFNO non-inferiority to NIV further underscores the need for prehospital head-to-head trials of respiratory support strategies in confirmed AECOPD [56]. Clearer separation of COPD-specific cohorts from mixed respiratory failure populations and greater consistency in patient-centered outcome reporting would strengthen the evidence base for prehospital decision-making.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. The search was restricted to PubMed and Embase, which cover most biomedical literature, though some studies may have been missed. Exclusion of non-English and inaccessible full-text articles introduces potential language, publication, and selection bias and may leave gaps in an already sparsely investigated field. No formal quality assessment was conducted, consistent with scoping review methodology, but this limits evaluation of methodological rigor and certainty of individual findings. Substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity was present, including variation in disease confirmation and inclusion of mixed respiratory failure populations. Approximately one-third of studies did not enroll confirmed AECOPD populations, which limits COPD-specific inference. Finally, studies published after the search period may affect the completeness and currency of the evidence base. Because the search was completed in January 2025, we cite GOLD 2024 summaries and did not incorporate later draft updates (e.g., GOLD 2026). These limitations reflect the overall scarcity and variability of the available evidence and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

Controlled oxygen therapy has the largest prehospital evidence base, and the included literature suggests that titrated oxygen targeting an SpO₂ of 88–92% has been associated with more favorable physiological profiles and lower observed mortality than liberal oxygen therapy, although certainty is limited by study design and the absence of formal quality appraisal. CPAP and NIV consistently improve short-term physiological parameters and can support patient stabilization during transport, but their effects on patient-centered outcomes remain uncertain. Evidence for other interventions, including glucocorticoids, nebulized treatments, lung ultrasound, and strategies to reduce hyperoxemia, remain sparse. This review maps the current literature and clarifies where evidence is relatively more developed and where important gaps remain.
