Unlocking student voice in the first year: a change laboratory approach to curriculum co-design in undergraduate medical education
Authors: Abdulaziz Althewini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFirst-year undergraduate medical students are rarely positioned as active participants in the design of the curricula through which they are learning. This marginalisation of student voice is particularly consequential when students are simultaneously forming their professional identities and adapting to the norms and structures of medical education. The Change Laboratory (CL) is a structured intervention grounded in Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) that creates deliberate conditions for participatory curriculum inquiry. This study investigated how engagement in a CL intervention affected first-year medical students’ and educators’ experiences of the undergraduate learning environment, with particular attention to how the CL functioned as a mechanism for curriculum diagnosis, the structural contradictions it surfaced, and the changes it produced in participants’ relationship to the curriculum.MethodsA qualitative study was carried out within a first-year undergraduate medical programme. A total of fourteen participants — comprising first-year students (n = 7), academic educators (n = 5) and programme administrators (n = 2) — took part in seven structured Change Laboratory sessions focused on identifying and collaboratively addressing curriculum tensions in the first year. Semi-structured interviews were subsequently conducted to elicit participants’ accounts of the CL process and its effects on their relationship to the curriculum. Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsAlthough the themes are constructed from participant accounts, they describe a process of curriculum diagnosis with programme-level consequences: the structural contradictions identified through the CL informed governance discussions and contributed to changes affecting the entire first-year cohort. Three themes were constructed from the data: (1) Reclaiming voice — describing how the CL created conditions in which students’ perspectives were legitimised and their contributions actively sought; (2) The diagnostic power of shared dialogue — capturing how structured conversation enabled participants to identify the underlying sources of curriculum tensions that had previously resisted resolution; and (3) Building durable investment — reflecting how co-design processes generated sustained personal and professional commitment to curriculum quality that extended well beyond the sessions themselves.ConclusionCL participation generated a qualitative shift in how first-year students and educators understood their respective roles in relation to the curriculum. Crucially, the CL functioned not as a student participation event but as a curriculum diagnosis mechanism: it surfaced structural contradictions affecting the entire first-year programme that conventional feedback mechanisms had consistently failed to detect. Introduction

First-year medical students occupy an ambiguous and often disempowering position in relation to the curricula through which they are learning. On one hand, they are the primary occupants and beneficiaries of the learning environment; on the other, they are rarely consulted about its design, and their capacity to contribute meaningfully to it is frequently discounted [1, 2]. This tension is not incidental. It reflects broader patterns in medical education whereby learners are constructed as recipients of expert-designed programmes rather than as knowledgeable agents who bring important and actionable perspectives to curriculum questions [3, 4]. For students in the first year, this positioning can be particularly destabilising: navigating an unfamiliar and cognitively demanding curriculum while being excluded from any legitimate role in shaping it may compound the well-documented difficulties of the first-year transition, including disorientation, motivational fragility and a nascent professional identity that is still finding its footing [5, 6].

Research across health professions education has documented consistently that the design of educational programmes benefits substantially from the active inclusion of learner perspectives [1, 7–9]. Student partnership and co-creation approaches have been associated with higher-quality learning environments, greater curriculum relevance and stronger learner commitment to the educational process [2, 10–12]. Yet translating these principles into practice in undergraduate medical programmes — and especially in the first year — remains a persistent challenge. Institutional hierarchies, time pressures and assumptions about what counts as legitimate educational expertise combine to exclude students from the processes through which curricula are designed, revised and evaluated [13–15]. Addressing this requires not only attitudinal change but concrete, facilitated mechanisms capable of creating genuine conditions for student voice. The challenge is particularly acute in large undergraduate programmes, typically enrolling several hundred students, where conventional feedback mechanisms — surveys, module evaluations, student representative systems — produce descriptive data about what students find problematic but cannot reliably explain why structural problems persist or how they might be resolved. What large programmes need is not more feedback volume but a different quality of curriculum inquiry: one capable of generating structural explanations and actionable solutions rather than symptom catalogues [16–18].

The Change Laboratory (CL) is one such mechanism. Grounded in Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT), the CL creates a structured setting in which participants collectively examine contradictions in a shared activity system and engage in collaborative redesign [19–22]. Unlike consultation exercises that gather student opinion retrospectively, the CL positions all participants — regardless of formal role — as co-investigators of the curriculum and its conditions, with an equal stake in understanding and improving it. Importantly, this means the CL functions primarily as a curriculum diagnosis mechanism rather than a student engagement activity: its purpose is to identify and resolve structural contradictions in the activity system on behalf of the whole programme, and its benefits extend beyond those who participate directly [23, 24]. Participants work with data drawn from their own experience to interrogate assumptions, surface hidden tensions and generate practical solutions, thereby engaging in what CHAT theorists describe as a process of expansive transformation: a collective reconstruction of the purpose and structure of shared activity [25, 26].

Applications of CL in medical education to date have been concentrated in postgraduate and residency training contexts, where it has demonstrated a capacity to improve learning environments, strengthen engagement and build a sense of shared responsibility for educational quality [24, 27, 28]. Broader work in higher education has similarly confirmed that CL-based approaches foster durable transformation at institutional as well as individual levels [29–31]. Whether and how these effects translate to the undergraduate first-year context — where the structural dynamics of student marginalisation, professional identity formation and cognitive adaptation intersect — has not been investigated. The first year is also a developmentally critical period: the stage at which students begin forming their professional identities and initial orientations to medical culture [3, 5]. Addressing student marginalisation at this juncture may therefore carry consequences that extend beyond immediate curriculum quality to the formation of the professional self. This study addresses that gap by asking: in what ways does participation in a Change Laboratory affect first-year medical students’ and educators’ experiences of the undergraduate learning environment, and how does the CL function as a mechanism for curriculum diagnosis and improvement? More specifically, the study attends to the structural curriculum contradictions surfaced through the CL process and to the changes it produced in how participants understood and related to those contradictions. These conditions — the structural exclusion of student voice, the failure of conventional feedback to generate diagnostic insight, and the persistence of curriculum problems that resist standard review — characterise large undergraduate medical programmes internationally. The findings are therefore intended to contribute to the field beyond the single site in which they were generated.

Methods

Design

A qualitative study design was employed, underpinned by reflexive thematic analysis as described by Braun and Clarke [32]. This methodology was selected because of its capacity to generate rich, interpretive accounts of experience and meaning, and its established use in research exploring participants’ engagement with educational interventions.

Setting

The study was situated within the first year of an undergraduate medical degree programme at a national university. The programme is structured around an integrated, systems-based curriculum and is delivered through a combination of lectures, small-group tutorials, laboratory sessions and early clinical skills activities. Prior to the Change Laboratory intervention (see Table 1), the programme faced a number of persistent curriculum tensions that had resisted resolution through existing feedback and review mechanisms. These included: (1) assessment timing difficulties, with recurring misalignments between assessment schedules and the sequencing of taught content; (2) gaps between taught content and learning activities, whereby a disconnect between what was delivered in lectures and what was expected in tutorials and laboratory sessions created confusion about learning priorities; (3) misaligned expectations about feedback, with students and faculty holding divergent understandings of what formative feedback was intended to achieve and how it should be acted upon; (4) structural ambiguity in timetabling, where staff and students operated with fundamentally different understandings of scheduling decisions, a discrepancy that was not recognised as structural until examined jointly; and (5) exclusion of student voice from curriculum governance, as no structured mechanism existed through which first-year students could contribute analytically to curriculum review — existing channels such as module evaluations and student representatives produced descriptive feedback without diagnostic depth.

Participants

A total of fourteen participants were purposively recruited from three stakeholder groups: first-year students (n = 7), academic educators responsible for first-year teaching (n = 5), and programme administrators with responsibility for curriculum coordination (n = 2). Purposive sampling was used to ensure representation from each position within the curriculum activity system. For the student group, recruitment targeted first-year students who had completed at least one full semester and who had previously articulated substantive observations about curriculum organisation or delivery through tutorial discussions or module evaluations. Initial contact was made by the researcher through the programme’s student affairs office, using a written invitation describing the study aims, the nature of CL participation, and the subsequent interview. Students who expressed interest received a participant information sheet and a minimum of seven days to consider participation before providing written consent. Educators and administrators were recruited by direct written invitation from the researcher, sent to all staff with substantive first-year teaching or coordination responsibilities. No participant was recruited through a direct line-management relationship with the researcher. The participant group comprised nine males and five females, with ages ranging from 19 to 47 years. All were fluent in the language of instruction.

This sample size reflects the methodological design of the CL as a system diagnosis intervention: the CL is intentionally structured around small stakeholder groups — typically 8–15 participants — drawn from different positions within the activity system, whose purpose is to identify structural contradictions on behalf of the wider programme [20]. The seven students were recruited as analytically positioned representatives of the wider first-year cohort: their role was to contribute the lived curriculum experience of students to a structured diagnostic inquiry, not to constitute a statistically representative sample of the cohort as a whole. This is consistent with published CL methodology in medical education, including recent work in this journal in which nine participants examined programme-level assessment redesign in a postgraduate context [24].

The five curriculum tensions examined — assessment misalignment, lecture–tutorial disconnect, timetabling ambiguity, feedback expectation mismatch, and structural exclusion of student voice — were features of the first-year programme as a whole, affecting the entire cohort. The CL comprised seven structured sessions of approximately two hours each. Sessions incorporated the use of mirror data drawn from institutional sources including student feedback, assessment data and timetabling records, together with activity system mapping and collaborative redesign exercises, consistent with the CL protocol described by Virkkunen and Newnham [20]. All participants had previously agreed to take part in the CL and consented to subsequent interview.

Data collection

Data were collected via individual semi-structured interviews conducted approximately eight weeks after the conclusion of the final Change Laboratory session, following an initial implementation period during which collaboratively designed curriculum changes were put into effect. This interval was intended to allow participants sufficient time to observe whether proposed changes had been enacted before being invited to reflect on the CL process and its consequences. An interview schedule was developed specifically for this study to elicit accounts of participants’ experiences of the CL process, any changes in their understanding of the curriculum or their role in relation to it, and their perceptions of the significance of those changes for their ongoing engagement with the programme. Interviews were conducted via video-conferencing, lasted between 35 and 60 min, were audio-recorded with participants’ consent and subsequently transcribed. Data collection was carried out iteratively, such that emerging analytical insights from earlier interviews were used to refine the focus of subsequent ones.

Analysis

Interview transcripts were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis as described by Braun and Clarke [32]. Analysis proceeded through the six phases recommended by those authors. In Phase 1 (familiarisation), all transcripts were read and re-read in full, with preliminary notes recorded to capture initial impressions and observations. In Phase 2 (coding), systematic line-by-line coding was applied to the entire dataset, generating an initial set of codes that captured both manifest and latent content. In Phase 3 (generating initial themes), codes were sorted into potential theme groupings, with attention to both internal coherence within each grouping and meaningful differentiation between them. In Phase 4 (reviewing themes), the candidate themes were tested against the full dataset: extracts were re-read in context to assess whether the proposed themes were adequately grounded in the data and whether the boundaries between them were analytically justified. In Phase 5 (defining and naming themes), each theme was refined and given a name that captured its analytical essence. In Phase 6 (producing the report), themes were written up with interpretive commentary and anchoring quotations, with care taken to ensure that interpretive claims remained traceable to the data. Coding and theme development were conducted by the sole author; a reflexive analytic journal was maintained throughout to document key interpretive decisions and to support accountability in the analytical process. The study was conducted by a single researcher without a co-coder; this is acknowledged as a limitation in Sect. 6, and the mitigation strategies described above are offered in that context.

Reflexivity

Reflexivity is a core requirement of reflexive thematic analysis and is particularly important in research where the investigator has a substantive prior relationship to the phenomenon under study [33]. The author is an academic educator with medical education background, and was familiar with the curriculum tensions described in the Setting section, having encountered them through routine academic work prior to designing the CL intervention. The author designed and facilitated the Change Laboratory sessions as part of a curriculum development initiative and subsequently conducted the research reported in this article. This dual role — as both CL facilitator and researcher — is a significant feature of the study’s design and requires transparent acknowledgement. The author entered the study with a belief that student co-construction of the curriculum is both educationally valuable and underutilised in undergraduate medical education, and with a prior commitment to the CL as a mechanism for achieving it. These commitments created a risk of confirmatory interpretation — a tendency to read participant accounts in ways that supported the approach’s value — and this risk was actively managed through the reflexive journal described above, through deliberate attention to disconfirming data, and through careful anchoring of all interpretive claims in the participants’ own words.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the institutional research ethics committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to interview. All participants were provided with full information about the study aims, procedures, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequence. Identifying information has been removed from all quotations presented in this article.

Results

Three themes were constructed from the interview data, each describing a distinct dimension of participants’ experience of the CL intervention and its effects on their relationship to the first-year curriculum. The first theme, reclaiming voice, describes the process by which students came to understand their perspectives on the curriculum as legitimate and worth contributing. The second, the diagnostic power of shared dialogue, addresses the way in which structured group conversation enabled participants to identify the root causes of curriculum problems that had previously seemed intractable. The third, building durable investment, captures the emergence of a committed, personally meaningful relationship to the quality of the learning environment that persisted beyond the formal conclusion of the CL sessions.

Reclaiming voice: students as legitimate contributors to curriculum

Prior to the CL, students consistently described a relationship to the curriculum characterised by passivity and a sense of exclusion from the processes through which it was shaped. The curriculum was experienced as something that arrived fully formed and required adaptation on their part; the idea that they might have something substantive to contribute to its design was not part of how they understood their role as first-year students. The CL challenged this assumption directly, by treating students’ observations and questions not as complaints to be managed but as analytical resources deserving careful attention. This shift in how student knowledge was positioned within the group had a significant effect on students’ confidence and on their willingness to contribute.

“I think I had just accepted that the curriculum was the curriculum. It had been decided by people who knew better than me. The sessions made me realise I actually had something to say that was useful — not just as a student telling people what was wrong, but as someone who understood something about how the learning worked, or did not work. (Student 3)”

“When my observation was taken seriously and used to drive a whole conversation, that was the moment something changed for me. It was not just that I felt heard; it was that what I said had actually mattered. That was new. (Student 1)”

“Students have a perspective on the curriculum that faculty simply cannot have, because they are not living it day to day. Before this, I do not think I had appreciated how much that perspective was being wasted. The sessions surfaced things I genuinely had not known about. (Educator 2)”

This account is significant because it comes from an experienced educator, not a student. The CL did not simply validate student voice in students’ own eyes; it made student perspectives analytically visible to faculty members who had previously — largely unconsciously — discounted them. The process was thus reorienting in both directions: students came to see their knowledge as legitimate, and educators came to recognise what they had been missing.

“I found it quite strange at first, that my views were being sought on something like curriculum design. I had thought that was not my domain. But over the sessions I began to feel that it was — that I had a responsibility to contribute, not just a right to. (Student 4)”

The shift described in this theme was not simply a matter of feeling included. It was a reconceptualisation of what students are — what kind of knowledge they hold and what kind of role they could legitimately play in an educational community. For educators, the process was similarly reorienting: several described becoming aware of a systematic blind spot in their understanding of the curriculum’s effects. The CL created conditions in which this recognition was possible because it structured the inquiry in a way that made student experience analytically visible, rather than anecdotally present.

The diagnostic power of shared dialogue

A second theme concerned the quality and consequences of the dialogue that the CL made possible. Participants across all stakeholder groups described having encountered curriculum problems before the CL — difficulties with assessment timing, gaps between taught content and learning activities, misaligned expectations about feedback. What was new was not the existence of these problems but the capacity to understand them. The structured inquiry of the CL, which included the use of mapped representations of the curriculum as an activity system, enabled participants to trace tensions back to their origins and to develop accounts of why they persisted, rather than merely documenting their effects.

“We had been circling around the same problems for two years without getting anywhere. The process gave us a way to actually dig into why things were not working, not just describe the symptoms. Once you understand the cause, you can do something about it. (Educator 1)”

“There is a difference between knowing something is frustrating and understanding why it is the way it is. The conversations we had helped me understand the structure behind the problem. That made it feel solvable, which it had not before. (Student 2)”

“I had assumed that the problems in the timetable were careless decisions. What changed was realising that there were competing pressures that nobody had ever explicitly named. That was not something I could have understood from a survey or a feedback form. (Student 6)”

“Bringing everyone into the same room and asking them to map out what they understood about the problem was revealing. Faculty and students had completely different accounts of the same situation. Getting those accounts into dialogue with each other produced something neither group could have reached alone. (Programme Administrator 1)”

“I realised that many of the tensions I had attributed to individual decisions or personalities were actually built into the way the curriculum was structured. That reframing was important. It meant the conversation could be about fixing the system rather than blaming people. (Educator 3)”

“From an administrative standpoint, what struck me was how the shared mapping exercise revealed discrepancies in how staff and students understood timetabling decisions. These were not communication failures — they reflected genuine structural ambiguity. The CL gave us a framework for naming that ambiguity rather than working around it. (Admin 1)”

What emerges from this theme is something distinctive about the CL as an analytical method. The structured use of activity system representations, combined with the deliberate inclusion of multiple stakeholder perspectives, created conditions for a depth of curriculum understanding that informal conversation or standard feedback mechanisms cannot reliably produce. This diagnostic capacity had programme-level consequences: the shared understanding of why curriculum contradictions existed provided the conceptual basis for targeted structural changes that surveys of the full cohort could not have generated. A large-scale student survey might confirm that assessment timing was a source of frustration; it could not have produced the structural account of why the misalignment persisted, nor the shared framework among students, educators, and administrators for resolving it. Participants repeatedly described a movement from description to explanation — from naming problems to understanding them — as the most intellectually significant aspect of the process. This diagnostic capacity was understood not merely as a cognitive outcome but as the precondition for the committed, purposeful engagement described in the third theme. The diagnostic work produced by the CL was not purely analytical; it led directly to a set of concrete curriculum changes that were implemented in the programme following the sessions. These included: a revision of the assessment calendar to align more closely with the sequence of taught content; the introduction of structured briefing sessions at the start of each module to make explicit the connections between lectures, tutorials, and laboratory activities; a reformulation of the formative feedback policy to clarify its purposes and the responsibilities of both students and staff in acting on it; and the creation of a standing student–faculty curriculum dialogue group, meeting termly, through which first-year student representatives could contribute structured observations to curriculum review. These changes were not unilateral decisions but products of the collaborative redesign work carried out within the sessions, and their implementation was tracked by participants — a behaviour described in Theme 3 as itself a marker of the new relationship to the curriculum that the CL had generated.

Building durable investment in the learning environment

The third theme addresses a quality of engagement with the curriculum that participants described as meaningfully different from anything they had experienced before the CL. This quality was characterised by its durability and its personal meaningfulness: participants did not simply care about the changes because they had been involved in making them, but because the process of making them had generated a kind of ownership that was felt as a genuine stake in the outcomes. This investment was expressed in concrete behaviours — attending to whether changes were being implemented, raising issues through other channels, and actively contributing to conversations about curriculum quality with peers and colleagues who had not been part of the CL.

“It is hard to explain, but the curriculum feels partly mine now in a way it did not before. Not because I control it — I obviously do not — but because I understand it and I contributed to it. When something goes wrong, I feel the need to do something about it. (Student 1)”

“I have found myself raising things in informal conversations with colleagues that came directly out of the CL process. Not formally, just — this is what we learned, this is how we thought about it. The thinking did not stay inside the sessions. (Educator 2)”

“When you have genuinely contributed to designing something, you invest in whether it works. You notice whether the change is having the effect that was intended. I check. I would not have done that before. (Student 3)”

“I started going back to check whether the changes we had proposed had actually been made. Not because I was told to, but because I wanted to know if what we said had made a difference. That felt like a different relationship to the programme than I had had before. (Student 5)”

“Something shifted in how I think about my responsibility to this programme. I feel as though I have been let in on something — a way of thinking about curriculum as something that can be improved, and that I can play a role in improving. That feels significant. (Student 4)”

What this theme describes is not satisfaction or even enthusiasm, but something more structurally important: the internalisation of curriculum quality as a personal and professional concern. Participants described a change in their identity in relation to the curriculum — from student-as-user to student-as-stakeholder — that was expressed not only in how they felt but in what they did. For educators, a parallel shift was observed: several described a renewed sense of commitment to understanding and responding to student experience as a professional responsibility, not merely a teaching obligation.

Discussion

The findings of this study make a substantive contribution to understanding how methods such as the CL can address this persistent challenge in undergraduate medical education. The three themes — reclaiming voice, the diagnostic power of shared dialogue, and building durable investment — collectively describe a process by which participants came to relate to the curriculum not as a fixed external structure but as a shared practice amenable to critical inquiry and collaborative improvement. While these themes are presented separately for analytical clarity, they are understood as deeply interrelated: each created conditions for the others to develop. This is precisely the kind of reorientation that co-creation researchers have argued is necessary for genuinely participatory educational design to take root. Together, these three dimensions suggest that the CL addresses student marginalisation not through a single mechanism — greater inclusion or stronger engagement — but through a mutually reinforcing process in which legitimation, understanding and investment develop in relation to each other [2, 12, 34]. What is notable here is not simply that students were given a voice, but that the conditions created by the CL enabled them to develop the competence and confidence to use it in ways that were analytically meaningful and practically consequential. The practical consequence extended beyond the participating group: the curriculum knowledge produced through the CL informed programme governance structures and contributed to changes affecting the entire first-year cohort. This system-level consequence is central to understanding the CL’s value for large undergraduate programmes [15, 29, 30].

The diagnostic depth described in Theme 2 warrants particular attention. Existing co-creation literature often focuses on the relational and motivational outcomes of student involvement — the increased sense of belonging, ownership and engagement that participation tends to produce [17, 35, 36]. These outcomes were evident in this study, but participants placed equal emphasis on the cognitive value of the CL process: its capacity to generate shared understanding of why problems existed, not just that they existed. This is consistent with the CHAT notion of expansive learning [23, 25, 32], in which the goal of inquiry is not merely problem-solving but the collective reconceptualisation of the activity system itself. The finding suggests that CL offers something that more conventional student feedback mechanisms — surveys, end-of-module evaluations, student representatives — cannot provide: a structured analytical process capable of producing curriculum knowledge that is actionable rather than merely descriptive. This distinction has direct implications for large undergraduate programmes. The standard response to curriculum problems in large programmes is to gather more descriptive feedback from more students. The argument here is that what large programmes need is not more feedback volume but a different quality of inquiry — one capable of explaining structural causes rather than cataloguing surface effects. The CL provides that quality precisely because it works through a small, purposefully composed group: diagnostic depth, not participatory breadth, is what produces the curriculum knowledge that large programmes need but conventional mechanisms cannot generate [13, 14, 37].

The identity-related dimension of the findings is equally significant. First-year students in medical education are engaged in an active process of professional identity formation, negotiating what it means to become a doctor and what kind of learner they are expected to be [3, 5, 44]. The conventional curriculum positions them as novices who receive and apply the expertise of others; the CL, by contrast, positioned them as knowledgeable agents with a legitimate and necessary perspective on their own learning [2, 4, 38]. This repositioning had visible consequences for students’ engagement, confidence and sense of investment, consistent with Self-Determination Theory’s account of how autonomy, competence and relatedness support intrinsic motivation [39–41]. The implication is that participatory curriculum design in the first year is not only a quality improvement strategy but a pedagogical intervention with direct consequences for professional identity development [11, 16, 42]. This finding resonates with recent work by Androni and colleagues on agentic engagement in pre-clinical medical education, who similarly observe that positioning students as active agents in their learning environment — rather than as passive recipients of curricula designed by others — produces a qualitatively different form of educational investment, one characterised by a sense of personal ownership and accountability that conventional pedagogies do not readily generate [43]. The convergence between their account of agentic engagement and the findings reported here strengthens the case that agentic positioning is a generative educational condition in the pre-clinical years, with consequences extending beyond the immediate learning context.

Implications for practice

This study points to several practical implications for those responsible for designing and delivering first-year medical curricula, including those leading large programmes enrolling several hundred students. First, the CL should be understood not as a student participation exercise but as a curriculum diagnosis mechanism: its value lies in its capacity to generate structural understanding of curriculum problems that large-scale feedback mechanisms cannot produce. A programme director whose institution enrols four hundred first-year students does not need four hundred CL participants; they need a well-facilitated CL with a representative stakeholder group — students, educators, and administrators — whose diagnostic outputs are integrated into curriculum governance. The evidence presented here suggests that such a group can surface and resolve structural contradictions affecting the entire cohort, while simultaneously supporting the professional development of those who participate [4, 5, 44]. Second, facilitators of CL in undergraduate contexts should attend explicitly to the dynamics of student legitimacy: the conditions under which students come to experience their perspectives as worth contributing are not automatic and require deliberate facilitation [42, 18], with attention to the creation of a psychologically safe environment in which student voice is structurally enabled. Third, institutions seeking to embed this approach at scale might integrate CL cycles within existing governance infrastructure: rotating stakeholder groups — drawn from students, faculty, and administrators across successive cohorts — could engage in CL processes at regular intervals, generating successive rounds of curriculum diagnosis and redesign. In this model, the CL becomes a standing mechanism for curriculum improvement rather than a one-off intervention, and its analytical contribution accumulates across programme cycles [15, 21, 29].

Limitations and future studies

The study’s scope is limited by its single-site design and purposive sample of fourteen participants. The sample size reflects the methodological requirements of the CL as a system diagnosis intervention — a small-group structured inquiry designed to produce diagnostic depth rather than statistical breadth — and is consistent with comparable CL studies in medical education, including recent work published in this journal with fewer participants [20, 24, 27, 28]. The findings should be interpreted as theoretically transferable rather than statistically generalisable: the structural conditions examined here — the marginalisation of student voice, the failure of conventional feedback to produce diagnostic insight, and the persistence of curriculum contradictions in large undergraduate programmes — are near-universal features of undergraduate medical education, and give these findings relevance well beyond the single site in which they were produced. Participants were self-selected through their prior agreement to engage in the CL, and may therefore differ from students and educators who declined participation or were not approached. The data capture a particular moment in participants’ trajectories — shortly after the CL concluded — and cannot speak to longer-term outcomes. Future research should examine the CL’s applicability across diverse undergraduate medical education settings, with attention to how institutional culture, programme structure and student demographic characteristics shape the conditions for productive co-design. The durability of the changes described by participants — particularly the shift in personal investment captured in Theme 3 — was assessed only at a single point following the CL; whether these orientations persist through subsequent years of study and into clinical practice remains an important open question. Despite the mitigation strategies described in the Methods, the possibility that participants’ accounts were shaped by a desire to respond positively to an intervention the researcher had designed and delivered cannot be fully discounted [9, 45]. Investigation of the longitudinal effects of CL participation on student engagement, academic wellbeing and professional identity development would further strengthen the evidence base for this approach [3, 5].

Conclusion

This study has demonstrated that the Change Laboratory can function as a curriculum diagnosis mechanism in a first-year undergraduate medical programme — surfacing structural contradictions affecting the entire cohort and generating the quality of actionable curriculum knowledge that conventional feedback mechanisms, however large in scale, are unable to produce. The changes it produced in how participants related to the curriculum and to one another were not incidental outcomes but consequences of the diagnostic process itself, and their significance extends beyond the participants to the programme as a whole. Specifically, it has shown that the CL can create conditions in which student voice is reclaimed as analytically valuable, curriculum problems are understood diagnostically rather than symptomatically, and a durable sense of personal investment in curriculum quality is developed by participants across different stakeholder roles.

In so doing, these findings make a distinct contribution to the literature on student partnership and co-creation in health professions education by locating the CL’s value not only in its relational outcomes — the sense of inclusion, ownership and engagement that participation produces — but in its epistemological ones: its capacity to generate a quality of curriculum knowledge that other forms of student involvement cannot easily replicate [9, 17, 35, 36]. This is an important complement to existing accounts of co-creation, which have tended to foreground motivational and identity outcomes [12, 15].

For educators and curriculum designers in undergraduate medical education, this study highlights the value of deliberately creating facilitated spaces in which students are positioned as genuine co-investigators of the curriculum rather than passive sources of feedback. The first year represents both a point of significant need — given the well-documented academic and professional vulnerabilities students experience at this stage [6] — and an important opportunity. Early experiences in medical school play a formative role in shaping professional identity [3, 5, 4, 44], including how students understand their role within educational and professional systems. Supporting students to see themselves as participants in shaping the curriculum may therefore represent an important contribution that first-year programmes can make to longer-term professional formation.
