Preimplantation genetic testing for polygenic diseases: A novel paradigm in embryo selection
Authors: Tianrui Jing, Yanzhi Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polygenic diseases are caused by a number of independently acting or interacting polymorphic genomic variants in conjunction with environmental factors, and show an increasing global prevalence, posing a significant threat to individual and public health. Given the limited curative prospects for polygenic diseases owing to their complex etiology, preventive strategies have gained paramount importance. Preimplantation genetic testing for polygenic disorders (PGT-P) is an advanced in vitro fertilization technique that screens embryos for genetic susceptibility to polygenic diseases, aiming to reduce the risk of offspring developing conditions such as diabetes, schizophrenia, polycystic ovary syndrome, and certain cancers. This technology calculates polygenic disease risks across sibling embryos relying on the use of a polygenic risk score (PRS) that counts the effects of risk alleles derived from genome-wide association studies. Using PGT-P, each embryo's PRS is computed for any disease or trait of interest, and the euploid embryo with the lowest PRS is prioritized for implantation. As a result, PGT-P may decrease the disease risk for future offspring and provide a novel strategy for embryo ranking, selection, or even discarding. In this review, we systematically summarize the current knowledge regarding PGT-P, detailing its workflow, potential benefits, predictive power, expected risk reduction and significant limitations. We further evaluate its utility and practical implementation in clinical practice and outline critical directions for future research.

Introduction

According to the World Health Statistics 2024 by the World Health Organization, non-communicable diseases (NCDs) remain largely responsible for death globally [1]. Most NCDs, including cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease, cancer, and diabetes, are predominantly influenced by polygenic factors. Polygenic diseases are a kind of genetic disorder in which an individual's risk is determined by the combined influence of multiple risk loci and environmental exposures [2]. Many of these diseases exhibit familial aggregation, with recurrence risks in affected families typically ranging from 1% to 10% [3,4]. Consequently, the prediction of risk is crucial for preventing and guiding the clinical management of polygenic diseases.

For three decades, preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) has been integral to infertility treatment via in vitro fertilization (IVF), effectively preventing the transmission of chromosomal abnormalities and monogenic disorders to offspring [5,6]. In standard PGT, the trophectoderm (TE) biopsy is obtained from day-5 or day-6 embryos to analyze their genetic composition, after which unaffected embryos are selectively transferred [7]. PGT for aneuploidy (PGT-A) screens embryo euploidy, aneuploidy, and mosaicism before transferring embryos to eliminate aneuploid embryos and improve clinical outcomes [8]. PGT for structural rearrangement (PGT-SR) is widely performed for couples carrying balanced translocations and/or inversions to reduce the risk of miscarriage and prevent diseases associated with an unbalanced karyotype [9]. PGT for monogenic disorders (PGT-M) identifies embryos carrying specific disease-causing variants, enabling carriers of high-penetrance pathogenic mutations to select an embryo free of without those defects [6]. However, these three technologies are unable to predict the polygenic disease risk of embryos.

Large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have uncovered many genetic loci associated with polygenic diseases, paving the way for polygenic risk scores (PRS) that predict individual's genetic predisposition by aggregating the weighted effects of these risk loci [10,11]. Consequently, an embryo's PRS for a polygenic disease or trait can be computed once its genome-wide data are obtained. Current approach for reconstruction of an embryo's whole-genome data is primarily based on haplotype phasing techniques coupled with Hidden Markov Model-based inference [[12], [13], [14]]. The core principle of this technology lies in comparing the sparse genotyping data obtained from embryo biopsy with the pre-phased haplotype maps of both parents. By utilizing statistical modeling algorithms based on linkage disequilibrium (LD) and shared chromosomal segments, the system can accurately impute genotypes across millions of loci not directly sequenced, thereby reconstructing the embryo's complete genomic sequence. In the context of PGT-P, this reconstructed genome is used to compute PRSs that estimate an embryo's susceptibility to various polygenic diseases, such as diabetes, certain cancers, schizophrenia, and coronary artery disease [15,16]. Embryos are subsequently ranked based on their PRS, followed by the prioritization and selection of a single embryo for transfer, aiming to minimize the predicted polygenic disease risk. The first report of PGT-P was published in 2019, presenting a mathematical model for embryo screening to reduce the risk of type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), with results showing a 45–72% risk reduction compared with that of blinded embryo selection [17]. Subsequently, a larger-scale study evaluated the approach using 11,883 sibling pairs to simulate embryo selection across multiple conditions. This analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in the relative risk for all polygenic diseases tested concurrently, including various cancers, diabetes, and heart disease [18]. In a more recent clinical application, researchers developed a PRS model incorporating pedigree information to select an embryo with the lowest genetic risk for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). The resulting child was born with the predicted low-risk genotype and showed normal development [19]. Despite these promising proofs of concept, research on PGT-P remains in its early stages, with its clinical utility, standardized guidelines, and long-term outcomes yet to be fully established.

In this review, we synthesize the latest advances in knowledge surrounding PGT-P, encompassing its key technical processes, estimated clinical benefits, and existing limitations. We further provide an in-depth discussion on the clinical utility of PGT-P and outline future research directions, with the aim of evaluating its efficacy in mitigating polygenic disease risk and facilitating its transition from a novel concept to integrated clinical practice.

Brief introduction to PGT-P

Evolution of PGT-P

The rapid growth in research on PGT and genomics has progressively enabled the clinical translation of PGT-P (Fig. 1). The development of PGT-P represents the convergence of advances in genomic technologies, computational biology, and reproductive medicine. Early foundations were laid by the clinical establishment of PGT-A and PGT-M, which standardized the critical procedures of embryo biopsy and single-cell genomic analysis [20,21]. The advent of GWAS enabled the identification of polygenic risk loci, catalyzing the conceptual shift from single-gene to polygenic risk prediction [22,23]. By the late 2010s, the establishment of large-scale biobank datasets (e.g., UK Biobank) and the maturation of machine learning algorithms refined the construction and application of PRS. These developments, coupled with large-scale biobank data, enabled more powerful probabilistic modeling of individual disease susceptibility based on the cumulative effects of risk alleles [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Key innovations included the adaptation of single-cell sequencing protocols for embryos, and advanced haplotype phasing techniques [[28], [29], [30]], which rendered it technically feasible to accurately infer a complete inherited genomic sequence of an embryo from a biopsy, thereby enabling the reliable calculation of embryo-specific PRS for multiple diseases. These cumulative technological advances culminated in the emergence of PGT-P. The first proof-of-concept report, published in 2019, mathematically modeled the use of embryo screening to reduce the risk of T1DM and breast cancer, marking the field's inception [17]. Overview of PGT-P workflow

PGT-P, also termed polygenic embryo screening (PES), is a type of genetic testing designed to assess the genetic risk for polygenic diseases within the framework of assisted reproductive technology (ART). Unlike conventional PGT, PGT-P targets diseases caused by the cumulative effect of numerous small-effect genetic variants [15]. The technology is underpinned by the PRS method, which aggregates the weighted effects of thousands of disease-associated genetic variants into a single quantitative metric. The procedure involves a TE biopsy of each blastocyst-stage embryo, followed by genomic analysis to calculate disease-specific PRSs. Embryos are then ranked based on these scores, and the euploid embryo with the lowest PRS is prioritized for transfer (Fig. 2). As the figure describes, the procedure of PGT-P can be briefly summarized in four key stages: (1) Consultation and evaluation: Prospective parents undergo comprehensive counseling with a multidisciplinary team, typically including a reproductive endocrinologist and a clinical geneticist. This critical step involves evaluating the familial and medical indications for PGT-P, discussing its evidence-based potential benefits (e.g., relative and absolute risk reduction), and thoroughly reviewing its technical limitations, and ethical considerations. A key component is establishing a family-specific risk baseline to contextualize potential outcomes, moving beyond general population-averaged risks. (2) PRS model construction: A disease-specific PRS model is constructed by assigning statistical weights to risk-associated single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). These weights are derived from the effect sizes published in large-scale GWAS summary statistics. The model must then undergo rigorous calibration and independent validation in an external cohort to verify its predictive accuracy and the correlation between the PRS and the actual phenotype or disease risk in the target population. (3) Genetic risk prediction of embryos: Following standard IVF, blastocyst-stage embryos undergo TE biopsy (5–10 cells). The limited DNA is whole-genome amplified and genotyped. Haplotype phasing then compares sparse embryonic data with parental haplotypes to accurately impute a complete genome-wide profile for each embryo. This reconstructed genome enables the calculation of PRS for target diseases. After parallel PGT-A, euploid embryos are ranked by PRS, and the one with the lowest risk profile is prioritized for transfer. (4) Prenatal diagnosis: To audit the technical accuracy of the PGT-P process, prenatal diagnostic testing (e.g., amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling) is recommended during pregnancy. Genotypes from amniotic fluid or placental cells serve as a high-confidence postnatal biological reference. The concordance between these fetal genotypes and the genotypes originally predicted from the embryo biopsy is analyzed, providing definitive verification of the genetic predictions made during the PGT-P cycle.Fig. 2General process of preimplantation genetic testing for polygenic disease (PGT-P). Indications of PGT-P

In principle, PGT-P is theoretically applicable to any disease or trait with polygenic influences, and may be considered in several clinical scenarios (Table 1). Most complex diseases or traits influenced by hundreds or thousands of common variants across the genome, each conferring a small individual effect on phenotype [31,32]. Polygenic diseases include, but are not limited to, metabolic diseases (e.g. diabetes), cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases (e.g. hypertension), neurological disorders (e.g. Alzheimer's disease), and many common cancers (e.g. breast cancer). Similarly, quantitative polygenic traits such as height, intelligence, and blood pressure are also influenced by aggregated genetic effects. In clinical practice, the use of PGT-M is widely accepted when targeting pathogenic variants that unequivocally cause serious, penetrant diseases (e.g., cystic fibrosis). The application of PGT for non-lethal diseases (e.g., achondroplasia, hereditary deafness) also provided by some clinics, albeit within more nuanced ethical frameworks [33,34]. The first reported live birth following PGT-P occurred in 2021. In this case, the parents utilized the technology to select one embryo from a cohort of 33 based on a multi-disease polygenic risk profile. The selected embryo, predicted to have the lowest genetic risk for several adult-onset conditions including diabetes, breast cancer, schizophrenia, and cardiovascular disease, resulted in the birth of a child with a theoretically reduced lifetime risk for these disorders [36]. Notably, this case lacked specific, high-risk polygenic disease as the primary indication, a factor that subsequently ignited sustained ethical and policy debate regarding the appropriate scope of polygenic embryo screening. A demonstrative case of PGT-P in China concerned a couple with male-factor infertility and a multi-generational history of early-onset T2DM. With ethical approval, a family-specific PRS model was employed to stratify embryos by T2DM risk in conjunction with ploidy screening. The transfer of the euploid embryo with the lowest polygenic risk led to a successful pregnancy [19]. This case illustrates that PGT-P may be considered acceptable for prospective parents with an increased familial risk of transmitting a heightened genetic predisposition to polygenic disorders. Critically, the decision to pursue PGT-P must be preceded by a structured and comprehensive pre-procedural counseling with a multidisciplinary team, including reproductive endocrinologists, clinical geneticists, and genetic counselors. The goals are to ensure that prospective parents fully understand the probabilistic nature of the benefits, the current technical limitations, and the necessary weighing of financial, ethical, and psychosocial implications.

Predictive accuracy of PGT-P

Evaluating the predictive accuracy of PGT-P represents a multifaceted challenge, encompassing both technical complexity and ethical sensitivities. This stems from its unique premise: PGT-P does not provide a definitive diagnosis but instead offers a probabilistic assessment of future disease risk, which may remain latent until adulthood. The accuracy of PGT-P predictions is influenced by multiple factors, including the comprehensiveness of genomic data, the precision of embryo genome reconstruction, trait heritability, and polygenic disease complexity.

First, the predictive power of PGT-P is directly dependent on the quality of the PRS model derived from GWAS [37]. Models that incorporate a more comprehensive genetic loci and more precise effect size estimates exhibit stronger predictive performance. For instance, certain PRS models for T1DM demonstrate high discriminative accuracy [38], whereas models for other diseases may perform less robustly of conditions with less maturity in GWAS research. Furthermore, the predictive power of a PRS is limited for diseases involving intricate gene-gene and gene-environment interactions [39]. A fundamental constraint of the PRS cannot anticipate future, dynamic environmental exposures (e.g., diet, lifestyle, stress), thereby limiting the comprehensiveness of its predictions. In addition, the accurate reconstruction of an embryo's complete genome via haplotype phasing is an absolute prerequisite for reliable PRS calculation. A recent meta-analysis suggested the TE biopsy not fully reflect the genetic status of embryos [40], potentially leading to genotype misclassification and consequently a PRS that deviates substantially from the true genetic risk. Collectively, these factors mean that PGT-P does not guarantee absolute certainty but instead provides a probabilistic relative risk assessment [41]. Current evaluations of PGT-P accuracy primarily focus on verifying the technical precision of genotype inference. This validation is typically achieved by assessing the concordance between the genotype predicted from the embryo biopsy and the fetal genotype later confirmed through prenatal diagnostic procedures, such as amniocentesis. A study compared the genotypes derived from TE biopsy with those from the corresponding born child, reporting a genome-wide prediction accuracy as high as 98% [13]. In addition, a recent investigation revealed that the total genotype concordance between the embryo, the embryo and the fetus, and between the embryo and the born child, was 96.9% and 97.5%, respectively [19]. Given this high degree of genotype concordance, it is now reasonable to equate the performance of the PRS in transferred embryos to its performance in the fetus or newborn, indicating that PGT-P achieves a relatively high level of predictive accuracy.

Estimating risk reduction in PGT-P

Current estimates of risk reduction in PGT-P are based on the scenario of a single IVF cycle, where embryos are screened using PRS for one or more diseases. The embryo selected for transfer is the one with the lowest PRS profile. PGT-P have been consistently reported the potential for substantial relative risk reduction (RRR) of polygenic diseases [[16], [17], [18],42]. For instance, Treff et al. estimated that screening for T1DM in a high-risk family could achieve a 72% RRR by selecting the sibling with the lowest PRS among five [17]. In a larger analysis of 11,883 sibling pairs from the UK Biobank, the same group calculated that selecting the sibling with the lowest PRS yielded relative risk reduction ranging from 2.5% for malignant melanoma to 46.9% for heart attack across 11 diseases [18]. However, these findings contrast with PGT-P models for quantitative traits such as height or intelligence, where potential gains are projected to be minimal and highly uncertain [42,43].

For clinical interpretation, absolute risk reduction (ARR) is often considered more informative than RRR, as a large RRR can be misleading when the actual change in absolute risk is small. Turley et al., using a simulation model with an optimistic assumption of 10 viable embryos per couple (to model an upper bound of benefit), calculated potential RRR of 7-77% and ARR of only 0.07-8.5% for 11 conditions, with variation based on disease and ancestry [42]. This highlights that for rarer diseases, ARR is generally small, leading to a high number needed to screen. Similarly, Lencz et al. estimated that selecting from among five embryos in PGT-P could yield a RRR of approximately 50% for both schizophrenia and Crohn's disease. However, given the low baseline prevalence of these conditions, the corresponding absolute risk change is very minimal. Conversely, for a high-prevalence disease like T2DM, the achievable RRR is typically lower, but the ARR is substantially greater. Therefore, comprehensive clinical decision-making regarding PGT-P should incorporate a balanced consideration of both RRR and ARR metrics. Furthermore, patients at an elevated baseline risk with affected first-degree relatives or a higher parental PRS, are predicted to achieve a greater ARR from PGT-P compared to those without such risk factors [16,18]. To illustrate, for a disease with a general population prevalence of 1%, a 50% RRR translates to an ARR of only 0.5%. In contrast, for a high-risk family with a baseline risk of 10%, the same 50% RRR yields a substantially more meaningful ARR of 5%. This underscores the necessity for high-risk families to establish a family-specific risk baseline and to calculate the ARR within that personalized context.

Nevertheless, for families already undergoing preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy PGT-A, the integration of PGT-P may provide a novel decision-making axis for embryo selection. By evaluating susceptibility to common polygenic diseases, PGT-P augments the conventional criteria of ploidy and morphology, thereby refining the selection framework for embryo transfer.

Necessary prerequisites for PGT-P implementation

PGT-P is a specialized form of preimplantation genetic testing designed to assess the genetic risk of polygenic diseases in embryos. The procedure combines molecular biology and statistical genetics, including a TE biopsy, genome-wide PRS calculation, and the selective transfer of a euploid embryo exhibiting the lowest PRS for the diseases of concern. The clinical application of PGT-P depends on several critical technical prerequisites: the identification of susceptibility loci through GWAS, the development of validated and effective PRS prediction models, and the accurate reconstruction of each embryo's inherited genome sequence via advanced haplotype phasing.

Identification of susceptibility loci

High-quality lists of susceptibility loci and their effect sizes derived from GWAS and constitute the foundational dataset for computing embryo PRS [44,45]. Rigorous quality control (QC) steps (e.g., removing low-quality variants and samples), are critical to minimize spurious associations and ensure the accuracy of SNP effect size estimates [46]. This QC is particularly vital for PGT-P, as the resulting PRS models must maintain high predictive accuracy when applied to the sparse genotyping data imputed from embryo biopsies. The predictive power of the resulting PRS model is directly determined by the sample size, ancestral diversity, and analytical rigor of the underlying GWAS.

The NHGRI-EBI GWAS Catalog, a landmark repository established in 2008, has curated data from thousands of studies, documenting over 40,000 unique SNP-trait associations across a vast spectrum of human phenotypes [47]. Foundational genomic maps from the International HapMap Project and 1000 Genomes Project, together with the high-throughput genotyping technologies, have catalyzed the rapid expansion of large-cohort GWAS [48]. These studies have successfully identified numerous associations between genetic variants and complex traits, precisely estimating the effect size and statistical significance of SNPs [2,49]. The genetic variants implicated by GWAS encompass a broad spectrum of systemic diseases, including various cancers, metabolic disorders, and neurological conditions [50]. A GWAS of 8226 Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) cases and 7578 controls in a Chinese population identified ten susceptibility loci. Functional enrichment analysis indicated these loci are involved in pathways related to sex hormone function, T2DM, and insulin signaling, implicating them in PCOS pathogenesis [51]. Hsu et al. conducted the largest GWAS of breast cancer in a Taiwanese population (2496 cases and 9984 controls). They identified 113 associated SNPs, 50 of which were novel and showed oncogenic or tumor-suppressive potential, while others were located near previously known breast cancer genes [52]. As a major polygenic neurodegenerative disorder accounting for 60-80% of dementia cases, late-onset Alzheimer's disease (AD) has seen at least 95 risk loci through large-scale GWAS. Pathway analyses reveal these variants converge on biological processes involving microglia, immune response, and lipid metabolism [53]. Cardiovascular GWAS exemplify the power of large-scale discovery. A 2020 meta-analysis of over 1 million individuals identified approximately 300 risk loci for coronary artery disease (CAD). The PRS polygenic risk score (PRS) integrating these signals shows a strong risk gradient in populations, thereby providing a direct pathway from genetic association to stratified risk prediction [54]. Furthermore, GWAS also elucidate the genetic architecture of specific conditions, such as a common acute leukemia, where a meta-analysis identified a genome-wide significant risk locus offering new etiological insights [55].

Effective prediction models of polygenic disease risk

PRS are a fundamental component for accurate polygenic disease risk prediction and hold significant potential for refining population screening strategies. A PRS aggregates the effects of thousands of susceptibility variants identified through GWAS data across the entire genome into a single, quantifiable value [56]. In its most common additive form (PRSj=Σi=1nGijβi), where Gij represents the genotype of individual j at SNP i, and βi is the effect size for that SNP derived from GWAS summary statistics. The score represents an individual's cumulative genetic burden for a specific trait.

PRS calculation requires two independent datasets (1) a base dataset of summary statistics (e.g., beta coefficients, P values) from GWAS, and (2) a target dataset containing genotypes and phenotypes of the sample for prediction. Both require stringent quality control, including sufficient heritability in the base data and an adequate target sample size to ensure robustness. A critical step is the adjustment of SNP effect sizes. Common strategies include applying statistical shrinkage methods (e.g., LASSO, ridge regression, Bayesian approaches) to all SNPs, or selecting only SNPs with GWAS P-values below a predefined threshold. Furthermore, advanced PRS methods (e.g., LDpred, SBayesR) explicitly model LD structure to account for correlations among variants, thereby refining SNP weights and improving prediction accuracy and portability. The predictive performance of a PRS is typically evaluated by the variance explained (R2) or discriminative metrics such as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), the area under the precision-recall curve (AUC-PR), or Harrell's C-index. Standardized software (e.g., PRSice, PLINK, LDpred) enables implementation, with tool selection guided by data structure and analysis goals [37].

Clinical management of T2DM typically relies on traditional risk factors (e.g., diet, obesity, elevated blood glucose) to lower blood glucose levels and reduce the disease risk [57]. However, PRS derived from large-scale GWAS have greatly enhanced the accuracy of risk stratification. A high T2DM PRS is positively associated with disease severity and accelerates disease progression from normoglycemia to prediabetes and to overt T2DM [58]. Another study found that a high PRS with eight SNPs was associated with a 10-fold increased risk of T2DM compared to those with a low PRS. Mechanistically, these SNPs often associated with pathways that are critical to pancreatic β-cell function and exhibit interactions with lifestyle factors [59], thereby providing valuable insights to guide the development of personalized preventive strategies. Although the APOE is the most significant genetic risk factor in AD, it explains only about 10% of the heritable risk [60]. An AD-associated PRS incorporating 131 non-APOE risk loci demonstrates significant independent predictive power. When combined with APOE status, such a model achieved an AUC of 0.755. Importantly, among patients with mild cognitive impairment, a high PRS conferred a hazard ratio of 2.22 for progression to AD, even in APOE ε4 non-carriers [61]. For T1DM, an elevated T1DM PRS is associated with increased disease risk. Integrating the PRS with high-risk HLA haplotypes (e.g., DQA1∗03:02–DQA1∗05:01) further improves the accuracy of T1DM risk prediction [62]. In cardiovascular disease, integrating of a CAD-specific PRS into established clinical risk assessment tools, such as the Framingham Risk Score or ACC/AHA Pooled Cohort Equations, has been shown to improve the accuracy of 10-year cardiovascular risk prediction [63]. PRS can refine risk assessment even in the context of monogenic high-penetrance variants. For example, in a couple carrying a pathogenic BRCA1 variant, the odds ratio for breast cancer risk among embryos varied 4.5-fold based on the mutation alone. However, when combined with a breast cancer PRS, this difference expanded to 15-fold across embryos, highlighting the added discriminatory value of polygenic risk information within a high-risk family [13]. From a technical perspective, these examples underscore that PRS can quantify an individual's or an embryo's genetic liability for a polygenic disease. Accurate reconstruction of entire genome sequence of embryo

PRS analysis within the framework of PGT-P integrates the contributions of hundreds to thousands of genetic variants across the embryonic genome into a single quantitative predictor. This approach fundamentally requires acquiring the embryo's complete genome sequence. However, the limited quantity and quality of DNA obtained from TE biopsies necessitate a preliminary whole-genome amplification (WGA) step, typically using methods such as multiple displacement amplification (MDA) [64]. A major technical challenge arises from the need to amplify these minute DNA quantities, a process inherently prone to stochastic biases. These amplification artifacts can compromise the accurate determination of inherited alleles,and may also introduce artifactual mutations [65,66]. To overcome these limitations and enable reliable genome-wide prediction, current methods for reconstructing embryonic genomes rely on haplotype-based imputation [[12], [13], [14],67]. This approach uses phased parental genomic information as a scaffold to infer the embryo's complete genome sequence from sparse genotyping data, thereby mitigating the errors introduced by direct amplification and sequencing of biopsy samples.

Haplotype phasing techniques for embryo genome reconstruction are typically based on SNP microarrays and next-generation sequencing (NGS) platforms. Their core principle involves constructing a complete parental haplotype scaffold through deep whole-genome sequencing (WGS), then using sparse embryo genotyping data to pinpoint meiotic recombination sites against this scaffold, and finally employing statistical imputation models to infer the embryo's complete genome. [13,[68], [69], [70]]. For example, the team led by Akash Kumar developed a method that combines rapid embryo genotyping with molecular haplotype phasing of parental genomes to predict the complete genome sequence of embryos. Using this method, they achieved over 99% accuracy in predicting the transmission of approximately 3 million heterozygous parental sites [12]. The team further extended this methodology to establish a workflow for whole-genome reconstruction (WGR) of embryos, integrating molecular and statistical techniques to predict both rare and common inherited variants [13]. The specific steps include: partitioning parental genetic variants into chromosome-specific haplotypes, identifying meiotic recombination sites for each embryo, and assembling the haplotype segments between these sites to infer the full inherited genome of each embryo. Applying this WGR technique to 110 embryos from 10 couples, the researchers computed PRS for 12 polygenic diseases, including cardiometabolic disorders, cancers, and autoimmune diseases. Compared to the genomes of born children, the technique achieved a whole-genome prediction accuracy of 99.0% to 99.4% for day-5 embryo biopsy samples [13]. Another significant advancement comes from the work of Zhang et al., who utilized optimized linked-read sequencing technology to develop a precise, efficient, and lower-cost haplotype analysis technique termed proband-independent haplotype phasing. Applying this technique to perform complete haplotype analysis on 99 embryos, a 100% concordance rate with diagnoses obtained by conventional methods [71].

Long-read sequencing now routinely achieves read lengths exceeding 10 kb, with accuracies rivaling those of established NGS platforms [72]. This capability directly addresses a fundamental limitation of previous methods. Because individual long reads can physically link multiple heterozygous single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) on a single DNA molecule, they enable the determination of contiguous haplotype blocks across extensive genomic regions. As a result, long-read WGS has become a powerful method for obtaining high-resolution, phased parental genomes. Recent advances in the field have further systematically evaluated the effectiveness of long-read whole-genome sequencing for phasing at the single-cell level and its potential for universal PGT applications. For instance, Wu et al. utilized single molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing to perform haplotype phasing of parental genomes and TE biopsy samples, enabling haplotype linkage analysis for the HBB gene [73]. Similarly, Zhang et al. employed PacBio long-read sequencing data at approximately 30 × coverage to accomplish parental haplotype phasing and implemented reference genome-free PGT-M for three monogenic diseases [14]. In this direction, Zhao et al. performed long-read whole-genome sequencing at a depth of approximately 24 × on human single cells, enabling simultaneous analysis of SNVs, insertions and deletions, structural variations, and haplotype phasing. This method was validated in two PGT families, achieving 100% diagnostic concordance with conventional SNP array-based PGT results [67]. A major advantage of long-read sequencing data lies in its ability to perform de novo phasing of both parental genomes and embryo biopsy samples without relying on genetic information from other relatives, thereby enabling the simultaneous detection of potential de novo mutations in prospective parents [67,74]. This integrative capability paves a new path for performing PGT-P and PGT-M within a unified genomic workflow.

Clinical potential of PGT-P utility

The primary objective of PGT-P is to reduce polygenic disease risk in future offspring. This section reviews the literature concerning four common polygenic diseases for which PGT-P shows predictive potential, including T2DM, T1DM, breast cancer, and PCOS. For each disease, we summarize the established risk factors, examine the utility and performance of associated PRS, and evaluate recent progress in translating this genetic knowledge into a PGT-P. A central theme across these conditions is the prospective ability of PGT-P to stratify embryos by their predicted genetic risk, thereby informing embryo prioritization for transfer and laying a foundation for future, health screening and preventive strategies.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus

T2DM is a common polygenic disease caused by complex inheritance-environment factors. It represents a major and growing global public health challenge, with estimating that the number of affected individuals will reach 439 million by 2030 [75]. The disease is clinically characterized by elevated fasting glucose, an increased 2-h glucose level on an oral glucose tolerance test, or elevated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels [76]. T2DM is associated with severe short- and long-term complications, including macrovascular diseases, microvascular diseases, and an increased risk of certain cancers, contributing to high rates of global morbidity and mortality [[77], [78], [79]]. Current therapeutic strategies primarily aim to alleviate symptoms and manage complications through pharmacotherapy to lower blood glucose, calorie restriction, and physical exercise to reduce weight [80,81]. However, some medications, notably insulin, can lead to weight gain. Although surgery is the most effective treatment for both severe obesity and T2DM, it carries inherent surgical risks and potential long-term nutritional complications, highlighting it as an imperfect solution [82].

Genetic factors play a substantial role in T2DM etiology, as evidenced by its high heritability, distinct familial clustering, and difference in incidence rates between dizygotic and monozygotic twins [83]. This genetic architecture enables the construction of PRS. For instance, a PRS comprising 1000 SNPs demonstrated a strong association with T2DM risk in a population-based cohort, with individuals in the highest PRS quintile having a 3.45-fold higher incidence compared to those in the lowest quintile. Integrating this PRS into a 5-year T2DM risk prediction model yielded a continuous net reclassification improvement of 0.324, significantly enhancing risk stratification [84]. A modeling study of 11,883 sibling pairs further suggested that selecting embryos based on a genomic index could achieve a relative risk reduction of 42.3% for T2DM compared to random selection [18]. The potential of PGT-P to mitigate T2DM risk has progressed from modeling to clinical application. A study from China in 2022 documented the first birth of an infant following PGT-P specifically to reduce T2DM risk [19]. The prospective father had a strong multi-generational family history of early-onset T2DM requiring long-term medication. To enable a precise, family-informed assessment, the researchers developed a family-specific PRS model incorporating 114 T2DM-associated SNPs. From seven biopsied embryos screened for aneuploidy, the euploid embryo with the lowest predicted T2DM polygenic risk was selected for transfer. Follow-up evaluation at 16 months confirmed that the child carried the anticipated lowest-risk genetic profile of T2DM and exhibited normal development. This case provides a proof-of-concept that PGT-P, guided by a family-specific PRS, can be successfully implemented to select embryos with a reduced genetic burden for T2DM. Collectively, current evidence indicates that PGT-P may offer a novel, preventive strategy to reduce the transmission of elevated polygenic risk for T2DM.

Type 1 diabetes mellitus

T1DM is an autoimmune disorder that affect millions globally and is characterized by the immune-mediated destruction of insulin-producing pancreatic β-cells. This process involves the infiltration of islets by CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and macrophages. The pathogenesis of T1DM arises from complex interactions between multiple genetic variants and environmental factors, which vary across individual patients [85,86]. Genetic risk factors are determined by specific alleles, that mainly influence T-cell recognition and tolerance to autologous and foreign molecules, as well as variants that alter β-cell vulnerability to inflammatory mediators [86]. Notably, the incidence of T1DM has been rising in recent decades, particularly among young children. Despite advances in management with exogenous insulin, achieving glycemic goals remains challenging for most patients, and T1DM-associated complications continue to pose significant health problems [87]. Current strategies to prevent or delay T1DM in at-risk individuals have shown limited success, highlighting the need for novel preventive paradigms for disease prevention.

A T1DM PRS integrates complex genetic information and demonstrated utility in classification, prognosis, and prediction of T1DM [88]. This capability forms the technical foundation for applying PRS within a PGT-P framework. Treff et al. established a mathematical model to screen T1DM risk for embryos, and led to a 45–72% reduction in the incidence of T1DM compared to random selection [17]. A pivotal case involved a couple with a strong family history of T1DM, including their 3-year-old son and multiple extended family members. The prospective parents opted for PGT-P to prevent transmission. In this cycle, embryo risk was stratified based on PRS percentiles derived from a reference population (e.g., the UK Biobank), with embryos in the top 2% of the PRS distribution classified as high risk. Among four euploid embryos evaluated, two were identified as high risk for T1DM [18], directly informing the selection decision and offering the possibility for preventing the development of T1DM in born children. In summary, PGT-P represents a promising strategy for the primary prevention of T1DM in families with an elevated genetic risk. Its integration into clinical practice will depend on accumulating evidence, refinement of risk models, and ongoing ethical discourse.

Breast cancer

Breast cancer is the most prevalent malignancy and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women globally. In 2020, approximately 2.3 million new cases were diagnosed, resulting in an estimated 685,000 deaths [89]. The disease incidence increases with age and affects women across all post-pubertal stages, making early diagnosis a critical factor for long-term survival. While high-penetrance germline mutations in genes such as BRCA1, BRCA2, and TP53 significantly increase risk, they account for only a small fraction of breast cancer cases in the general population [90]. Most heritable risk is attributed to the combined effect of numerous common genetic variants, each conferring a modest individual effect. GWAS have identified hundreds of these susceptibility loci, which can explain more than 30% of the heritability breast cancer [91]. PRS models for breast cancer have advanced considerably. Well-validated scores like SNP143 (based on 172 variants) demonstrate a diagnostic area under the curve (AUC) of 0.650 [92], and the larger SNP313 score achieves an AUC of 0.630 in populations of European ancestry [93]. These scores effectively help stratify women into broad risk categories.

The inaugural clinical report of PGT-P included breast cancer as a screened condition. Among five euploid embryos, two were identified as having a high polygenic risk for the disease, directly influencing embryo selection [17]. This demonstrates the feasibility of using PRS alone to guide transfer decisions even in the absence of a known major gene mutation. Integrated PGT-P and PGT-M may provide the greatest utility for risk assessment in known pathogenic carriers. A pivotal study demonstrated this in a couple where the female partner was a BRCA1 pathogenic variant carrier, analysis of 20 euploid embryos revealed that the presence of the BRCA1 mutation alone created a 4.5-fold range in odds ratio (OR). However, when the BRCA1 status was combined with a breast cancer PRS, the OR range expanded dramatically to 15-fold (from 0.35 in embryos without BRCA1 and with low PRS, to 5.35 in embryos with both BRCA1 and high PRS). Crucially, among embryos that did not inherit the BRCA1 mutation, the risk variation based on PRS alone was less than 2-fold [13]. Thus, PGT-P may offer the greatest clinical utility for breast cancer prevention when applied in conjunction with PGT-M in families carrying high-penetrance mutations. In such scenarios, it provides a risk assessment that can guide the selection of the BRCA-negative embryo with the lowest PRS, thereby maximizing risk reduction for the offspring. For families without known monogenic mutations, PGT-P based on PRS offers a novel primary prevention, representing a frontier in reproductive decision-making.

Polycystic ovary syndrome

PCOS is a prevalent metabolic and reproductive disorder with complex pathogenesis, affecting 6%-20% of reproductive aged women [94,95]. It is characterized by the presence of at least two of the following phenotypes: hyperandrogenism, chronic oligo-ovulation or anovulation, and polycystic ovarian morphology [96]. PCOS is also associated with metabolic disorders that include T2DM, obesity, insulin resistance, cardiovascular disease, and dyslipidemia, and an increased risk of cardiovascular disease [97,98]. The syndrome also elevates the risk of pregnancy complications such as gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, and preterm birth. PCOS exhibits strong heritability, with an estimated 60–80% of daughters born to affected mothers eventually developing the syndrome [99,100]. Compared with controls, daughters born to women with PCOS have a 5-fold increased risk of being diagnosed with PCOS in the future [101]. Given that IVF is a common treatment for PCOS-related infertility [102], there is a compelling rationale to integrating PGT-P to mitigate the transmission of this genetic predisposition. Large-scale GWAS in European and Asian populations have identified numerous susceptibility loci for PCOS, enriched in biological pathways governing steroid hormone biosynthesis and regulation, insulin signaling, and cellular processes like endocytosis [51,[103], [104], [105]]. This genetic foundation supports the development of PCOS-specific PRS. A PRS for PCOS is positively correlated with related metabolic phenotypes, including obesity, T2DM, and markers of coronary artery disease risk [106]. Another study found that a higher PCOS PRS in children was associated with an elevated risk of obesity in early childhood, higher body mass index in mid-childhood, and earlier timing of pubertal maturation [107]. These findings underscore the role of PCOS PRS in identifying metabolic, and developmental trajectories influenced by the underlying genetic predisposition.

Despite advances in understanding of the genetic basis of PCOS, the direct clinical application of PGT-P specifically for PCOS remains largely prospective and is an active area of translational research. Several factors contribute to this gap. First, PCOS is a clinically heterogeneous syndrome, making it more complex to define a single, binary “disease state” for embryo selection compared to conditions like T2DM. Second, infertile couples with PCOS undergoing IVF often prioritize immediate fertility success and the selection of euploid embryos via PGT-A in their clinical decision-making, rather than opting for PGT-P screening for a condition that typically manifests in adulthood. Future research should focus on modeling the expected risk reduction, develop standardized counseling frameworks, and report initial clinical cases to evaluate the real-world utility and ethical dimensions of employing PGT-P to reduce the intergenerational transmission of PCOS and its associated metabolic burdens.

Ethical considerations and limitations in PGT-P

PGT-P is an emerging technology that has been commercialized by several companies, primarily in the United States, such as Genomic Prediction, Orchid Health, and MyOme. For individuals, accessing PGT-P necessitates IVF, potentially exposing couples to invasive and costly fertility treatments. A critical ethical dilemma arises from the disposal of embryos deemed “high-risk,” these embryos may be discarded, but the embryos have a high possibility of developing into adults without the screened diseases. The pleiotropic effects of genetic variants, wherein a single variant may increase risk for one condition while decreasing risk for another, further complicate decision-making, requiring prospective parents to balance complex and competing risk indications [35]. Moreover, the probabilistic nature of PGT-P is difficult to explain effectively, potentially causing significant psychological distress and decisional burden for couples [42,111]. On a societal level, the high cost of PGT-P risks exacerbating existing health disparities. Large-scale implementation also raises concerns about a potential loss of population genetic diversity due to the selective pressure against certain risk alleles [112]. Ethically, the most profound concern is the potential slide towards ‘designer babies’, particularly using PGT-P for non-medical trait selection such as height, appearance, intelligence, lifespan, or athleticism, which could undermine the child's right to an open future and perpetuate discrimination against individuals with disabilities or undesirable traits [111,113]. Moreover, the rapid commercialization of PGT-P is intensifying these longstanding debates and introducing pressing new ethical challenges. The high cost of services threatens to exacerbate health inequities by making such interventions accessible primarily as a luxury, while market-driven expansion may outpace robust clinical validation and adaptive regulation. In this commercial landscape, ensuring truly autonomous and unbiased informed consent becomes even more complex yet critical. Consequently, a full ethical appraisal must now address not only the intended medical uses of the technology but also the socioeconomic and market forces that are actively shaping its real-world deployment.

As a predictive tool, PGT-P has significant technical constraints. Most PRS models are trained on GWAS data from populations of European ancestry, leading to substantially reduced predictive accuracy for non-European individuals [114,115]. These bias risks creating a genomic healthcare disparity, wherein the benefits of PGT-P are disproportionately limited to people of European ancestry. Furthermore, PRS calculates only inherited common genetic variants, without regarding to critical components such as de novo mutation, gene-gene interactions, gene-environment correlations, and the substantial role of non-genetic factors [116,117]. Moreover, the accuracy of a PRS is highly dependent on the underlying GWAS sample size, trait heritability, and appropriate ancestry matching [118]. For diseases where PRS is limited, the potential risk reduction from embryo selection is correspondingly modest [119]. In addition, for rarer diseases, even a high RRR may translate into a very ARR, challenging the clinical significance and cost-effectiveness of the intervention. Current PGT-P protocols cannot reliably account for mosaicism or aneuploidy in the TE biopsy, which can distort the PRS calculation, leading to inaccurate risk predictions [13].

Despite these challenges, public interest in PGT-P is notable; a recent survey found that 68% of participants viewed the use of PGT-P as reasonable [120]. Progress hinges on addressing key scientific gaps: expanding diverse GWAS biobanks, developing more accurate and equitable PRS, and elucidating gene function and biological mechanisms. Concurrently, there is an urgent need for multidisciplinary collaboration among geneticists, clinicians, ethicists, policymakers, and patient advocates to develop transparent clinical guidelines, robust informed-consent protocols, and equitable access frameworks. The future of PGT-P will be determined by our ability to balance technological innovation with ethical considerations, ensuring its responsible translation into clinical practice for those it can truly benefit.

Conclusion, limitations and future perspectives

PGT has been used in ART for decades, primarily to improve pregnancy outcomes and increase the chance of a healthy birth for couples at risk of transmitting genetic disorders, but PGT-P represents a novel and rapidly evolving frontier. We comprehensively synthesized the current knowledge of PGT-P, examining its scientific foundation, evaluating its clinical potential, and critically analyzing the significant challenges that accompany its translation into practice.

PGT-P is emerging as a novel strategy to reduce polygenic diseases in offspring, while technical limitations and ethical concerns persist [43,121,122]. First, a key step in PGT-P is calculating PRS derived from GWAS, aggregating the effects of thousands of alleles associated with polygenic diseases [15]. However, a major limitation constraining the clinical utility of PGT-P is its reliance on GWAS data from predominantly European-ancestry populations, which in turn leads to poor transferability and reduced accuracy of PRS models in non-European groups [123]. To address this ancestry imbalance and improve the global applicability of PGT-P, efforts are underway on two aspects: expanding genomic resources for non-European ancestries and developing statistical methods for cross-ancestry PRS prediction. A fundamental requirement for accurate genetic analysis in any population is a high-quality, population-specific reference panel. The efforts have been launched to diversify genomic databases. An important contribution is the NyuWa Genome Resource, established from high-depth whole-genome sequencing of 2999 Chinese individuals, encompassing 71.1 million SNPs and 8.2 million indels. The researchers then constructed a reference panel of 5804 haplotypes, representing the first large-scale, Chinese population-specific panel. This resource dramatically improved imputation accuracy for Chinese populations, reducing the error rate by 30% to 51% compared to using panels based on other ancestries, thereby providing a critical foundation for genetic research and precision medicine in Chinese and broader Asian populations [124]. Similar large-scale projects in other ancestries (e.g., the African Genome Variation Project [125], the GenomeAsia 100K project [126]) are gradually mitigating the historical bias in genomic resources. In parallel, modified statistical methodologies are being developed to leverage existing data and improve PRS portability across ancestries [[127], [128], [129]]. A prominent example is PRS-CSx, an extension of the PRS-CS framework [128]. PRS-CSx innovatively incorporates genetic data across diverse ancestry groups, using Bayesian regression to share information across populations. It accounts for both the correlation of genetic effects and the differences in LD patterns across populations, thereby improving the localization of true association signals and increasing statistical power. PRS-CSx has demonstrated accurate prediction of traits and disease risks in non-European populations, even when the ancestry-matched GWAS sample size are substantially smaller than those available for European cohorts. Other methods, such as CT-SLEB [130], also employs similar strategies of trans-ancestry learning and LD adjustment, consistently showing superior performance over ancestry-specific models in diverse populations.

Second, for low prevalence polygenic diseases, the achievable ARR may be slightly decreased even given a high RRR. For example, a 50% relative risk reduction for schizophrenia translates to a change in absolute prevalence from only about 1% to 0.5% in the general population [131]. For families with an elevated baseline risk, however, the utility of PGT-P must be evaluated differently. Applying the general population risk baseline to these families would significantly underestimate the potential value of PGT-P. It is therefore essential to establish a family-specific risk baseline via calculated from parental genotypes and family history to compute a relevant ARR that reflects the true potential benefit for that specific couple. A further complication arises from the generalizability of PRS models. Polygenic risk scores are typically trained on biobanks of unrelated individuals. Evidence suggests that their predictive accuracy can diminish when applied to distinct individuals within the same family, a phenomenon particularly noted for psychiatric and cognitive traits. This indicates that population-derived models may not fully capture or accurately predict risk within familial genetic contexts. Furthermore, the PRS model from research cohorts may not generalize to embryos in PGT-P, leading to a reduction in predictive ability. These biobanks are composed mainly of unrelated individuals; some research has reported a reduction in the prediction accuracy of PRS trained by distinct individuals within families, especially for psychiatric and cognitive traits [121,132]. This indicates that models derived from population cohorts may not fully capture or accurately predict risk within familial genetic contexts. Therefore, for prospective parents at high genetic risk, a family-specific risk assessment is not merely a refinement but a foundational prerequisite for ethically sound and clinically relevant PGT-P counseling. It represents the critical evolution of the technology from broad population screening to targeted, precision prevention.

Third, the PGT-P technology cannot directly assess or quantify the specific future environmental exposures of an individual embryo. Current efforts to account for environmental influences therefore focus on parental genetic counseling, the development of conceptual risk assessment frameworks, and research into prospective models. To address this limitation, researchers are exploring two primary avenues. The first approach involves building models that integrate gene-environment interactions. This requires large-scale databases that link genetic data, detailed environmental exposure histories, and long-term health outcomes. For example, studies have found that specific environmental exposures during pregnancy, such as bisphenol A, have a significant interactive effect with the polygenic risk score for T2DM, jointly influencing fetal growth [133]. Future iterations of PGT-P may incorporate such interaction data to enable more dynamic and personalized risk assessments. The second strategy aims to develop genetic proxy indicators for environmental exposure. Chen et al. has proposed the exposure-specific polygenic risk score (EpPRS), which does not directly measure the environment but instead uses genetic variants related to an individual's susceptibility to or metabolism of environmental pollutants to create a score, serving as a “genetic proxy” for estimating long-term environmental exposure burden [134]. Theoretically, the EpPRS could be combined with disease-specific PRS in the future to establish a more comprehensive assessment framework.

Fourth, the reliability of polygenic disease risk prediction by PGT-P for mosaic embryos remains unknown. The fundamental premise of PGT-P is that a TE biopsy fully represents the embryo's genetic constitution, while an assumption invalidated by mosaicism [135]. Specifically, PGT-P relies on precise phasing of parental haplotypes to impute sparse embryonic genotyping data into this parental haplotype framework. Mosaicism disrupts this process by introducing non-Mendelian genotype patterns, which can lead to haplotype phasing errors and incorrect mapping of recombination sites. As a result, the computationally imputed embryonic genome may substantially deviate from the embryo's true genetic state, invalidating any downstream risk calculation. Furthermore, mosaicism distorts the PRS, which requires accurate genotyping across hundreds of thousands of loci. If the biopsied cells belong to only one cell line of the mosaic embryo, the detected allele frequencies will not reflect the true genetic background of the whole embryo, rendering the calculated PRS unreliable.

Finally, the comprehensive risk profiles generated by PGT-P bring complex choices for prospective parents, as they must weigh and balance risk indications for multiple potential diseases [35,42]. This complexity underscores the urgent need to establish clear clinical guidelines and ethical frameworks for the responsible implementation of PGT-P. Given that PGT-P and PGT-M share the common objective of embryo risk assessment, the well-established framework for PGT-M offers a logical foundation for adaptation [111]. Building upon and extending existing the existing PGT-M guidance will be essential to navigating the novel ethical landscape of polygenic risk assessment in the embryo. Furthermore, establishing a dedicated multicenter clinical validation network for PGT-P is critical for advancing PGT-P from translational research to evidence-based practice. The proven framework of the Implementing Genomics in Practice Pragmatic Trials Network (IGNITE PTN) provides a directly applicable blueprint, with its focus on pragmatic trial design, collaborative coordinating center-clinical site infrastructure, explicit enrollment of diverse populations, and integration with electronic health records [136]. It can offer a validated framework to systematically assess PGT-P's long-term outcomes, cost-effectiveness and feasibility. Adapting this model for PGT-P may build a protocol-standardizing consortium, generate high-quality evidence for ethical implementation, and define a concrete pathway from translational research to evidence-based practice.

Despite current practical limitations, the potential of PGT-P continues to grow, driven by advances in sequencing technology, more accurate polygenic risk scores, sophisticated haplotype-phasing techniques, and strengthened ethical frameworks. These developments are paving the way for reliable prediction of polygenic disease risk in embryos, offering at-risk couples undergoing IVF a new paradigm for embryo selection.
