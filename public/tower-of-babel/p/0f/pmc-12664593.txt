Mechano-Ino-Chronotropic Unloading in Cardiogenic Shock Due to Acute Myocardial Infarction—Hemodynamic Insights From Simulation Modeling
Authors: Fatimah A. Alkhunaizi, Yonatan Mehlman, Maya Guglin, Daniel Burkhoff, Michael I. Brener
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe mortality rate of cardiogenic shock complicating acute myocardial infarction (AMI-CS) remains high and underscores an unmet clinical need for more effective therapeutic strategies in this space. Reducing myocardial O2 consumption (MVO2) improves myocardial O2 supply–demand balance, even in the presence of ongoing ischemia, and limits infarct size.MethodsUsing a comprehensive cardiovascular simulation, we aimed to examine the impact of mechanical unloading with a percutaneous left ventricular assist device (pVAD) combined with heart rate and contractility reduction, also referred to as pharmacologic chronotropic and inotropic unloading (mechano-ino-chronotropic unloading [MIC]), on MVO2 and hemodynamic parameters in a model of AMI-CS.ResultsWe found that MIC unloading is more effective at reducing MVO2 than mechanical unloading alone, without adverse hemodynamic effects when adequate mechanical unloading is utilized. Furthermore, we show that the degree of mechanical unloading achieved by pVAD is enhanced at lower heart rates due to longer periods of outflow from the left ventricle during diastole; that is, pVAD and lower heart rate are synergistic with regards to unloading.ConclusionsOverall, these findings suggest that MIC unloading may be more effective than mechanical unloading in reducing infarct size in AMI-CS, potentially leading to greater short-term and long-term left ventricular recovery. Future clinical investigations of this therapeutic approach are encouraged.

Introduction

Cardiogenic shock (CS) occurs in 5% to 12% of all acute myocardial infarctions (AMI) and remains the primary cause of morbidity and mortality.1 Despite significant overall improvement in AMI outcomes with the advent of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), outcomes for CS complicating AMI (AMI-CS) remain unacceptably poor, with mortality rates remaining at ∼50%.1, 2, 3

The central pathophysiology of AMI-CS involves acute myocardial O2 supply–demand mismatch secondary to coronary thrombosis, which results in myocardial ischemia, profound reductions in left ventricular (LV) contractility and cardiac output (CO), and then critical end-organ hypoperfusion. If this cycle is not promptly interrupted, it leads to a potentially fatal downward spiral of hypotension, further myocardial ischemia, and progressive pump failure. By restoring myocardial O2 supply, prompt reperfusion with PCI has been, until recently, the only intervention shown to improve AMI-CS outcomes. However, restoration of epicardial flow with PCI is also associated with ischemia/reperfusion injury and microvascular obstruction,4 both of which can contribute to myocardial dysfunction in AMI. Thus, reducing O2 demand and the supply–demand mismatch in AMI may also have benefits, and there remains a significant need for novel strategies to address this key factor leading to poor AMI-CS outcomes.

In 2024, the long-awaited DanGer Shock trial showed that implantation of a microaxial flow pump, in addition to revascularization, reduced 6-month mortality in select patients with AMI-CS,5 marking the first treatment strategy to impact survival in a subset of patients with AMI-CS since the SHOCK trial.6 The mechanism of benefit in DanGer Shock relates to the microaxial flow pump’s ability to reduce myocardial O2 consumption (MVO2) and restore myocardial O2 supply–demand balance.

MVO2 is linearly correlated with pressure–volume area (PVA), which can be represented on the pressure–volume (PV) diagram by the sum of the area within the PV loop (ie, stroke work) and potential energy (ie, the space bound by the end-systolic and end-diastolic PV relationships, but outside the PV loop) (Figure 1). Accordingly, the key determinants of MVO2 are LV stroke work as well as contractility (represented by end-systolic elastance [Ees]) and afterload (effective arterial elastance [Ea]), which influence the position of the PV loop and, consequently, impact potential energy.7 Although heart rate (HR) minimally impacts the PV loop (which is a depiction of hemodynamics during 1 cardiac cycle) through its modest effect on afterload, it does influence overall MVO2, proportional to the number of cardiac cycles in a given unit of time.8Figure 1Pressure-volume loop basics. For decades, it has been known that reducing MVO2 in the setting of AMI limits the extent of myocardial necrosis.9 However, only recently have efforts been directed toward using percutaneous ventricular assist devices (pVAD) for this purpose in the setting of AMI.10 pVADs applied during ischemia (prior to reperfusion) have been associated with other cardioprotective effects linked with specific molecular pathways that preserve mitochondrial and myocyte integrity during ischemia and following reperfusion.11

Therefore, in this framework, MVO2 can be reduced in patients with AM-CS by decreasing HR and reducing PVA by reducing loading conditions and modulating contractility and afterload. Clinical efforts at reducing MVO2 in the acute phase of AMI have thus far focused primarily on reducing PVA through the use of pVADs, specifically the Impella transvalvular microaxial flow pump (Abiomed/Johnson & Johnson). In animal models of ischemia/reperfusion injury, mechanically unloading (M-U) the left ventricle with pVAD has been shown to reduce wall stress and stroke work, thereby PVA and MVO2, and to limit infarct size.12,13 The degree to which M-U can reduce MVO2 is limited, however, by the HR-dependent MVO2 component.14 Indeed, the ability of negative chronotropic agents such as β-blockers to reduce MVO2 is among the reasons why their early administration is safe, beneficial, and recommended in patients with stable AMI without heart failure (Killip class I-II).15

Combining both PVA reduction and HR reduction as a therapeutic strategy in the setting of AMI has the potential to more effectively reduce MVO2 and salvage valuable myocardium. This strategy can be termed mechano-ino-chronotropic (MIC) unloading (MIC-U), as it involves 3 components: mechanical ventricular unloading (mechano-), contractility reduction (ino-), and HR reduction (chronotropic) (Central Illustration). The combined mechanochronotropic unloading component of this strategy has been tested in a dog model of ischemia/reperfusion, in which M-U with a pVAD and pharmacologic chronotropic unloading with ivabradine resulted in more significant reductions in MVO2 and infarct size, compared with M-U alone.16 However, the impact of MIC-U in the setting of AMI-CS has yet to be explored.Central IllustrationThe 3 components of Mechano-Ino-Chronotropic Unloading (MIC-U): mechanical ventricular unloading, pharmacologic contractility reduction, and pharmacologic heart rate reduction. In this study, we used a previously validated, comprehensive, cardiovascular simulation to test the hypothesis that MIC-U in AMI-CS, using a combination of mechanical ventricular unloading and pharmacologic contractility and HR reduction (eg, β-receptor blockade), results in more potent MVO2 reduction than M-U alone. We specifically examined the individual components of this strategy and tested whether their individual effects are additive or synergistic. The results provide a compelling theoretical foundation for a clinical study of MIC-U in AMI-CS.

Methods

Theoretical considerations

We used a comprehensive cardiovascular simulation that has been described in detail previously and successfully used to describe the physiologic effects of various heart failure interventions, including the impact of temporary mechanical circulatory support devices, and various other therapies, on MVO2.17, 18, 19, 20 The simulator allows for the selection and adjustment of model inputs to match a specific hemodynamic profile—in this analysis, a theoretical patient with AMI-CS. Using previously detailed differential equations,17 the simulator yields a multitude of outputs, including PV loops for each of the 4 cardiac chambers, time-dependent tracings of pressures, flows, and volumes, and all possible hemodynamic parameters derived from these signals.

For context, MVO2 was calculated using the following formula, first proposed by Suga et al21 in a canine model:MVO2=α×(PVA×HR)+β×(Ees×HR)+γ×(HR)where α, β, and γ are constants that have been defined previously as follows:α=1.8×10−5mLO2·mmHg−1·mL−1β=2.4×10−3mLO2·beats−1·mmHg−1·mL·g·100−1γ=1.4×10−2mLO2·beats−1·g·100−1

Clinical simulations

A series of simulated experiments were conducted to assess the impact of 4 levels of mechanical unloading with a pVAD (Impella 5.0) on PVA and MVO2 in the prototypical AMI-CS patient described earlier. While Impella CP is the device of choice in the acute AMI-CS setting, Impella 5.0 was selected for this simulation to demonstrate the additional benefits incurred by higher levels of mechanical unloading. In order to extrapolate the impact of Impella CP, mean pVAD flow rate was varied throughout the simulations, from 2.2 to 4.3 L/min. At each level of mechanical unloading, the impact of chronotropic unloading was concomitantly assessed by stepwise HR slowing, from a baseline of 120 beats per minute (bpm) to a nadir of 60 bpm, in 20-bpm intervals. The impact of negative inotropy was assessed by varying contractility (indexed by Ees) from 0.5 to 1.5 mm Hg/mL. Selected combinations of HR, Ees, and pVAD flows were tested over the range of HRs to determine how they impacted hemodynamics and energetics (ie, were these interventions additive or synergistic).

Baseline AMI-CS state

The baseline AMI-CS state was modeled after a patient with acute occlusion of the left anterior descending artery, resulting in a large anterior myocardial infarction and severe LV dysfunction, but without significant right ventricular (RV) dysfunction. The impact of mechanical unloading on PVA and MVO2

In the first simulation, we introduced a pVAD to our baseline AMI-CS state. The pVAD was set to 4 different speeds, beginning with a low level of support at 17,000 revolutions per minute (RPM) (P2), followed by 22,000 RPM (P4), then 27,000 RPM (P6), and finally full support at 33,000 RPM (P8). Figure 2 shows the effect of M-U on PVA (green line) and MVO2 (purple line) at the various pump speeds. We note a near 50% reduction in PVA at maximal M-U with the pVAD set at 33,000 RPM. In the second simulation, we added a negative chronotropic agent and examined the impact of progressive HR slowing on PVA and MVO2 in the setting of various levels of Impella support, as demonstrated in Figure 3. We note that M-U remains the greatest determinant of PVA, with higher pVAD pump speeds resulting in greater PVA reduction. The impact of inotropic unloading on hemodynamic parameters, PVA, and MVO2 at various levels of mechanical unloading

In the third simulation, we considered the impact of negative inotropy, which may accompany negative chronotropy, when agents such as β-blockers are used to lower HR, compared with an agent such as ivabradine, an If current inhibitor, which does not have negative inotropic effects. By varying Ees (a load-independent measure of LV contractility), we evaluated the impact of M-U on various hemodynamic parameters at 3 different levels of contractility, as demonstrated in Figure 4. We noted that with no or minimal pVAD support, the lowest Ees (lowest contractility) was associated with the lowest MAP, the lowest CO, and the highest PAWP. However, when the pVAD speed was increased to allow for full mechanical unloading, the MAP, CO, and PAWP improved significantly, and the hemodynamic differences became irrelevant across various Ees levels. Additionally, at a given HR, both PVA and MVO2 reduced markedly at lower Ees levels, suggesting a cumulative benefit to inotropic unloading when combined with mechanochronotropic unloading. Discussion

Mechano-ino-chronotropic unloading, using a combination of mechanical ventricular unloading and pharmacologic contractility and HR reduction, has the potential to be an effective therapeutic strategy in patients with AMI-CS. In this study, using simulation modeling, we found the following: (1) MIC-U is more effective at MVO2 reduction in a model of AMI-CS, compared with M-U alone and mechanochronotropic unloading; (2) at any given pVAD speed, the effectiveness of M-U, as demonstrated by the degree of PVA reduction, is greater at lower HRs; and (3) the adverse effects of negative inotropy on hemodynamic parameters during chronotropic unloading are negated by full hemodynamic support and M-U. Overall, these findings improve our understanding of the important role that MIC-U may play in the acute management of patients with AMI-CS (Central Illustration).

It has been previously shown by Sunagawa et al16 that the combined reduction of PVA with a pVAD and HR lowering with ivabradine plays a synergistic role in MVO2 reduction in a dog model of ischemia/reperfusion. Importantly, they also showed a major reduction in infarct size.16 This study replicates these findings in a simulation model of AMI-CS and reemphasizes the important role of the HR-dependent component of MVO2.7

Currently, the optimal HR target in patients with profound hemodynamic compromise remains uncertain. It is unclear to what extent tachycardia serves as a compensatory response and when it becomes hemodynamically unfavorable. With pVAD support, circulation is maintained by 2 components: the continuous flow from the pVAD and the pulsatile output from the left ventricle. Optimizing LV output can further improve overall hemodynamics. Our simulations demonstrated that lower HRs are beneficial in AMI-CS, particularly at higher levels of pVAD support (P8). Notably, reducing HR to as low as 60 bpm improved hemodynamics (Figure 3). While this finding may seem counterintuitive, it aligns with the physiological benefits of prolonged diastole, resulting in improved LV filling and enhanced coronary perfusion. While the pVAD provides adequate output, a lower HR reduces MVO2, maximizing the benefits of this combined mechanical and chronotropic unloading strategy.

These findings align with early clinical data from Barillà et al,23 who conducted a randomized trial of individuals with AMI-CS in the setting of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. They showed that ivabradine is safe and associated with favorable changes in systemic blood pressure, NT-proBNP, and LV ejection fraction, as well as with less mortality than the standard of care, although the latter finding did not reach statistical significance in this small trial of 58 individuals.23 Nevertheless, this finding has stimulated broader use of ivabradine in individuals with decompensated heart failure who have heightened adrenergic tone driving deleterious degrees of tachycardia.24, 25, 26

Additionally, our study shows further benefit to MIC-U, compared with mechanochronotropic unloading, without adverse effects on hemodynamics when adequate mechanical unloading is used. A major benefit of achieving effective MVO2 reduction in the AMI-CS setting is that this may allow the safe delay of coronary reperfusion, thus allowing time for the activation of anti–ischemia/reperfusion injury molecular pathways and limiting infarct size. Indeed, several preclinical models of AMI have shown that mechanical unloading with pVAD activates the reperfusion injury salvage kinase pathway, and other antiapoptotic pathways, as well as limits mitochondrial damage and ultimately reduces myocardial injury and scar formation.12,27, 28, 29 Thus, it stands to reason that more effective unloading with a synergistic MIC-U approach would result in greater myocardial salvage during AMI.

Based on the aforementioned extensive preclinical data, several clinical trials have been completed or are underway to assess the efficacy of mechanical unloading in the AMI setting: the DTU-STEMI trial is investigating the efficacy of Impella CP unloading and delayed reperfusion in AMI without shock10; DanGer Shock, as previously described, showed a survival benefit of Impella in patients with ST-segment elevation AMI-CS5; and the RECOVER IV trial, which also aimed to investigate the efficacy of Impella in AMI-CS, was terminated after the positive results of DanGer Shock were released. Combined MIC-U, however, has not been tested clinically in the AMI-CS setting, driven in part by concerns that the administration of negative chronotropic and negative inotropic agents to patients with CS can be hemodynamically detrimental. Our results show that the adverse effects of lowering HR and reducing contractility on hemodynamic parameters including MAP, CO, and PAWP can be fully mitigated by the concomitant use of adequate mechanical unloading (Figure 4). This suggests that combining β-blockers with high levels of pVAD support in the AMI-CS setting may not only be safe, but beneficial. Given the benefit of maximal MVO2 reduction in limiting infarct size, thus improving post-AMI outcomes,30 our results provide compelling support to begin clinically investigating MIC-U in the AMI-CS setting.

Limitations

To isolate the individual effects of HR and contractility reduction, our simulations examined these strategies separately, and thus, the model may not fully capture the effects of combined dual-negative chronotropic and negative inotropic agents such as β-blockers, nor the doses required to achieve the proposed effects. In addition, our simulations do not take into consideration the timing of MIC-U with respect to revascularization, which will be an important consideration for future clinical translation.

Conclusions

Mechano-ino-chronotropic unloading may be a safe and effective therapeutic strategy in patients with AMI-CS and warrants further clinical investigation.
