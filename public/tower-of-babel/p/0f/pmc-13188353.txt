Preliminary modeling of brain metastases in breast cancer: a neural network approach to risk factor analysis
Authors: Hourieh Ansari, Amirparsa Hajigholami, Hamed Aslani, Ali Hajigholami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer accounts for one third of cancer-related deaths in women and has complications such as bone and brain metastases in other organs. Investigating the factors influencing this disease and its associated metastases is particularly important for treatment trends. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the predictive risk factors and their risk scores for brain metastases in breast cancer patients.MethodsThis case-control study was conducted on breast cancer patients in two groups: those with (13 subjects) and those without (39 subjects) metastases, in two medical centres between the 2022–2024. The data of patients were obtained from their medical records. Chi-square tests, multivariable logistic regression, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves, and a two-layer perceptron neural network model (NNM) with a hyperbolic tangent activation function were employed to compare differences and predict risk factors of brain metastasis. All data were analysed using SPSS v.28 software.ResultsIn univariable logistic regression analyses, among significant predictors i.e. younger age (≤ 40 years), HER2–HR+ status, tumor size, and lymph node involvement which inclufed in multivariable analysis younger age and HER2–HR+ status remained independently significant predictors of brain metastasis (BBM). A point-based scoring system with a cutoff of 8 achieved sensitivity of 77%, specificity of 62%, and with an overall discriminative ability of AUC = 0.88 (95% CI: 0.78–0.98, P < 0.001).Neural network analysis provided complementary insights by quantifying the relative importance of predictors. HER2-HR+ (100% relative importance) and progesterone receptor status (79% relative importance) emerged as the most influential variables. The model achieved an AUC of 0.843, with test accuracy of 91.7%. Internal validation confirmed moderate but consistent performance but some miscalibration due to sample size limitations.ConclusionThe scoring model developed in this study demonstrated acceptable sensitivity and specificity for predicting the occurrence of brain metastases in women with breast cancer. However, given the hospital-based design and relatively small sample size, the estimates may be unstable and should be interpreted with caution. These findings suggest that the proposed scoring system may serve as a preliminary tool for risk stratification, while highlighting the need for further refinement and validation in diverse patient populations.

Background

Breast cancer is the third most prevalent cancer among women worldwide [1]. Although men might develop breast cancer, it includes only 1% of all cases of this disease [2]. This disease accounts for one-third of mortality in women with cancer worldwide, and the disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) attributed to breast cancer are higher in developed countries [2, 3]. Reports revealed that breast cancer ranks first among cancers diagnosed in Iranian women, with an age-standardized incidence rate of about 35.8 per 100,000 women [4]. Though breast cancer is less common in Iran compared to developed countries, the average age of patients is about a decade lower than the global average, and the proportion of patients under under the age of 50 is higher compared to other countries [5, 6]. The highest DALYs in patients with breast cancer are related to risk factors such as high fasting blood sugar, alcohol consumption, and obesity [7].

Breast cancer causes complications, including metastasis to other organs, the primary cause of death [8]. Metastasis is so lethal that the prognosis of patients with stage four breast cancer depends on the metastasis location [9]. The liver, lung, lymph nodes, ovarian, and brain are the most common possible sites for breast cancer metastasis [10, 11]. The prognosis of breast cancer metastases differs depending on the metastasis location, where bone metastasis has the best and brain metastasis has the worst prognosis [12, 13].

Brain metastasis can affect up to 30% to 50% of patients with breast cancer [14]. Among patients diagnosed and treated for early-stage breast cancer, approximately 3–6% will develop cranial nervous system (CNS) metastases, while an estimated 10–16% of patients with stage four disease develop CNS metastases at diagnosis [15]. The existing evidence corroborated the increasing incidence of brain metastases from breast cancer [9]. This is assumed to be due to improved imaging detection and increased overall survival of breast cancer patients through advanced multimodal treatment approaches [16–18].

Various factors such as age, tumor size, the number of affected lymph nodes, and the expression of estrogen and progesterone receptors, as well as the HER2 factor in tumor tissue, are recognized as risk factors for cancer metastasis [19, 20]. The expression of HER2, cytokeratin 5.6 (CK5.6), and high Ki-67 receptors in breast cancer cells with higher invasive degrees, which subsequently ended in brain metastasis, was higher compared to non-metastatic cancers [21]. Besides gaining a better understanding of disease mechanisms, a more detailed examination of these risk factors seems to help identify at-risk patients and improve the outcomes.

Numerous targeted therapies have been developed that target specific molecular signalling pathways involved in cancer progression [22–26]. Researchers might be able to develop more effective targeted therapies by identifying risk factors associated with brain metastasis of at-risk patients [27, 28]. Moreover, determining more accurate cut-off values for laboratory findings can help increase the precision of these tests in predicting at-risk patients. Iranian women develop breast cancer a decade earlier than women in other countries; its prevalence is higher in central regions, including Isfahan, and the likelihood of cancer metastasis increases over time. Therefore, the present study aimed to determine the influence of factors affecting brain metastasis in breast cancer patients. By predicting the influence of risk factors in women with breast cancer without metastasis and high risk of brain metastasis, it is possible to reduce the risk of metastasis through preventive methods For example, using optimization of systemic therapies such as long-term endocrine therapy or targeted adjuvant low molecular weight drugs and other preventive therapies.

Method

Study design

This case-control study was conducted on breast cancer patients with medical records in Seyed al-Shohada and Milad Hospitals in Isfahan, Iran between 2022 and 2024. The study approve by research ethics comitee of Isfahan University of Medical Science (IR.MUI.MED.REC.1399.298). To ensure comparable diagnostic procedures and data availability, both cases and controls were selected from the same hospital archives. The case group included female patients over 16 years suffering from breast cancer with pathological confirmation and definitive diagnosis of breast cancer as the primary source of malignancy with brain metastasis, confirmed through MRI findings. Controls were randomly selected from breast cancer patients without brain metastasis who met the same inclusion criteria. To optimize statistical efficiency, the control group was selected at approximately three times the size of the case group. This ratio was chosen to increase the precision of odds ratio estimates and reduce variance in regression analyses, while maintaining comparability between groups. Such an approach is widely recommended in case–control studies with limited case numbers [29]. This overall strategy was intended to minimize selection bias by applying identical eligibility criteria and random sampling for controls. The exclusion criterion was the presence of missing data in any of the key study variables required for the complete case analysis. Only patients with fully available information across all predefined variables were retained in the final dataset. This ensured methodological consistency and avoided potential bias introduced by imputation or partial records.

Data collection

The required information included age, tumor size, number of involved lymph nodes, estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, HER2 factor, brain metastasis status, tumor clinical stage, and tumor pathological stage. Patients were classified into four HER2/HR subgroups according to immunohistochemical assessment of HER2 and hormone receptors, defined as estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) expression. Hormone receptor positivity was determined when ER and/or PR staining was present in ≥ 1% of tumor cell nuclei, while negativity required both ER and PR to be absent. Based on this definition, the subgroups were: HER2 + HR+ (HER2 positive with ER and/or PR positive), HER2 + HR– (HER2 positive with both ER and PR negative), HER2–HR+ (HER2 negative with ER and/or PR positive), and HER2–HR– (HER2 negative with both ER and PR negative) [30].

Data analysis

The collected data were analyzed in SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Quantitative variables were summarized by mean and standard deviation, and categorical variables by counts and percentages. Group comparisons between patients with and without brain metastases (BBM) used the chi-square test. For etiologic and predictive inference, we first fitted univariate logistic regressions to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals, then specified a multivariable logistic regression including predictors significant at the 10% level while excluding variables that induced multicollinearity. Model discrimination for the multivariable logistic regression was evaluated via the ROC curve and AUC.

To derive a clinically interpretable risk score, regression coefficients from the final multivariable model were translated into integer weights proportional to each predictor’s log-odds contribution. Coefficients were normalized by dividing each β coefficient by the smallest absolute coefficient in the model, then rounded to the nearest integer to generate a point value for each risk factor. This ensured that the relative magnitude of effect was preserved while producing a simple scoring system. The cumulative score for each patient was obtained by summing the assigned points across predictors.

The cut-off threshold for high-risk classification was determined empirically using ROC curve analysis. Specifically, the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity – 1) was applied to identify the optimal cut-off point that maximized the balance between sensitivity and specificity.

Internal calibration of the scoring model was assessed by comparing predicted and observed probabilities using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. For the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, a non-significant p-value (P > 0.05) suggests adequate agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, whereas a significant result (P < 0.05) indicates miscalibration [31]. In addition, discrimination across subgroups (e.g., age categories, hormone receptor status) was examined descriptively to ensure that the scoring system maintained predictive ability across clinically relevant strata. This stepwise approach deriving integer scores from regression coefficients, normalizing weights, selecting the cut-off via Youden index, and evaluating calibration and subgroup discrimination provides a transparent and reproducible framework for risk score development. We also considered the events-per-variable (EPV) criterion to evaluate the adequacy of sample size for logistic regression. The EPV was calculated as the number of outcome events divided by the number of predictors included in the multivariable model. A threshold of ≥ 10 events per variable is commonly recommended to ensure model stability and reduce overfitting risk [32].

In parallel, a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) neural network was applied to identify predictive factors for brain metastases in breast cancer patients. The network architecture consisted of two hidden layers, each with 10 neurons, using hyperbolic tangent activation functions, and the output layer also employed a hyperbolic tangent function to generate binary classification probabilities. The model was trained using the backpropagation algorithm with a learning rate of 0.01, batch size of 8, and a maximum of 500 training epochs. To prevent overfitting, early stopping rules were applied, halting training if validation loss did not improve after 20 consecutive epochs, and L2 regularization was incorporated into the loss function. Ten predictor variables (HER2, ER, PR, age, number of lymph nodes, tumor size, ALL+, ALL–, HER2-HR+, HER2 + HR–, HER2 + HR+, HER2-HR– ) were included as predictors.

To evaluate the stability and generalizability of the model, we implemented 3-fold cross-validation, which is widely considered acceptable for small biomedical datasets as it balances bias and variance while ensuring that each observation is used both for training and validation. In addition, bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations was performed, a standard approach to estimate the variability of performance metrics and provide confidence intervals for accuracy and AUC. Calibration metrics were calculated through the Brier score. A Brier score below 0.25 is generally regarded as acceptable for binary prediction models, with lower values indicating better calibration [33]. Variable importance was extracted from the trained network to quantify the relative contribution of each predictor to the classification task.

Missing data were handled prior to model training by complete-case analysis; patients with missing values in any of the predictor variables were excluded from the neural network analysis. No statistical imputation was applied, given the small sample size and the limited number of missing values, to avoid introducing additional bias.

These procedures ensured that the neural network results were not only internally valid but also robust to sampling variation, thereby strengthening the reliability of the findings in a small dataset context. A statistical significance level of P-Value < 0.05 was considered for all analyses.

Results

A total of 52 females participated in this study, with a mean (standard deviation) age of 48.5 (6.7) years. Participants were categorized into two groups: those with brain metastasis (13 females) and those without brain metastasis (39 females). The mean (standard deviation) ages of the two groups were 49.2 (5.9) years for the metastasis group and 45.5 (7.9) years for the non-metastasis group, respectively. The chi-square analyses demonstrated significant associations between several clinicopathological variables and BBM status. Age distribution showed that patients aged ≤ 40 were more frequently observed in the BBM group compared to those aged > 40, with a highly significant difference (P < 0.001). The number of lymph nodes also revealed a borderline association, where cases with zero nodes were more common in the non-BBM group (p = 0.054). Tumor size categories indicated a significant relationship, with smaller tumors (T1) predominating in the non-BBM group, while larger tumors (T3) were more frequent among BBM cases (P = 0.029). Hormone receptor status was also relevant: progesterone negativity was significantly associated with the non-BBM group (P = 0.008), and estrogen negativity showed a similar pattern (P = 0.017). HER2 status alone did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.105), but combined HER2 and HR categories revealed important differences. Specifically, the distribution across HER2/HR subgroups showed that HER2–HR + and HER2–HR– patterns were unevenly represented between BBM and non-BBM groups, with chi-square tests indicating significant or near-significant differences (P = 0.043 and P = 0.085, respectively) (Table 1).

Logistic regression analyses

In the univariate logistic regression analyses, several clinical and pathological factors were significantly associated with the occurrence of brain metastases in breast cancer patients. Females younger than 40 years, estrogen receptor–negative, progesterone receptor–negative, with at least one involved lymph node, larger tumor size, and absence of HER2-HR+ status demonstrated markedly higher risks of metastasis. The univariate odds ratios of these risk factors are presented in Table 1. For example, patients aged ≤ 40 years had an OR of 23.75 (95% CI: 2.43-231.82, P < 0.001), and those with PR– status had an OR of 6.53 (95% CI: 1.64–25.93, P = 0.008). In the HER2 & HR subgroup analysis, using HER2 + HR- as the reference, the odds of BBM presence were markedly reduced in HER2–HR+ (OR = 0.09, p = 0.043), slightly reduced in HER2 + HR+ (OR = 0.14, P = 0.115), and modestly increased in HER2–HR- (OR = 1.50, p = 0.694), though only the HER2–HR+ comparison reached statistical significance.

Risk factors with statistical significance at the 10% level in univariate analyses were subsequently entered into the multivariable logistic regression model, while variables leading to multicollinearity were excluded. The final multivariable model included age ≤ 40 years, HER2-HR+ status, number of lymph nodes involvement, and tumor size, of which age ≤ 40 years, HER2-HR+ status remained independently associated with increased risk of brain metastasis (Table 2). The discriminative ability of the fitted model was evaluated using the ROC curve, yielding an AUC of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.78–0.98, P < 0.001), which indicates high predictive efficacy.

To enhance clinical interpretability, regression coefficients from the multivariable model were transformed into a point-based scoring system. Each predictor was assigned a weight proportional to its log-odds coefficient, reflecting the relative strength of association with metastasis. For example, age ≤ 40 years was assigned 7 points, HER2-HR+ status 6 points, tumor size T3 3 points, and number of lymph nodes involvement 2 points. The cumulative risk score was then calculated for each patient. ROC curve analysis identified a cutoff value of 8 points, which provided a sensitivity of 77% and specificity of 62% for predicting brain metastasis (Fig. 1). Patients with total scores above this threshold were classified as high risk. This scoring approach, derived directly from logistic regression coefficients, offers a transparent and reproducible method for quantifying the relative importance of predictors and stratifying patients according to their risk profiles.

In the multivariable logistic regression model, four predictors (age, HER2-HR+, tumor size, and lymph node involvement) were included based on their significance in univariate analyses. Given that only 13 brain metastasis events occurred in the cohort, the events-per-variable (EPV) ratio was approximately 3.25 (13/4). This EPV is well below the recommended threshold of 10, indicating a high risk of overfitting and unstable estimates and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (χ²=25.75, p = 0.0012) also suggested some miscalibration, likely due to the limited sample size.

Neural network results

The importance percentage of the variables in the NNM to predict the occurrence of BBM in breast cancer patients is shown in Table 3. This table shows that HER2-HR+ with 100% importance and progesterone receptor with 79% importance are the most significant variables. The area under the curve in Fig. 2 of this model was 84.3%. The recognition accuracy of this model was 82.5% for the training group and 91.7% for the test group. To address concerns of potential optimism, we subsequently performed 5-fold cross-validation, which produced a mean accuracy of 71.3% (SD = 11.6%) and a mean AUC of 0.727 (SD = 0.168), indicating moderate but consistent discriminative ability across folds. Bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations further confirmed model stability, with average accuracy of 82.8% (SD = 11.6%) and mean AUC of 0.841 (SD = 0.169). Calibration analyses demonstrated a Brier score of 0.220, reflecting acceptable not optimal overall agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. Taken together, these results show that the MLP model captures relevant predictive signals for brain metastases in breast cancer, though its calibration requires refinement and larger datasets to achieve more reliable generalizability.

Discussion

Research indicates that breast cancer in younger females tends to be more aggressive and of a higher grade, primarily due to a greater prevalence of HER+ type tumors in this demographic, which often exhibit resistance to drug treatments. Moreover, elevated levels of sex hormones in younger females can stimulate tumor growth [34]. However, this study examined and compared breast cancer patients with and without brain metastasis, focusing on the predictive roles of age, hormone receptors, the number of involved lymph nodes, and tumor size in the occurrence of brain metastasis.

The findings of the research reveal that breast cancer in younger women, particularly those less than 40 years of age, is significantly more aggressive and is associated with a 46-fold increase in the risk of brain metastasis. This age group, considering a cutoff point of 40 years, received the highest score in the relevant scoring system. Numerous studies have shown that women with brain metastases resulting from breast cancer (BCBM) generally tend to be younger; however, varying age limitations have been examined in this context. Consequently, a study involving over 200,000 breast cancer patients revealed that the highest risk of brain metastasis is observed in patients aged between 41 and 60 years [21, 35].

Findings should be contextualized within the growing body of literature dedicated to predictive models and mechanistic insights into brain metastasis in breast cancer. Notably, recent large-scale studies have developed robust clinical prediction models. For instance, Cacho-Díaz et al. (2023) built and validated a model using a cohort of 5,009 patients, identifying age, estrogen receptor status, tumor size, axillary adenopathy, clinical stage, Ki-67 expression, and histological grade as key predictors, achieving an AUC of 0.81 and stratifying patients into distinct risk groups [36]. This underscores the critical role of comprehensive clinical-pathological data available at diagnosis for risk stratification. Furthermore, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) is revolutionizing this field. AI models are enhancing the accuracy of imaging diagnostics and pathological assessments (e.g., HER2 and Ki-67 scoring) and are being leveraged to develop sophisticated predictive algorithms for risk stratification and treatment response, as reviewed in recent literature [37]. Concurrently, advanced mechanistic reviews have detailed the complex biology of BCBM, highlighting multi-step processes like epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), metabolic reprogramming, and intricate crosstalk with the brain microenvironment that facilitate metastasis and immune evasion [38, 39]. These large-scale models and biological frameworks provide a valuable benchmark and context for our study. While our sample size is more limited, our work contributes by applying a neural network approach to a specific regional cohort, exploring a similar set of clinical variables in a population with distinct epidemiological characteristics, and aligning our findings with these broader biological principles.

Regarding the examination of the pathological characteristics, imaging of the tumor, and the findings related to its invasion, previous studies reported that the risk of brain metastasis was significantly related to the levels of lymph node involvement at the time of initial cancer diagnosis [40–42]. In other words, the farther the affected lymph nodes are from the tumor site and the higher the number of involved lymph nodes, the more likely the patient will develop brain metastasis. This variable was not examined due to the limited information available in this study. However, more than one lymph node involvement was associated with an increased probability of brain metastasis.

Other findings of this study revealed that breast tumors in patients with brain metastasis are significantly larger, although this relationship was not statistically significant. Tumors classified as T2 and T3 had 1.7 and 5.8 times the likelihood of developing brain metastasis compared to T1 tumors, which aligns with previous studies [21, 40, 43]. This variable was also significant in the NNM, with a significance level of 54%. Furthermore, the present study observed a greater number of involved lymph nodes. Although this finding was not statistically significant, it was associated with an increased incidence of brain metastasis, in accordance with previous studies [21]. These findings highlight the importance of tumor size and lymph node status in understanding the risk factors associated with brain metastasis in patients with breast cancer.

Breast cancers are divided into several major sub-groups based on the expression status of Human growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) and the expression status of estrogen and progesterone receptors in immunohistochemical analysis or gene expression profile. These sub-groups have different gene expression statuses, activating various signaling pathways for metastasis. Therefore, these sub-groups show different metastasis patterns and locations [44]. Studies demonstrated that HER2 + breast cancers have a high risk of brain metastases, so anti-HER2 drugs were prescribed to improve the outcome of HER2 + brain metastases patients [35, 45–48]. Evidence indicated that ER-breast cancers are associated with a higher risk of brain metastasis than ER+ cancers and PR-breast cancers. Yet, this association was statistically significant in most univariate analysis studies.

However, there are contrasting insights regarding the significance of hormone receptor status. While this study identifies HER2-HR + as having a 100% importance and PR status as having 79% importance in predicting BM in the NNM, other studies suggest that the presence of HR may not significantly alter the risk profile for brain metastases in HER2-positive patients [49].

In another study, Alison et al. demonstrated that the lowest risk ratio for brain metastasis was observed in HER2-/HR+ patients, whereas HR+/HER2+, HR-/HER2-, and HR-/HER2 + patients had risks of 1.92, 2.61, and 3.25 times greater for brain metastasis, respectively [35]. These findings align with the results of our study, with the only difference being that some of these associations may not be statistically significant due to the small sample size of the current study. The role of HER2 in enhancing the likelihood of brain metastasis may be attributed to interactions between HER3 and other receptors. The signaling pathways and interactions involving HER2 and related receptors, such as HER3 and EGFR, are recognized as predisposing factors for brain metastasis. Cerebral vessels are rich in ligands, including neuregulins, which can activate these receptors in the context of brain metastasis. Furthermore, the exchanges between HER2 and HER3 can facilitate the passage of cancer cells through the blood-brain barrier by promoting the release of matrix metalloproteinases, thereby disrupting this barrier [47, 50]. Despite these insights, there is limited literature addressing the relationship between HR negativity and brain metastasis [51]. Generally, estrogen receptor negativity is associated with elevated levels of Ki-67—a proliferation marker linked to breast cancer and potentially to brain metastasis—alongside a higher histological grade and increased tissue invasiveness [52].

The present study had limitations. Foremost, our cohort included only 52 patients with 13 brain metastasis events. This sample size is substantially smaller than those used to develop validated clinical prediction models, such as the one by Cacho-Díaz et al. involving over 5,000 patients [36]. This limitation restricts the stability of our multivariable models, increases the risk of overfitting, and underscores that our findings are preliminary and hypothesis-generating. To minimize this risk, we first performed univariable analyses and only included predictors that were statistically significant in subsequent multivariable models. We also report the events per variable (EPV) to provide transparency regarding model complexity. Nevertheless, the wide confidence intervals observed for odds ratios reflect the instability inherent in small-event data. The neural network approach was applied to explore potential nonlinear associations and to address collinearity among predictors, but its predictive performance should be interpreted with caution. Accordingly, the proposed scoring system is hypothesis-generating and not intended for immediate clinical application [53]. Other limitation of this study was its hospital-based design and relatively small sample size. The selection of patients from two referral centers in Isfahan may have introduced selection bias, as the characteristics of these patients are not necessarily representative of the broader breast cancer population. Moreover, the limited number of brain metastasis cases in categories of some predictors, resulting in wide confidence intervals and potentially unstable estimates in the both univariate and multivariable logistic regression model. These issues should be considered when interpreting the findings, and external validation using larger, population-based datasets is required to confirm the generalizability of the proposed scoring model. Besides, the present study was conducted retrospectively, so we have no information about the follow-up status of the participants, and some of them might develop brain metastasis in the future. Some variables predicting the development of brain metastasis were also not investigated as they were not included in the patients’ medical records. Some variables, e.g., the level of lymph node involvement, which can be significantly related to the incidence of brain metastasis, were also not investigated in this study.

Considering that the age of occurrence of breast cancer in Iran is lower than in other countries, the investigation of this cancer and the predictors of metastases, which can play an essential role in the prognosis of patients, is particularly important. Despite the limitations, the present study had strong points, such as investigating brain metastasis and determining its predictive factors in breast cancer patients at the main cancer treatment center in one of the provinces with a high prevalence of breast cancer. Additionally, HER2 and HR were investigated simultaneously and with other predictor variables in this study by multivariate analysis, revealing these receptors’ predictive role in brain metastasis. An attempt was made to incorporate a scoring system to predict the incidence of brain metastasis in this study, which could be more practical in this population by being close to the epidemiological characteristics of the Iranian population and also the NNM, which has been less investigated so far, was used to achieve this goal.

Conclusion

The present study findings demonstrated that patients with HER2+/HR- breast cancer and those under 40 years of age are at high risk of developing brain metastasis. The scoring model developed in this study demonstrated acceptable sensitivity and specificity for predicting the occurrence of brain metastases in women with breast cancer. However, given the hospital-based design and relatively small sample size, the estimates may be unstable and should be interpreted with caution. These findings suggest that the proposed scoring system may serve as a preliminary tool for risk stratification, while highlighting the need for further refinement and validation in diverse patient populations. These findings can assist oncologists and cancer therapists identify breast cancer patients at high risk of brain metastasis by employing methods such as advanced surveillance, risk-adapted brain MRI strategies, or inclusion in prospective trials (long-term endocrine therapy or targeted adjuvant low molecular weight drugs or preventive cranial radiotherapy.)
