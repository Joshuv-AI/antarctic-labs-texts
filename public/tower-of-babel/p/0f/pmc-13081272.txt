Association between temporomandibular disorders and sleep quality among undergraduate dental students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: İlhan Kaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTemporomandibular disorders (TMD) are common among undergraduate dental students and have been associated with sleep disturbances. Dental students may be particularly vulnerable due to the academic and clinical demands of their education. This study aimed to evaluate the association between TMD-related clinical signs and sleep quality among undergraduate dental students.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 210 undergraduate dental students classified as TMD-positive (≥ 1 clinical sign: TMJ pain, clicking, or functional limitation, n = 115) or TMD-negative (no clinical signs, n = 95). Students with a history of temporomandibular joint treatment, current orthodontic treatment, diagnosed psychiatric disorders, chronic systemic diseases, or regular use of medications affecting sleep were excluded. TMD status was determined based on clinical findings, including joint pain, clicking, and functional limitation. Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Total PSQI scores were analyzed as both continuous and categorical variables (cut-off > 5). Data were analysed using non-parametric tests and Chi-Square/Fisher’s Exact tests and associations with poor sleep quality (PSQI > 5) were examined using modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors to estimate aPRs (95% CIs) including a TMD×sex interaction (two-sided α = 0.05).ResultsPoor sleep quality (PSQI > 5) was more frequent among students with TMD than those without TMD (100% and 93.68%, respectively; p = 0.008) and in females than in males (100% and 92.86%, respectively; p = 0.004). PSQI scores increased across academic years and proportion of students classified as poor sleepers differed by year (p = 0.0008). In modified Poisson regression adjusted for sex, academic year and age, TMD was independently associated with poor sleep (aPR = 1.04, 95% CI 1.006–1.075; p = 0.019). A significant TMD×sex interaction indicated effect modification by sex; the association was evident among males (male-stratified aPR = 1.11, 95% CI 1.02–1.21; p = 0.015), while estimation within females was not feasible due to universal poor sleep.ConclusionsTMD-related clinical signs were associated with poorer sleep quality among dental students. These findings indicated that sleep disturbances and TMD-related symptoms frequently co-occur in this population. Given the cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be drawn, and longitudinal studies are warranted.Keywords: Pittsburgh sleep quality index, Sleep quality, Temporomandibular disorders

Background

Temporomandibular disorders (TMD) are a group of multifactorial conditions that affect the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), masticatory muscles, and the function of related structures. They have a complex aetiology and are clinically manifested by symptoms such as orofacial pain, joint sounds, and functional limitations [1–6]. TMD have been associated with impairments in physical function, pain-related disability, and psychosocial wellbeing, which together may contribute to reduced quality of life [2, 4, 5, 7, 8].

It is evident that university students, with a particular emphasis on dental students, constitute a high-risk demographic for the development of TMD [5, 9, 10]. The academic intensity, stress in clinical practice, extended working hours, and an educational environment requiring constant psychomotor attention have been identified as contributing factors to elevated psychological stress and the emergence of parafunctional habits (e.g., teeth clenching, bruxism) in these individuals [9, 10]. These factors play a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of TMD by creating chronic tension on the musculoskeletal system [11, 12].

However, sleep disorders have also been identified as a critical component in the symptomatology of TMD. Impaired sleep quality has been associated with lower the pain threshold, increase the inflammatory response, and negatively affect emotional regulation, leading to increased pain perception. Emerging evidence suggests that sleep disturbances may act bidirectionally, both exacerbating and being exacerbated by TMD-related pain through shared neurophysiological pathways [9, 13–15]. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) is a widely utilised and validated measure for the assessment of sleep quality in this context [16, 17].

Although the relationship between TMD and sleep disturbances has been examined in various populations, evidence among university students -particularly dental students, who are exposed to substantial academic and psychosocial stressors- remains relatively limited and heterogeneous. Existing studies suggest that sleep impairment may influence pain modulation and psychosocial wellbeing in individuals with TMD, but findings across student cohorts are not fully consistent. Therefore, evaluating this association within a homogeneous group of undergraduate dental students may offer additional insight into how TMD and sleep quality interact in a high-stress educational environment. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the relationship between TMD and sleep quality among undergraduate dental students. By investigating this association within a specific population that experiences high levels of academic and psychosocial stress, the study sought to contribute to a better understanding of how TMD may affect sleep patterns in young adults under intense academic education.

Methods

Study design and reporting

This observational and analytical cross-sectional study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for observational research [18].

Sample size calculation

No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because a census-based recruitment approach was used. All enrolled undergraduate dental students during the study procedure (N = 288) were invited. 266 students agreed to participate (92.4% participation rate), and after excluding 56 students based on predefined criteria/incomplete responses, 210 were included in the final analysis (72.9% of the total enrolled undergraduate dental student body).

Ethical approval and participant consent

The study was approved by the Bursa Uludağ University Faculty of Medicine Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2024/19, Decision Date: 20.02.2024). The purpose, scope, and principles of voluntariness of the research were explained to all participants; information was provided regarding confidentiality, anonymity and data usage. Written informed consent forms were obtained from the participants. Also, the research protocol was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles for medical research (involving human participants, including research using identifiable human material or data) of World Medical Association (WMA) Declaration of Helsinki [19].

Participants

The present study was conducted between April 2024 and February 2025. All undergraduate dental students (1st to 5th year) enrolled at Bursa Uludağ University Faculty of Dentistry (N = 288) were assessed for eligibility and invited to participate. 22 students declined participation and of the remaining 266 students who agreed to participate, 56 were excluded based on predefined exclusion criteria/incomplete responses (Fig. 1). Thus, the final analytic sample consisted of 210 participants (participation: 92.4% and analytic: 72.9%).

At the faculty where this study was conducted, the 1st and 2nd years correspond to the preclinical phase of the curriculum, during which students mainly receive basic medical and dental science education with limited or no direct patient contact. The 3rd year represents a transitional phase, combining preclinical laboratory courses with an introductory orientation to clinical practice (also includes clinical observation). The 4th and 5th years constitute the intensive clinical phase, in which students spend most of their time in the clinics with substantial patient care responsibilities and higher clinical workloads. In the present study, “class level” or “grade level” was dichotomized as the student’s current academic year (from 1st to 5th year) and was used as an indicator of curriculum stage and typical clinical exposure.

Examination procedure

Clinical examinations were carried out in a quiet, well-illuminated preclinical laboratory, with participants seated in an upright position. The examiner -an oral and maxillofacial surgeon experienced in temporomandibular assessment- had completed formal training in TMD clinical evaluation. For reliability assessment, the examiner evaluated the same 15 students on two separate occasions one week apart, using the same TMD screening criteria. Agreement between the two assessments was quantified using Cohen’s kappa (κ = 0.87), indicating excellent intra-examiner consistency. Although a test–retest design could provide additional information on temporal stability, this approach was not considered optimal for TMD-related clinical signs, which may fluctuate over short periods. Therefore, intra-examiner reliability was assessed using repeated examinations within a short interval to evaluate measurement consistency rather than clinical stability.

A detailed clinical examination was performed based on the following parameters.

Jaw pain: Presence of pain felt by palpation or spontaneously in the masseter muscle region,

Headache: Self-reported pain originating in the temporal region,

Joint sounds: Detection of clicking or crepitation sounds during mouth opening and closing by palpation or stethoscopic auscultation,

Mouth open locking: Difficulty closing the jaw after maximum opening,

Mouth open locking: Difficulty closing the jaw after maximum opening,

Mouth closed locking: Restriction or catching sensation during mouth opening movement.

Mouth closed locking: Restriction or catching sensation during mouth opening movement.

The presence of at least one of these five parameters was considered sufficient to classify the student as “TMD present” [TMD (+)]. Students who did not show any of these findings were classified as “TMD absent” [TMD (–)]. This operational definition was used as a pragmatic screening approach to identify students with TMD-related clinical signs in the educational setting and does not represent a formal DC/TMD diagnosis. These procedures reflect the standard TMD clinical screening methods used in the current institution and were informed by the DC/TMD recommendations, although a full DC/TMD diagnostic protocol was not implemented in this study [3].

Data collection tools and application procedure

Following the TMJ examination, the PSQI self-report scale was administered to all participants under standard conditions. To reduce measurement errors and ensure consistency of application, the PSQI scale was administered face-to-face by the same researcher in a quiet, distraction-free classroom.

Prior to this, the purpose of the study was explained to each participant and standard instructions on how to complete the forms were provided verbally. Participants completed the forms individually and without time constraints, and responses were collected anonymously. The average time taken to complete all scales was 20–25 min.

Assessment of sleep quality Using PSQI

PSQI was used to evaluate the subjective sleep quality and sleep disturbances of the participants over the preceding month [16]. The PSQI is a validated self-report instrument that is freely available for non-commercial academic research. The validated Turkish version of the PSQI was used in accordance with the original authors’ guidelines [20]. The scale consists of 19 items to be completed by the participants themselves and 5 additional items that can be completed by their spouse or roommate (these additional items are not included in the scoring). The self-report items are grouped into seven subscales: subjective sleep quality, sleep onset latency, total sleep time, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, medication use, and daytime dysfunction. Each subscale is scored from 0 to 3, with total scores ranging from 0 to 21; higher total scores indicate poorer sleep quality. A score of five or higher is widely accepted as denoting poor sleep quality. The validity and reliability study of the scale in Turkish was conducted by Ağargün et al. [20]. The optional five observer-rated items of the PSQI (completed by a bed partner or roommate) were not administered, as they are not required for scoring and were not applicable to the present student population.

Prior to the completion of the form, the researcher provided the participants with standard instructions, which were communicated verbally. Subsequent to the completion of the forms, a visual inspection was conducted to ascertain the presence of any missing or inaccurate markings. Participants who did not respond to any items were excluded from the subsequent analyses. The mean time taken to complete the PSQI form was ap-proximately 7–10 min. Additionally, higher PSQI scores indicate poorer sleep quality, whereas lower scores reflect better sleep quality. In accordance with the original validation by Buysse et al. [16], a PSQI score > 5 was used as the standard cut-off for defining poor sleep quality.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS v29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of continuous variables (age and PSQI) was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD, median and range; categorical variables as n (%). PSQI was analysed both as a continuous variable and dichotomized as poor sleep quality (PSQI > 5). Because PSQI and age (years) were not normally distributed across academic years, comparisons across ≥ 3 groups were performed using Kruskal-Wallis test, and two-group comparisons were performed using Mann-Whitney U test. Associations between categorical variables were examined using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Where relevant, effect sizes for chi-square tests were summarized using Cramér’s V.

Factors associated with poor sleep quality (PSQI > 5) were examined using modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors to estimate adjusted prevalence ratios (aPR) and 95% confidence intervals. Primary models included TMD status, sex, academic year, and age; an interaction term (TMD×sex) was additionally tested. Because poor sleep was observed in 100% of female students, sex-stratified prevalence ratio estimation was not feasible for females; therefore, sex-stratified regression was reported for males and adjusted for academic year. All tests were two-sided with α = 0.05.

Results

Age increased progressively across academic years and differed significantly between classes (Kruskal-Wallis test, H(4) = 188.64, p < 0.001). Specifically, mean ± SD (median; range) values were: Class 1, 18.41 ± 0.50 (18.0; 18.0–19.0); Class 2, 19.65 ± 0.48 (20.0; 19.0–20.0); Class 3, 20.60 ± 0.50 (21.0; 20.0–21.0); Class 4, 21.51 ± 0.51 (22.0; 21.0–22.0); and Class 5, 23.00 ± 0.82 (23.0; 22.0–24.0) (Table 1). The within-class variability was low (SD approximately 0.5–0.8), reflecting narrow age bands consistent with academic progression (Table 1). Sex-stratified analyses by academic year showed that female undergraduate dental students generally reported higher PSQI scores than males. Female-male difference was borderline in Class 1 (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.052), however became statistically significant in Class 2 (p = 0.005), Class 3 (p < 0.001), and Class 4 (p < 0.001). In Class 5, females had higher PSQI scores than males, with a modest but significant statistical difference (p = 0.021) (Table 1). In addition, in sex-stratified analyses, PSQI scores were significantly higher in students with TMD than in those without TMD for both females and males (both p < 0.001) (Table 1). Poor sleep quality (PSQI > 5) was observed in all female students regardless of TMD status (100% in both groups; p = 1.000). Among male undergraduate dental students, poor sleep was present in 100% of those with TMD compared with 88.68% of those without TMD. However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.080) (Table 1).

Discussion

The present study evaluated the association between TMD-related clinical signs and sleep quality among undergraduate dental students and yielded three main findings. First of all, poor sleep quality was highly prevalent in this population and was significantly associated with the presence of TMD. Second, sleep quality progressively worsened across academic years, suggesting a cumulative effect of academic and clinical demands. Third, a significant interaction between TMD and sex was observed, indicating that the association between TMD and poor sleep was evident among male undergraduate dental students, whereas estimation within females was limited by the uniformly high prevalence of poor sleep quality. These findings highlight the complex and sex-specific nature of the relationship between TMD and sleep quality in dental students.

This study was among the early investigations in dental undergraduate students using PSQI to compare students with and without TMD-related clinical signs. The observed worsening of sleep across academic years should be interpreted cautiously because no non-dental comparison group was included, and age/cohort effects may also contribute.

In this study, undergraduate students diagnosed with TMD had significantly higher PSQI scores compared to their peers without TMD. Impaired sleep quality is associated with pain, muscle tension, and stress-related neurophysiological changes, particularly in individuals with TMD. Although there are a limited number of studies in the literature showing that that TMD is associated with poorer sleep quality, similar findings have been reported [13–15]. However, not all studies have observed a clear association between TMD and poor sleep quality/outcomes [9]. Discrepant findings across the literature may reflect differences in study populations (clinical vs. student/community samples), the operational definition of TMD (screening signs vs. DC/TMD diagnostic criteria), and the measurement of sleep (subjective questionnaires vs. objective assessments). In the present work, the very high prevalence of poor sleep (particularly among female undergraduate dental students) likely reduced outcome variability (ceiling effect), which may also contribute to null findings in subgroup or dichotomized analyses. Studies evaluating sleep quality in patients with TMD report that more than half of TMD patients have poor sleep quality, revealing that subjective assessments of poor sleep are associated with increased clinical pain intensity and psychological distress [13, 21, 22]. On the other hand, impaired sleep quality has been proposed as a potential contributing factor to symptom persistence, although causal direction mostly remains unclear [13, 15, 23, 24]. Also, Cannatà et al. [7] examined the prevalence of TMD among Italian university students, and the relationship between emotional factors and parafunctional oral habits. In their study, which involved 321 students, it was reported that pain-related TMJ symptoms were observed in 37% of cases, while joint dysfunction was observed in 28% of cases. The prevalence of pain-related TMJ findings was found to be 2.5 times higher in females than in males. It was emphasised that this difference could be related to biological and psychosocial factors. The study demonstrated that students with TMD exhibited significantly elevated levels of anxiety and depression. Additionally, Oral Behavior Checklist scores demonstrated a strong correlation with both these psychological parameters and the presence of painful TMD. The researchers emphasized that TMD symptoms are closely linked not only to biomechanical factors but also to stress, anxiety, and parafunctional behaviours, and that this cycle is particularly pronounced in groups with high stress levels, such as university students. The present study, in congruence with the above-mentioned research, lends further credence to the notion that TMD is prevalent among university students, underscoring the significance of acknowledging the role of psychosocial factors in its etiology. It should also be noted that, in the current sample, the prevalence of poor sleep reached 100% among students with TMD when using the conventional PSQI > 5 threshold. This finding should be interpreted with caution. It may reflect the combination of a highly stressed dental student population, the sensitivity of the chosen cut-off, and potential sampling or response biases, rather than an absolute absence of good sleepers among all individuals with TMD.

From a literature perspective, the present study added to the growing body of evidence on the relationship between TMD and sleep quality in dental student populations. Recent scientific data have showed associations between TMD symptoms, sleep disturbances and psychosocial factors in university and dental student cohorts. Present findings contribute additional data from a large sample of Turkish dental students spanning all academic years, with a high participation rate, and simultaneously consider TMD-related clinical signs, sleep quality and sex differences within this educational context. Indeed, Alqarni et al. [10] evaluated the relationship between psychosocial stress factors and TMD symptoms in clinical dentistry students. In the study, 89 students completed the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System and Oral Health Impact Profile for Temporomandibular Disorders questionnaires. Analyses showed that female students had significantly higher levels of both TMD symptoms and psychological stress. The authors noted that increased higher anxiety scores were closely related to TMD symptoms, but the overall impact on oral health was mild in most participants. These results support the notion that psychosocial stress may be an important determinant of TMD symptomatology in dental students [10]. Tarevici et al. [25] evaluated the prevalence of TMD symptoms and associated psychosocial factors among dental students in Romania. Using self-report questionnaires based on the DC/TMD, data from 356 volunteer students were analyzed, and approximately 5% of respondents reported TMD-related pain (orofacial pain) in the past 30 days. Higher depression and anxiety levels were significantly associated with TMD symptoms. Ahuja et al. [26] investigated the correlation between stress and TMDs among Indian dental students (n = 450; age range 17–32, with most participants aged 21–25). They reported that stress was an important etiologic factor, and stress scores showed significant associations with certain TMD symptoms; however, gender differences in stress levels and overall TMD prevalence were not statistically significant. More recently, Gaikwad et al. [27] conducted a cross-sectional survey in 255 dental students and reported that 89.8% of participants had poor sleep quality according to the PSQI. In their analyses, poorer sleep quality was significantly associated with higher perceived stress levels, whereas the association between PSQI scores and TMD symptoms (FAI) was not statistically significant. These findings underscore the high burden of sleep disturbance in dental students and support the view that sleep impairment and psychosocial stress frequently co-occur in this population. Consistent with these findings, Gaş et al. [28] examined 699 Turkish dental students during the COVID-19 pandemic and reported high prevalence of TMD symptoms (77.5%). Although most participants were classified as having good sleep quality by the PSQI, higher depression, anxiety and stress levels were significantly associated with higher PSQI and FAI scores, indicating that psychological distress may coincide with worse sleep parameters and greater TMD severity in this population.

Similar to the current study, Zamani et al. [29] evaluated 150 patients with different TMD severity levels and 50 non-TMD controls using the PSQI and found that mean PSQI scores were significantly higher in all TMD groups than in controls, with sleep quality worsening as TMD severity increased. Also, the previous study conducted by Lee et al. [15], but performed on chronic TMD patients instead of dental students, strongly demonstrated the effect of TMD on sleep quality. The study compared sleep quality between 503 patients with chronic TMD and 180 healthy controls matched for age and gender, reporting significantly higher PSQI scores in the TMD group. Poor sleep quality was observed in 56.9% of TMD patients, compared to only 22.2% in the control group. Although there are differences in terms of sample size and study design, this finding is consistent with the results of the present study. Furthermore, it has been shown that individuals with TMD have a significantly higher risk of obstructive sleep apnea (STOP-Bang ≥ 3) and higher levels of daytime sleepiness (Epworth Sleepiness Scale ≥ 10). The authors emphasized that female gender, increased daytime sleepiness scores, and TMD-related headaches are the main determinants of poor sleep. These findings indicate that TMD is not only a local musculoskeletal disorder but also a multidimensional condition that affects sleep quality at a systemic level. Similarly, in the current study, the presence of TMD was found to be associated with impaired sleep quality, and this parallel supports the biopsychosocial impact model reported in the literature.

The extant literature also reports that the relationship between TMD and sleep disorders may involve a bidirectional mechanism. While it is generally accepted that sleep disorders can exacerbate chronic pain, recent literature has revealed a reciprocal relationship between TMJ related pain and sleep quality, thereby intensifying the cycle. A plethora of previous studies have revealed that individuals afflicted with painful TMD report a greater degree of sleep disturbances [9, 15, 29]. Research utilising self-report scales, such as the PSQI, has repeatedly indicated a substantial correlation between the presence of TMD and substandard sleep quality [13, 14, 30, 31]. However, Al-Jewair et al. [9] emphasised that this relationship should be interpreted with caution due to methodological differences, noting that many studies did not include matched control groups and relied solely on subjective assessments. Nevertheless, the extant data provide substantial support for the hypothesis that existing evidence suggests a strong association between TMD and poorer sleep quality. The need for longitudinal studies to elucidate the causal nature of this relationship is indicated [9].

Furthermore, sleep disturbances associated with TMD have been associated with additional parafunctional habits, such as bruxism. In their study, Ekici [32] evaluated the psychological status and sleep quality of 464 patients diagnosed with TMD, focusing on the presence of sleep bruxism. The study reported a significant increase in the incidence of sleep bruxism with increasing anxiety and depression levels. Furthermore, TMD patients who exhibited bruxism demonstrated lower sleep quality in comparison to those who did not. A number of significant differences were identified, particularly in the “sleep delay” and “sleep disturbances” subcomponents, in cases where TMD and sleep bruxism co-occurred. The authors emphasised that bruxism, characterised by non-physiological jaw muscle activity, has the potential to disrupt the restorative nature of sleep and result in muscle pain and fatigue upon awakening. Psychological factors and sleep disturbances have been linked to TMD-related symptoms and pain sensitivity, which may perpetuate a reciprocal pain-sleep cycle [13, 32]. Although the bruxism parameter was not evaluated in the present study, it should be considered that the decline in sleep quality associated with TMD observed in undergraduate students may also be related to such parafunctional activities.

The current results also highlight sex differences. Poor sleep quality was higher in females than males and interacted with TMD status. Hormonal factors, pain sensitivity, and emotional regulation may contribute to this pattern [11, 12, 15]. Because poor sleep was nearly universal among females, sex-related contrasts were less distinguishable in this high-stress setting; in males, TMD-related clinical signs appeared more discriminative for poor sleep.

This study had some limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the directionality of the TMD-sleep relationship. Second, sleep quality was assessed using the self-reported PSQI, which may be subject to response bias, and objective sleep measures (e.g., actigraphy or polysomnography) were not feasible. Third, the extremely high prevalence of poor sleep (particularly the 100% classification among students with TMD) may indicate reduced outcome variability, limiting the discriminative capacity of dichotomized analyses and regression estimates. Finally, TMD status was defined using a pragmatic clinical-sign screening approach rather than a full DC/TMD protocol, which may have introduced misclassification. Therefore, findings should be interpreted as associative and may not generalize beyond dental students.

Nevertheless, the present study also has certain strengths. Firstly, all clinical examinations of TMJ were conducted by a single researcher, thus ensuring the elimination of inter-observer differences. The cross-sectional design of the study enabled a direct comparison of changes in sleep quality associated with the presence of TMD. Moreover, the integration of students from all academic classes (from 1st to 5th year) facilitated the evaluation of individuals at disparate stages of dental education, thereby contributing to the generation of results that reflected changes associated with the academic year. Furthermore, the utilisation of the PSQI form, which has been validated in Turkish for sleep quality assessment [20], served to enhance the measurement validity of the data obtained. Finally, the exclusion of students undergoing psychiatric treatment, orthodontic treatment, or temporomandibular joint treatment served to reduce the effect of possible confounding variables and thereby increase the internal validity of the results.

Future studies should include longitudinal and prospective designs to clarify the direction of the relationship between TMD and sleep quality. Specifically, the question of whether sleep disorders trigger TMD development or whether TMD impairs sleep quality through pain and muscle dysfunction remains controversial and unresolved. Therefore, the combined use of objective sleep measurements (actigraphy, polysomnography, or wearable sensor technologies) and subjective scales will contribute to validating current findings. Additionally, the simultaneous assessment of psychosocial and behavioral variables such as stress levels, anxiety, and bruxism will enable a more comprehensive understanding of the effects of TMD on sleep through mediation models. Furthermore, considering gender-specific factors such as hormonal cycles, differences in pain thresholds, and changes in biological rhythms in future studies could deepen the biopsychosocial aspects of this relationship. At the clinical level, it may be beneficial to consider sleep hygiene education, stress management and behavioural strategies as adjuncts to TMD management, although further interventional studies are required to determine their impact on clinical outcomes.

The present work demonstrates that the presence of TMD and gender have significant effects on sleep quality in dental students. Since sleep quality is a critical determinant not only of individual quality of life but also of academic performance and overall health status, the current findings emphasize the importance of routinely assessing sleep quality in TMD evaluations and intervening when necessary.

Conclusions

Within the limitations, the present study found that both TMD-related clinical signs and sex were significantly associated with sleep quality among undergraduate dental students. Students classified as TMD (+) had a higher prevalence of poor sleep quality than those without TMD-related findings, indicating a strong association between TMD status and sleep disturbances in this cohort; however, the cross-sectional design does not allow any conclusions about the direction of this relationship. Sleep quality is a critical determinant not only of individual well-being but also of academic performance and clinical competence. In this context, it may be useful to routinely consider sleep-related factors when evaluating students with TMD-related symptoms and to provide support when poor sleep is identified. The present findings highlight the potential value of interdisciplinary approaches that incorporate sleep hygiene, stress reduction and behavioral strategies into broader TMD prevention and management, although further longitudinal and interventional studies are required to clarify their impact on clinical outcomes.
