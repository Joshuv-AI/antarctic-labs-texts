Long-term intensive forest management ultimately leads to imbalances in soil nutrient and extracellular enzyme stoichiometry
Authors: Cheng Huang, Lan Yao, Linyun Mou, Yuhua Ma, Songling Fu, Fasih Ullah Haiderd, Hua Liu, Xu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryLong-term intensive forest management ultimately leads to imbalances in soil nutrient and extracellular enzyme stoichiometry, making it crucial to understand microbially mediated nutrient cycling for sustainable practices. We investigated Carya cathayensis forests under three management intensities—no management (CK), extensive management (EM, annual understory clearance), and intensive management (IM, biannual understory clearance with fertilization)—implemented for five durations (0, 3, 8, 15, and 20 years). Management duration primarily drove changes in soil properties and enzyme activities, with intensified management amplifying these effects. Under IM, sucrase and β-1,4-glucosidase activities initially increased then declined, while acid phosphatase activity showed a similar trend. EM resulted in higher urease and protease activities than IM. Overall, microbial carbon and phosphorus were severely limited, particularly under prolonged IM. These findings suggest that retaining understory vegetation and applying phosphorus and carbon fertilizers more frequently may help maintain soil ecological function under long-term intensive management.

Introduction

As an essential component of forest ecosystems, soil plays a crucial role in regulating the decomposition of organic matter, transforming minerals, and maintaining the balance of nutrients between plants, microbes, and the environment.1,2,3 In conjunction with increased forest management activities, variable levels of land degradation have emerged worldwide, which significantly impacts the functioning of forest ecosystems. In particular, modifications in soil structure and properties resulting from land use have severely affected soil nutrient cycling, enzyme activities, and microbial functions.4,5,6 In recent years, considerable attention and effort have been devoted to reducing land degradation from forest management while maintaining the soil’s ecological service functions.7,8

To enhance forest productivity and generate more products (e.g., fruits, rubber, and timber), intensive forest management methods have been globally implemented.9,10 In China’s economic forests and timber plantations, various intensive forest management methods are commonly practiced, including fertilization, removal of understory vegetation, and deep tillage.11,12 Intensive management methods can enhance productivity and yield substantial economic benefits in the short term; however, prolonged intensive management may lead to imbalances in soil nutrients and even constrain soil microbial resources.13,14 Research has shown that specific measures applied to forest soils during forest management can alter the structure of soil aggregates and diminish the capacity of soil microbes to decompose organic matter. This disruption impacts nutrient balances within forest ecosystems, which subsequently translates to changes in the relative content of soil nutrients.15,16 Studies have suggested that sustained high-intensity forest management activities can progressively deplete soil organic carbon (C) and reduce levels of unstable C pools.8,17 Research findings from subtropical bamboo forests revealed that intensive forest management significantly reduced C, nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P) levels in the rhizospheric soil.12 Moreover, as forest management intensity increased, the transformation of soil C and N decreased. In boreal coniferous forests following vegetation removal, soil pH increased significantly, while MC, MBC, SOC, DOC, TN, and AN all decreased significantly.18 A meta-analysis revealed that sustained understory vegetation removal significantly reduces soil C and N stocks, available potassium, microbial biomass C, and fine root biomass, indicating that understory removal may substantially alter soil properties.19

Extracellular enzymes, as important catalysts utilized in the decomposition of complex organic matter by soil microbes, play a crucial role in nutrient cycling in the soil, and serve as key drivers of soil nutrient dynamics,20,21 Changes in forest management methods and their prolonged duration exert varying degrees of influence on the soil microbial biomass and extracellular enzyme activities.14 Research has shown that after 15 years of understory vegetation removal and fertilizer application (primarily urea and superphosphate), the activities of soil β-glucosidase and phosphatase significantly decreased. Yao et al.22 demonstrated that the activities of β-glucosidase and cellobiohydrolase are predominantly regulated by soil pH and water-soluble organic C content, and this regulatory effect intensifies with prolonged intensive management duration.23 In tropical Hevea brasiliensis plantations, monoculture cultivation and intensive forest management methods severely disturbed soil microbial communities and enzyme activities.24 Ecological enzyme stoichiometry can delineate the relationships between microbial nutrient demands and soil nutrient supplies. When soil microbes are deprived of specific nutrient elements, they boost the secretion of associated extracellular enzymes, thereby altering their stoichiometric ratios.25 In recent years, many studies have revealed the limitations in microbial resources and substrate quality in forest soils via changes in the stoichiometric characteristics of ecological enzymes,26,27 which provides a foundation for further elucidating the relationships between soil microbial metabolic constraints under intensive management and environmental variables.6

Intensive forest management alters vegetation characteristics and soil hydrothermal conditions, thereby affecting various soil properties and indirectly influencing the secretion of microbial extracellular enzymes.28 For instance, studies have found that moderate thinning intensity in forests can increase the input of exogenous organic matter into the soil, stimulate plant diversity, and ultimately enhance soil microbial diversity and extracellular enzyme activity, improving the turnover efficiency of soil nutrient cycling.29 Intensive forest management has both direct and indirect effects on soil biological processes, mediated through humus forms and topsoil pH. Research indicates that soil C-, N-, and P-acquiring enzymes in forest ecosystems are influenced by different microbial taxa. In Cinnamomum camphora plantations, soil C-acquiring enzymes and vector length are primarily regulated by bacterial communities. In contrast, N-acquiring enzymes, P-acquiring enzymes, and vector angle are mainly affected by fungal communities. The removal of understory vegetation increased fungal diversity, thereby alleviating P limitation.30 Furthermore, long-term application of N fertilizer in forest management may also regulate enzyme activities by altering soil N and P balances and influencing microbial resource limitations.31 Although current studies have identified patterns in changes in soil nutrients and enzyme activities during intensive forest management, the temporal dynamics of soil extracellular enzyme activities and their stoichiometric characteristics over long-term management remain poorly understood. Additionally, given the diversity of subtropical economic forests, targeted research is essential to develop optimized management strategies for specific types.

Carya cathayensis var. dabeishansis (C. cathayensis) is a unique genetic resource endemic to the Dabie Mountains of China, where extensive natural forests were discovered in the 1970s. Due to the rich nutritional content and superior taste of its fruits, it has been extensively cultivated over the last two decades.32 Long-term forest management methods have led to soil degradation in certain areas of C. cathayensis forests. However, the impacts of different management methods and their duration on soil nutrient characteristics and enzyme activities in these forests remain unclear, which is the focus of this study. We aimed to observe and elucidate the distinct attributes of soil nutrients and extracellular enzyme activities in this subtropical economic forest ecosystem, as influenced by varying forest management strategies and periods. This is the first systematic assessment of the long-term management of the ecological effects of the C. cathayensis forest. Our objectives were to: (1) investigate changes in the soil nutrients and extracellular enzyme activities in C. cathayensis forests under different management methods with increased duration, and (2) determine whether sustained forest management translates to nutrient limitations in the soils of these forests. We propose the following hypotheses: (1) under equivalent management durations, intensive management methods will induce more pronounced soil acidification than extensive management approaches; (2) prolonged management duration will lead to progressive depletion of soil organic carbon and phosphorus pools, thereby generating co-limitation of carbon and phosphorus nutrients on soil microbial metabolism; (3) soil acidification serves as the primary direct driver responsible for phosphorus limitation in soil microbial metabolic processes.

Discussion

The soil pH declined over prolonged management periods, with more pronounced decreases in IM forests than in EM forests. Several studies have suggested that heavy fertilization and high-intensity land use are the primary contributors to soil acidification,33 which may be induced by reduced soil nitrate concentrations due to increased leaching and surface runoff.13 Furthermore, frequent understory vegetation clearance in IM forests reduced vegetation coverage, exacerbating soil erosion. The substantial loss of base cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, K+, and Na+) may play a significant role in the more rapid decline in soil pH observed in these IM forests.13,34 Soil nutrient content, microbial biomass, and enzyme activities were highest in the topsoil (0–10 cm) and decreased with increasing soil depth. This phenomenon may have been attributed to the direct effects of fertilization, which significantly enhanced the concentrations of various topsoil elements. Additionally, the accumulation of plant residues following the removal of litter and vegetation further enriched the surface soil layer, thereby increasing topsoil organic matter content and intensifying microbial activity.14 Higher organic matter content increased the functional diversity of microbial decomposition in soil.12,35 With greater soil depth, the quantities of litter and plant residues within the soil decreased, and the leaching penetration of inorganic salts declined progressively. Consequently, soil nutrient levels decreased in deeper soils.36

The extracellular enzymes secreted by microbes and plant roots serve as vital catalysts in the transformation of organic compounds into inorganic ones.11,37 Compared to CK, IM forests increased microbial investment in P-acquiring enzymes while reducing investment in N-acquiring enzymes, thereby altering microbial nutrient acquisition strategies. In the early stage of IM forest management, the activities of soil SC, BG, and UE enzymes increased, likely because annual substantial fertilizer applications in IM forests replenished soil N, P, and K content to some extent. This increase in nutrients promoted soil microbial proliferation, enabling the secretion of more extracellular enzymes for the decomposition of materials.38 With prolonged continuous management, soil enzyme activity showed a moderate decline, which may be attributed to the following three factors. On the one hand, this could be due to the long-term removal of understory vegetation, which led to a decline in the quantity and quality of soil organic matter, thereby limiting microbial activity due to substrate constraints.39 On the other hand, deterioration of soil physical structure can reduce root and soil microorganism vitality, thereby decreasing the secretion of extracellular enzymes.40 Additionally, some studies have found that long-term excessive application of nitrogen fertilizer can lead to soil acidification, increase the accumulation of toxic substances, and exert harmful effects on microorganisms and plant roots, inhibiting their activity and enzyme production.41 For instance, in the intensive-managed subtropical bamboo forest, BG, cellulose hydrolase, UE, Pro, and ACP all decreased as soil pH decreased.23,42

Additionally, soil microbes thrive most robustly in environments with pH levels ranging from weakly acidic to weakly alkaline. The dramatic reduction in soil pH in IM forests constrained the propagation and activity of these microbes, thereby decreasing the secretion of extracellular enzymes.43,44 The secretion of extracellular enzymes is the primary mechanism through which soil microbes acquire limited resources. When access to these resources is restricted, extracellular enzyme exudation tends to increase.39 In this study, the soil UE enzyme activities in IM forests decreased significantly with prolonged management duration, which may have been attributed to two factors. On the one hand, the application of fertilizers in IM forests markedly increased N content, thereby reducing the difficulty microbes had in accessing it. This reduction in resource acquisition likely contributed to the observed decrease in soil UE enzyme activities.45,46 Conversely, soil UE enzymes are primarily secreted by soil microbes. Thus, decreased soil microbial biomass may be a significant factor contributing to reduced secretion.47 During short management durations (EM3 and IM3), the activities of SC and BG enzymes increased significantly, likely due to increased substrate availability for enzymatic reactions. Following the clearance of understory vegetation, a sizable quantity of plant residues remained, providing an abundant organic matter resource. This influx of organic material facilitated the proliferation of soil microbes, which, in turn, stimulated the production of enzymes for the decomposition of C.48,49 Soil enzymes, which are secretions produced by plant roots and microorganisms, are influenced by both microbial and plant diversity.50 Numerous studies have shown that higher plant diversity in subtropical economic forests promotes the activity of UE, SC, and soil nitrate reductase.10 Our previous research demonstrated a drastic decline in plant diversity with increasing management duration, which aligns with the observed decrease in soil enzyme activities over time in the present study.32 This reduction in enzyme activity is primarily attributed to diminished root exudates and litter input resulting from vegetation removal. Consistent with our findings, similar studies have reported lower levels of C-acquiring enzymes in soils under both extensive and intensive management of Phyllostachys edulis forests, which is believed to be associated with substrate limitation caused by reduced litterfall.42

As hypothesized, soil microbes experienced varying degrees of constraints on C and P availability across all stands. In IM forests, both VA and VL steadily increased with increasing management duration. This suggests that the dual restrictive effects of soil microbes on soil C and P contents may become more pronounced in these environments. The composition of fertilizers applied in the IM forests (N: P2O5: K2O = 13:5:7) indicated that N inputs far exceeded P inputs, thereby increasing the challenge for soil microbes to acquire P. Furthermore, the reduction in soil pH facilitated the fixation of AP as iron or aluminum phosphate compounds, thereby further diminishing P availability.51 The present study revealed that both management methods and duration exerted significant negative effects on soil pH (Figure 8A), indicating that long-term intensive management exacerbates soil acidification. A significant negative correlation between pH and VA was detected, providing robust evidence that lower soil pH intensifies microbial P limitation. These findings are consistent with results reported in similar studies.52 Additionally, this study observed a gradual decrease in soil AP content with increasing management duration, while ACP activity remained relatively constant. This phenomenon, documented in other similar studies, may be explained by microbial “P-mining” strategies and functional redundancy within the community.53 Specifically, Saiya-Cork et al.54 suggested that soil N enrichment leads to relative P scarcity, prompting microorganisms to “invest” more resources in phosphatase synthesis to mobilize P from organic pools. Moreover, soil microbial communities exhibit substantial functional redundancy, meaning that even if some microbial taxa decline due to management disturbances, other taxa with similar functions can compensate, thereby maintaining the stability of key ecological processes such as phosphatase production.55 The stoichiometric characteristics of soil enzymes suggested that IM forests may have imposed C constraints on soil microbes, potentially linked to soil properties and vegetation attributes. Zheng et al.44 found that changes in soil substrate quality and availability, as well as their alignment with current microbial energy allocation strategies and resource allocation toward enzyme production, can lead to significant alterations in EC:N. Since microbial growth relies primarily on C rather than N, the increasing trend in EC:N observed in the IM forests over time may be attributed to reduced understory vegetation. This reduction likely impedes microbial access to exogenous C, thereby prompting microorganisms to allocate more energy to producing C-degrading enzymes to acquire additional C sources for growth and metabolism.56 Furthermore, studies have indicated that soil bacteria respond more strongly to microbial elemental limitations than fungi.57 Whether this discrepancy further alters microbial communities’ investment strategies in resource acquisition remains unclear. Addressing this question requires further in-depth monitoring of soil microbial community dynamics, respiration rates, and growth rates, which is essential for advancing our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms underlying microbial resource limitation in soils. Previous studies have confirmed that the extensive application of N fertilizers during forest management has led to restrictions in both microbial P and C.58,59 P is an essential nutrient element for plant growth and metabolism. Rapid plant growth intensifies resource competition for P between plants and soil microorganisms. The results of this study indicate that the experimental area is characterized as a low-P region (Figure 1), where P limitation is widespread.10 Previous research has demonstrated that N addition promotes the growth of C. cathayensis in the Dabie Mountains, potentially exacerbating soil microbial P limitation, particularly in intensively managed forests.14

To sum up, forest management modes and their duration significantly impacted the physicochemical properties of soil in a C. cathayensis forest. The influence of prolonged management duration on soil nutrient content and physical properties was found to be greater than that of the various management methods. Increased forest management intensity magnified the effects of management duration on soil nutrients and extracellular enzyme activity. Short-term forest management methods are advantageous for enhancing soil nutrient content and stimulating nutrient cycling. Under prolonged management periods, the soil C and N pools of the C. cathayensis forest gradually became unstable.

In summary, under prolonged forest management periods, the soil C and N pools of the C. cathayensis forest gradually became unstable. Further, soil porosity and pH decreased with longer management durations, exacerbating compaction and acidification. Continuous forest management negatively affects soil enzyme activity; as operating time increases, both ACP and UE exhibit a downward trend. At the same time, their chemical characteristics suggested that soil microbial C and P limitations were prevalent in C. cathayensis forests. To maintain soil nutrient supply capacity and nutritional balance, alleviate microbial nutrient limitation, and mitigate soil acidification in C. cathayensis forests, the following three recommendations are proposed: (1) reduce the application of nitrogen fertilizers while increasing phosphorus fertilizers as well as organic or fermented farmyard manure to enhance soil organic matter content; (2) preserve understory shrubs and herbs, and introduce leguminous cover plants (such as Astragalus sinicus or Medicago sativa); after harvest, apply these materials as mulch to improve soil quality; (3) adopt soil formula fertilization based on soil testing and employ leaf diagnosis techniques to accurately identify nutrient deficiencies in both trees and soil, thereby enabling precise fertilization, avoiding resource waste, and preventing soil ecological imbalance caused by excessive fertilizer application.

Limitations of the study

Although our findings provide a comprehensive analysis of the dynamics of soil physicochemical properties, microbial biomass, and extracellular enzyme activities, they reveal, to some extent, the intensification of carbon and phosphorus limitations in soil under high-intensity management over time. However, certain limitations remain regarding spatial scale and mechanistic interpretation. Firstly, the influence of inter-annual climate variation on the interpretation of long-term trends was not considered, preventing further analysis of microbial nutrient limitation under climatic drivers. Secondly, the assessment of microbial resource limitation was based solely on the stoichiometric characteristics of soil enzyme activities, without incorporating studies on microbial community function and metabolic activity. A more holistic validation of microbial resource limitation is therefore lacking. For example, Zheng et al.27 found that when labile C dominates as a microbial C source, a higher EC:N ratio does not necessarily indicate more substantial microbial C limitation; instead, it may reflect greater labile carbon availability and higher microbial metabolic rates. Furthermore, although we selected forest stands with relatively consistent site conditions and stand characteristics for establishing permanent plots, the potential influence of spatial heterogeneity cannot be ruled out entirely. Therefore, future studies should include the deployment of environmental sensors to monitor climatic variables accurately; the application of laboratory incubation and metagenomic sequencing to analyze microbial growth rates; and the expression of functional genes related to nutrient cycling. Finally, the use of analytical methods such as geographically weighted regression could help mitigate the impact of spatial heterogeneity on experimental outcomes.

Method details

Study area

The study sites are located in the Dabie Mountain Forest Area, specifically in Tiantangzhai Town (115°50′28″E, 31°15′32″N) and Guanmiao Township (115°23′13″E, 31°27′57″N), both within LuAn City, Anhui Province, China. This region experiences a humid subtropical monsoon climate, with an annual average precipitation of 1300 mm (average summer and winter temperatures are 26°C and 2°C, respectively). The soil types are primarily Humic Cambisols (thicknesses range from 30 cm to 100 cm, pH ranges from 4.5 to 6.5).14,60 C. cathayensis dominates over 90% of the forest area in the tree layer vegetation, with minor components including Cunninghamia lanceolate, Fortunearia sinensis, and Liquidambar formosana. The understory vegetation consists mainly of Duchesnea indica, Erigeron annuus, and Aster lautureanus.

Sampling design

This study focuses on C. cathayensis forests. To ensure that differences in stand characteristics were primarily driven by management methods and their duration rather than pre-existing spatial variability, the site selection process adhered to stringent criteria to maximize initial environmental homogeneity. Candidate stands were first identified from historical forest survey data provided by the local forestry station and were required to meet the following conditions: (1) similar site conditions, including consistent parent rock, topographic position (lower to middle mountain slopes), slope (20-30°), aspect (northern slope), stand density, and canopy closure; (2) identical stand origins, being naturally regenerated forests following harvesting in the 1980s; and (3) comparable stand ages (35-40 years) and initial species composition. The final selection of target stands from this pool was further validated through field visits to confirm soil texture similarities and to verify uniform management methods with precisely documented initiation times.

To investigate the temporal dimension of management, we established a chronosequence representing continuous durations of sustained management. This approach substitutes spatial variation for temporal sequence, allowing us to study the effects of management over time. The selected stands represented management histories of 0 years (unmanaged control, CK), 3 years, 8 years, 15 years, and 20 years of continuous management application. It must be explicitly clarified that the time span indicated (e.g., IM3) refers to the annual and consistent implementation of the corresponding intensive management methods throughout the entire duration (i.e., over the 3 years until sampling), rather than indicating that the management operation was conducted only once within that interval.

Following definitions from prior studies on forest management, methods for C. cathayensis forests were categorized into three types: unmanaged (CK), extensive management (EM), and intensive management (IM). The specific measures for each were as follows. CK forests preserved the original stand composition, with only the seasonal collection of mature C. cathayensis seeds and no other anthropogenic disturbances. EM forests involved the removal of almost all competing tree and shrub species except C. cathayensis, followed by annual understory vegetation clearance once before the nut harvest in late August, with no fertilization. IM forests also removed non-target trees and shrubs. Still, they implemented more intensive interventions: application of compound fertilizer (N:P2O5:K2O = 13:5:7) at 375 kg ha-1 in May each year, understory vegetation clearance twice annually (in July and late August), and application of a commercial organic fertilizer (45% organic matter, primarily fermented sheep manure) at 1500 kg ha-1 in late September.

To minimize spatial autocorrelation and ensure statistical independence, 45 independent experimental plots (20 m × 20 m) were established, with five replicates for each of the nine management-duration combinations (CK, EM3, EM8, EM15, EM20, IM3, IM8, IM15, and IM20) (Below table). All plots were situated on northern slopes at a distance of over 100 m from roads and farmland. Critically, the edge distance between any two plots was maintained at greater than 200 m, and the distance between the broader forest areas containing these plots exceeded 300 m, with each forest area spanning more than 2 hectares. Sample collection

In July 2022, on summer mornings with continuous sunny weather for three consecutive days (from 8 to 10 O'clock), soil samples (0-10 cm, 10-20 cm, and 20-30 cm) were randomly sampled in each stand of C. cathayensis forests five times. From each soil layer, five sub-samples were collected using an auger (inner diameter: 3.5 cm) and combined to form a composite sample. A total of 135 soil samples (after removal of fine roots and stones) were collected from nine stands (three layers × five plots × nine stands), which were sealed and transferred to the laboratory in refrigerators at 4°C.

Laboratory analysis

We followed the methodologies of Feng et al.61 and Huang et al.14 to determine soil physicochemical properties and enzyme activities. Soil samples were divided into two portions in the laboratory. The fresh subsamples were required to be analyzed within 48 hours for total soil protease (Pro), sucrase (SC), acid phosphatase (ACP), β-1,4-glucosidase (BG), microbial biomass C (MBC), microbial biomass N (MBN), and microbial biomass P (MBP). The other portion was used for measuring soil bulk density (BD) and moisture content (MC) using the cutting ring method, and soil porosity (SP) was determined using the specific gravity method. After air-drying and sieving through a 100-mesh sieve, the samples were analyzed for soil urease (UE) activity, pH, electrical conductivity (EC), soil organic C (SOC), total N (TN), total P (TP), total potassium (TK), alkali-hydrolyzable N (AN), available P (AP), and available K (AK) contents. Soil pH was measured in a 1:2.5 (w/v) soil-water suspension using a pH meter (Mettler Toledo, FE28-Standard, Switzerland). EC was determined in a 1:5 (w/v) soil-water suspension at 25°C using a conductivity meter (Mettler Toledo, FE38-Standard, Switzerland). Soil TN was measured using the Kjeldahl method. SOC content was determined using the potassium dichromate oxidation method with external heating. AN was measured using the alkali hydrolysis diffusion method. TK and AK were determined using a flame photometer (SHERWOOD, M410, United Kingdom). TP and AP were determined using the molybdenum-antimony anti-spectrophotometric method. MBC, MBN, and MBP were determined following the chloroform fumigation extraction method, and microbial biomass concentration was calculated using the difference between nonfumigated and fumigated soils.60 The activities of Pro, SC, ACP, BG, and UE were assayed using commercial reagent kits (ADS-W-D008, ADS-W-TR007-96, ADS-W-TR008, ADS-W-TR003-96, and ADS-W-TR001-96, respectively; all kits provided by Aidisheng Biology Technologies Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China). Before enzyme activity assays, reagents were sequentially added and mixed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The mixed samples with the respective enzyme substrates were pipetted into calibrated plates using a pipette gun, incubated in a constant temperature incubator, and finally measured using a microplate reader set at the corresponding wavelengths (Pro: 366 nm, SC: 540 nm, ACP and BG: 405 nm, and UE: 578 nm). Enzyme activities were expressed in units of micromoles of substrate converted per gram of dry soil per day (U g-1). Standard curves with an R2 value greater than 0.999 were considered acceptable for the enzyme activity assays.

A vector angle (VA, °) < 45° suggests the degree of N limitation for soil microbes, while a vector angle (VA, °) > 45° specifies the degree of P limitation for soil microbes. The experimental data were initially organized using Excel 2019, and descriptive statistics for soil characteristics were calculated using SPSS 26.0. The statistical analyses of soil physicochemical properties and enzyme activities were conducted using a multivariate ANOVA, and the differences were assessed using LSD. We employed linear mixed models with duration as a fixed effect and plot as a random effect to determine the impact of continuous management duration on soil physicochemical properties and enzyme activities. Pearson correlation analysis and the Mantel test were employed to quantify correlations among soil nutrients, enzyme activities, and their stoichiometric relationships. Graphs were plotted in R 4.1.3 using the “ggplot2”, “vegan”, “dplyr “, and “ggcor” packages. Based on the key factors affecting soil enzyme stoichiometric characteristics, the hypothesized pathways of forest management methods and duration on soil enzyme stoichiometric vector characteristics were analyzed using structural equation modeling (SEM) in AMOS 24.0.
