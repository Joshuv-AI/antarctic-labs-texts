Molecular basis for the inhibition of de novo DNA methylation by TCL1A
Authors: Qingting Liu, Jinhong Li, Xiaoxiao Wang, Yaozong Li, Yu Wu, Zhuo Han, Zixin Guo, Li Guo, Xiang Wang, Gang Yuan, Zheng Gao, Lei Li, Dong Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDNA methyltransferases DNMT3A/B mediate de novo DNA methylation, essential for embryonic development and cell fate determination. Dysregulation of DNMT3A/B causes developmental defects and tumorigenesis. TCL1A is critical for embryogenesis but promotes lymphomagenesis when deregulated. Previous studies suggested TCL1A binds DNMT3A/B and inhibits their activity, but the mechanism remained unclear. Here, we report the cryo-EM structure of the DNMT3A-TCL1A complex, which comprises a DNMT3A dimer bound by two TCL1A dimers. TCL1A interacts with the catalytic domain of DNMT3A, overlapping with the DNMT3L-binding site, and induces extended conformational rearrangements. The target recognition domain and catalytic loop shift markedly, reducing DNA accessibility, while the catalytic loop occupies the SAM-binding pocket, thereby blocking methyltransferase activity. Supported by biochemical assays and molecular dynamics simulations, we propose a dynamic inhibition mechanism in which TCL1A exploits DNMT3A conformational plasticity to suppress de novo DNA methylation.Subject terms: DNA methylation, Electron microscopy, Enzyme mechanisms

Introduction

DNA methylation, an essential epigenetic modification, is vital for regulating gene expression, genome stability, and cell fate decisions1. DNMT3A and DNMT3B (the major de novo DNA methyltransferases), which are stimulated by the nonenzymatic paralog DNMT3L2,3, add a methyl group to the cytosine (C5) position in the CpG dinucleotide to establish de novo DNA methylation4,5. DNMT3A and DNMT3B are highly conserved and structurally similar in that both contain an N-terminal Pro-Trp-Trp-Pro (PWWP) domain, a central ATRX–DNMT3–DNMT3L (ADD) domain for histone recognition and a C-terminal catalytic methyltransferase domain (CD, also known as the MTase domain)6. DNMT3 also shows diverse expression patterns at different developmental stages and in different tissues. Deletion mouse models of Dnmt3a and Dnmt3b demonstrate that they are both critical for embryonic development with subtle differences in phenotypes. Dnmt3L also plays an important role in establishing maternal imprinting in mice4,7–9.

Aberrant DNA methylation due to mutations in DNMT3A and DNMT3B is closely associated with the onset and progression of various diseases, especially cancer10,11. DNMT3A mutations are associated with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and Tatton-Brown-Rahman syndrome12–14. In particular, the R882 hotspot mutation of DNMT3A causes aberrant DNA methylation by facilitating polymerization, contributing to the pathogenesis of AML15–17. Interestingly, introducing DNMT3B-converting mutations in DNMT3A enhances substrate accessibility, thereby eliminating the dominant negative effect of the DNMT3A R882 mutation in cells18. DNMT3B mutations are associated mainly with immunodeficiency, centromeric instability, and facial anomalies (ICF) syndrome (ICF)19–21. For DNMT3B, ICF syndrome-associated mutations affect its DNA-binding and heterochromatin-targeting abilities, resulting in decreased DNA methylation levels in cells20. These studies provide a mechanistic understanding of DNMT3A/B-mediated DNA methylation and its dysregulation in disease10.

TCL1 (T-cell leukemia/lymphoma 1) is an oncogene initially identified in T-cell leukemia/lymphoma22. In the following years, several TCL1 family members were identified, including three members (TCL1A, TCL1B, and MTCP1) in humans and seven members (Tcl1a/Tcl1, Tcl1b1, Tcl1b2, Tcl1b3, Tcl1b4, Tcl1b5, and Mtcp1) in mice. The expression of TCL1 family members is primarily detected in early embryos, germ cells, and precursor/immature T and B lymphocytes23. Notably, overexpression of TCL1A has been implicated in the pathogenesis of T-cell and B-cell lymphomas23. High levels of TCL1A expression are associated with more aggressive disease characteristics and poorer patient survival24. A mouse model of human chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) was established by targeted expression of TCL1A, suggesting that TCL1A and other genes in its pathway play important roles in the pathogenesis of B-CLL25,26. In addition, aberrant activation of the TCL1A gene in clonal hematopoiesis promotes stem cell expansion, and enforced expression of TCL1A promotes the expansion of human hematopoietic stem cells in vitro and mouse HSCs in vivo, indicating that TCL1A plays an essential pathological role in the development and progression of hematologic tumors27. TCL1A is also expressed in oocytes and early embryos. Knocking out the ortholog of Tcl1 in mice leads to embryonic arrest, indicating the essential roles in the early embryonic development28,29. Recently, we reported that in human oocytes and early embryos, the maternal factor NLRP7, a member of the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor (NLR) family, sequesters TCL1A in the cytoplasm and blocks its ability to inhibit DNMT3-mediated DNA methylation30. Pathogenic mutations in NLRP7 disrupt the interaction between NLRP7 with TCL1A, causing TCL1A aberrant nuclear accumulation. This alteration may facilitate the interaction of TCL1A with DNMT3A, leading to loss of DNA methylation at maternal imprinted loci. This molecular disruption contributes to the pathogenic features of NLRP7 mutations, such as recurrent hydatidiform moles (RHMs)31,32.

However, the underlying molecular mechanism by which TCL1A inhibits DNMT3 activity is largely unknown. Here, we report the cryo-electron microscopy structure of DNMT3A-TCL1A. Combined with biochemical assays, molecular dynamics simulation investigations, and methylation sequencing, we elucidate the inhibitory mechanism of TCL1A.

Results

TCL1A inhibits the activities of DNMT3A and DNMT3B in vitro

To verify the interaction between TCL1A and DNMT3, we overexpressed flag-tagged TCL1A and performed the co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays in HCT116 cells. TCL1A coimmunoprecipitated endogenous DNMT3A and DNMT3B (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). In addition, the exogenously expressed DNMT3A/3B, but not DNMT3L pulled down TCL1A (Fig. 1a, b). Given the deregulated expression of TCL1A in lymphomas33, we selected Daudi cells to determine the interaction between endogenous TCL1A and DNMT3A/B26. The results showed that TCL1A could also be captured by endogenous DNMT3A/B (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d), a finding that is consistent with a previous report26. Furthermore, using domain mapping analysis, we identified that the CDs of DNMT3A/B interact with TCL1A (Fig. 1c).

To investigate the effect of TCL1A binding on DNMT3A/B’s activity, we reconstituted the DNMT3ACD-TCL1A (Fig. 1a, and Supplementary Fig. 1e, f) and DNMT3BCD-TCL1A complexes (Fig. 1a, and Supplementary Fig. 1g, h). Both complexes with a molecular weight of approximately 150 kDa exhibited similar monodisperse behavior following gel filtration. Next, DNA methylation assays were performed in vitro (Fig. 1d, e). Consistent with a previous report34, DNMT3L dramatically increased the DNMT3A-mediated DNA methylation activity (Fig. 1d). In contrast, DNMT3ACD-TCL1A exhibited extremely low activity, even compared with that of DNMT3ACD alone. Furthermore, DNMT3L did not enhance the activity of the DNMT3A-TCL1A complex, while TCL1A slightly inhibited DNMT3A-DNMT3L activity (Fig. 1d). TCL1A bound to both DNMT3A and DNMT3B within their complexes with DNMT3L, supporting its inhibitory role (Supplementary Fig. 1i, j). Although TCL1A inhibited DNMT3A and DNMT3B to a similar extent, DNMT3L partially alleviated TCL1A-mediated inhibition of DNMT3B (Fig. 1e). Consistently, DNMT3L failed to bind DNMT3A in the DNMT3A-TCL1A complex but retained binding to DNMT3B in the DNMT3B-TCL1A complex (Supplementary Fig. 1i, j). These results suggested that TCL1A inhibits the activity of DNMT3A and DNMT3B simultaneously.

Overall structure of the DNMT3A-TCL1A complex

The activity of DNMT3 is also allosterically regulated by two N-terminal domains, the PWWP and ADD domains35,36. The PWWP domain recruits DNMT3 enzymes to H3K36-methylated chromatin within gene bodies and intergenic regions, promoting transcriptional fidelity and stabilizing splicing37. The ADD domain recognizes the histone H3 tail with unmethylated H3K4 to release the autoinhibitory state of DNMT3A38. Therefore, we selected the reconstituted DNMT3A-TCL1A (DNMT3AT1-TCL1A) complex, which contains the PWWP and ADD domains (Fig. 1a), for structural investigation. DNMT3AT1-TCL1A exhibited similar behavior to that of DNMT3ACD-TCL1A following gel filtration (Fig. 2a). We further solved the structure of DNMT3AT1-TCL1A (Supplementary Fig. 2). The 3.4-angstrom resolution density enabled the identification of the CD and ADD of DNMT3A. Although DNMT3AT1 contains a PWWP domain (Fig. 1a), it was not defined due to the absence of corresponding density. In addition, the density of the lateral molecules of dimeric TCL1A is incomplete. However, this finding did not affect our analysis of the interaction pattern between DNMT3A and TCL1A or the dimerization of TCL1A (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3, Supplementary Table 1).

DNMT3A-TCL1A, which is constructed from two innerl DNMT3As and two outer dimeric TCL1As, forms a linear structure similar to that of DNMT3a-DNMT3L and DNMT3A-DNMT3B7,39. Two dimeric TCL1As bind to the CDs of two DNMT3As (Fig. 2b, c). According to the calculation by PISA40, the interface between DNMT3A and TCL1A is 1043.6 Å2. TCL1A binds to the outward helixes 8, 10, and 11 of DNMT3A via extensive interactions, especially the polar contact pairs (Ser89-Asp740, Asp16-Arg729, and Tyr79-Glu733) (Fig. 2d). Interestingly, three aromatic residues (Phe731, Phe732, and Phe772) of DNMT3A construct a hydrophobic hole into which Trp19 and Met75 of TCL1A insert (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). The alanine substitutions of these residues of TCL1A reduce the interaction between TCL1A and DNMT3A (Fig. 2g). Notably, the interaction interface between DNMT3A and TCL1A is highly conserved on DNMT3B (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Mutations of TCL1A also impaired the interaction with DNMT3B (Fig. 2h). The DNMT3A dimer interface between two inner DNMT3A molecules is constructed by several charged residues, His873, Asp876, Asn879, Arg882 and Arg885. Notably, Arg882 forms hydrogen bonds with Met880, Asp876, and Asn879, thereby contributing to DNMT3A dimer stabilization (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 4c).

In addition, the ADD of DNMT3A directly interacts with the CD, which blocks the DNA binding of DNMT3A, resulting in an autoinhibiting state (Supplementary Fig. 5b)38. However, compared with the previously reported crystal structure, the contact between the ADD and CD of DNMT3A in DNMT3A-TCL1A results in structural changes. In DNMT3A-TCL1A, the negatively charged residues of ADD (Asp529, Asp530, and Asp531), which typically form hydrogen bonds with histone H3, are attracted to the polar residues (Lys844 and Gln846) of the CD (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 4d). This interaction causes a shift of approximately 9 Å in the ADD and a movement of approximately 13 Å for Asp531 (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Based on the structural changes observed in the ADD, we sought to investigate whether the binding of TCL1A affects the ability of DNMT3A to bind histone H3. To this end, we conducted an in vitro binding assay to measure the interaction between DNMT3AT1-TCL1A/DNMT3AT1-DNMT3LCD and the H3K4me0 peptide (Supplementary Fig. 5c). A comparison of the binding between DNMT3A-DNMT3L and H3K4me0 revealed that TCL1A did not affect the binding of DNMT3A to histone H3. Notably, TCL1A showed dramatic inhibitory activity against both DNMT3A and DNMT3B in vitro, suggesting that the interaction of TCL1A with the catalytic domain of DNMT3A/3B directly affects its DNA methylation activity.

TCL1A inhibits DNMT3 methyltransferase activity through allosteric regulation

Interestingly, DNMT3A alone has low DNA methylation activity and exists in an oligomerization state, similar to the linear assembly of DNMT3A-DNMT3L18,39,41,42. Our biochemical study detected the DNA methylation activity of DNMT3A alone. However, the activity of DNMT3A-TCL1A was almost undetectable (Fig. 1d), indicating that additional structural interpretation should be considered.

To further elucidate the inhibitory mechanism of TCL1A on DNMT3, we performed a series of structural comparisons. The binding of TCL1A affects the intramolecular interactions between the ADD and CD (Supplementary Fig. 5b), whereas the binding of DNMT3A does not alter the dimeric conformation of TCL1A (Supplementary Fig. 5d)41. We further analyzed and compared the interaction interfaces either between the DNMT3A-TCL1A and DNMT3A-DNMT3L complexes in three different states, including the autoinhibitory state, the DNA-free state, and the DNA-bound state, or between the DNMT3A-TCL1A complex and the DNMT3A-DNMT3B complex (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Fig. 3)7,38,39,42.

Superposition of one of the catalytic domains in the DNMT3A-TCL1A and DNMT3A-DNMT3L revealed that the CDs adopt an identical conformation with an RMSD of 1.01 Å over 226 Cα atoms. TCL1A occupies the DNMT3L binding site of DNMT3A, suggesting that TCL1A may inhibit DNA methylation by preventing the formation of DNMT3A-DNMT3L (Fig. 3). Moreover, the interface of DNMT3A-TCL1A differs from the DNMT3A-DNMT3L interaction pattern without the conserved π-π interaction, although TCL1A occupies a similar region as DNMT3L (Supplementary Fig. 6a, c and e). This interface of DNMT3A-TCL1A also differs from the DNMT3A-DNMT3B interface (Supplementary Fig. 6g). Notably, the DNMT3A-TCL1A complex adopts a less compact conformation than both the DNMT3A-DNMT3L and DNMT3A-DNMT3B complexes, exhibiting an approximately 5 Å extension (Fig. 3a–c). This conformational change is also observed in comparisons with cryo-EM and other crystal structures containing the DNMT3A dimer (Fig. 3a–c), indicating the extension of dimeric DNMT3A occurs upon interaction with TCL1A. Therefore, the interaction between TCL1A and DNMT3A allosterically induces such an extension.

This conformational change further resulted in significant rearrangements of the DNA-binding motifs in the dimerized DNMT3A of the DNMT3A-TCL1A complex. Notably, previous studies suggested that DNMT3A-DNMT3L prefers to recognize DNA with two CpG islands linked by 14 bp DNA42. Therefore, DNMT3A-TCL1A may lose its ability to bind DNA due to the structural extension, especially the DNA fragment used in the in vitro DNA methylation assay. Previous studies have shown that the DNMT3A-DNA interaction is mediated by three motifs: a loop (Arg831-Phe848) from the TRD (penetrating the major groove of the DNA for intermolecular contacts), the catalytic loop (Gly707-Lys721, intercalating into the vacated DNA cavity), and the homomeric interface of DNMT3A to form hydrogen bonds with the DNA backbone42,43. In the DNA-free state, the TRD loop and catalytic loop of DNMT3A-DNMT3L are invisible2,18,38. However, DNMT3A-TCL1A in the DNA-free state exhibits discontinuous densities near the corresponding positions, which aids in constructing the model of the TRD loop and catalytic loop (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). Due to density quality limitations, we avoided analyzing the interactions between the TRD and catalytic loops of DNMT3A-TCL1A and the surrounding residues, focusing on their different conformations from those of the DNA-bound state.

Further structural comparison revealed that the binding motifs with DNA in the DNMT3A-TCL1A complex differ from those in the DNMT3A-DNMT3L-DNA complex (Fig. 3c–h)42. First, the binding of TCL1A with DNMT3A resulted in a slight expansion of helices 8, 10, and 11 of DNMT3A (Fig. 3d). The Ser714, Ile715, and Val716 residues of the catalytic loop participate in the formation of hydrogen bonds with DNA in the DNMT3A-DNMT3L-DNA complex42. However, this catalytic loop of the DNMT3A-TCL1A complex flips out, embedding the SAM binding sites and staying away from the DNA (Fig. 3e, f and Supplementary Fig. 4g). Compared with the TRD loop of DNMT3A-DNMT3L in DNA-bound state, the TRD loop of DNMT3A-TCL1A also shifted approximately 3.5 Å (Fig. 3g, Supplementary Fig. 4h). In DNMT3A-TCL1A, Arg882 turns over into the dimer interface of DNMT3A. It forms hydrogen bonds with Met880, Asp876 and Asn879, resulting in a 1.8 Å movement (Figs. 2e, 3h and Supplementary Fig. 4c). In contrast, Arg882 plays a crucial role in DNA binding and forms an intramolecular hydrogen bond with Ser837 to stabilize the TRD loop when DNMT3A is bound to DNA, whereas Arg882 does not engage in the hydrogen bond formation for DNMT3A dimerization in other states complexes (Supplementary Fig. 6b, d, f, and h)7,38,39,43.

In summary of these findings, we speculate that DNMT3A-TCL1A reduces the ability to bind both SAM and DNA. To verify our speculations, we detected DNMT3ACD-TCL1A binding to SAM and DNA, respectively. Consistent with the previously reported data42, the binding affinities (kd) for DNMT3ACD-DNMT3LCD with SAM and DNA were 2.06 μM (Figs. 3i) and 3.92 μM (Fig. 3j), respectively. However, both the SAM binding and DNA binding affinities of DNMT3ACD-TCL1A were undetectable (Fig. 3i, j). Consequently, TCL1A affects the binding of DNMT3A to SAM and DNA simultaneously.

Considering the sequence and structural similarities between DNMT3A and DNMT3B (Supplementary Fig. 5a), we speculated that TCL1A inhibits DNMT3B in a similar pattern. After the superposition of DNMT3A-TCL1A with DNMT3B-DNMT3L-DNA20,44, we found a similar extension between the DNMT3 dimer, and the catalytic loop may also occupy the binding site of SAM (Supplementary Fig. 7a–d). Therefore, we compared the SAM and DNA binding capacities of the DNMT3BCD-DNMT3LCD and DNMT3BCD-TCL1A complexes. As expected, the binding of SAM and DNA of DNMT3BCD-TCL1A was not detected, whereas the binding affinities of SAM and DNA to DNMT3BCD-DNMT3LCD were 8.44 μM and 4.38 μM, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 7e, f). Consequently, TCL1A binding functions as an allosteric inhibitor to regulate the activity of DNMT3.

Allosteric activation and inhibition mechanisms of DNMT3A revealed by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

Indeed, DNMT3L and TCL1A have opposite regulatory effects despite their binding to a similar region of DNMT3A. These results indicate differences in the allosteric regulation of DNMT3A by the two regulators. We generated four different systems (DNMT3A-apo1 and DNMT3A-apo2 systems, DNMT3A-DNMT3L system, and DNMT3A-TCL1A system) and performed the MD simulations on DNMT3A under different conformational and binding states to validate the opposite regulatory effects of the two proteins (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Data 1–8). Each system was simulated for 500 ns per trajectory with five independent replicates, and the cluster analysis was based on the combined trajectories.

Briefly, the 2.5-μs MD simulation on the DNMT3A-DNMT3L complex (DNMT3A-DNMT3L system) was performed, and observed that the interface between the two proteins was tightly packed, mainly through hydrophobic contacts (Fig. 4a). The catalytic loop of DNMT3A stayed in an active conformation throughout the simulations and was poised for DNA engagement. This stability was highlighted by Tyr724, which anchored the loop between helices from both proteins and promoted the conformational change that constructs the SAM-binding pocket (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Movie 1). When DNMT3L was removed, and DNMT3A was simulated in isolation (DNMT3A-apo1 system), the catalytic loop exhibited more significant fluctuations than the DNMT3A-DNMT3L system (Fig. 4a, 1.5 Å vs. 6.1 Å), indicating a loss of stability in the absence of DNMT3L (Supplementary Movies 1 and 2).

In contrast, the DNMT3A-TCL1A complex adopted an inhibitory conformation, with the catalytic loop extended to block the SAM-binding site (Supplementary Movie 3). Unlike the DNMT3A-DNMT3L complex, our 2.5-μs MD simulations showed that the catalytic loop in DNMT3A-TCL1A was not stably maintained (9.8 Å in Fig. 4c vs. 1.5 Å in Fig. 4a). Instead, it remained highly flexible, sampling a wider range of conformations than in the apo form of DNMT3A (DNMT3A-apo2 system) (Fig. 4c, d and Supplementary Movie 4). Trajectory analysis suggested that the catalytic loop dynamically switches contacts between the C-terminal tail of TCL1A and the TRD loop of DNMT3A, which further increased its mobility compared with the DNMT3A-DNMT3L complex. In particular, the catalytic loop behaves like a “seaweed” waving in solution when TCL1A binds to DNMT3A. This extended loop also limited TRD loop movement, leading to reduced mobility of the TRD loop (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Movie 3).

Our structural and MD analyses of DNMT3A-TCL1A suggest a dynamic inhibition mechanism. Unlike the stabilized conformation observed in the DNMT3A-DNMT3L model, this inhibitory DNMT3A-TCL1A state consists of a range of conformations in which the catalytic loop remains flexible but persistently blocks the SAM-binding site (Fig. 4c–e and Supplementary Fig. 8). Consistently, the average SAM binding site volume in DNMT3A-TCL1A is notably smaller than that in the DNMT3A-DNMT3L system (13.4 Å³ vs. 158.9 Å³), supporting the notion that conformational plasticity underlies this dynamic inhibition.

Overexpressing human TCL1A alters the DNA methylation pattern during the mouse naive ES differentiation process

In mouse models, inactivation of DNMT3a leads to leukemias and lymphomas, including CLL45. Notably, targeted expression of TCL1A in B cells successfully induces the CLL transgenic mouse model25, suggesting that TCL1A may bind mouse DNMT3a and have a conserved inhibitory effect on its activity. The mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) act as a classic model for studying the activity of DNMTs42. During the transition of mESCs to their differentiation lineage, formative pluripotent stem cells (fPSCs), the level of DNA methylation increases from 10–20% to 70–80%, which partially mimics the de novo DNA methylation of post-implantation embryo1,46,47. Therefore, the ability of TCL1A to inhibit DNA methylation was investigated using whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) analysis of mESCs and fPSCs48.

We established the gene-editing mESCs lines with inducible overexpression of DD-Flag tagged wild-type TCL1A or TCL1AW19A-M75A, and further induced differentiation into fPSCs (referred to as the fPSC-TCL1A and fPSC-DMTCL1A, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 9a–d)49,50. Of note, the W19A-M75A double mutations at the DNMT3-binding interface of TCL1A exhibited reduced interactions with both DNMT3A and DNMT3B in vitro (Fig. 2g, h). The PLA demonstrated the interaction between TCL1A and DNMT3 in the nuclear (Fig. 5a). And the co-IP further confirmed that wild-type TCL1A interacted with endogenous DNMT3a/b, whereas the TCL1AW19A-M75A showed impaired binding (Fig. 5b).

As the WGBS results showed, the fPSC-TCL1A control transited from mESC without Shield1 exhibited a normal DNA methylation level, reaching around 75%. Once Shield1 was added to enrich TCL1A, the fPSC-TCL1A clones failed to acquire a hypermethylated state (25% in fPSC-TCL1A6# and 50% in fPSC-TCL1A). Notably, the induced TCL1A expression levels in these two clones exhibited a negative correlation with their respective DNA methylation levels. (Supplementary Fig. 9c, d). Subsequently, we generated an additional Dnmt3a/b double knockout cell line (referred to as fPSC-DKO) to disrupt the de novo DNA methylation (Supplementary Fig. 9e and Fig. 5c). The fPSC-DKO clone also exhibited a lower DNA methylation level (~20%) than that of fPSC-TCL1A clones without Shield addition, supporting the role of DNMT3a/b during the transition from mESCs to fPSCs. Notably, the fPSC-DMTCL1A clone displayed an equal DNA methylation level (75%) compared with that of the fPSC-TCL1A without an additive. Therefore, the overexpression of TCL1A inhibits de novo DNA methylation during the transition. (Fig. 5c).

We further analyzed the methylomes of different cell lines. Globally, fPSC-TCL1A, similar to fPSC-DKO had more hypomethylated regions (<10% methylation rate) and fewer hypermethylated regions (>50% methylation rate) (Fig. 5d). Moreover, methylation levels in specific genomic regions in fPSC-TCL1A across the genome, including CpGI, the gene body, intergenic region, repeat elements, and promoters, are also dramatically declined, similar to those in fPSC-DKO, while fPSC-DMTCL1A and control fPSCs present global hypermethylation (Fig. 5e–j). All of these results indicate that nuclear TCL1A inhibits the genome-wide reestablishment of DNA methylation by suppressing DNMT3a/3b activity.

Discussion

Our investigation provides the molecular basis for the inhibition of de novo DNA methylation by TCL1A. Under pathological conditions such as CLL and RHM, overexpression and abnormal localization of TCL1A lead to impaired DNA methylation26,30. Therefore, reducing the expression or changing the localization of TCL1A is a potential therapeutic strategy. However, it is still unclear whether this inhibitory function of TCL1A occurs under physiological conditions. Physiologically, TCL1A is primarily expressed in early embryos and immature T/B lymphocytes28,33,48. Therefore, TCL1A may have a slight regulation on the activity of DNMT3A/3B during the early embryonic development and the maturation of lymphocytes. The physiological function of TCL1A in early embryonic development and B-cell differentiation remains to be further studied.

On the other hand, various studies suggest that TCL1A is a multipotent regulator. By shuttling between the cytoplasm and nucleus, TCL1 regulates the proliferation of early mouse embryos28,29. The cleavage of embryos in TCL1-deficient mice is hampered at the 8-cell stage, although these embryos normally exhibit major differentiation features until compaction28. The cytoplasmic retention of TCL1A may be functionally independent of the inhibition of DNMT3A during oogenesis. The other concern is that TCL1A is involved in several signaling pathways23. TCL1 enhances the phosphorylation of downstream targets through its interactions with AKT, thereby promoting cell proliferation51,52. TCL1 also regulates essential signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT/mTOR and NF-κB pathways, through interactions with multiple proteins (e.g., ATM, HSP70, and TP63 etc.)53–55.

Notably, both TCL1A and DNMT3 proteins exhibit strong conservation among mammals (Supplementary Fig. 9f, g), suggesting that their interaction may also be evolutionarily maintained. This potential conservation implies a fundamental role of the DNMT3-TCL1A interaction in epigenetic regulation during mammalian development. According to our structural insights, TCL1A and DNMT3L share a similar binding interface for DNMT3A. However, differences in the allosteric regulation of inner DNMT3A determine whether DNMT3A activity is inhibited or enhanced. DNMT3L also has an ADD domain, which is another important regulator of the catalytic activity of DNMT3A56. The structural investigation of the DNMT3A-TCL1A complex revealed the inhibition mechanism of TCL1A and helped us understand in depth the underlying mechanism by which DNMT3L activates DNMT3A instead.

Moreover, our MD simulations were limited to dimeric (truncated DNMT3A-DNMT3L) and trimeric (truncated DNMT3A-TCL1A) systems due to computational constraints, and therefore do not capture the full dynamics of the DNMT3A tetramer, particularly at the interfaces within the oligomer. Nevertheless, our structural and biochemical data consistently demonstrate that DNMT3L and TCL1A exert distinct regulatory effects on the DNMT3A’s activity. Future studies taking advantage of enhanced computational resources will enable a more comprehensive characterization of the catalytic loop dynamics within the full oligomeric assembly.

Methods

Plasmid construction

The human DNMT genes, including DNMT3A (UniPort ID: Q9Y6K1), DNMT3B (UniPort ID: Q9UBC3), DNMT3L (UniPort ID: Q9UJW3), and TCL1A (UniPort ID: P56279) were purchased from Sino Biological. All plasmid construction was conducted using the CloneExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The DNMT3AT1 (residues 220–912 of DNMT3A) and DNMT3B with a C-terminal strep tag, TCL1A and DNMT3L with an N-terminal 10× His tag were subcloned and inserted into modified pFastBac1. The DNMT3AT1, full-length DNMT3B, and DNMT3L were subcloned and inserted into the PCAG vector with an N-terminal flag tag as well. The TCL1A was inserted into the PCAG vector with an N-terminal strep tag. The C-terminal truncations of DNMT3A (DNMT3ACD, residues 628–912 of DNMT3A) and the DNMT3BCD (residues 571–853 of DNMT3B) were subcloned and inserted into a modified pET15b vector with a 6× His -SUMO tag and a pGEX-6p-1 vector. The DNMT3LCD (residues 178–379) and TCL1A (WT and mutants) were inserted into a modified pET21a vector with a C-terminal strep tag.

Protein expression and purification

The Bac-to-Bac system (Invitrogen) was used to express DNMT3AT1, DNMT3L, DNMT3B, and TCL1A in SF9 cells. After 48–72 h of infection, the SF9 cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in binding buffer (100 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM EDTA) containing protease inhibitors (1 mM phenylmethylsulfonylfluoride, 0.8 μM aprotinin, 2 μM pepstatin A, and 5 μg/ml leupeptin). After cell disruption using a high-pressure cell disrupter at 4 °C, the lysate was centrifuged at 27,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was incubated with Strep-Tactin XT resin (IBA Lifesciences, Germany) for 30 min at 4 °C. After washing with binding buffer, the proteins were eluted with binding buffer containing 50 mM biotin (BBI, cat. no. A600078). The eluted sample was further purified using a heparin column (GE Healthcare) and subjected to size-exclusion chromatography (Superose 6 Increase 10/300GL; GE) in a buffer containing 25 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 2 mM DTT. The DNMT3AT1-TCL1A complex from the peak fractions was concentrated to 2 mg/ml for EM sample preparation and stored at −80 °C.

To reconstruct DNMT3AT1-DNMT3L complex, DNMT3AT1-TCL1A complex, DNMT3B-DNMT3L complex, and DNMT3B-TCL1A complex for Strep-pull down assay, the corresponding viruses were mixed and added to SF9 cells. After 48–72 h of infection, the SF9 cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in lysis buffer (25 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl) containing protease inhibitors as mentioned above. After disruption and centrifugation, the supernatant was passed through Ni-NTA(Qiagen) resin 2 times, followed by washing 4 times with 30 mM imidazole (Sigma-Aldrich, 288-32-4) in lysis buffer. The resin was eluted with lysis buffer containing 300 mM imidazole. The elution underwent additional purification using Source 15Q (buffer A: 25 mM Tris 8.0, buffer B: 25 mM Tris 8.0, 1 M NaCl). The peak fractions were concentrated for size-exclusion chromatography (Superose 6 Increase, 10/300GL, GE) in 25 mM Tris 8.0, 150 mM NaCl. Finally, the peak fractions of each complex were collected and aliquoted for subsequent experiments.

To reconstruct DNMT3ACD-DNMT3LCD complex, DNMT3ACD-TCL1A complex, DNMT3BCD-DNMT3LCD complex, and DNMT3BCD-TCL1A complex, all proteins were expressed independently in the Escherichia coli strain BL21(DE3, Invitrogen, EC0114) and mixed in pairs. The cells were cultured in LB medium at 37 °C until the OD600 reached 0.6–0.8, followed by induction with 0.2 mM IPTG for 16–18 h at 18 °C. Harvested cells were centrifuged and resuspended in lysis buffer containing 1 mM PMSF. Protein complexes were purified using Strep-Tactin XT resin. The eluted sample was further purified using a heparin column (GE Healthcare) and subjected to size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (25 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT). For ITC and MTase-Glo methyltransferase assays, proteins were digested with Drice protease before purification using ion exchange chromatography.

For the purification of DNMT3ACD and DNMT3BCD, the supernatant was incubated with Ni-NTA at 4 °C for 30 min. The resin was rinsed with lysis buffer containing 20 mM imidazole and eluted with lysis buffer containing 200 mM imidazole. After digestion with Drice for 16–18 h at 18 °C, the eluate was further purified using heparin HP (GE, 17-0407-03) and size-exclusion chromatography (Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL, GE).

Cell lines and cell culture

Sf9 cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 11496015) were maintained in SIM SF expression medium (serum-free; Sino Biological; MSF1). HEK-293F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific; R79007) were cultured in SMM 293-TII expression medium (serum-free; complete medium; Sino Biological; M293TII). The HCT116 cells (HyCyte, TCH-C185) were cultured in the high-glucose DMEM base medium (Thermo Fisher; 11995065) supplemented with 10% of fetal bovine serum (FBS; Invitrogen). Daudi cells (Procell, CL-0072) were cultured in 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher; A1049101) with 10% FBS. For transfection, Sf9 cells were transfected with Cellfectin™ II reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 10362100) to generate baculovirus. HEK293F cells were transfected with polyethylenimine (linear; MW 25000; Polysciences; 23966), and HCT116 cells were transfected with Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher; L3000015).

Pull-down assay

For the interaction interface site-directed mutagenesis His-pull-down assay, the supernatants containing DNMT3ACD and DNMT3BCD were aliquoted, and equal amounts of purified WT or mutant TCL1A were added. The mixtures were incubated for 2 h at 4 °C, followed by 1 h incubation with Ni-NTA resin at 4 °C. The resin was washed with lysis buffer containing 20 mM imidazole, and bound proteins were eluted with lysis buffer containing 200 mM imidazole. Input and eluate fractions were analyzed by western blotting.

For the series of DNMT3L and TCL1A with DNMT3A/B complex Strep-pull down assays, equal concentrations (2 μM) of purified complexes were incubated with increasing concentrations of competitor proteins (2, 8, 16 μM TCL1A or DNMT3L) at 4 °C for 3 h. Mixtures were then incubated with Strep XT resin at 4 °C for 1 h, followed by five washes with 100 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.05% Triton X-100. Bound proteins were eluted with 100 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 50 mM biotin. Input and eluate fractions were analyzed by western blotting.

MTase-Glo methyltransferase assay

The MTase-Glo Methyltransferase Assay kit (Promega) was used to measure the activity of DNMTs (DNMT3ACD and DNMT3BCD). According to the instructions, the reaction system was optimized to 12.8 μL containing 0.5 μM DNMTs,10 μM (S-adenosyl methionine, SAM), and 2 μM DNA (CG: AATTCGAAAAAATTTTTTCGAATT) in buffer (25 mM Tris pH 8.0, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 5% glycerol, 0.2 mg/mL BSA). 5 μM DNMTs, DNMTs- TCL1A proteins were preincubated with or without 20 μM DNMT3LCD, and 5 μM DNMT3ACD-DNMT3LCD complex were preincubated with or without 75 μM TCL1A. The proteins were added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, 3.2 μL of 5× methyltransferase-Glo reagent was added and allowed to react at 30 °C for 30 min. Finally, 16 μL of methyltransferase-Glo detection solution was added, and the reaction was carried out at 30 °C for 30 min. Luminescence detection was performed using a microplate reader (Synergy H1; Biotech). All the reactions were performed in triplicate, and all the data were analyzed using Graphpad 8.0.2.

Isothermal titration calorimetry

The binding affinities of DNMT3A with various ligands were measured using isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) on a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC Control Software (v.1.41) (Malvern) at 25 °C. The interactions analyzed included: H3 peptide (residues 1-12: ARTKQTARKSTG; final concentration: 1 mM) binding to DNMT3AT1-TCL1A and DNMT3AT1-DNMT3LCD heterodimeric complexes (final concentration approximately 20 μM); and a DNA duplex containing (GAC)₈ repeats (200 μM) or S-adenosylmethionine (SAM, 200 μM) binding to purified heterodimeric DNMT3A/BCD-associated heterodimeric complexes (final concentration approximately 20 μM). All proteins and substrates were prepared in ITC buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl). Each titration consisted of 19 injections with a spacing of 120 s and a reference power of 5 μcal/s. The ITC curves were processed with software MicroCal PEAQ-ITC Analysis Software (v.1.41) using a one-site fitting model. All titrations were performed in triplicate, and the binding affinities (Kd) are reported as the mean ± S.D. from three independent experiments.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

DNMTs and TCL1A were cloned and inserted into the pCAG vector and cotransfected into HEK293F cells with polyethylenimine (plasmid: PEI = 1:3) or into HCT116 cells with Lipofectamine™ 3000. After 48–72 h of transfection, the cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in the lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. After 5 cycles of repeated freezing and thawing, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation. The supernatant was incubated with anti-Flag affinity resin (Sigma, A2220) for 1 h at 4 °C. The resin was rinsed with wash buffer (lysis buffer containing 0.05% Triton X-100) and was eluted with elution buffer (lysis buffer containing 0.4 mg ml−1 of flag peptide). The supernatant (input) and eluate (immunoprecipitation) were analyzed by western blotting. For endogenous immunoprecipitation in Daudi cell, lysates from Daudi cells were precipitated with Pierce™ Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (ThermoFisher Scientific, 88802) by using anti-DNMT3A (PROTEINTECH, 20954-1-AP, for WB 1:2000, for immunoprecipitation 1.4 μg for 2 mg of total protein lysate), anti-DNMT3B (PROTEINTECH, 26971-1-AP for WB 1:2000, for immunoprecipitation 1.4 μg for 2 mg of total protein lysate) antibodies. Isotype immunoglobulin G (IgG) was used as a negative control: rabbit IgG (Beyotime, A7016).

Western blotting

All protein samples were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were incubated in blocking solution (5% nonfat milk in TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. After washing, the proteins in the membranes were detected by using an ECL western blotting substrate kit (BL520A; Biosharp) and images were captured using a ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA).

The antibodies used for western blotting as following: rabbit-anti-Tcl1(abcam; ab108978, 1:2000), DYKDDDDK tag Monoclonal antibody (PROTEINTECH; HX1988; 1:5,000), mouse anti-Strep tag (Huaxingbio; HX1988; 1:5,000), mouse anti-His tag (Huaxingbio; HX1988;1:5,000), HRP-conjugated rabbit anti-β-Actin mAb (ABclonal, cat AC028, 1:10000)., goat-anti-mouse IgG H&L (HRP) (ZenBio; 511103, 1:5000), and goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) (ZenBio; 511203, 1:5000).

Proximity Ligation Assay (PLA)

The proximity ligation assay was performed using Duolink® PLA Fluorescence kit (rabbit plus probe DUO92002, mouse minus probe DUO92004, detection reagents DUO92013, washing buffer DUO82049, Sigma-Aldrich), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the cells were fixed in 2% PFA and 0.1% Triton X-100 at RT for 30 min, and permeabilized in Enhanced Immunostaining Permeabilization Buffer (Beyotime, P0097) at RT for 3 h. Then, the samples were blocked with Duolink® Blocking Solution in a humidified chamber at 37 °C for 1 hours. At the end of the incubation, the samples were transferred into the primary antibody buffer supplemented with Duolink® Antibody Diluent at 4 °C overnight. The cells were washed with PBST (PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100) and then incubated with the probes mixture in 37 °C humidity chamber for 1 h the next day. Subsequent to washing the samples with 1×Duolink® washing buffer A, ligation attached to the PLA probes was performed for 45 min, and amplification was carried out for 100 min at 37 °C in the dark. After several times washes with 1×Duolink® washing buffer B and 0.01×Duolink® washing buffer B, the cells were stained with DAPI (Beyotime, C1006), and images were captured with a Zeiss LSM 880 Confocal Microscope. The following antibodies were used: mouse-anti-DNMT3a (Santa Cruz, sc-373905, 1:200); mouse-anti-DNMT3b (IMGENEX, IMG-184a, 1:200); DDDDK-Tag Rabbit mAb (ABclonal, AE092, 1:500).

EM sample preparation and data acquisition

Fresh DNMT3AT1-TCL1A complexes were purified for EM sample preparation as described above. Glow discharge (15 mA, 50 s) was performed using Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 grids (Au300, Quantifoil®). A Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI) was used for EM sample preparation with the conditions set to 4 °C and 100% humidity. A 300 kV Titan Krios microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a K2 Summit detector (Gatan) and the software EPU2.5 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at Sichuan University was used for data collection. The details are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Single-particle data analysis

Data processing was performed using CryoSPARC4.1.257. A total of 1777 movies were collected and processed for correction motion and contrast transfer function estimation. Using the blob picker, a total of 2,226,401 particles were automatically picked, and particles were Bin2 extracted with a box size of 300 pixels. After several rounds of 2D classification, ab-initio reconstruction, and heterogeneous refinement, a low-resolution map was generated to create templates. Utilizing the templates derived from this map, an additional 1,762,146 particles were automatically picked and Bin2 extracted. Following multiple rounds of 2D classification, ab-initio reconstruction, and heterogeneous refinement, a refined map was reconstructed from 225,251 particles. These particles were then unbinned and extracted for precise map reconstruction. Ultimately, 143,015 particles were used for homogeneous refinement and local refinement, resulting in a map with a resolution of 3.16 Å.

We performed 3D classification using a mask focused on the density of the ADD and TCL1A, which resulted in the generation of two improved maps. However, during model building, the density of the ADD domain and TCL1A was found to be fragmented. The initial 2,226,401 raw particles obtained from the blob picker were classified as “Pool” particles and evenly divided into four subgroups, while 225,251 high-quality particles from the template picker were classified as “seed” particles. A round of seed-facilitated 3D classification was performed, and after removing duplicate particles, we obtained 517,036 high-quality particles. These particles were unbinned and extracted with a box size of 300 pixels. Heterogeneous refinement was performed using the two maps as references, leading to the identification of two distinct classes of particles. Subsequently, a further 3D classification with a mask applied to the density of the ADD domain and TCL1A was conducted, and after local refinement, we obtained a map with a resolution of 3.36 Å.

Model building and refinement

The atomic structures of DNMT3A and TCL1A were individually generated using Alphafold2 and then docked into cryo-EM with ChimeraX 1.458–60. Manual adjustments were then made to the models using COOT 0.9.8.161. Each model was refined against the cryo-EM map and validated with the real-space refinement tool in PHENIX 1.20.162.

Models for molecular simulations

Four molecular systems were constructed to investigate the protein dynamics in the active and inhibitory conformational states of DNMT3A. These systems include the DNMT3A-DNMT3L dimer (named in DNMT3A-DNMT3L), the monomeric DNMT3A (named in DNMT3A-apo1) in its active conformation, the DNMT3A-TCL1A trimer (named in DNMT3A-TCL1A), and the monomeric DNMT3A (named in DNMT3A-apo2) in its inhibitory conformation. Detailed information regarding the model building process is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

System preparation

We take the dimer system as an example for the detailed presentation, and the other systems follow the same procedure. The protonation states of protein residues were determined at the neutral pH conditions via ProPka63, and hydrogen atoms were added by the CHARMM program64. The dimer systems were solvated in a 130 Å rhombic dodecahedron (RHDO) TIP3P water box to ensure 12 Å buffer space between the macromolecular atoms and the boundary of the water box. To neutralize the system and mimic the physiological conditions, Na+ and Cl− ions at 0.15 M concentration were added to the solvated systems65.

Minimization equilibration and MD simulations

Each simulation system was initially minimized for 10,000 steps under a series of restraints and constraints on the solute atoms to release its bad contacts and poor geometry. The minimized structure was heated to 300 K and equilibrated in NVT condition (constant volume and temperature). Finally, the structure was further equilibrated in the NPT condition (constant pressure and temperature). All the equilibration phases lasted for 10 nanoseconds (ns) using the CHARMM program (version 42b2)64. Production runs of 500 ns each were carried out in NPT conditions using the NAMD program (version 2.14)66. The pressure was controlled by the Nosé-Hoover Langevin piston method with a 200-picosecond (ps) piston period and 100 ps piston decay time67. The temperature was maintained at 300 K using a Langevin thermostat with a 5-ps friction coefficient. The integration time step was set to 2 fs by constraining all the bonds involving hydrogen atoms using the SHAKE algorithm. Van der Waals energies were calculated using a switching function with a switching distance from 12 to 14 Å, and electrostatic interactions were evaluated using the particle mesh Ewald summation (PME) method68. The CHARMM36m force field was used for proteins69. Five independent runs with random initial velocities were carried out for each system for a total of 2.5 microseconds (µs). MD snapshots were saved every 20 ps along the MD trajectories for further analysis. Geometric measurements, e.g., distance, dihedrals, root mean square deviation (RMSD), and contact map analysis, were performed with CHARMM routines.

The SAM-binding site volumes of the DNMT3A-TCL1A and DNMT3-DNMT3L systems were analyzed using MDpocket70. The binding site was defined based on the position of SAH in the crystal structure 4U7P38. To ensure accurate localization, only the DNMT3A domain was superposed onto the first frame of the corresponding trajectory for each system. Pocket volumes were then sampled using the Monte Carlo algorithm with 2,500 iterations per frame, while all other parameters were kept at their default settings.

Constructing mouse embryonic stem cell lines (mESCs) and inducing formative pluripotent stem cells (fPSCs)

The mESCs (CMT line) were maintained in N2B27/2i/Lif medium, which included N2B27 medium supplemented with 1 μM PD035901 (LC Laboratories; Cat# P-9688), 3 μM Chir99021 (LC Laboratories; Cat# C-6556), and 1 × 103 units/mL hLIF (human leukemia inhibitory factor; Millipore; Cat# ESG1107). The CRISPR/Cas9 system was used to construct Dnmt3a/Dnmt3b double knockout (DKO) mESC. The guide RNAs (Dnmt3a, 5′-GGACCCTGCTTCTCCGACTG-3′; Dnmt3b, 5′-GAGGAGTGCATTATCGTTAA-3′) were subsequently subcloned and inserted into the PX459 vector. Both vectors with a Cas9 expression vector were electrotransfected into mESCs. The positive clones were subsequently screened in N2B27/2i/Lif media supplemented with G418 for 1 week. The positive clones were identified by PCR and Sanger sequencing.

To construct the inducible TCL1A expressing mESCs (mESC-TCL1A), a destabilizing domain (DD) was fused with the N-terminus of Flag-TCL1A and cloned and inserted into a modified piggyBac transposon vector. Next, the mESCs were electrotransfected. The positive clones were screened in 2i/Lif medium with G418 for 1 week. The clones of cells, expressing TCL1A after induction with Shield1 (MCE), were selected for further research.

fPSCs were generated as previously reported48. Briefly, on day 0, the mESCs were digested into single cells. Then, the cells were suspended and mixed with an equal volume of Matrigel (BD; Cat# BD356230). The cells were plated in a dish and further incubated for 1 h at 37 °C until the Matrigel solidified. The cells in Matrigel were subsequently cultured in prewarmed fPSC induction medium (N2B27 medium supplemented with KSR, activin A, and bFgf2) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 3 days. To induce the expression of Flag-hTCL1A, the small chemical Shield1 at a final concentration of 0.5 μM was added to the fPSC induction medium on day 0. On day 3, the fPSCs were harvested.

Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) and data processing

The genomic DNA of mESCs and fPSCs cultured in 6-well plates was isolated with a DNA isolation mini kit (Vazyme, DC112). Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing was conducted, following the instructions of the bisulfite kit (Vazyme, EM101) and library kit for bisulfite conversion and library preparation (Vazyme; NE103), sequentially. The libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq X plus platform to generate 150-base-pair paired-end reads, achieving an average sequence yield of 20 gigabases per sample. Each experimental condition was represented by two biological replicates to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the data. The quality of the raw sequencing reads was assessed using FastQC v0.11.9. Adapter contamination and low-quality bases were subsequently trimmed from the reads using Trim_galore v0.6.10 with default parameters. The processed read pairs were aligned to the mm9 mouse genome assembly using Bismark v0.22.3. Postalignment, the deduplicate_bismark command within Bismark was employed to identify and remove PCR duplicates, thereby optimizing the dataset for downstream analyses. Methylation states were extracted from deduplicated reads using MethylDackel v0.6.1. Following a consistency check between replicates, methylation profiles were combined to improve coverage depth and resolution by aggregating C/T counts at each CpG site.

For genomic segment analysis, the genome was segmented into continuous intervals of 100 base pairs (bp), 1 kilobase (kb), or 1 megabase (Mb). On average, a minimum coverage criterion of three CpG sites per 100 bp was applied. The methylation level within each bin was determined as the ratio of methylated cytosines (C) to the sum of methylated and unmethylated cytosines (C + T) across all CpG sites within the interval. When comparing overexpressed samples to controls, only bins covered in both conditions were included in the analysis. The total methylation score for each interval was calculated as the sum of the methylation levels across all the CpG sites within that region. All analyses were performed using custom LINUX and R scripts, which are available upon request to facilitate verification and replication of our results.
