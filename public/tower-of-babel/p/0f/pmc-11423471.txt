Continuous sampling of healthy and mastitic quarters of lactating cattle by ultrafiltration after intramammary ceftiofur hydrochloride administration
Authors: Danielle A Mzyk, Jennifer L Halleran, Hannah J Sylvester, Claire B Giles, Megan E Jacob, Ronald E Baynes, Derek M Foster
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPharmacological activity of intramammary drugs depends on adequate drug concentrations within the cistern, but sampling is often limited. Insight into the active drug concentration within the mammary cistern may assist in determining effective and appropriate therapeutic decisions for cows being treated for mastitis.ObjectiveEvaluate the disposition of ceftiofur hydrochloride administered intramammary in diseased and nondiseased quarters. Whole milk and ultrafiltrate sampling techniques were compared.AnimalsTen mature, late lactation Holstein (n = 9) and Jersey (n = 1) dairy cows (422‐670 kg) with naturally occurring clinical mastitis, producing between 1.4 and 15.9 kg/day of milk.MethodsUltrafiltration probes were placed in both mastitic and healthy quarters. Each quarter was treated with 2 doses of 125 mg ceftiofur hydrochloride suspension, and whole milk and milk ultrafiltrate samples were collected. Ceftiofur concentrations in composite whole milk and milk ultrafiltrate were analyzed.ResultsThe maximum concentration of ceftiofur was higher in ultrafiltrate samples, but no differences were identified in healthy or mastitic quarters. The use of ultrafiltration probes provides a novel technique for free drug concentrations within the mastitic and healthy bovine mammary gland.Conclusions and Clinical ImportanceSignificant inter‐ and intracow variability and lower daily milk weights may overestimate ceftiofur concentrations available within the cistern. The pharmacokinetic (PK) parameters reported in milk ultrafiltrate will help establish a link between the PK and the corresponding drug effect, potentially providing a meaningful rationale for the selection of a safe and effective dose in cows with mastitis.Keywords: antimicrobial, dairy cattle, gland cistern, mastitis, pharmacokinetic

INTRODUCTION

Mastitis is a leading cause of economic losses in dairy herds. 1 , 2 , 3 These losses include decreased milk production, diagnostic and treatment costs, labor, dumping of unsaleable milk, and decreased product quality. 4 Bacterial infections are the predominant cause of infectious mastitis in dairy cattle, and targeted antimicrobial use remains an important component for mastitis treatment and control. 5 , 6 Treatment of infectious mastitis requires a thorough understanding of the antimicrobial susceptibility of the organism, as well as the distribution of a therapeutic agent within the mammary gland. 7 , 8

For mastitis in cattle, the goal of treatment is to attain effective antimicrobial concentrations at the site of action. The targeted site for treatment includes the milk and mammary epithelial surfaces. 5 The use of intramammary (IMM) treatments leads to high concentrations of antimicrobials in milk that have been shown to be effective against bacterial pathogens. 9 Understanding the distribution and elimination of active drug concentrations in milk provides insight into the likelihood of successful treatment of intramammary infections (IMI).

Ceftiofur, a broad‐spectrum third‐generation cephalosporin antibiotic, is used clinically to treat a range of gram‐negative and gram‐positive bacteria, including many that produce β‐lactamase, in cattle. 10 Previous studies evaluating the elimination kinetics of ceftiofur in milk after IMM administration in lactating dairy cows identified initially high concentrations of ceftiofur 12 hours after dosing and its metabolites in milk, but considerable variability of concentrations was detected in different quarters. 11 , 12

Antimicrobial agents have been shown to distribute unevenly in milk and mammary tissues. 9 Distribution within the mammary gland is affected by the drug's affinity for different tissues, lipophilicity, and the formulation or the vehicle used. In addition, milk composition (made up of aqueous and lipid fractions) may influence the distribution of drugs within the gland, and measured drug in whole milk may not equal concentrations in the aqueous fraction. 13 Whole milk sampling also can vary depending on timing of whole milk sampling. After IMM administration, concentrations of a cephapirin determined in foremilk were more than twice as high and more variable than those in bucket milk or samples collected at the end of milking. 14 This uneven distribution may be the result of several factors, including physicochemical properties of the antimicrobial agent and the presence of inflammation. 9

Because of difficulty in accessing samples within the mammary gland in between typical milking times (every 8‐12 hours) to measure drug concentrations, whole milk drug concentrations often are collected with the hope that they will correlate with or be informative about drug exposures within the mammary gland for pharmacokinetic (PK) analysis. 15 The use of whole milk or foremilk exposure in PK‐pharmacodynamic models may lead to suboptimal assessment of exposure‐response relationships and inaccurate dose selection. 14 , 16 To address these concerns, a previously described method using a continuous sampling technique with ultrafiltration probes was developed to determine drug concentrations within the gland cistern. 17 Utilizing this sampling method, our objective was to determine the disposition of a commonly used IMM antimicrobial agent (ceftiofur hydrochloride) in healthy and mastitic quarters by comparing whole milk and ultrafiltrate sampling techniques in lactating dairy cattle.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Ten mature, late lactation Holstein (n = 9) and Jersey (n = 1) dairy cows (422‐670 kg) were obtained from the North Carolina Department of Agriculture Cherry Research Farm and the North Carolina State University Dairy Educational Unit. A statistical power calculation determined a sample size of 9 cows based on previous maximum ceftiofur composite milk concentrations in healthy and mastitis cows, allowing for detection of differences in concentrations at a 95% confidence level with a power of 80% with an alpha of .05, including a 10% fall‐out rate. Cows were enrolled in the study when they had visually abnormal milk (clots, flakes, or watery secretion) and a previous somatic cell count in the current lactation of >250 000 cells/mL. Upon arrival at the laboratory animal housing facilities, a pretreatment milk sample was obtained from each quarter and cultured for the presence of organisms associated with mastitis. An analysis of herd records indicated that none of the cows had been treated with antimicrobials during the present lactation. All cows were between 250 and 300 days in milk and ranged from the second to the fourth lactation. Daily milk yield during the experimental treatment period ranged between 1.4 and 15.9 kg/day throughout the duration of the study. Each cow served as its own control and had at least 1 healthy and 1 mastitic quarter at the time of enrollment. Cows were housed individually in stalls bedded with wood shavings and fed grass hay with daily ration grain and had free access to water for the duration of the study.

Experimental protocol

Baseline milk samples were collected before the start of study from each individual quarter and assessed for microbial growth using bacterial culture before probe insertion. Enrolled animals were identified as having mastitis if a cow had at least 1 culture‐positive (mastitic) quarter and 1 culture‐negative (nonmastitic) quarter. Cows were fitted for placement of ultrafiltration probes in the gland cistern of the udder. Probes were placed in 1 mastitic and 1 nonmastitic (control) quarter of each cow. Experimental treatments were administered to both the mastitic and control quarters of each cow, 24 hours after probe placement. Treatment consisted of 2 IMM infusions of 125 mg ceftiofur hydrochloride suspension (Spectramast LC, Zoetis Animal Health, Parsippany, NJ) administered 24 hours apart.

Placement of intramammary ultrafiltration probes

Cows were restrained in a head gate and sedated using a combined IM injection of butorphanol (0.025 mg/kg, Butorphic Injection, Akorn, Inc, Lake Forest, IL), xylazine (0.05 mg/kg, AnaSed injection, Akorn, Inc, Lake Forest, IL), and ketamine (0.1 mg/kg, KetaVed, Vedco, Inc, St Joseph, MO). To avoid major blood vessels and hemorrhage into the gland cistern during placement, and to minimize trauma to tissues, the probe insertion sites in the gland cistern of 1 mastitic and 1 nonmastitic (control) quarter were determined by ultrasound examination (IBEX PRO, E.I. Medical Imaging, Loveland, CO). The insertion site was clipped, sterilely prepared, and then locally anesthetized using 1 to 2 mL of lidocaine (2% Lidocaine HCl Injection, Hospira, Inc, Lake Forest, IL). A stab incision was made using a #10 scalpel blade, a sterile 3.8‐cm, 10‐gauge needle was fitted with an adaptor and then advanced under ultrasound guidance until the tip of the needle was inserted into the cistern. The adaptor then was removed to allow milk to drain from the quarter. Once drained, the collecting loops of an ultrafiltration probe (UF‐3‐12; Bioanalytical Systems, West Lafayette, IN) were pushed through the hypodermic needle and into the gland cistern of the udder so that the entire collecting apparatus was within the lumen. The needle then was removed, leaving the ultrafiltration probe in place within the lumen of the cistern. Using 2‐0 monofilament nylon suture (Monosoft, Covidien, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN), the stab incision was closed, and the external tubing was secured with butterfly tags to the skin of the udder. The free end of the tubing was connected to a vacuum vial needle (MD‐1320; Bioanalytical Systems, West Lafayette, IN) placed within a vacutainer rubber stopper (Sodium heparin, Becton‐Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ), and the rubber stopper sutured to the skin on the lateral (front quarters) or caudal (rear quarters) aspect of the udder so as to keep the tubing out of the way of both milking and the animal's feet. After implantation of the probes, we observed no clinically relevant effects on the appearance of the milk (in color or viscosity) or the ability to milk the cows in healthy or mastitic quarters.

Sample collection

Milk ultrafiltrate

Once placed, the probes were prepared to collect ultrafiltrate from the gland cistern. A 3 mL no‐additive vacutainer (Becton‐Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ) was inserted onto the secured needle at the terminal end of each probe. Drug administration and sample collection began approximately 24 hours after probe placement. Samples from probes placed in the cistern were collected at time 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 18, 24, 26, 28, 30, 32, 36, 48, 60, 72, 84 and 96 hours after drug administration by changing the tubes at the predetermined time points. The collected fluid was stored briefly on ice and then frozen at −80°C until assayed.

Composite milk collection

Cows were milked using a portable milking machine (GEA Group, Dusseldorf, Germany) every 12 hours before and after drug administration. Total milk volume was recorded at each milking. Foremilk, mid‐milking, and residual whole milk samples were collected separately from each mastitis and control quarter during milking at approximately 0, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72, 84, and 96 hours after drug administration by hand‐milking 2 mL of milk into a 15‐mL conical tube during pauses in the machine‐milking process. Each foremilk, mid‐milking, and residual milk sample was thoroughly mixed, and then 0.75 mL of each was pipetted into a sterile 5‐mL test tube. This composite sample was thoroughly mixed by vortex before aliquoting, and then aliquots were immediately frozen at −80°C until assayed. Plasma samples were not collected because the objective of our study was to evaluate milk and ultrafiltrate concentrations after IMM infusion.

Microbial assessment

Milk samples were collected from both the mastitic and control quarters, just before milking, at time 0, 5, 7, and 14 days after first drug administration. After forestripping and predipping, the tip of each teat was scrubbed with a series of cotton balls soaked in ethyl alcohol. Once clean and dry, samples were hand milked aseptically into sterile sampling cups and stored briefly on ice before microbiological analysis. Each milk sample was submitted for aerobic milk microbiological analysis to the North Carolina State University Clinical Microbiology and Molecular Diagnostics laboratory. All isolates were quantified and isolated for purity onto fresh 5% sheep blood agar plates (Remel, San Diego, CA). Identification of each isolate was completed using matrix‐assisted laser desorption ionization‐time of flight (Biomerieux, Marcy‐l'Étoile, France) according to laboratory standard operating procedures.

Ceftiofur analysis

Analysis of ceftiofur hydrochloride concentrations in whole milk and milk ultrafiltrate was completed using ultra high‐performance liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometric detection (UPLC/MS/MS). Whole milk and milk ultrafiltrate samples were extracted by a protein precipitation method. Briefly, 200 μL of each sample was combined with 800 μL of acetonitrile and vortexed for 30 seconds at speed 8 on the Vortex Genie 2 Mixer. Samples then were centrifuged for 5 minutes at 10 000×g at room temperature. Eight hundred microliters of acetonitrile and 200 μL of water were combined into the 16 × 100 mm borosilicate glass tubes. Then 800 μL of supernatant was added and vortexed. The supernatant/solvent mixture was added onto a solid phase extraction cartridge (Agilent Captiva EMR Lipids 3 mL, 300 mg) and pulled through by gravity or low pressure. The collected sample was evaporated to dryness under air in the Labconco Rapidvap Vertex evaporator at 55°C for 20 minutes. The sample was reconstituted in 300 μL of 1:1 acetonitrile: water mixture and vortexed for 30 seconds. Each sample was filtered using a syringeless filter (Whatman mini uniprep syringeless 0.2 μm PVDF filters) and analyzed by UPLC/MS/MS. Injection volume for UPLC/MS/MS analysis was set at 10 μL and concentrations were derived by comparing peak areas of samples with those from an external standard curve. An Acquity BEH C18 column (1.7 mm inside diameter, 2.1 × 50 mm; Waters, Milford, MA) and VanGuard Pre‐Column were used. Method validation was performed according to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Bioanalytical Guidelines. 18 Standard curves for each matrix were prepared by fortifying untreated matrix with ceftiofur standard (Sigma‐Aldrich, VETRANAL Analytical Standard, 99.3% purity), which produced a linear concentration range of 0.005 to 50 μg/mL with a correlation coefficient, R 2 of 0.99. Recovery, accuracy, and precision were determined by analyzing 5 replicates at low, medium, and high concentrations within the concentration range of the curve for each matrix. Intraday precision and accuracy were obtained by analyzing 3 different ceftiofur concentrations repeated 5 times each on the same day. Interday precision and accuracy were obtained by analyzing 7 different concentrations on 5 different days. A limit of detection (LOD) of 0.001 μg/mL was selected for both matrices because it was the lowest amount of analyte in our samples that could be detected with acceptable certainty but not quantitated as an exact value. The limit of quantification for ceftiofur in both matrices was determined to be 0.005 μg/mL, and the linear range for milk was 0.005 to 30 mg/mL. Relative SD for both interday and intraday accuracy was <15% at all concentrations (R 2 >0.99).

After initially quantifying the parent compound (ceftiofur), a significant time delay occurred in measuring metabolites. Quantification of desfuroylceftiofur acetamide (DCA) which includes ceftiofur and its metabolites was attempted, but extreme variability in the results suggested substantial degradation in some of the samples. Therefore, we elected to not report this data. In addition, the small sample volume obtained from the ultrafiltration tubes limited the amount of sample available for analysis. Evaluation of the original new animal drug approval data showed that parent ceftiofur was the predominant milk residue in the first and second milkings posttreatment. In the reported metabolism data in that same approval, desfuroylceftiofur (DFC)‐related residues in milk after 2 daily IMM doses of ceftiofur HCl showed the mean ± SD milk concentration to be 29.73 ± 14.25 μg/mL after the second dose. 10 The maximum concentrations (geometric mean) of the parent ceftiofur in composite milk was 30.3 μg/mL in healthy quarters, and therefore only parent ceftiofur concentrations are presented.

Pharmacokinetic analysis

Measured milk and ultrafiltrate ceftiofur concentrations were plotted on linear and semilogarithmic graphs for visual assessment of the best fit for PK analysis. A noncompartmental analysis was fitted using an extravascular model with uniform weighting and was used to obtain PK parameters for both composite milk and ultrafiltrate (Phoenix WinNonlin, version 8.0, 191 Certara, Inc, Princeton, NJ). Pharmacokinetic parameters analyzed are presented as geometric mean and geometric SD with reported range. These parameters included: mean residence time (MRTlast), maximum concentration (C max), area under the curve after last dose to 96 hours (AUC24‐96), terminal elimination half‐life (T 1/2λz), and slope of terminal phase (λ z). The T 1/2λz was determined from at least 3 time points on the terminal portion of the plasma profile after the second dose was administered. Multiple variables (ie, drug concentrations and PK values) were not normally distributed. Therefore, nonparametric methods were used to analyze this data. Differences in PK parameters between healthy and mastitic quarters were assessed by a Wilcoxon rank sums test. Statistical significance was established when P < .05. The C max and time to maximum concentration (T max) for both composite milk and ultrafiltrate samples are reported directly from data sets for each quarter. The T max is presented as median and range. Statistical analysis was performed by GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Prism 7.04, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Time points at which the concentrations of ceftiofur were below the limit of quantification of both composite milk and ultrafiltrate (0.005 μg/mL) were not included in the PK analysis.

RESULTS

Microbial results

Initial milk cultures of enrolled cows with visibly abnormal milk yielded a variety of microorganisms including coagulase‐negative staphylococci species (CNS; n = 3), Staphylococcus aureus (n = 2), Aerococcus viridans (n = 1), Escherichia coli (n = 1), and Streptococcus uberis (n = 1). Two of the 3 cases of CNS‐positive cows were clear by Day 5, but 1 continued to be positive on milk culture for CNS 14 days after initial infusion. Of the 2 S. aureus infections, 1 cow cleared the IMI by Day 5 and 1 remained positive through Day 14. For the cows with mastitis caused by A. viridans, E. coli, or S. uberis bacteria, all cleared by Day 5 and were negative on final milk cultures by Day 14. All healthy (control) quarters remained culture negative throughout the study.

Healthy samples for composite (1 cow) and ultrafiltrate samples (2 cows) were removed from the dataset because of lack of production in the quarter. Two cows had the probe detached from the udder in between milkings. For the mastitis quarters, 2 cows were removed because of no growth on initial culture results, 2 cows were removed because of lack of production in the affected quarter, and 1 cow had no sample collected from tube for the duration of the study. Animals included in final dataset for healthy quarters are as follows: composite milk (n = 9) and ultrafiltrate (n = 6). Animals included in final dataset for mastitic quarters were composite milk (n = 6) and ultrafiltrate (n = 5).

Ceftiofur concentrations and pharmacokinetics

No cow had detectable ceftiofur concentrations in milk at enrollment (time 0). The semilogarithmic concentration‐time curves and PK parameters are presented for composite milk (Figure 1 and Table 1) and ultrafiltrate (Figure 2 and Table 2). Individual cow PK parameters are reported in Supporting Information Data S1. After IMM administration, maximum concentrations in composite milk samples were similar (P = .12) in healthy (30.3 μg/mL) and mastitic quarters (29.5 μg/mL). Composite milk samples from 6 healthy quarters reached C max by 12 hours, and 3 healthy quarters showed highest concentrations at 36 hours after the first IMM dose. A majority (5 of 6) of mastitic quarters had maximum concentrations at 36 hours after the first dose. Ultrafiltrate sampling allowed for an increased number of data points to be evaluated, and peak concentrations were noted to occur earlier than those noted in composite milk in both healthy (Figure 3) and mastitic quarters (Figure 4). For ultrafiltrate samples after the first dose of IMM ceftiofur, no identifiable differences were found between ceftiofur concentrations in mastitic vs healthy quarters (P = .54). Mean residence time showed no difference in healthy vs mastitis quarters for ultrafiltrate (P = .70) or composite (P = .85) milk samples. The terminal elimination half‐life in composite milk was similar in both healthy and mastitic quarters (P = .68). The T 1/2λz also was similar in the ultrafiltrate from healthy (7.6 hours) and mastitis (6.2 hours) quarters (P = .69). The percentage of area under the curve extrapolated to infinity for all animals and quarters was <1%.

DISCUSSION

Ceftiofur is a third‐generation cephalosporin marketed for use in dairy cattle for systemic and IMM use. When ceftiofur reaches the plasma after parenteral administration, it is rapidly metabolized by an esterase enzyme to DFC and small concentrations of other metabolites. 19 , 20 However, specific identification and quantification of ceftiofur‐metabolizing esterases in milk and mammary tissue is lacking. Many studies evaluating the PK of ceftiofur in cattle have reported only the concentrations of ceftiofur and its metabolites after derivatization with iodoacetamide, resulting in a stable derivative, DCA as a marker. 11 , 12 , 21 Current regulatory methods in the United States for detection of ceftiofur in bovine tissues involve a chemical reaction to convert various DFC‐related metabolites containing an intact β‐lactam structure to DCA. 22 Clinically, by maintaining the integrity of the β‐lactam ring during metabolism, ceftiofur and its primary metabolite DFC, retain some antibacterial activity against many veterinary pathogens. 23 Although quantifying all ceftiofur metabolites is critically important for milk residue studies, our aim was to evaluate the parent compound using an ultrafiltration device between typical milking time points. For IMI, quantifying the concentrations of free drug in the gland cistern can help determine active drug concentrations in the udder, because total drug concentration in composite milk may not accurately reflect the exposure of the drug within the cistern. 14

Antibiotics for IMM use are formulated to produce high therapeutic concentrations within the gland cistern of the affected quarter. Composite milk samples represent the total concentrations of drugs, including those bound to tissue or milk proteins. Quantifying drug exposure at the primary site of infection represents an opportunity to relate the pharmacologically active concentrations to an observed pharmacodynamic parameter, such as the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). Passive sampling of the milk ultrafiltrate allows for the measurement of sequential concentrations in the cistern over time between milkings, which could result in higher drug concentrations as compared with composite milk sampling. Ceftiofur concentrations in composite milk were similar in healthy and mastitis quarters at 12 hours (P = .85) and 24 hours (P = .09). Ultrafiltrate from mastitis quarters had similar ceftiofur concentrations at 12 hours (P = .67) and 24 hours (P = .31) compared with healthy quarters. No significant differences were observed in ceftiofur concentrations of composite vs ultrafiltrate samples at 12 hours in healthy (P = .89) or mastitic (P = .52) quarters or at 24 hours in healthy (P = .44) and mastitic (P = .75) quarters. This observation is likely a consequence of large variability and small sample size within the milk ultrafiltrate concentrations. These findings may reflect differences in sampling technique or the impact of milk components on changes in drug binding or ionization within milk. 13 , 14 , 24

Two important PK parameters that describe the elimination of a drug from the mammary gland are the mean residence time (MRT) and half‐life in elimination phase (T 1/2λz). The basis of calculating MRT is that after administration, drug molecules spend different amounts of time in the body. In our study, the MRT reflects the average time these molecules are within the cistern of the mammary gland. This value is calculated using the equation MRT=AUMCAUC, where AUMC is the area under the first moment curve and AUC is area under the concentration time curve. Mean residence times in composite milk samples were lower than values reported in ultrafiltrate but not significantly different between healthy and mastitic quarters in either matrix. Smaller number of time points collected from composite milk samples may lead to lower values for AUC and AUMC, and therefore may not accurately represent the overall exposure of ceftiofur in the gland cistern. This distinction is critical because MRT can be used to assist in interpreting the duration of effect for some molecules. The ultrafiltration probes allowed for more complete sampling over the dosing interval, and therefore a better estimation of overall drug exposure and increased MRT.

The therapeutic goal when using cephalosporins is to maintain drug concentrations above the MIC of target pathogens for a duration of at least 40% of the treatment period. 25 Ceftiofur's antimicrobial properties are time‐dependent, meaning the extent of bacterial killing is determined by the duration of exposure to the drug. Although each cow had a milk culture performed before and during the study period, the MIC of any isolated pathogen was not available, which limits the pharmacodynamic interpretation of presented parameters. Cephalosporins also have a period of time that bacterial growth is inhibited after the antibiotic concentration falls below the MIC, known as the postantibiotic effect, and this factor likely contributed to the negative culture results obtained during the study period. 26 The most common isolated pathogen from mastitic quarters in our study was CNS (n = 3), followed by S. aureus (n = 2) and 1 cow each with A. viridans, E. coli, and S. uberis. Previously determined MICs for the 168 CNS species isolated from quarter milk from dairy cattle in Tennessee indicated that 95% of isolates were susceptible to ceftiofur concentrations ≥1 μg/mL based on microdilution performed according to methods described by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute. 27 This finding was similar to reported ceftiofur MIC results for coagulase‐negative staphylococcus species identified in the original new animal drug application for the ceftiofur IMM preparation. 28 Frequent sampling using the ultrafiltration probes allowed for extensive determination of concentrations over a 24‐hour dosing interval, whereas composite samples relied on only 2 samples at 12 and 24 hour milking time points after infusion.

Composite milk concentrations of the parent ceftiofur in our study (Figure 1) were similar to previously reported total DFC‐related residue concentrations in whole milk, indicating that metabolism to metabolites within the milk is likely slower than reported in plasma. 28 In mastitic quarters, carboxylesterase concentrations in milk were markedly increased when compared with healthy quarters. 29 Although the exact esterase enzyme responsible for ceftiofur metabolism is unknown, understanding differences in drug metabolism at the level of the mammary gland is critical for evaluation of efficacy of any active metabolites.

Although cows enrolled in our study were at the end of their lactation curve with very low milk production compared with previous studies, similar concentrations were noted from whole milk concentrations. Several factors including dilution effects, vehicle differences and disease all may influence values reported. Also, milk storage within the udder typically is described in 2 compartments: cisternal and alveolar milk fractions. 30 Cisternal milk fraction refers to the milk stored within the large ducts and the gland and teat cisterns whereas secreted milk stored within the small ducts and alveoli reflects alveolar milk fraction. The ultrafiltration probes removed samples from milk within the gland cistern, reflective of cisternal milk in between milkings. Cows in late lactation have decreased volume of cisternal milk stored in the udder, which may lead to higher concentrations noted in ultrafiltrate vs composite milk. 31

LIMITATIONS

The enrollment design used in our study allowed for a lower number of animals because each cow served as its own control. The cows enrolled in our study were at the end of their lactation, producing very low volumes of milk. This feature is important when comparing different PK studies because intercow variability in IMM antimicrobial distribution can be high. 32 A major source of variability in ceftiofur concentrations may be related to milk fraction sampled. Milk from the bovine mammary gland consists of 2 main compartments: the cistern and the alveolus. When composite sampling was performed in our study at the time of milking, an equal amount of milk was pooled together to form our sample. Doing so may increase the individual variability in each sample because concentrations in the foremilk possibly could be higher than those in the strippings and in milk stored in the alveoli because of proximity to the site of infusion. A quarter milking machine was not used in our study, which may explain the variability seen within each quarter. However, it has been suggested that samples taken postmilking may be the more appropriate sample for PK studies than foremilk samples. 14 The use of an ultrafiltration probe within the cistern allowed for sampling of milk ultrafiltrate in between milkings without the influence of milk let down from the alveolus. The removal of some animals from the final data set because of loss of the ultrafiltration probe from the udder allowed for limited data interpretation between composite and milk ultrafiltrate. Plasma samples were not collected as part of our study. Ceftiofur has been shown to cross into systemic circulation at low concentrations but appears to accumulate at sites of infection. 33 Although not evaluated in our study, disease impact on the blood‐milk barrier, alterations in drug transport from treated to healthy quarters, as well differences in bacterial disease states may have contributed to the high variability of reported PK parameters. 34 Future studies should evaluate these probes in early or midlactation as well as in high production cows where cisternal volumes are larger and may allow for easier placement and retention of ultrafiltrate probes.

The variability in milk production, especially in the mastitis cows, necessitates a discussion on the comparison of ceftiofur concentrations in whole milk vs ultrafiltrate. Whole milk samples represent total drug concentrations, bound and unbound to milk proteins. The milk ultrafiltrate represents unbound drug within the cistern. Unlike whole milk, a time delay of the milk ultrafiltrate sampling occurs because of the continuous vacuum of the sampling tube, leading to higher accumulation at each time point between the 12‐hour whole milk samples. In addition, drug binding not only to udder tissue, but to protein and lipid components of milk was not evaluated in our study and may differ in healthy and diseased mammary glands. In the original FDA approval, enrolled cows were categorized as low milk producers (13.2‐24.0 kg/day) and high milk producers (25.0‐31.9 kg/day). Cows from our study produced substantially lower daily milk weights, which may overestimate ceftiofur concentrations available within the cistern. Future studies should evaluate milk protein‐drug interactions in milk from healthy and diseased cows.

CONCLUSION

Differences in ceftiofur concentration and exposure were noted between composite milk and ultrafiltrate samples, but no differences were identified in healthy or mastitic quarters. The use of ultrafiltration probes provides a unique opportunity to evaluate free drug concentrations within the bovine mammary gland. This procedure can more directly compare drug concentrations to clinical end points in vivo across a wide range of clinical scenarios. Because only the unbound drug is pharmacologically active, the PK parameters reported in milk ultrafiltrate will help establish a link between the PK and the corresponding drug effect. This information could help provide a meaningful rationale for the selection of a safe and effective dose in cows with mastitis.
