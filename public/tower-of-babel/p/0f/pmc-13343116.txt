Impact of climatic variables on postoperative outcomes in various surgical disciplines: a systematic review
Authors: Elias Schnoell, Emerson Monteiro, Philipp Stiegler, Robert Sucher, Andreas Bogner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe impact of climate change on health is well recognised, yet its influence on postoperative complications remains underexplored. This systematic review examines how climatic factors—particularly temperature, humidity and seasonal variations—affect surgical outcomes.DesignSystematic review with narrative synthesis following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting guidelines.Data sourcesA comprehensive search of PubMed, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library from June 2015 to June 2025 was conducted. An additional supplementary PubMed search was performed to identify further relevant studies.Eligibility criteria for selecting studiesIncluded were studies published in English or German that examined associations between climatic factors, including temperature, humidity, precipitation or seasonality and postoperative outcomes in surgical patients. Studies without a surgical or climatic focus, animal studies, reviews and commentaries were excluded.Data extraction and synthesisTwo reviewers independently screened studies and extracted data using predefined criteria. Risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Due to methodological heterogeneity, findings were synthesised narratively.ResultsFifteen studies were included. High ambient temperatures were frequently associated with increased risks of surgical site infections (SSIs). Some large studies reported that even a one-degree rise significantly elevated infection rates. Humidity was also linked to higher infection rates, with evidence suggesting an independent effect in some cases. Seasonal changes influenced complication rates across regions and surgical disciplines, with infection and pneumonia peaks in summer or winter months. A few studies reported increased risks during heavy rainfall or institutional transitions such as the ‘July effect’.ConclusionOverall, elevated temperature and humidity appear to be associated with increased postoperative risks, especially SSIs. Seasonal patterns may further influence outcomes depending on geography and procedure type. While the available evidence supports a link between climate and surgical complications, methodological differences and limited adjustment for confounders highlight the need for more rigorous studies.

Introduction

In recent years, climate change has become an increasingly pressing global issue. The WHO emphasises that climatic changes such as rising temperatures, increased humidity and more frequent extreme weather events can have serious health consequences. Climate change may, among other effects, promote the spread of infectious diseases, increase health risks for particularly vulnerable population groups and place additional strain on healthcare systems.1

While the impact of climate on chronic diseases and infectious processes has already been well documented in many areas, the field of postoperative medicine has so far received only limited attention. The literature suggests that climatic conditions may influence postoperative outcomes, particularly regarding surgical site infections (SSIs), infectious diseases and the need for reoperations.24 Particular attention is given to climatic factors such as heatwaves, seasonal changes, humidity and precipitation, as they can affect physiological and microbial conditions that are relevant for wound healing.3 4

Although several studies in recent years have examined associations between climate and postoperative outcomes, the available evidence remains heterogeneous. Existing studies originate from various regions and medical disciplines, but they differ considerably in terms of methodology and research focus.2 5 6

According to several studies, specific climatic conditions are associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications. Seasonal factors, temperature and humidity are the primary focus in this context.7 8 Findings from various surgical disciplines suggest that climatic influences may play a significant role in the development of postoperative complications. However, a structured review that systematically summarises and evaluates the existing studies is still lacking.2 4

This review aims to investigate, through a systematic literature review, how extensively and in what depth the influence of climatic factors, especially temperature, humidity and seasonal changes, has so far been studied in relation to postoperative complications in surgery. Studies addressing climate-related effects in different surgical disciplines were, therefore, considered. The objective is to summarise the available evidence and identify gaps for future research.

Methods

This systematic review examined the influence of climatic conditions such as temperature, humidity and seasonal fluctuations on postoperative outcomes in various surgical disciplines. The focus is on complications including impaired wound healing, infections and other postoperative events. The literature search was conducted independently by two authors. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and if no consensus was reached, a third reviewer was consulted to reach a final decision. This systematic review was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and conducted according to a purpose-built study protocol (online supplemental material 1). A registration was not performed.

Search strategy

The systematic literature search was conducted in the databases PubMed (MEDLINE), Web of Science and the Cochrane Library. To ensure broad coverage of the topic, search terms related to climatic factors, postoperative complications and various surgical disciplines were combined. Selected search terms were intended to capture the most used terminology related to climatic exposures and postoperative outcomes across different surgical disciplines. The exact search query was: (“climate change” OR “temperature” OR “humidity” OR “season”) AND (“surgical site infection” OR “wound healing” OR “postoperative complications” OR “reoperation” OR “readmission”) AND (“general surgery” OR “visceral surgery” OR “trauma surgery” OR “vascular surgery” OR “cardiac surgery” OR “neurosurgery” OR “thoracic surgery” OR “plastic surgery” OR “aesthetic surgery” OR “pediatric surgery” OR “oral surgery” OR “maxillofacial surgery” OR “urologic surgery” OR “gynecologic surgery” OR “orthopedic surgery”) NOT (allergy OR allergies OR asthma OR psychological OR sustainability). By specifically applying the NOT operator, publications unrelated to the topic, such as those focusing on mental health, allergies or sustainability, could be excluded. To reduce the risk of missing relevant studies due to heterogeneous terminology in this developing field, an additional supplementary PubMed search using more general search terms (“climate change” AND “operation” AND “surgical” NOT “sustainability”) was conducted. Studies identified through this approach underwent the same screening and eligibility assessment as records identified through the primary database search. Four additional studies fulfilled the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the final analysis. The study selection process is summarised in figure 1.

A total of 630 studies were identified using the specified search term, including 427 via PubMed, 132 via Web of Science and 70 via the Cochrane Library. After removing 66 duplicates, 564 publications remained and were screened based on title and abstract. During this step, 531 records were excluded. A full-text analysis was then conducted for 33 studies, supplemented by four additional studies identified through the supplementary PubMed search. In total, 37 full texts were reviewed. After applying the exclusion criteria, 15 studies remained for final evaluation. A PRISMA flowchart was created, with 5 June 2025, set as the cut-off date for study publication. The diagram was designed using Miro app (Realtime Board San Francisco) and is presented in figure 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Included were studies that examined a relationship between climatic factors such as temperature, humidity or seasonal fluctuations and postoperative outcomes, had a clear reference to a surgical specialty and were published in German or English between June 2015 and June 2025. Excluded were studies without a surgical or climatic focus, those with a psychological emphasis, purely animal experimental or in-vitro approaches and studies without original data such as reviews or commentaries.

Data extraction, analysis and risk of bias

All included studies were systematically assessed using predefined criteria. Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion or, where necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. Extracted data comprised author, year of publication, study design, surgical specialty, climatic exposures assessed, type of postoperative complications and key findings. The data were recorded in a structured Microsoft Excel database (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond). In addition, information on the source and processing of climatic data was extracted, which varied across studies and included daily or monthly records from local weather stations, national meteorological services or large gridded climate datasets linked with hospital or insurance data. Climatic exposure variables were defined based on meteorological data reported in the included studies. While some studies mentioned perioperative or hospital-related environmental factors, this review focused on outdoor climatic parameters such as temperature, humidity and precipitation, which were consistently reported across studies and typically obtained from regional weather databases. Data extraction was based on the full-text analysis of the included studies. Due to substantial differences in content and methodology across the included studies, no statistical meta-analysis could be performed. Instead, a thematically structured narrative analysis was conducted. The presentation of results is organised by climatic factors investigated, namely temperature, season and humidity. Within these categories, the reported postoperative complications and surgical disciplines are described. Risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies selection, comparability and outcome, with a maximum score of nine points. The overall score for each study was incorporated as an additional column in table 1. A detailed breakdown of the NOS assessment is provided in online supplemental table 1.

Results

Study selection

A total of 630 records were identified through the predefined search strategy, including 427 from PubMed, 132 from Web of Science and 71 from the Cochrane Library. After removal of 66 duplicates, 564 records remained for title and abstract screening, of which 531 were excluded. Thirty-three articles underwent full-text review, supplemented by four additional studies identified through a targeted supplementary search, resulting in 37 full texts assessed for eligibility. Following application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 15 studies were included in the final analysis. The study selection process is illustrated in a PRISMA flow diagram (figure 1). The cut-off date for inclusion was 5 June 2025.

Temperature

Temperature is one of the most frequently studied climatic factors in relation to postoperative complications, particularly regarding the risk of SSIs. SSIs are infections that occur at the site of a surgical incision, typically within 30 days after the procedure, and they represent a significant cause of morbidity, prolonged hospital stays and reoperation. Several large-scale studies reported associations between higher outdoor temperatures and an increased risk of SSIs.

The included studies, which mentioned SSIs, used varying definitions. Most of them relied on the criteria published by the Centers of Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) or variations thereof. Several publications described SSIs “according to the CDC criteria and included superficial, deep and organ/space infections”,8 while others applied the same categorisation, saying that ‘SSI was defined in accordance with the CDC criteria and categorised as superficial, deep or organ/space’.4 Matching this, further studies adopted definitions based on CDC, for example, noting that ‘SSI was defined according to CDC criteria as superficial, deep or organ/space infection within 30 days (or 1 year if implant present)’.3 Some studies used related systems. Shuman and Henson applied the NSQIP (National Surgical Quality Improvement Program), a US registry by the American College of Surgeons that tracks surgical outcomes with CDC-based 30-day definitions.9 10 Liou et al relied on the NHSN (National Healthcare Safety Network), the CDC’s national system for monitoring healthcare-associated infections, defining SSI as ‘presence of ICD9 codes 998.51 and 998.59 in either admission or follow-up records’.2 Overall, the definitions were largely comparable.

In a US analysis by Liou et al from 2022, a total of 7 702 846 surgical procedures were evaluated. The findings revealed that an increase in the minimum daily temperature was linked to a higher SSI risk. However, when humidity was included as an additional variable, this effect was partially reversed, suggesting possible collinearity between the two climatic factors. The study included procedures from a variety of surgical specialties.2

Similarly, Fortaleza et al reported a significant association between the average temperature on the day of surgery and the incidence of SSIs in a Brazilian cohort. In a sample of 36 429 patients, the risk of wound infections increased by 1.3% per degree Celsius. This effect was particularly pronounced from the 90th percentile of the temperature distribution onward (>24.7°C), with a risk ratio of 1.196 (95% CI 1.055 to 1.355; p=0.005).5

A retrospective study from Germany reported similar findings. Aghdassi et al analysed over two million surgical procedures performed between 2000 and 2016 and found that monthly average temperatures above 20 °C were associated with a significantly higher risk of SSIs compared with temperatures around 5°C. In total, 32 118 infections were recorded, corresponding to an incidence of 16 per 1000 procedures. The effect was particularly pronounced for superficial SSIs caused by gram-negative pathogens, with an adjusted OR of 1.38 (95% CI 1.16 to 1.64). Across the entire cohort, the risk increased by 0.7% for each additional degree Celsius, and by 1.3% for Gram-negative infections.8 Further evidence for a connection between temperature and postoperative complications is provided by smaller clinical studies. In an analysis by Reinisch et al from 2017, 680 appendectomies were retrospectively evaluated and linked to daily weather data. The results showed that complicated appendicitis occurred significantly more often on days with extreme temperatures, referred to as non-ambient days, compared with other days (21.1% vs 14.2%; p=0.016). Postoperative SSIs were also significantly more frequent on hot days compared with moderate ones (18% vs 10.6%; p=0.021).11

Similar observations were reported by Froschauer et al in the context of elective procedures. In a cohort of 1385 patients undergoing open carpal tunnel release, an increase in the average monthly temperature by one degree Celsius was associated with a 7.0% higher risk of impaired wound healing. As a possible cause, increased colonisation of the skin with Staphylococcus aureus due to excessive sweating was discussed.12

The temperature effect was particularly pronounced in a study by Min et al from 2022, which focused on 460 breast patients with cancer undergoing implant-based reconstruction in Seoul. When the average outdoor temperature on the day of surgery was at least 25°C, the risk of postoperative infections increased by 45.2% for each additional degree. The OR was 1.452 with a 95% CI of 1.19 to 1.76 (p<0.001). Below this threshold, no significant association was observed.3

Seasonality

Seasonality and the time of year are important climatic factors that influence postoperative outcomes. They reflect not only natural fluctuations in temperature and humidity but also undergo long-term changes because of climate change. This can lead to shifts in typical seasonal patterns of postoperative complications, such as extended heat periods, more pronounced seasonal extremes or altered infection cycles. Several of the studies included in this review indicate that the risk of postoperative infections or other complications varies significantly depending on the season or specific seasonal period.34 6 7 916

Some studies examined the impact of seasonal variation depending on climatic regions. In an Australian study by Parkinson et al from 2018 involving 219 983 total knee arthroplasties, the risk of infection was significantly higher in tropical regions during the monsoon season, which corresponds to summer and autumn, compared with the dry season. In non-tropical regions, however, no seasonal differences in infection risk were observed.7

Ogawa et al also identified an increased incidence of SSIs in spring and summer compared with autumn and winter in a nationwide Japanese study involving 3 30 803 patients who underwent surgery for hip fractures. Possible contributing factors include elevated temperatures, increased humidity and the so-called ‘July effect’, which refers to a rise in postoperative complications at the beginning of the academic training year in hospitals.4 Additional studies from various surgical disciplines also reported seasonal variations in the risk of SSIs. In a retrospective analysis by Ichida et al from 2024 involving 8436 general and gastrointestinal surgical procedures in Japan, the SSI rate for general surgeries was significantly higher in summer at 3.9% compared with 1.7% in winter. The OR was 1.87 with a p value of 0.025. In contrast, gastrointestinal procedures showed the highest SSI rate during winter. The authors discuss climatic factors such as heat and humidity as well as a possible seasonal weakening of the immune system in winter as contributing influences.6

In another Japanese study by Tian et al, an increased incidence of postoperative bacterial meningitis was observed during autumn and winter in a cohort of 6407 neurosurgical procedures. Possible contributing factors included poor indoor ventilation and the use of heating systems in enclosed environments.13 Seasonal differences were also observed in cardiac surgery. In a retrospective analysis of 5 16 698 patients who underwent coronary artery bypass grafting, Martin et al reported a significantly higher incidence of pneumonia during the cold season at 5.74% compared with 5.24% during the warm season. This effect was partly attributed to seasonal variations in pathogen profiles, such as increased prevalence of influenza.14

In patients with an implanted left ventricular assist device, Imamura et al reported an association between winter implantation and a poorer 1-year prognosis. Only 44% of patients remained free from major complications in the cold season compared with 61% in the warmer months. This difference was statistically significant with a p value of 0.024. The findings were attributed to haemodynamic instability and more difficult preoperative optimisation during the winter.15

Additional evidence of seasonal effects is provided by studies in spinal surgery. In a 6-year prospective cohort study involving 275 operations, Spatenkova et al in 2021 found a significantly increased risk of SSIs during procedures performed in the summer months from June to August. The OR was 2.92 with a p value of 0.044.16 Henson et al also reported seasonally varying rates of postoperative complications in a cohort of 208 291 spinal surgeries. Urinary tract infections occurred significantly more often during the warmer months from April to September, with a rate of 1.21% compared with 1.08% (OR 1.16; p=0.0002). Clostridium infections were also more frequent during this period (p=0.032). A ‘July effect’ was observed for thromboembolic events.9 In an analysis based on the same dataset, Shuman et al reported a significantly increased risk of SSIs during the warm season, with an OR of 1.15 and a p value below 0.0001. Additionally, the reoperation rate was higher during this period, with an OR of 1.29 and a p value of 0.005.10

Seasonal influence was also addressed in smaller studies. Reinisch et al identified two distinct peaks in postoperative complications following appendectomies, one during the summer months from June to August and another during winter, particularly in December and January.11 Froschauer et al found a significantly higher risk of impaired wound healing during the warm season in patients undergoing carpal tunnel surgery, with a rate of 9.6% compared with 6.1% (p=0.016). However, there was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of SSIs between seasons, with rates of 3.3% in the warm period and 2.1% in the cooler months (p=0.162).12

Finally, Min et al reported a significantly increased infection rate during the hot season in a study on breast reconstruction. The infection rate in July and August was 17.4% compared with 7.2% during the rest of the year. The OR was 2.639 with a p value of 0.003.3

Humidity

Humidity is a potentially relevant climatic factor influencing postoperative complications. It can promote microbial growth and skin colonisation, thereby increasing the risk of infections. Several of the studies included in this review described an association between humidity and the risk of SSIs.2 5 12 In an analysis of more than seven million surgical procedures, Liou et al reported that an increase in specific humidity by one gram per kilogram was associated with a 1.03 times higher risk of SSIs.2 Fortaleza et al also found a borderline association between relative humidity and the risk of SSIs in Brazil. The relative risk was 1.045 with a p value of 0.05, narrowly missing the threshold for statistical significance.5 Froschauer et al observed a significant association between higher relative humidity and the risk of infections requiring revision surgery in a cohort of 1385 open carpal tunnel procedures. The OR was 1.113 with a p value of 0.038. This effect was observed independently of temperature or season.12

Precipitation

Precipitation may influence postoperative outcomes, for example, through changes in humidity, microbial environment or logistical challenges. In the studies analysed, this factor was rarely examined. However, evidence of an association was reported by Liou et al. An increase in daily precipitation by 10 centimetres was associated with a 1.09 times higher risk of SSIs. This suggests that precipitation should also be considered when evaluating climatic risk factors.2

Discussion

The results of this review suggest that climatic factors such as temperature, humidity, seasonality and precipitation may be associated with postoperative complications, particularly SSIs. Several large-scale studies reported an increased risk of infection with rising outdoor temperatures,2 5 8 and similar associations were also observed in smaller studies focusing on specific surgical procedures.3 11 12 Possible explanations include temperature-related and humidity-related changes in the cutaneous microbiome, increased colonisation by pathogenic organisms such as Staphylococcus aureus and Gram-negative bacilli, impaired skin barrier function due to perspiration and moisture and enhanced bacterial biofilm formation under warm and humid conditions.5 12 Together, these mechanisms may contribute to the observed increase in SSIs during periods of elevated ambient temperature and humidity.

Seasonal differences were described in several studies, with complication patterns varying by geographic region and the type of surgical procedure.34 6 7 916 In tropical regions, complication rates increased during the rainy season,7 whereas temperate regions showed higher rates during both summer and winter months.4 6 13 Several studies also reported a seasonal increase in postoperative complications at the beginning of the clinical training year, commonly referred to as the ‘July effect’.4 9 10 In addition to infections, seasonal effects were observed for pneumonia, urinary tract infections, Clostridium infections, thromboembolic events and reoperations.9 10 14 These findings suggest a bimodal pattern, with different mechanisms potentially contributing to summer and winter peaks of postoperative complications. Warmer months were frequently associated with SSIs. In contrast, winter months were more often associated with pulmonary and cardiovascular complications, potentially related to respiratory virus circulation, impaired tissue perfusion and increased healthcare system burden during colder periods.

Higher humidity was also associated with an increased risk of wound infections in several studies.2 5 12 In the US database analysis by Liou et al, this association remained significant even after adjusting for temperature, suggesting that humidity may be independently associated with infection risk rather than solely reflecting higher temperatures.2 Similarly, Austrian data suggested that elevated relative humidity was linked to a higher risk of infections requiring revision surgery, again independent of temperature and season.12 Several mechanisms may underline this association. Elevated ambient humidity may impair skin barrier function, promote microbial proliferation and alter the local wound environment.17 Gram-negative bacteria are known to thrive under humid conditions, which may contribute to the excess of superficial and Gram-negative SSIs reported by Aghdassi et al.8 In addition, excess indoor humidity may reduce ventilation efficiency and promote contamination of surfaces or medical equipment.2 8 12

Precipitation was investigated less frequently but shows relevant associations. In the US analysis by Liou et al, an increase in daily rainfall of 10 centimetres was associated with a 9% higher risk of SSI.2 While mechanisms remain incompletely understood, precipitation may indirectly raise ambient humidity, alter microbial ecology or challenge perioperative logistics, for instance, by affecting transport, hygiene or infrastructure during periods of heavy rainfall. In resource-limited settings, flooding during rainy seasons may further amplify these risks.

Some studies also pointed to less frequently investigated climatic parameters. Reinisch et al reported that extreme atmospheric and vapour pressure values were associated with more complicated disease courses following appendectomy.11 Tian et al discussed that postoperative meningitis during winter months may partly be related to insufficient ventilation and the use of heating systems in enclosed environments.13 Martin et al further highlighted that seasonal effects may not only be explained by temperature alone but also by altered viral circulation, such as influenza during the winter months.14 These findings indicate that postoperative risks are influenced not only by classical factors such as temperature and humidity but also by less frequently considered parameters such as air pressure, indoor climate or indirect infectious patterns.

Postoperative complications appear to cluster during defined high-risk periods. Based on the included studies, these periods include phases of extreme heat above 25°C or prolonged hot spells, rainy or monsoon seasons in tropical climates, winter months with increased viral prevalence such as influenza, and the ‘July effect’. Recognition of these high-risk intervals may support targeted preventive measures before, during and after surgery, seasonally adjusted staffing strategies, or more intensive postoperative follow-up for elective procedures during these periods.

Despite these associations, the available evidence remains considerably heterogeneous. The included studies differed in design, geographic setting, surgical specialty, outcome definitions and the source and processing of climate data.268 Some studies used daily measurements from local weather stations close to the treating hospital,11 12 others relied on national meteorological services,6 13 14 while large-scale datasets combined gridded climate data with administrative records.2 The temporal resolution ranged from daily values3 11 12 to monthly averages or seasonal categories.5 7 8

Interpretation of the available evidence should take the methodological differences between studies into account. Larger multicentre registry and surveillance studies allow broader observations because of their sample size. In contrast, findings from smaller single-centre cohorts may be more influenced by institutional characteristics, unmeasured confounding factors and random variation. Nevertheless, these studies still provide important insights because they allow a more detailed evaluation of specific surgical procedures or patient groups.

Most included studies relied on outdoor meteorological data, which may not reflect the actual conditions experienced by patients in hospital settings. Operating rooms and wards are generally climate-controlled, with regulated temperature and humidity levels. Outdoor climate parameters should, therefore, not be interpreted as direct exposure measures but rather as proxies for broader environmental and organisational influences, including seasonal variation in microbial burden, respiratory virus circulation, patient behaviour, postdischarge conditions, hospital workload and staffing patterns. The observed associations are, therefore, best understood as indirect rather than direct effects of ambient climate. These differences introduce variability in exposure assessment and may lead to misclassification, for example, if hospitals were far from the recording station or patients resided in other climatic zones. In addition, several studies relied on aggregated environmental data, such as daily or monthly averages, which limits the ability to accurately capture individual patient exposure and introduces the risk of ecological fallacy.

A further limitation of this review is the potential for residual confounding. Many studies lacked information on relevant variables such as comorbidities, duration of surgery, perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis or hygienic standards.4 6 12 Several factors known to influence postoperative outcomes, particularly SSIs, were not consistently reported or adequately adjusted for across the included studies (see online supplemental table 3). These included patient-level characteristics such as BMI, diabetes or immunosuppression, as well as procedural factors including urgency, wound classification and duration of surgery. According to the WHO, central elements of SSI prevention include timely administration of perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis, appropriate preoperative skin antisepsis and maintenance of perioperative normothermia throughout surgery.17 While perioperative care in many high-income healthcare systems generally follows standardised clinical and infrastructural protocols, which may enhance comparability across settings, detailed information on operating room environments and institutional practices was largely lacking. In addition, seasonal variations in staffing levels or clinical workflows may have influenced outcomes but were not systematically captured. Consequently, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be excluded and should be considered when interpreting the observed associations between climatic factors and postoperative outcomes.

Another limitation of this review is the variability in how SSIs were defined across the included studies. While some studies used standardised CDC criteria, others relied on administrative codes or surrogate outcomes such as revision surgery. This may affect the comparability of results and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Additionally, the lack of a systematic search of grey literature represents a possible source of bias. Relevant information on the impact of climatic factors on health outcomes may be available in reports from international humanitarian or environmental organisations. However, such sources typically focus on population-level health effects and rarely provide detailed or procedure-specific data on postoperative outcomes. Nevertheless, the omission of grey literature may have led to incomplete capture of available evidence and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

The included studies primarily focused on increasing temperatures, while other aspects of climate change were rarely addressed. Evidence on the effects of lower temperatures, acute climate events or environmental changes such as desertification remains limited. These factors are less frequently studied in surgical contexts and may be more difficult to capture in clinical datasets. As a result, the overall impact of climate change on postoperative outcomes may be underestimated and warrants further research.

Beyond the direct associations identified in this review, climate change may also affect postoperative care through its impact on healthcare systems and infrastructure. Postoperative outcomes depend on reliable supply chains, adequate infrastructure, sufficient staffing and effective follow-up care. Climate-related disruptions, including extreme weather events or resource shortages, may, therefore, indirectly influence recovery and complication rates. In this context, aspects such as healthcare system resilience, supply chain stability, telemedicine and remote follow-up may become increasingly relevant. Although interactions between climate change and infectious diseases have been described in broader healthcare settings, their specific impact on postoperative outcomes remains unclear and has not been systematically investigated. While environmental sustainability and decarbonisation in surgical are increasingly discussed, their potential impact on postoperative outcomes and system resilience has not been well studied. Even though these factors were not specifically addressed in the included studies, they represent important areas for future research.

Future studies should use standardised assessment of climatic variables and clearly defined postoperative outcomes with adequate adjustment for confounding factors. Prospective and specialty-specific studies may help identify particularly vulnerable patient groups and high-risk surgical procedures more precisely. Interventional studies evaluating preventive strategies during extreme weather conditions may also be valuable. Overall, the findings suggest that climatic factors may play a previously underestimated role in postoperative complications and should receive greater attention in future research, healthcare planning and clinical practice.

Conclusion

The findings of this review indicate that climatic factors, particularly temperature, humidity and seasonality, may be associated with an increased rate of postoperative complications. SSIs were most frequently reported under warm and humid conditions. In addition, individual studies reported seasonal peaks in other complications such as pneumonia, urinary tract infections or reoperations. However, the body of research shows methodological differences, for example, in the collection of climatic data, the definition of endpoints or the control of relevant influencing factors. To enable reliable conclusions, future studies should use standardised designs and systematically account for climatic conditions. In view of the increasing frequency of extreme weather events, greater integration of climatic factors into research and clinical practice appears to be a meaningful approach.
