In vitro evaluation of the mechanical properties of the resin-based composite three-dimensional digital space maintainers containing glass ionomer cements as fluoride sources in pediatric dentistry
Authors: Şükran Altinay, Fırat Mavi, İbrahim Etem Saklakoğlu, Aylin Ziylan, Nazan Ersin, Arzu Aykut Yetkiner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThree-dimensional (3D) printing technologies are increasingly used in the fabrication of space maintainers in pediatric dentistry. However, studies on improving the properties of space maintainer materials produced by digital methods remain limited. The aim of this study was to evaluate the potential of a 3D printing resin modified with glass ionomer cement powders as a new-generation composite space maintainer material for pediatric dentistry in terms of mechanical properties. This study addresses the question of whether a 3D printing resin modified with glass ionomer cement powders can serve as a new-generation space maintainer material with favorable mechanical properties.Materials and methodsTwo different glass ionomer cement powders as fluoride sources were added to a 3D printing resin at different concentrations, and four experimental groups were formed. Unmodified resin was used as the control group, while acrylic was used as the clinical reference group. Mechanical properties of the composite space maintainers were assessed using three-point bending and tensile tests.ResultsGroups with lower filler levels generally exhibited a more balanced mechanical performance. In the tensile analysis, filler type had a significant effect on maximum stress (p = 0.0135), while filler level had a significant effect on maximum stress (p < 0.0001), maximum strain (p = 0.0191), and elastic modulus (p < 0.0001). Low-level filler groups showed mechanical behavior closer to that of the acrylic reference material.ConclusionThe addition of glass ionomer powder altered the mechanical behavior depending on the filler ratio. Low filler levels provided better preservation of mechanical properties. Although these materials show potential as new-generation space maintainers in pediatric dentistry, further in vitro and in vivo studies are required to confirm their clinical applicability.Keywords: Space maintainer, Three-dimensional printing resin, Mechanical properties, Glass ionomer, Pediatric dentistry

Introduction

Space maintainers are widely used in pediatric dentistry to preserve the space of primary teeth lost prematurely due to reasons such as caries or trauma [1]. Traditionally, these appliances are fabricated using conventional methods involving impression taking and acrylic-based materials. However, errors may occur during the impression and laboratory stages, polymerization shrinkage may be observed in impression materials, and the production process may be prolonged [2].

With advances in digital technologies, the use of computer-aided design and manufacturing systems in dentistry has increased. Three-dimensional (3D) printing technologies have attracted attention because they improve impression accuracy, accelerate the production process, reduce chairside time, and make the treatment process more comfortable, particularly for pediatric patients [3]. Despite these advantages, in current clinical practice, 3D printing systems are mostly used for model production, while space maintainers are still commonly fabricated using conventional methods. One of the main reasons for this is the lack of sufficient information regarding materials suitable for direct clinical use [4].

Various materials have been reported for digitally produced space maintainers. Polyetheretherketone (PEEK), BruxZir, Trilor, and certain polyamide, polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), and resin-based materials are examples of these materials [5–8]. Space maintainers made from these materials can be fabricated using different digital methods. In dentistry, computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) or 3D printing methods are selected according to the material to be used.

Resin-based materials have become prominent in digital production because they are compatible with digital workflows, can be produced rapidly and reproducibly with 3D printers, and can be obtained as monoblock structures [4]. In addition, the production of resins using 3D printing contributes to less material loss compared with CAD/CAM systems, which are based on a subtractive manufacturing principle [9]. Their metal-free structure, esthetic appearance, ease of production, and lightweight properties are among their advantages [10]. In addition, the highly cross-linked polymer network of 3D-printing resins contributes to maintaining surface integrity, which is important for the long-term performance of dental appliances [11]. Three-dimensional printing resins can be used both for model production and for the fabrication of direct clinical products; therefore, they are considered alternatives to conventional materials in modern dentistry. Fixed and removable space maintainer examples produced using resin materials have been reported in the literature [10, 12].

It has been reported that resins used in appliances produced with 3D printers may show adequate performance for clinical use in terms of certain mechanical properties, such as flexural strength and wear resistance. In addition, the mechanical performance of these materials is influenced by post-curing conditions, which affect the degree of conversion and cross-link density of the polymer network [13]. However, in materials used in the oral environment for long periods, fatigue resistance may decrease over time, and mechanical deformation and structural deterioration may occur. Therefore, the development of materials used in three-dimensional production has become an important research area [14]. In this field, improving the mechanical properties of 3D printing resins has recently become a topic of interest. Overall, although conventional 3D-printing resins offer several advantages in digital dentistry, their limitations have encouraged continuous research to improve their mechanical performance. Current studies aim not only to enhance the mechanical strength of these materials but also to provide them with clinically beneficial functional characteristics. In this context, new materials capable of fluoride release have been developed by adding fluoride complexes or bioactive glass particles to 3D printing resins [15, 16].

Bioactive glasses are glass-based particles capable of ion release. When added to dental materials, they can interact with the surrounding environment through ion exchange, promote apatite formation and provide biological activity [17]. Therefore, in recent years, bioactive glass has been incorporated into polymer-based materials such as acrylics and 3D printing resins with the aim of improving both their functional and mechanical properties [16, 18].

In dentistry, various additives have been incorporated into dental materials to improve their properties. Among these additives, glass ionomer cements have attracted considerable attention due to their unique composition and potential to modify the properties of the host material. Because of the fluoroaluminosilicate glass particles in their structure, they can release fluoride ions. As a result of the acid–base reaction that occurs during setting, Ca²⁺, Al³⁺, and F⁻ ions are released from the glass particles, and this ion release provides the material with caries-preventive properties [19, 20]. This fluoride-releasing capability is considered one of the functional characteristics of glass ionomer cements. Fluoride released from glass ionomer cements may support the remineralization of adjacent dental tissues by promoting mineral deposition and inhibiting demineralization. However, fluoride release alone does not indicate that a material possesses bioactive properties [21]. Furthermore, previous studies have incorporated glass ionomer cements into acrylic-based materials and reported changes in their mechanical properties. These findings suggest that the mechanical effects of incorporating glass ionomer may vary according to the polymer matrix and should therefore be carefully evaluated [22].

Before improving the functional properties of 3D-printing resins, it is essential to ensure that their mechanical properties are adequate for clinical use. Therefore, improving the mechanical properties of these materials has become an important focus of recent research. One approach is the incorporation of filler particles into resin matrices, which may influence their mechanical behavior depending on the type and concentration of the incorporated filler [15, 16].

The literature shows that various ion-releasing fillers, including bioactive glass and fluoride-containing particles, have been incorporated into 3D-printing resins to improve their mechanical performance and introduce additional functional characteristics. However, there is limited evidence regarding the incorporation of glass ionomer cement powders into 3D printing resin as fluoride source and the evaluation of this experimental material as a space maintainer material in pediatric dentistry.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the suitability of the 3D-printed composite materials obtained by adding glass ionomer cement powders as fluoride sources at different ratios into a phenoxyethyl acrylate-based 3D printing resin for space maintainer fabrication in terms of mechanical properties. Furthermore, this study investigated whether these modified 3D resin materials could serve as a new-generation material for digitally fabricated space maintainers in pediatric dentistry.

The null hypothesis of this study was that the incorporation of glass ionomer cement powders at different concentrations into a phenoxyethyl acrylate-based 3D printing resin would not result in significant differences in the mechanical properties of the resulting 3D-printed composite materials compared with the unmodified resin.

Materials and methods

Study design and groups

In this in vitro study, the mechanical properties of the 3D-printed composite materials produced from phenoxyethyl acrylate-based 3D printing resin modified by glass ionomer cement powders were evaluated. Conventional acrylic was used as the clinical reference group, while an unmodified phenoxyethyl acrylate-based 3D printing resin (NextDent Ortho Flex; Vertex-Dental, Soesterberg, Netherlands) was used as the control group. The experimental groups were prepared by incorporating different ratios of Kavitan Plus (SpofaDental, Jičín, Czech Republic; Pentron) and Fuji IX (GC, Tokyo, Japan) glass ionomer cement powders into the resin matrix.

The study groups were defined as follows: conventional PMMA acrylic (A), unmodified pure resin (R), 12 wt% Kavitan Plus-modified resin (K12), 24 wt% Kavitan Plus-modified resin (K24), 15 wt% Fuji IX-modified resin (F15), and 30 wt% Fuji IX-modified resin (F30).

Selection of the resin material

A phenoxyethyl acrylate-based 3D printing resin was used as the matrix material in this study. This resin was considered a suitable material for space maintainer fabrication because of its compatibility with 3D printing systems, viscosity and polymerization properties suitable for digital production, esthetic appearance, and lightweight structure. In addition, our preliminary trials showed that it could be mixed with glass ionomer cement powders and was compatible with the 3D printing process, which supported the selection of this material.

Selection of the fluoride source

Preliminary trials were conducted to determine the materials that could be used as fluoride sources. In this context, the miscibility of fluoride gel, fluoride varnish, and different glass ionomer cement powders with the phenoxyethyl acrylate-based resin was evaluated. Fluoride gel and fluoride varnish were not included in the main study because homogeneous distribution could not be achieved and phase separation was observed in these mixtures.

Glass ionomer cements were selected as fluoride sources because they contain fluoroaluminosilicate glass particles with fluoride release capacity and are widely used in clinical dentistry. In the preliminary study, both Kavitan Plus and Fuji IX glass ionomer cement powders could be successfully incorporated into the resin matrix, and the resulting mixtures were successfully 3D-printed. Therefore, the powder forms of Kavitan Plus and Fuji IX were used in this study. The composition of the glass ionomers used in the study is presented (Table 1).

Mixing of resin and glass ionomer cements

The ratios of glass ionomer cement powders added to the resin were determined by considering the amount of powder used in a clinical restoration and the assumption that approximately 20 mL of resin would be used to produce a digital space maintainer. Accordingly, 2.7 g of Kavitan Plus powder and 3.6 g of Fuji IX powder, corresponding to one clinical dose of each material, were added to the resin to prepare the 12 wt% Kavitan Plus and 15 wt% Fuji IX groups, respectively. To evaluate the effect of filler ratio, twice these amounts were used to prepare the 24 wt% Kavitan Plus and 30 wt% Fuji IX groups.

The resin and glass ionomer cement powder mixtures were stirred using a magnetic stirrer at 1000 rpm for 1 h to ensure homogeneous distribution of the particles within the resin matrix. After mixing, the fluoride-containing composite resins were prepared for 3D printing.

Specimen fabrication

The prepared resin and modified resin mixtures were fabricated as test specimens using a DLP-based 3D printer (Anycubic Photon Mono, Shenzhen, China). All specimens were printed with their orientation parallel to the XY plane. Printing was performed using optimized printing parameters in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. Printing parameters were set as follows: layer thickness, 50 μm; exposure time, 70 s for initial layers and 8 s for subsequent layers; platform speed, 2 mm/s. After printing, the specimens were cleaned, dried, and allowed to rest for at least 10 min to ensure complete evaporation of residual ethanol. Subsequently, the printed specimens were post-cured in a UV-light curing unit for 30 min. Prior to mechanical testing, all fabricated specimens were stored under constant laboratory room-temperature conditions. (Fig. 1).

Experimental workflow showing the mixing of 3D printing resin with glass ionomer cement powder and the fabrication of specimens by a 3D printer for use in different tests

The acrylic specimens used as the clinical reference group (Integra Orthodontic Acrylic, Istanbul, Türkiye) were prepared by mixing powder and liquid according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The acrylic material was shaped during the dough stage and kept under pressure to promote more homogeneous polymerization and reduce porosity. After polymerization, finishing and polishing procedures were performed. Prior to mechanical testing, the raw resin material, glass ionomer cement powders, and all fabricated specimens were stored under constant laboratory room-temperature conditions. The powder-mixing procedure was also carried out at room temperature, and all mechanical tests were performed under the same laboratory conditions.

Mechanical tests

Three-point bending and tensile tests were performed to evaluate the mechanical performance of the materials. All mechanical tests were carried out using a universal testing machine (Shimadzu AG-IG 100 kN, Kyoto, Japan).

The three-point bending test was performed to evaluate the behavior of the materials under flexural loading. The test was conducted with a support span of 60 mm and a crosshead speed of 2 mm/min. Parameters such as maximum stress, stress at break, maximum strain, strain at break, and elastic modulus were assessed.

The tensile test was performed to evaluate the strength and deformation behavior of the materials under tensile loading. The test was performed at a crosshead speed of 5 mm/min. Mechanical properties including maximum stress, stress at break, strain, and elastic modulus were determined (Table 2).

Statistical analysis

Data obtained from the mechanical tests were evaluated as independent variables, and analyses were performed separately for each mechanical parameter. In the modified resin groups, the effects of filler type and filler level were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. The unmodified resin and acrylic groups were considered reference groups and were not included in the factorial model. Planned pairwise Welch tests were used to compare the acrylic group with the modified resin groups, and p values were adjusted using the Holm correction. The significance level was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Findings related to the printability of the experimental resin groups

The phenoxyethyl acrylate-based 3D printing resin modified with different ratios of glass ionomer cement powder was homogenized using a magnetic stirrer, and the experimental resin groups were prepared. The prepared mixtures were found to be suitable for fabrication with a 3D printer, and the test specimens could be produced in the desired dimensions. In the modified groups, the specimens were successfully printed, and no visible phase separation was observed during specimen fabrication.

Results of the three-point bending tests

According to the three-point bending test results, filler type (p = 0.0135) and filler level (p < 0.0001) had significant effects on maximum stress, whereas the interaction between filler type and filler level was not significant (p = 0.1639). Maximum strain (p = 0.0191), strain at fracture (p = 0.0047), and elastic modulus (p < 0.0001) were significantly affected only by filler level. For fracture stress, only filler type showed a significant effect (p = 0.0329), with Fuji IX-modified groups showing higher values than Kavitan-modified groups. Overall, low filler levels better preserved flexural performance, whereas high filler levels reduced mechanical performance. Among the experimental groups, F15 showed the mechanical behavior closest to that of the acrylic clinical reference group in the three-point bending test (Table 3).

Findings of the tensile test

According to the tensile test results, filler level had a significant effect on maximum stress (p < 0.001), and the interaction between filler type and filler level was also significant (p = 0.0185). However, filler type alone did not show a significant effect (p = 0.257). Maximum strain and strain at break were significantly affected only by filler level (p < 0.0001 and p < 0.001, respectively), with reduced deformation capacity at higher filler levels. For stress at break, only the interaction between filler type and filler level was significant (p = 0.0498), while elastic modulus was significantly affected only by filler level (p = 0.0016). Overall, filler level was the main factor influencing tensile behavior; as the filler ratio increased, strength and deformation capacity decreased, whereas stiffness increased. Among the experimental groups, F15 showed the highest tensile strength (Table 4).

Discussion

With the increasing use of digital technologies in dentistry, faster, reproducible production methods that may improve patient compliance have also come to the forefront in pediatric dentistry. Particularly for appliances such as space maintainers, which remain in the oral cavity for long periods, digital production offers important advantages in terms of reducing clinical chair time and ensuring standardized production quality [3]. In the literature, PEEK, Trilor, and resin-based materials have been reported to be evaluated for the digital production of space maintainers [23, 24]. The rapid production of resin-based materials with three-dimensional printers, their compatibility with digital workflows, and their ability to be produced as monoblock structures make these materials attractive for clinical applications [25]. It has been reported in the literature that resin-based space maintainers produced using digital methods may serve as an alternative to conventional methods [10, 26]. In this regard, in the present study, a phenoxyethyl acrylate-based 3D printing resin was evaluated as a space maintainer matrix material, and fluoride-containing glass ionomer cement fillers were added to this resin to investigate its mechanical properties.

The phenoxyethyl acrylate-based resin used in this study is a biocompatible photopolymer resin developed for the production of orthodontic appliances. It has been reported that this resin can exhibit adequate mechanical properties with suitable elastic modulus and flexural strength values [27]. However, conventional 3D-printing resins still have several limitations. Compared with conventional PMMA materials, some 3D-printed dental resins have been reported to exhibit lower flexural strength and hardness, while being more flexible. In addition, their mechanical properties can be affected by post-curing conditions and may change during long-term intraoral use under functional loading. These findings indicate that further optimization of 3D-printing resin formulations is required for long-term clinical applications [14, 28, 29]. Based on these considerations, in the present study, the resin matrix was modified with Kavitan and Fuji IX powders to improve its mechanical properties while maintaining its suitability for space maintainer fabrication. Kavitan was included in the study because it had previously been used in the modification of acrylic materials, allowing comparison with data in the literature [18]. Fuji IX was selected due to its fluoride release potential and its mechanical effects on the resin matrix.

When the mechanical findings were evaluated, the acrylic group showed higher maximum stress, fracture stress, and elastic modulus values in the three-point bending test, suggesting that this material exhibited a more rigid structure and better maintained its form under flexural forces. In contrast, the unmodified resin group showed a lower elastic modulus and higher fracture strain, indicating that this material was more flexible and more prone to deformation. In the literature, it has been reported that the 3D printing resin used in the present study may show lower values than other materials in terms of some tensile and flexural properties [30]. This finding is consistent with the results of the pure resin group in our study.

In the low-filler groups, F15 and K12, the increase in maximum stress and elastic modulus values compared with pure resin suggests that glass ionomer cements added at lower concentrations act as a reinforcing filler within the resin matrix. Conversely, the decrease in mechanical values in the K24 and F30 groups may be explained by factors such as difficulty in particle distribution and the formation of local agglomerations at high filler concentrations. Previous studies have reported that the addition of bioactive glass to 3D printing resins can provide fluoride release but does not always result in a clear improvement in mechanical properties [16]. Therefore, the mechanical effect of the filler is thought to be associated with filler ratio, particle distribution, and matrix compatibility.

When compared with similar studies evaluating modified 3D-printing resins, the findings of the present study are consistent with the literature, indicating that the mechanical performance of these materials is strongly influenced by the type and concentration of the incorporated filler [15, 16]. Similarly, the present study demonstrated that low filler concentrations better preserved the mechanical properties of the resin, whereas higher filler concentrations adversely affected its mechanical performance, emphasizing the importance of optimizing filler content.

The tensile test findings also support this evaluation. Among the resin-based groups, the F15 group showed the highest maximum stress value and had one of the closest elastic modulus values to the acrylic reference group. In the high-filler K24 and F30 groups, the decrease in elongation values indicates that as the filler ratio increased, the material transformed into a more rigid structure with lower deformation capacity. Previous studies have reported that tensile strength may increase at low filler concentrations, whereas elongation at break may decrease at high filler concentrations [31]. The findings of the present study are also consistent with these results.

When the experimental groups were compared, the differences in mechanical performance were influenced not only by the filler ratio but also by the type of glass ionomer cement incorporated into the resin matrix. Previous studies have reported that Fuji IX and Kavitan Plus differ in terms of the physical characteristics of their glass powders, including particle size distribution and particle morphology [32]. These differences may have influenced the dispersion of the fillers within the resin matrix and consequently contributed to the differences in the mechanical properties observed in the present study.

For space maintainer materials, mechanical strength, rigidity, and dimensional stability are important for clinical success [33]. From this perspective, the F15 group showed the most balanced mechanical profile in terms of strength, elastic modulus, and deformation behavior in both flexural and tensile tests. Therefore, among the resin-based experimental groups in this study, the F15 group is considered to be the closest to the acrylic clinical reference material in terms of mechanical properties.

Artificial aging protocols are commonly applied in studies evaluating the mechanical properties of resin-based dental materials. Previous studies have also reported that artificial aging procedures may affect the mechanical properties of 3D-printed dental resins [28]. However, no artificial aging simulation was performed in the present study. Therefore, the long-term effects of aging on the mechanical performance of the experimental materials could not be evaluated. In addition, the mechanical performance of the modified materials was evaluated using only one commercially available 3D-printing resin and two glass ionomer cement powders at selected filler concentrations. Therefore, the findings cannot be directly generalized to other resin systems or filler formulations. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the results.

Overall, the addition of fluoride-containing glass ionomer cements affected the mechanical properties of the 3D printing resin depending on the filler type and ratio. Low filler levels better preserved the mechanical properties, whereas high filler levels reduced mechanical performance. In terms of this balance, the F15 group stands out as the most suitable experimental profile. However, since the findings are limited to in vitro conditions, long-term and clinical studies are needed to confirm clinical applicability. Accordingly, the null hypothesis was partially rejected, as the incorporation of glass ionomer cement powders affected some, but not all, of the evaluated mechanical properties of the 3D-printed composite materials.

Conclusion

In this study, the development of a space maintainer material suitable for digital production in pediatric dentistry was aimed. For this purpose, two different types of fluoride-containing glass ionomer cements were added to the selected 3D printing resin as a filler material, and it was demonstrated that the mechanical properties of the material could be improved at selected filler ratios. In particular, low filler concentrations better preserved the mechanical performance of the resin, whereas higher filler concentrations increased stiffness but adversely affected strength and deformation behavior. Among the experimental groups, the F15 formulation exhibited the most balanced mechanical performance. Further studies should evaluate different 3D-printing resin systems and filler materials, as well as the long-term mechanical behavior of these materials using artificial aging protocols and under repeated loading conditions, to further improve their clinical applicability as digitally fabricated space maintainers. This study contributes to the limited literature on mechanically enhanced 3D-printed space maintainer materials for pediatric dentistry.
