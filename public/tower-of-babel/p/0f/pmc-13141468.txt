Paleoproteomics of pottery food residues reveals plant cupin superfamily proteins preserved for thousands of years
Authors: Takumi Nishiuchi, Masataka Nakano, Shinya Ohki, Kenichi Kobayashi, Guoping Sun, Joonho Son, Shinya Shoda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study focuses on ancient proteins preserved as carbonized food residues on pottery from East Asian archaeological sites associated with plant use. Food residues on pottery vessels were carbonized hundreds to thousands of years ago when plant and animal materials were heated; so, only trace amounts of proteins and other biomolecules are expected to remain. Paleoproteomic analysis showed that many peptides derived from cupin superfamily proteins of plant origin, such as the glycinin proteins of the genus Glycine, the 11S globulin protein from the Juglandaceae family, and the glutelin protein from the genus Oryza, were detected in the charred food residues. Lipid and isotope data were consistent with the proteomic results. Furthermore, 3D protein structure mapping showed that the detected peptides fall within β-barrel regions of the proteins, the structurally stable cores of these seed storage proteins. Thus, pottery foodcrusts preserved plant cupin proteins over 7,900–2,500 years.Subject areas: Paleobiology, Paleobiochemistry, Archeology

Introduction

Studies on the stability and conformation of ancient proteins are expected to enhance our understanding of the biochemical functions and evolution of proteins. The analysis of ancient proteomes, termed paleoproteomics, originally focused on the identification of animal species through the analysis of collagen sequences in animal bones.1 Paleoproteomics can be applied to a wide variety of archaeological samples, including visible residues such as ancient dairy products and starchy food.1 More recent technological advances in mass spectrometry have made it possible to analyze ancient proteins preserved in complex samples containing very small amounts of protein from diverse biological species (e.g., dental calculus1). Collagen is known to be very stable due to its rope-like triple-helical structure,2 and other proteins also gain stability from their structural and chemical interactions. However, information on the preservation of plant proteins is scarce, although one of the first reports of ancient proteins recovered from bioarcheological samples targeted seed storage proteins from grapeseeds.3 It is expected to benefit from ongoing improvements in purification methods and protein identification techniques, enabling significant insights into ancient plant and animal usage.

Among the archaeological sample types, carbonized residues on pottery (charred foodcrusts left by ancient cooking) are particularly complex and challenging for proteomic analysis. These residues likely contain mixtures of biomolecules from animal and plant materials. Because they were exposed to heat and charring, very little protein is expected to survive, which makes proteomic analysis difficult. However, lipids are often recovered in much higher yields from archaeological samples. Accordingly, lipid residue analysis of cooking vessels has been widely applied across many regions and time periods.4,5,6,7,8,9,10 Besides being abundant in excavations, lipids are relatively resistant to chemical and microbial degradation.11 With a few exceptions,12 lipid residues alone rarely allow the identification of plant ingredients at the species level. This is because lipids generally lack species specificity, and animal lipids tend to dominate residue profiles due to their higher concentrations in foods. Recent studies have expanded the scope beyond lipids to include proteins, another class of molecules preserved in food residues.13 Proteins (chains of amino acids) contain phylogenetic information and, thus, can provide much more specific details about the biological origin of the ancient ingredients. For example, milk proteins from ancient dairy foods have been identified in the cooking residues preserved as calcite deposits on pottery.14,15 In particular, even though plant proteins are infrequently recovered, they can give direct evidence of the plant species used by early agricultural societies in this region. Such species-level insight has been difficult to achieve for most of the C3 plants using lipid residues alone.8

Pottery sherds from water-logged archaeological sites often bear adhered carbonized (charred) residues, so-called “foodcrusts,” which are the remains of ancient cooking. These residues provide a unique opportunity to investigate what was cooked in the past. However, carbonized residues are generally assumed to preserve few biomolecules beyond lipids, unlike calcite deposits, which have yielded well-preserved proteins.1 Moreover, calcite deposits on pottery form only under specific geographical conditions with mineral-rich environments, which are particularly uncommon in East Asia, the focus of this study.

Our study investigates whether proteins are preserved in the charred foodcrusts on pottery from three prehistoric water-logged sites in East Asia where extensive plant use has been documented. The reason for this is that there are far more archaeological examples of pottery-attached carbonized material than those of calcite-attached material, and if proteins can be extracted and identified from these charred residues (which are more widespread than calcite deposits), it would greatly expand the applicability of paleoproteomic methods. This study also introduces the filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) method for archaeological carbonized residues to improve protein recovery. The FASP method efficiently isolates proteins, which are then digested into peptides, by filtering out impurities and other biomolecules, thereby increasing the reliability of the results.

Archaeological sites and sample descriptions

We selected charred foodcrust samples from three archaeological waterlogged sites in East Asia: the Tianluoshan Neolithic site in China (ca. 7.9–5.7k cal BP), the Majeon-ri Bronze Age site in Korea (ca. 2.8–2.5k cal BP), and the Shimoyakebe Jomon period site in Japan (ca. 3.5–2.9k cal BP). Figure 1 shows the locations of these sites. Lipid residue analysis was conducted for all three sites,8,12 and bulk isotope analysis with direct radiocarbon dating was done for the Shimoyakebe site.16 The charred deposit from the Shimoyakebe sherds used in our study was radiocarbon dated to 2911 ± 23 BP (TKA-24200), confirming that it dates to the Final Jomon period.

Locations of the archaeological sites investigated in this study

The Tianluoshan site (30°01′N 121°22′E) is well known for its early rice agriculture practiced, alongside a broad-spectrum subsistence strategy that included rice (Oryza sativa), water chestnuts (Trapa spp.), foxnuts (Euryale ferox), and acorns (Lithocarpus spp. and Cyclobalanopsis spp.).17 Legumes (Fabaceae) are also reported in low abundance.17 A previous lipid residue analysis of pottery and their attached charred residues at Tianluoshan indicated the frequent cooking of starchy plant ingredients.8 For our proteomic study, we sampled the charred residues attached to the interior bottom of the Tianluoshan cooking vessel.

The Majeon-ri site (36°10′N 127°08′E) is a complex site consisting of settlement and cemetery areas as well as paddy field features with wooden structures. A variety of seeds and fruit remains were recovered and identified, including rice (Oryza sativa), foxtail millet (Setaria italica), perilla (Perilla frutescens), oak (Quercus sp.), and other fruits and weeds.18

The Shimoyakebe site (35°46′N 139°27′E) is known for evidence of intensive use and management of nuts (Juglans) and acorns (Quercus), chestnut (Castanea crenata), horse chestnut (Aesculus turbinata), lacquer (Toxicodendron vernicifluum), as well as legumes such as Glycine and Vigna.19 For this study, we sampled charred foodcrusts adhered to the interior wall of rim sherds from a Shimoyakebe cooking vessel (Figure 2).

“Foodcrust,” the charred deposit on the archaeological pottery sherd excavated from the Shimoyakebe site, Tokyo, Japan

All analyzed samples consisted of charred foodcrusts adhered to the interior walls of pottery vessels. These residues are interpreted as the result of high-temperature carbonization during cooking. The samples underwent standard archaeological water washing for visual inspection prior to sampling.

Results

Plant protein identification from charred foodcrusts on the archaeological pottery

To ensure the repeatability of our findings, we performed two independent sample preparations and subsequent liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analyses for specimens from each of the three archaeological sites. These duplicate analyses yielded consistent protein identifications, confirming the reliability of the experimental workflow and the observed protein preservation. We developed an experimental workflow to identify residual proteins in carbonized foodcrusts on pottery. Using this approach, we identified seven trypsin-digested peptides of Glycine max glycinin G1 and G2 in the TLS17F_prep1 (Tianluoshan site; see Tables 1 and S3). Another Tianluoshan sample (TLS17F_prep2) contained five peptides of Glycine max glycinin G1 protein (Tables 2 and S4). Keratin and trypsin (which appeared in these samples) were also present in the blank controls (Tables S1 and S2), indicating that these were contaminants introduced during sample preparation. In the Shimoyakebe sample SYB029FI_prep1, three peptides of the Glycine max glycinin G1 protein were detected (Tables 1 and S5). The SYB029FI_prep2 extract yielded glycinin G1, G2, and G4 proteins (genus Glycine; Tables 1 and S6), none of which were present in the blank samples (Tables S1 and S2). The SYB029FI extracts also showed many proteins from bacteria, fungi, and animals (Tables S5 and S6). Most of these proteins were not present in blanks. We checked the taxonomic specificity of these proteins by BLASTp against the NCBI protein database. Apart from common contaminants (keratin, ovalbumin, and trypsin), most animal proteins neither could be assigned to a specific genus or species nor showed sufficient specificity to determine the exact tissue of origin. In contrast, several plant proteins could be confidently identified to specific taxa (Table 1). For example, as shown in Figure 3, many of the detected peptides correspond to glycinin G1 and G2 proteins because these proteins share very similar sequences, causing multiple peptides to match both proteins. Deamidation of asparagine and glutamine was prevalent in the C-terminal region represented by the four peptides ANSIIYALNGR, ALIQVVNCNGER, VFDGELQEGR, and VLIVPQNFVVAAR that we detected. Deamidation of asparagine and glutamine has previously been observed in ancient proteins from archaeological samples.20 Specifically, this region contains five Asn and three Gln residues, and we found deamidation at four Asn and three Gln sites.

In contrast, the three peptides MIIIAQGK, NLNANSVIYVTR, and VVNCQGNAVFDGELR were identified as being unique to the glycinin G4 protein and were not found in the G1 and G2 proteins. Subsequently, sequence specificity of the identified peptides was evaluated using a BLASTp search. Table 1 shows that certain peptide sequences from glycinin G1 and G2 proteins were found exclusively in the Glycine max and Glycine soja. Glycine soja is the wild ancestor of domesticated Glycine max. When the amino acid sequences of the glycinin G1 protein from Glycine max and Glycine soja were compared, they were found to differ by four amino acids in the N-terminal region (Figure 3); however, the amino acid sequence differences in glycinin proteins observed between these taxa may be attributable to varietal diversity.

Furthermore, two peptide sequences of the glycinin G4 protein were observed in both Glycine max and Glycine soja (Table 1). These findings indicate that the foodcrusts adhered to the archaeological pottery contained glycinin proteins belonging to the genus Glycine. In summary, the charred residues clearly contained glycinin proteins from the genus Glycine. Notably, we detected glycinin G1 peptides in the multiple independent preparations of TLS17FI (Tianluoshan) and SYB029FI (Shimoyakebe) samples, reinforcing the authenticity of this plant protein signal.

As illustrated in Table 1 and Figure 6, six regions comprising seven peptide fragments of the 11S globulin seed storage protein 1 from Carya illinoinensis (pecan) were identified in the Shimoyakebe foodcrusts (sample SYB029FI; Table 1 and Figure 6). A BLASTp search revealed that two amino acid sequences (ADIYTEEAGR and LNAHSVVYALR) are also present in Juglans regia (English walnut). These results indicated that this 11S plant globulin seed storage protein originates from a plant in the Juglandaceae family (walnut family). Notably, a significant quantity of walnut shells (Juglans mandshurica Maxim var. sieboldiana and Juglans mandshurica var. cordiformis) was excavated at Shimoyakebe.22 Table 1 also shows three peptide regions of the 11S globulin protein containing asparagine or glutamine (N/Q); these residues showed evidence of deamidation (as expected for ancient proteins). Three other regions of the protein had no N or Q residues. This pattern suggests that the 11S globulin is indeed an ancient Juglandaceae protein exhibiting deamidation typical of its age.

We also analyzed the foodcrusts (MJR37FI) from pottery excavated at the Majeon-ri site (Figure 1). As shown in Figure 7 and Tables 1, S7, and S8, we identified four and three peptides of rice granule-bound starch synthase 1 in the MJR37FI_prep1 and prep2 samples, respectively. Among these peptides, only one peptide from MJR37FI_prep1 contained a glutamine residue, which was deamidated. To confirm the taxonomic specificity, we performed a BLASTp search. The peptides VMVISPR and TSVVAEIK, separated by a gap of 12 amino acids, were found exclusively in the Poaceae family and were present in Oryza sativa, Setaria italica, and Panicum miliaceum. The other two peptides, AGILEADR and GCELDNIMR, were also found across multiple Poaceae species. Two peptides (YMVISPR and AGILEADR) were commonly observed in both MJR37FI_prep1 and MJR37FI_prep2. An additional peptide, YDATTAIEAK, was present only in MJR37FI_prep2 and was also specific to the Poaceae family. Furthermore, two peptides with an 11-amino acid gap in the sequence, SVVYIT[11 aa]TVFDGE belonging to a glutelin type-A3 protein (an 11S globulin of the Cupin family) were observed in MJR37FI_prep1. BLASTp results indicated that these glutelin peptides were specifically found in the genus Oryza (Figure 7 and Table 1). Thus, the foodcrusts (MJR37FI) from pottery excavated at the Majeon-ri site contained Oryza-derived proteins (i.e., rice).

In this study, we applied our optimized proteomic workflow to a total of 11 carbonized foodcrust samples collected from six archaeological sites. Despite the challenges associated with protein survival in charred matrices, endogenous ancient plant proteins were successfully recovered and confidently identified exclusively in three samples (TLS17F, SYB029FI, and MJR37FI), resulting in a success rate of approximately 27%, with no authentic plant peptides detected in the remaining eight samples or procedural blanks. Detailed archaeological contexts for the samples yielding positive results are provided in Table 1.

Mapping detected peptides onto 3D protein structures

We attempted to identify seed storage proteins in the Tianluoshan foodcrust sample. At Tianluoshan, as Table 1 shows, we detected seven peptides from glycinin G1 and G2 (genus Glycine). Two of these peptide sequence regions were found to be unique to Glycine max and Glycine soja. In addition, five glycinin G1 peptides were identified in a second TLS17F extract. Two regions of the G1 sequence were specific to the Glycine max and Glycine soja, confirming the taxonomic assignment. Overall, glycinin G1 and G2 proteins (genus Glycine) were present in the charred residues from two different sites (Tianluoshan and Shimoyakebe). In particular, the glycinin G1 protein of the genus Glycine was commonly observed in all four sample preparations across two sites. It is notable that the amino acid sequences (VFDGELQEGRVLIVPQNFVVAARSQSDNFEYVSFK), comprising three peptides, were found in three out of a total of four preparations. We mapped this conserved region onto the 3D structure of the glycinin G1 and 11S seed storage globulin proteins. Figure 8 shows that this peptide cluster maps onto the β-sheet region of both proteins. A β sheet consists of multiple β strands (extended protein chain) aligned in parallel and linked by hydrogen bonds, forming a stable, wavy structure that is highly resistant to degradation and denaturation.

In summary, our analyses identified glycinin proteins (genus Glycine) and an 11S seed storage globulin from a Juglandaceae plant in the charred pottery residues. All of these proteins contained conserved β-barrel domains and belong to the cupin superfamily,23 a class of proteins known to be highly stable and widely present as seed storage proteins across many plant lineages. These findings suggest that the cupin family proteins can serve as reliable marker molecules for ancient plant residues preserved in carbonized pottery deposits.

Lipid residue and bulk isotope analyses

Table 2 shows the result of the lipid residue analysis of the foodcrust sample from the Majeon-ri and Shimoyakebe sites, together with the published data on lipid residue and bulk isotope analyses of the same samples from both Shimoyakebe and Tianluoshan sites.8,16 From TLS17F, a series of free saturated fatty acids and mono-unsaturated fatty acids were identified, in addition to levoglucosan and beta-sitosterol originating from plant organisms. From MJR37FI and SYB029FI, a series of free saturated fatty acids and mono-unsaturated fatty acids were identified as well, coupled with ω-(o-alkylphenyl) alkanoic acids of C18 carbon length (APAAs-C18). It is known that the ratio of APAAs-C18 E/H isomers reflects the difference in original resources.24,25 SYB029FI shows a ratio of 5.8, corresponding to non-leafy plants.

Table 2 also shows the result of the bulk isotope analysis for the foodcrust samples. Stable carbon and nitrogen isotope values of archaeological legumes were from −26.4 to −24.8‰ in δ13C, from 1.1‰ to 4.6‰ in δ15N, and from 13.0 to 18.4 in C/N ratio.26 Although it is difficult to assess plant input by this approach alone,8 at least these results do not contradict the existence of legumes and nuts in these samples.

Discussion

We successfully detected ancient plant proteins in the carbonized food residues on pottery from three East Asian archaeological sites. Food residues on pottery are complex and challenging for proteomics studies because they were carbonized by heating of plant and animal materials thousands of years ago and are expected to contain small amounts of proteins and other biomolecules (polysaccharides, nucleic acids, various metabolites, and lipids) plus soil particles. The FASP method efficiently removes impurities (<3,000 Da), such as salts, soil components, metabolites, and lipids, as a first step.27 Conversely, high-molecular weight substances (>3,000 Da), including proteins, are retained on the membrane filters. In our workflow, proteins retained on the filter were reduced, alkylated, and then digested with trypsin on the membrane. The resulting peptides passed through the filter during centrifugation. Any other high-molecular weight substances would be retained by the filter and thus be excluded from the peptide flow-through. Thus, using FASP, ancient proteins can be effectively recovered from highly complex foodcrust samples. In fact, glycinin proteins from the genus Glycine were consistently observed in the Tianluoshan (TLS17F) and Shimoyakebe (SYB029FI) samples. Notably, despite the risk of modern contamination,1 we did not detect any plant proteins in the blank controls, supporting the authenticity of the glycinin protein signals. Furthermore, many of the recovered peptides showed extensive deamidations, consistent with the known susceptibility of asparagine (N) and glutamine (Q) to deamidation in ancient proteins.18 In some peptide sequences (Figure 7 and Table 1), glutamine or asparagine residues are scarce or absent, making it more difficult to gauge the level of deamidation. We also observed that the number of glycinin peptides varied between different extracts of the same sample, indicating that protein distribution within foodcrusts is heterogeneous. In other words, other microscale factors (e.g., the presence of minerals, lipids, or burning intensity) within the residue might have affected protein preservation over thousands of years.

The recovery of such endogenous proteins from archaeological ceramics remains a significant analytical challenge compared with other matrices like dental calculus or bone, primarily due to their degradation and irreversible binding within the porous clay matrix.28 Early methodologies established by Solazzo et al. (2008)29 and optimization protocols by Barker et al. (2012)30 highlighted these difficulties, particularly regarding the extraction efficiency of cooking residues. More recently, advancements such as the application of deep eutectic solvents (DESs) have shown promise in improving protein recovery rates from ceramic matrices,31 paving the way for more comprehensive paleoproteomic profiling. Unlike copper-alloy vessels, which benefit from the antimicrobial preservation properties of metal ions,32 ceramics lack such intrinsic protection, further underscoring the necessity for matrix-specific extraction strategies. In paleoproteomic studies, sample preparation methods such as Gel-Aided Sample Preparation (GASP) and FASP have been widely employed,33,34 and more recently, the use of single-pot, solid-phase-enhanced sample preparation (SP3) has enabled the detection of extremely low amounts of residual proteins.35,36 However, considering the physicochemical characteristics of pottery-associated charred residues, these approaches are not necessarily equally suitable. Charred foodcrusts are composed of a complex organic-dominated solid-phase matrix containing carbonized material, glycation-derived polymers, and lipid-derived components formed during cooking and carbonization. Under such conditions, protein-derived components are likely to be physically and/or chemically immobilized within the matrix, rather than existing freely as soluble proteins. Consequently, the primary limitation in protein recovery is expected to arise at the solubilization and release stage, rather than during downstream purification. FASP allows the use of strong denaturing agents such as SDS for forced solubilization, followed by detergent removal through ultrafiltration. Moreover, by employing a low-molecular weight cutoff membrane, fragmented protein-derived components generated by thermal degradation can be physically retained, irrespective of their altered chemical properties. Taken together, these considerations indicate that, among the currently available preparation strategies, FASP is the most compatible with the preservation state and matrix constraints of pottery-associated charred residues and, therefore, represents a rational choice for their paleoproteomic analysis. However, the loss of low-abundance proteins due to the filter is not negligible. It is expected that the future application of optimized SP3 protocols will enable the detection of a wider range of proteins.

Contextualizing our findings within the literature reveals both the challenges and potential of identifying specific plant proteins. While previous ceramic residue studies, such as those from prehistoric Malta,37 have predominantly identified animal-derived proteins or durable dairy fractions (e.g., β-lactoglobulin), evidence for plant processing has been more elusive. Similarly, Lyu et al. (2024)13 analyzed pottery foodcrusts using a protocol similar to the standard FASP method. However, their identification of plant resources relied primarily on lipid analysis rather than proteomics. An important exception is the study by Hendy et al. (2018)14 at Çatalhöyük West, which successfully identified plant proteins, though preservation there was largely facilitated by surface calcified deposits, rather than the ceramic matrix itself. Building on this, recent investigations are beginning to uncover plant-based evidence directly from absorbed residues within the ceramic matrix. Notably, Chowdhury et al. (2021)21 identified soybean cupin family proteins, including glycinin and β-conglycinin, in the absorbed residues extracted from archaeological sherds in Southern Iraq. Given this relevance, we compared the detected regions of the glycinin G1 protein with their results (Figure 5). Of the seven peptides they detected, four overlapped with our results. As the remaining three peptides were unique to the absorbed residues (ceramic matrix) and absent in our foodcrust samples, we suggest that the complementary analysis of both charred deposits and absorbed residues will lead to the detection of a greater number of peptides. This improved protein sequence coverage would allow for more accurate plant species identification and deeper insights into the preservation mechanisms of ancient proteins.

Cupin family proteins are widely distributed across numerous plant species, including legumes and nuts. Their ubiquity in legumes, nuts, and other taxa underscores their essential biological functions and explains their high degree of evolutionary conservation. Several of the proteins that we identified in the foodcrusts in the present study belong to this cupin superfamily. Evans et al. (2024)38 recently demonstrated that when chestnuts were cooked in pottery to form charred residues, cupin proteins from chestnuts seeds were the most abundant proteins recovered. Cupin proteins (especially seed storage proteins) are, indeed, widespread across plants,39 which likely contribute to both the evolutionary adaptation and their long-term preservation potential.40 One of the defining characteristics of cupin proteins is their remarkable heat stability and resistance to environmental stresses, attributed to their conserved β-barrel structure. For example, glycinin G1, a member of this protein family, contains a highly conserved β-barrel motif, consisting of antiparallel β strands that form a cylindrical, barrel-like structure.41 This structure provides significant stability and resistance to denaturation, even under harsh conditions. Furthermore, survival of plant storage proteins in archaeological contexts is likely influenced by their molecular architecture. Pedergnana et al. (2025)36 recently reported that the compact, globular tertiary structure of legume proteins (e.g., legumins and vicilins) contributes to their preferential preservation in dental calculus by protecting the hydrophobic core from hydrolytic damage. A similar structural preservation bias has been observed for dairy proteins, where rigid β-lactoglobulins survive better than flexible caseins.35 The glycinin G1 identified in our study belongs to the cupin superfamily, which shares this robust globular structure. This structural stability likely facilitated its persistence within the charred deposits, even in the absence of the antimicrobial protection found in metal vessels. Indeed, Figure 8 shows that peptides we recovered from glycinin G1 protein map onto its β strands, the very elements of the β-barrel structure that confer exceptional stability.

In our results (Figure 7), we observed four peptides of granule-bound starch synthase 1 (a non-cupin family protein) across the Poaceae family but, in contrast, only two peptides from a glutelin (cupin seed protein) specific to the genus Oryza. While we frequently detected peptides derived from the structurally stable β-barrel regions of cupin proteins, the results from sample MJR37FI demonstrate that peptides from other protein families (i.e., non-cupins) can also be detected in abundance. These results suggest that while plant cupin family proteins serve as highly specific biomarkers at the family/genus level, other proteins also possess preservation potential. Given the limited number of successful detection cases using our current method, we recognize the need to increase the sample size in future studies to more accurately compare the detection rates and preservation bias of cupin versus non-cupin proteins. Furthermore, considering the challenges inherent in analyzing carbonized matrices, further improvements in protein sample preparation and mass spectrometry methods are required to enhance detection efficiency. To maximize the success rate in future studies, we plan to adopt a multi-proxy approach, prioritizing the analysis of samples that show potential for plant processing based on prior C/N isotope or lipid analyses.

For modern crops consumed by humans and livestock, well-established genome and protein databases facilitate their identification and analysis.42 However, for non-domesticated or less-studied plant species, such databases remain largely underdeveloped. This disparity presents considerable challenges for identifying plant-derived proteins, particularly in archaeological materials. Archaeological evidence indicates that walnuts were significant food resources in Japan during Jomon period.43 In this study, proteins derived from Juglandaceae (walnut family) were successfully identified; however, proteins from Fagaceae family were not detected. A plausible explanation for this limitation is the lack of comprehensive and well-curated protein databases for Fagaceae. Existing registered Fagaceae protein data are primarily derived from chloroplast DNA sequences, which are predominantly associated with photosynthetic tissues, such as leaves.44 Consequently, there is a notable absence of protein sequences specifically linked to fruit tissues within these taxa. This deficiency hinders the reliable identification of Fagaceae-derived proteins in archaeological samples, as current reference databases lack sufficient taxonomic and tissue-specific coverage. Addressing this challenge requires the development of protein databases derived from RNA-seq analysis of fruit tissues from a wide range of Fagaceae species. Establishing such a proteomic database would substantially improve our ability to identify and characterize Fagaceae-derived proteins in archaeological samples. Ultimately, this advance would yield deeper insights into prehistoric dietary practices, plant resource management, and the broader subsistence strategies of early human populations.

Lipid residues recovered from charred crusts adhered to the pottery from the Shimoyakebe site indicated the presence of plant-derived lipids. Because lipids are exceptionally durable, lipid analysis is well suited to determining the biological origins of archaeological samples. While lipid profiling provides only broad taxonomic assignments, amino acid sequences of ancient proteins can pinpoint organisms with much finer resolution. To reconstruct past diets more fully, it is, therefore, essential to analyze multiple nutritional components from the same food resource, because protein-to-lipid ratios vary widely among ingredients.

Overall, our proteomic analyses showed the presence of several plant proteins derived from the genus Glycine, the family Juglandaceae, and the genus Oryza in the charred foodcrusts. These results suggest that soybeans and walnuts were utilized by Jomon people at the Shimoyakebe site and that soybeans were also cooked at Tianluoshan. However, it is difficult to determine whether those soybeans were domesticated or wild. Rice was likely under cultivation at the Majeon-ri site; accordingly, our detection of Oryza proteins supports the interpretation that rice grains were processed in pottery there.

Limitations of the study

While this study provides important insights into ancient plant proteins, such residues are not necessarily present on all excavated pottery sherds, which further limits the availability of samples suitable for analysis. The scarcity of archaeological charred foodcrusts represents a major constraint. Furthermore, in addition to lipid and isotope analyses, the limitation in sample volume made it difficult to employ multiple destructive analytical methods that require more material. Consequently, we prioritized high-resolution LC-orbitrap MS/MS analysis to maximize the retrieval of sequence-specific information from these limited archaeological materials.

Experimental model and study participant details

Archaeological materials and sampling

Archaeological materials analyzed in this study consisted of charred food residues (foodcrusts) adhering to the interior surfaces of pottery vessels excavated from East Asian archaeological sites. In total, 11 carbonized foodcrust samples from six archaeological sites were subjected to paleoproteomic screening. Ancient plant proteins were detected in three samples from the Tianluoshan, Shimoyakebe, and Majeon-ri sites. These foodcrusts are interpreted as carbonized cooking residues formed during thermal processing of food and preserved on the interior surfaces of pottery vessels. Prior to proteomic sampling, the archaeological specimens had undergone standard water-washing as part of routine post-excavation processing and visual inspection.

Site context and environmental background

The Tianluoshan site is a Neolithic settlement associated with the intensive use of starchy plant resources. The Shimoyakebe site, a Jomon wetland settlement, has yielded evidence for the exploitation of nuts, lacquer, and legumes, including Glycine and Vigna. The Majeon-ri site is a Bronze Age settlement and cemetery complex associated with paddy field features and the recovery of diverse plant remains, including rice, millet, perilla, and oak. Detailed information on individual samples and their archaeological contexts is provided in Table 1.

Method details

The preparation of foodcrust samples for paleoproteomic studies

The sample preparation protocol was adapted from the standard FASP methods used for ancient dental calculus.33,34 However, to address the specific physicochemical properties of carbonized residues, we introduced four key modifications: (1) omission of the EDTA demineralization step, as the matrix is carbonized rather than mineralized; (2) mechanical disruption of the tough carbonized matrix; (3) enhanced solubilization using SDS and high heat; and (4) the use of a 3 kDa molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) filter to retain fragmented ancient peptides.

Approximately 10 mg of foodcrust sample was placed into a 2 mL tube and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. All sample preparation was carried out in a dedicated room to minimize contamination with modern proteins. Sample handling, especially tube opening, was performed in a laminar-flow clean bench. A procedural blank (containing only reagents and no sample) was processed alongside the sample to monitor potential contamination. To mechanically pulverize the tough carbonized matrix (Modification 2), the sample tubes containing cooled stainless-steel beads were subjected to crushing by the bead crusher (Shake Master Neo; Bio Medical Science Inc., Tokyo, Japan). The protein extraction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 50 mM DTT, and 1% (v/v) sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)) was added to the fine powdered samples, and the extraction solution was mixed thoroughly with a vortex mixer at room temperature. To facilitate the solubilization of proteins from the hydrophobic residues (Modification 3), the extraction solutions were incubated at 95°C for 10 minutes with gentle shaking, after which they were centrifuged. The supernatant was then applied to the 3 kDa centrifugal membrane filter of the ultrafiltration spin column and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 20 minutes. The use of a 3 kDa filter (Modification 4), rather than larger pore sizes, ensures the retention of fragmented ancient peptides while removing low-molecular-weight contaminants. The flow-through was then discarded. To facilitate protein denaturation, Urea buffer (8 M urea, 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0)) was added to the membrane filter to denature proteins, and the unit was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 minutes. This urea wash was performed twice, discarding the flow-through each time. Next, for the cysteine alkylation, 50 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) in urea buffer was added to the filter and centrifuged at 6,000 × g for 25 minutes. The IAA flow-through was discarded, and the filter membrane was washed twice with IAA-free urea buffer, then washed twice with 50 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate buffer (TEAB). The filter unit was transferred to a new tube. For protein digestion, 50 mM TEAB buffer containing MS-grade trypsin was applied to the filter unit and incubated overnight at 37°C with gentle agitation. After digestion, the filter unit was eluted with water and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 25 minutes to collect the flow-through, which contained the trypsin-digested peptides. The peptides were then purified using a strong cation exchange (SCX) capillary column (GL Sciences Inc., Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Desalting was performed using a Stage tip #84850 (Thermo Pierce, Tokyo, Japan) and the peptides were eluted with 70% acetonitrile (ACN). Finally, the elute was dried by vacuum centrifugation to remove residual solvent and reconstituted in 5% ACN containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid.

Protein identification by nano-liquid chromatography mass spectrometry

The trypsin-digested peptides were analyzed using an Orbitrap QE plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) coupled with a nano-liquid chromatography system (EASY-nLC 1200; Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA). The purified peptides were loaded onto the Aurora column (25 cm x 75 μm ID, 1.6 μm C18; IonOptics, Fitzroy, Austria) and separated using a linear gradient of ACN (0-40%) in 0.1% formic acid at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. Peptide ions were identified by Orbitrap QE plus MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode using the Xcalibur (version 4.4; Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA). Full MS scans were acquired over the m/z range of 375-1,500 with a resolution of 70,000. Raw data files were processed using PEAKS studio 10 (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc., Waterloo, Canada). De novo peptide sequencing was performed with a precursor mass tolerance of 10 ppm and a fragment ion mass tolerance of 0.02 Da, assuming trypsin semi-specificity and allowing up to two missed cleavages. Protein identification was carried out by matching the amino acid sequences of the de novo peptides to entries in the UniProt Sprot database. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification. Variable modifications included deamidation (asparagine and glutamine), oxidation (methionine), N-terminal acetylation, and pyroglutamic acid formation from glutamic acid, glutamine, and proline. Additionally, protein identification was validated using the Spider search mode, which accounts for ∼200 post-translational modifications and amino acid substitutions.

To ensure robust detection and minimize false positives, we applied the following criteria.

•Detection of at least two unique peptides per protein.

Lipid residue extraction and identification

Lipids were extracted using two methods: acidified methanol extraction (AE) and solvent extraction (TLE). The former followed the established methods.8,47 4 mL of methanol (CH3OH) was added to sterilized glass test tubes with 1 g (± 5 %) of ceramic powder and 10 μL of a n-tetratriacontane internal standard (1 μg μL-1) before being sonicated (25°C, 15 minutes). 800 μL of high-purity sulfuric acid (H2SO4) was added to each tube that was heated at 70°C for 4 hours. They were centrifuged (4000 rpm, 5 minutes) after cooling to room temperature. The supernatant was transferred to another sterilized tube. 2 mL of hexane (C6H14) was added to the second tube, mixed using a vortex mixer, and allowed for static separation. The supernatant layer of hexane was transferred to a third tube. This process was repeated twice. The extract was dried under a gentle nitrogen stream. The dried extract was transferred into a sterilized GC vial containing 10 μL of another internal standard n-hexatriacontane (1 μg μL-1) dissolved in hexane to produce a final extract in 200 μL of hexane. The batch included one blank sample to identify any potential contamination that may have occurred during the extraction process.

For TLE, we also followed the established protocol.48 Tubes containing 1 g (± 5 %) of ceramic powder and 10 μL of a n-tetratriacontane internal standard (1 μg μL-1) were prepared. 5 mL of dichloromethane-methanol (DCM:MeOH, 2:1 v/v) was added to each tube sonicated at 25°C, for 15 minutes afterwards. The tubes were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 minutes, and then the supernatant was transferred to another sterilized tube. This procedure was repeated twice. The extract was dried under a gentle nitrogen gas flow. Hexane was added to the dried extract, mixed, and transferred to a sterilized GC vial containing 10 μL of the internal standard n-hexatriacontane (1 μg μL-1) to produce a final extract in 150 μL of hexane. One blank was processed with the batch to identify any potential contamination that may have occurred during the extraction process. TLE extracts were derivatized using BSTFA + TMCS, 99:1 (N, O-bis (trimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide with 1 % trimethyl-chlorosilane) before analysis by gas chromatography – mass spectrometry (GC-MS).

Both extracts were analyzed by GC-MS using a Shimadzu GCMS-QP2010 Ultra (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The inlet temperature was set to 300°C, and 1 μL sample was introduced to the GC-MS using splitless injection. Ultra ALLOY-5 (Frontier Laboratories Ltd., Koriyama, Japan: 30 m × 0.25 mm, film thickness 0.25 μm) column was used. The oven temperature was set to 50°C for 2 min, then raised to 325°C at 10°C/min, and held for 12 min. The scan range was from m/z 50 to 800, with a total acquisition time of 41.5 minutes. The ion source temperature was set to 230°C, and the ionization voltage to 70 eV. Helium was used for carrier gas, with a flow rate of 3 mL/min.

Quantitation and statistical analysis

Sample size and statistical design

Because of the rarity and limited availability of the archaeological foodcrust samples, two independent sample preparations were analyzed for each specimen (n = 2). The study was designed as a descriptive analytical investigation rather than a hypothesis-testing quantitative comparison; therefore, no inferential statistical analyses were performed. Reproducibility was assessed by the consistency of protein identifications across the two independent preparations.

Protein identification criteria

For proteomic data interpretation, confidence in protein identification was ensured using predefined analytical criteria. Proteins were retained only when supported by at least two unique peptides and a protein confidence score (-10lgP) > 30 in PEAKS Studio 10. The authenticity of ancient peptides was further supported by the presence of deamidation at asparagine and glutamine residues, and manual inspection of MS/MS spectra for agreement between observed and theoretical fragment ions. Taxonomic assignments were validated by BLASTp searches against the NCBI protein database.

Data processing

No data normalization was applied, no imputation was performed, and no samples were excluded as outliers to maintain the integrity of the original MS signals.
