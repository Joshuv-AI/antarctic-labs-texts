Weight loss ameliorates symptoms of osteoarthritis and is correlated with alterations in soluble bone and cartilage markers: an analysis of patient-reported outcomes and biomarkers
Authors: Anne-Christine Bay-Jensen, Khaled Mohamed, Peder Frederiksen, Asger Bihlet, Christian Thudium, Kim Henriksen, Morten Karsdal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDrug development for osteoarthritis (OA) has faced significant challenges, mainly due to the lack of alignment between joint structure observations and patient-reported outcomes (PROs), such as pain. Weight loss has been linked to positive effects on symptomatic outcomes. Blood-based biomarkers indicating collagen and extracellular matrix turnover can be used to measure injury severity in specific tissues, offering a more precise assessment of disease progression. This study aimed to explore the relationship between obesity and PROs and its impact on serum and urinary biomarkers of bone (CTX-I and osteocalcin), synovial (type III collagen degradation and C3M), and cartilage (type II collagen degradation, CTX-II, and C2M) turnover.MethodsThis post hoc exploratory analysis examined data from 806 patients with persistent knee OA pain who participated in two clinical trial of oral salmon calcitonin (NCT00704847 [2008–06] and NCT00486434 [2007–06]. Participants were categorized by baseline BMI (lean, overweight, and obese) and two-year weight change (gain/loss ≥5%). Biomarkers were measured at baseline and at the 2-year follow-up.ResultsThe cohort primarily consisted of white women, with a median age of 64 years and a median BMI of 29 kg/m2. WOMAC total scores, function, and stiffness were significantly higher in obese patients than in overweight and lean patients; however, pain scores did not differ significantly. WOMAC total, function, and pain scores significantly improved in patients who lost or maintained their weight compared with those who gained weight. The bone resorption biomarker CTX-I increased by 1.58-fold [95% CI 1.39, 1.81], 1.37-fold [1.28, 1.47], and 1.11-fold [0.95, 1.28] in the weight loss, stable weight, and weight gain groups, respectively. The cartilage degradation marker C2M increased by 1.15-fold [0.95, 1.39], whereas the interstitial matrix degradation marker C3M decreased by 0.84-fold [0.73, 0.97] in the weight loss group.ConclusionObesity exacerbates OA symptoms. While weight loss improves symptoms and reduces synovial inflammation, measured by biomarkers, it may also heighten the risk of excessive bone and cartilage loss, when using specific collagen degradation biomarkers. This study is a post-hoc analysis of data from the CSMC trials with clinicaltrial.gov numbers NCT00704847 (2008–06-24) and NCT00486434 (2007–06-14).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41927-026-00628-w.

Introduction

Drug development for osteoarthritis (OA) has proven extremely difficult [1, 2], with discordance between structure and pain [3]. One risk mitigation of this has been to focus on treatable endotypes, thereby facilitating more personalized treatments [4]. However, before treatments are developed, we need a clearer understanding of the enabled development paths for specific treatable molecular endotypes. Several treatable OA molecular endotypes have been suggested: bone, inflammation, and, recently, a metabolic endotype, driven largely by complications arising from obesity [5, 6].

The growing obesity pandemic has increased attention to weight-loss treatments [7]. Obesity is a central component of metabolic syndrome (MetS) and drives adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Obesity and MetS are important risk factors for OA development and progression [7–9]. The PROOF study showed the preventive effect of moderate weight loss in overweight women after one year on clinical knee OA incidence [10]. Recent UK Biobank data have illustrated patients with MetS ents have a 15% higher risk of developing OA, whereas obese individuals have a 53% increased risk [11]. Weight loss had a significant positive effect on WOMAC pain in the IDEA study but was considered a failure due to the lack of effect on joint structure by MRI [12]. With recent FDA guidelines focusing on patients function, feel, and survive, attention may shift to weight loss therapies and patient-reported outcomes, enabled by accelerated approval targeting pain, with a demonstrated delay in total joint replacement [3, 13]. Hence, there are growing reports on the positive effect of weight loss on OA outcomes; however, there is a gap in knowledge on the potential negative effects of rapid drug-induced weight loss.

For example, bariatric surgery is the most effective method of weight loss, resulting in a weight loss of over 30%. Studies on pain in bariatric surgery patients show substantial improvement in joint pain, spurring interest in testing novel weight loss drugs to treat osteoarthritis, particularly in obese/overweight patients [13–15]. Recent data indicate that GLP-1 pharmacotherapy, resulting in 13% weight loss, showed a meaningful reduction in WOMAC pain (14 points vs. placebo) in knee OA patients with a BMI > 30 kg/m2, underscoring this approach’s potential [16]. The concern with weight loss is its association with increased bone resorption and bone loss, leading to higher fragility fractures, up to 23% in a 10-year follow-up after bariatric surgery [17–19].

[20–22]. The joint consists of different compartments, where bone, cartilage, and synovial membrane are the primary tissues undergoing changes in OA; bone resorption is increased, cartilage is lost, and the synovial membrane becomes inflamed. Thus, OA can be characterized by assessing changes in these tissues using either imaging techniques or soluble biomarker quantification of tissue turnover fragments [23]. Bone degradation and formation can be monitored using well-established serum and urine biomarkers, including C-telopeptide of cross-linked type I collagen (CTX-I, bone resorption by osteoclasts), pro-peptide of type I collagen (PINP, bone formation), and osteocalcin (for example, NMID, bone formation). These markers have been found to be associated with radiographic progression [20]. Several agents targeting bone resorption have been evaluated in OA clinical trials, with mixed results [21, 22, 24, 25]. While type I collagen is the main structural protein of bone, type II and III collagen are the main structural proteins of the cartilage and synovial membranes. Biomarkers such as urinary CTX-II and serum C2M measure type II collagen degradation, and C3M measuring inflammation-induced synovial turnover [23].

The aim of the current study was to investigate the relationship between weight change, OA patient-reported outcomes, and joint tissue changes using serum-based joint tissue biomarkers in the largest randomized clinical trial population currently available in the OA field by combining two Phase III clinical studies. Joint tissue biomarkers provide insights into the influence of weight loss on tissue damage and repair.

Matrials and methods

The design of the post-hoc analysis

The two oral salmon calcitonin trials (CSMC021C2301 and CSMC021C2302) were conducted between 2007 and 2010, and a brief description of the study can be found in the supplementary material (Table S1) and a full description in the original paper from Karsdal et al. [26]. This study is a post-hoc analysis of data from the CSMC trial with clinicaltrial.gov numbers NCT00704847 (2008–06-24) and NCT00486434 (2007–06-14). No new data was generated for this post-hoc analysis. The original clinical trial was a global study approved by the Institutional Review Boards in China, Poland, Romania, Denmark, Estonia, and the Czech Republic and adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided consent for the use of serum and urine samples to study joint tissue turnover biomarkers. Both randomized controlled trials were designed and reported according to the CONSORT 2010 Statement [27]. All study patients had significant pain at screening and a Kellgren-Lawrance score of 2 or 3 in the target knee. Patients were randomized (1:1) to receive oral salmon calcitonin 0.8 mg twice daily or placebo (PBO) for 24 months. The primary efficacy objectives were to examine the treatment effect compared to placebo on change over 24 months in joint space width (JSW) in the target knee measured by X-ray and to examine the change in pain and function using the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis (WOMAC) questionnaire.

Both studies failed to meet their structural endpoints; however, studies including a significant number of well-characterized knee OA patients have been surveyed over the past two decades to better understand OA and clinical trial design. One observation was that a significant number of patients (n = 933, Fig. 1 and supplementary Table S2) did not have painful radiographic OA in the target knee at baseline or at month 1 (M01), indicating a marked fluctuation in patient-reported pain at screening versus baseline and early follow-up time points. Today, clinical trials are designed differently; thus, in the current study, we “simulated” a more relevant design. To ensure that patients had pain at study, we only included patients who had persistent pain: WOMAC pain at baseline (≥200 points on a 500-mm scale) AND at M01 (≥140 points). The rationale for the 140 points can be found in the Supplementary Material (Description S3). We assessed data on both target and non-target knees and included knees with persistent pain and KL grades of 2 and 3. Biomarkers

Biomarkers were measured in samples from baseline and at the two-year follow-up at various time periods. Serum and urine CTX-I, serum NMID, and urinary CTX-II were measured at the finalization of the trials (n = 501–522, in 2007–2010), while C2M and C3M were measured in 2018 (n = 435, Fig. 1). Serum samples were retrieved as morning fasts and urine samples were collected for 24 h. Urine measurements were corrected for the creatinine concentration [26]. The biomarkers were run by the Central Laboratory at Nordic Bioscience under GXP. All assays were validated according to the CSLI criteria prior to sample measurement. The run and plate performance criteria were as follows: three quality controls (QCs) and patient samples were run in duplicate. Runs or plates with one or more QC samples not meeting the target values (±15%) were rerun. Samples on accepted runs/plates with a CV less than 20% were accepted, while samples with high CVs were rerun up to two times. No sample required more than two remeasurements. Samples measured below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) were assigned the value of the LLOQ.

Statistics

This was a post-hoc and exploratory analysis; thus, statistical significance was only included to provide a better understanding of the trends in the datasets. Differences in PROs or biomarkers between the groups were assessed using ANCOVA. Analyses at baseline were adjusted for sex, age, and race, and at follow-up for sex, race, age, and treatment arm baseline PROs. Biomarker data were log-transformed before analysis. Data are shown as least square (LS) means or differences in LS means with 95% confidence intervals. We used LS means due to unbalanced groups or potential confounding factors and to provide a more accurate estimate of treatment effects using ANOVA. In addition, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by comparing the model results with raw descriptive statistics (data not shown). A nominal significance level of 5% was used to test against two-sided alternative hypotheses, and within each PRO or biomarker analysis, p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni’s method. As this study is an exploratory analysis aimed at gaining new insights into the links between biomarkers, weight loss, and pain, no imputation of missing values was performed.

Link between changes in connective tissue biomarkers, bone destruction, and weight loss

Next, we analyzed the relative change [95% CI] in soluble biomarkers associated with joint tissue remodeling from baseline to year 2. Fig. 3 (and supplementary Table S5) shows the change in the entire population, as well as the Lean/Overweight and Obese groups. The trend was across the different groups; thus, the focus will stay on the All group. The bone resorption marker sCTX-I increased 1.58-fold [1.38, 1.80] and 1.36-fold [1.26, 1.46] in the weight loss and stable weight groups, respectively, in contrast to the weight gain group, in which it did not change (Fig. 3A). The urinary bone marker UCTX-I decreased 0.90-fold [0.83, 0.98] and 0.72-fold [0.61, 0.85] in patients who were weight stable and gained weight (Fig. 3B). No significant differences were observed between the groups for the bone formation marker NMID (Fig. 3C). The cartilage degradation marker C2M increased 1.15-fold [0.96, 1.38] in the weight loss group, which was significant compared to that in the weight gain group (Fig. 3D). No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of urinary CTX-II levels (Fig. 3E). Discussion

In this exploratory and post-hoc study of a randomized clinical trial for OA, we investigated the association between significant weight change, patient-reported outcomes, and biomarkers of bone and connective tissue degradation in patients with persistent painful radiographic knee OA. Here, “verified” means that significant pain was reported at baseline and month 1. WOMAC total and sub-scores were higher in obese patients than in lean and overweight patients. Weight gain was associated with a lower reduction in WOMAC scores in the obese population but not in the lean/overweight population. Bone resorption measured by serum CTX-I levels was significantly higher at year 2 in patients with stable weight or weight gain. Similar trend was observed for urinary CTX-I, where levels decreased in patients gaining weight. Bone formation, NMID, did not follow. Cartilage degradation was higher in patients with weight gain than in those with stable weight. However, this was only observed with the serum Type II collagen marker C2M, not with urinary CTX-II. Connective tissue degradation, C3M, was lower in patients losing weight than in weight-stable patients and those with weight gain. The patterns were the same across the three weight groups.

This study is not the first to show that weight change is associated with WOMAC score changes in overweight patients with radiographic OA or musculoskeletal pain. Cooper et al. examined 17 studies on weight changes and pain outcomes in chronic musculoskeletal pain patients and found a 4.9 kg weight loss associated with a 7.3/100-point pain reduction [28]. This corroborates the results of OA studies, such as the Intensive Diet and Exercise for Arthritis (IDEA) study and the Osteoarthritis Healthy Weight for Life weight loss program (OAHWFL) [12, 29, 30]. In the IDEA study, participants in an exercise intervention for 18 months lost an average of 9–11 kg in response to diet alone or diet combined with exercise intervention. This was followed by enhanced WOMAC function and pain scores of 5.5–7.1/68 and 1.7–1.8/20 points, respectively [30]. The five-year follow-up study illustrated that patients sustained weight loss, accompanied by an attenuation of reduced WOMAC pain and function scores compared to the baseline values [31]. Further analysis found that greater weight loss leads to greater clinical benefits over the long term, with benefits increasing as weight decreases [32]. [32] The OAHWFL study examined the pain and functional outcomes of weight loss in a community program. Results showed weight loss magnitude directly affected pain and function scores, with 7.7% reduction needed for minimal clinically important functional improvement [29]. These examples show the link between pain functional scores and weight changes in overweight and obese individuals. In the SMC study, WOMAC score changes were observed only in obese patients who lost or gained weight [33]. This could explain why the greatest differences were observed in patients gaining weight rather than in those losing weight. Interestingly, weight-loss drugs such as liraglutide have been tested in clinical trials with pain outcomes. While demonstrating an anticipated weight loss of approximately 5%, this was not sufficient to mitigate pain [14], a finding that corroborates the OAHWFL study. However, more potent weight loss drugs, such as semaglutide and tirzepatide, which cause weight loss over 15%, are being explored for OA pain improvement, with recent evidence that semaglutide may reduce WOMAC pain significantly [34, 35]. While earlier studies focus on the effect of weight loss in diet or exercise programs, little is still known about drug induced rapid weight loss and the risk-benefit ratio. The interest in weight loss therapies for the treatment of OA pain is at an all-time high.

Weight-loss interventions raise concerns regarding bone health. Studies have shown that weight loss reduces bone quality and increases the risk of osteoporosis. Our results indicate that bone resorption (CTX-I) is higher during weight loss than during stable weight or weight gain. The trend was the same for serum and urinary levels CTX-I, however where serum CTX-I increased patients with weight loss, urinary levels remained the same and decreased in patients experiencing weight gain. The mismatch observed changes might be due to the use of relative changes in this exploratory study. It could also result from varying sensitivity among the assays and the adjustment of urinary levels using creatinine. Our study did not detect any impact on bone formation as assessed by NMID. Other studies have shown that bone formation lags behind resorption, with a 30% difference, leading to lower bone mineral density (BMD) and fracture risk. Similar studies have shown that reduced resorption results in 2–4% BMD changes [32]. The biological mechanisms driving bone loss during weight reduction primarily involve hormonal shifts and a “unloading” effect that tips the balance of bone remodelling toward resorption. This involves disrupting the RANKL/OPG pathway. When leptin levels, a bone-building hormone, decrease and pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 increase, RANKL expression rises, promoting the maturation of osteoclasts [36, 37]. In addition, mechanical loading is important for maintenance of the bone turnover balance. Reduced mechanical loading can lead to in to increased production of sclerostin by osteocytes. Sclerostin inhibits the Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway, which is essential for osteoblast activity, thereby suppressing bone formation [38, 39]. Moreover, weight loss is often accompanied by lower levels of IGF-1 and changes in sex hormones like oestrogen [40]. Less is known about the effect of weight loss on cartilage health, but some of same risk factors may play a key regulatory role on chondrocytes and maintenance of cartilage health.

Several studies have described the impact of OA on bone health. In the Framingham cohort study, the BMD of study participants with mild-to-moderate OA was 5–9% higher than that of non-OA participants, whereas no difference was found between participants with severe OA and the controls [23, 41]. Another report from the Framingham study described an association between increased BMD and an increased risk of incident OA. However, in people with known OA, inverse associations exist between BMD, increasing BMD, and the risk of structural deterioration in existing OA [42]. The findings suggest that bone loss in the subchondral area is associated with the structural worsening of existing OA. The relationship between subchondral bone and cartilage health remains unclear, although the literature describes known associations. Findings show synovial fluid added to bone cultures increased RANKL expression, suggesting how OA could contribute to bone resorption near joints [39].

Interestingly, cartilage degradation, assessed by serum C2M, also seems to follow the trend of bone resorption, which may be due to the close regulation of cartilage and bone remodelling. Urinary CTX-II does in this study does not follow the trend of C2M or CTX-I. The two cartilage markers are derived from different processing; C2M is mainly released by the action of gelatinases such as MMP-9, while CTX-II is released because of collagenases [43]. CTX-II is believed to be one the best validated markers in the OA field, but levels are sometimes hampered by correction creatinine levels. Type III collagen degradation, C3M, reflects inflammation-driven destruction of connective tissue, such as the synovial membrane and bone marrow lesions [44, 45]. The C3M level was lower in year 2 than in other groups. This aligns with the notion that weight loss resolves low-grade inflammation in joints and body connective tissue. Studies show that connective tissue degradation biomarkers correlated to patient-reported outcomes such as WOMAC scores [46, 47]. Thus, our data provides insights into the patho-mechanistic influence of obesity on OA.

There are several limitations of this exploratory and post-hoc analysis. The two studies were similar in design; however, none of the studies were designed to monitor weight loss. However, Study 1 had WOMAC and X-ray assessments in both knees but no baseline assessment, using screening data instead. Many patients were excluded due to missing data and loss to follow-up. These cannot only be explained by the adverse event records; thus, this may give rise to a bias. This bias could result in blind spot where for example, only patients with certain characteristics are included and therefore the results cannot be generalized to a real-world OA population. Weight loss in SMC studies cannot be attributed solely to drug response, as a significant placebo effect occurred. Our analyses showed weight loss was independent of treatment arm, indicating it was induced by extrinsic factors not recorded (e.g. lifestyle changes, physical activity, concomitant drug use).

Conclusion

In conclusion, these data suggest that increased attention should be directed toward the development of drugs that not only trigger weight loss and thereby improve pain and function but also prevent bone and cartilage loss. We used blood-based biomarkers as biological indicators of cartilage, synovial and bone turnover changes. The data suggests that alterations in the joint are complex: bone loss may increase even as inflammation in the synovial membrane decreases. Further research should be conducted to explore the relationship between these findings, weight loss, and patient-reported outcomes using a specifically designed study. While weight reduction may be an attractive therapeutic strategy for OA, losing weight rapidly or without proper guidance can lead to decreased bone density, muscle loss (sarcopenia), and joint instability. Rapid weight loss can cause bone resorption and cartilage degradation [48, 49]. From the perspective of clinical applications for bone and cartilage blood biomarkers, there is a need for improved methods to monitor joint health in real-time during weight loss, rather than relying on X-rays to detect structural damage years later. The clinical application of biomarkers could revolutionize the management of this risk: Bone Resorption Markers (e.g., CTX-I): During a weight-loss program, elevated levels of C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX-I) could serve as an early warning system. Clinicians could use these markers to identify patients experiencing excessive bone mass loss, indicating a need to adjust caloric intake or increase resistance training. Monitoring osteocalcin (NMID) ensures that bone formation is keeping pace. The perfect treatment for obesity and OA may be a drug that induces weight loss and improves pain and function while protecting the bone and cartilage and lowering synovial inflammation and the inflammatory burden.
