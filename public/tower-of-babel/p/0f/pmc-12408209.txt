Turn-Over Technique Using a Balloon Guiding Catheter for Stable Transfemoral Neuroendovascular Therapy in Type III Aortic Arch with Severe Vessel Tortuosity
Authors: Yugo Maeda, Osamu Hamasaki, Yuki Yasuoka, Koki Ikeda, Yuki Matsuda, Shinya Nabika, Tetsuhiko Sakoguchi, Naohiko Ohbayashi, Nobutaka Horie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveEndovascular treatment of cerebrovascular diseases can be challenging in patients with difficult access routes. We describe a turn-over technique using a balloon guiding catheter (BGC) via the transfemoral approach to perform neuroendovascular treatment.Case PresentationAn 80-year-old female with a recurrent right middle cerebral artery (MCA) aneurysm after coil embolization underwent successful stent-assisted coiling via the transfemoral approach. The turn-over technique using a BGC proved effective for the difficult access route via the transfemoral approach. The guidewire and inner catheter were turned over above the aortic valve, and the BGC was advanced to the right subclavian artery. The guiding balloon was inflated, and the catheter shaft was pulled to release the loop on the aortic valve. Under roadmap guidance, the guidewire and inner catheter were advanced into the right internal carotid artery (ICA). After that, by using the balloon inflation anchoring technique, we carefully guided them to the right cervical ICA while preventing slippage.ConclusionThe turn-over technique using a BGC via the transfemoral approach may offer a viable treatment option for patients with difficult vascular access.

Introduction

In recent years, neuroendovascular therapy has become common in older patients,1) due to the expanding indications for cerebrovascular disease and advancements in endovascular devices. Challenging vascular access is often associated with anatomical factors such as type Ⅲ aortic arch and a sharp angle of the common carotid artery (CCA) or brachiocephalic artery (BCA).2,3) Various catheter systems, including flex-type guiding catheters, stiff-type guidewires, and Simmons-type catheters, can aid in difficult vascular access.4) Among access techniques, the balloon inflation anchoring technique—in which a guiding balloon is inflated and stabilized in the target vessel to advance the inner catheter distally—is particularly useful.5) However, if the catheter access remains challenging, alternative routes such as the transbrachial, transradial, or direct carotid puncture should be considered.6,7) The turn-over technique is known as an induction method to advance the guidewire and catheter to target vessels by turning the guidewire and catheter above the aortic valve8); however, there is currently no detailed report of the turn-over technique via the transfemoral approach. This study reports a case in which the turn-over technique enabled advancement of a balloon guiding catheter (BGC) to the target vessel via the transfemoral approach by looping the guidewire and inner catheter on the aortic valve.

Case Presentation

An 80-year-old female presented with an unruptured 4.1-mm right middle cerebral artery (MCA) aneurysm, which was treated with balloon-assisted coil embolization approximately 1 year earlier (Fig. 1A and 1B). DSA showed aneurysm recurrence due to coil compaction (Fig. 1C). CTA showed a type Ⅲ aortic arch and a sharply angled bifurcation of the BCA (Fig. 1D and 1E). Since the take-off angle between the BCA and aortic arch was 24° and between the right CCA and right subclavian artery was 21°, both the transfemoral and transbrachial approaches were considered difficult. At the patient’s request, a stent-assisted coil embolization was attempted under local anesthesia via the transfemoral approach.

An 8-Fr Optimo Flex 90 cm (Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan) was selected as the BGC to manage potential intraoperative aneurysm rupture. Although we attempted to advance the 8-Fr Optimo Flex using a 6-Fr SY-6 (Gadelius Medical, Tokyo, Japan) and a 0.035-inch half-stiff guidewire (Radifocus; Terumo, Tokyo, Japan), the steep bifurcation angle of the BCA and the type Ⅲ aortic arch hindered catheter access (Fig. 2A). Therefore, the inner catheter was replaced from a 6-Fr SY-6 to a 6-Fr JB2 (Medikit, Tokyo, Japan). However, as expected, guiding the Optimo Flex using the standard method—hooking the 6-Fr JB2 to the BCA origin—was difficult. Given the absence of valvular disease on preoperative echocardiography, we proceeded with BGC advancement using the turn-over technique. We subsequently advanced the guidewire to the ascending aorta and pushed it to make the guidewire loop along the aortic valve, and the guidewire tip was advanced to the right subclavian artery (Fig. 2B). The 6-Fr JB2 and Optimo Flex were advanced stepwise along the guidewire loop above the aortic valve to the right subclavian artery (Fig. 2C). After the Optimo Flex was advanced to the right subclavian artery with the 6-Fr JB2 and guidewire, the balloon of the Optimo Flex was inflated. By stabilizing the tip of the Optimo Flex within the right subclavian artery and pulling its shaft, the loop above the aortic valve was taken (Fig. 2D). The Optimo Flex was guided to the right cervical portion of the internal carotid artery (ICA) using the balloon inflation anchoring technique (Fig. 2E). This allowed the BGC to be advanced safely to the target vessel without cardiac complications such as catheter entry into the left ventricle or arrhythmias. The 6-Fr Sofia Select (Terumo) was guided to the petrous portion of the ICA, and stent-assisted coil embolization was performed with 7 coils (Fig. 3A). Postoperative MRI revealed no new cerebral infarction (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

A type Ⅲ aortic arch and sharp bifurcation angle of CCA or BCA are known factors of difficult catheter access.2,3) No standardized threshold for the degree of the bifurcation angle—correlating with difficult catheter access—exists. As for the approach to the right lesion, Kaymaz et al. reported that a take-off angle of <100° between the BCA and aortic arch was associated with the longer approach times in mechanical thrombectomy (MT).3) Seki et al. reported that induction of the catheter to the target vessels takes longer when the distance from the apex of the aortic arch to the target vessel is greater, or when the angle between the inferior margin of the aortic arch and the target vessel is acute.9) Regarding the transbrachial approach, Mori et al. reported that the BGC induction via the transbrachial approach was successful when the take-off angle between the left CCA and the BCA was ≥23°, or between the right CCA and the right subclavian artery was ≥25°.10) It is necessary to choose a better approach route based on various preoperative evaluations of access routes. In this case, since the take-off angle between the BCA and aortic arch was quite steep and between the right CCA and right subclavian artery was <25°, induction of the BGC to the right CCA with either the transfemoral or transbrachial approach would be difficult.

The turn-over technique is an access method that advances the guidewire and catheter to target vessels by looping the guidewire and catheter above the aortic valve.8) Kato et al. previously reported 2 cases in which the guiding sheath was flipped over the aortic valve using a turn-over technique via the upper arm, allowing carotid artery stenting (CAS) to be performed while maintaining the loop on the aortic valve.11) In contrast, in the present case, the BGC was too short to reach the cervical ICA from the femoral artery, necessitating the release of the BGC loop over the aortic valve. While this method raises concerns regarding the load on the aortic valve, the use of a flex-type BGC minimized the load on the aortic valve compared to a normal-type BGC. In other words, when approaching a target vessel via the femoral artery using the turn-over technique, both the use of a flex-type BGC and releasing the loop above the aortic valve are also necessary to reduce the load on the aortic valve.

The BGC is usually used for proximal flow control to prevent distal thrombus migration during MT and CAS, as well as in cases of unexpected aneurysm rupture. Aoki et al. reported that the use of a BGC was associated with good clinical outcomes following intraprocedural rupture during coil embolization for unruptured aneurysms.12) With appropriate catheter induction techniques using a BGC, distal catheter advancement is feasible in many cases, as demonstrated in this and previous reports.5)

If it is difficult to access either the CCA or ICA using the standard method via the femoral artery approach, this method might be worth considering. Below, we describe the turn-over technique using a BGC via the transfemoral approach for right lesions. First, the guidewire was advanced retrogradely into the ascending aorta and pushed to create a loop above the aortic valve. Subsequently, the tip of the guidewire was guided sufficiently distally to the right subclavian artery, and the JB2-type catheter was advanced to the right subclavian artery along the guidewire (Fig. 4A). The BGC followed the JB2 and was guided to the right subclavian artery (Fig. 4B). The guiding balloon was then inflated to stabilize the BGC in the right subclavian artery. Subsequently, the loop over the aortic valve was gradually released by pulling the BGC catheter shaft. The balloon was then deflated and slowly retracted until the tip reached the BCA bifurcation. The guidewire and the 6-Fr JB2 were then passed through the right cervical ICA under roadmap guidance using a balloon inflation anchoring technique to prevent slipping (Fig. 4C). Finally, the BGC was carefully advanced to the right cervical ICA by slightly deflating the balloon as a flow-guide.

As a complication of the turn-over technique, Tamaki et al. reported a case in which a Simmons catheter was entrapped in an aortic valve fenestration and was difficult to remove after being turned above the aortic valve to form a loop.13) It is essential to minimize the load on the aortic valve and ensure that the guidewire does not stray beneath the aortic valve or into the left ventricle. Although there are no reports of aortic valve damage associated with the turn-over technique during neuroendovascular therapy, several reports on cardiovascular interventions have been published, such as iatrogenic aortic regurgitation after radiofrequency ablation and acute aortic syndrome due to coronary intervention.14,15) Although the turn-over technique using a BGC via the transfemoral approach is effective, it must be employed with careful consideration and a clear understanding of potential complications.

Conclusion

The turn-over technique using a BGC via the transfemoral approach is a useful treatment option for neuroendovascular therapy in patients with difficult access routes.
