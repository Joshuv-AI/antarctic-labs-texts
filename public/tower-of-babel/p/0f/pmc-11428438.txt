Acceptance and use of mobile health technology in post-abortion care
Authors: Zahra Zolfaqari, Haleh Ayatollahi, Fahimeh Ranjbar, Arezoo Abasi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAbortion and its complications have always been associated with challenges such as the lack of awareness, high cost of receiving in-person services, lack of access to the skilled healthcare workers, distance from healthcare centers in remote areas, and cultural sensitivities. Therefore, the use of mobile health technology in post-abortion care has been suggested. This study aimed to investigate factors influencing the acceptance and use of mhealth technology in post-abortion care.MethodA mixed-methods study was completed in 2023. Initially, a literature review was conducted. Then, a five-point Likert-scale questionnaire was designed based on the results of the literature review. The research participants were gynecologists (n = 24), midwives (n = 38), and women who had experience of abortion (n = 183). Data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics.ResultsThe findings revealed that the participants had different opinions about the importance of motivating and inhibiting factors which included individual, technical, economic, organizational, and ethico-legal aspects. It seems that the individual, organizational, economic, and ethico-legal motivating factors as well as the ethico-legal inhibiting factors were more important than other factors and had higher mean values. In addition, there was a statistically significant difference between the mean values of gynecologists and patients regarding the individual motivating (P = 0.014) and technical inhibiting factors (P = 0.016). The gynecologists’ opinions regarding the technical motivating factors were also significantly different from midwives (P = 0.044) and patients (P = 0.010).ConclusionMultiple factors may influence the acceptance and use of mhealth technology in post-abortion care. Therefore, mhealth service developers must consider these factors to improve the quality of services and increase technology adoption. This will lead to saving costs, improving health, reducing abortion complications, and ultimately improving quality of life for women. However, further research in patient engagement and health interventions tailored to diverse cultural contexts are recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11633-5.

Background

Abortion is an intended, complex, sensitive, and multifaceted issue, entwined with dimensions of healthcare, ethics, and culture, resonating as a significant global concern [1]. Similarly, miscarriage, defined as the spontaneous loss of pregnancy often before the 20th week due to natural causes, presents both physical and emotional challenges, necessitating appropriate medical care and psychological support [2]. Post-abortion care, which encompasses care following both spontaneous miscarriage and induced abortion, is essential in addressing the diverse medical, emotional, and social needs of women [3, 4]. While these two experiences share certain healthcare requirements, they also present distinct challenges that require tailored, comprehensive, and compassionate care approaches to ensure optimal outcomes [5, 6]. A substantial number of women annually terminate their pregnancies, either by choice or unintentionally. The emotional experiences associated with these terminations are diverse, encompassing feelings of relief, empowerment, or distress, contingent upon individual circumstances and cultural contexts [7–9]. However, inadequate access to safe and comprehensive post-abortion care is a critical challenge [10]. In many areas, particularly in developing countries with restrictive abortion laws, unsafe abortion practices are prevalent, leading to severe medical complications and even maternal death [10]. While advances in healthcare technology and reproductive rights have improved access to care, many individuals still face significant barriers to comprehensive reproductive healthcare, including post-abortion care [11, 12]. These barriers may stem from logistical challenges, social stigma, legal restrictions, and limited availability of skilled healthcare providers, which together hinder the full realization of reproductive health benefits for all [13–15].

Among solutions to improve post-abortion care, mhealth technology has emerged as a promising tool to facilitate communication, provide information, and support self-care in the post-abortion period [16, 17]. This technology, which includes mobile–based applications and personal health records, provides a platform for remote monitoring, personalized care, and timely interventions [4, 18]. Mhealth technology empowers individuals to monitor their health, access healthcare resources, and connect with healthcare providers remotely [18]. The effectiveness of this technology depends on various factors that affect its acceptance and use [19]. Gynecologists, midwives, and patients play a fundamental role in determining the success of mhealth programs in post-abortion care [20–22]. Their understanding of factors such as perceived usefulness, ease of use, trust, and social norms significantly affects the behavior and acceptance of these technologies [23].

Mhealth technology empowers women to take control of their health, track symptoms, get access to educational resources, and communicate with healthcare providers remotely [24]. These technologies have the potential to increase adherence to follow-up care and reduce barriers to access to services [24]. Despite the high potential of mhealth technology in improving post-abortion care, there are numerous barriers to its widespread adoption and use. These barriers can be categorized into four groups: individual, social, cultural, and systemic barriers [20]. A deep understanding of these factors is necessary for designing effective interventions and strategies to maximize the benefits of this technology in post-abortion care [25]. Technological literacy, access to smartphones and the Internet, cultural attitudes towards technology-based care, privacy concerns, and healthcare providers’ views of digital health tools are among the key factors that influence the acceptance and use of mhealth technology in post-abortion care [17, 26–28].

This study was conducted in Iran, where access to reproductive healthcare, including post-abortion care, remains a challenge due to legal restrictions, healthcare infrastructure limitations, and sociocultural barriers [29, 30]. Abortion is legally restricted in most cases, except when the mother’s life is at risk or in cases of severe fetal anomalies, which creates significant obstacles to obtaining safe post-abortion care [29]. In addition, the healthcare system faces resource constraints and disparities, particularly in rural areas which cause further complication in access to essential reproductive services [31]. The region’s unique healthcare landscape is characterized by geographical remoteness, underdeveloped healthcare infrastructure in rural areas, and the cultural stigma surrounding abortion [30, 31]. In many parts of Iran, rural and remote populations face difficulties accessing healthcare facilities due to the long distances and limited transportation options. This is compounded by technological limitations, as digital health infrastructure and internet access may be less reliable in these areas. Furthermore, sociocultural factors, including the stigma associated with abortion, discourage women from seeking post-abortion care, making the need for innovative solutions like mhealth technologies even more pressing [30, 31]. These barriers provide a relevant context for exploring the adoption of mHealth technologies in post-abortion care, which could offer a confidential and accessible means of support for women in need. The current study aimed to investigate factors influencing acceptance and use of mhealth technology in post-abortion care. It appears that examining the multifaceted nature of technology acceptance in post-abortion care will lead to generating better strategies to overcome barriers and maximize the benefits of mhealth technology.

Methods

This was a mixed-methods study completed in 2023. In the first phase of the research, a literature review was conducted and factors influencing the use of mhealth technology in post-abortion care were identified [20]. Then, a questionnaire was developed based on the results of the first phase of the research to investigate these factors in a wider population. The results of the quantitative study were presented in this paper.

Research participants

The study participants were categorized into three distinct groups. The first group consisted of gynecologists with at least 3 years of work experience (n = 24), the second group consisted of midwives with at least 3 years of work experience (n = 38), and the third group was patients who had experienced abortion in the past 12 months, had computer literacy and had access to mhealth technologies (n = 183). Convenience sampling was used to select individuals in each group.

Research instrument

A questionnaire that was developed based on the findings of a literature review [20] was used for data collection (Appendix I). The validity of the questionnaire was assessed by the experts in reproductive health, obstetrics and gynecology, and medical informatics. Its reliability was also calculated using Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.932). The questionnaire was designed based on a five-point Likert scale (from “very important” (5) to “unimportant” (1)) and consisted of participant’s characteristics and two other sections: “motivating factors” and “inhibiting factors.” These two sections had a total of nine sub-sections and 45 questions. The motivating factors included 14 questions about individual factors, seven questions about technical factors, four questions about economic factors, two questions about organizational factors, and two questions about ethico-legal factors. The inhibiting factors included six questions about individual factors, seven questions about technical factors, one question about economic factors, and two questions about ethico-legal factors.

Data analysis

The data analysis was conducted in four stages. In the first stage, the collected data were entered into the SPSS software, version 24. Descriptive statistics, including frequency distribution, mean value, and standard deviation were used to analyze the data. In the second stage, to compare the views of different groups, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was first performed for each of the three groups participating in the study. Considering the normal distribution of data for all three participating groups, in the third stage, the analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was used to compare the means of different factors. Then, in the fourth stage, the post-hoc test was performed for further comparisons between the groups.

The decision to rank factors from the most to the least important was strategically guided by the objective of identifying the most critical motivational and inhibiting factors influencing the use of mhealth technology in post-abortion care. This approach allows policymakers and healthcare providers to effectively allocate resources and tailor interventions by focusing on the most pivotal determinants that affect the uptake and acceptance of these technologies. Ranking offers a nuanced and comprehensive overview of the key issues, thereby enhancing the ability to make informed, evidence-based decisions in resource-constrained settings. This prioritization not only aids in optimizing the deployment of mhealth solutions, but also ensures that critical barriers and facilitators are addressed in a manner that maximizes impact and effectiveness. By focusing on the most influential factors, we can improve the accessibility and quality of post-abortion care through the effective use of mhealth technologies.

Results

Among the eligible participants, 50% of gynecologists (n = 12), 60.5% of midwives (n = 23), and 44.2% of patients (n = 81) participated in this study. Demographic data analysis showed that most gynecologists (66.6%), almost all midwives (91.3%), and a significant proportion of patients (86.4%) belonged to the age group of 21–40. Additionally, most midwives (95.6%) and patients (76.5%) had a bachelor’s degree. In terms of the employment status, 48.2% of patients were employed and 51.8% were not employed. Based on the previous study [20], the motivational and inhibiting factors influencing the acceptance and use of mhealth technology in post-abortion care were divided into individual, technical, organizational, economic, and ethico-legal factors.

In terms of the individual motivating factors, gynecologists believed that the ownership of a mobile phone and the ease of access to care from anywhere at any time, both were important (4.75 ± 0.62) and receiving emotional support from the professional staff (3.91 ± 1.16) was the less important factor. From the midwives’ views, the desire to receive information about the complications of abortion (4.39 ± 0.471), obtain information about the methods of preventing re-pregnancy (4.34 ± 0.72), and have an interactive communication with professional staff (4.30 ± 0.82) had the highest mean values, and the level of patient’s education had the lowest mean value (3.60 ± 1.19). From the patients’ point of view, saving time (4.28 ± 0.91) and receiving unique support for each patient (4.22 ± 0.95) had the highest mean value, and the lowest mean was related to the level of patient’s education (3.22 ± 1.31).

The majority of the participants believed that all the mentioned technical motivational factors influenced the acceptance and use of mhealth technology in post-abortion care. From the gynecologists’ perspectives, ease of information retrieval (4.83 ± 0.38), ease of using mobile phones (4.75 ± 0.45), and user-friendliness (4.66 ± 0.49) were the most important technical motivational factors affecting the acceptance and use of mhealth technology. Midwives and patients thought that factors, such as the readability of messages and user-friendliness were the most important factors affecting the acceptance and use of mhealth technology in post-abortion care. The ability to support multiple languages also had the lowest mean value in all three groups (Table 2).

Distribution of the participants’ responses regarding the technical motivational factors

Among the organizational motivational factors affecting the acceptance and use of mhealth technology in post-abortion care, healthcare providers’ skills and elimination of the patient’s sense of shame had the highest mean values from the gynecologists’ (4.5 ± 0.52) and patients’ perspectives (4.23 ± 0.92). Most of the participants also believed that economic motivational factors were important in the acceptance and use of mhealth technology in post-abortion care.

Among the gynecologists, the highest mean values were related to comparing costs for different services (4.5 ± 0.52) and saving costs (4.5 ± 0.67), respectively. Among the midwives, the highest mean values were related to providing inexpensive mhealth services compared to face-to-face visits (4.3 ± 0.87) and saving costs (4.13 ± 0.86), and among patients, the highest mean values belonged to comparing costs for different services (4.19 ± 0.96), saving costs (4.14 ± 0.88), and providing inexpensive mhealth services compared to face-to-face visits (4.12 ± 1.05). It is noteworthy that in all three groups, the lowest mean value belonged to the patient’s financial status.

The ethico-legal motivational factors were also important for most of the participants in the study. From the gynecologists’ perspectives, information security and confidentiality (4.58 ± 0.90) and registering on the websites and passing identity check (4.5 ± 0.79) were important. From the perspective of midwives (4.43 ± 0.78) and patients (4.46 ± 0.85), information security and confidentiality had the highest mean values, too.

From the gynecologists’ perspectives, the most important individual inhibiting factors influencing the acceptance and use of mhealth technology were the lack of time to use mhealth applications (4.25 ± 0.75) and remembering difficulties of abortion (4.16 ± 1.52). From the midwives’ perspectives, concerns over incorrect and invalid information (4.21 ± 0.85), fear of talking about abortion (4.0 ± 1.04), and remembering difficulties of abortion (4.04 ± 0.82) were the most important individual inhibiting factors to the acceptance and use of mhealth technology in post-abortion care.

However, from the patients’ perspectives, concerns over incorrect and invalid information (4.12 ± 1) were the most important individual inhibiting factors. In terms of the technical inhibiting factors, gynecologists thought that receiving duplicate messages (4.75 ± 0.45), problems with the mobile network or the Internet (4.66 ± 0.65) were the most important technical factors, and the lack of multilingual support was the least important one (4.08 ± 0.79). From the midwives’ perspectives, the highest mean value was related to the problems with the mobile network or the Internet (4.13 ± 0.86), and the lowest mean values belonged to inappropriate number of messages received via mobile phone (3.52 ± 1.03) and usability issues (3.56 ± 0.99).

From the patients’ perspectives, the highest mean values were related to the problems with the mobile network or the Internet (4.02 ± 1.02) and the same problem in the deprived areas (3.96 ± 1.08). The lowest mean values were related to the lack of multilingual support (3.50 ± 1.26) and receiving duplicate messages (3.55 ± 1.23).

In terms of the economic factors, gynecologists believed that patient inappropriate financial status influenced acceptance and use of mhealth in post-abortion care (4.25 ± 0.86). In addition, the most important ethico-legal challenge in all three groups was “concerns over the lack of privacy”, and the mean values for gynecologists, midwives, and patients were 4.66 ± 0.77, 4.34 ± 0.88, and 4.35 ± 0.89, respectively.

Discussion

The use of mhealth technologies can improve the quality of care and service delivery, reduce human error, and decrease healthcare cost [32]. Despite the high potential of this technology to provide healthcare services anytime and anywhere, user acceptance and adoption are the main concerns of system developers [33]. In fact, the use of mhealth technology does not necessarily mean its high acceptance [34] and this area needs further investigations [35]. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate factors influencing acceptance and use of mhealth technology in post-abortion care.

The results of the study showed that from the gynecologists’ perspectives, the highest mean value was related to the technical motivational factors and ethico-legal inhibiting factors, and the lowest mean value belonged to the individual inhibiting factors. Among midwives, the highest mean value was related to the ethico-legal motivational and inhibiting factors, and the lowest mean value belonged to the technical inhibiting factors. Among patients, the highest mean value was related to the organizational motivational factors, and the lowest mean value was for the individual and technical inhibiting factors. Although all the factors examined in this study were important, it seems that the individual, organizational, economic, and ethico-legal motivating factors as well as the ethico-legal inhibiting factors were more important than other factors for different groups of the participants and had higher mean values.

According to the literature, one of the reasons for willingness to use mhealth technology is the need to talk about the health condition and, if necessary, get advice through getting easy access to care from anywhere and at any time [36]. In fact, receiving constant support and having access to healthcare providers whenever needed can motivate people to use the technology [37]. In addition, a sense of trust in healthcare providers, confidence in receiving care and improving health [38], a sense of not being judged, and being followed-up by care providers are other factors that can affect the acceptance and use of these technologies by patients [39].

In terms of the economic motivational factors, it can be said that saving money is one of the benefits and reasons for the acceptance and use of mhealth technology. The patients will be only responsible to pay for the cost of visiting healthcare providers, using a mobile phone, and connecting to the Internet which is much lower than the cost of in-person visits. Therefore, patients are more likely to use mhealth technologies to monitor their health [40]. It seems that transparency in reimbursements and clear pricing of services will be other factors affecting the use of mhealth technologies [41]. The study by Zhou et al. pointed out that users are more likely to accept free mhealth applications [42].

Regarding the ethico-legal motivational factors, the concepts of trust, privacy and confidentiality of information are of paramount importance. Features like a private user profile, authentication methods, and robust data security practices are crucial for encouraging users to accept the technology. The level of trust is formed based on the reliability and predictability of the technology [43]. Trust in the providers of mhealth technologies, data protection mechanisms, and physicians who recommend the technology can also be effective in increasing acceptance and use of these technologies [44–46]. If security features, such as regular password updates, the ability to delete information remotely, obtaining user consent, and access control are strengthened, the possibility of accepting and using these applications will increase [27, 42]. Defining the access level to information is also another effective motivational factor in the acceptance of mhealth technologies [47].

Another issue related to the ethico-legal inhibiting factors is the concerns of users over the confidentiality and privacy [48] which directly affects the acceptance and use of mhealth technology [49]. Privacy means that users determine who can access their personal health information, what type of data are collected, stored, and used, what kind of rights they have over the data, and what the potential risks of information disclosure are. However, in most cases, users have low technological literacy and mhealth designers should develop systems that allow users to have control over their data [50]. Although users are generally aware of the need to protect the privacy of their data, they accept the risk of possible misuse of private data when using technology. Therefore, privacy and confidentiality of information can affect the acceptance and use of mhealth technology depending on the type of data and the type of mhealth technology [51]. One of the reasons why doctors do not recommend the use of mhealth technology to patients is their concerns about information confidentiality and patient privacy [52].

The one-way ANOVA analysis revealed significant differences in responses related to the individual (F = 4.18, p = 0.018) and technical (F = 4.47, p = 0.014) motivating factors, suggesting variability in how different groups of participants perceive the importance of these factors. The technical inhibiting factors also showed significant differences (F = 3.99, p = 0.021), indicating variations in technical challenges faced by the participants. However, the organizational and economic motivating factors did not exhibit significant differences, nor did individual and economic inhibiting factors. Moreover, the findings showed that there was a statistically significant difference between the opinions of gynecologists and patients regarding the individual and technical motivating factors, and technical inhibiting factors. There was also a statistically significant difference between the opinions of gynecologists, midwives, and patients regarding the technical motivating factors. It is noteworthy that the mean values obtained for the gynecologists were higher for the individual and technical motivating factors, and the lowest mean value was related to the individual inhibiting factors. For midwives and patients, the highest mean values belonged to the individual motivating factors, and the lowest mean values were related to the technical inhibiting factors. Similarly, some studies highlighted the importance of the individual and technical motivating factors by physicians and other healthcare providers. For example, Kaphle et al. [53] discussed the role of individual motivating factors such as education level, experience of using technology, age, and training in using the the technology. Regarding the technical motivating factors, Jacob et al. [54] found that factors such as the usefulness of the technology, appropriate design, compatibility with other systems, content quality, personalization, and ease of use were some technical factors influencing the acceptance and use of mhealth technology [55]. Overall, it seems that healthcare providers recommend the use of mhealth technologies, because of the ease of access to care and the patient’s comfort in expressing problems in a non-face-to-face environment [56].

The differences between opinions might be related to the individual’s experience of using various technologies. For example, gynecologists paid more attention to the technical motivating and inhibiting factors, while patients and midwives highlighted the role of other factors. The results of various studies indicated that individual motivating factors have a higher priority among patients. For example, Stefanicka et al. reported that factors such as motivation to change lifestyle, improve self-management skills, monitor health status, reduce the number of visits, and save time were effective factors influencing the acceptance and use of mhealth technology [41]. In the study of Simblett et al., factors such as the desire to receive accurate and reliable information, perceived usefulness, access to clinical support, and level of education were considered effective factors in the use of mhealth technology [46]. Several other studies have also pointed to the impact of age on the use of technology [57, 58]. Therefore, it can be said that the results of the present study are in line with the findings of other similar research.

Research implications

Mhealth technology presents both opportunities and challenges when applied to post-abortion care, particularly in stigmatized settings [25, 59, 60]. For many, the stigma surrounding abortion can make seeking in-person care difficult, with fears of judgment. Mhealth platforms offer a discreet al.ternative, allowing individuals to access care privately with greater autonomy [25, 59, 60]. However, this sensitivity can amplify concerns about data privacy and security, potentially discouraging the use of technology [25, 60]. To address these concerns, developers must prioritize strong privacy protections and secure communication features, ensuring that mhealth solutions are trusted and accessible, especially in the environments where abortion is heavily stigmatized [25, 59, 60]. Addressing these factors, particularly during the design and implementation of new technologies can significantly enhance their acceptance and use.

Research limitations

One of the limitations of his study was related to the limited participation of gynecologists, midwives, and patients in completing the questionnaire. This might be due to the reluctance to the subject of the study or their workload. However, as data were collected from three different teaching hospitals, we believe that the results can be generalizable to a bigger population. Another limitation might be related to the nature of the research which was quantitative. In fact, conducting qualitative studies can help to explore users’ experiences and other factors which may influence the use of mhealth technology in post-abortion care.

Conclusion

The results showed that several factors with various degrees of importance play pivotal roles in the acceptance and use of mhealth technology in post-abortion care. Organizational factors, including easy access to care, receiving continues support from healthcare providers, and a sense of trust in the healthcare system, emerged as the influential factors of the use of technology in post abortion care. Economic considerations, such as cost savings and transparent pricing structures underscored the significance of financial factors. Ethico-legal factors, particularly concerns surrounding patient privacy and data security, were also deemed crucial in shaping acceptance behaviors. Individual motivating factors, such as the desire to receive personalized care and emotional support, were rated highly by both healthcare providers and patients, indicating the importance of tailoring mhealth interventions to the individual needs. Technical factors, including usability, reliability, and accessibility, were also considered important but ranked slightly lower in terms of importance compared to the organizational, economic, and ethico-legal factors. These findings highlight the multifaceted nature of technology acceptance and emphasize the importance of addressing a diverse range of factors to promote the successful implementation and use of mhealth solutions in post-abortion care.

In addition, the significant differences between the opinions of the technology users suggested that when developing new technologies, the opinions of end users (healthcare providers, patients, etc.) should be considered separately to increase the level of the technology acceptance by different users. In future studies, the level of acceptance and use of mhealth technology in post-abortion care can be compared before and after considering motivating and inhibiting factors to understand their impact. In addition, it is recommended to evaluate different types of relevant health information technologies in post-abortion care from the users’ perspectives to gain a deeper insight into their preferences and needs. Finally, examining the effectiveness of mhealth technology in reducing post-abortion complications will provide valuable information for improving healthcare outcomes in this area.
