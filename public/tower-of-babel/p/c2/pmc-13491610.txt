Associations of the atherogenic index of plasma and its modified indices with the incidence and progression of cardiometabolic multimorbidity in individuals with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome stages 0–3: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Fei Xue, Qingmei Han, Yifei Li, Cheng Cheng, Lin Xie, Jianmin Yang, Jiye Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome stages 0–3 reflect a heterogeneous continuum of metabolic, kidney, and cardiovascular abnormalities that are closely associated with the development of cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs). Although the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) and obesity-related composite indices have been associated with individual CMDs, their associations with cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) among individuals with CKM syndrome remain unclear. This study primarily investigated whether AIP-related indices were associated with the incidence and progression of CMM in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 346,868 UK Biobank participants without baseline type 2 diabetes (T2DM), coronary heart disease (CHD), or stroke. CMM was defined as the coexistence of at least two of these conditions. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate associations with incident CMM, while multistate models were applied to characterize disease progression. As secondary analyses, the added predictive value of each index was assessed using discrimination and reclassification metrics. Exploratory biomarker analyses examined whether inflammatory, liver, and renal biomarkers statistically accounted for part of the associations.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 15.9 years, 8573 participants developed CMM. All eight AIP-related indices were positively associated with incident CMM, with hazard ratios per 1-SD increment ranging from 1.12 to 1.37 (all P < 0.001). Multistate analyses suggested stage-specific associations during CMM progression, with stronger associations observed for the transition from baseline to T2DM and from single CMD to CMM involving CHD (all P < 0.001). In secondary prediction analyses, adding each index to conventional risk factors improved discrimination and reclassification, with the AIP-body roundness index (AIP-BRI) showing the greatest overall incremental predictive value. Exploratory biomarker analyses indicated that inflammatory, liver, and renal biomarkers jointly accounted for 32.5–46.4% of the associations, although these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of causal mediation.ConclusionAmong individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3, AIP-related indices were positively associated with the incidence and progression of CMM, supporting their potential utility for CMM risk stratification within the CKM framework. These indices, particularly AIP-BRI, may provide additional predictive information. Exploratory biomarker findings might provide preliminary clues for future mechanistic research.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03255-4.

Introduction

Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome, recently defined by the American Heart Association (AHA), describes the interrelated dysfunction of metabolic health, kidney function, and the cardiovascular system [1]. The AHA classifies CKM syndrome into five stages [1]. Stages 0–3 represent a heterogeneous continuum from apparently healthy status to subclinical or clinically evident CKM abnormalities before advanced cardiovascular disease (CVD), offering opportunities for prevention, risk stratification, and disease monitoring that may differ across stages [2, 3]. However, existing studies in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 have mainly focused on CVD and mortality [4–11], with less attention given to cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) [12]. CMM, commonly defined as the coexistence of at least two major cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs), including type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), coronary heart disease (CHD), and stroke [13], represents an advanced and clinically important manifestation of disease accumulation within the CKM continuum. Compared with a single CMD, CMM is associated with greater mortality risk, reduced life expectancy, and a higher overall disease burden [14, 15]. Accordingly, identifying practical markers associated with both the incidence and progression of CMM in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 may help improve risk assessment and longitudinal monitoring.

The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), derived from triglyceride and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels, has increasingly been recognized as a marker of insulin resistance (IR) and lipid metabolism [16–18]. Epidemiological studies have linked AIP to incident CVD in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 [4–8, 11]. However, most available evidence has mainly addressed single-disease endpoints rather than multimorbidity or disease progression across the CKM continuum [4–8, 11]. In addition, obesity-related AIP composite indices, including AIP-body mass index (AIP-BMI), AIP-waist circumference (AIP-WC), AIP-waist-to-height ratio (AIP-WHtR), AIP-body roundness index (AIP-BRI), AIP-a body shape index (AIP-ABSI), AIP-weight-adjusted waist index (AIP-WWI), and AIP-visceral adiposity index (AIP-VAI), may capture the combined burden of atherogenic dyslipidemia and adiposity. Although previous studies have associated AIP with CMM in the general population [19–21], and a recent UK Biobank study reported associations between AIP-BRI and cardio-renal-metabolic disease progression [22], it remains unclear whether AIP and its obesity-related composite indices are associated with the incidence and dynamic progression of CMM among individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. Additionally, although AIP has been associated with biomarkers reflecting systemic inflammation [23, 24], liver function [25, 26], and kidney function [26, 27], and these biomarkers have also been linked to CMM [28], whether they statistically account for part of the associations between AIP-related indices and CMM remains uncertain. Addressing these questions may help clarify the clinical relevance of AIP-related indices within the CKM framework.

To address these gaps, we leveraged data from the UK Biobank cohort to investigate, among individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3, the associations between eight AIP-related indices and the incidence and dynamic progression of CMM. Furthermore, we compared the predictive performance of these indices for incident CMM. As exploratory analyses, we examined whether selected baseline inflammatory, liver, and renal biomarkers statistically accounted for part of the associations.

Methods

Study participants

Data for this study were drawn from the UK Biobank, an ongoing large-scale population-based prospective cohort that enrolled more than 500,000 middle-aged and older adults aged 37–73 years from 22 assessment centers across England, Scotland, and Wales during 2006–2010. Health-related data were collected using touchscreen questionnaires, standardized physical measurements, face-to-face interviews, medical diagnostic information, and biological samples such as blood, urine, and saliva. The study design and data collection procedures have been described in detail previously [29]. Ethical approval was provided by the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee (reference: 21/NW/0157), and all participants gave written informed consent. The current study was conducted under the UK Biobank application number 151350.

Among the 502,112 participants enrolled in the UK Biobank, those with unavailable data for AIP-related indices were first excluded (n = 75,254), leaving 426,858 participants for further screening. We subsequently excluded 11,649 participants with insufficient information required for CKM staging, resulting in 415,209 participants with complete data on CKM staging (Table S1). Participants with prevalent CHD (n = 22,424), stroke (n = 5711), or T2DM (n = 7977) at baseline were then removed (n = 36,112), leaving 379,097 individuals without CMDs at baseline. After excluding 32,229 participants with missing covariate data, 346,868 individuals were included in the main association analysis. Additionally, a further 11,942 participants were excluded because of missing biomarker data, yielding a final sample of 334,926 for the biomarker mediation analysis. CKM syndrome stages 0–3

CKM syndrome was classified into stages 0–4 according to established criteria [2, 3], with stages 0–3 representing the subclinical phases and constituting the target population of this study. The stage definitions were consistent with those reported previously [10]. Stage 0 indicated no evidence of CKM-related risk factors, including normal BMI, WC, blood glucose, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), lipid levels, and renal function, and no symptoms of CVD. Stage 1 was characterized by the presence of at least one metabolic abnormality, namely BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, elevated WC (≥ 88 cm in women or ≥ 102 cm in men), or prediabetes [glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) between 5.7% and 6.4%]. Stage 2 was defined by the presence of metabolic risk factors, moderate- to high-risk chronic kidney disease (CKD), or a combination of both. Metabolic risk factors included hypertension, diabetes, triglycerides (TG) ≥ 135 mg/dL, or metabolic syndrome. Moderate- to high-risk CKD was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) between 30 and 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, calculated using the CKD-EPI 2021 creatinine-based equation. Hypertension was defined as self-reported hypertension, SBP ≥ 130 mmHg, DBP ≥ 80 mmHg, or use of antihypertensive medications. Diabetes was defined as HbA1c ≥ 6.4%, previous diabetes diagnosis, or use of glucose-lowering medications. Metabolic syndrome was defined as the presence of at least three of the following criteria: increased WC, reduced HDL-C < 40 mg/dL in men or < 50 mg/dL in women, TG > 150 mg/dL, elevated BP (SBP ≥ 130 mmHg or DBP ≥ 80 mmHg), or prediabetes. Stage 3 included individuals with subclinical CVD or advanced CKD, defined as eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2. Subclinical CVD was defined as high predicted cardiovascular risk according to the Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation 2 (SCORE2) model, which estimates the risk of adverse cardiovascular events in European individuals without diagnosed CVD [30].

AIP-related indices

After providing written informed consent, peripheral venous blood samples were collected from randomly selected UK Biobank participants. All biospecimens were processed, fractionated, and stored at − 80 °C according to standardized protocols [29, 31]. A comprehensive panel of blood biomarkers, including hematological indices and biochemical markers, was assessed at baseline in approximately 500,000 participants. Blood count parameters were measured in EDTA-anticoagulated samples using Beckman Coulter LH750 analyzers for automated cell counting and leukocyte differentiation. Biochemical markers, including serum TG and HDL-C concentrations, were quantified using a combination of immunoassay analyzers (DiaSorin Liaison XL and Beckman Coulter DXI 800) and clinical chemistry analyzers (Beckman Coulter AU5800 and Siemens Advia 1800). Quality control procedures were rigorously applied to ensure data accuracy and reliability. Candidate biomarkers for exploratory mediation analyses

To explore whether selected baseline biomarkers statistically accounted for part of the associations between AIP-related indices and incident CMM in individuals with CKM stages 0–3, we assessed a panel of blood biomarkers informed by previous studies [23–28]. This panel included both blood count and blood biochemistry markers and was categorized into three groups: (1) liver function markers, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), total bilirubin (TB), total protein (TP), and albumin; (2) kidney function markers, including urea, creatinine, cystatin C, and urate; and (3) inflammation-related markers, including C-reactive protein (CRP), white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophil count, monocyte count, lymphocyte count, and platelet count. Additional details on selected biomarkers are presented in Table S2.

Assessment of CMM

Incident CMM was the outcome of interest and was defined as the coexistence of at least two CMDs in individuals at CKM stages 0–3. We identified incident CMDs using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes: E11 for T2DM, I60-I64 and I69 for stroke, and I20-I25 for CHD. Following previous studies [28], we used the UK Biobank ‘First occurrence’ fields (Category ID: 1712), which capture information from hospital records, primary care data, self-reported conditions, and death registrations, to determine disease onset. Hospital admission data were obtained through linkage with national inpatient databases, including Hospital Episode Statistics for England, Scottish Morbidity Records for Scotland, and the Patient Episode Database for Wales, and mortality data were obtained through linkage with the National Health Service death registry [29]. The incidence date of CMM was defined as the date when the second CMD was diagnosed. Follow-up time was calculated from baseline recruitment until the earliest of second CMD diagnosis, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of follow-up.

Covariates

Drawing on previous literature and clinical considerations [7, 8, 10, 11, 13, 28], covariates were prespecified. We adjusted for multiple sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors that may confound the associations between AIP-related indices and incident CMM. Sociodemographic variables were age (years), sex, ethnicity (White/non-White), Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI; higher values indicating greater socioeconomic deprivation), and educational attainment (college degree or above/lower). Lifestyle variables comprised smoking status (never, former, or current), frequency of drinking (never, special occasions only, 1–3 times/month, 1–2 times/week, 3–4 times/week, or daily/almost daily), and physical activity (PA) levels (adequate/inadequate). Adequate physical activity was defined as ≥ 75 min/week of vigorous activity, ≥ 150 min/week of moderate activity, or an equivalent combination, or moderate activity ≥ 5 times/week, or vigorous activity ≥ 1 time/week [33]. Clinical covariates included HbA1c (continuous, mmol/mol), pre-existing hypertension and cancer (yes/no), use of antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and antihyperglycemic medications (yes/no), and a family history of CMDs, defined as self-reported CMD diagnosis in a father, mother, or sibling (yes/no). These clinical covariates were included because they represent important baseline factors that may influence both AIP-related indices and subsequent CMM risk. Specifically, HbA1c reflects baseline glycemic status; pre-existing hypertension indicates established cardiometabolic risk burden [34]; pre-existing cancer may be associated with altered metabolic profiles and subsequent cardiometabolic risk through disease- or treatment-related pathways [35]; medication use reflects the clinical management of major cardiometabolic risk factors and may influence metabolic measurements [36]; and family history of CMDs captures familial susceptibility to cardiometabolic outcomes [37]. Participants with missing covariate data were excluded from the primary analysis. In the sensitivity analysis, they were addressed using multiple imputation. The proportion of missing values for each covariate is presented in Table S3, and detailed definitions of the covariates are provided in Table S4.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive analyses

For the baseline descriptive analysis, participants were grouped according to CMM status or by quartiles of the AIP (Q1–Q4). Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations (SD), whereas categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. Group differences in continuous variables were assessed using independent-samples t tests or one-way analysis of variance, and categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests.

Prospective association analyses

To assess the relationship between AIP-related indices and incident CMM, we fitted multivariable Cox proportional hazards models in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3, using follow-up time as the time axis. The proportional hazards assumption was examined by Schoenfeld residual testing, with no evidence of significant violation. Effect estimates were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for both continuous exposure analyses, expressed per SD increase, and categorical analyses by quartiles, using the lowest quartile (Q1) as the reference. Quartile-based trend tests were conducted by modeling the quartile variable as a continuous term. Three progressively adjusted models were constructed. Model 1 adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics, including age, sex, ethnicity, TDI, and educational attainment. Model 2 additionally accounted for lifestyle factors, including smoking status, frequency of drinking, and PA levels. Model 3 further controlled for clinical factors, including HbA1c, pre-existing hypertension, pre-existing cancer, use of antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and antihyperglycemic medications, and family history of CMDs. Kaplan-Meier curves were used to depict crude cumulative incidence across quartiles, and differences were assessed with log-rank tests. Reference to previous work [5], restricted cubic spline (RCS) functions with four knots placed at the 5th (reference), 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the AIP-related indices were fitted within the fully adjusted Cox model to examine their dose-response relationships with incident CMM.

Longitudinal progression analyses

A multi-state modeling framework was employed to further evaluate the contribution of AIP-related indices to CMM progression. This framework expands upon traditional competing-risk models by allowing risk factor effects to be examined simultaneously across different stages of disease progression [38, 39]. Following previous work [28], six transition pathways were constructed (Fig. 2): (A) from healthy status at baseline to T2DM (n = 18,160; 5.2%); (B) from healthy status at baseline to stroke (n = 12,104; 3.5%); (C) from healthy status at baseline to CHD (n = 25,743; 7.4%); (D) from T2DM to CMM (n = 2852; 15.7%); (E) from stroke to CMM (n = 1984; 16.4%); and (F) from CHD to CMM (n = 3737; 14.5%). All transition-specific analyses used the same covariate adjustment structure as the primary Cox models (Model 3). As described previously [13], when participants transitioned through multiple disease stages on the same date, the preceding stage was assigned an entry date 0.5 days earlier than the subsequent stage. Accordingly, the onset date of the first CMD was defined as occurring 0.5 days before the onset of CMM.

Predictive analyses

Consistent with prior literature [5, 12], we evaluated the predictive performance of eight AIP-related indices for incident CMM using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Pairwise AUC comparisons were conducted with the DeLong test to identify the best-performing index [40]. To investigate whether AIP-related indices offered additional predictive value beyond established risk factors, two nested models were built according to previous studies [10, 12, 28]. In the primary incremental prediction analyses, the conventional model was defined as the fully adjusted Model 3 used in the association analyses. Since no widely accepted prediction model has yet been established for incident CMM across CKM syndrome stages 0–3, we employed a comprehensive set of prespecified sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors to capture the baseline risk of incident CMM as thoroughly as possible. Specifically, the conventional model included age, sex, ethnicity, TDI, educational attainment, smoking status, frequency of drinking, PA levels, HbA1c, pre-existing hypertension, pre-existing cancer, use of antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and antihyperglycemic medications, and family history of CMDs. Each extended model was constructed by adding one AIP-related index individually to the conventional model. The incremental predictive contribution of each index was assessed using the net reclassification index (NRI), integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), and C-index [41, 42]. The NRI and IDI capture improvements in risk classification and discrimination, respectively, whereas the C-index reflects the overall discriminatory performance of the prediction model. To assess whether the incremental predictive value of AIP-related indices was robust to alternative specifications of the reference model, we additionally repeated the incremental prediction analyses using three alternative reference models. These included two established cardiovascular risk prediction equations, namely SCORE2 and the AHA Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease EVENTs (PREVENT) Eqs [30, 43]., and a lipid biomarker-based model incorporating non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (Non-HDL-C), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), and apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1). For each reference model, each AIP-related index was added separately to examine whether these indices provided incremental predictive information beyond the corresponding baseline predictors.

Exploratory mediation analyses of baseline biomarker associations

To examine whether selected baseline biomarkers statistically accounted for part of the association between AIP-related indices and incident CMM among individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3, we conducted exploratory mediation analyses. Consistent with previous analytical frameworks [28, 44], candidate blood biomarkers were screened using a two-stage selection procedure. First, multivariable-adjusted linear regression models based on Model 3 were used to assess the associations between AIP-related indices and individual biomarkers. Second, Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for the covariates in Model 3 were applied to evaluate the associations between each biomarker and incident CMM. Biomarkers that were significant in both stages and showed consistent directions of association were included in the exploratory mediation analyses [45, 46]. Following established methods [46, 47], these exploratory mediation analyses were performed using the CMAverse R package [47] within a regression-based framework for Cox proportional hazards models to estimate direct and indirect associations and the proportion statistically accounted for, reported using the conventional term proportion mediated (PM), with all estimates expressed on the HR scale. Statistical inference was based on 1,000 nonparametric bootstrap resamples to derive 95% CIs and P values. All biomarker variables were standardized before analysis. Importantly, because both the AIP-related indices and candidate biomarkers were measured at baseline, these analyses cannot determine temporal precedence between exposures and biomarkers and should be interpreted as exploratory decompositions of associations rather than evidence of causal mediation or biological mechanisms.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

The following analyses were conducted to test the stability of the findings. First, to address potential reverse causation, the analyses were repeated after excluding individuals who developed CMM during the first 5 years of follow-up. Second, missing covariate data were further handled using multiple imputation with chained equations. Third, to account for the competing risk of death from causes other than CMM, Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models were fitted in the overall population. Given the long follow up period and the high likelihood of death as a competing event among older participants, the competing risk analyses were further repeated after stratifying participants by baseline age into < 60 years and ≥ 60 years groups. Fourth, to account for potential changes in medication use during follow up, additional Cox proportional hazards models were fitted with lipid-lowering medication use, antihypertensive medication use, and antihyperglycemic medication use modeled as time-varying covariates. Fifth, we redefined CKM stage 3 using PREVENT equations and repeated the association analyses. Sixth, to better reflect the full CKM framework, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis by expanding the analytical population to individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–4. Moreover, subgroup analyses were performed according to age (< 60 vs. ≥60 years), sex (female vs. male), CKM stage (0–3), and baseline lipid-lowering medication use (no vs. yes) to examine the consistency of the observed associations across subgroups. Interaction analyses were conducted by adding product terms between each AIP-related index and the corresponding subgroup variable to the fully adjusted model. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.0). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In biomarker-related analyses, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied, and FDR-adjusted P values < 0.05 were regarded as significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the 346,868 participants with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 (mean [SD] age, 56.1 [8.1] years; 55.3% women) are presented in Table 1. Participants who developed CMM differed significantly from those who did not (all P < 0.001). Specifically, they were more likely to be older, male, non-White, less educated, socioeconomically deprived, and at CKM stage 3. They also tended to have less adequate PA, were more likely to be current or ever smokers, and showed a less favorable pattern of alcohol consumption. In addition, they had higher HbA1c levels and a greater prevalence of self-reported hypertension and cancer, as well as more frequent use of antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and antihyperglycemic medications. A family history of CMD was also more common among participants who developed CMM. Moreover, all baseline AIP-related indices were significantly higher in participants with incident CMM than in those without CMM. Similar baseline characteristics were observed when participants were grouped according to quartiles of the baseline AIP (Table S5).

Prospective associations of AIP-related indices with incident CMM

During a median follow-up of 15.9 years (interquartile range: 13.8–16.9 years), 8573 participants (2.5%) developed CMM. KM curves by quartiles of AIP-related indices presented a stepwise increase in cumulative CMM hazard, with the highest quartile exhibiting the greatest risk (all log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 3). As shown in Fig. 4, RCS analyses revealed significant nonlinear associations of AIP-related indices with incident CMM (all P for nonlinearity < 0.001). Specifically, the associations for AIP, AIP-BMI, AIP-WC, AIP-WHtR, AIP-ABSI, AIP-VAI, and AIP-WWI were generally positive, with hazards increasing as index levels increased, particularly in the higher exposure range. In contrast, AIP-BRI showed a U-shaped relationship, suggesting elevated risk at both relatively low and high levels. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), all eight AIP-related indices were significantly associated with a higher risk of incident CMM (all P < 0.001; Table 2). Per 1-SD increment, the HRs ranged from 1.12 to 1.37. When analyzed by quartiles, participants in the highest quartile of each index had a significantly greater risk of incident CMM compared with those in the lowest quartile, with HRs of 2.27 (95% CI 2.10–2.46) for AIP, 2.19 (95% CI 2.03–2.36) for AIP-BMI, 2.19 (95% CI 2.03–2.35) for AIP-WC, 2.17 (95% CI 2.02–2.34) for AIP-WHtR, 2.02 (95% CI 1.88–2.16) for AIP-BRI, 2.20 (95% CI 2.04–2.37) for AIP-ABSI, 2.25 (95% CI 2.08–2.44) for AIP-VAI, and 2.22 (95% CI 2.06–2.39) for AIP-WWI. A significant positive trend was observed for all indices (all P for trend < 0.001), further supporting a monotonically increasing risk of CMM with ascending quartile levels.

Role of AIP-related indices in the CMM progression

During follow-up, among participants with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 and free of CMDs at baseline, 18,160 (5.2%) developed T2DM, 12,104 (3.5%) developed stroke, and 25,743 (7.4%) developed CHD. Among those with incident CMDs, progression to CMM occurred in 2852 (15.7%) with T2DM, 1984 (16.4%) with stroke, and 3737 (14.5%) with CHD. Multistate analyses showed that all eight AIP-related indices were significantly associated with an increased risk of transition from baseline ‘healthy’ status to T2DM and CHD, as well as subsequent progression from any individual CMDs to CMM. The strongest associations were observed for the transition from baseline ‘healthy’ status to T2DM, with HRs per 1-SD increment ranging from 1.24 to 1.91, whereas associations for transition to CHD were moderate (HRs: 1.13–1.30, all P < 0.001). In contrast, no clear association was observed for the transition to stroke. For subsequent progression to CMM, the associations were more pronounced for transitions from CHD (HRs: 1.13–1.27) to CMM and stroke to CMM (HRs: 1.13–1.23, all P < 0.001), while weaker for transition from T2DM to CMM but remained statistically significant for all indices (HRs: 1.02–1.06, all P < 0.05).

Predictive performance of AIP-related indices for CMM incidence

The ROC curve analysis revealed that AIP-BRI exhibited the strongest predictive ability for CMM (AUC = 0.667, 95% CI 0.661–0.673) (Fig. 5A). Pairwise comparisons revealed that the AUC value of AIP-BRI was higher than all other indices (all P < 0.01; Fig. 5B). As shown in Table 4, adding each AIP-related index to the conventional model significantly improved the prediction of incident CMM, as reflected by increases in the C-index and significant improvements in both NRI and IDI (all P < 0.001).

Exploratory biomarker associations in relation to AIP-related indices and incident CMM

We further investigated the associations of AIP-related indices with 17 baseline biomarkers reflecting systemic inflammation and hepatic and renal function, the associations between these biomarkers and incident CMM, and whether these biomarkers statistically accounted for part of the observed associations between AIP-related indices and CMM. The exploratory mediation analyses were restricted to biomarkers showing statistically significant associations with consistent directions in both models. All biomarkers fulfilled the inclusion criteria except albumin in the AIP-VAI model (all FDR-adjusted P < 0.01; Table S9). Furthermore, all selected biomarkers other than TP were significantly associated with incident CMM (all FDR-adjusted P < 0.001; Table S10). Therefore, 16 biomarkers were included in the exploratory mediation analyses for all AIP-related indices except AIP-VAI, for which 15 biomarkers were included. Detailed findings are summarized in Table S11 and Fig. 6. Specifically, all renal-related biomarkers statistically accounted for part of the observed associations (all FDR-adjusted P < 0.05), with urate accounting for the largest estimated proportion of the associations (PM: 12.5%-23.5%), followed by cystatin C (6.4%-9.3%), whereas the estimates for urea and creatinine were generally less than 1%. Moreover, all liver function-related markers except TB demonstrated statistically significant contributions across AIP-related indices (all FDR-adjusted P < 0.01). ALT accounted for the largest estimated proportion of the associations (6.6–14.2%), followed by GGT (4.0–9.0%), ALP (3.9–6.4%), AST (1.3–4.7%), and albumin (1.5–1.7%). Additionally, inflammatory markers, except lymphocytes in the AIP-BMI model, were significantly associated with the observed associations (all FDR-adjusted P < 0.05). Specifically, neutrophils accounted for the largest estimated proportion of the associations (PM: 6.6–9.3%), followed by CRP (4.6–5.9%) and WBCs (3.4–5.2%). Overall, these baseline biomarkers collectively accounted for an estimated 32.5% (95% CI: 29.6–35.0%) to 46.4% (95% CI: 43.4–49.3%) of the observed associations between AIP-related indices and incident CMM. Given the concurrent baseline assessment of the AIP-related indices and candidate biomarkers, these results should be interpreted as exploratory statistical decompositions of the observed associations rather than evidence of temporal or causal biological pathways.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

Several sensitivity and subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the associations between AIP-related indices and incident CMM. The associations remained stable after excluding events occurring during the first 5 years of follow-up (Table S12). Comparable findings were observed after imputing missing covariate data (Table S13). In the competitive risk analyses, the associations were attenuated but persisted in the overall population as well as among participants with baseline ages < 60 and ≥ 60 years, although the associations appeared to be more pronounced among relatively younger participants (Tables S14 and S15). Furthermore, after modeling medication use as time-varying covariates, the observed associations remained generally consistent with the primary findings (Table S16). When CKM stage 3 was redefined using high predicted cardiovascular risk estimated by the PREVENT equations, the associations were also largely unchanged (Table S17). When the analytical population was expanded to CKM syndrome stages 0–4, the associations remained generally consistent with the primary analyses (Table S18). Moreover, subgroup analyses stratified by age and sex indicated that the associations persisted across subpopulations, but were more pronounced in younger individuals (< 60 years) and in women (all P for interaction < 0.001; Tables S19 and S20). In analyses stratified by CKM stage, AIP-related indices showed broadly consistent associations with incident CMM across CKM stages 0 to 3 (all P for interaction > 0.05; Table S21). The estimates were generally similar among participants in stages 0–2 and tended to be slightly stronger among those in stage 3, although the interaction tests were not statistically significant. The estimates in this subgroup analysis represented the stage-specific relative associations between each AIP-related index and incident CMM, rather than direct comparisons of the baseline risk or absolute incidence of CMM across CKM stages. In additional analyses stratified by baseline lipid-lowering medication use, the positive associations were observed in both participants, but were consistently stronger among participants not using lipid-lowering medication (all P for interaction < 0.001; Table S22).

Discussion

In this large prospective cohort of 346,868 participants with CKM syndrome stages 0–3, all eight AIP-related indices were positively associated with the risk of incident CMM during long-term follow-up. These associations remained robust across multiple sensitivity analyses. Beyond conventional risk factors, each AIP-related index provided additional predictive value for CMM, with AIP-BRI showing the best overall predictive performance. Multi-state analyses further indicated that the associations varied across disease transitions, with stronger associations for the transition from baseline to T2DM and from CHD to CMM. In exploratory mediation analyses, baseline biomarkers related to inflammation and liver and kidney function statistically accounted for part of the observed associations, although these findings should not be interpreted as causal mediation.

AIP is a surrogate marker of IR and atherogenic dyslipidemia and has been associated with several cardiometabolic outcomes in the general population [48–52]. However, evidence among individuals with CKM syndrome remains limited. Previous studies in CKM populations have mainly focused on single disease endpoints, such as CVD or stroke [4–8, 11]. However, a few studies have examined whether AIP and its adiposity-related composite indices are associated with CMM. This distinction is important because CMDs tend to cluster and progress along shared metabolic and vascular pathways. Therefore, evaluating CMM rather than isolated CMDs may better capture the cumulative disease burden and clinical complexity of CKM syndrome. To our knowledge, only a limited number of studies conducted in the general population have evaluated the association between the AIP and CMM [19–22]. Our findings extend prior evidence by showing that AIP and its adiposity-related composite indices are positively associated with incident CMM among individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. The subgroup analyses showed that the associations were more pronounced in women and in participants aged < 60 years. This sex-specific pattern may be partly related to differences in insulin sensitivity, fat distribution, and hormonal status. Although women generally have more favorable insulin sensitivity and adiposity distribution than men, this advantage may diminish with worsening IR, hyperglycemia, or menopause [53]. Therefore, metabolic abnormalities reflected by elevated AIP-related indices may correspond to a relatively greater increase in cardiometabolic risk among women. The more pronounced associations in relatively younger participants may reflect earlier exposure to adverse metabolic profiles and a longer lifetime burden of vascular and cardiometabolic injury. These findings suggest that AIP-related indices may be useful for identifying high-risk subgroups among relatively younger individuals and women with CKM syndrome. We also observed significant interactions by baseline lipid-lowering medication use. The associations between AIP-related indices and incident CMM persisted regardless of lipid-lowering medication use, but were stronger among participants not using lipid-lowering medication. Since AIP is derived from triglycerides and HDL-C, lipid-lowering therapy may directly alter these lipid components and attenuate the observed association. In contrast, among participants not using lipid-lowering medication, elevated AIP-related indices may more directly reflect untreated atherogenic dyslipidemia and related metabolic disturbance. These findings indicate that lipid-lowering medication use should be considered when interpreting the risk information conveyed by AIP-related indices.

Our study also adds evidence regarding the incremental predictive value of composite AIP-related indices. A recent study suggested that combining the TyG index with adiposity measures improved the prediction of CMM among individuals with CKM syndrome [12]. However, whether a similar advantage exists for AIP-based composite indices has been unclear. Most previous studies have evaluated AIP alone [4, 6–8, 11], and only limited evidence has compared AIP with composite indices incorporating anthropometric measures [5]. In the present study, all eight AIP-related indices improved CMM risk prediction beyond conventional risk factors, supporting the value of integrating lipid-related and adiposity-related information in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. Among these indices, AIP-BRI showed the strongest predictive performance. This may be because AIP-BRI combines information on atherogenic dyslipidemia with body fat distribution. Similar to our findings, a prior cohort study reported that TyG-BRI had superior predictive performance for cardiovascular outcomes compared with other related composite indices [54]. Another cohort study observed that, among a range of TyG-related indices, TyG-BRI exhibited the best predictive performance for adverse cardiovascular events in rural Chinese residents [55]. The superior predictive performance of AIP-BRI may be attributable to the fact that it integrates information on both atherogenic dyslipidemia and body fat distribution. Compared with traditional anthropometric indices, BRI may better reflect central adiposity and visceral fat accumulation [56, 57], both of which are closely linked to IR and cardiometabolic risk. Overall, AIP-related indices, especially AIP-BRI, may hold promise as prognostic markers for risk stratification in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. However, the incremental prediction analyses should be interpreted cautiously. The conventional model was based on the fully adjusted Model 3 because there is no widely accepted conventional model specifically designed to predict incident CMM among individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. Although similar results were observed when SCORE2 and the AHA PREVENT equations were used as alternative conventional models, these tools were developed for CVD rather than CMM prediction. Therefore, these findings should be considered robustness analyses rather than validation against established CMM-specific prediction models.

Previous studies have primarily examined the associations between AIP-related indices and individual CMDs [4–6, 49, 58, 59]. The dynamic contributions of AIP-related indices to the development of CMM remain insufficiently understood, particularly in individuals with CKM syndrome. Our multi-state analyses provided further insight into the dynamic role of AIP-related indices during CMM progression. Specifically, AIP-related indices exhibited the strongest associations with the transition from baseline to T2DM. Similar findings have been reported in studies examining AIP-BRI or TyG-related indices in the progression of multimorbidity [22, 28, 60]. Contrary to some previous reports [4–6], we did not detect significant associations between AIP-related indices and the transition from baseline to stroke. This discrepancy with some previous studies may be partly due to differences in stroke ascertainment, as several earlier studies relied on self-reported stroke events [4–6], whereas our study primarily used clinically diagnosed outcomes. The heterogeneous pathophysiology of stroke may also contribute to the weaker associations observed in this transition. Previous studies have indicated that higher AIP-related indices are linked to increased risks of adverse cardiovascular outcomes in patients with pre-existing CMDs [59, 61, 62]. Supporting these findings, we observed that elevated levels of AIP-related indices were consistently associated with higher risks of transitions from single CMD to CMM, particularly for the transition from CHD to CMM. For example, a cohort study in 721 patients with premature coronary artery disease found that each SD increase in the AIP is associated with a higher risk of major adverse cardiovascular events [61]. CHD is often accompanied by insulin resistance, atherogenic dyslipidemia, chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and other cardiometabolic abnormalities [63]. Therefore, elevated AIP-related indices in this context may reflect more severe residual metabolic risk and a higher likelihood of progression to multimorbidity. These findings highlight the importance of considering CMM as a dynamic process and suggest that AIP-related indices may help inform stage-specific risk assessment among individuals with CKM syndrome.

In exploratory mediation analyses, biomarkers related to renal function, liver function, and systemic inflammation statistically accounted for part of the associations between AIP-related indices and incident CMM. Among these biomarkers, renal function-related markers, particularly urate, showed relatively large estimated proportions. This is consistent with previous evidence linking AIP with renal dysfunction [27]. Additionally, our previous work reported that urate accounted for the largest estimated proportion of the association between TyG-related indices and CMM risk among the biomarkers examined [28]. Liver function biomarkers, including ALT, GGT, ALP, and AST, also statistically accounted for part of the associations, in line with studies showing relationships between lipid-related indices, liver injury markers, and cardiometabolic outcomes [26, 64–66]. For example, a large cross-sectional study found that AIP is not only associated with ALT and AST levels but also predicts early liver damage [26]. Inflammatory markers, including neutrophils, CRP, and WBCs, also showed moderate indirect roles. Similar to our findings, a prospective study reported that inflammatory markers statistically accounted for part of the relationship between the AIP and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in individuals with CKM syndrome [67]. These findings are biologically plausible, as atherogenic dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, visceral adiposity, and chronic low-grade inflammation are closely interrelated and may jointly contribute to the development of CMDs [68–71]. In addition to the observed biomarker associations, AIP reflects lipid profile characteristics and has been associated with IR severity [72], which is itself associated with CMD risk [73]. Additionally, HDL-C, the key component of AIP, exhibits extensive beneficial biological activities, such as anti-atherosclerotic effects [74], while TG-rich remnant particles may contribute to atherosclerotic plaque formation and progression [2]. However, the biomarker findings should be interpreted cautiously. Because AIP-related indices and candidate biomarkers were measured concurrently at baseline, the mediation analyses cannot establish temporal sequence or causal pathways. These results should therefore be viewed as hypothesis-generating evidence suggesting that biomarkers associated with inflammation and liver and kidney function might partly characterize the adverse cardiometabolic profile associated with elevated AIP-related indices.

This study represented the first prospective investigation to evaluate the contributions of eight AIP-related indices to the onset and progression of CMM among over 340,000 individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. The study’s strengths include a prospective design, large cohort, extended follow-up (median follow-up: 15.9 years), careful confounder adjustment, and robust findings validated through multiple sensitivity and subgroup analyses. Moreover, comprehensive blood biomarker data allowed us to conduct exploratory analyses of biomarker associations with the observed relationship between AIP-related indices and CMM risk, thereby generating hypotheses for future longitudinal and mechanistic research. While the study offers several strengths, several limitations should be recognized. First, the UK Biobank has a relatively low participation rate of 5.5%, and participants are generally healthier, more socioeconomically advantaged, and more health conscious than the general population, which may have led to selection bias and may have influenced the absolute incidence of CMM observed in the present study. Nevertheless, previous evidence has suggested that risk factor-outcome associations in the UK Biobank are often broadly applicable to external populations, even when disease incidence estimates are not fully representative [75]. Second, the study population was predominantly White, accounting for 95.6% of participants, which may limit the broader generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups. External validation in more ethnically heterogeneous cohorts is warranted. Third, the observational design precludes firm causal inference. Although we adjusted for a wide range of sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical-related covariates, unmeasured or residual confounders cannot be fully excluded. Reverse causation is also possible, although the results remained stable after excluding CMM events occurring during the first 5 years of follow-up. Fourth, medication use was primarily adjusted at baseline in the main analyses, whereas treatment patterns may change substantially during follow up, especially after the occurrence of the first CMD. Although the sensitivity analysis using time-varying medication covariates supported the robustness of our findings, this analysis could not fully account for all treatment-related complexities, such as medication dose, duration, adherence, treatment intensification, treatment discontinuation, and indications for treatment initiation. Therefore, residual confounding related to dynamic treatment changes cannot be completely ruled out. Finally, although incident CMM was ascertained prospectively, the AIP-related indices and candidate biomarkers were measured concurrently at baseline. Consequently, the exploratory mediation analyses cannot determine whether changes in the biomarkers occurred after changes in the AIP-related indices, and the findings should not be interpreted as causal mediation, biological mechanisms, or actionable intervention targets. Future studies incorporating repeated biomarker measurements, formal temporal assessment, or experimental designs are warranted to validate our findings.

Conclusions

In this large prospective cohort of 346,868 participants with CKM syndrome stages 0–3, all eight AIP-related indices were positively associated with incident CMM and provided additional predictive value, indicating their potential utility in risk stratification. Among them, AIP-BRI demonstrated the strongest overall predictive performance. Baseline biomarkers reflecting inflammation and liver and kidney function statistically accounted for part of these associations. However, because the indices and candidate biomarkers were measured concurrently at baseline, these findings should be interpreted as correlational and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of biological mechanisms. Moreover, these indices play distinct associations across stages of CMM progression, which may inform future research on risk assessment and prevention across the CKM continuum.
