Age and trauma center-specific disparities in splenectomy rates for pediatric trauma patients
Authors: Caitlin J Cain-Trivette, Emily Manin, Anjile An, Yaffa M Vitberg, P Stephen Oh, Shaun Steigman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-operative management (NOM) of blunt splenic injury (BSI) is the target for pediatric trauma care. Yet, the rates of splenectomy have historically been higher at non-pediatric trauma centers (PTCs). We sought to determine if this difference improved contemporaneously.MethodsUsing the National Trauma Data Bank, 26 707 pediatric patients (age ≤18 years, 2013 to 2021) with BSI were identified and analyzed based on care at adult trauma centers (ATCs), dual trauma centers (DTCs), or pediatric trauma centers (PTCs) using descriptive statistics and logistic regressions. The primary outcome was splenectomy rate.ResultsOf the 26 707 pediatric patients with BSI, 5569 (20.9%) were treated at PTCs, 11 904 (44.6%) at ATCs, and 9234 (34.6%) at DTCs. In a multivariable logistic regression model, pediatric patients had significantly higher odds of splenectomy at ATCs (OR 4.89; 95% CI 3.82 to 6.45) and DTCs (OR 3.31; 95% CI 2.57 to 4.31) versus PTCs. In a subanalysis, older patients (15 years to 18 years) also had a significantly higher odds of splenectomy at ATCs (OR 7.27; 95% CI 4.58 to 12.1) and DTCs (OR 5.10; 95% CI 3.18 to 8.57) versus PTCs. Of patients aged 15 years to 18 years the majority were treated at ATCs (58.6%) or DTCs (32.1%) compared with PTCs (9.3%), whereas 32.6% of patients aged <15 years were treated at PTCs. These differences persisted across the study period.ConclusionPediatric patients with BSI continue to have higher splenectomy rates when they are treated at ATCs and DTCs compared with PTCs. To improve these rates, healthcare systems should expand access to PTCs and investigate causality to improve pediatric trauma care rendered at ATCs and DTCs.Level of evidenceLevel II.Keywords: abdominal injuries, splenectomy, pediatrics, spleen

Background

The spleen is the most commonly injured organ after blunt abdominal trauma, and in children older than 1 year of age, abdominal trauma is the leading cause of death.1 2 In 2000, the American Pediatric Surgical Association published guidelines for management of blunt splenic injury (BSI) in children, establishing the target of care as non-operative management (NOM).3 4 Splenic artery embolization (SAE), first developed in the 1970s, has also been increasingly used as an alternative to splenectomy in select patients.3 5

The advent of NOM for BSI in pediatrics originated in 1968 at the Hospital for Sick Children in Toronto.6 This approach, though initially criticized for its presumed higher mortality and transfusion rates, became more widely accepted as the risks of overwhelming post-splenectomy syndrome in children, as well as the morbidity and mortality of the operation itself, were recognized.5 7 8 This was further supported by pediatric surgeons’ observations that on laparotomy for children with BSI, transverse splenic fractures were largely hemostatic.9

In adults, NOM of BSI—first proposed in the 1980s—has been adopted more slowly due to the differential mechanisms and severity of injuries in adults.10 11 Surgeons were concerned that spontaneous hemostasis was less likely in adults than in children and that delayed rupture of the spleen or a post-traumatic cyst may ensue.12 These factors, taken together with the relatively lower risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy syndrome in adults, delayed adaptation of NOM for adult BSI. However, it is now the established standard of care for adult trauma.12 13

The guidelines established by the American Pediatric Surgical Association maximize patient safety and outcomes with the aim of splenic preservation.14 However, in practice, children treated at adult trauma centers (ATCs) rather than pediatric trauma centers (PTCs) have experienced a higher rate of splenectomy with a lower adherence to the established NOM criteria.115,19 Given the differences in management and outcomes based on historical and institutional standards, we examined modern trends in managing BSI at ATCs and dual trauma centers (DTCs) versus PTCs to see if the management gap has improved over time.

Methods

Data from the National Trauma Data Bank (NTDB) were extracted from 2013 to 2021 to identify 1 165 968 pediatric patients (age 18 years and younger). We excluded 1 136 670 pediatric patients who did not have an Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) spleen code, 2093 patients with a penetrating splenic injury, and 498 patients who had missing hospital designations. The final cohort included 26 707 patients with BSI. The AIS spleen codes used in this study were: 544210, 544212, 544214, 544220, 544222, 544224, 544226, 544228.20

For all patients included in the cohort, demographic and clinical data were gathered, including sex, age, race, ethnicity, hospital setting (pediatric, adult, community, non-teaching, or university), AIS injury grade for spleen, overall AIS score, admission heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and admission Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS). Overall AIS score refers to the combined severity of all injuries a patient suffers, where each individual injury is assigned a score by body region and the highest score across all body regions represents the overall AIS score for the patient (ranging from minor injury, moderate injury, serious injury, severe injury, critical, or maximum/virtually un-survivable). HR and SBP were classified as high or low, depending on age-adjusted norms (see online supplemental table 1). For each patient, data were collected on the management of BSI, including the need for transfusion, SAE, and splenectomy.

Hospital status was collected from the NTDB. PTCs were defined as any hospital with a standalone pediatric verification designated by the American College of Surgeons (ACS) or at the state level. ATCs were defined as any hospitals with a standalone adult verification designated by the ACS or at the state level. DTCs were any hospitals that carried either an ACS or state pediatric designation and either an ACS or state adult designation. Data were collected on university status versus non-teaching status versus community settings for all hospitals defined as PTCs, ATCs, or DTCs. Additionally, patient transfer data were obtained.

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study sample with respect to demographic and clinical factors of interest. Continuous variables are reported as mean (SD) and categorical variables are reported as n (%). Where appropriate, the χ2 test and ANOVA were used to examine the association between clinical characteristics and outcomes of interest and location of treatment. A combination of statistically significant variables identified by univariate analysis (p<0.10) and clinically significant variables was identified for the multivariable logistic regression models for receipt of splenectomy: location of treatment, age, sex, overall AIS score, admission HR, admission SBP, transfusion, and hospital mortality. We attempted to use both overall AIS score and AIS injury grade for spleen in the multivariable model; however, the variables were highly collinear, and we thus selected overall AIS score for our model as it represented a better marker of overall clinical severity. In our analysis, we defined isolated splenic injury as any patient having AIS 1–6 specifically for the spleen, whereas all other region-specific AIS scores (head/neck, chest, face, extremities, external, spine) were required to be 0/not assigned to a patient record. The models were used to evaluate the independent effect of demographic and clinical variables on splenectomy rates. 1220 patients (4%) were excluded from the multivariable analysis due to missing HR, SBP, or GCS. Of note, patients who died in the emergency department were excluded from the in-hospital death numbers. Adjusted ORs and 95% CIs were estimated from the multivariable models. All p values are two-sided with statistical significance evaluated at the 0.05 alpha level. All analyses were performed in R V.4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

This study was deemed exempt from institutional review board approval as it involved analysis of deidentified data from the NTDB.

Results

To evaluate the study cohort, the characteristics of pediatric patients with BSI were compared between those treated at PTCs, ATCs, and DTCs. Of the 26 707 patients who underwent treatment for BSI between 2013 and 2021, 5569 (21.0%) were treated at PTCs, 11 904 at ATCs (44.6%), and 9234 (34.6%) at DTCs. Demographic and clinical characteristics, including overall AIS severity, were similar between patients treated at PTCs and ATCs (table 1). However, the mean (SD) age of patients treated at PTCs (10.7 (4.3) years) was lower than that of patients treated at ATCs (14.5 (4.1) years) or DTCS (12.7 (4.6) years) (figure 1). Patients treated at all trauma center types had a similar distribution of AIS splenic injury grades as well as a similar overall AIS severity. The proportion of patients who were hemodynamically unstable by age group was also similar for patients treated at all trauma center types. Only a relatively small number of patients had isolated splenic injuries (4.2% at PTCs, 4.6% at ATCs, 3.6% at DTCs). Patients treated at PTCs and DTCs were significantly more likely to be treated at a University Center compared with ATCs (52% and 58%, respectively, vs 26%, p<0.001) (table 1).

In examining factors associated with splenectomy rates at different trauma center types, unadjusted splenectomy rates were noted to be consistently higher at ATCs and DTCs compared with PTCs over time (figure 2a). Potential confounders in clinical status were accounted for by performing a multivariable logistic regression analysis adjusting for hospital type, age, sex, overall AIS severity, admission HR, admission SBP, transfusion, in-hospital mortality, and admission GCS. Patients treated at ATCs and DTCs had a significantly higher odds of splenectomy compared with PTCs (ATCs: OR 4.89; 95% CI 3.82 to 6.35, DTCs: OR 3.31; 95% CI 2.57 to 4.31), and independent predictors of splenectomy included hypotension for age, tachycardia for age, male sex, and overall AIS severity (table 2).

To look at trends in care delivery over time, the proportion of BSI patients treated at PTCs, ATCs, and DTCs was examined from 2013 to 2021 (figure 2b). There were no major changes in the proportion of children treated at PTCs, ATCs, and DTCs from 2013 to 2021. The patients were broken up into two cohorts to see if older children (15 years to 18 years) were less likely to be seen at PTCs than younger children (<15 years). In 2013, only 8.4% of children aged 15 years 18 years were seen at PTCs versus 33.2% of children aged <15 years who were seen at PTCs. By 2021, 11.7% of children aged 15 years to 18 years were seen at PTCs compared with 35.9% of children aged <15 years who were seen at PTCs (figure 3a,c).

In evaluating transfer rates to PTCs, ATCs, and DTCs: 3376 (61%) of PTC patients were transfers, 3255 (27%) of ATC patients were transfers, and 4520 (49%) of DTC patients were transfers. Of the transfers to PTCs, 2578 (79%) were <15 years and only 798 (21%) were aged 15 years to 18 years. Of the transfers to DTCs, 2743 (61%) were <15 years and 1777 (39%) were aged 15 years to 18 years. Given the limitations of the NTDB, we do not know where these patients were transferred from.

Finally, we sought to determine if there were differences in outcomes for children aged <15 years and aged 15 years to 18 years at PTCs compared with ATCs and DTCs. A significant disparity persisted in unadjusted splenectomy rates for children aged 15 years to 18 years treated at ATCs and DTCs compared with PTCs where the rates of splenectomy were much lower over time (figure 3d). Although there was still a disparity in unadjusted splenectomy rates for children age <15 years between PTCs versus ATCs and DTCs, it was less pronounced than the older cohort (figure 3b). A multivariable logistic regression model was performed adjusting again for hospital type, age, sex, overall AIS severity, admission HR, admission SBP, transfusion, in-hospital mortality, and admission GCS in the two age groups. Compared with PTCs, patients aged <15 years were 3.59 times more likely to undergo splenectomy at an ATC (95% CI 2.58 to 5.04) and were 2.66 times more likely to undergo splenectomy at a DTC (95% CI 1.93 to 3.71). Children aged 15 years to 18 years were in turn 7.27 times more likely to undergo splenectomy at an ATC (95% CI 4.58 to 12.1) and 5.10 times more likely to undergo splenectomy at a DTC (95% CI 3.18 to 8.57) (table 3).

Rates of SAE were examined at all trauma center types. Across all age groups, ATCs and DTCs performed more SAEs compared with PTCs (1.6% ATCs, 1.4% DTCs vs 0.4% PTCs). This trend was similar for the older and younger age groups (table 3). Despite similar clinical presentations at ATCs, PTCs, and DTCs, in-hospital mortality was significantly higher for children treated at ATCs (2.9%) and DTCs (2.9%) versus PTCs (1.6%) (p<0.001) and this trend was similar for the older and younger cohorts. In ED, mortality was highest at ATCs (1.4%) compared with PTCs (0.5%) and DTCs (0.8%) (p<0.001) (table 3).

Discussion

This is the largest known study to date to look longitudinally during the course of 9 years (2013 to 2021) at rates of splenectomy for pediatric BSI at ATCs, DTCs, and PTCs. We hypothesized that ATCs and DTCs would increasingly adopt NOM for pediatric BSI with splenectomy rates that would approach that of PTCs. We also hypothesized that DTCs would have similar outcomes to PTCs as they carry an ACS or state level pediatric designation. However, we found that rates of unadjusted and adjusted pediatric splenectomy remained nearly twice as high at ATCs as well as DTCs during the study period, despite equivalent degrees of patient acuity between all centers. Surprisingly, this discrepancy in outcomes extended more so to older pediatric patients aged 15 years to 18 years, who had a bigger disparity in splenectomy outcomes compared with the younger cohort, and this did not change from 2013 to 2021.

The ACS Committee on Trauma does not stipulate a universal age cut-off to define pediatric trauma patients. However, for certain reporting and verification benchmarks—such as trauma center volume requirements—the ACS commonly uses patients younger than 15 years as an operational threshold for pediatric patients, and many trauma centers and trauma systems follow this convention when developing clinical protocols and data classifications.21 As a result, standalone ATCs are typically more likely to transfer trauma patients under 15 years of age to PTCs, whereas patients aged 15 years and older are more often treated at ATCs or dual trauma centers (DTCs).22 This study finds that patients with BSI who were 15 years to 18 years old had better outcomes when they were treated at PTCs versus ATCs or DTCs. A recent NTDB study of more than 50 000 pediatric trauma patients showed that younger children benefit most from care at PTCs, but adolescents also have some benefits from being treated at PTCs.23 24 This study augments the finding that adolescents with BSI would benefit from care at a PTC in terms of splenic salvage. Thus, pediatric patients with BSI who are aged 15 years to 18 years should not simply be considered adults and may truly benefit from being transferred to PTCs for treatment. Despite the use of age <15 years as a benchmark for certain ACS metrics, the age cutoffs for triage to ATCs, DTCs, and PTCs still vary across the nation, and even among trauma centers in the same city. To clarify the optimal age cutoffs, age-related outcomes need to be further explored, which may have major implications for patient triage, and ultimately patient outcomes such as splenectomy.

We also saw that most patient transfers were children <15 years who went to PTCs. Given the limitations of the NTDB, we do not know what type of hospital these patients were transferred from. It would be interesting to know how many children were transferred from ATCs compared with other community centers without a trauma designation. Since older patients aged 15 years to 18 years did not make up the majority of transfers to PTCs or to DTCs, we can theorize that adult designated centers may have been more comfortable treating these patients in-house. Nonetheless, the patient cohort aged 15 years to 18 years was identified as the most vulnerable by our study, as despite presenting with a similar degree of clinical acuity at all sites, they were more likely to undergo splenectomy in an adjusted analysis at ATCs and DTCs compared with PTCs. Although the rates of SAE at ATCs and DTCs were significantly higher than at PTCs, this still did not translate to lower splenectomy rates at these centers. It is possible there is a greater availability of interventional radiology at ATCs and DTCs leading to increased utilization of SAE at these centers.

A limitation to note in our multivariable analysis is the missingness of 4% of the data. We thought that it was important to have an adjusted OR of splenectomy based off of clinical factors which may directly impact splenectomy and did not want to include patients with missing vital signs, GCS (which was used as a surrogate for traumatic brain injury), or missing AIS overall severity. A further limitation to our analysis is that it is difficult to know if a child seen at a DTC was seen by a pediatric-specific or adult-specific team, as the availability of a pediatric trauma surgeon may impact a patient’s care.

Our findings agree in part with a similar but smaller study of 8300 pediatric patients with BSI from the Kids’ Inpatient Database.19 This study reported that the rates of high-grade BSI treated, as well as the number of splenectomies performed, at PTCs increased between 2012 and 2019. However, this study finds that a stable proportion of splenectomies were performed over time, with a higher proportion performed at ATCs and DTCs versus PTCs. This Kids’ Inpatient Database study also did not capture emergency department or in-hospital mortality rates like this study. We found that the latter was significantly higher for patients treated at ATCs and DTCs versus PTCs.

To further solidify the benefits of PTCs for pediatric trauma patients, a 2023 systematic review of 56 studies with 286 051 participants showed that PTCs were associated with a 41% lower risk of mortality, 50% lower risk of CT use, and 64% lower risk of operative management for blunt solid organ injury.20 Although this study did not stratify by older and younger children, our study shows a clear advantage with improved outcomes by treating older pediatric patients with BSI at PTCs.

Pediatric patients face challenges in accessing specialized trauma centers. According to the US Government Accountability Office, between 2011 and 2015, only 57% of the 74.7 million children in the USA lived within 30 miles of a PTC that can treat all injuries, regardless of their severity. Additionally, patient access to these PTCs was distributed inequitably by race, ethnicity, and geography.25 Although the USA has 157 PTCs, only about one-half to three-quarters of the pediatric population had timely access to level I to III PTCs by ground (55%) and air (74%) in 2020.25 Nationwide efforts to improve access to PTCs for pediatric patients with BSI could improve outcomes for this population.

The disparity in outcomes between ATCs and DTCs versus PTCs for pediatric BSI is likely multifactorial and may be due to hesitation to accept NOM as safe for pediatric patients at ATCs, less experience with and relatively low volumes of pediatric traumas compared with adult traumas at ATCs, limited access to pediatric-specific equipment or resources (eg, pediatric interventional radiology), and lack of up-to-date knowledge about the management of BSI in pediatric patients, or other unidentified reasons.26,29 In future work, it will be crucial to decipher what factors contribute to the disparate outcomes in pediatric patients with BSI who are treated at ATCs and DTCs versus PTCs so that they may be remedied.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate a clear current advantage for pediatric patients with BSI to receive care at PTCs. We acknowledge that as a retrospective cohort study using an administrative database, the granularity of our data are limited. Also, given that the NTDB has a predisposition for larger tertiary care centers, the data may be biased.30 However, our study is the largest to date on this specific topic, and the longitudinal analysis shows that there has been little improvement in splenectomy rates at DTCs and ATCs, particularly for patients aged 15 years to 18 years. Our findings support that to improve splenectomy outcomes for pediatric patients with BSI, healthcare systems need to expand access to PTCs and to improve trauma care rendered at ATCs and DTCs across the USA.
