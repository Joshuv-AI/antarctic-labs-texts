Focal Aneurysm Wall Enhancement in Vessel Wall Imaging as a Surrogate Marker for Predicting Aneurysm Instability
Authors: Yukishige Hashimoto, Toshinori Matsushige, Reo Kawano, Takeshi Hara, Shohei Kobayashi, Mayumi Kaneko, Chiaki Ono, Nobutaka Horie
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe establishment of a risk stratification method for unruptured intracranial aneurysms (UIAs) remains an interdisciplinary challenge. The present study attempted to identify unstable UIAs using magnetic resonance vessel wall imaging in prospective data sets. Hemodynamic parameters in unstable UIAs were also examined to clarify the mechanisms by which segmented aneurysm wall enhancement (AWE) affects longitudinal morphological changes.MethodsPatients with UIAs who underwent contrast‐enhanced vessel wall imaging between 2017 and 2022 and were followed up for more than 6 months were included. Two readers independently rated AWE patterns (no, focal, and circumferential AWE) on vessel wall imaging and morphological changes on time‐of‐flight magnetic resonance angiography. Computational fluid dynamics studies were performed on unstable UIAs to evaluate the hemodynamic features of evolved or ruptured points in aneurysm walls.ResultsAneurysm growth was observed in 13 of 114 UIAs in the present study during a median follow‐up of 34 months. Of the 13 growing UIAs, their AWE patterns were as follows: no AWE in 6 and focal AWE (FAWE) in 7 UIAs. Univariable Cox regression analysis showed that aneurysm size and FAWE were associated with aneurysm growth. On multivariable Cox regression analysis, FAWE (hazard ratio, 4.59 [95% CI, 1.29–16.3]; P=0.019) was independently associated with aneurysm growth. Kaplan–Meier curve revealed significant differences between AWE patterns and UIA growth (P<0.001). Aneurysms ruptured in the 4 UIAs with FAWE during the follow‐up and all rupture points corresponded to nonenhanced lesions in aneurysm walls. Nonenhanced areas had higher wall shear stress than enhanced areas (1.59±1.02 versus 0.53±0.32; P=0.022).ConclusionFAWE may be associated with aneurysm growth and rupture during follow‐up. A comprehensive analysis of nonenhanced areas of UIAs with FAWE using vessel wall imaging and computational fluid dynamics provides insights into the mechanisms underlying aneurysm instability.Keywords: aneurysm wall enhancement, segmentation, vessel wall imaging

The segmentation of aneurysm wall enhancement may increase the diagnostic accuracy of predicting unstable unruptured intracranial aneurysms.

Unruptured intracranial aneurysms (UIAs) are present in approximately 3% of the population. 1 Although the risk of rupture is generally low in UIAs, aneurysm rupture is associated with high fatality rates, and, thus, it is important to predict the risk of rupture of UIAs. 2 — 4 Several patient‐based factors and imaging features, including aneurysm size, location, and morphological modifications during follow‐up, have been identified as risk factors for aneurysm rupture. 2 , 3 , 5 , 6 , 7 Nevertheless, rupture risk stratification remains insufficient; therefore, additional surrogate markers for unstable UIAs are needed to maximize the appropriate selection of patients for preventive interventions.

The potential of magnetic resonance (MR) vessel wall imaging (VWI) as an imaging marker to predict aneurysm instability has been examined in the past decade. 8 — 18 The lack of aneurysm wall enhancement (AWE) in UIAs indicates aneurysm stability for a short term of approximately 2 years, 19 whereas the clinical significance of AWE in UIAs is debatable. 8 — 11 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 18 , 20 Recent studies have shown that the segmentation of AWE may be used to upgrade the accuracy of detecting unstable aneurysms. 9 , 11 , 21 Among the 3 segmented AWE patterns (no AWE [NAWE], focal AWE [FAWE], and circumferential AWE [CAWE]), UIAs with FAWE have been associated with risk for aneurysm growth. 11

In the present study, we investigated the potential of the segmentation of AWE on VWI to predict unstable UIAs in a cohort of prospective patients. We also assessed hemodynamic parameters in growing UIAs to clarify the mechanisms by which segmented AWE affects longitudinal morphological changes.

Methods

The present results are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Patients

The present study was approved by the local institutional review board. The board waived the need for patient consent. The local prospectively maintained VWI database of UIAs between April 2017 and April 2022 was retrospectively investigated to identify patients with asymptomatic UIAs for whom conservative management was selected in consultations with patients. We decided whether an aneurysm needed to be treated based on UCAS Japan (Unruptured Cerebral Aneurysm Study) data for estimating the annual rate of rupture, 3 the assumed risk of complications from preventive endovascular or neurosurgical aneurysm treatment, life expectancy, and patient anxiety. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who underwent contrast‐enhanced VWI at baseline only, and (2) a follow‐up period of more than 6 months after VWI. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) fusiform or dissecting aneurysms, and (2) a maximal diameter <2 mm due to image resolution constrictions.

Imaging Protocol

All patients were scanned using a 1.5T MR scanner (Signa Explorer, GE Healthcare Ltd.) equipped with an 8‐channel head coil. The protocol included 3‐dimensional time‐of‐flight MR angiography (MRA) for the localization of subsequent scans and T1‐weighted 3‐dimensional black‐blood fast spin echo with high‐resolution MR imaging before and after the administration of Gd‐BT‐DO3A (Gadovist, Bayer Schering Pharma) at 0.1 mmol/kg. The imaging parameters for CUBE T1 were as follows: TR/TE=550/10 ms, field of view=240×240 mm2, acquired matrix=256×256, and slice thickness=1.0 mm. The total scanning time was 3 minutes and 39 seconds per sequence. Details of the scanning parameters for the imaging protocol are listed in Table S1.

Imaging Analysis

The standard follow‐up imaging protocol at our center includes an MRA evaluation at every 6 or 12 months. Two readers (T.H. and S.K., neurosurgeons with more than 5 years of experience in vascular neuroimaging, respectively) reviewed multiplanar reformatted 3‐dimensional time‐of‐flight‐MRA to evaluate aneurysm growth defined as an enlargement >1 mm in any direction or an obvious morphological change. The patterns of morphological changes were classified as follows: whole sac expansion (defined as the enlargement of the whole aneurysm sac) and bleb formation. Aneurysm growth was evaluated on the basis of the first and last available imaging. Two different readers (T.M. and Y.H., neurosurgeons with more 5 years of experience in VWI, respectively) independently compared precontrast and postcontrast images of each aneurysm to categorize AWE patterns into the following 3 groups: (1) NAWE (no enhancement of the aneurysm wall); (2) FAWE (partial AWE); and (3) CAWE (global AWE), as previously described. 11 , 22 Discordances were resolved by a third reader (25 years of experience in neurovascular imaging).

Computational Fluid Dynamics Study

Computational fluid dynamics (CFDs) was performed using a commercial CFD package (Hemoscope v1.4, EBM Corp.). Blood was assumed to be an incompressible Newtonian fluid with a density of ρ=1050 kg/m3 and viscosity of μ=0.004 Pa s. Vascular geometries were reconstructed from computed tomographic angiography or digital subtracted angiography data sets based on baseline and mainly filled with unstructured hexahedral cells. The mesh size was 0.25 mm in far‐wall regions. The near‐wall mesh consisted of 3 layers, with a width of 0.125 mm and a height of 0.05 mm at the mesh nearest the wall. Unsteady pulsatile simulations were performed as the time step was adjusted to maintain the Courant number at <1. Regarding boundary conditions, the time‐averaged flow rate Q was initially estimated using the formula Q=(τπ⁄32μ) D, where τ is the magnitude of wall shear stress (WSS) (τ=1.5 Pa) and D is the diameter of the inlet artery. The distribution of the flow rate at vascular bifurcations was set to be a ratio of the cubic diameters of the branches. A physiological pulsatile waveform was imposed on the steady flow rate. We investigated WSS because it is the most frequently reported hemodynamic characteristic in the literature. 5 , 22

Statistical Analysis

Interrater agreements for AWE patterns and aneurysm growth were assessed by calculating the κ coefficient and Gwet AC1. 23 In the present study, AWE patterns were classified into focal and FAWE (−) because FAWE correlated with aneurysm growth according to our retrospective study. We performed univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses, including putative risk factors for aneurysm growth (ie, age, sex, hypertension, smoking, aneurysm size at diagnosis, aneurysm location, and AWE patterns) and calculated hazard ratios with corresponding 95% CIs and P values. The variable selection method by the adaptive least absolute shrinkage and selection operator was used for multivariable Cox regression model. 24 In adaptive least absolute shrinkage and selection operator selection, weights were applied to each of the parameters in forming the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator constraint to enjoy the oracle properties. The weights were obtained through ridge regression, and 10‐fold cross‐validation was employed to select the minimal penalty term (λ). FAWE (−) was then classified into NAWE and CAWE. UIA growth on MRA in the follow‐up was analyzed using a Kaplan–Meier method and compared according to AWE patterns using a log‐rank test. Differences in patient and aneurysm characteristics between stable and growing UIAs were evaluated in each AWE pattern. Fisher exact test for categorical variables and Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables were conducted to assess the significance of intergroup differences. Receiver operating characteristics curves were plotted for independent factors to differentiate evolution from stable aneurysms, and areas under the curves (AUCs) were calculated. Statistical analyses were performed with JMP statistical package (JMP 16, SAS Institute, Inc.). All P values were based on 2‐tailed statistical tests, with significance set at P<0.05.

Results

The study population (Figure 1) included 89 patients (mean age, 72 years [range, 35–87 years]) with 114 UIAs (mean size, 4.7 mm [range, 2.0–15]). The median follow‐up was 34 months (interquartile range, 15–43 months).

Flow chart of patients in the present study.

Interrater agreement for evaluating AWE patterns (κ=0.80 [95% CI, 0.68–0.92] and Gwet AC1=0.85 [95% CI, 0.75–0.94]) and aneurysm growth (κ=0.86 [95% CI, 0.70–1.0] and Gwet AC1=0.97 [95% CI, 0.93–1.0]) was excellent.

AWE was detected in 38 of 114 UIAs (FAWE in 23 and CAWE in 15). Among the 3 AWE patterns, aneurysm size in diameter was the largest in UIAs with CAWE (P<0.001). Thirteen of 114 UIAs (mean size, 7.3 mm [range, 3.3–15 mm]) exhibited aneurysm growth during the follow‐up in the present study (11%; annual growth rate, 3.9%). Of the 13 growing UIAs, their AWE patterns were as follows: NAWE in 6 UIAs and FAWE in 7 UIAs. The median interval between VWI and aneurysm growth was 21 months (interquartile range, 12–44 months). Significant differences between the 2 AWE groups were observed in the rate of bleb formation and the interval between contrast‐enhanced VWI and aneurysm growth (86% versus 17% [P= 0.029], 16±9.2 versus 38±17 months [P=0.018], respectively) (Table 3). Aneurysm growth was not detected in UIAs with CAWE during the follow‐up (median, 14 months [interquartile range, 12—19 months]).

In the present study, 4 of 13 UIAs ruptured during the follow‐up period (3.5%; annual rupture rate, 1.2%). Discussion

In our prospective cohort of 114 UIAs, aneurysm growth was observed in 13 UIAs (11%; annual growth rate, 3.9%), and 4 ruptured during the follow‐up period (3.5%; annual rupture rate, 1.2%). Although this single‐center VWI study included a small number of UIAs, annual growth and rupture rates were similar to those observed in larger cohorts. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 The median follow‐up period was longer in the present study (34 months) than in 2 prospective VWI studies (24 and 27 months) that investigated the relationships between aneurysm instability and the presence of AWE. 10 , 18 The rate of AWE was 32% in the present study, which was consistent with previously reported values. 10 , 25 Prospective data sets in the present study suggest that FAWE might be associated with aneurysm growth and rupture. Especially, aneurysm rupture was observed in 4 UIAs with FAWE, and all 4 rupture points corresponded to nonenhanced lesions with high WSS in the aneurysm wall.

VWI studies previously reported the potential to detect unstable aneurysms. 8 , 9 , 11 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 20 However, the latest meta‐analysis rejected the clinical utility of AWE in UIAs due to the low positive predictive value of predicting aneurysm growth during the follow‐up. 19 The prediction of aneurysm instability based on the segmentation of AWE has recently been attracting increasing attention. 9 , 11 , 21 UIAs with AWE have been classified into 2 segmented AWE types (FAWE and CAWE) and the AWE pattern associated with aneurysm growth during the follow‐up currently remains unclear. 8 , 9 , 15 , 20 We speculate that FAWE may be associated with aneurysm instability because a heterogenous aneurysm wall played a key role in unstable aneurysms from the perspective of morphological, histopathological, and hemodynamic characteristics. 6 , 16 , 17 , 22 We presumed the potential of FAWE as a novel surrogate marker for predicting unstable aneurysms for the following reasons. The multivariable Cox analysis showed that FAWE remained a significant risk factor for aneurysm growth (Table 2). Furthermore, among 13 growing UIAs, FAWE presented in a shorter period between VWI and aneurysm growth than NAWE (Table 3). Moreover, UIAs with FAWE correlated with bleb formation, which is a strong imaging marker for impending rupture. 26 , 27 , 28 In addition, although the number of subsequent ruptured aneurysms was small, all 4 ruptures were observed in UIAs with FAWE only. Among prospective VWI studies, only one showed a representative image of UIA with FAWE leading to subsequent rupture. 10 , 18

In the present study, we also examined the subgroup showing other wall enhancement patterns, such as CAWE or NAWE. Seventy of 76 UIAs (92%) without AWE showed aneurysm stability, which is consistent with the findings of a previous meta‐analysis. 19 Cross‐sectional studies demonstrated that CAWE was associated with a high PHASES score, 13 which mainly depended on the maximum diameter of an aneurysm. 10 , 13 The present study also showed that maximum diameter in UIAs with CAWE was the largest among the 3 AWE patterns (Table S2). On the other hand, recent histopathological studies showed that homogenous AWE was associated with larger aneurysms, but was more stable than heterogenous AWE. 16 , 17 Difficulties are associated with concluding whether CAWE contributes to aneurysm instability due to the correlation with a high PHASES score. Further longitudinal follow‐ups are warranted to clarify whether CAWE is associated with aneurysm stability because the follow‐up period of the subgroup in the present study was short (median, 14 months [interquartile range, 12—19 months]).

The majority of prospective studies performed follow‐up MRA every year to evaluate aneurysm growth; however, the annual rate of aneurysm growth was very low. 1 , 2 A previous VWI study conducted contrast‐enhanced VWI every year and showed that UIAs with increased AWE showed subsequent aneurysm growth with a high positive predictive value (100%). 10 However, there is currently no recommended interval for follow‐up VWI to screen for unstable UIAs. The present results may imply an optimal interval for follow‐up MR imaging for each AWE pattern. The Kaplan–Meier curve in the present study revealed that UIAs with FAWE showed aneurysm growth at a specific time during the follow‐up (Figure 2). Therefore, annual follow‐up imaging examinations for these UIAs may be needed to screen for aneurysm growth. On the other hand, the interval to demonstrate aneurysm growth in UIAs without AWE was long. A large prospective cohort study is warranted in the future to establish follow‐up management based on segmented AWE for the efficient detection of aneurysm growth.

Risk stratification for conventional aneurysm rupture has been based on aneurysm sizes. 2 — 5 Investigators of the ISUIA (International Study of Unruptured Intracranial Aneurysms) reported a threshold of 7 mm as a cutoff value above which surgical interventions may be indicated due to a high rupture risk. 4 In addition, the SUAVe (Small Unruptured Aneurysm Verification) study identified a diameter of >4 mm as a significant risk factor for aneurysm growth in small UIAs. 7 Among UIAs without AWE or FAWE, aneurysm sizes were larger in growing UIAs than in stable UIAs, respectively (Tables 4 and 5). The cutoff values for aneurysm sizes to differentiate between evolving and stable aneurysms in NAWE and FAWE were 4.2 mm (AUC, 0.78 [95% CI, 0.61–0.94]; sensitivity: 0.83, specificity: 0.72) and 7.6 mm (AUC, 0.87 [95% CI, 0.68–1.00]; sensitivity: 0.86, specificity: 0.94), respectively. Although the cutoff value for aneurysm sizes itself cannot be generalized due to the small number of recruited UIAs, cutoff values in the present study were equivalent to those from previous studies. 4 , 7 These cutoff values in each segmented AWE imply that histopathological and structural wall changes trigger the switch from stable to unstable UIAs (Figure 4).

Proposed classification of aneurysm growth scenarios based on aneurysm wall enhancement (AWE) patterns and aneurysm sizes in unruptured intracranial aneurysms (UIAs). When the size of an aneurysm is <4 mm in diameter, most UIAs have no AWE (NAWE) and aneurysm stability. Aneurysm growth patterns in UIAs of >4 mm in diameter are classified into the following 2 types: homogenous remodeling or focal (FAWE). When UIAs with FAWE are >7 mm in diameter, they are likely to show heterogenous remodeling, resulting in a high risk of aneurysm rupture. CAWE indicates circumferential aneurysm wall enhancement.

To date, AWE has been more frequently detected in growing or ruptured intracranial aneurysms. 8 , 9 , 14 However, the part of the aneurysm wall that will evolve and rupture in UIAs with AWE remains unclear. Although previous studies showed that the enhanced lesions corresponded to thickening aneurysm walls with atherosclerosis, 12 , 17 aneurysm growth and rupture has been considered to occur at the thinning wall with degeneration. 1 In the present study, adjacent nonenhanced lesions were enlarged in all 7 growing UIAs with FAWE (Figure 3). In addition, all 4 rupture points corresponded to adjacent nonenhanced lesions presenting with bleb formation. Two representative figures were similar to the present results. 8 , 18 On the other hand, 5 of 6 growing UIAs without AWE showed whole sac expansion, which implies a homogenous remodeling process maintaining the structure of the aneurysm wall. 14 , 16 Based on these findings and the present results, adjacent nonenhanced lesions (oftentimes aneurysm tip) in UIAs with FAWE may have the potential to change into evolved or ruptured points. Although cross‐sectional studies using a CFD analysis demonstrated that high and low WSS may drive intracranial aneurysm growth and rupture, 5 , 22 , 27 a recent prospective study showed that aneurysm growth was associated with complex intrasaccular flow patterns that induce a heterogenous WSS distribution with areas of concentrated high WSS and large areas of low WSS. 27 , 28 In all 4 ruptured aneurysms in the present study, adjacent nonenhanced lesions with high WSS became enlarged and ruptured, while enhanced lesions with low WSS had no morphological changes. A multimodal evaluation of aneurysm walls using CFD and VWI will reveal the exact mechanisms underlying aneurysm growth and predict subsequent aneurysm rupture points.

All patients underwent scanning using a 1.5T MR scanner in the present study. A previous study recommended a field strength of 3T or higher due to high spatial resolution and the signal‐to‐noise ratio. 29 However, histopathological studies confirmed that AWE lesions corresponded to atherosclerotic wall thickening with inflammation, 12 , 16 , 17 which may be visualized even by conventional 1.5T MR‐VWI. The rate of AWE in the present study was 33%, which was equivalent to previous VWI studies. 10 , 25 The present results suggest that nonenhanced lesions in UIAs with FAWE included the pivotal mechanisms of aneurysm growth and rupture. We previously reported that aneurysm wall thickness was <200 μm in diameter in UIAs without AWE, 16 , 17 which was invisible even with the highest resolution (400 μm isotropic) in 7T ultra–high‐resolution black‐blood MR imaging. 9 , 30 Therefore, we recommend a comprehensive evaluation of nonenhanced areas in UIAs with FAWE using other modalities rather than increasing the field strength of VWI.

Limitations

The present study has some limitations. This single‐center cohort study included a bias for data collection because 51% of patients with UIAs who underwent VWI preferred surgical or endovascular treatment. Prospective observational studies on UIAs have to consider ethical issues regarding the prevention of aneurysm rupture. The rationale behind not treating growing aneurysms was as follows: old age, activities of daily living, and patient wishes. Moreover, the small number of cases and the broad CIs regarding aneurysm instability limits the generalizability and robustness of the findings obtained from this study. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether AWE patterns were associated with aneurysm growth in small aneurysms because the mean aneurysm size in growing UIAs was 7.3 mm. Even small UIAs may develop rapidly and subsequently rupture. 1 , 7 It was not possible to predict this minority using VWI findings. Another limitation is that we were unable to estimate when FAWE at diagnosis was originally acquired. Nevertheless, VWI that focuses on the segmentation of AWE represents a gamechanger in the risk stratification of UIAs.

Conclusions

The segmentation of AWE may increase the diagnostic accuracy of predicting unstable UIAs. FAWE may be associated with aneurysm growth and rupture. Rupture points corresponded to nonenhanced lesions with high WSS. A comprehensive analysis of nonenhanced areas of UIAs with FAWE using VWI and CFD will increase our understanding of the mechanisms underlying aneurysm growth and rupture.
