Redefining Obesity and Risk: Extending the 2024 European Association for the Study of Obesity Framework with Morbidity Stratification in the UK Biobank
Authors: Mingliang Tan, Xianming Tang, Wenbin Nan, Zhenyu Peng, Zhenhua Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe 2024 European Association for the Study of Obesity (EASO) framework redefines obesity by incorporating central adiposity and morbidity. However, the phenotypic characterization of risk heterogeneity across the full body mass index (BMI) spectrum under this framework – and its prognostic implications for mortality and cardiovascular outcomes – remain to be elucidated. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the ability of this new framework to improve phenotypic stratification and describe risk heterogeneity across BMI categories.MethodsIn 451,615 UK Biobank participants (median follow-up, 15.1 years), obesity was defined using (1) the EASO framework (which reclassifies individuals with BMI 25 to <30 kg/m2 into “EASO overweight” [WHtR <0.5 and/or absence of morbidity] and “EASO new obesity” [WHtR ≥0.5 and the presence of at least one morbidity]), and (2) an extended EASO framework incorporating morbidity stratification across all BMI categories. Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) for all-cause mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).ResultsApplying the EASO framework reclassified 15.62% of individuals previously categorized as having overweight into the category of people with obesity. Consequently, the prevalence of obesity increased from 24.50% to 40.12%. Two distinct risk patterns emerged: all-cause mortality showed a V-shaped association across adiposity categories, whereas MACE risk increased linearly with higher adiposity. Compared with normal weight, EASO overweight had the lowest mortality (HR 0.82 [95% CI: 0.80–0.84]), while EASO new obesity showed a comparable risk (0.96 [0.94–0.99]). The highest mortality risk was observed in BMI obesity (1.05 [1.02–1.08]). For MACE, EASO overweight had no excess risk (1.04 [0.99–1.09]), whereas both EASO new obesity and BMI obesity faced the highest and most substantial risks, with HRs of 1.35 (1.29–1.42) and 1.38 (1.32–1.45), respectively. When stratified by morbidity, the V-shaped mortality association was preserved and MACE risk remained linear. Individuals with morbidity consistently exhibited higher risks across all adiposity categories, indicating a vertical shift in risk rather than a change in association shape.ConclusionThe EASO framework substantially increases obesity prevalence and better identifies individuals with adverse metabolic and cardiovascular profiles. Morbidity does not alter the overall pattern of mortality and cardiovascular risk across adiposity levels but consistently amplifies absolute risk, supporting a morbidity-integrated approach to obesity phenotyping and risk assessment.

Background

Obesity has become a major public health concern and is increasingly recognized as a complex, multifactorial chronic disease with heterogeneous causes and clinical consequences [1, 2]. However, how to define obesity remains controversial. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends using body mass index (BMI) to define obesity [3], whereas the National Institutes of Health (NIH) advocates incorporating waist circumference (WC) assessment when evaluating individuals considered overweight or living with obesity based on BMI alone [4]. In 2024, the European Association for the Study of Obesity (EASO) proposed a novel framework for the diagnosis and management of obesity that integrates both anthropometric and clinical components [5]. By integrating BMI with waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) and the presence of obesity-related morbidity, this framework provides a nuanced stratification for individuals with a BMI of 25 to <30 kg/m2. Specifically, it reclassifies these individuals into two distinct categories: “EASO overweight” (characterized by a WHtR <0.5 and/or the absence of morbidity) and “EASO new obesity” (defined by a WHtR ≥0.5 and the presence of at least one obesity-related morbidity). This framework defines obesity not merely by excess body weight but by increased adiposity accompanied by adverse health effects including medical, functional, or psychological morbidity [5]. By incorporating measures of central adiposity and obesity-related complications, the EASO framework reclassifies a considerable proportion of individuals who were previously categorized as overweight under the conventional BMI-based system. This reclassification has potential implications for clinical management, public health policy, and early intervention strategies as individuals newly identified as having EASO obesity may warrant earlier lifestyle or pharmacologic interventions.

A recent US-based analysis using NHANES data applied the EASO framework and found that individuals classified as EASO new obesity had a higher all-cause mortality risk than those categorized as EASO overweight [6]. This study provided important early evidence supporting the clinical relevance of the EASO classification. While mortality risk increased from EASO overweight to EASO new obesity, the latter exhibited a risk level similar to normal weight and notably lower than BMI obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2). Importantly, morbidity status appeared to play a key role in determining risk within both EASO overweight and EASO new obesity categories, rather than BMI per se. Therefore, the framework primarily distinguishes individuals within the overweight category, leaving potential heterogeneity among people with normal weight or obesity – particularly with respect to morbidity status – largely unexplored. Moreover, the analysis was limited to all-cause mortality, and the relationship between EASO-defined obesity and cardiovascular outcomes, one of the most important obesity-related complications, has yet to be investigated [1, 7].

To address these gaps, we used data from the UK Biobank, a large, prospective population-based cohort, to apply the EASO obesity framework as a phenotypic classification system and examine its associations with all-cause and cardiovascular outcomes across the full BMI spectrum. Specifically, we aimed to (1) assess the impact of the EASO definition on obesity prevalence in a large-scale population and (2) investigate its associations with all-cause and cardiovascular outcomes, and to assess how morbidity (medical, functional, or psychological morbidity) may modify these associations across the full BMI spectrum.

Methods

The UK Biobank is a large, prospective population-based cohort comprising over 500,000 adults aged 40–69 years recruited across the UK between 2006 and 2010. Baseline assessments included standardized physical measurements, biospecimen collection, and detailed questionnaires capturing demographics, lifestyle, and medical history. Data were anonymized to preserve confidentiality, and participants did not influence study design, data collection, or analysis. Full study protocols and data dictionaries are available at https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/.

Obesity Definitions

For this study, three complementary approaches were applied to characterize adiposity phenotypes: (1) BMI alone, (2) the 2024 EASO framework, and (3) an EASO-based classification further stratified by morbidity status. Participants with missing BMI or WHtR data, those with BMI <18.5 kg/m2, or those with a history of cancer were excluded. Using BMI alone, participants were categorized as normal weight (BMI 18.5 to <25 kg/m2), overweight (BMI 25 to <30 kg/m2), or obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2). According to the EASO framework, participants were categorized as having normal weight if their BMI was 18.5 to <25 kg/m2; as having EASO overweight if their BMI was 25 to <30 kg/m2 and either their WHtR was <0.5 and/or they had no medical, functional, or psychological morbidity; and as having EASO new obesity if their BMI was 25 to <30 kg/m2 with WHtR ≥0.5 and at least one morbidity, or as BMI obesity if their BMI was ≥30 kg/m2. For this classification, normal weight served as the reference group. For the EASO-based morbidity-stratified classification, both normal weight and BMI obesity were further divided according to morbidity status, resulting in six mutually exclusive groups: normal weight without morbidity (reference), normal weight with morbidity, EASO overweight, EASO new obesity, BMI obesity without morbidity, and BMI obesity with morbidity.

Study Outcomes and Morbidity Definitions

The study outcomes included all-cause mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), defined as a composite of acute myocardial infarction, cardiac death, or stroke occurring after baseline assessment. Outcome events were ascertained using the UK Biobank “First occurrences” data fields (Field IDs), with event dates applied to distinguish incident events during follow-up from pre-existing conditions recorded prior to enrollment. Participants were followed from baseline until the earliest occurrence of any outcome or until September 30, 2024, for those without events. For the analysis of MACE, participants who died without experiencing a MACE were censored at their date of death.

Morbidities used for stratification were limited to conditions documented before study enrollment and included hypertension, cardiovascular disease (CVD: angina, myocardial infarction, or stroke), diabetes, arthritis, chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and depression. The morbidities used for stratification included both cardiometabolic and non-cardiometabolic conditions (specifically hypertension, CVD, diabetes, arthritis, CKD, COPD, and depression) to align with the EASO framework’s holistic inclusion of medical, functional, and psychological complications. CKD was defined at baseline as an estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, calculated using the Cockcroft-Gault equation. This approach was chosen to ensure consistency with baseline clinical assessments in the UK Biobank and to account for weight-adjusted renal clearance, which is particularly relevant in the context of obesity-related research. Arthritis was identified using UK Biobank Field ID 120000. All other morbidities were ascertained via the UK Biobank “First Occurrences” fields (Category 1712). These fields identify the earliest diagnosis date by integrating data from four primary sources: hospital inpatient records (ICD-9/10 codes), primary care records (Read v2/v3 codes), death registry data, and self-reported conditions previously diagnosed by a doctor and verified by a nurse during the assessment interview. For all categories, only diagnoses with an occurrence date prior to the baseline assessment were included to ensure they represented prevalent conditions at enrollment. Detailed mappings of study outcomes and morbidity conditions to their respective Field IDs, along with coding and derivation rules, are provided in online supplementary Table 1.

Covariates

Baseline covariates were obtained from questionnaires, physical measurements, laboratory assays, and linked health records at UK Biobank assessment centers. Demographic factors included age at baseline (enrollment), sex (men or women), race (White or non-White), and educational attainment (college or university, below college). Socioeconomic status was assessed using the Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI), derived from national census data to indicate area-level deprivation [8]. Lifestyle factors included smoking status (never, previous, current, or no answer) and alcohol consumption (never, previous, current, or no answer). Dietary habits were evaluated using a diet score (range: 0–4) reflecting adherence to key dietary recommendations – consuming at least four tablespoons of vegetables daily, three pieces of fruit per day, fish at least twice per week, and limiting processed meat to no more than twice weekly [8]. Employment status was categorized into employment, retired, or unemployed/no answer. Physical activity was expressed as total metabolic equivalent minutes per week (MET) derived from baseline questionnaires. Clinical and anthropometric measures included systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP, DBP), grip strength (adjusted for weight) [9], hip circumference and WC, BMI, and WHtR. Laboratory biomarkers comprised fasting plasma glucose (FPG), serum lipids (high-density lipoprotein, low-density lipoprotein, triglycerides), urate, creatinine, and C-reactive protein. A polygenic risk score for cardiovascular disease (CVD PRS) was included to account for inherited susceptibility (Field ID 26224).

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests, while continuous variables were assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Mann-Whitney U tests, depending on distributional assumptions. Associations between obesity classifications and outcomes – including all-cause mortality and MACE – were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models. The underlying time scale was time-on-study, defined as the interval between the date of enrollment and the date of the first occurrence of the MACE, death, or the last follow-up. For the analysis of MACE, individuals were censored at the date of death if it occurred prior to a MACE event. In the primary model (Model A), based on the 2024 EASO framework, individuals with normal weight (BMI <25 kg/m2) served as the reference group. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated for participants classified as EASO overweight, EASO new obesity, and BMI obesity (≥30 kg/m2). In the secondary model (Model B), EASO-defined categories were further stratified by morbidity status, using normal-weight participants without morbidities as the reference group. This model enabled direct comparisons across normal-weight individuals with morbidity, EASO overweight, and EASO new obesity, while BMI obesity was further evaluated separately for participants with and without morbidity. All models were adjusted for demographic, socioeconomic, behavioral, and functional confounders, including age at baseline (enrollment), sex, race, education, employment status, smoking, alcohol consumption, diet score, physical activity, and grip strength. The proportional hazards assumption was verified for all primary models using Schoenfeld residuals, with no significant violations observed (all p > 0.05). Sensitivity analyses additionally incorporated clinical and laboratory measures, including SBP, DBP, high-density lipoprotein-C, low-density lipoprotein-C, FPG, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and CVD PRS. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using age as the underlying timescale, with entry time defined as age at enrollment and exit time as age at the event or censoring, to evaluate the robustness of our primary findings against potential residual age-related confounding. To explore potential heterogeneities in the observed associations, subgroup analyses were conducted by stratifying participants according to age-at-risk (age at event or censoring, dichotomized at the median), sex (men and women), and CVD PRS (dichotomized at the median). The statistical significance of potential interactions between the EASO classifications and these subgroup variables was formally evaluated using likelihood ratio tests. All statistical tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using Stata/MP 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) and R software, version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Study Population

Of 501,991 participants in the UK Biobank, 489,328 had available BMI and WHtR data after excluding those with missing anthropometric measures or BMI <18.5 kg/m2. After further excluding 37,713 individuals with a history of cancer, 451,615 were included in the mortality analysis, and 447,767 (after removing 3,848 with CVD) were included in the MACE analysis (online suppl. Fig. 1).

Prevalence and Characteristics

Based on BMI, 32.68% of participants were normal weight, 42.82% overweight, and 24.50% obesity. Under the EASO criteria, 15.62% of those previously considered overweight were reclassified as having obesity, increasing overall obesity prevalence to 40.12% (Fig. 1). Reclassification was more frequent in men (19.75%) than in women (12.03%) and similar across ethnic groups. Participants newly identified as having obesity by EASO were older (59.3 ± 7.2 years), more often male, and had higher rates of smoking, hypertension, and elevated CVD PRS compared with those with overweight or BMI obesity (Table 1). They showed higher TDI and intermediate cardiometabolic profiles – higher levels of SBP, DBP, C-reactive protein, triglycerides, urate, FPG, BMI, WC, and WHtR than those with normal weight or overweight, but lower than BMI obesity. Education level, MET, and grip strength showed inverse trends.

Newly identified obesity was associated with a greater morbidity burden: 73.46% had one chronic condition and 26.54% had multiple, compared with 37.84% and 19.75% among BMI obesity. The most common morbidites were hypertension (64.28%), arthritis (23.89%), depression (17.45%), and CVD (15.02%). CKD, diabetes, and COPD were relatively infrequent in both groups. For MACE components, incidence was highest in newly identified obesity (7.41%), followed by BMI obesity (5.87%), EASO overweight (3.02%), and normal weight (2.91%), with AMI being the predominant event.

All-Cause Mortality

Over a mean follow-up of 15.1 ± 2.2 years, 44,366 deaths occurred among 451,615 participants. A clear V-shaped association was observed between BMI and all-cause mortality. Compared with normal weight (BMI <25 kg/m2), EASO overweight had the lowest mortality risk (HR 0.82, 95% CI: 0.80–0.84), followed by EASO new obesity (HR 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.99), whereas BMI obesity was associated with a modestly higher risk (HR 1.05, 95% CI: 1.02–1.08) (Model A, Fig. 2). After full adjustment for clinical and biochemical covariates, the inverse associations for EASO overweight and EASO new obesity remained significant, while the excess risk for BMI obesity was fully attenuated (HR 0.99, 95% CI: 0.95–1.02) (online suppl. Fig. 2). Subgroup analyses demonstrated a consistent V-shaped pattern across all subgroups, with stronger associations observed among older adults, men, and participants with high CVD PRS (online suppl. Fig. 3).

When mortality was stratified by morbidity status, the overall V-shaped association across BMI categories (Model B) was preserved (Fig. 2), with all estimates derived using normal weight without morbidity as the reference group. Within each BMI category, individuals with morbidity consistently exhibited higher mortality risk than those without, demonstrating a clear vertical shift in risk rather than a change in the underlying association shape. Among participants without morbidity, EASO overweight showed the lowest mortality risk (HR 0.90, 95% CI: 0.87–0.93), while normal weight and BMI obesity showed modestly higher risks. In contrast, within the morbidity group, mortality risks were uniformly elevated across all BMI categories, yet the V-shaped pattern was preserved with EASO new obesity showing the lowest risk (HR 1.05, 95% CI: 1.01–1.08), although still higher than all non-morbidity groups. Normal weight with morbidity and BMI obesity with morbidity demonstrated similarly elevated risks (HR 1.24 and 1.25, respectively). After further adjustment for clinical and laboratory variables, the modest excess mortality observed in EASO new obesity was fully attenuated (online suppl. Fig. 2). Sensitivity analyses using age as the underlying time scale yielded hazard ratios for all-cause mortality that were nearly identical to those in the primary time-on-study models (online suppl. Fig 4), reinforcing the reliability of our findings. Subgroup analyses showed stronger mortality associations among older adults, men, and those with high CVD PRS (online suppl. Fig. 5).

Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events

During 15.1 ± 2.7 years of follow-up, 19,724 MACEs occurred among 447,767 participants. Compared with normal weight, EASO overweight had a similar risk (HR 1.04, 95% CI: 0.99–1.09), while EASO new obesity (HR 1.35, 95% CI: 1.29–1.42) and BMI obesity (HR 1.38, 95% CI: 1.32–1.45) were associated with higher risks (Model A) (Fig. 3). These associations remained robust after further adjustment for clinical and laboratory variables and subgroups defined by age-at-risk, sex, and CVD PRS (online suppl. Figs. 6, 7).

When stratified by morbidity status, MACE risk increased progressively across BMI categories within each stratum, with BMI obesity representing the highest-risk group in both strata (Model B, Fig. 3). Among participants with morbidities, those with normal weight had the lowest MACE risk (HR 1.46 [95% CI: 1.37–1.56]), yet their risk exceeded that of BMI obesity without morbidities, which represented the highest-risk group among participants without morbidities (HR 1.34 [95% CI: 1.25–1.44]). The robustness of our primary findings was further supported by subgroup analyses and models adjusted for clinical and laboratory covariates, with no significant interactions observed for age-at-risk, sex, or CVD PRS (online suppl. Fig. 6, 8). Sensitivity analyses using age as the underlying timescale yielded hazard ratios for MACE that were nearly identical to those in the primary time-on-study models (online suppl. Fig 9), reinforcing the reliability of our findings.

Discussion

In this large, population-based cohort, we systematically compared two complementary obesity classifications – the 2024 EASO framework, and an extended EASO-based model incorporating morbidity stratification – to elucidate their epidemiologic and prognostic implications. Applying the EASO framework markedly increased the prevalence of obesity by reclassifying overweight individuals with elevated WHtR and obesity-related morbidity, thereby capturing a phenotype characterized by greater metabolic dysfunction and clinical burden. Importantly, this reclassification identifies a high-risk group whose cardiovascular risk profiles are comparable to those with BMI obesity, rather than traditional overweight. Conversely, those classified as EASO overweight represented a lower risk subgroup with the most favorable survival outcomes. When morbidity stratification was incorporated, morbidity status emerged as the predominant determinant of mortality: individuals with morbidity – irrespective of BMI – had substantially higher all-cause mortality, whereas EASO overweight without morbidity exhibited the lowest risk across all models and subgroups. For cardiovascular outcomes, however, risk increased progressively with higher adiposity within each morbidity stratum, and participants with morbidity consistently demonstrated higher MACE incidence than those without. These findings indicate that morbidity shifts risk levels upward without altering the overall association pattern between adiposity and outcomes.

Previous epidemiologic studies have reported a nonlinear, often U- or J-shaped association between BMI and all-cause mortality, with excess risk observed at both low and high BMI levels, whereas the relationship between BMI and MACE tends to be more linear and positive [10–12]. EASO framework represented an important step toward metabolic and morbidity heterogeneity within the overweight population [5, 6], but also reveal an important limitation: the framework primarily differentiates overweight individuals, leaving potential heterogeneity within normal weight and obesity categories insufficiently characterized. Given that metabolically unhealthy phenotypes and morbidity exist across the full BMI spectrum [13, 14], risk stratification should focus primarily on metabolism and morbidity status rather than body weight alone, emphasizing the need for clinical evaluation of metabolic health and management of morbidity over anthropometric thresholds [15, 16]. By examining both all-cause mortality and MACE, we demonstrate that cardiometabolic risk is not confined to obesity alone – individuals with normal weight but existing morbidity and EASO new obesity face substantial adverse outcomes, while some individuals with obesity without morbidity may maintain relatively favorable cardiometabolic prognoses in terms of mortality and MACE. These findings challenge the conventional paradigms centered on BMI or central adiposity, emphasizing that body weight and fat distribution alone are insufficient markers of health. Incorporating morbidity into obesity classification enables more precise risk stratification and supports a shift toward individualized, phenotype-based prevention and management strategies across the full spectrum of adiposity.

The distinct patterns observed for mortality and MACE likely reflect different underlying mechanisms. For all-cause mortality, the presence of morbidity – particularly CVD, renal, and inflammatory diseases – may capture systemic vulnerability and chronic disease burden more accurately than BMI or WHtR alone. Importantly, morbidity should not be viewed as an independent stratifier, but rather as both a marker of accumulated disease burden and a potential mediator linking excess adiposity to adverse clinical outcomes [17]. From this perspective, it reflects progressive physiological impairment and reduced reserve resulting from long-term metabolic stress. In addition, unintentional weight loss among individuals with chronic illness may contribute to reverse causation, further amplifying mortality within morbidity groups [18, 19]. Consequently, morbidity status thus provides an integrative representation of overall health status that helps explain differential risk beyond adiposity measures alone. MACE risk increased progressively with higher BMI across morbidity strata, indicating that both adiposity and morbidity contribute to cardiovascular risk [20]. Excess visceral fat promotes systemic inflammation, dyslipidemia, and insulin resistance, while morbidities – such as hypertension, diabetes, and depression – further exacerbate vascular and cardiac dysfunction [8, 21, 22]. Notably, the presence of morbidity amplified the adverse cardiovascular effects of adiposity, shifting the entire risk curve upward across BMI categories. This synergistic interaction suggests that morbidity acts not merely as a cofactor but as a risk multiplier and a key mediator, magnifying the impact of excess adiposity on cardiovascular outcomes by reflecting the cumulative burden of metabolic stress on the vasculature. Together, these findings highlight that MACE risk reflects the combined effects of adiposity and obesity-related morbidity, emphasizing the need for comprehensive risk assessment that integrates both phenotypic classification and disease-burden markers beyond BMI alone.

From a clinical perspective, these findings underscore the need to move beyond adiposity-centric assessment toward a more integrated, phenotype-based approach to obesity management. The marked increase in obesity prevalence under the EASO framework carries profound clinical implications: it formally recognizes a high-risk population previously labeled as “overweight” who, despite not meeting the BMI ≥30 kg/m2 threshold, exhibit substantial cardiovascular vulnerability. By validating the “EASO new obesity” phenotype, our results provide a clinical rationale for earlier access to intensive therapies – such as GLP-1 receptor agonists or metabolic surgery – that were historically restricted by BMI-centric eligibility criteria. Importantly, our results highlight that obesity and morbidity interact to shape long-term health outcomes. Morbidity amplifies the adverse impact of adiposity on both cardiovascular and mortality risks, while excess adiposity in turn predisposes individuals to developing metabolic and functional comorbidities, forming a reinforcing cycle of risk. Clinically, this implies that cardiometabolic risk is not confined to obesity alone – individuals with normal weight but existing morbidity and EASO new obesity face comparable or even higher risks, whereas some individuals with obesity without morbidity may maintain relatively favorable cardiometabolic prognoses in terms of mortality and MACE. Therefore, comprehensive management of obesity should prioritize the early detection, prevention, and control of morbidity across all BMI categories rather than focusing solely on weight reduction. This morbidity-integrated framework may enhance risk stratification, improve prognostic characterization, and inform more personalized prevention and treatment strategies in both clinical and public health settings.

The strengths of this study include its large, prospective design, standardized measurements, and comprehensive ascertainment of mortality and cardiovascular outcomes. Nonetheless, several limitations should be noted. The UK Biobank population is predominantly of European ancestry and generally healthier than the general population, which may limit generalizability. Importantly, while this “healthy volunteer” bias primarily affects absolute risk estimates and prevalence rates, previous methodological studies have demonstrated that it does not significantly compromise the validity of relative risk associations (e.g., hazard ratios) investigated in large-scale cohorts. Residual confounding may persist due to imprecise measurement or incomplete control of lifestyle and socioeconomic factors included in the models. Finally, our study focused exclusively on hard clinical endpoints, namely, all-cause mortality and MACE. While certain obesity phenotypes showed relatively lower risks for these outcomes, obesity is a multisystem disorder associated with numerous other complications, including musculoskeletal issues and impaired physical functioning, which significantly impact quality of life but were not assessed in this analysis.

Conclusions

Applying the 2024 EASO framework substantially increased the estimated prevalence of obesity and improved the identification of individuals with adverse metabolic and cardiovascular profiles. When extended to include morbidity stratification, morbidity did not alter the overall pattern of risk across adiposity levels but consistently amplified absolute risk at all levels. These findings highlight the complementary roles of adiposity and morbidity in shaping long-term health outcomes and support morbidity-integrated obesity phenotyping as a more clinically meaningful approach for risk stratification and management.
