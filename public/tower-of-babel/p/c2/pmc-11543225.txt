Domestic single-port robot-assisted laparoscopic surgery for pyelotomy combined with shockwave lithotripsy: the first case report
Authors: Ruixiao Li, Jianxin Ni, Xuelian Li, Bin Wu, Bo Li, Rui-Ping Su, Song Xue, Guojun Wu
Published: 2024

Introduction

The incidence of kidney stones is rising due to changes in people’s lifestyles1. This condition often manifests as severe, unbearable pain in the kidneys and can negatively affect kidney function and reduce the quality of life for patients. Staghorn kidney stones are complex stones with extensive branches, making them difficult to remove completely as they occupy a large proportion of the renal pelvis and calyces. Currently, percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) is the most commonly used method to treat staghorn kidney stones. However, this procedure requires the establishment of multiple channels and may need to be performed multiple times to completely remove the stones. This not only increases the pain and economic burden on the patient but also elevates the surgical risks2,3. Some studies have proposed combined treatments, but no unified plan exists. Combining percutaneous nephroscopy and flexible transurethral ureteroscopy to treat staghorn kidney stones can reduce trauma and complications for patients while achieving ideal stone removal outcomes. Laparoscopic and flexible ureteroscopy combined with pyeloplasty and lithotomy, as well as robot-assisted laparoscopic pyeloplasty, are new treatment approaches for these patients4,5. We report a patient with a large staghorn-shaped renal calculus accompanied by low back pain and hematuria. After evaluating the patient’s condition, we performed a single-port robot-assisted laparoscopic pyelotomy combined with EMS lithotomy, referring to previously discussed combination therapies for staghorn nephrolithiasis in the published literature.

Case report

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xi’an People’s Hospital (Approval No. 20220019). The patient provided informed consent. A 75-year-old woman initially presented to our urology clinic with right lower back pain and hematuria in January 2024. Her medical history was unremarkable except for diabetes. Physical examination showed tenderness and percussion pain in the right kidney but no abdominal abnormalities. The patient’s baseline hematologic and biochemical parameters were normal. Urine tests suggested infection and hematuria. Computed tomography without contrast showed right renal pelvis and calyceal stones ~6.1×5.8×3.5 cm in size without evidence of hydronephrosis. Imaging studies did not reveal any signs of ureteropelvic junction obstruction (Fig. 1).

Considering the patient’s age, the size of the stone, and her history of diabetes, a thorough evaluation was conducted to determine the most appropriate treatment method. Although PCNL is considered the gold standard for treating staghorn kidney stones, it was decided that single-port robot-assisted laparoscopic pyelotomy combined with EMS lithotomy would be a safer and more effective option for this patient. The reasoning behind this decision included minimizing surgical trauma, reducing the risk of complications, and optimizing stone removal. After treating the patient’s urinary tract infection, she accepted the urology department’s advice and chose single-port robot-assisted laparoscopic pyelotomy combined with EMS lithotomy6. After administering general anesthesia, the patient was placed in a supine position with the waist of the affected side elevated and tilted 30–45° to the healthy side. All stress-bearing parts were properly protected and fixed. An incision was made around the umbilicus on the right side in an arc of 180–270°. The pedicled skin flap faced the surgical field. The skin and subcutaneous tissue were incised layer by layer, the ligaments were separated, and the periumbilical muscle gap was incised. The peritoneum was opened, and the abdominal cavity was accessed. A single-port multichannel puncture device was used to establish pneumoperitoneum with a pressure of 13 mmHg (1 mmHg=0.133 kPa) and a CO2 flow of 5 l/min. Instruments were inserted, and the right lateral peritoneum was freed to expose the renal pelvis and ureter. The paraureteral tissue was separated along the ureter to completely expose the enlarged renal pelvis. During surgery, under robotic endoscopy, the renal pelvis was dissected, and no signs of ectopic vascular compression or ureteral obstruction were observed. Cutting the renal pelvis longitudinally with scissors about 2 cm revealed a large stone. Due to the stone’s size and location in the renal calyces, it could not be removed directly. Furthermore, the renal pelvis incision was close to the renal vein, and expanding the interface could cause renal vein damage and bleeding. The nephroscope and EMS system were inserted through the auxiliary trocar to crush the stones, which were then suctioned out. After confirming there were no remaining stones in the renal pelvis and calyces, the nephroscope was removed. The right renal pelvis was sutured intermittently with a 5-0 micro suture, and an F6 ureteral (double J) stent was inserted. After verifying there was no urine leakage from the anastomosis and no bleeding from the surrounding tissues (Fig. 2), an F-20 silicone catheter was placed in the operative region for postoperative drainage. The robotic instruments were removed, and the incision was closed layer by layer. During surgery, under robotic endoscopy, the renal pelvis was dissected, and no signs of ectopic vascular compression or ureteral obstruction were observed.

The drainage tube was removed on postoperative day 5. The patient stayed in the hospital for 10 days without any complications (Fig. 3). At her follow-up visits, CT showed stone clearance without hydronephrosis. The double J stent was removed 8 weeks after discharge.

Discussion

Because the stones fill the renal pelvis and calyces, the clinical treatment for staghorn kidney stones is mainly percutaneous nephrolithotomy. However, due to the distribution of the stones in the calyces, intraoperative and postoperative bleeding, postoperative infection, and residual stones are common complications that can affect the patient’s prognosis7–9. Additionally, reports indicate that in surgical plans using percutaneous renal treatment alone, the establishment of intraoperative channels can impact the treatment outcome. Intraoperative B-ultrasound imaging is required to assist with positioning when establishing a percutaneous renal channel. If the kidneys are not filled with water during the operation, the image clarity is reduced, increasing the surgical risk10,11.

In recent years, advancements in minimally invasive concepts and medical equipment have driven urology towards ‘minimally invasive surgery, precision surgery, and minimally invasive functionalization’. People have increasingly recognized the benefits of reducing surgical wounds, leading to the emergence of the concept of ‘ultra-minimally invasive’12. Single-port laparoscopy offers the benefits of minimally invasive surgery, hidden scars, reduced pain, and speedy recovery. However, it comes with limitations such as the loss of the operating triangle, chopstick effect, tube-shaped field of view, and instability, leading to fatigue for the surgeon and scope assistant, making precise treatment challenging. Robot-assisted single-port laparoscopic surgery overcomes some of these limitations by using robots to precisely control instruments with 7° of freedom and restoring the operating triangle. Nevertheless, during actual operations, issues like external collisions of the robot arm and limited instrument movement in the surgical field arise. Hence, the development of single-port robotic surgical systems addresses these challenges13,14. Compared to open surgery, laparoscopic pyelolithotomy, and PCNL, robot-assisted laparoscopic pyelolithotomy can serve as an alternative treatment for large, multiple, and complex kidney stones. It enables stone removal without passing through the renal parenchyma, thereby reducing the risk of bleeding, kidney damage, and increasing the stone clearance rate15. In 2006, Badani et al. 16 reported on 13 cases of such surgeries, initially demonstrating the feasibility of this technology. In 2014, Naitoh et al. utilized a 2.5 cm incision at or below the umbilicus to perform single-port laparoscopic pyelolithotomy combined with pyeloplasty on four cases involving kidney stones and pelvis-ureteral junction obstruction. No significant complications were observed, and all patients were stone-free upon confirmation, indicating that robot-assisted single-port technology is equally safe and effective in treating complex kidney stones17.

This case represents the first instance of domestic single-port robot-assisted laparoscopic pyelotomy combined with EMS lithotripsy. The transabdominal approach in this patient offers advantages such as full exposure of the renal pelvis, a spacious operating area, and easy maneuverability during the incision. Opening the renal pelvis allows convenient insertion of the nephroscope from all directions, compensating for surgical blind spots left by laparoscopy due to operating limitations, thereby maximizing stone removal and improving stone clearance rates.

Moreover, once the nephroscope is inserted into the renal pelvis, the location and shape of the stones can be observed through the window on the renal pelvis surface. This ensures a clear and broad surgical field without damaging the renal parenchyma, thus reducing intraoperative bleeding and facilitating postoperative recovery for the patient. This clinical trial provides preliminary evidence that the domestic single-port robotic surgical system is safe and feasible for clinical practice.

Compared to the gold standard PCNL, this technique also has its shortcomings and limitations. First, technical complexity and learning curve: single-port robot-assisted laparoscopic surgery is technically demanding and requires specialized training and experience. Surgeons must undergo extensive training to master the use of robotic systems, which can be a significant barrier to widespread adoption. Secondly, accessibility and cost: the use of robotic systems and advanced laparoscopic techniques can be expensive and may not be accessible in all medical institutions. In our hospital, we have three robotic systems (Da Vinci and two domestic robots) to ensure the smooth execution of surgeries. Additionally, the surgery was performed by a urologist with experience in over 600 robotic procedures.

Conclusion

In this case, an elderly female patient presented with right-sided back pain and hematuria caused by staghorn-shaped kidney stones. Due to the patient’s large renal pelvic stones, advanced age, and history of diabetes, we performed single-port robot-assisted laparoscopic pyelotomy combined with EMS lithotomy. The surgery proceeded smoothly, with no evident postoperative complications, and the stones were completely removed. This procedure appears to be a safe and effective treatment option. However, whether it is noninferior or superior to traditional PNL or laparoscopic combined PNL requires further prospective clinical trials for verification.
