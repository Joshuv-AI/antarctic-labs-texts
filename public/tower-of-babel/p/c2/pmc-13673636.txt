Predictors of routine HPV-based cervical cancer screening adoption in federally qualified health centers within a multi-state practice research network 2012–2022: retrospective observational study
Authors: Michelle B Shin, Connor R Volpi, Warren Szewczyk, Peggy A Hannon, Paula Lozano, Jennifer Tsui, Allison M Cole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer screening guidelines in the United States have evolved with the introduction of human papillomavirus (HPV)-based modalities, including co-testing in 2012 and primary HPV testing in 2018, in addition to Pap-only screening. However, adoption of these modalities in federally qualified health centers (FQHCs) has been underexplored. We examined longitudinal patterns of HPV-based cervical cancer screening adoption (e.g., proportion screened with co- and primary HPV testing) across three FQHC systems in Washington and Idaho and explored factors associated with receipt of HPV-based modalities.MethodsUsing electronic health record data, we identified the first routine cervical cancer screening among 11,316 individuals from 2012–2022 across 25 clinics within three FQHCs. Screening modality at the screening was classified as Pap-only, co-testing, or primary HPV testing. We quantified the proportion of each screening modality by year and FQHC from 2012–2022 and used multivariable logistic regression to estimate associations of factors (age, race, ethnicity, rurality, insurance status, primary care visits, and FQHC) with HPV-based screening for 2012–2018 and 2018–2022 guideline periods.ResultsThe proportion of up-to-date screening in 2022 was 34.0%, 22.7%, and 37.6% for FQHCs A, B, and C, respectively. Among individuals receiving their first routine screening from 2012–2022, 59.1% underwent Pap-only, 38.8% co-testing, and 2.0% primary HPV testing, respectively. Pap-only was nearly universal in 2012 but declined to 57.0% by 2022, while co-testing increased to 41.0%. Primary HPV testing remained < 7.0% across all years and FQHCs. During the 2012–2018 guideline period, odds of co-testing (vs. Pap-only) were higher among Hispanic (aOR = 1.56; 95% CI, 1.35–1.80) and Black (aOR = 1.64; 95% CI, 1.18–2.28) individuals and screened at FQHC C (aOR = 4.21; 95% CI, 3.41–5.22). Odds were lower among rural (aOR = 0.20; 95% CI, 0.17–0.24) and uninsured patients (aOR = 0.51; 95% CI, 0.44–0.58). From 2018–2022, odds of co-testing were higher among individuals aged 40–59 and Black, lower at FQHC B (aOR = 0.48; 95% CI, 0.37–0.63), and higher at FQHC C (aOR = 1.34; 95% CI, 1.06–1.68).ConclusionsAdoption of HPV-based screening modalities increased over time and varied by patients' rurality, race, and ethnicity. Introduction

In the United States (US), the advancements in cervical cancer screening modalities—particularly the use of human papillomavirus (HPV) testing—have resulted in frequent revisions in cervical cancer screening guidelines over the past decade [1–3]. In 2012, the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF), the American Cancer Society (ACS), and the American College of Gynecology incorporated co-testing (a combination of HPV testing and Pap testing every 5 years) into their screening guidelines, alongside the existing triennial Pap-only screening [4–6]. Subsequently, the ACOG, USPSTF, and ACS introduced primary HPV testing as a screening option in 2016, 2018, and 2020, respectively [4, 7, 8]. At the time of writing, the USPSTF is in its final stages of updating its recommendations [9], and ACS has released a new guideline that incorporates HPV self-sampling [10], signaling that changes in cervical cancer screening modalities are expected to continue.

Adoption or uptake of HPV-based cervical cancer screening, defined here as the proportion and representativeness of settings that initiate or employ co-testing and primary HPV testing in addition to Pap-only screening [11], has varied considerably across geography and demographic characteristics in the US [12, 13]. One study found that co-testing adoption was lower in Midwestern and Southern states than in the Northeast [14], and others suggest that HPV testing availability has declined due to the COVID-19 pandemic [15]. Despite a growing number of screening modalities, limited evidence exists on how these are being implemented in safety-net settings such as federally qualified health centers (FQHCs) and rural primary care clinics, where cervical cancer incidence and mortality remain disproportionately high [16, 17].

Safety-net settings play a critical role in providing preventive care to populations vulnerable to cervical cancer, such as racial/ethnic minorities and socioeconomically disadvantaged communities [16, 18, 19]. A recent study showed that in 2023 alone, safety-net settings provided cervical cancer screening to more than one-third of all under-screened individuals in the US (e.g., unscreened or screened less frequently than the guideline recommendation) with public insurance and one-quarter of under-screened individuals living in rural areas [19]. While striking, these findings are consistent with the sociodemographic characteristics of patients that safety-net settings predominantly serve. More than 65% of safety-net setting patients are racial or ethnic minorities, 42% are rural residents, and 90% have low income—populations that face the greatest barriers to cervical cancer screening [20]. However, cervical cancer screening rates in safety-net settings have remained approximately 50% for a decade [21, 22], compared to the Healthy People 2030 goal of 79% and the national average of 76% in 2023 [23, 24].

Safety-net settings, including FQHCs, often face unique financial [25], structural [26], workforce [27], and clinical complexities in general [28], and may face unique challenges in adopting HPV-based screening modalities. These include limited funding, staffing shortages, competing clinical priorities, and reduced access to HPV testing infrastructure (e.g., laboratory capacity, provider training, and follow-up systems), which may hinder timely integration of evolving screening guidelines compared to higher-resourced health settings [29, 30]. Despite these challenges, prior studies have largely focused on cross-sectional patterns of HPV-based screening or geographic variation, with limited attention to how screening modalities have changed over time within safety-net settings. In particular, there is a lack of longitudinal evidence examining the adoption of HPV-based screening within FQHC’s and how this varies across organizations and patient populations [30, 31].

Addressing this gap is important for identifying potential barriers to implementing newer screening modalities in safety-net settings that care for medically underserved populations, such as insufficient resources and infrastructure for new equipment or technology and limited training for staff and providers. Understanding these barriers will be essential for facilitating equitable integration of emerging screening modalities. Therefore, this study aimed to:

Describe trends in the use of Pap-only, co-testing, and primary HPV testing across three safety-net setting primary care organizations from 2012–2022, with comparisons across the 2012 and 2018 guideline periods.

Describe trends in the use of Pap-only, co-testing, and primary HPV testing across three safety-net setting primary care organizations from 2012–2022, with comparisons across the 2012 and 2018 guideline periods.

Identify patient characteristics associated with the receipt of HPV-based screening (co-testing or primary HPV testing) versus Pap-only screening among adults aged 30–65, stratified by the 2012 and 2018 guideline periods.

Methods

Data source

This retrospective observational study used electronic health record (EHR) data from 25 primary care clinics belonging to three FQHCs in Washington and Idaho affiliated with the Washington, Wyoming, Alaska, Montana, and Idaho region Practice and Research Network (WPRN). These FQHCs serve as primary care providers for medically underserved urban and rural communities with limited access to medical care. EHR data were obtained through DataQUEST [32, 33], a clinical data repository that captures structured data on patient demographics, clinical diagnoses (International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions [ICD-9/10]), procedures (Current Procedural Terminology/Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System [CPT/HCPCS]), and encounter information, which allowed for identification of cervical cancer screening practices.

Eligibility criteria

We identified patients with assigned female sex at birth, aged 30 to 65 years, with at least one primary care visit in a given year between January 1, 2012, and December 31, 2022. The sampling methods are summarized in the supplementary material. We identified all the cervical cancer screenings they received during the observation period using diagnostic/procedural codes for Pap testing, co-testing, and primary HPV testing, which applied for all years of the study (Appendix 2, Supplementary Material). Individuals aged 21–29 were excluded, as co-testing was not recommended for this group per the 2012 and 2018 USPSTF guidelines [4, 34].

Several steps were followed to ensure capturing routine cervical cancer screening, which involved excluding individuals who previously had abnormal screening results or conditions requiring frequent screening. First, we ensured that no individual had undergone cervical cancer screening within 1 year before the first cervical cancer screening recorded between 2012 and 2022. We compiled a list of diagnostic/procedural codes that would put the individual at higher risk or require non-routine screening, according to the USPSTF guidelines, such as those with a history of high-grade precancerous cervical lesions or cervical cancer (Appendix 3, Supplementary Material) [4]. We also excluded individuals with a positive HPV test or dysplasia who would be recommended to screen at more frequent intervals. We kept only the first cervical cancer screening modality recorded during the study period for each patient to avoid capturing repeat Pap smear or reflex HPV testing that would be required to follow up on abnormal results according to the American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology’s risk-based management guidelines, meaning that the analysis was limited to one screening event per patient [35, 36]. These codes were curated from other studies that examined cervical cancer screening using claims data [37–39].

We identified 11,316 average-risk females aged 30–65 with at least one medical encounter and cervical cancer screening during the study period without a history of hysterectomy, cervical cancer, or other conditions that would make them ineligible for routine cervical cancer screening (Fig. 1).

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was the cervical screening modality used for routine cervical cancer screening between 2012 and 2022 across 3 FQHCs. Screening modalities were defined as Pap-only, co-testing (Pap and HPV testing within 30 days), or primary HPV testing. Because Pap and HPV testing for co-testing do not always occur simultaneously, we considered claims for Pap and HPV testing occurring within ≤ 30 days of each other as co-testing [39]. Screening with the same modality for index and subsequent screening (i.e., Pap-Pap) ≤ 60 days from each other was considered one test, and > 60 days was considered different tests.

The 2012 USPSTF guideline recommended Pap or co-testing, and the 2018 USPSTF guideline (released August 21, 2018) recommended Pap, co-testing, or primary HPV testing (HPV testing only) for individuals aged 30–65 [40, 4]. Hence, we divided our analysis and outcomes into mutually exclusive “2012 guideline period” (January 1, 2012, to August 31, 2018) and "2018 guideline period" (September 1, 2018, to December 31, 2022). We categorized the screening modalities into mutually exclusive categories of Pap-only and co-testing, and primary HPV testing for both guideline periods. Primary HPV testing was excluded from the period-specific models due to a lack of recommendation in the 2012 guideline period and small cell sizes in the 2018 guideline period. However, we enumerated all screening modalities and conducted sensitivity analysis to ensure consistent outcomes (available upon request).

Predictors of interest

Our patient-level predictors of interest included age, race, ethnicity, insurance status, rurality, and the number of primary care visits. These variables were selected based on prior literature showing their relevance to screening modalities [13, 41–44]. Age at screening was categorized as 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, or 60–65. Race categories available in DataQUEST were White, Black or African American, American Indian/Alaskan Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, and multiple races, and ethnicity categories were Hispanic and non-Hispanic. Rurality was categorized into urban and rural according to the Rural–Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes. Insurance status included insured and not insured, and the number of primary care visits was categorized into ≤ 2 versus > 2 visits across the observation period 2012–2022.

Statistical analysis

First, we described the organizational characteristics of the 3 FQHCs, including the number of clinics, patient panel size, providers (full-time equivalents), and rurality. Clinical staff were defined as members of the clinical team outside of the PCP, such as medical assistants, nurses, and clinical administrators. We calculated the proportion of patients with up-to-date cervical cancer screening by FQHC for 2022, defining up-to-date status for individuals aged 30–65 years as having completed a Pap test within the past 3 years or either co-testing or primary HPV testing within the past 5 years, according to USPSTF guidelines [4].

We compared the distribution of screening modalities by sociodemographic and clinical factors using chi-squared tests. We fit bivariate logistic regression models to analyze the unadjusted association between covariates and the screening modalities. Finally, we fit a multivariable logistic regression model for each guideline period to examine the adjusted associations of covariates that had a statistically significant unadjusted association (age, race, ethnicity, rurality, primary care visits, insurance status, and FQHC) with screening modality. Missing values for race (n = 997; 8.8%), ethnicity (n = 1,468, 13.0%), and rurality (n = 405, 3.6%) were coded as a separate group for each variable and included in regression models. For both guideline periods, we estimated associations of each variable with being screened with co-testing versus Pap (i.e., co-testing = 1, Pap testing = 0). While our initial regression modeling approach used a multinomial logistic regression model to compare Pap testing, co-testing, and primary HPV testing, the small proportion of primary HPV testing (< 6.4% annually across FQHCs) led to model convergence issues and unstable estimates after inclusion of covariates. Therefore, we restricted the outcome to Pap testing and co-testing and used a binary logistic regression model. For all analyses, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and Stata 17 was used (StataCorp, College Station, TX). The Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the University of Washington determined this study to be exempt (STUDY00018274). The REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely collected health Data (RECORD) Statement was followed (Appendix 4, Supplementary Material).

Overall, 11,316 individuals with an average risk of cervical cancer across 3 FQHCs from 2012 to 2022 were included in the analysis, with 7,880 individuals (69.6%) screened during the 2012 guideline period and 3,436 (30.4%) during the 2018 period (Fig. 1). The mean age at the time of screening was 45.8 years. The majority were White (84.7%), not Hispanic (70.4%), lived in urban areas (67.6%), had insurance (85.4%), and had ≤ 2 primary care visits (62.6%), and the largest proportion received care at FQHC B (46.9%). Overall, Pap testing accounted for 59.1% of screenings across the study period, followed by co-testing (38.8%) and primary HPV testing (2.0%).

In 2012, all three FQHCs performed Pap tests almost exclusively (Fig. 2). By 2022, Pap testing decreased to approximately 58.1% of all screenings, while co-testing increased to about 40.9%, reflecting a gradual adoption of HPV-based screening approaches. Primary HPV testing remained low throughout the study period and did not exceed 7% of total screenings in any given year, even after its inclusion in screening guidelines in 2018.

FQHC A showed a steady increase in co-testing beginning in 2012, rising to approximately 58.8% by 2022, while HPV testing remained minimal. In contrast, FQHC B had a more rapid uptake of co-testing, reaching approximately 63.8% in 2019 prior to the COVID-19 pandemic and declining sharply to 0% and returning to performing Pap testing almost exclusively by 2022. FQHC C experienced the steepest early increase in co-testing, reaching approximately 70.4% of all screenings by 2014; however, this trend reversed and declined approximately 41.9% in 2019 before trending upward again to roughly 76.8% by 2022.

Patient-level associations with screening modality

In the adjusted multivariable model for the 2012 guideline period (Fig. 3A), the odds of co-testing were significantly greater among 40–49 year-olds and 50–59 year-olds when compared to 30–39 year-olds (aOR = 1.34; 95% CI, 1.17–1.52; aOR 1.22; 95% CI, 1.06–1.39; p < 0.05, respectively), Hispanic individuals compared with non-Hispanic (aOR = 1.56; 95% CI, 1.35–1.80; p < 0.05), Black or American patients compared with White (aOR = 1.64; 95% CI, 1.18–2.28; p < 0.05), and those screened at FQHC C (aOR = 4.21; 95% CI, 3.41–5.22; p < 0.05) compared with FQHC A. Conversely, the odds of co-testing were significantly lower among individuals in rural areas compared with urban areas (aOR = 0.20; 95% CI, 0.17–0.24; p < 0.05) and among those without insurance compared with insured (aOR = 0.51; 95% CI, 0.44–0.58; p < 0.05).

Forest plot of logistic regression model results showing associations of demographic and clinical variables with co-testing (versus Pap testing) for the 2012 (A) and 2018 (B) guideline periods. Estimates for missing categories for race, ethnicity, and rurality are not shown

From 2018–2022 (Fig. 3B), the odds of co-testing were significantly greater among 40–49 year-olds and 50–59 year-olds when compared to 30–39 year-olds (aOR = 1.40; 95% CI, 1.21–1.78; p < 0.05; aOR = 1.47; 95% CI, 1.21–1.78; p < 0.05), respectively. Compared to White patients, Black or African American patients had significantly greater odds of co-testing (aOR = 1.95; 95% CI, 1.25–3.05; p < 0.05), but no other racial differences were observed. Lastly, patients screened at FQHC B had substantially lower odds of co-testing compared with FQHC A (aOR = 0.48; 95% CI: 0.37–0.63; p < 0.05), whereas patients screened at FQHC C had higher odds of co-testing compared with FQHC A (aOR = 1.34; 95% CI: 1.06–1.68; p < 0.05).

Discussion

We found significant variation in the adoption of newer cervical cancer screening modalities, including co-testing and primary HPV testing, among patients served by FQHCs during a period when national guidelines were evolving to incorporate these approaches. Specifically, patients living in rural areas, those without insurance, and those with fewer primary care visits were significantly less likely to receive co-testing. These patterns suggest that access to care, visit frequency, and continuity of care may influence whether patients are offered or receive HPV-based screening. Rurality may also partially explain why Black and Hispanic patients were more likely to undergo co-testing, reflecting demographic patterns in the northwestern US, where Black and Hispanic populations are more concentrated in urban areas and rural populations are predominantly non-Hispanic White [45]. Potential structural barriers to HPV-based screening in rural communities are concerning, given that rural populations already experience lower cervical cancer screening rates and that rural–urban disparities may have widened following the COVID-19 pandemic [46–48].

While heterogeneity in modality adoption is not inherently problematic, modality-specific characteristics may create unintended barriers. Longer screening intervals associated with HPV-based testing (e.g., five years versus three years for Pap-only) may be challenging for transient or underserved populations and may generate anxiety among some patients [41]. Providers may also prefer more frequent Pap screening to maintain regular patient contact in safety-net settings [44]. Prior work, including studies from our group, has demonstrated that modality preferences vary by education, age, and rurality, factors that also influence screening adherence [1, 49]. As observed with changes in colorectal cancer screening modalities, differences in modality uptake can interact with structural and patient-level factors in complex ways, potentially contributing to disparities [50]. Ensuring clear screening recommendations from providers and consistently communicating new guidelines and policies across organizations will be important for supporting the adoption of newer screening modalities.

In our study, adoption of HPV-based screening varied substantially across the three FQHCs, including a marked decline in HPV-based screening in FQHC B by 2022, with distinct temporal patterns and fluctuations that underscore the influence of organizational characteristics. Prior research demonstrates that such characteristics shape both the uptake of specific screening modalities and overall screening performance [51–53], highlighting the need to better understand the heterogeneity of safety-net setting contexts. Although FQHCs share a mission to serve medically underserved populations, they differ in staffing models, laboratory access, payer mix, panel size, and primary care provider-to-support staff ratios [18]. Adoption of HPV-based modalities requires laboratory infrastructure, workforce capacity, and workflow adaptation [2]. Without adequate funding and technical support, introducing new screening approaches may strain already resource-constrained systems and inadvertently exacerbate disparities [54, 55].

The sharp reduction of HPV-based screening observed across organizations during the later observational period may be attributed to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. Cervical cancer screening declined significantly during the pandemic, as preventative services were deferred and patients avoided healthcare settings [13, 47, 48]. Molecular laboratory capacity was redirected toward SARS-CoV-2 testing, resulting in shortages of reagents, consumables, and laboratory personnel [56–59]. Because HPV testing relies on similar molecular platforms, it is plausible that these disruptions may have disproportionately affected HPV-based screening, along with testing of other sexually transmitted infections [60]. However, additional studies are needed to more fully characterize how the pandemic may have influenced the adoption of newer screening modalities.

Concurrently, the pandemic accelerated the interest and implementation of HPV self-sampling as a strategy to maintain up-to-date screening while reducing in-person visits. HPV self-sampling has demonstrated high acceptability, particularly in rural settings, and has since been incorporated into evolving guidelines [61]. However, rapid shifts in recommended modalities may also have created confusion for patients and providers if guidance is not clearly communicated and consistently implemented [41]. Without qualitative data, we are unable to fully examine the contextual and decision-making factors underlying abrupt changes in screening modalities in certain sites, which is a limitation of this study. However, our findings highlight the importance of further investigating these patterns, particularly in communities served by FQHCs whose screening rates have not returned to pre-pandemic levels, as persistent disruption could widen existing screening disparities.

Another limitation of this study is that we did not directly assess the relationship between screening modality and overall screening uptake or adherence, limiting conclusions about the impact of HPV-based screening adoption on coverage or patient outcomes. We also did not gather FQHC-level data on clinical protocols, provider training, laboratory partnerships, or availability of HPV testing platforms. In addition, as the dataset was drawn from Washington and Idaho with a predominantly White population, it may limit the applicability of our findings to regions with higher racial/ethnic minority populations or areas with different healthcare infrastructure. Because the primary care visits were measured over the study period rather than prior to screening, it may have introduced temporal misclassification and limited its reflection of access to care at the time of screening. Nevertheless, this study is among the few to examine longitudinal trends in modality use within FQHCs providing screening for medically underserved populations across multiple states, offering valuable insight into how new screening modalities diffuse within safety-net settings.

A key next step is to identify the drivers of variation across FQHCs through focused qualitative and implementation research. For example, comparative case studies of FQHCs with differing adoption patterns could elucidate distinct barriers: a rural clinic may face limited laboratory infrastructure or specimen transport challenges, while an urban clinic may encounter staffing constraints or clinical priorities. Both settings may also rely on legacy workflows not optimized for primary HPV testing. Although workflow redesign may be a common solution, these contexts would require tailored strategies to address their unique constraints. Identifying and addressing these needs, spanning provider training, health systems infrastructure, and patient education, will be essential for developing context-specific interventions that enable FQHCs to adopt evidence-based screening practices and ultimately reduce disparities in cervical cancer outcomes among underserved populations.

In conclusion, significant variation in adoption of HPV-based screening across a multi-state PBRN likely reflects unequal access to cervical cancer screening resources, HPV testing capacity, and organizational readiness among FQHCs and the populations they serve. These findings have direct implications for safety-net settings, and targeted investments in laboratory access and workflow design considerations for multiple modalities will be necessary to inform specific policies—such as support for provider training or reimbursement—to help FQHCs adopt evidence-based clinical practices. As new screening modalities, such as HPV self-sampling, are introduced, understanding site-specific barriers to adoption will be critical to ensuring they improve, rather than widen, screening disparities.

These data were accessed under a data use agreement and contain sensitive electronic health record information; therefore, they are not publicly available. Researchers seeking access to the data should apply directly to DataQUEST and will be subject to DataQUEST’s data access policies, including review and approval of a data request and execution of a data use agreement. The authors do not have the authority to grant access to these data.
