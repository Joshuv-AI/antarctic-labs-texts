Theoretical model and parameter optimization of embryo eggshell perforation under impact
Authors: Xin Wang, Mingyan Zhao, Chenyu Chen, Haoke Chen, Mingxuan Liang, Chuan He, Duo Fu, Xiaojing Zhu, Huiming Sun, Cheng Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prior to in-ovo vaccination, the eggshell must be perforated at the air cell region; however, inappropriate perforation parameters can easily cause hole-shape deformation, crack propagation, and shell membrane damage, thereby affecting hatching safety. To obtain low-damage perforation parameters, this study independently designed an impact perforation experimental device for embryo eggs, established a Pogorelov-Griffith bending-fracture coupled model, and optimized punch diameter D, impact velocity V, and impact force F by combining single-factor experiments, three-factor three-level orthogonal experiments, and GA-BP neural network optimization. The mechanical damage score S, constructed from the hole-shape deviation index DI and crack length CL, was used as the primary evaluation indicator, and the effects of the optimized parameters on hatch-of-viable, late embryonic mortality, visible contamination rate, methylene blue penetration, and shell membrane damage were further evaluated through simulated in-ovo manipulation experiments. The results showed that the effects of D, V, and F on S followed the order of “D > V > F”. An increase in punch diameter significantly aggravated hole-shape deformation and crack propagation; impact velocity exhibited an optimal window within the range of 2.2-2.3 m/s; and results of impact force tended to stabilize near 1.5-1.6 N. The GA-BP model showed good predictive ability for S, with an overall R² of 0.91 and an RMSE of 0.02. Based on the combined results of mechanical optimization and biological validation, the recommended parameters were D = 2.0 mm, V = 2.25 m/s, and F = 1.6 N. This parameter group had the lowest S, milder dye penetration and shell membrane damage, a lower visible contamination rate, and the highest hatch-of-viable among the perforation-treated groups. This study indicates that S can serve as a mechanical evaluation indicator for screening low-damage perforation parameters, and the recommended parameter window can provide experimental evidence for optimizing eggshell perforation in in-ovo vaccination.

Introduction

In-ovo injection of Marek’s disease vaccine into 17-day-old embryo eggs provides chicks with immunity immediately upon hatching, offering significant higher efficiency than manual vaccination of hatched chicks (Peebles, 2018; Fatemi et al., 2024; El-Ghany, 2024). With the maturation of the in-ovo vaccination technology, this technique has gained rapid and widespread adoption. For instance, in-ovo vaccination has been widely adopted in commercial broiler hatcheries, where automated systems are used to vaccinate late-stage embryo eggs before transfer to the hatcher (Peebles et al., 2016). This method, which involves injecting vaccines into chicken embryos during late incubation, significantly shortens the the immunity gap compared with post-hatch vaccination while reducing stress responses and improving vaccination uniformity (Peebles, 2018; Fatemi et al., 2024; El-Ghany, 2024). However, the eggshell is a typical thin-walled structure with complex composition (approximately 95% CaCO₃, 3.5% proteinaceous organic matrix, and the remainder water), and numerous pores and inorganic particles are unevenly distributed across its surface (Athanasiadou et al., 2018; Gautron et al., 2021; Le Roy et al., 2021; Moreau et al., 2022). Consequently, producing a controlled opening over the air cell while minimizing crack propagation and shell membrane disruption remains a technical challenge during in-ovo manipulation. (Gildersleeve et al., 1993; Huang et al., 2022). Commercial in-ovo systems generally include a shell-perforation step followed by vaccine delivery through an injection needle. In dual-needle systems, one component first opens the eggshell and a second needle subsequently delivers the vaccine into the egg. Therefore, local eggshell damage generated during the shell-perforation step may affect sealing reliability, leakage risk, and potential contaminant entry. (Gildersleeve et al., 1993; Peebles et al., 2016) Therefore, identifying process parameter combinations that minimize eggshell damage is of critical importance for successful in-ovo vaccination (Huang et al., 2022). The solid flat-ended cylindrical punch used in the present study was not intended to replicate the complete geometry or fluid-delivery function of a commercial hollow injection needle. Instead, it was used as a controlled mechanically equivalent model of the eggshell-perforation step during in-ovo manipulation. This design allowed the effects of the punch diameter, impact velocity, and impact force on local eggshell deformation and crack propagation to be isolated. Therefore, the recommended parameters should be interpreted as a low-damage eggshell-perforation window that provides mechanical guidance for in-ovo vaccination equipment optimization, rather than as final operating parameters for all commercial hollow-needle injectors. However, the key to in-ovo manipulation is not merely the formation of an injectable opening. As shown in Fig. 1, hole-shape deformation, crack propagation, and shell membrane damage generated during perforation may compromise the integrity of the eggshell-membrane barrier, increase the risk of exogenous contaminant entry, and further affect the embryonic mortality and hatch-of-viable. Therefore, optimization of eggshell perforation parameters should consider both control of mechanical damage and biological safety, rather than relying solely on hole formation or crack length as the final evaluation criterion.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of in-ovo vaccination.Fig 1 dummy alt text

Existing research has primarily focused on the macroscopic mechanical properties of table eggs, encompassing static tests (compression, tension, and pressure-bearing tests) and dynamic tests(drop, vibration, and impact) (Hahn et al., 2017; Gautron et al., 2021). These studies have established correlations between eggshell strength and parameters such as shape and thickness (Hahn et al., 2017; Chiang et al., 2021). However, most studies have concentrated on evaluation of whole-shell loading or fracture strength, with limited research devoted to localized perforation and crack propagation on embryo eggs under low-velocity impact (Hahn et al., 2017; Cheng and Ning, 2023). Impact tests have revealed that perforative force varies significantly with impact location and velocity (Hahn et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2021). Finite element and modal analyses have successfully classified eggshell failure modes into distinct categories and demonstrated that fracture force under dynamic impact is consistently higher than that under static loading (Hahn et al., 2017). Studies on biological materials have concluded that eggshell toughness and brittleness are governed by shell thickness, curvature, and overall geometry collectively (Hahn et al., 2017; Chiang et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2024). In industrial applications, the low strength and high fragility of eggshells necessitate stable supporting mechanisms during dynamic processing and grading, and therefore, considerable research has focused on improving egg tray design and developing automated crack-detecting systems (Yu et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2025). On the other hand, although existing preforation mechanics and fracture models for brittle materials (e.g., the Holmquist-Johnson-Cook (HJC) model and the JH-2 model) have been widely and successfully applied to ceramics, concrete, and other engineering materials, they are not well-suited for interpreting the failure behaviour of eggshells, which are porous, multi-component (organic-inorganic composite), thin-shell biological structures (Hahn et al., 2017; Gautron et al., 2021).

In addition, the current embryo egg perforation process mainly rely on empirical adjustment of the punch diameter and impact velocity, whereas commercial in-ovo manipulation are more concerned with how to ensure successful perforation while reducing crack formation, lowering contamination risk, and maintaining stable hatching performance. Existing studies have mainly focused on eggshell mechanical properties, crack detection, or optimization of mechanical parameters during perforation, but few have linked impact parameters with local eggshell damage, shell membrane barrier disruption, and production outcomes such as embryonic mortality and hatch-of-viable. Therefore, simply demonstrating that a given parameter combination can reduce hole-shape deformation or crack propagation remains insufficient to fully explain its practical application value in in-ovo vaccination. A more critical question is whether the optimized perforation parameters can reduce the barrier damage and visible contamination risk under simulated in-ovo manipulation conditions while maintaining favorable embryo viability, hatching performance, and chick quality. Although genetic algorithms, particle swarm optimization, and backpropagation neural networks have been widely used in engineering structural optimization and egg tray design, these intelligent optimization techniques have not yet been integrated with biological validation of embryo egg perforation. This study attempts to combine physical analysis, data-driven optimization, and biological validation to establish a parameter optimization approach that is interpretable, systematically optimizable, and relevant to poultry production applications.

Based on the above background, this study aims to address the following scientific questions: (1) Under low-velocity rigid punch impact, does eggshell perforation and subsequent crack propagation on embryo eggs follow a predictable bending-fracture coupled pathway ? (2) What are the effects and relative importance of the punch diameter, impact velocity, and impact force on perforation quality (quantified by the deviation index and crack length) ? (3) Is it possible to obtain a robust and reproducible optimal process window by integrating physics-based prior knowledge with intelligent optimization algorithms ? (4) To validate the biological and production-related significance of the optimized parameters by assessing the hatch-of-viable, visible contamination rate, methylene blue penetration, and shell membrane damage.

To address these issues, this study was conducted to: (1) propose and establish a Pogorelov-Griffith bending-fracture coupled model for thin-shell materials, formulate a testable mechanical hypothesis, and provide theoretical support through combined experimental-modelling cross-verification; (2) quantify the effects of the punch diameter, impact velocity, and impact force on perforation quality using single-factor and orthogonal experiments, thereby validating the predictive capability of the proposed model; (3) introduce a genetic algorithm-optimized back-propagation neural network (GA-BP) optimization framework to achieve global optimization and accurate prediction of parameters based on experimental data, ultimately yielding a reproducible and high-precision process window.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

Fertilized embryo eggs of Jiujinhong chickens from the same commercial hatchery and the same batch were used as the experimental material. The breeders were approximately 52 weeks old and all hatching eggs were set for incubation on April 30. The incubation temperature was set at 37.8°C with 58% relative humidity, and the automatic egg-turning interval was 120 min. All experimental eggs were obtained from healthy breeder flocks, and the feeding and incubation management conditions were consistent. To minimize the effects of differences in flock, batch, and rearing conditions on the experimental results, only embryo eggs from the same chicken breed, and from the same source and batch with intact appearance were included in the experiment. To minimize the influence of geometric differences on the results, eggs were screened out according to the Egg Shape Index (ESI), defined as (Chiang et al., 2021):(1)ESI=L2L1×100%where L1 represents the egg’s long axis (mm) and L2 represents the short axis (mm). The Egg Shape Index (ESI) is used to characterize the external morphology of embryo eggs (Chiang et al., 2021). An ESI value closer to 100% indicates a more spherical egg, whereas lower values correspond to more elongated shapes. In general, eggs with an ESI between 72% and 76% exhibit a balanced morphology with an appropriate length-to-width ratio and are therefore considered ideal for experimental use (Duman et al., 2016).

Before the experiment, all embryo eggs were candled to confirm embryo viability, and infertile eggs, eggs with dead embryos, eggs with shell damage, malformed eggs, eggs with abnormal air cells, and eggs with obvious shell contamination were excluded. Before treatment, the embryo eggs were placed with the blunt end facing upward, and the air cell region was identified by candling to ensure that the perforation site was located near the air cell at the blunt end. For each embryo egg, the weight, long axis, short axis, egg shape index, and eggshell thickness at the blunt end were measured and recorded for assessment of sample uniformity and subsequent statistical analysis.

In the mechanical perforation parameter optimization experiment, each treatment condition included at least 10 embryo eggs, and mechanical damage indicators, including DI, CL, and S, were recorded. To further validate the biological and production-related significance of the optimized perforation parameters, subsequent biological validation experiments were conducted using 3 independent incubation batches. In the main hatching validation cohort, each batch included 25 viable embryo eggs per group, with a total of 450 viable embryo eggs, which were used to evaluate the hatch-of-viable, embryonic mortality, visible contamination rate, and chick body weight. An additional 90 independent viable embryo eggs were used for methylene blue penetration and shell membrane damage assessments. Experimental device

The experimental platform consisted primarily of four modules: an impact loading module, a fixation and support module, a high-speed imaging system, and a synchronized force acquisition module. The overall configuration is shown in Fig. 2. The impact loading module employed a vertical linear guide rail to ensure stability of the punch during free-fall motion. Punches were machined from 316L stainless steel with a flat-ended cylindrical tip. Punch diameter (2-6 mm) and mass (20-100 g) could be varied by replacing the punch assembly as required. The impact velocity was precisely controlled by adjusting the drop height using scale markings on the guide rail and an electromagnetic release mechanism.Fig. 2Embryo egg impact test system. (a) Schematic diagram. (b) Real product.Fig 2 dummy alt text

Embryo egg impact test system. (a) Schematic diagram. (b) Real product.

Embryo egg samples were placed in the fixation and support module in which a soft-padded platform cradled the egg with the blunt end facing upward. To ensure positional stability throughout the impact, the support device was equipped with an egg tray that effectively secured the sample.

The impact process was recorded using a high-speed imaging system consisting of a Phantom VEO710 camera (Vision Research, USA) capable of a maximum frame rate of 7500 fps, coupled with a high-brightness LED cold light source to provide flicker-free illumination. The camera was positioned to maintain a perpendicular view relative to the impact loading module, enabling capture of the entire process of depression formation, crack initiation, and crack propagation during impact.

A miniature force sensor (range: 0-20 N, accuracy: 0.02 N) was mounted beneath the support platform to measure the peak impact force and load duration. The force acquisition module was synchronized with the high-speed camera via a trigger, ensuring one-to-one correspondence between the force signal and the imaging data. The overall apparatus allowed adjustable impact velocity, replaceable punch parameters, and synchronized mechanical-visual data acquisition, thereby providing reliable test data for subsequent analysis of eggshell mechanical behavior under impact.

The solid flat-ended punch was selected to isolate the mechanical eggshell-perforation process from the effects of the needle lumen structure, needle tip angle, and liquid delivery. Thus, the device was designed to quantify local eggshell deformation and crack propagation under controlled impact conditions. The punch geometry does not fully replicate a commercial hollow needle; therefore, the results should be interpreted as controlled mechanical perforation data and require further validation using commercial in-ovo injection systems.

Theoretical model of eggshell perforation under impact

To investigate the perforation and crack formation processes on eggshells under low-velocity slender-rod impact, a coupled “localized depression bending-crack propagation” framework was adopted in this study. Drawing on the approximate solution of Pogorelov’s theory, the shell near the impact point forms 3 distinct zones: a depressed zone, a ridge zone, and an undeformed zone, as shown in Fig. 3d(RegionⅠ,Ⅱand,Ⅲ) (Li et al., 2021; Liu and Li, 2022). The dynamic evolution of this process was captured through high-speed imaging: (Fig. 3a) in the contact stage, the punch began to contact the eggshell surface, thereby inducing initial deformation; (Fig. 3b) in the depression formation stage, a localized depression (depressed zone, region I) appeared near the impact point, accompanied by surrounding ridge-like folds (ridge zone, region II), while the outer region remained undeformed (region III); (Fig. 3c) in the crack formation stage, stress accumulation in the ridge zone led to the appearance of initial cracks. Evaluation metrics

To comprehensively assess the perforation quality of embryo eggs, two metrics were introduced: the Deviation Index (DI) and Crack Length (CL). On this basis, a comprehensive scoring function was constructed to quantitatively reflect the perforation performance under different combinations of process parameters.(1) Deviation Index (DI)

To comprehensively characterize the local mechanical damage caused by hole-shape deformation and crack propagation during perforation, a mechanical damage score S was constructed in this study. S consisted of normalized DI and CL, where DI reflects the degree to which the hole morphology deviates from an ideal circle, and CL reflects the extent of crack propagation. These two indicators represent two aspects of perforation-induced damage, namely morphological deviation and crack damage; therefore, they were assigned equal weights of “a1=a2=0.5” in this study:(10)Si=0.5Yi,DInorm+0.5Yi,CLnormwhere Yi,DInorm and Yi,CLnorm are the normalized values of the deviation index and crack length, respectively, in the i-th trial. A smaller mechanical damage score S indicates less severe hole-shape deformation and crack propagation, and therefore a lower level of local mechanical damage caused by perforation. It should be noted that S is not a direct biological endpoint, but rather a mechanical evaluation indicator used to screen out low-damage perforation parameters. Its biological interpretive value should be assessed together with subsequent results for the hatch-of-viable, visible contamination rate, methylene blue penetration, and shell membrane damage.

Experimental design

To systematically investigate the effects of the punch diameter D, impact velocity V, and impact force F on embryo egg perforation quality and to identify the optimal parameter combination under multi-factor conditions, a three-stage experimental design consisting of single-factor experiments, orthogonal experiments, and validation experiments was adopted (Montgomery, 2020).

Single-factor experiments

Single-factor experiments were first conducted to analyze the independent effect trends of each factor on the Deviation Index DI, Crack Length CL, and mechanical damage score S. During testing, only one factor was varied at a time while the others were held constant, with graded levels selected within the predetermined range. By measuring DI and CL and calculating the mechanical damage score S, the direction and magnitude of each factor’s effect on perforation quality were intuitively revealed. The results of the single-factor experiments not only facilitated interpretation of mechanistic trends (Section 3.1) but also provided the basis for level selection in the subsequent orthogonal experiments.

Orthogonal experiments

Building on the single-factor experiments, orthogonal experimental design was further employed to examine the combined effects of multiple factors (Montgomery, 2020). 3 factors, D, V, and F, were selected, with 3 levels assigned to each factor based on the single-factor results (Table 6, Section 3.2). The experimental scheme followed an L9(33) orthogonal array, comprising 9 experimental runs (Montgomery, 2020). Each run was replicated 3 times, and the average values were used to minimize random error.

To enhance the power of statistical tests, 3 replicate points were positioned at the center of the design space (D=3.0 mm, V=2.0 m/s, F=1.5 N) to estimate pure error and to perform the lack-of-fit (LoF) test. Simultaneously, 2-3 supplemental points were incorporated in key factor-interaction regions (e.g.,D×V) to facilitate the identification of potential interaction trends. All experimental runs were conducted in randomized order, with egg batch/collection date included as a blocking factor in the subsequent statistical model. In the data analysis stage, range analysis was first applied to determine the relative importance of the factors, followed by analysis of variance (ANOVA) in combination with effect size (partial η2), power analysis, and residual diagnostics to comprehensively assess factor significance and model robustness (Section 3.2) (Montgomery, 2020).

Validation experiments

To verify the reliability of the orthogonal experimental conclusions, 2-3 additional validation experiments were conducted after obtaining the optimal parameter combination (Montgomery, 2020). By comparing the DI, CL, and S values from the validation experiments with those predicted by the model, the repeatability and robustness of the optimal combination were evaluated. The results of the validation experiments not only served to confirm the statistical conclusions of the orthogonal design (Section 3.3) but also functioned as an independent test set for the GA-BP optimization framework to assess model generalization performance.

Optimization framework

On the basis of the experimental data, a genetic algorithm-optimized back-propagation neural network (GA-BP) model was further developed to predict and globally optimize embryo egg perforation quality. The core idea of this framework is to employ the BP neural network to establish a nonlinear mapping relationship between process parameters and the mechanical damage score, and then use the genetic algorithm to optimize the network weights and thresholds, thereby overcoming the susceptibility of the BP model to local optima and attaining higher prediction accuracy and generalization capability (Chen et al., 2020; Xu and Zhao, 2022).

The input layer of the BP neural network comprised 3 nodes corresponding to the punch diameter D, impact velocity V, and impact force F; the output layer consisted of a single node, namely the mechanical damage score S. The number of hidden-layer nodes was determined based on empirical formulas and cross-validation to balance fitting capability and computational efficiency. In the data processing stage, the experimentally measured DI and CL were first converted to the mechanical damage score S and then normalized to ensure that all input variables participated in training on the same scale.

To avoid the BP network falling into local minima during training, the genetic algorithm was introduced in this study to optimize its initial weights and thresholds (Chen et al., 2020; Xu and Zhao, 2022). The genetic algorithm achieves the global parameter search through the iterative process of “encoding-selection-crossover-mutation”, thereby improving network convergence speed and reducing training error. During optimization, the mean square error (MSE) between the network predicted values and experimental measured values was used as the fitness function until convergence conditions were met or the preset number of generations was reached.

Ultimately, the GA-BP model could establish a nonlinear mapping relationship between D, V, F, and S within the given parameter space and obtain the optimal parameter combination through optimization. This framework not only improves the accuracy of perforation quality prediction for embryo eggs but also provides an effective optimization approach for multi-factor combinations that are difficult to exhaustively enumerate under experimental conditions, laying a methodological foundation for process optimization and large-scale application.

Biological validation experiments

To validate the biological and production-related effects of different perforation parameter combinations under simulated in-ovo manipulating conditions, 6 validation groups were established based on mechanical parameter screening and model prediction. These groups represented the intact hatching baseline, center-point/empirical parameters, candidate optimal parameters from the orthogonal experiment, model-predicted parameters, high mechanical damage parameters, and same-diameter low-velocity parameters. The specific grouping was as follows: the intact control group underwent no perforation, injection, or hole sealing; all other groups were perforated in the air cell region at the blunt end of the embryo eggs, followed by injection of 50 μL of sterile 0.9% physiological saline and hole sealing with sterile paraffin.

Sterile 0.9% physiological saline was used as a sham injection fluid to simulate the physical process of in-ovo fluid delivery while avoiding potential confounding effects caused by vaccine composition, adjuvants, or vaccine-specific embryonic responses. Sterile paraffin was used as a standardized laboratory sealing material to provide consistent closure of the perforation site across treatment groups. These procedures were not intended to represent commercial in-ovo vaccination practices, but were used to isolate the effects of eggshell perforation parameters on barrier integrity and hatching outcomes under controlled experimental conditions.

G1 was the intact control group, with no perforation, injection, or hole sealing; G2 was the center-point parameter group, using D = 3.0 mm, V = 2.0 m/s, and F = 1.5 N; G3 was the orthogonal candidate optimal parameter group, using D = 2.0 mm, V = 2.0 m/s, and F = 1.5 N; G4 was the model-predicted parameter group, using D = 2.0 mm, V = 2.25 m/s, and F = 1.6 N; G5 was the high mechanical damage parameter group, using D = 4.0 mm, V = 2.6 m/s, and F = 1.5 N; and G6 was the same-diameter low-velocity parameter group, using D = 2.0 mm, V = 1.6 m/s, and F = 1.5 N.

The main hatching validation experiment was conducted using 3 independent manipulation batches. For all 3 manipulation batches, embryo viability was determined by candling at Day 18 of incubation, immediately before treatment. Within each batch, eligible viable embryo eggs were randomly assigned to 6 treatment groups, with 25 eggs per group, totaling 450 viable embryo eggs used to evaluate the hatch-of-viable, visible contamination rate, late embryonic mortality, and chick body weight. Additional independent viable embryo eggs were used for methylene blue penetration and shell membrane damage assessments, with 5 eggs per group in each batch, totaling 90 eggs. Outcome assessments for all groups were performed by observers who were blinded to treatment group assignment.

Sham injection and sealing procedure

Except for the intact control group, all other treatment groups underwent perforation and sham injection in the air cell region at the blunt end of the embryo eggs. Before treatment, the air cell position was confirmed by candling, and the central region of the air cell at the blunt end was marked. After the eggshell surface was disinfected with 75% ethanol and allowed to air-dry, perforation was performed using a self-developed impact perforation device according to the D, V, and F settings assigned to each treatment group. Immediately after perforation, images of the perforated region were captured, and visible cracks and perforation success were recorded. Subsequently, 50 μL of sterile 0.9% saline was slowly injected into each embryo egg through the perforation site to simulate the in-ovo manipulation process. After injection, the hole opening was sealed with sterile paraffin, and the eggs were transferred to hatching baskets and returned to the hatcher. The eggs were maintained at 37.8°C and 65% relative humidity until hatching. All eggs were placed with the blunt end upward, and egg turning was discontinued after the transfer. No further turning was applied during the hatching period. During the hatching period, the number of late embryonic mortality, hatch-of-viable, and the number of eggs with visible contamination were recorded. hatch-of-viable was calculated using the number of viable embryo eggs at the time of treatment as the denominator, and chick body weight was recorded within 6-12 h after hatching.

Visible contamination assessment

Since microbial cultivation and strain identification were not performed in this study, contamination was evaluated based on visible contamination on eggs. A positive visible contamination case was defined as the presence of any of the following signs during incubation or shell-opening examination: mold spots on the eggshell surface or around the perforation site, fluid leakage from the perforation site or cracks, an obvious putrid odor, cloudy or viscous contents, abnormal discoloration of the contents, spoilage-like changes in embryonic tissues, and eggs at risk of cracking or bursting. The visible contamination rate was calculated as follows: visible contamination rate = (number of embryo eggs positive for visible contamination / number of viable embryo eggs at the time of treatment) × 100%. This indicator reflects visually identifiable contamination or spoilage-risk on eggs during incubation and is not equivalent to cultivation-confirmed infection by specific microorganisms.

Dye penetration and shell membrane damage assessment

To evaluate the integrity of the eggshell-membrane barrier after perforation, a methylene blue dye penetration test was performed in this study. An additional 15 independent viable embryo eggs from each group were used for this assay, and these samples were not included in the hatch-of-viable analysis. After the embryo eggs in each group were treated according to the corresponding perforation parameters and sham injection procedure, they were incubated for an additional 48 h. The eggs were then removed from the incubator, and the paraffin around the hole opening was removed without artificially enlarging the hole or cracks. A 50-μL volume of 1.0% methylene blue solution was applied dropwise to the perforated region, and after 10 min of exposure, the outer surface was gently rinsed 3 times with distilled water.

The eggshell was then opened using laser cutting, and staining of the inner eggshell surface and shell membrane was observed. Images were captured under fixed illumination, distance, and magnification. ImageJ software was used to measure the maximum dye penetration length and dye penetration area. Local microscopic images were also captured for each group to observe shell membrane tearing, rupture, and dye penetration along cracks.

Shell membrane damage was assessed using a 0-3-point semiquantitative scoring system: a score of 0 indicated no visible dye penetration and no obvious shell membrane damage; a score of 1 indicated mild localized staining or slight indentation at the hole edge, with largely intact shell membrane continuity; a score of 2 indicated obvious dye penetration along cracks or visible local shell membrane rupture/tearing; and a score of 3 indicated extensive staining, severe shell membrane disruption, exposure of the contents, or penetrating damage. Scoring was performed independently by two observers who were blinded to treatment group assignment, and inter-observer agreement was calculated. Statistical analysis methods

All experimental data were analyzed using the mechanical damage score S as the primary optimization objective, with lower values indicating superior perforation quality. The Deviation Index (DI) and Crack Length (CL) served as secondary evaluation metrics. Statistical analysis followed the sequence below:(1)Range analysis: the relative importance of the factors was initially ranked;(2)Analysis of variance (ANOVA): a fixed-effects model was used to test the significance of the factors and their interactions, with partial η2 calculated to evaluate effect size (Montgomery, 2020);(3)Power analysis: post-hoc power was calculated for significant factors to confirm the robustness of the conclusions (Montgomery, 2020);(4)Lack-of-fit (LoF) test: pure error was estimated from center-point replicates and compared with the residual error of the quadratic regression model to verify the adequacy of the quadratic fit (Montgomery, 2020);(5)Interaction analysis: potential interaction effects were visually examined using pairwise interaction plots (D × V, D × F, V × F), with further validation from supplemental points.;(6)Two one-sided tests (TOST) for equivalence: to confirm consistency between the orthogonal experiment predictions and validation experiment results, equivalence was assessed using TOST with an equivalence margin of Δ=0.05 and a significance level of α=0.05. Equivalence was concluded if the 90 % confidence interval of the mean difference between the two groups fell entirely within [−0.05, +0.05] (Lakens, 2017; Lakens et al., 2018).

GA-BP optimization and prediction model

Model construction and training

To further improve the precision and generalization ability of parameter optimization, a GA-BP neural network model was developed based on the results of orthogonal and validation experiments. The model inputs were the punch diameter D, impact velocity V, and impact force F along with their quadratic terms and interaction terms, totaling 9 feature variables; the output was the mechanical damage score S. The neural network adopted a three-layer structure: 9 nodes in the input layer, 12 nodes in the hidden layer, and 1 node in the output layer. The activation function was tansig, and the loss function was mean square error (MSE).

The traditional BP neural network is prone to falling into local optima under small-sample conditions. To avoid this issue, a genetic algorithm (GA) was introduced in this study to optimize the initial weights and thresholds (Chen et al., 2020; Qin et al., 2022; Huo et al., 2025). The GA parameters were set as follows: population size of 50, crossover probability of 0.8, mutation probability of 0.1, and maximum number of iterations of 120. Through GA optimization, the model could obtain a better parameter combination in the early stage of training, thereby improving convergence speed and prediction accuracy.

The dataset was divided into a training set (70%), a validation set (15%), and a test set (15%). In addition, the validation experiment results from Section 3.3 were used as an independent test set to examine the model's external generalization performance.

Model performance evaluation

To evaluate the predictive capability of the GA-BP model for the mechanical damage score S of embryo egg perforation, a comparison plot of predicted values versus actual values was drawn (Fig. 10). It can be seen that, for both the training set and test set, the data points were closely distributed around the ideal line y=x, indicating high fitting accuracy. The overall data yielded a coefficient of determination R2≈0.91 and root mean square error (RMSE) ≈ 0.02.This result showed that the GA-BP model not only accurately fitted the training samples but also maintained strong generalization performance on the independent test set, with predicted results highly consistent with experimental values.

Furthermore, learning curves (Fig. 11) were used to compare the error convergence of GA-BP and traditional BP models during the iteration process. The results showed that the GA-BP model fell significantly below the BP model after approximately 15 iterations and tended toward convergence after approximately 90 iterations, with the final training error stabilizing at 0.0030 compared to 0.0079 for the BP model. In comparison, GA-BP exhibited faster convergence speed, lower error, and a smoother convergence process, demonstrating the advantage of the genetic algorithm in optimizing initial weights and thresholds. In summary, the GA-BP model exhibits high accuracy, high robustness, and excellent convergence characteristics when predicting the mechanical damage score of embryo egg perforation, providing a reliable modeling tool for subsequent parameter optimization and mechanistic analysis.

Model comparison

Discussion

In this study, eggshell perforation parameters before in-ovo manipulation were systematically evaluated by integrating a bending-fracture mechanical model, orthogonal experiments, GA-BP optimization, and biological validation. The mechanical results showed that the punch diameter was the primary factor affecting perforation quality, followed by impact velocity, whereas impact force had a relatively limited effect within the range examined in this study. A larger punch diameter increased the local contact area and the extent of damage around the hole edge, thereby aggravating hole-shape deformation and crack propagation. Impact velocity exhibited an appropriate window, with excessively low velocities potentially leading to unstable perforation and excessively high velocities potentially increasing the risk of crack propagation. Therefore, optimization of eggshell perforation parameters should be understood as coordination among the punch diameter, impact velocity, and impact force, rather than as a simple reduction in any single parameter.

The biological validation results further indicate that differences in mechanical damage have practical biological significance. The GA-BP optimal parameter group (D = 2.0 mm, V = 2.25 m/s, F = 1.6 N) had the lowest S among the perforation groups, accompanied by a lower degree of methylene blue penetration and less severe shell membrane damage. Hatch-of-viable in this group was numerically close to that of the intact control group, and the visible contamination rate also remained at a low level. Consistent with the significant differences shown in Table 13, the high-damage group, which had the highest S, showed significantly lower hatch-of-viable and higher late embryonic mortality and visible contamination rate than the optimized low-damage groups, together with the most evident dye penetration and the highest shell membrane damage score. Although the small-diameter low-velocity group used a 2.0-mm punch, its DI, CL, S, dye penetration, and visible contamination rate were all higher than those of the GA-BP optimal group, indicating that a smaller punch diameter must still be matched with an appropriate impact velocity to achieve a lower-damage perforation effect.

For in-ovo vaccination, the perforation step must not only create a hole suitable for injection but also preserve the integrity of the eggshell and shell membrane as much as possible. Tiny cracks or deformation around the hole edge caused by perforation may affect the reliability of subsequent hole sealing and increase the risk of fluid leakage or entry of exogenous contaminants. In this study, the methylene blue penetration test provided bridging evidence linking mechanical damage to biological outcomes. Dye penetration did not represent actual microbial infection, but it could be used to evaluate whether the eggshell-membrane barrier in the perforation region was damaged. Treatment groups with lower S generally showed less severe dye penetration and shell membrane damage, whereas the high-damage group with higher S showed a higher visible contamination rate and lower hatch-of-viable. These findings indicate that the low-damage parameter window has potential value for production applications.

It should also be noted that the solid flat-ended cylindrical punch used in this study was not intended to fully replicate the geometry and fluid-delivery function of a commercial hollow injection needle. Instead, it was used as a mechanically equivalent model of the shell-perforation step during in-ovo manipulation. By excluding the effects of the needle lumen structure, needle tip angle, and liquid delivery, this experimental design allowed the independent effects of punch diameter, impact velocity, and impact force on local eggshell deformation, crack propagation, and shell membrane barrier disruption to be quantified. Therefore, the optimized parameter window identified in this study should be interpreted as a low-damage shell-perforation window that provides mechanical guidance for the shell-perforation stage of in-ovo equipment optimization, rather than as final operating parameters for all commercial hollow-needle or dual-needle injection systems.

In this study, S was defined as the mechanical damage score and was used to integrate 2 mechanical damage indicators, DI and CL. DI and CL reflect hole-shape deformation and crack propagation, respectively, and the use of either indicator alone fails to comprehensively evaluate the damage status of the perforation region. The biological validation results showed that a lower S was generally associated with a lower degree of methylene blue penetration, a lower shell membrane damage score, a lower visible contamination rate, and better hatching performance. Therefore, S can serve as a mechanical evaluation indicator for screening low-damage perforation parameters. It should be noted that S is still not a direct biological endpoint and cannot replace production-related indicators such as the hatch-of-viable, late embryonic mortality, visible contamination rate, and chick body weight.

This study still has several limitations. First, sterile 0.9% physiological saline was used for sham injection to minimize the effects of vaccine components themselves on embryonic development and hatching outcomes, thereby focusing on the mechanical damage and barrier damage caused by the perforation parameters. Therefore, the current results mainly reflect the safety and feasibility of the perforation process itself and cannot fully represent its application performance under actual vaccine formulation conditions. Second, microbial cultivation or strain identification was not performed in this study; therefore, the visible contamination rate can only be interpreted as a visually observable contamination or spoilage, rather than cultivation-confirmed microbial infection. Third, this study used embryo eggs obtained from approximately 52-week-old Jiujinhong breeder hens and differences in the chicken breed, breeder age, eggshell thickness, and shell membrane characteristics may affect perforation damage and hatching outcomes. Finally, this study was conducted using a self-developed impact perforation device under controlled experimental conditions. The recommended parameters, D = 2.0 mm, V = 2.25 m/s, and F = 1.6 N, should be regarded as a candidate low-damage perforation parameter window and require further validation through commercial in-ovo vaccination equipment, actual vaccine injection, and commercial hatchery conditions.

Conclusion

In this study, we independently designed an eggshell perforation parameter optimization device for embryo eggs and systematically optimized eggshell perforation parameters before in-ovo manipulation by integrating the Pogorelov-Griffith bending-fracture mechanical model, orthogonal experiments, a GA-BP intelligent optimization framework, and biological validation experiments. The mechanical model established based on local shell deformation and crack energy criteria was able to explain the overall pattern observed during perforation, namely that the process was “diameter-dominated, with an appropriate velocity window and marginal regulation by impact force,” and this pattern was consistent with the results of the single-factor and orthogonal experiments.

The results of the single-factor and orthogonal experiments showed that the effects of the punch diameter, impact velocity, and impact force on perforation quality followed the order of “D > V > F”. An increase in the punch diameter significantly aggravated hole-shape deformation and crack propagation. Impact velocity yielded better perforation performance within the range of approximately 2.2-2.3 m/s. Results of impact force tended to stabilize near 1.5-1.6 N, and further increases had limited effects on improving perforation quality. The GA-BP model showed good predictive ability for the mechanical damage score S, with overall predictive performance reaching R² ≈ 0.91 and RMSE ≈ 0.02, and produced consistent parameter convergence results under different optimization strategies.

Based on the combined results of mechanical optimization, model prediction, and biological validation, the recommended perforation parameters were D = 2.0 mm, V = 2.25 m/s, and F = 1.6 N. Under this parameter combination, DI, CL, and S all remained at low levels, and hole-shape deformation and crack propagation were effectively suppressed. Further biological validation showed that this parameter group exhibited a lower degree of methylene blue penetration and less shell membrane damage, a lower visible contamination rate, and hatch-of-viable close to that of the intact control group. This study provides mechanical and biological evidence for optimizing eggshell perforation parameters in in-ovo vaccination.
