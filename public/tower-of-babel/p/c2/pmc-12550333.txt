Proteomic profiling links complement activation to thrombosis in JAK2V617F myeloproliferative neoplasms
Authors: Blanca De Moner, Julia Martinez-Sanchez, Silvia Escribano-Serrat, Alex Ramos, Ana Belén Moreno-Castaño, Eduardo Arellano-Rodrigo, Ginés Escolar, Enric Carreras, Alberto Álvarez-Larrán, Maribel Diaz-Ricart
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) are a group of hematologic malignancies characterized by the clonal expansion of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, resulting in excessive production of mature blood cells [1]. The three main classical Philadelphia chromosome-negative MPNs comprise polycythemia vera, essential thrombocythemia, and primary myelofibrosis [2]. A defining molecular feature of MPNs is the presence of the JAK2V617F mutation found in approximately 95 % of PV cases and 50–60 % of ET and PMF cases [3,4], resulting in the constitutive activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, which promotes aberrant cell proliferation and leads to abnormal hematopoiesis [5,6]. Additionally, proteomic studies have identified mTOR signaling as a key pathway in MPN pathogenesis, linking chronic inflammation and cytokine dysregulation to disease progression [7].

The thrombophilic nature of MPN patients results in severe complications, such as splanchnic vein thrombosis (SVT), which is primarily driven by the JAK2V617F mutation, present in 40–60 % of unexplained SVT cases [8]. This mutation induces chronic inflammation and cytokine dysregulation [[9], [10], [11]], promoting hematopoiesis and the activation of platelets, leukocytes, endothelial cells, and the coagulation system, thereby fostering a hypercoagulable environment [[12], [13], [14], [15]].

Beyond the well-characterized JAK-STAT pathway, the complement system has emerged as a critical player in thrombo-inflammatory diseases [16,17]. This innate immune cascade can be activated via multiple pathways (classical, lectin, alternative), all culminating in the generation of potent anaphylotoxins (C3a, C5a) and the terminal membrane attack complex (C5b-9). These products not only mediate pathogen clearance but also interact with endothelial cells, platelets, and leukocytes to amplify inflammation and thrombosis. Importantly, complement activation exhibits extensive cross-talk with the coagulation cascade, including direct effects on tissue factor expression, thrombin generation, and platelet activation [18,19]. In the context of MPN, where chronic inflammation and endothelial stress are already present, dysregulated complement activity may serve as a key driver of vascular events such as SVT [20,21].

Despite advances, significant gaps remain in the understanding of the precise mechanisms underlying thrombosis in MPNs, particularly in patients with SVT. We conducted a comprehensive proteomic analysis of plasma samples from MPN patients with and without SVT to address these gaps. By identifying alterations in the complement cascade and related pathways, we aimed to elucidate a possible role of complement activation in the pathogenesis of thrombosis in MPNs. Additionally, our study wanted to identify potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets to mitigate thrombotic complications in this high-risk population.

Material and methods

Patients and sample collection

Twenty-eight patients with JAK2V617F-mutated MPN and six age- and gender-matched healthy donors (controls) were included. Plasma and serum were collected by centrifuging citrated and non-anticoagulated blood, respectively. All participants provided informed consent, and the study was approved by the Hospital Clinic Ethical Committee (HCB/2018/1155).

Protein extraction and quantification

The most abundant proteins (e.g., albumin, IgG, fibrinogen) were depleted using Human-14 MARS spin cartridges (Agilent Technologies) to reduce plasma protein complexity. Twelve microliters per sample were processed, and the resulting low- and high-abundance protein flow-through fractions were collected [22]. These were concentrated and buffer-exchanged to ∼100 µL of 6 M urea in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate using 5 K MWCO spin filters (Agilent 5185–5991).

Protein digestion and TMT 11-Plex labeling

Protein (30 µg) was reduced with 4 mM DTT (1 h, 37 °C), alkylated with 8 mM IAA (30 min, dark), and digested overnight at pH 8.0, 37 °C with Trypsin/Lys-C (1:50 enzyme: protein). Digestion was stopped by 1 % formic acid, followed by peptide desalting with Oasis HLB SPE columns. Samples were labeled with TMT 11-plex reagents (ThermoFisher Scientific), using a pooled sample tagged with TMT-126 in each batch for normalization. Labeled samples were desalted before liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis.

NanoLC- (Orbitrap)MS/MS analysis

Labeled peptides were loaded onto a trap nano-column and separated on a µPAC™ Neo C18 column (ThermoFisher) using a 180-min gradient with water and acetonitrile (0.1 % formic acid) at 300 nL/min. MS data were acquired on an Orbitrap Eclipse using enhanced FT-resolution for MS1 (R = 60,000) and data-dependent MS2 (R = 50,000) with HCD (30 % NCE). Dynamic exclusion was set to 0.7 min, and ions with charge states 2–6 were selected.

Protein identification and quantification

Proteome Discoverer v2.5 with Mascot v2.8 was used for protein identification against the Homo sapiens and contaminant databases. Trypsin specificity was assumed; mass tolerances were 10 ppm for precursor and 20 mmu for fragment ions. Carbamidomethylation was set as a static modification; methionine oxidation and N-terminal acetylation were dynamic [23]. TMT-6plex was used for quantification. FDR was calculated by Percolator, with a peptide ID cutoff of 1 %. Protein quantification was based on unique and razor peptides using the reporter ion quantifier.

Validation of the proteomic results

To evaluate the activation of the complement, serum complement components were quantified using the MicroVue™ Complement Multiplex ELISA kit (QuidelOrtho) based on chemiluminescence, following the manufacturer's protocol. Samples were appropriately diluted and analyzed using a multiplex detection system and the provided analysis software.

Statistical analysis

Proteins identified in >50 % of samples were included. Missing values were imputed using the KNN algorithm in MetaboAnalyst 5.0. Data were log10-transformed and auto-scaled. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) were used to assess global differences [24]. For univariate analysis, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (FDR < 5 %). ANOVA with Fisher's post-hoc test was applied when appropriate. Hierarchical clustering was performed based on significant proteins, which were also used for pathway enrichment using STRING v11.0 and KEGG. For the results obtained from validation experiments, a statistical analysis was carried out with raw data using parametric or non-parametric tests as needed: one-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparisons and Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn's multiple comparisons, respectively. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS statistical software (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA), and results were considered statistically significant when *p< 0.05.

Role of funding source

The funding received was allocated to sample collection, analysis, and data interpretation.

2. Proteomic Landscape of MPN: Dysregulation of Complement and Coagulation Pathways

Using an unsupervised PCA, we discovered a clear separation between the proteomic profiles of C and MPN patients (Fig. 1a and Fig. 1b). While controls conformed to a tightly grouped cluster, MPN samples were broadly distributed, reflecting their heterogeneous proteomic landscape. This pattern was further refined in supervised PLS-DA, where the first two components achieved clear stratification of the two groups (Fig. 1c). The Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) analysis (Fig. 1d) further accentuated specific proteins that strongly contributed to this differentiation. The top-ranked proteins included calreticulin (CALR), glycoprotein Ib alpha polypeptide (GP1BA), vascular cell adhesion protein 1 (VCAM1), intercellular adhesion molecule 2 (ICAM2), and complement component C2, all of which were significantly upregulated in MPN_ALL. Conversely, selenoprotein P (SELENOP), beta-Ala-His dipeptidase (CNDP1), and vitamin K-dependent protein S (PROS1) were downregulated. A total of 57 proteins were significantly different between MPN and C after FDR correction (q-value < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 1).

As shown in Table 2, pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant involvement of multiple biological pathways. The complement and coagulation cascade was the most prominently dysregulated pathway in MPN patients (FDR < 0.001). Several complement components (C1QB, C1QC, C4BPA, C7, C2) were upregulated, whereas key coagulation factors, including F9, F2, F5, and PROS1, were significantly downregulated. The results highlight significant proteomic alterations in MPN patients, particularly immune response, coagulation, and cellular adhesion.

a. Enhanced Coagulation and Endothelial Dysfunction in SVT vs Controls

As shown in Fig. 2c, PLS-DA displayed a robust separation between C and SVT patients. The VIP analysis (Fig. 2d) identified several key proteins driving this distinction, including PROS1, CALR, and VCAM1. Among these, PROS1, a key anticoagulant protein, was significantly reduced in SVT_ALL compared to controls (Mean ± SD: −0.372 ± 0.754 vs 1.364 ± 0.414; q < 0.0015), reflecting a prothrombotic state. The univariate analysis revealed significant differential expression of 75 proteins between the plasma samples of patients with SVT and healthy controls (Supplementary Table 2).

Pathway enrichment analysis highlighted significant alterations in several biological pathways (Table 3), with complement and coagulation cascades being the most significantly dysregulated (FDR < 0.001). Upregulated components included C1QA, C1QB, C1QC, C7, and C2, while downregulated factors included F2, F9, F10, PROS1, F5, C4BPA, C4BPB, PLG, SERPIND1, and PROC. Additional pathways related to immune and inflammatory responses were also enriched. For instance, the leukocyte trans-endothelial migration pathway (FDR = 0.001) was enriched with proteins like ICAM1, VCAM1, RAP1B, and VCL, indicating potential alterations in cell adhesion and vascular inflammation. The cell adhesion pathway (FDR = 0.006) includes ICAM1, ICAM2, VCAM1, and PVR. This was further supported by changes in the platelet activation pathway (FDR = 0.007), where proteins such as RAP1B and GP1BA were upregulated in SVT, while F2 was downregulated. The cholesterol metabolism pathway (FDR = 0.04) was also identified, with Apolipoprotein A-II (APOA2), Apolipoprotein A-IV (APOA4), and Apolipoprotein C-III (APOC3) being downregulated in MPNs. Finally, the regulation of the actin cytoskeleton pathway (FDR = 0.01) revealed significant changes in actin-associated proteins like VCL, TMSB4X, and CFL1, which were upregulated in SVT. In contrast, other proteins involved in actin regulation, such as F2 and FN1, were downregulated.

c. Effects of Therapy

To evaluate the potential confounding effects of anticoagulant therapy, we compared proteomic profiles between patients receiving vitamin K antagonists (VKAs, n = 11), low molecular weight heparin (LWMH, n = 6), and no anticoagulation (NO, n = 12). While unsupervised hierarchical clustering revealed broad trends in protein expression patterns across treatment groups (Supplemental Fig. 2a), formal ANOVA testing (Supplementary Table 4) identified only six proteins with statistically significant differences after FDR correction (q < 0.05): SH3BGRL3, THBS1, CST3, PROZ, LBP, and PFN1. The clustering analysis showed that LWMH-treated patients tended to cluster together based on elevated acute-phase reactants (CRP, LBP, SAA). In contrast, VKA-treated patients showed relative reductions in platelet-derived proteins (PF4, THBS1). Notably, the core thrombosis-associated signature, including complement components (C7, C1QB) and endothelial markers (VCAM1), maintained consistent elevation across all treatment groups. Among vitamin K-dependent proteins, only PROZ showed significantly lower levels in VKA-treated patients versus both LWMH and NO groups. Classical coagulation factors (F2, F9, F10) did not differ significantly between treatment groups, though F5 showed a non-significant trend toward reduction in VKA patients (p = 0.067). The persistence of coagulation factor dysregulation across treatment modalities, particularly for non-vitamin K-dependent factors like F11 (consistently reduced in SVT patients, q = 0.017 vs controls) and PLG (lower in SVT, q = 0.027), suggests these changes reflect intrinsic disease biology rather than treatment artifacts.

To evaluate the potential modulation of thrombosis-associated pathways by cytoreductive therapies, we compared proteomic profiles of SVT patients receiving ruxolitinib, hydroxyurea, or pegylated interferon alpha-2a (SVT-T) with those of untreated patients (SVT-NT). Multivariate analysis revealed distinct proteomic profiles between treated and untreated patients, as demonstrated by PLS-DA (Supplementary Fig. 2b). VIP analysis identified key contributors to this separation (Supplementary Fig 2c), including upregulated proteins in treated patients such as FSD2 (linked to endothelial repair), CLU (associated with apoptosis and inflammation regulation), and COLEC10 (involved in vascular homeostasis). Conversely, downregulated proteins in treated patients (PCYOX1, CALR, ITGA7, PFN1, and THBS1). Additional PROZ, APOA2, and PROCR reductions pointed to altered coagulation balance, lipid metabolism, and vascular integrity. However, univariate analysis did not identify any statistically significant proteins distinguishing SVT-T from SVT-NT. When further stratified by treatment type, no individual proteins reached statistical significance, indicating that any single therapy did not drive proteomic trends.

Discussion

A comprehensive proteomic analysis of plasma samples from JAK2V617F-mutated MPN patients with and without SVT has been conducted to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying thrombotic complications in this high-risk population. The findings of the present study reveal significant dysregulation of the complement and coagulation cascades, as well as alterations in platelet, immune, and endothelial cell activation pathways. These results provide new insights into the pathogenesis of thrombosis in MPNs, uncovering novel pathways and potential therapeutic targets that could be explored in future studies.

Activation of the JAK-STAT pathway in MPN patients has been related to overproduction of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP [25]. These cytokines exacerbate endothelial dysfunction and platelet activation, establishing a prothrombotic environment. Our present results substantiate that this inflammatory state may potentiate complement activation, thus linking JAK-STAT signaling to enhanced thrombo-inflammatory responses in SVT patients. In our study, CALR was significantly upregulated in JAK2V617F MPN patients, especially in those with SVT. This upregulation was consistent across the entire MPN cohort, regardless of therapeutic intervention, indicating that it may be an intrinsic feature of JAK2V617F disease biology rather than a treatment effect. We propose that the chronic inflammatory state and cellular stress induced by the mutation are key drivers of this CALR overexpression [26]. Furthermore, it is well-established that JAK2V617F induces replication stress and genomic instability [27]. Thus, the DNA damage potentially caused by the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway could be a contributing mechanism to cellular stress leading to CALR dysregulation [28]. However, this was not directly measured in our study. Also, our results align with the observed overexpression of CALR in JAK2V617F granulocytes compared with JAK2 wild type, independently of the JAK2V617F allele burden [29]. Future prospective studies in larger and independent cohorts are needed to validate the value of CALR as a soluble biomarker to stratify MPN patients at higher risk of SVT.

We also observed significant dysregulation of coagulation pathways in MPN patients, particularly those with SVT, characterized by downregulation of key anticoagulant proteins (PROS1, SERPINA10) and impaired fibrinolysis (reduced PLG). These findings align with the known hypercoagulable state in MPNs and are supported by previous reports linking natural anticoagulant deficiency to thrombotic risk [[30], [31], [32]]. Notably, we observed downregulation of multiple coagulation factors (F9, F10, F11) in SVT patients, which may reflect either compensatory mechanisms in chronic thrombosis or secondary hepatic involvement. Anticoagulation therapy analysis provided critical context for these findings. While VKAs did significantly affect some vitamin K-dependent proteins such as PROZ, the classic coagulation factors (F2, F9, F10) showed no significant differences between VKAs, LMWH, and untreated patients. Most compellingly, the downregulation of non-vitamin K dependent factors (F5, F11, F13A1) and fibrinolysis markers (PLG) persisted across all treatment groups, strongly suggesting these alterations reflect intrinsic disease biology rather than therapeutic effects.

Our findings indirectly support a role of endothelial dysfunction in SVT, which is a hallmark of MPN [33,34]. VCAM1 and ICAM1 were significantly upregulated in MPN patients, particularly in those with SVT. These results are consistent with the growing evidence that endothelial dysfunction is a key feature of MPNs, potentially related to the JAK2V617F-driven cytokine release and chronic inflammation [11,33,35], which could explain the thrombo-inflammatory phenotype.

Platelet activation is a critical contributor to MPN-associated thrombosis [15,36,37]. In our study, we observed increased GP1BA levels in SVT patients, suggesting enhanced platelet activation. However, we also observed decreased levels of THBS1 and PF4 in SVT patients compared to those without thrombosis, indicating potential dysregulation in platelet release mechanisms. Moreover, VWF, a critical mediator of platelet adhesion and aggregation, was markedly upregulated in SVT patients compared to non-thrombotic patients, further supporting the role of platelet and endothelial activation in MPN-related thrombosis [34].

Our findings also point toward significant immune dysregulation in MPN patients, particularly in those with SVT. The upregulation of LYZ, FCGR3A, and CD5L in SVT patients points to an enhanced immune response, which may contribute to the chronic inflammation observed in these patients [11,38]. The upregulation of CD163 found in patients with SVT in comparison to patients without thrombosis and control individuals may indicate a shift toward activated macrophage responses, likely linked to chronic inflammation and endothelial |damage typical of MPN. This observation suggests possible activation of anti-inflammatory or tissue-repair mechanisms [[39], [40], [41]].

The present results also highlight a significant upregulation of complement pathway components, particularly in SVT patients. Compared to controls, MPN patients exhibited increased C1QA, C1QB, C1QC, C7, and C2, indicating enhanced activation of both the classical and terminal complement pathways [19,42]. These findings were further corroborated by serum analyses, showing marked elevation of alternative pathway components (Ba, Bb) and effector molecules (C3a, C4a) in all MPN patients. The terminal pathway activation products (C5a, sC5b-9) were particularly elevated in SVT patients. These results suggest that complement activation is central to the thrombotic complications associated with MPNs [16]. The complement system has increasingly been recognized as a key player in thrombotic processes in chronic inflammatory conditions [43]. The upregulation of these proteins, combined with impaired regulatory responses, evidenced by Factor I and H downregulation in SVT patients, indicates that complement activation enhances thrombo-inflammation in MPN patients through multiple mechanisms: classical pathway initiation, alternative pathway amplification, and terminal pathway effector functions. This cascade contributes to endothelial cell injury and platelet activation, both critical components of the thrombotic process [44,45]. The marked elevation of sC5b-9 specifically implicates membrane attack complex formation as a potential final common pathway linking complement activation to thrombotic events in SVT. Although some serum markers did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited sample size, the consistent trends across patient groups reinforce the biological relevance of complement dysregulation in MPN-related thrombosis.

The hierarchical clustering and heatmap analysis revealed significant heterogeneity in the proteomic profiles reflecting the diverse clinical presentations and thrombotic risk profiles observed in SVT patients. The heterogeneity observed in our proteomic profiles aligns with previous findings highlighting protein expression variability across MPN subtypes and the JAK2 mutation status [29]. The observed heterogeneity emphasizes the need for personalized risk stratification and treatment approaches in SVT patients. Recent studies have stressed the potential of biomarker-driven therapeutic strategies to address the heterogeneity in MPN patients. Ruxolitinib has shown efficacy in reducing inflammation and thrombotic risk in MPN patients; however, its effectiveness may vary depending on the patient's proteomic profile [46,47]. The effect of cytoreductive therapy on the proteomic profile of SVT patients was also evaluated. However, the lack of statistically significant proteins in the univariate analysis between SVT-T and SVT-NT suggests that the proteomic effects of therapy may be subtle and require further investigation in larger cohorts. The identification of complement activation or dysregulation, particularly of the terminal pathway (elevated C5a, sC5b-9), unveils a possible novel therapeutic target for MPN-associated thrombosis. Complement inhibitors, such as anti-C5 monoclonal antibodies (eculizumab) or C5a receptor antagonists (avacopan), are already used for the treatment of other diseases (serious pregnancy complications, glomerular diseases, complications of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and ABO incompatible kidney transplant, as well as immune-mediated neuronal pathologies). Therefore, this group of compounds could be an alternative to mitigate the thrombotic complications in high-risk MPN patients, especially those with SVT. Furthermore, some of the markers identified (VCAM1, CALR, C5a, sC5b-9) could be validated in prospective studies to develop a biomarker-based risk stratification model. Future clinical trials are warranted to explore the efficacy of complement inhibition in this setting.

While our study provides valuable insights into the proteomic landscape of MPN-associated thrombosis, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size, particularly in the non-thrombotic MPN group comprising 6 patients, may limit the generalizability of our findings. It should therefore be considered a hypothesis-generating study and needs to be further investigated in larger and independent cohorts. Additionally, the lack of in vitro functional assays to confirm the mechanistic role of complement activation represents a key constraint, as it prevents definitive conclusions about causality. Future studies incorporating longitudinal sampling and additional mechanistic experiments would help strengthen the biological and clinical relevance of these findings.

In conclusion, our proteomic analysis supports that thrombotic events in MPN patients result from a complex interplay between immune activation, vascular dysfunction, and coagulation imbalance. The significant upregulation of complement pathway components, particularly in SVT patients, underlines the critical role of complement activation in driving thrombo-inflammation and thrombotic complications in MPNs. These findings enhance our understanding of MPN-related thrombosis and provide potential targets for risk stratification and therapeutic intervention. Further research is needed to explore how therapeutic modulation of these pathways may reduce thrombotic risk in MPN patients.
