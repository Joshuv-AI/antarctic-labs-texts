Disparities in Lung Cancer Screening: Demographic and Socioeconomic Influences
Authors: Elie Mulhem, Elkhansa Sidahmed, Yuying Xing, Hany Eraqi, Holly MacIntyre, Ramin Homayouni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•This study is a retrospective analysis of 3 years of lung cancer screening (LCS) data from 8 geographically distinct locations in Metro Detroit.•It compared LCS rates and associated factors among U.S. Preventive Services Task Force–eligible patients who were screened versus not screened.•Higher screening rates were found in patients aged 60–80 years, females, former smokers, and Asians.•Lower screening rates exist among Middle Eastern/Arab, Black individuals, Medicaid recipients, and those in deprived ZIP codes.•LCS rates remain low, with disparities by race, ethnicity, and SES. Targeted, equitable strategies are needed to improve uptake.Keywords: Lung cancer screening, disparities, demographic and socioeconomic factors

INTRODUCTION

Lung cancer (LC) continues to be the leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the U.S., accounting for nearly 20% of all cancer deaths.1 The American Cancer Society estimates that approximately 234,580 new cases of LC were diagnosed in the U.S. in 2024, with over 125,000 deaths attributed to the disease.1 A key factor contributing to these high mortality rates is the fact that LC is often diagnosed at an advanced stage when survival rates are significantly lower.2 American Cancer Society estimates that only about one quarter of LCs are diagnosed at a localized stage, with 5-year survival rates at 63%, whereas more than 40% of cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage, with an overall 5-year survival rate of 8%.1

Early detection through screening can greatly reduce LC mortality, particularly among high-risk individuals, such as current and former smokers. LC screening (LCS) using low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) has proven to be an effective tool in identifying early-stage LCs, leading to better prognoses and more treatment options.1,3

The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) has recognized the importance of early detection and, in March 2021, updated its 2013 recommendations to expand the eligibility criteria for LCS.4 The updated recommendations lowered the minimum age for screening from 55 to 50 years and reduced the smoking history requirement from 30 pack-years to 20 pack-years, thereby including a larger proportion of high-risk individuals.4 However, despite these efforts to broaden access to screening, LCS rates remain suboptimal nationwide.5 It is estimated that only 18.1% of eligible individuals in the U.S. were screened in 2022.6,7

Factors contributing to low screening rates may include socioeconomic barriers, lack of awareness, limited access to health care, and disparities in screening rates among different ethnic groups.1,6,7 Michigan’s LCS rates were reported at 20.2% in 2022.8 More recent data from the American Lung Association reported Michigan’s LCS rate of 18.2% in 2024, ranking the state 15 of 51 in LCS and 20 of 47 states in 5 years LC survival.9,10

The objectives of this study are to assess LCS uptake and to identify factors associated with LCS uptake among eligible patients under the updated USPSTF recommendations, using electronic medical records (EHR) data from a large multi-hospital healthcare system in Southeast Michigan. By identifying the demographic and socioeconomic factors that are associated with screening behavior, this study will inform future research to evaluate structural and behavioral barriers for screening utilization and help design interventions to improve LCS.

METHODS

Study Population

This retrospective study was conducted at Corewell Health East (CHE), comprising eight geographically distinct locations in Metro Detroit, between April 1, 2021, after the publication of the new USPSTF recommendations, and December 31, 2023. The study included eligible adult patients who met the USPSTF criteria for annual LCS and completed baseline LDCT imaging during the study period at one of the eight CHE hospitals. The study included patients aged 50 to 80 years with no prior history of LC and a documented smoking history in the electronic health records (EHRs). Patients who had smoking information documented in the EHR during the study period were included in the study. The study was approved by the Corewell Health IRB (Study Number 2023-201).

Measures

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from the EHR data warehouse. Data elements included age, sex (male and female), primary health insurance (commercial, Medicaid, or Medicare), race (Asian, Black or African American, and White or Caucasian), ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino and Middle Eastern or Arab), and ZIP code of residence. Because 9-digit ZIP codes were not available for a large number of individuals, an average Area Deprivation Index (ADI) for the 5-digit ZIP codes was used for the study. The average ADI was represented in quintiles, with 1 being the least disadvantaged and 5 being the most disadvantaged.11

The authors compared LCS rates and factors associated with LCS among eligible patients who were screened with those of patients who were not. Screened patients were defined as individuals who met the USPSTF recommendations and received at least 1 LDCT scan during the study period. If a patient underwent multiple LDCTs during this period, only the first LDCT was considered for the primary analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to summarize LCS-eligible patient characteristics. Continuous variables were presented as means and SDs, whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The t-test was used to compare continuous variables between screened and nonscreened groups, and the chi-square test was used for categorical variables.

Multivariable logistic regression was performed to explore the association between patient characteristics and LCS status, adjusting for covariates, including age group, sex, race and ethnicity, payer class, ADI, and smoking status. To reduce the number of categories for the analysis, ADI national ranking deciles were collapsed into quintiles. Covariates were selected on the basis of previously reported factors influencing LCS.6,7,12 The results were reported as AORs with 95% CIs and p-values. The regression model did not include interaction terms, such as interaction between race and ADI and payer class and ADI. All tests of statistical significance were 2 sided, with a significance level of p<0 .05. The analysis was conducted using R, Version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

RESULTS

A total of 829,238 patients aged 50–80 years who were free of LC between April 1, 2021 and December 31, 2023 were filtered on the basis of the inclusion criteria. Among these individuals, 575,104 (69.4%) had their smoking status documented in the EHR, of which 313,904 (54.6%) were never smokers; 180,273 (31.3%) were former smokers; and 80,927 (14%) were current smokers (Figure 1). Among current and former smokers, the number of patients with at least 20 pack-year smoking history was higher for current smokers (23,776, 29.4%) than for former smokers (18,993, 10.5%). Of 42,769 patients who met the USPSTF 2021 LCS eligibility criteria, only 9,249 (21.6%) underwent at least 1 LDCT test during the study period (Figure 1).

Flow diagram of the study population.

Eligible are patients who were aged 50–80 years, had a 20 pack-year smoking history, were currently smokers or had quit within the past 15 years, had never been diagnosed with lung cancer, and were cancer free.

The proportion of individuals who were eligible for LCS remained consistent throughout the study period (Figure 2). However, the LCS rate increased from <10% in 2021 and reached 33% by the end of 2023. The annual screening rates were 4.1%, 7.6%, and 26.4% for 2021, 2022, and 2023, respectively (Appendix Figure 1, available online). LCS rates were notably higher among former smokers (22.3%) than among current smokers (21.1%) (Figures 1 and 2).

Lung cancer screening rates and trends.

Among patients who were eligible for LCS, there were significant differences in characteristics between those who did and those who did not have LDCT (Table 1). Patients aged 60–69 years had the highest rates of LDCT screening: 24.5% compared with 17.4% and 21.5% for patients in age groups 50–59 and 70–80 years, respectively. The proportion of females (23.1%) who were screened was significantly (p<0.001) higher than that of males (20.3%). With respect to race/ethnicity, Asian patients had the highest screening rates (34.5%), followed by White or Caucasian (22.4%), Middle Eastern or Arab (17.5%), Black or African American (19.1%), and Hispanic or Latino patients (8.4%). In addition, Medicaid beneficiaries had a lower screening rate of 18.1% than Medicare beneficiaries (22.7%) and commercial insurance holders (22.4%). Finally, among eligible individuals living in ZIP codes categorized by state-level ADI quintile rankings from ADI 1 (least disadvantaged) to ADI 5 (most disadvantaged), the proportions were 24.6% for ADI 1, 24.7% for ADI 2, 22.1% for ADI 3, 20.8% for ADI 4, and 19.9% for ADI 5. Individuals in the most disadvantaged areas (ADI 5) had the lowest screening rates.

DISCUSSION

This retrospective study, conducted within a large healthcare system in Southeast Michigan, examined LCS rates and the factors associated with screening among patients who met the updated 2021 USPSTF eligibility criteria. This is the first study to report LCS rates in Michigan from 2021 to 2023 using EHR data. Previous reports encompassed 2022 data from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), which relies on self-reported data rather than clinical data from the EHR. In addition, Michigan was not included in the BRFSS 2023 LCS assessment, highlighting the importance of the updated and EHR-based findings.9

Moreover, to the authors’ knowledge, this is the first comprehensive analysis of LCS to include Middle Eastern or Arab individuals in addition to other racial and ethnic groups. This study revealed several significant findings, which have important implications for efforts to improve LCS and reduce disparities in screening. The authors found that the overall LCS rate over the 3 years of the study period among eligible patients who met USPSTF eligibility criteria was low, with only 21.6% of these patients undergoing at least 1 LDCT scan or only the first scan if patients had multiple scans during the 33-month study period.

This observation is consistent with that of another study using data from the BRFSS, which reported that 20.2% of eligible patients were up to date with LCS in Michigan.9 Together, these studies highlight the ongoing challenge of achieving high rates of LCS among eligible individuals in Michigan. The authors also observed that screening rates improved from <10% in 2021 to 33% by the end of 2023. This positive trend is likely due to multiple factors, mainly the implementation of an LCS best practice alert in the Corewell Health East EHR in 2022. The alert was triggered for all patients aged 50–80 years who were current or former smokers. In addition, primary care physicians were educated on ordering LDCT for qualified patients in response to the EHR alerts. Other factors may include gradual increase in screening by primary care physicians, inclusion of LCS as a preventive service under the Affordable Care Act, and increased patient awareness of the screening recommendations.13,14

One of the most important findings of this study is the existence of disparities in LCS rates based on demographic factors such as age, sex, race/ethnicity, and SES. Multivariable analysis showed that age played a significant role in LCS adherence, with patients aged 50–59 years being the least likely to undergo screening than older age groups. Similar to previous studies using national cohorts, the authors found that the highest LCS rate was in the age group of 60–69 years, likely owing to enrollment in Medicare during this time period.6 Consistent with this, the authors found that Medicare beneficiaries had the highest odds of LCS than Medicaid beneficiaries and commercial health insurance holders. Taken together, these results are consistent with those of other studies and represent a missed opportunity for increasing the impact of LCS because younger patients are more likely than older patients to be diagnosed with late-stage LC.9,15, 16, 17 Although the USPSTF recommendations were changed to include younger smokers (aged 50–54 years), there is a critical need for additional interventions to increase LCS in this age group, including educational campaigns targeting patients and physicians to increase awareness of the benefits of LCS in younger smokers.

Sex was another important factor influencing screening rates. More men in this population were eligible for LCS, reflecting the higher rate of smoking among men than among women in Michigan. However, regression analysis showed that female patients have significantly higher odds of undergoing LCS than male patients. This aligns with previous research that shows that men are less likely to engage in preventive health behaviors.18 The results suggest that more effort should focus on engaging male patients in LCS, particularly those at an elevated risk of LC.

Significant racial and ethnic disparities were also apparent, with Asian and White or Caucasian patients showing higher screening rates than Black or African American, Middle Eastern or Arab, and Hispanic or Latino patients. These findings are particularly concerning because these minority groups probably face additional barriers to screening, such as limited access to health care, lower health literacy, and mistrust in the healthcare system.19,20 The expansion of the updated USPSTF recommendations has increased the number of Black individuals who are eligible for LCS.21 This is important because Black individuals can develop LC at a younger age and with fewer pack-years of smoking than White individuals.22,23 However, this study showed that screening rates are lower among Black individuals than among White individuals. In addition, 61.9% of Black patients who had LCS were Medicare beneficiaries, compared with 55.4% of White patients, reflecting the fact that more Black patients had LCS later in life than White patients. This is consistent with other studies showing that nearly 80% of Black adults in the U.S. who were screened were in the Medicare age group.17

The lower screening rates among Middle Eastern or Arab patients are of particular interest because the Detroit Metro area has the second highest density of Middle Eastern or Arab population in the U.S. Importantly, this is the first study to report LCS rates in this ethnic group compared with those in other minority groups. Unless directly assessed, Middle Eastern or Arab individuals often identify as White non-Hispanic Latino, yet they are a unique community with specific health-related disparities. For example, the wide use of hookah to smoke tobacco by Middle Eastern or Arab individuals complicates eligibility for LCS.24 Although hookah smoking can increase the risk for LC, there are currently no USPSTF screening criteria that includes hookah smoking or its equivalency to pack years.25 This study highlights that USPSTF recommendations may need to be adjusted to account for the increasing use of hookah in the U.S. among younger individuals from all races and ethnicities.

Socioeconomic factors, as measured by the ADI, were significantly associated with LCS in this study. Patients living in the most socioeconomically disadvantaged neighborhoods (average ADI Quintile 5) were less likely to undergo screening than those in more affluent areas. This finding is especially concerning because LC prevalence and mortality are highest among the most disadvantaged populations, on the basis of reports that people in lower socioeconomic classes tend to smoke longer and heavier than people in the higher socioeconomic classes.26, 27, 28 People from disadvantaged neighborhoods face challenges such as limited transportation, lack of insurance, lower accessibility to primary care physicians, language barriers, and competing priorities that make it difficult to prioritize cancer screening.29 In addition, higher levels of environmental pollution in disadvantaged neighborhoods can exacerbate health challenges, including increased LC risk.29, 30, 31 The lower LCS screening rates in this population, even after adjusting for other factors, point to the need to address access barriers to LCS in people living in disadvantaged neighborhoods. These barriers may be addressed through education and active outreach through collaboration with community organizations and local health systems.

Limitations

This study is limited by its retrospective nature and reliance on existing clinical data in the EHR, which may have been subject to documentation inaccuracies. In addition, the inclusion criteria relied on USPSTF eligibility recommendations, which may result in an underestimation of the screening rates in this population. In addition, the study was conducted within a single healthcare system in southeast Michigan, limiting the generalizability of the findings to other regions with different population demographics and healthcare infrastructure.

CONCLUSIONS

This study shows some improvements in screening rates after the updated 2021 USPSTF recommendations, although significant disparities in LCS based on age, sex, race/ethnicity, SES, and insurance coverage remain. It also raises a concern that expanding the eligibility criteria by the USPSTF did not achieve its goal of increasing the rate of LCS in younger populations. In addition, the authors report, for the first time, LCS disparities in Middle Eastern or Arab populations compared with those in the White/Caucasian population.

One of the key strengths of this study is its large sample size, which allows for a comprehensive analysis of LCS across various demographic and socioeconomic groups. In addition, the inclusion of Middle Eastern or Arab individuals as a distinct ethnic category provides valuable insights into a population that is often overlooked in health research.

Addressing disparities in LCS will require targeted interventions that consider the unique barriers faced by different demographic groups, particularly minority populations and those living in disadvantaged neighborhoods. In addition, the study highlights the importance of increasing public awareness of the benefits of LCS as well as efforts to improve access to care and reduce socioeconomic barriers to screening that are essential to reducing LC mortality and achieving equity in LC outcomes.
