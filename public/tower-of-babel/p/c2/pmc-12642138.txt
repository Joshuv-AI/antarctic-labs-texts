Impact of prediabetes on postoperative outcomes following short-segment transforaminal lumbar interbody fusions
Authors: Syed I Khalid, Ryan Wang, Jakov Tiefenbach, Tatiana Abou-Mrad, Elie Massaad, Aladine Elsamadicy, Ankit I Mehta, John H Shin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a well-established risk factor for adverse surgical outcomes, but the impact of prediabetes on spine surgery remains unexplored. This study evaluated the influence of prediabetes on postoperative complications in patients undergoing short-segment (≤3 levels) transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF).MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed patients undergoing 1-3 level TLIF in a national administrative database. Patients were categorized into 3 groups: no diabetes, prediabetes, and T2DM. Patients with type 1 diabetes were excluded. A 1:1:1 exact match was performed to balance demographics, comorbidities, and the number of spinal levels fused. Thirty-day postoperative medical and surgical complications were analyzed.ResultsA total of 17,796 patients (5,932 per group) were included. Patients with T2DM demonstrated a significantly higher incidence of urinary tract infection (OR, 1.49; 97.5% CI, 1.19–1.86), pneumonia (OR, 1.59; 97.5% CI, 1.10–2.31), acute kidney injury (OR, 2.32; 97.5% CI, 1.70–3.18), surgical site infection (OR, 1.34; 97.5% CI, 1.04–1.74), overall medical complications (OR, 1.50; 97.5% CI, 1.28–1.75), and surgical complications (OR, 1.29; 97.5% CI, 1.05–1.58). In contrast, patients with prediabetes did not demonstrate a statistically significant increase in postoperative complications.ConclusionsT2DM is associated with a higher risk of medical and surgical complications following short-segment TLIF. However, prediabetes does not appear to affect postoperative complication rates. These findings suggest enhanced perioperative management is warranted for patients with T2DM, while standard protocols may be sufficient for patients with prediabetes.Keywords: Prediabetes, Transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion, TLIF, Spine surgery, Postoperative outcomes, Surgical complications, Diabetes mellitus

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistently elevated blood glucose levels due to insulin resistance [1,2]. Globally, it affects approximately 415 million people and accounts for 90% of all patients with diabetes, with projections estimating 700 million cases by 2045 [2,3]. Although T2DM is widely recognized as a significant risk factor for adverse surgical outcomes [4], [5], [6], an equally pressing concern is the large population at risk for developing overt diabetes. This at-risk group includes individuals in a prediabetic state, defined by glycemic levels above normal thresholds yet not meeting diagnostic criteria for diabetes [7,8].

Prediabetes has been linked to metabolic abnormalities such as dyslipidemia, hypertension, and insulin resistance [9], and evidence suggests that microvascular and macrovascular complications seen in T2DM may also manifest in prediabetic individuals. This has important surgical implications, as even mild hyperglycemia can impair wound healing [10,11], promote a pro-inflammatory milieu, and increase susceptibility to bacterial infections [12]. Indeed, studies in cardiac surgery have shown that prediabetic patients experience elevated risks of mortality, infection, and prolonged hospital stays when compared to normoglycemic individuals—albeit at lower rates than those with diabetes [13,14]. A linear relationship between hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and adverse perioperative events, including major complications and length of stay, further underscores the potential relevance of prediabetes to surgical outcomes [15].

In contrast, the effects of prediabetes on spine surgery outcomes, particularly in short-segment transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF), remain inadequately characterized. T2DM has been well-documented to increase perioperative morbidity in spinal procedures, with higher rates of wound complications, infection, and systemic sequelae partly driven by impaired tissue perfusion and inflammatory dysregulation [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]. However, whether prediabetes similarly constitutes a significant risk factor in spine surgery has not been rigorously investigated.

Accordingly, the objective of this study was to examine the influence of prediabetes on postoperative complications following short-segment TLIF. By clarifying whether this metabolic state confers a measurable risk of adverse events—comparable to that of T2DM or closer to normoglycemic patients—these findings may enhance perioperative risk stratification and inform clinical decision-making for individuals with borderline glycemic profiles.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the MARINER database marketed by PearlDiver Inc. (Denver, CO). The database contains de-identified administrative claims data from multiple US healthcare institutions. This study was reviewed and approved by the appropriate institutional review board, with a waiver of informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the analysis, and STROBE guidelines were followed.

Patient population

All patients who underwent 1-, 2-, or 3-level TLIF procedures between January 1, 2018, and April 30, 2022, were initially identified using relevant procedural codes. We excluded patients aged ≤17 or ≥75 years; those with type 1 diabetes mellitus; those with lumbar or compression fractures, spinal metastases, spinal abscesses, or osteodiscitis; those with a history of prior spine surgery; and those lacking 1-year postoperative follow-up data. After applying these exclusions, 142,648 patients met the initial inclusion criteria.

Exposure classification

Patients were stratified into 3 groups based on clinical and billing codes indicating glycemic status at the time of surgery: (1) no diabetes, (2) prediabetes, and (3) T2DM. The database’s diagnostic fields (ICD-10) were used to identify these conditions—specific ICD-10 codes defined prediabetes without a formal diagnosis of type 2 diabetes (Supplementary Table 1).

Matching protocol

To mitigate confounding, we performed exact matching in a 1:1:1 ratio for the 3 groups (no diabetes, prediabetes, and T2DM). Covariates in the match protocol included age, sex, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, obesity, smoking status, depression, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), osteoporosis, and the number of fused levels (1, 2, or 3). The unmatched demographics and comorbidities are summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the occurrence of any postoperative complication within 30 days of surgery. Medical complications included urinary tract infection (UTI), pneumonia, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism (PE), cardiac arrest, acute kidney injury (AKI), and composite medical complications. Surgical complications included wound dehiscence, hematoma, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak, surgical site infection (SSI), and composite surgical complications. The secondary outcome was all-cause additional lumbar surgery at 24 months after index TLIF. ICD-10 and CPT codes were used to identify complications, and details of these codes are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including means (± standard deviations) and frequencies (percentages), were used to characterize demographics, comorbidities, and surgical characteristics. We compared baseline variables among the 3 cohorts using standardized mean differences after matching. We then performed logistic regression to estimate the associations between glycemic status (prediabetes or T2DM) and the primary outcome, using the no-diabetes group as the reference. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R, version 4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Cohort characteristics

After exact matching, 17,796 patients were included in the analysis, with 5,932 patients in each of the 3 groups (no diabetes, prediabetes, and T2DM) (Fig. 1). The demographic and clinical characteristics of the cohorts are presented in Table 1. The median age was over 50 years across all groups, with a similar age distribution. Female patients comprised 59.51% of each group. Hypertension was the most prevalent comorbidity, affecting 51.37% of patients in all groups. Other commonly observed comorbidities included obesity (21.61%), smoking (21.56%), and depression (19.81%). The rates of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (7.11%), osteoporosis (3.19%), and chronic kidney disease (2.75%) were lower but consistent across the groups.

Surgical characteristics were evenly distributed among the groups. Single-level TLIF was performed in 73.3% of patients, 2-level procedures in 11.4%, and 3-level procedures in 15.31%, with no significant differences in the distribution of surgical levels between groups.

Postoperative complications

In the T2DM group, the incidence of UTI was 3.32% compared with 2.26% in the no-diabetes group (p < 0.001, Table 2). Pneumonia occurred in 1.23% of T2DM patients compared with 0.78% of patients in the no-diabetes group (p = 0.046). The incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI) was 2.21% in the T2DM group compared with 0.96% in the no-diabetes group (p < 0.001, Table 2). Logistic regression models demonstrated that T2DM was associated with significantly higher odds of UTI (odds ratio [OR], 1.49; 97.5% CI, 1.19–1.86), pneumonia (OR, 1.59; 97.5% CI, 1.10–2.31), and AKI (OR, 2.32; 97.5% CI, 1.70–3.18) compared with the no-diabetes group. The odds of experiencing at least 1 medical complication were also significantly higher in the T2DM group (OR, 1.50; 97.5% CI, 1.28–1.75; p < 0.001, Table 3).

Surgical complications were more common in the T2DM group. The surgical site infection (SSI) rate was 2.36% in the T2DM group compared with 1.77% in the no-diabetes group (p = 0.015, Table 2). The overall rate of surgical complications was 3.56% in the T2DM group compared with 2.78% in the no-diabetes group (p = 0.007). Logistic regression models indicated higher odds of SSI (OR, 1.34; 97.5% CI, 1.04–1.74) and any surgical complication (OR, 1.29; 97.5% CI, 1.05–1.58, Table 3) in the T2DM group compared with the no-diabetes group.

In contrast, the prediabetes group did not exhibit a statistically significant increase in medical or surgical complications relative to the no-diabetes group. The incidence of UTI in the prediabetes group was 1.90% compared with 2.26% in the no-diabetes group (p > 0.05), with no significant difference in odds (OR, 0.84; 97.5% CI, 0.65–1.08). Similarly, the odds of experiencing any medical complication (OR, 0.92; 97.5% CI, 0.78–1.10) or any surgical complication (OR, 0.95; 97.5% CI, 0.76–1.19) were not elevated in the prediabetes group. The incidence of all-cause additional lumbar surgery within 24 months was similar across the 3 cohorts (3.83% in no-diabetes, 4.42% in prediabetes, and 4.28% in T2DM; p = 0.199, Table 3).

Discussion

The rising prevalence of prediabetes, alongside the established risks of T2DM in surgical patients, prompted our investigation into perioperative outcomes among different glycemic statuses in short-segment TLIF. Our results confirm that T2DM is associated with a significantly higher risk of medical and surgical complications while prediabetes did not confer an elevated risk. These findings underscore the importance of enhanced perioperative management in T2DM patients, while standard care protocols may be adequate for those with prediabetes.

Our study reinforces T2DM as a notable risk factor for postoperative UTI, pneumonia, AKI, and SSI [21,22]. The increased rates of UTI, SSI, and pneumonia are likely driven by chronic hyperglycemia-associated immune dysfunction and enhanced pathogen virulence [21,22]. In the context of AKI, T2DM can cause chronic kidney damage via the accumulation of glycosylated products in the basement membrane [23], rendering patients more vulnerable to renal insults during and after surgery [24]. The overall rise in surgical complications was primarily driven by SSI and a nonsignificantly higher incidence of wound complications, which is consistent with the known challenges in wound healing among T2DM patients [10,11,25].

Although 1 might expect prediabetes to be associated with modest increases in surgical risk, our study found no significant difference compared with nondiabetic patients. This contrasts with findings in other surgical specialties like cardiac surgery, where prediabetes correlates with higher infection rates, more extended hospital stays, and reduced survival [13,14]. A possible explanation is that short-segment lumbar fusion, being relatively less invasive or involving different anatomical structures, may reduce the systemic impact of mild hyperglycemia, thereby attenuating its contribution to postoperative complications. This is consistent with prior spine literature showing that less invasive spine surgery in diabetic patients does not carry higher complication rates compared with nondiabetic patients, further supporting the absence of excess risk in prediabetes [26].

Tissue-specific and biological factors may also play a role to explain why prediabetes may not translate to increased complications in short-segment TLIF. Spinal tissues may exhibit a different immune response than other surgical sites, possibly resulting in a more robust local defense even in mild hyperglycemia. This is particularly evident following spinal cord injury (SCI), where activation of immune cells plays an important role in determining outcomes. The immune response in spinal cord tissue can either exacerbate damage by releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines that promote oxidative stress or facilitate tissue repair by clearing debris and promoting angiogenesis [27,28]. Beyond injury-specific responses, microglia in the spinal cord have also been found to exhibit higher immune reactivity compared to microglia in the brain, suggesting intrinsic regional differences in immune behavior unrelated to injury [29]. Additionally, variations in wound healing dynamics—potentially influenced by the relatively lower invasiveness of short-segment TLIF and less extensive soft tissue disruption—might mitigate the impact of prediabetic metabolic disturbances.

An important consideration is how the severity of hyperglycemia, often measured by markers like HbA1c, correlates with complication rates. Although our study differentiates between T2DM and prediabetes, the degree of glycemic control within these groups could influence outcomes [30]. It is plausible that only beyond a certain threshold of hyperglycemia do risks escalate sharply, a potential explanation for why the prediabetic state did not show significant short-term complications. The Congress of Neurological Surgeons (CNS) guidelines indicate that an HbA1c greater than 7.5% is associated with a higher risk of SSI and complications following spine surgery [31]. This further substantiates the concept of an inflection point, where the body’s compensatory mechanisms are overwhelmed, resulting in significant metabolic dysregulation. Investigating whether tighter glycemic control within the T2DM cohort might reduce complication rates or if a similar threshold exists within prediabetic ranges could further clarify the relationship between glycemic variability and surgical outcomes and inform tailored perioperative strategies.

Limitations

Despite the study’s considerable strengths—such as a large sample size, use of a national database, and a robust matching methodology to control for key baseline characteristics—several limitations warrant discussion. First, the retrospective design introduces inherent risks of selection bias, miscoding, and missing data. Reliance on administrative coding for the identification of T2DM, prediabetes, and complications can result in classification errors, potentially affecting the accuracy of our findings. Additionally, coding-based diagnoses may not always reflect contemporaneous metabolic status, as entries can persist from earlier encounters and may not indicate whether the patient was actively prediabetic at the time of surgery. Without access to laboratory measures such as HbA1c or the date of most recent testing, it is difficult to confirm the accuracy or recency of the diagnosis. Second, although we stratified patients based on glycemic status, individual variations in metabolic control, diabetes-related end-organ damage, or perioperative glucose management strategies were not accounted for. These factors may influence surgical risk and outcomes but could not be fully captured in this dataset. Focusing solely on 30-day outcomes limits insights into the longer-term trajectory of complications, including the potential for reoperations or late-onset infections—highly relevant to spinal fusion procedures. An administrative database also cannot fully control variation in institutional protocols (eg, glucose monitoring thresholds, perioperative antibiotic regimens). Finally, the database may be subject to reporting inconsistencies, with incomplete documentation of perioperative events or complications in some cases. Future prospective studies with granular clinical data, direct measures of glycemic control, and extended follow-up periods could address these limitations and further elucidate the complex relationship between glycemic status and surgical outcomes in spine procedures.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that T2DM is a substantial comorbidity in patients undergoing short-segment TLIF, significantly elevating the risk of medical and surgical complications such as SSI, UTI, AKI, and pneumonia. As such, these patients may benefit from more vigilant perioperative management and thorough preoperative evaluations. In contrast, a prediabetic state was not associated with increased early postoperative complications in this setting, suggesting that standard care protocols may be sufficient for this patient population.
