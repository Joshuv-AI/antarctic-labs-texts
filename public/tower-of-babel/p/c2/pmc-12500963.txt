New insights to effective carbon nanofiber features due to defective interphase for prediction of tunneling conductivity in composites
Authors: Yasser Zare, Muhammad Tajammal Munir, Kyong Yop Rhee, Soo-Jin Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMany nanocomposites include a defective interphase influencing the charge transfer through network. However, the impact of defective interphase on the conductivity of composites including carbon nanofiber (CNF), mentioned as PCNFs, has often been overlooked. In this study, the defective interphase is characterized by the parameter Lc, representing the minimum CNF length necessary for effective conduction transfer from the CNF to the matrix. The effective concentration and length of CNFs in PCNFs, along with the percolation threshold and network concentration, are thus defined by Lc. Furthermore, Weber-Kamal model is refined and extended using effective terms and tunneling properties to estimate PCNF conductivity accurately. The influence of all contributing factors on PCNF conductivity is validated, and the experimental conductivity results support the model predictions. A maximum conductivity of 0.11 S/m is reached at Lc = 10 μm and polymer tunnel resistivity (ρ) of 50 Ω m, while the values of Lc > 16 μm result in an insulating sample. Thus, minimizing Lc and polymer tunnel resistivity maximizes nanocomposite conductivity, whereas higher values of Lc do not enhance conductivity. The lowest Lc and highest interphase conductivity are observed in the sample with the highest conductivity, corroborating the impacts of Lc and interphase conductivity on the measured PCNF conductivity.Keywords: Polymer composite; Carbon nanofiber; Defective interphase; Electrical conductivity; Tunneling properties, modelingSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Numerous studies have demonstrated that carbon nanofiber (CNF) enhances both the electrical conductivity and rigidity of polymer composites, attributed to the high conductivity and favorable modulus/strength properties of CNFs1–4. For instance, Bhawal et al.5 observed that CNF increases the tensile strength, modulus, rigidity, and conductivity of poly(ethylene methylacrylate) composites. Their study reports a threshold of percolation by 1.4 wt% in melt-mixed samples, while the composites produced via the solution mixing show a slightly higher threshold of 1.8 wt%, highlighting CNF potential as an additive for optimizing polymer composite properties.

In polymer CNF composites (PCNFs), electrical conductivity is largely influenced by the concentration and dimensions of conductive CNFs6–8. Thin and elongated CNFs provide low percolation thresholds, facilitating network formation in PCNFs even at minimal CNF concentrations9. Additionally, conductivity in PCNFs is governed by both tunneling effects and interphase characteristics9–11. Electron tunneling, which describes electron transport through narrow gaps between neighboring particles, is a critical determinant of nanocomposite conductivity12–16, as depicted in Fig. 1. Although electron tunneling significantly impacts PCNF conductivity, it remains underexplored in existing literature. Consequently, parameters such as polymer tunnel resistivity, tunneling dimension, and contact diameter are essential considerations in accurately modeling of PCNF conductivity17,18.

Schematic of tunneling zone and interphase in PCNF.

The interphase, moreover, can swell the filler network, reducing the percolation threshold in nanocomposites19–24. Figure 1 illustrates the interphase zone, which decreases the spacing between adjacent CNFs, thus enlarging the conductive network. Therefore, incorporating interphase properties is vital for predicting of PCNF conductivity. However, some studies indicated that nanocomposites often feature a deficient interphase due to limited compatibility between nanofiller and matrix25–27. This weak interphase degrades composite properties, as it restricts the operational transfer of nanoparticle benefits to the polymer matrix28,29. For composites with a defective interphase, the parameter Lc denotes the minimum nanofiller length required for competent stress transference from medium to filler30. This concept is also applicable to conductivity, as a strong interphase effectively conveys CNF conductivity to the matrix, whereas a weak interphase cannot. Both Lc and interphase conductivity significantly influence PCNF conductivity, yet previous studies have often neglected these parameters. Actually, the previous articles have considered the perfect interphase network in the PCNFs, while an imperfect interphase is created affecting the conductivity of system.

The present work introduces a novel approach to modeling the PCNF electrical conductivity by the impact of a defective interphase on electron transport. Unlike previous models, which often neglected the role of an imperfect interphase, this work incorporates the minimum CNF length (Lc) necessary for effective conductivity transfer to the polymer matrix, providing a more comprehensive understanding of how interphase properties influence conductivity. Additionally, this paper refines the Weber-Kamal model by integrating effective terms and tunneling properties, enabling precise estimation of conductivity in PCNFs. The model is validated against experimental data across various CNF dimensions, interphase characteristics, and network configurations, revealing that conductivity can be optimized by adjusting interphase thickness, CNF concentration, and contact characteristics. This comprehensive framework offers significant insights for designing conductive samples with controlled electrical features, making it valuable for electronics, energy storage, and advanced materials engineering.

Results and discussion

Examination of factors

The inspiration of each factor on the composite conductivity (hereafter referred to as conductivity) is illustrated and analyzed using Eq. 22. Both 3-D and 2-D plots show the effects of two terms on conductivity, holding other variables constant. The average ranges of the factors are as follows: t = 20 nm, ϕf = 0.02, R = 60 nm, l = 50 μm, u = 1.2, ψ = 200 S/m, ρ = 200 Ω m, m = 100, d = 20 nm, λ = 5 nm, and σf = 104 S/m9.

Figure 2 illustrates the effects of Lc and ρ on conductivity. The maximum result of 0.11 S/m is achieved at Lc = 10 μm and ρ = 50 Ω m, while values of Lc > 16 μm result in no conductivity. These findings indicate that minimal values of Lc and polymer tunnel resistivity are essential for maximizing conductivity, while higher values of Lc do not enhance it. The data suggest that both a shorter Lc and lower polymer tunnel resistivity are required to optimize conductivity, thus Lc and polymer tunnel resistivity inversely affect the charge transfer.

Dependence of conductivity on Lc and polymer tunnel resistivity as shown in (a) three and (b) two dimensions (generated by Mathematical software).

A lower Lc indicates a stronger interphase, which effectively facilitates conductivity transfer due to the rigid interfacial bonds between polymer chains and nanoparticles31. This robust interface enhances the efficient relocation of CNF conductivity to the polymer medium, thereby increasing the overall conductivity. Thus, a lower Lc is expected to improve conductivity. Conversely, a high Lc negatively impacts the percolation threshold and network fraction, resulting in reduced conductivity. A high Lc weakens the effective terms (Eqs. 8 and 9), further diminishing conductivity. The correlation between conductivity and Lc aligns with the predictions of the proposed model.

Polymer tunnel resistivity significantly influences tunneling resistance, as higher tunnel resistivity limits charge transfer through tunnels17. Charge transport through tunnels is primarily governed by tunneling resistance, and increased tunnel resistivity raises this resistance, negatively impacting the conductivity. Conversely, lower tunnel resistivity reduces the overall resistance of tunnels, facilitating electron tunneling and thereby enhancing conductivity, authorizing the advanced model.

Figure 3 illustrates the relationship between conductivity and both CNF quantity and length. The maximum conductivity of 1.05 S/m is found at ϕf = 0.04 and l = 100 μm, whereas ϕf = 0.01 and l = 50 μm result in a conductivity of 0.03 S/m. A higher quantity of longer CNFs significantly improves conductivity, while a low amount of shorter CNFs results in poor conductivity. Both the amount and length of CNFs directly impact conductivity, which is optimized by higher quantities and longer CNFs.

(a) 3-D picture and (b) 2-D plot for the relationship between conductivity and CNF quantity and length (generated by Mathematical software).

The amount of CNF plays a crucial role in charge transfer within PCNFs, as it dictates network size, thereby facilitating electron conduction. A larger quantity of CNFs creates an extensive network that enhances electron transfer. More CNFs increase the number of conductive paths, raising conductivity. However, a low CNF amount may not reach the percolation threshold required for network formation, resulting in a diminished network and lower conductivity. This observation aligns with predictions made by the proposed model. Numerous studies have reported similar role of nanofiller quantity in measured conductivity36–38, supporting the present model.

Larger CNFs increase their effective amount in the system (Eq. 9) and simultaneously reduce the percolation threshold (Eq. 15), expanding the network size. Consequently, longer CNFs create a larger network, enhancing conductivity. Contrariwise, shorter nanofibers reduce the effective filler amount and raise the ϕp, thereby decreasing the network size and weakening conductivity, as predicted by the model.

Figure 4 appearances the effect of contact diameter and number of contacts on the conductivity. The highest output as 0.45 S/m is detected at d = 40 nm and m = 200. In contrast, conductivity declines to 0.01 S/m at d = 10 nm and m = 20. Larger contact diameters and higher numbers of contacts yield the greater conductivity, while smaller diameters and fewer contacts reduce it significantly. An increase in contact diameter and number of contacts enhances conductivity, highlighting that both parameters play a direct role in managing conductivity.

Estimation of conductivity by contact diameter and number of contacts by (a) three- and (b) two-dimensional patterns (generated by Mathematical software).

Smaller contact diameters increase tunneling resistance, restricting electron transfer across tunnels9,17. Limited contact areas reduce electron mobility due to the fewer connection points between CNFs. On the other hand, larger contact areas lower tunneling resistance, strengthening electron transport. Increased contact areas facilitate stronger connections between nanofibers, improving charge transport. Thus, contact diameter has a direct influence on conductivity, as predicted by the proposed model.

A greater number of contacts among nanofibers also enhance the charge transfer, as interconnected CNFs establish conductive paths that facilitate electron transport. Fewer contacts, however, isolate CNFs, hindering electron movement. Reduced contact numbers decrease conductive pathways, thereby lowering conductivity. Hence, the current model accurately captures the influence of m on the conductivity of samples.

Figure 5 illustrates the relationship between conductivity and varying CNF radius and interphase depth. Optimal conductivity of 0.24 S/m is succeeded at R = 55 nm and t = 40 nm, while an insulating PCNF is noticed at R = 100 nm and t = 5 nm. Reedier CNFs and denser interphases optimize conduction, whereas thicker nanofibers and narrower interphases fail to contribute significantly to conductivity. Thus, higher conductivity is attained with thinner CNFs and a denser interphase.

Conductivity variation based on CNF and interphase size: (a) 3-D image and (b) 2-D scheme (generated by Mathematical software).

Thinner CNFs decrease Lc (Eq. 7), enhancing conductivity transfer from nanofibers to the matrix. A shorter Lc increases both the effective length and quantity of CNFs within the system. Furthermore, thinner CNFs reduce the percolation threshold (Eq. 15), expanding the network (Eq. 13). This network expansion strengthens conductivity transfer, thus improving overall conductivity. In contrast, thicker CNFs increase Lc, which hampers conductivity transfer in the PCNF. Additionally, thicker CNFs negatively impact the actual length and quantity of CNFs and raise the percolation threshold, reducing the network size. This description clearly illustrates the inverse relationship between conductivity and CNF radius, supporting the proposed model.

A greater interphase depth reduces the percolation threshold, thereby increasing the network portion9. A thicker interphase expands the network, enhancing charge transport, as the interphase acts as a conductive medium adjacent to the CNFs39. Conversely, a thinner interphase raises the percolation threshold, reducing the network size. A tinny interphase cannot sufficiently inflate the net, so reducing conductivity. Hence, the developed model effectively captures the relationship between conductivity and interphase thickness.

Figure 6 illustrates the effects of interphase conduction and network fraction on conductivity. The maximum output of 0.35 S/m is reached at high interphase conductivity (ψ = 400 S/m) and network fraction (f = 0.4). By contrast, minimum values of ψ = 50 S/m and f = 0.1 decrease conductivity to 0.04 S/m. Both interphase conduction and net portion directly influence conductivity, optimizing it at higher values. In contrast, low ranges of these terms cause minimal improvement in the conductivity.

Conductivity at different levels of net portion and interphase conductivity: (a) three-D and (b) two-D plots (generated by Mathematical software).

Increased interphase conductivity reduces Lc (Eq. 7), enhancing conductivity transfer through the defective interphase. A lower Lc boosts the effective length and concentration of CNFs, thereby increasing network percentage. A network formed by both CNF and interphase benefits from higher interphase conductivity, which further enhances network conductivity. This shows that increased interphase conductivity improves charge transfer in PCNFs. Contrariwise, low interphase conductivity raises Lc, reducing the actual features of CNFs, which in turn decreases network size. Low interphase conductivity thus weakens overall conductivity, supporting the developed model.

The network fraction (f) directly influences network concentration in PCNFs (Eq. 16). A higher f results in a larger CNF-interphase network, boosting conductivity, as a conductive network is crucial for achieving electron transfer40,41. By contrast, a lower f reflects a more distribution of nanofibers and interphase in the polymer matrix, insufficient for effective electron transport. A reduced f shrinks the network, thereby lowering conductivity. Thus, maximizing f is key for achieving higher conductivity, in line with the proposed model.

Figure 7 depicts the influence of tunneling size and CNF waviness on conductivity. The maximum conductivity of 0.77 S/m occurs at λ = 1 nm and u = 1, while λ > 8 nm reduces conductivity to 0.04 S/m. Narrow tunnels and straight nanofibers optimize conductivity, whereas larger tunnels and greater waviness diminish it. This fact underscores the importance of narrow tunnels and straight CNFs for optimal conductivity, as tunneling size and waviness inversely affect conductivity.

Conductivity estimation based on tunneling size and twistiness by (a) 3-D and (b) 2-D patterns (generated by Mathematical software).

Larger tunnels impede electron transfer, as the insulating polymer within the tunnels limits conductivity. Distant CNFs struggle to facilitate charge transfer due to the high tunneling resistance of large tunnels. Conversely, narrower tunnels enhance electron transfer between CNFs, as smaller tunnels present lower resistance to electron movement. So, the proposed model effectively associates conductivity with tunneling size.

Although greater waviness reduces Lc (Eq. 7), it also shortens the effective CNF length, thereby decreasing both the effective length and concentration of CNFs (Eqs. 8 and 9). Increased waviness raises the percolation threshold, thereby shrinking the network. This reveals that higher waviness diminishes the effectiveness of CNFs in PCNFs, weakening conductivity. Conversely, reduced waviness promotes the effective length and amount of CNFs. Lower waviness decreases the percolation threshold, positively impacting network concentration (Eq. 17). Consequently, reduced waviness expands the network, enhancing electron transfer and conductivity, as indicated by the model.

Model validation by measured conductivities

The advanced model is assessed by actual conductivity results from the references. Table 1 presents the properties of real PCNF samples used in this study. At the first step, the measured percolation threshold is fitted to Eq. 15, which determines the interphase depth and Lc obtaining the interphase conductivity (ψ). Specialized probes, often with microelectrodes, can be used to measure the conductivity within a small region, potentially isolating the interphase for measurement, but the characterization of an interface/interphase part at nanoscale is a difficult work, which may cause some errors in the measurement. Since the values of both Lc and ψ are reasonable and valid at applicable range, the proposed model is an appropriate tool for characterizing the mentioned terms. Then, the effective length and concentration of CNFs are calculated and the proposed model needs the tunnelling properties to predict the conductivity. The measured percolation thresholds of the samples are compared to those predicted by Eq. 15, showing strong agreement. The average thickness and conductivity of the interphase are detailed in Table 1, with interphase depth from 4 to 35.4 nm, consistent with nanoscale properties. Similarly, interphase conductivity is estimated between 30 and 500 S/m, lower than the CNF conductivity (104 S/m), indicating the varied role of interphase features in controlling charge transfer within PCNFs.

Conclusions

The defective interphase was characterized by Lc to express the effective concentration and length of CNFs, as well as the percolation threshold and network concentration. Additionally, the Weber–Kamal model was refined by incorporating effective terms and tunneling properties to estimate PCNF conductivity accurately. The influence of all contributing factors on PCNF conductivity was clarified, and numerous experimental conductivity results confirmed the model’s calculations. A high CNF content (ϕf = 0.04) and length (l = 100 μm) significantly improved conductivity to 1.05 S/m, while a low CNF content (ϕf = 0.01) and shorter length (l = 50 μm) resulted in a reduced conduction of 0.03 S/m. Thus, maximizing CNF content and length enhances conductivity considerably. The peak conductivity of 0.45 S/m was realized with a contact diameter (d) of 40 nm and contact number (m) of 200, while smaller contact diameter and fewer contacts (d = 10 nm and m = 20) reduced conductivity to 0.01 S/m. Larger contact diameters and a greater number of contacts optimize conductivity. Similarly, R = 55 nm and t = 40 nm resulted in optimized conductivity of 0.24 S/m, while larger CNFs (R = 100 nm) and thinner interphases (t = 5 nm) produced an insulating PCNF. These results confirm that thinner CNFs and thicker interphases improve conductivity. The utmost conduction of 0.35 S/m was attained with maximum interphase conductivity (ψ = 400 S/m) and network fraction (f = 0.4), while minimal interphase conductivity (ψ = 50 S/m) and network fraction (f = 0.1) reduced conductivity to 0.04 S/m. Thus, conductivity increases with higher interphase conductivity and network fraction. Furthermore, calculations of Lc, interphase conductivity, and tunneling properties aligned closely with experimental conductivity results, validating the refined model. As observed, the length and concentration of CNFs play the main role in the PCNF conductivity, an important issue for optimization and designing of materials. After that, tunnelling distance and waviness factor have the most influence on the conductivity. Future researches can focus on the experimental characterization of interphase depth and conductivity and then Lc in the PCNFs. Also, more tunnelling features can be applied to develop the models for the conductivity of PCNFs.
