Physical exercise and school adaptation among junior high school students: the chain mediating roles of self-efficacy and mental health
Authors: Yong Jiang, Mengyao Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study examined the association between physical exercise and school adaptation among junior high school students, and tested the chain mediating roles of self-efficacy and mental health. A total of 500 students (239 boys, 261 girls) completed the Physical Activity Rating Scale, General Self-Efficacy Scale, Mental Health Rating Scale for junior high school students, and School Adaptation Scale for junior high school students. Pearson correlation analysis and PROCESS Model 6 were used, with gender and grade controlled. Physical exercise was positively correlated with school adaptation (r = 0.534, p < 0.001). Mediation analysis showed a significant direct association between physical exercise and school adaptation (Effect = 0.0025, SE = 0.0007, 95% CI [0.0011, 0.0038]) and a significant total indirect pathway (Effect = 0.0048, BootSE = 0.0007, 95% CI [0.0035, 0.0061]). The pathway through mental health accounted for 52.78% of the total effect, and the chain pathway through self-efficacy and mental health accounted for 4.17%. The pathway through self-efficacy alone was relatively weak and should be interpreted with caution. These findings suggest that physical exercise is closely associated with school adaptation, partly through mental health and the sequential relationship between self-efficacy and mental health. Given the cross-sectional design, the results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55278-2.

Introduction

School adaptation is an important developmental competence that enables adolescents to adjust to academic demands, interpersonal relationships, behavioral norms, and campus life. For junior high school students, school adaptation is closely related to academic performance, psychological well-being, and social development1. This competency manifests not only in students’ behavioral conduct during school hours but also encompasses their emotional cognition of the campus environment, attitudinal orientation, and proactive engagement in school activities2. It primarily involves adaptation to the school setting, academic demands, and interactions with teachers, peers, and classmates3. Good school adaptation is associated with academic engagement, campus participation, mental health, and social development. As educational environments grow increasingly complex, students face intensifying challenges in academic pressure, peer relationships, and psychological adjustment. Cultivating school adaptation capacity has thus become a major focus in international education. UNESCO’s Education 2030 Framework emphasizes developing children’s emotional regulation, behavioral management, and environmental adaptation skills4. Similarly, the OECD’s Future of Education and Skills 2030 Framework identifies school adaptation as a crucial foundation for students navigating future uncertainties5. In China, since 2012, the Ministry of Education has successively issued policy documents such as the “Guidelines for Mental Health Education in Primary and Secondary Schools” and the “Guidance on Advancing Mental Health Education in Primary and Secondary Schools in the New Era,” emphasizing the enhancement of students’ emotional management, behavioral regulation, and social adaptation abilities through curriculum instruction, physical activities, and campus culture development6. Although existing research has examined factors influencing school adaptation among junior high students from perspectives such as individual psychological traits, family support, and school environment, systematic analysis of the underlying psychological mechanisms remains relatively insufficient. In particular, the specific pathways through which physical exercise is associated with school adaptation require further clarification.

Physical exercise is an important activity related to the physical, psychological, and social development of junior high school students. It is often associated with more positive emotional experiences, higher self-efficacy, lower psychological stress, and more frequent social interaction. Existing research indicates that regular physical exercise is associated with adolescents’ positive emotions and mental health levels7, confidence in tackling academic tasks and social challenges8, and behavioral self-regulation, interpersonal skills, and emotional regulation in cooperative and competitive contexts, all of which are related to students’ adaptation to the school environment9. Based on this, this study examined the association between physical exercise and school adaptation using empirical data, with a focus on the potential mediating roles of self-efficacy and mental health. The findings may provide evidence for understanding the role of school physical education in students’ psychological development and school adaptation.

Theoretical foundations and assumptions

The association between physical exercise and school adaptation among junior high school students

Physical exercise refers to planned or structured physical activities characterized by specific intensity, frequency, and duration, and is commonly used as an indicator of students’ physical activity participation10. Physical exercise has been associated with lower levels of negative emotions, such as anxiety and depression, and with more positive emotional experiences11–15. School adaptation ability refers to students’ comprehensive capacity to respond positively and achieve effective adjustment to academic demands, interpersonal relationships, behavioral norms, and campus life within the school context. It serves as a crucial indicator for measuring students’ physical and mental development and educational outcomes. According to the school adaptation structure model, school adaptation mainly includes academic adaptation, interpersonal adaptation, emotional adaptation, and behavioral adaptation. This framework has been widely applied in adolescent school adaptation research and aligns closely with the current basic education objectives of “autonomous development, social participation, and physical and mental health.” The 2022 Edition of the Compulsory Education Physical Education and Health Curriculum Standards explicitly states that physical activities should foster students’ awareness of rules, cooperative abilities, emotional regulation, and social adaptation skills. This aligns with the key competency requirements outlined in the Ministry of Education’s core literacy framework.

Therefore, physical education courses may provide an important context related to students’ school adaptation. Existing research indicates that physical exercise is significantly associated with students’ school adaptation. Empirical studies by Yan Jun and Tao Baole16 reveal that participation levels in physical activities can significantly predict students’ academic adaptation, interpersonal adaptation, and emotional stability. Cooperative experiences, rule-based contexts, and emotional regulation in physical activities are closely related to students’ adaptive behaviors in school contexts. Malinauskas et al. noted that regular physical activity is associated with students’ self-control, social interaction skills, and academic engagement, all of which are closely related to overall school adaptation. Further evidence comes from school intervention studies. Zhang Jing17, while discussing adolescent adaptation promotion strategies, mentioned that participation in physical activities is related to key qualities such as discipline awareness, teamwork ability, curiosity, and persistence. These qualities are crucial for students to cope with changes in school settings, manage academic stress, and engage in interpersonal interactions. Gu Yuanyuan’s research also demonstrates that moderate physical exercise shows a significant positive correlation with academic adaptation among junior high students and is positively associated with mental health. Better mental health is further associated with better school adaptation. Evidently, physical exercise has a solid theoretical foundation and increasingly robust empirical support for its association with school adaptation18. However, current research predominantly focuses on descriptive analysis, with relatively insufficient exploration of the mediating mechanisms, such as mental health and self-efficacy, through which physical exercise is associated with school adaptation. As research deepens, the study of school adaptation is shifting from phenomenological descriptions toward mechanistic analysis. Consequently, Hypothesis 1 (H1) is proposed in this study.

H1: Physical exercise is positively associated with school adaptation among junior high school students.

The mediating role of self-efficacy

Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to successfully complete a specific behavior or task. It is a crucial psychological resource related to behavioral choices, effort levels, and emotional experiences.According to Bandura’s self-efficacy theory, an individual’s success or failure in activity contexts, vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion, and physiological-emotional states are closely related to the formation and development of self-efficacy, as well as behavioral motivation and adaptive outcomes.Existing research indicates that self-efficacy statistically and positively predicts adolescents’ school adaptation.Higher levels of self-efficacy correlate with superior performance across dimensions such as academic adaptation, interpersonal adaptation, and emotional adaptation. Furthermore, self-efficacy is an important psychological resource related to both psychological and social development19. Adolescents’ self-efficacy is often related to successful experiences in academic and social contexts, peer interactions, teacher feedback, learning motivation, and active participation in school life20. Adolescents with higher self-efficacy typically report more positive responses to academic pressure and campus challenges, which are closely related to better school adaptation. Consequently, self-efficacy has gained increasing attention in the cultivation of adolescents’ school adaptation capabilities.

Existing research indicates a stable and significant positive correlation between physical exercise and self-efficacy. The more frequently junior high students engage in physical exercise, the higher their self-efficacy scores. Physical exercise provides contexts in which students may experience physical competence, success, and task control. These exercise experiences may be associated with students’ confidence and behavioral efficacy expectations. Positive feedback, peer support, and achievement experiences gained through physical activities may be associated with adolescents’ self-efficacy, self-regulation, and positive behavioral performance in academic and school settings. Related studies also indicate that physical exercise is associated with individuals’ self-management skills, psychological energy levels, and self-efficacy in learning and daily life. The effort-achievement relationship experienced by junior high school students in physical education settings may be related to self-confidence, persistence, and resilience when confronting school tasks21. Therefore, physical exercise may be associated with higher self-efficacy, which may in turn be related to better school adaptation among junior high school students. Based on this reasoning, Hypothesis 2 (H2) is proposed.

H2: Self-efficacy mediates the association between physical exercise and school adaptation among junior high school students.

The mediating role of mental health

Mental health refers to a stable psychological trait where individuals maintain optimal functioning in cognition, emotions, and behavior. It is closely related to adolescents’ adaptation to campus life and social environments. Existing research indicates that robust mental health is associated with relatively stable and positive emotional states when confronting academic pressures, interpersonal conflicts, and multiple environmental demands. It is also related to self-regulation, behavioral control, and problem-solving abilities. Adolescents with higher levels of mental health typically exhibit stable emotional states during learning, possess stronger coping and self-management abilities, and tend to address campus challenges proactively, demonstrating superior school adaptation23. Students with robust mental health generally exhibit greater self-acceptance, stress resilience, and interpersonal adaptability—factors that foster positive development in both academic and social behaviors, closely linking to school adjustment. Evidently, mental health serves as a crucial indicator for individuals to maintain sound psychological functioning and achieve effective adaptation within the school environment. Research in the field of physical education demonstrates a significant positive correlation between physical exercise and mental health. Moderate physical exercise has been associated with positive nervous, emotional, and cognitive functioning, lower academic stress, and better emotional states, which may be related to adolescents’ overall mental health levels24. Physical exercise has also been associated with neurobiological and psychological processes related to lower anxiety and depressive symptoms, higher emotional resilience, and better psychological recovery capacity among adolescents, which may be further related to school adaptation25–27. Adolescents who regularly participate in physical activities demonstrate superior performance in self-esteem, self-identity, emotional management skills, and social engagement, with significantly higher levels of mental health compared to those with lower participation in physical activities28. Regular physical exercise is also related to positive psychological qualities in adolescents, partly through better sleep quality, physical vitality, and social connections29. Thus, physical exercise may be associated with adolescents’ mental health, which may further be related to their school adaptation. Based on this, Hypothesis 3 (H3) is proposed in this study.

H3: Mental health mediates the association between physical exercise and school adaptation among junior high school students.

The chain mediating roles of self-efficacy and mental health

Existing research indicates that physical exercise, self-efficacy, and mental health are all positively associated with adolescents’ school adaptation. Self-efficacy is also closely related to mental health, as adolescents with higher self-efficacy usually have stronger perceived control, better coping resources, and fewer negative emotional experiences when facing academic pressure and interpersonal challenges30–32. In physical activity contexts, successful performance, bodily feedback, peer interaction, and teacher encouragement may strengthen students’ sense of competence and confidence, thereby contributing to higher self-efficacy33. At the same time, physical exercise is associated with stress release, emotional regulation, and positive emotional experiences, which are important psychological resources for adolescent mental health34. Therefore, self-efficacy and mental health may form a sequential pathway through which physical exercise is associated with school adaptation. Based on this reasoning, Hypothesis 4 (H4) is proposed.

H4: Self-efficacy and mental health play a chain mediating role in the association between physical exercise and school adaptation among junior high school students.

Research subjects and methods

Participants and sampling procedures

This study examined the association between physical exercise and school adaptation among junior high school students, as well as the mediating roles of self-efficacy and mental health. The required sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1. With α set at 0.05, statistical power set at 0.99, and a medium effect size of 0.15, the minimum required sample size was 161. Therefore, the final sample size met the requirements for the planned statistical analyses.

A stratified random sampling method was used. Students from three grades of a junior high school in one city were recruited, with grade level used as the sampling stratum. Questionnaires were distributed by administrative classes, and students completed the questionnaires collectively under standardized instructions. Before completing the questionnaires, participants were informed of the purpose of the study, the anonymity of their responses, the voluntary nature of participation, and that the data would be used only for academic research. A total of 520 questionnaires were distributed. After excluding questionnaires with incomplete responses, patterned responses, or clearly invalid answers, 500 valid questionnaires were retained, yielding a valid response rate of 96.1%. The final sample included 239 boys and 261 girls.

Measures

Physical activity level scale

The Physical Activity Level Scale revised by Liang Deqing et al.35 was adopted. This scale aims to assess key indicators of an individual’s participation in physical activities over the past month, including intensity, duration, and frequency. Each indicator employs a 5-point Likert scale scoring method. According to the scale’s formula, physical exercise volume = intensity × (time − 1) × frequency. Based on the total score, individuals’ physical activity levels are categorized into three grades: low physical exercise volume (≤ 19 points), moderate physical exercise volume (20–42 points), and high physical exercise volume (≥ 43–100 points). This indicator system comprehensively reflects the quantity and quality of participants’ recent physical activity engagement, serving as an evaluation metric for their physical activity levels. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was 0.834.

School adaptation scale

The measurement of school adaptation employed the School Adaptation Scale for Secondary Students developed by Hou Jing36. Designed based on the school life context of Chinese secondary students, this scale comprises three dimensions—academic adaptation, interpersonal adaptation, and emotional adaptation—with a total of 27 items. It provides a comprehensive assessment of junior high school students’ overall adaptability within the campus environment. The scale employs a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “1 = Not at all true” to “5 = Completely true.” Higher scores indicate stronger school adaptation abilities. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was 0.727.

General self-efficacy scale

The measurement of self-efficacy employed the General Self-Efficacy Scale compiled by Wang Caikang et al.37. Originally developed by Schwarzer and Jerusalem, this scale has been widely applied among adolescent populations in China. Comprising 10 items, the scale assesses individuals’ confidence levels and coping abilities when confronting academic challenges, life pressures, and task difficulties. This study employed a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “1 = Not at all true” to “5 = Completely true,” with scores positively correlated to participants’ general self-efficacy. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was 0.857.

Mental health assessment scale for junior high school students

Mental health was measured using the Mental Health Assessment Scale for Junior High School Students developed by Tan Heping et al.38. Designed based on the physical and psychological developmental characteristics of Chinese junior high school students, this scale encompasses multiple dimensions including emotional stability, interpersonal relationships, academic stress adaptation, volitional qualities, and self-acceptance, enabling a comprehensive reflection of students’ mental health status. The scale comprises 60 items organized into multiple dimensions, each possessing clear psychological significance and good measurement stability. It employs a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “1 = Not at all true” to “5 = Completely true,” with higher scores indicating better mental health status. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was 0.980.

All scales were scored according to their original scoring procedures. The physical exercise score was calculated using the formula of the Physical Activity Rating Scale: physical exercise volume = intensity × (duration − 1) × frequency, with higher scores indicating higher levels of physical exercise. For self-efficacy, mental health, and school adaptation, the mean item score of each scale was used as the composite score for the corresponding variable. Higher scores indicated higher levels of the corresponding construct. These composite scores were used in the subsequent descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and chain mediation analysis.

Statistical analysis

During data processing, Excel was used for data entry, organization, and preliminary screening. Questionnaires with incomplete responses, patterned responses, or clearly invalid answers were excluded. The valid data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0, and the chain mediation analysis was conducted using PROCESS macro version 4.1. Before the formal statistical analyses, the distributional characteristics of the main variables and the assumptions for regression analysis were examined. Normality was assessed using skewness and kurtosis, and multicollinearity was examined using tolerance and variance inflation factor values. The results showed that the skewness and kurtosis values of the main variables were within acceptable ranges, and the tolerance and variance inflation factor values indicated no serious multicollinearity. Therefore, the data met the requirements for subsequent correlation analysis and regression-based mediation analysis.

The analytical procedures were as follows. First, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to preliminarily assess common method bias. Second, independent-samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA were used to examine differences in the core variables by gender and grade. Third, Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine the direction and strength of associations among the core variables. Finally, PROCESS Model 6 was used to test the chain mediation model, with 5,000 bootstrap samples used to estimate the 95% confidence intervals for indirect effects. Effects were considered statistically significant when the confidence intervals did not include zero.

In reporting the results, regression path analyses presented standardized regression coefficients, t values, p values, and model summary statistics, whereas mediation analyses presented unstandardized effects, standard errors, and bootstrap 95% confidence intervals. This distinction was made to clearly separate standardized path coefficients from unstandardized direct and indirect effects. Gender and grade were included as control variables in the PROCESS mediation model to account for the potential influence of basic demographic variables. However, other potentially relevant contextual variables, such as socioeconomic status, academic performance, parental education, and broader family background, were not collected in this study and therefore could not be included as covariates. Because this study used a cross-sectional design, the mediation model was interpreted as a statistical model of associations rather than evidence of causal mechanisms.

Results and analysis

Control and testing of common method bias

Because all data were collected using self-report questionnaires, anonymous responses were used to reduce the potential influence of common method bias. Harman’s single-factor test was then conducted to assess common method bias39. Results indicated 12 factors with eigenvalues exceeding 1, with the largest single factor explaining 39.97% of variance. This value falls below the 40% critical threshold proposed by Podsakoff et al.[ ]Consequently, the data exhibit no significant common method bias and meet requirements for subsequent statistical analysis.

Assumption checks

Before conducting correlation and mediation analyses, the assumptions of normality and multicollinearity were examined. The results showed that the skewness and kurtosis values of the main variables were within acceptable ranges, indicating that the distributions did not substantially deviate from normality. Multicollinearity diagnostics showed that the tolerance values of the predictors were above 0.10 and the variance inflation factor values were below 10, suggesting that multicollinearity was not a serious concern. Therefore, the data were considered appropriate for Pearson correlation analysis and regression-based PROCESS mediation analysis.

Testing the mediating effect

To examine the relationship between physical exercise and school adaptation and the chain mediating roles of self-efficacy and mental health, PROCESS Model 6 was used, with gender and grade included as control variables. Because information on socioeconomic status, academic performance, and family context was not collected, these variables could not be included as covariates in the present model. It should be noted that Table 5 reports standardized regression coefficients, t values, p values, and model summary statistics to present the regression paths, whereas Table 6 reports unstandardized effects, standard errors, and Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals to present the total, direct, and indirect effects.

The regression path analysis showed that, after controlling for gender and grade, physical exercise remained positively associated with school adaptation (β = 0.533, p < 0.001), with the model explaining 28.5% of the variance (R² = 0.285, F = 65.969, p < 0.001). Physical exercise was also positively associated with self-efficacy (β = 0.341, p < 0.001), with the model explaining 11.9% of the variance (R² = 0.119, F = 22.305, p < 0.001). In the model with mental health as the outcome variable, physical exercise (β = 0.669, p < 0.001) and self-efficacy (β = 0.170, p < 0.001) were both significantly associated with mental health, and the model explained 55.2% of the variance (R² = 0.552, F = 152.240, p < 0.001). In the model with school adaptation as the outcome variable, physical exercise (β = 0.182, p < 0.001), self-efficacy (β = 0.127, p < 0.001), and mental health (β = 0.423, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with school adaptation, and the model explained 40.0% of the variance (R² = 0.400, F = 65.961, p < 0.001). The effects of gender and grade were not statistically significant in any of the regression models. These results are shown in Table 5.

The standardized coefficients reported above were used to describe the direction and relative strength of the regression paths. The significance of the mediation effects was evaluated using unstandardized effects and their Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals, as shown in Table 6.

To validate the reliability of the chain mediation model, Bootstrap analysis was conducted with 5,000 resamples after controlling for gender and grade. The effect values reported in Table 6 are unstandardized effects, and the confidence intervals are Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals.

The results showed that the total effect of physical exercise on school adaptation was 0.0072, SE = 0.0005, 95% CI [0.0062, 0.0082], and the confidence interval did not include zero, indicating a significant association between the two variables. After self-efficacy and mental health were included as mediators, the direct effect of physical exercise on school adaptation was 0.0025, SE = 0.0007, 95% CI [0.0011, 0.0038], and the confidence interval also did not include zero, indicating that the direct association between the two variables remained significant. Meanwhile, the total indirect effect was 0.0048, BootSE = 0.0007, 95% CI [0.0035, 0.0061], and the confidence interval did not include zero, indicating that self-efficacy and mental health played an overall mediating role in the relationship between physical exercise and school adaptation. It should be noted that, because the effect estimates were rounded, the proportions were reported based on the ratio of each effect to the total effect.

Further examination of the specific indirect paths showed that: (1) the indirect effect of the path “physical exercise → self-efficacy → school adaptation” was 0.0006, BootSE = 0.0003, 95% CI [0.0000, 0.0012], accounting for 8.33% of the total effect. This path had a small effect size, and the lower limit of the confidence interval was close to zero, suggesting that the statistical evidence for this specific indirect path was relatively weak and should be interpreted with caution; (2) the indirect effect of the path “physical exercise → mental health → school adaptation” was 0.0038, BootSE = 0.0007, 95% CI [0.0026, 0.0052], accounting for 52.78% of the total effect, making it the relatively larger indirect path in the model; and (3) the chain indirect effect of the path “physical exercise → self-efficacy → mental health → school adaptation” was 0.0003, BootSE = 0.0001, 95% CI [0.0002, 0.0006], accounting for 4.17% of the total effect, indicating that this chain indirect path was statistically supported.

Overall, after controlling for gender and grade, the indirect effect through mental health and the chain indirect effect through self-efficacy and mental health were relatively clear. By contrast, the indirect effect through self-efficacy alone was relatively weak, suggesting that self-efficacy may be more closely related to school adaptation through mental health. These results supported H3 and H4 and provided partial statistical support for H2. Based on these findings, the chain mediation model is shown in Fig. 2.

Discussion

The association between physical exercise and school adaptation among junior high school students

This study found a significant positive association between physical exercise and school adaptation among junior high school students, which is consistent with previous findings showing that physical activity is closely related to adolescents’ emotional, social, and adaptive functioning41. This finding suggests that students who report higher levels of physical exercise also tend to report better adaptation to academic demands, peer relationships, and school life. However, this association should not be interpreted as evidence that physical exercise directly causes better school adaptation, because the present study used a cross-sectional design.

A possible explanation for this association lies in the structured and social nature of physical activity. Physical education classes and extracurricular sports provide students with repeated opportunities to follow rules, cooperate with peers, manage competition, receive feedback, and regulate emotions. These experiences are closely related to emotional regulation, self-management, stress coping, and social-emotional competence, all of which are important components of school adaptation42. Recent studies have also emphasized that physical activity is not only a form of bodily movement but also a social and educational context in which adolescents develop responsibility, interpersonal skills, and emotional stability43. Therefore, the present findings are consistent with the broader view that school-based physical activity may be meaningfully associated with students’ adaptive development.

At the same time, the strength of the association should be interpreted critically. School adaptation is a multidimensional construct that may also be shaped by academic achievement, family background, teacher support, peer relationships, and school climate. Therefore, physical exercise should not be regarded as the only factor related to school adaptation. Rather, it may represent one important behavioral context through which students accumulate social, emotional, and psychological resources. This interpretation helps clarify the contribution of the present study: it does not simply confirm that physical exercise is related to school adaptation, but further examines how psychological variables may help explain this association.

The mediating role of self-efficacy in the association between physical exercise and school adaptation

The results showed that the indirect pathway through self-efficacy alone was relatively weak, accounting for 8.33% of the total effect. The lower limit of its confidence interval was close to zero, suggesting that this pathway should be interpreted with caution. This finding indicates that although self-efficacy was involved in the association between physical exercise and school adaptation, its independent mediating role was limited in the present model.

This result is theoretically meaningful. Self-efficacy reflects students’ beliefs about their ability to deal with challenges and difficulties. In physical activity contexts, successful skill acquisition, bodily feedback, peer interaction, and teacher feedback may be related to stronger perceived competence and more positive self-evaluations44. Previous research has also shown that self-efficacy is associated with emotional regulation, negative emotional experiences, and learning engagement45. Therefore, it is reasonable to expect self-efficacy to be related to school adaptation.

However, the weak independent pathway suggests that general self-efficacy may not be sufficient to explain school adaptation by itself. School adaptation includes academic, interpersonal, emotional, and behavioral dimensions, whereas the self-efficacy scale used in this study measured general confidence rather than domain-specific school adaptation efficacy or academic efficacy. This may partly explain why the independent mediating role of self-efficacy was weaker than expected. In other words, physical exercise may be associated with students’ confidence, but this confidence may need to be connected with emotional stability and mental health before it is more clearly reflected in school adaptation. This interpretation also helps explain why self-efficacy played a clearer role in the chain pathway with mental health than as an independent mediator.

The mediating role of mental health in the association between physical exercise and school adaptation

Mental health showed a relatively clear mediating role in the association between physical exercise and school adaptation. The indirect pathway through mental health accounted for 52.78% of the total effect, making it the strongest indirect pathway in the model. This finding is consistent with previous research showing that physical exercise, mental health, and adolescent adaptation are closely related46.

Compared with self-efficacy, mental health may be more directly connected with school adaptation because it reflects students’ emotional stability, stress tolerance, interpersonal adjustment, and psychological functioning in daily school life. Students with better mental health may be more capable of coping with academic pressure, managing interpersonal challenges, maintaining emotional balance, and participating actively in school activities. These characteristics are central to school adaptation. Previous studies have shown that regular physical activity is associated with better emotional states, lower anxiety and depressive symptoms, and stronger psychological resources among children and adolescents47. The present findings are consistent with this line of research and further suggest that mental health may be an important psychological pathway linking physical exercise with school adaptation.

This result also has theoretical implications. It suggests that the relationship between physical exercise and school adaptation should not be understood only from a behavioral perspective, such as exercise frequency or exercise volume. Instead, the psychological quality of students’ exercise experiences may be important. Physical exercise may be more meaningfully associated with school adaptation when it is accompanied by positive emotions, stress relief, social interaction, and perceived psychological support. Therefore, school physical education programs may benefit from paying attention not only to exercise participation, but also to supportive, enjoyable, and psychologically safe physical activity environments.

The chain mediating roles of self-efficacy and mental health in the association between physical exercise and school adaptation

The chain pathway through self-efficacy and mental health was statistically supported, although its effect size was relatively small, accounting for 4.17% of the total effect. This finding suggests that physical exercise may be associated with school adaptation not only through mental health directly, but also through a sequential psychological pathway involving self-efficacy and mental health.

Theoretically, this result supports an integrated psychological explanation. Physical exercise may provide students with opportunities to experience competence, success, feedback, and social recognition. These experiences may be related to stronger self-efficacy. Students with higher self-efficacy may then report stronger perceived control, more positive coping strategies, and better emotional regulation when facing academic and interpersonal stress48,49. In this way, self-efficacy may be associated with mental health, which in turn is related to better school adaptation. This chain pathway helps connect physical activity participation, self-belief, psychological functioning, and school adaptation within a single explanatory model.

Nevertheless, the relatively small proportion of the chain indirect pathway indicates that this mechanism should not be overstated. The finding does not mean that self-efficacy and mental health fully explain the association between physical exercise and school adaptation. Other variables, such as peer support, teacher support, school climate, friendship quality, emotional intelligence, learning motivation, academic performance, socioeconomic status, and family context, may also be involved50. This point is particularly important because the present study did not include several contextual control variables. Therefore, the proposed chain mediation model should be viewed as a useful but partial explanation.

Overall, the academic contribution of this study lies in its attempt to move beyond a simple association between physical exercise and school adaptation by examining the psychological pathways involved. The findings suggest that mental health may be a more prominent pathway than self-efficacy alone, while self-efficacy may be more meaningful when considered together with mental health. This provides a more nuanced understanding of how physical exercise is related to school adaptation among junior high school students. Future studies should use longitudinal or intervention designs and include broader contextual variables to further test the stability and generalizability of this model.

Practical implications

The findings provide several practical implications for school physical education. First, physical education teachers may design graded physical activity tasks that allow students with different ability levels to experience progress and competence. Positive feedback should focus not only on performance outcomes, but also on effort, participation, cooperation, and improvement, which may help students build self-efficacy.

Second, physical activity programs should pay attention to students’ emotional experiences and mental health. Teachers may include cooperative games, moderate-intensity aerobic activities, group challenges, and brief reflection activities after exercise, so that students can recognize positive emotions, stress relief, peer support, and coping strategies during physical activity. These practices may help connect physical education with mental health education.

Third, schools could integrate these strategies into regular physical education classes, after-school sports clubs, and class-based activity programs. For students with low confidence, poor peer interaction, or adaptation difficulties, teachers and school mental health educators could provide supportive, low-pressure physical activity opportunities. Because this study is cross-sectional, these implications should be interpreted cautiously and further tested through longitudinal or intervention studies.

Conclusions and outlook

Conclusions

First, physical exercise, self-efficacy, mental health, and school adaptation among junior high school students were significantly correlated with one another. Second, after controlling for gender and grade, physical exercise remained directly associated with school adaptation. Third, mental health showed a relatively clear mediating role in the relationship between physical exercise and school adaptation, and the chain indirect pathway involving self-efficacy and mental health was statistically supported. By contrast, the indirect effect through self-efficacy alone was relatively weak and should be interpreted with caution. Overall, self-efficacy and mental health represent important psychological variables for understanding the association between physical exercise and school adaptation among junior high school students.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study adopted a cross-sectional design, which limits causal inference. Although the results showed significant associations among physical exercise, self-efficacy, mental health, and school adaptation, the temporal order of these variables cannot be determined. Future studies should use longitudinal or experimental designs to further examine these relationships.

Second, the present model controlled only for gender and grade. Other potentially important variables, such as socioeconomic status, academic performance, parental education, family structure, and broader family context, were not measured or included as control variables. These factors have been identified in previous research as relevant to students’ school adaptation and may also be associated with opportunities for physical exercise, psychological resources, and mental health. Therefore, the absence of these covariates may limit the precision of the estimated associations and may lead to possible omitted-variable bias. The findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. Future studies should collect and control for socioeconomic, academic, and family-related variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between physical exercise and school adaptation.

Third, the sample was drawn from junior high school students in one city, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future research should include students from different regions, school types, and sociocultural backgrounds. Fourth, this study focused only on self-efficacy and mental health as mediating variables. School adaptation may also be related to peer support, teacher support, family environment, school climate, learning motivation, friendship quality, emotional intelligence, and positive emotions. Future research could develop more comprehensive models to examine how physical exercise is associated with school adaptation through multiple psychological and social pathways.

Despite these limitations, the findings suggest that school physical education may be meaningfully associated with students’ psychological development and school adaptation. In practice, schools may support students’ school adaptation by designing physical activity programs that emphasize graded tasks, positive feedback, peer cooperation, emotional regulation, and psychological well-being. Physical education teachers could provide low-pressure activity opportunities for students with low confidence or adaptation difficulties, and school mental health educators could work with teachers to integrate brief reflection, stress-relief activities, and supportive peer interaction into regular physical education classes and after-school sports programs.
