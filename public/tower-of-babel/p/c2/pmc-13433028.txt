Signal detection and analysis of adverse events associated with sevelamer post-marketing based on the FAERS database
Authors: Junxia Cao, Na Yang, Cheng Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to evaluate adverse events (AEs) associated with sevelamer through data mining of the US FDA Adverse Event Reporting System, identify potential drug-related AEs, and provide guidance for clinical medication safety. AE reports related to sevelamer from the first quarter of 2004 to the second quarter of 2025 were extracted from the US FDA Adverse Event Reporting System database, classified by preferred term (PT) and system organ class, and risk signals were analyzed. Among 6780 AE reports in which sevelamer was the primary suspect drug, 123 PTs spanning 24 system organ classes were identified. Male reports exceeded females (20.66% vs 18.07%), with highest proportion in 60 to 74 years (11.81%). Median AE occurrence time – medication date was 119.00 (interquartile range, 14.00, 476.50) days, with most events occurring more than 60 days after administration (n = 353, 30.46%) and within 7 days (n = 121, 10.44%). Among the positive risk signal PTs, “constipation, gastric or intestinal ulcer/hemorrhage/perforation/necrosis/ischemia, colitis ischemic, erosive esophagitis/gastritis, abdominal pain, dysphagia, nausea, vomiting, peritonitis and pruritus” exhibited high signal strength, consistent with the label. Additionally, new signals included aorto-esophageal fistula, diabetic gastroparesis, large intestinal stenosis, short-bowel syndrome, bezoar, diverticulum intestinal, gastric polyps, large intestine polyp, feces pale, diverticulum, hemorrhagic intestinal diverticulum, crystal deposit intestine, appendicitis (complicated or perforated), renal cyst infection, colonic or abdominal abscess, pseudomembranous colitis, diverticulitis, gangrene, pseudopolyp, mucosal ulceration or necrosis, skin fibrosis, and excessive granulation tissue. Therapeutic use of sevelamer remains associated with risks of diverse AEs. Therefore, clinical practice requires close monitoring of gastrointestinal disorders, metabolic, skin disorders and infections, particularly GI disorders. Prompt intervention is essential to prevent severe complications.

1. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD), as a major global public health issue, is characterized by high prevalence and severe complications. Research data indicate that the global incidence of CKD reached approximately 674 million cases in 2021, with an age-standardized incidence rate of 233.56 cases per 100,000 population, and the prevalence rate has exceeded 10%.[1]

Patients with CKD may present with a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations, involving multiple aspects such as renal dysfunction, systemic symptoms, bone disorders, cardiovascular involvement, metabolic disturbances, and neurological complications.[2] Among these, CKD-mineral and bone disorder is a common complication of CKD, primarily characterized by metabolic disorders of calcium, phosphorus, parathyroid hormone, and vitamin D metabolism.[3] These include biochemical abnormalities (e.g., hyperphosphatemia and hypocalcemia), bone lesions, as well as calcification of the cardiovascular system and soft tissues.[3] Research has shown that hyperphosphatemia is strongly associated with increased mortality and the progression of cardiovascular calcification, which itself is an independent predictor of mortality.[4] Therefore, reducing serum phosphorus levels provides significant survival benefits for certain hemodialysis patient populations.

Sevelamer is a nonabsorbable polymeric phosphate binder devoid of calcium or metal ions.[3] It binds to phosphate in the gastrointestinal tract through ion exchange, thereby reducing its absorption and lowering serum phosphorus levels.[3] Compared to traditional calcium-based phosphate binders, sevelamer nonabsorbable nature avoids the risks of hypercalcemia and vascular calcification, offering an improved safety profile.[5] For example, in the “Treat-to-Goal” trial, 200 maintenance hemodialysis patients were randomly assigned to either the sevelamer group or the calcium-based phosphate binder group, and the results showed both groups achieved comparable phosphate control, but the incidence of hypercalcemia was significantly lower in the sevelamer group (5% vs 16%).[4,6] Moreover, C-reactive protein levels in the sevelamer group significantly decreased after 52 weeks, while no change was observed in the calcium-based binder group, so this suggests that sevelamer may have anti-atherosclerotic and anti-inflammatory effects.[4,6]

With the increasing clinical use of sevelamer, its safety profile has garnered increasing attention, and the number of AE reports has risen. Commonly reported sevelamer-related AEs include intestinal obstruction, gastrointestinal ulcers, necrosis, perforation, and dysphagia. Currently, evidence for these AEs primarily comes from short-term clinical trials, case reports, or meta-analyses. To comprehensively assess sevelamer real-world safety, leveraging large-scale post-marketing surveillance data and data mining algorithms to explore potential AE signals is crucial. This will help further confirm sevelamer long-term safety profile and provide clinicians with more comprehensive guidance on its use.

The U.S. FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) is a publicly available database that collects spontaneously reported AEs from healthcare institutions, physicians, healthcare professionals, patients, and other stakeholders.[7] Owing to its extensive data volume, diverse information sources, standardized structure, and free public access, FAERS is internationally recognized for pharmacovigilance and AE signal detection.[7] By mining and analyzing FAERS data, we can obtain a more comprehensive understanding of a drug’s safety profile and characterize its adverse reactions in clinical practice.[7] In this study, we conducted a systematic safety evaluation of sevelamer in actual clinical use by analyzing AEs reported in the FAERS database. The objective was to identify potential safety signals associated with sevelamer, thereby providing valuable reference for its safe use in clinical settings.

2. Data and methods

2.1. Data source

This study collected AE data from the U.S. FAERS database, a publicly available pharmacovigilance database, which has been publicly available free of charge since 2004 and is updated quarterly. As all records in this database have been completely de-identified, researchers could not access any information that could potentially identify individuals (including both direct and indirect identifiers) during data collection and analysis. Since the data were collected retrospectively for regulatory purposes and did not involve direct participation of human subjects, this study did not require approval from an ethics committee.

The database collects post-marketing adverse drug reactions and contains 7 categories of information: drug information (DRUG), patient outcomes information (OUTC), demographic and administrative information (DEMO), indications for drug administration (INDI), adverse drug reaction information (REAC), reported sources (RPSR), and drug therapy start/end dates (THER).[8] In this study, all AE data associated with sevelamer were downloaded from the FAERS database, covering the period from the first quarter of 2004 to the second quarter of 2025.

2.2. Data deduplication

All data were deduplicated following the recommendations of the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Fields extracted from the patient demographic and administrative information table (DEMO) included the unique FAERS report number (PRIMARYID), case number (CASEID), and FDA receipt date (FDA_DT). Records were sorted by CASEID, FDA_DT, and PRIMARYID in sequence. For records with identical CASEID, the observation with the latest FDA_DT was retained; and when both CASEID and FDA_DT were identical, the observation with the maximum PRIMARYID was retained. After deduplication, reports were excluded according to the CASEID values provided in the quarterly deletion files.

2.3. Standardization of drug names and adverse drug reactions

The search was conducted using the generic name “sevelamer” as the target drug. In accordance with the FAERS database documentation, records in the DEMO table sharing the same case number were deduplicated by retaining only the most recent report based on report date. Duplicate data were removed to ensure a clean and standardized dataset. Sevelamer involvement in AEs was classified into 4 categories: primary suspect, secondary suspect, concomitant, or interacting. This study focused exclusively on reports in which sevelamer was designated as the primary suspect drug.

AEs were coded using the preferred terms (PTs) from the version of the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA v25.0), with corresponding system organ classes (SOCs) listed to facilitate statistical analysis and categorization of the data. Additionally, We extracted clinical characteristics of sevelamer-related AE cases, including patient sex, age, reporter type, geographic region, report date, and outcome. 2.4. Signal detection algorithms and statistical methods

The 4-grid proportional imbalance method, known for its widespread application in pharmacovigilance studies, was employed for the disproportionality analysis investigating potential associations between sevelamer and all reported AEs.[9] Reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), information component (IC) in Bayesian confidence propagation neural network, and the empirical Bayesian geometric mean (EBGM) of multinomial gamma Poisson shrinker are the 4 main specific metrics computed using standard formulas for evaluating potential correlations involving sevelamer and AEs.[10,11] By combining these 4 algorithms, we leveraged their respective characteristics to maximize advantages, expand detection scope, and cross-validate results from multiple perspectives to reduce false positives. Additionally, by adjusting thresholds and variances, more rare potential AEs could be captured. Given that ROR offers advantages such as intuitive methodological transparency, ease of result interpretation, and mature application in the field of pharmacovigilance, this study designated it as the primary core analytical metric.[12] To address false positives resulting from multiple testing, the Benjamini–Hochberg method was applied to adjust two-sided P-values, controlling the false discovery rate (FDR) at a threshold of P.adj < .05, which served as the primary criterion for determining statistical significance and drawing conclusions.[13] Results from other algorithms were used as supplementary references to validate the consistency and robustness of the main signals. This analytical strategy helps clarify the research focus, enhances the readability of the results, and ensures the reliability of the conclusions.

All algorithms are based on comparing the observed frequency ratios between exposed and nonexposed populations using classic 2 × 2 contingency tables to analyze the association degree between the drug and AEs (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1). Specific formulas and threshold criteria are listed in Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2. A higher 95% confidence interval value indicates stronger signal intensity, implying a greater statistical likelihood of association between the target drug and the AE.[14] All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.0), and used ggplot2 for forest plot visualization.

3. Results

3.1. Basic characteristics of sevelamer-related AE reports

From the first quarter of 2004 to the second quarter of 2025, a total of 22,043,602 AE reports were extracted from the FAERS database. After excluding 3,625,624 duplicate records using a standardized deduplication algorithm, 18,417,978 unique reports remained. Among these, sevelamer was identified as the primary suspect drug in 6780 AE reports. In all sevelamer-related AE reports, gender distribution analysis based on the 38.73% of reports with documented sex (n = 2626) showed a higher proportion of male patients compared to female patients (20.66% vs 18.07%). Regarding age distribution, a substantial proportion of data (70.38%) lacked specific age information. Among the 29.62% of reports with documented age (n = 2008), the median age and interquartile range (IQR) for sevelamer-related AEs were 62.00 (IQR: 52.00–71.00) years. Further age analysis revealed that the 60 to 74 age group had the highest proportion of AEs (11.81%), followed by the 45 to 59 age group (8.23%). In terms of reporting sources, the majority of reports came from other health-professionals (62.60%), followed by consumers (15.03%) and physicians (11.45%). Among the reported countries, the majority of reports originated from the United States (82.71%). Regarding clinical outcomes, aside from unspecified serious AEs (39.01%), the most common outcome was hospitalization (35.62%), followed by death (18.78%). In addition, after excluding AE reports with uncertain, missing or unknown time of occurrence, a total of 596 reports with clearly documented time of onset were analyzed. Among reports with clear data, the median time and IQR for AE occurrence were 119.00 (14.00–476.50) days. Most events occurred more than 60 days after administration (n = 353, 30.46%), while 10.44% (n = 121) occurred within 7 days of starting sevelamer (Table 1). Finally, the year with the fewest reports was 2005 (63 reports), while the year with the most reports was 2014 (3417 reports). From 2013 to 2015, the number of AE reports reached a record high (Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 3).

3.2. Signal detection by SOC

This study analyzed the AEs of sevelamer and found that the AEs involved 24 SOCs. By combining 4 algorithms for analysis, positive signals were identified for certain SOCs. 3.3. Signal detection at the PT level

At the PT level, we applied 4 algorithms to analyze AE signals and assess adherence to various screening criteria, after excluding conditions related to “renal and urinary disorders, endocrine disorders, respiratory, thoracic and mediastinal disorders, vascular disorders, injury, poisoning and procedural complications” (which may be attributed to the progression of the underlying disease), resulting in 123 PTs (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content 4), of which 69 were selected for the main-text Table 3 based on higher reporting frequencies, stronger signal intensities, and clinical relevance, covering both documented events and potentially serious novel signals. Among these, the PT signals with higher report counts, stronger signals, and consistency with the drug label included: constipation, gastric or intestinal ulcer/hemorrhage/perforation/necrosis/ischemia, colitis ischemic, erosive esophagitis/gastritis, abdominal pain, dysphagia, nausea, vomiting, peritonitis, and pruritus. In addition to the AEs already mentioned in the drug label, this study also observed a notable incidence of the following AEs: aorto-esophageal fistula, diabetic gastroparesis, large intestinal stenosis, short-bowel syndrome, bezoar, diverticulum intestinal, gastric polyps, large intestine polyp, pale feces, diverticulum, diverticulum intestinal hemorrhagic, intestinal crystal deposits, complicated or perforated appendicitis, renal cyst infection, colonic or abdominal abscess, pseudomembranous colitis, diverticulitis, gangrene, pseudopolyp, mucosal ulceration or necrosis, skin fibrosis, and excessive granulation tissue. These findings warrant heightened attention.

4. Discussion

Post-marketing surveillance and monitoring AEs play a crucial role in ensuring medication safety and efficacy. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of sevelamer-related AEs reported to the FAERS database from the first quarter of 2004 to the second quarter of 2025. The research not only reconfirmed known safety information about sevelamer but also identified several novel potential risk signals, thereby providing more detailed and accurate evidence-based support for rational clinical use and risk management decision-making.

4.1. Demographic characteristics of sevelamer-related AEs

We further examined demographic characteristics of sevelamer AE reports and found that male patients (median age 62 years) experienced a higher proportion of AEs after approximately 60 days of treatment. From a gender perspective, the AE reporting rate was higher in males than in females, which aligns with findings from multiple studies. For instance, in a trial of sevelamer therapy in non-dialysis CKD patients, males accounted for 65% of participants, with a median age of approximately 62 years.[15] Similarly, research by Sartaj Alam et al also reported a higher proportion of male patients receiving sevelamer for hyperphosphatemia in CKD.[16] In summary, these observations may be attributed to the higher prevalence of male patients in CKD populations and their greater susceptibility to complications such as hyperphosphatemia, leading to increased sevelamer use among male patients. In terms of age stratification, the proportion of sevelamer‐related AEs was highest among patients aged 60 to 74 years (median age 62). The potential reasons for this observation include: first, The 60 to 74 age group has a larger population of individuals with CKD, leading to increased use of sevelamer. Data from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention indicate that the prevalence of CKD is significantly higher in individuals aged ≥ 65 years (38%) compared to those aged 45 to 64 (13%) and 18 to 44 (7%).[17] This aligns with findings by Claudia Yuste et al, who reported a mean age of 61 years in patients experiencing sevelamer crystal-induced gastrointestinal complications.[18] Second, physiological declines in organ function with advancing age – particularly hepatic and renal clearance – may reduce drug metabolism and excretion, thereby increasing the risk of AEs. Consequently, clinicians should conduct a comprehensive assessment of baseline physiological status in these patients and maintain vigilant AE monitoring throughout the treatment course. Our analysis also revealed that the median time to AE onset was 119 days; 30.46% (n = 353) of events occurred after 60 days of treatment, and 10.44% (n = 121) within the first 7 days. While most studies do not specify the exact timing of AEs, existing evidence suggests that sevelamer-associated AEs – particularly gastrointestinal reactions – are more common during the initial phase of treatment, with some patients developing improved tolerance over time.[19] These findings highlight the importance for clinicians to perform comprehensive baseline assessments and to maintain vigilant monitoring of AEs both during the initial treatment phase and beyond 60 days, enabling early identification, prevention, and management of adverse reactions and timely adjustment of the therapeutic regimen. Finally, from a geographical distribution perspective, the United States was the primary source of AE reports in this database. This predominance may be attributed to a robust regulatory framework mandating AE reporting by manufacturers and healthcare facilities, a mature pharmacovigilance infrastructure, and high public awareness of medication safety.[20] Together, these institutional and societal factors facilitate more comprehensive and timely collection of post-marketing safety data.

4.2. Gastrointestinal disorders: dominant signals and mechanistic insights

This study focused on signal detection at the SOC and PT levels for sevelamer-related AEs. The findings revealed significantly higher AE reporting rates and stronger signal intensities in the following categories: gastrointestinal disorders, metabolism and nutrition disorders, skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders, and infections and infestations. These results align with the safety profile described in the drug label and are consistent with multiple clinical studies. Specifically, numerous investigations have documented frequent gastrointestinal reactions in patients receiving long-term sevelamer treatment. For instance, research by James Todd et al reviewed 12 cases of sevelamer-associated gastrointestinal diseases characterized by abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, and hematochezia, along with endoscopic or surgical findings of gastrointestinal mucosal injury.[21] Claudia Yuste et al similarly reported multiple cases of sevelamer-induced gastrointestinal complications, including ulcers, mucosal injuries, and bleeding, suggesting these effects may be potentially linked to the drug’s crystalline properties, which may deposit in the gastrointestinal tract and possibly contribute to local inflammation and injury.[18] And our analysis similarly identified multiple AEs within the gastrointestinal disorders SOC that showed high reporting frequencies and strong signals, corroborating the drug label information. These included: constipation, gastric/intestinal ulcer/hemorrhage/perforation/necrosis/ischemia, ischemic colitis, erosive esophagitis/gastritis, abdominal pain, dysphagia, nausea, vomiting, peritonitis, and pruritus. Based on our findings, we observed a strong signal suggesting that gastrointestinal AEs are associated with sevelamer pharmacological mechanisms. In patients with chronic renal failure complicated by hyperphosphatemia, the high pill burden and the physical bulk of the polymer resin itself may directly contribute to mechanical gastrointestinal side effects, especially given that the crystalline structure of this polymeric compound may deposit in the gastrointestinal tract, potentially leading to local inflammation and injury.[18] These crystals may also embed and penetrate the gastrointestinal mucosa, possibly causing ulcers, inflammation, and perforation – effects that may be exacerbated in patients with compromised mucosal integrity (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease, intestinal infections, or ischemic bowel disease).[21] The significant PT signals for “bezoar” (n = 5, ROR = 17.74) and “crystal deposit intestine” (n = 138, ROR = 9151.49) identified in our study further support this hypothesis. Notably, intestinal crystal deposition exhibited an extremely strong signal, indicating a robust statistical association. However, given the limited number of reported cases, the absolute risk of these events is likely very low. Nevertheless, clinicians should remain attentive to this rare event in clinical practice, as early recognition may facilitate prompt diagnosis and appropriate management. In this context, it may be prudent for clinicians to consider endoscopic evaluation with biopsies in patients presenting with severe or unexplained gastrointestinal symptoms during sevelamer therapy. Additionally, proactively communicating with pathologists about the possibility of sevelamer crystal deposition could be helpful, as these crystals may be easily overlooked on routine histological examination unless specifically sought. At the same time, it is important to interpret these clinical suggestions with caution, given the limitations of our data source. As with all findings derived from spontaneous reporting data, these results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships, and further mechanistic studies are warranted to confirm these observations.

4.3. Gastrointestinal and infectious signals: insights into microecological mechanisms

Beyond direct mucosal injury, sevelamer may alter intestinal pH and microbial balance, potentially disrupting gastrointestinal function. Research indicates that bile acids and gut microbiota metabolites (e.g., indoles and phenolic compounds) play crucial roles in maintaining microbial homeostasis. By binding these substances, sevelamer could modify gut microbiota composition and function, thereby impacting intestinal health. Such alterations may manifest as impaired intestinal barrier function and gastrointestinal disturbances (e.g., constipation, diarrhea, or abdominal pain), while potentially increasing risks of opportunistic infections and inflammatory conditions like bacterial gastrointestinal infections, appendicitis, and intestinal abscesses.[22,23] This hypothesis shows strong concordance with our study findings. Within the “infections and infestations” SOC, we identified significant PT signals related to gastrointestinal and abdominal infections, including complicated appendicitis, colonic abscess, gastrointestinal bacterial infection, pseudomembranous colitis, perforated appendicitis, abdominal abscess, diverticulitis, peritonitis, and gangrene. Although these AEs were not mentioned in the drug label, their substantial signal strength warrants serious clinical attention. Furthermore, in gastrointestinal-related SOCs, our study detected several important new AE signals with high signal intensity that were not specified in the drug label, including aorto-esophageal fistula, diabetic gastroparesis, large intestinal stenosis, short-bowel syndrome, bezoar, intestinal diverticulum, gastric polyps, large intestine polyp, pale feces, diverticulum, and hemorrhagic intestinal diverticulum. Some of these severe AEs have been documented in previous studies, such as gastric polyps, large intestine polyps, and diabetic gastroparesis. Research has revealed pseudoinflammatory polyps during endoscopic examinations of sevelamer-induced gastrointestinal lesions, with diabetic patients appearing more susceptible to sevelamer-associated gastrointestinal complications.[18] Therefore, we recommend that clinicians should thoroughly evaluate patients’ gastrointestinal history (including prior ulcers, inflammatory bowel disease, intestinal obstruction, or surgical history) before initiating sevelamer therapy and maintain vigilant monitoring for abdominal pain, diarrhea, or constipation, with prompt dose adjustment or discontinuation if severe symptoms emerge.

4.4. AEs in other organ systems

Additionally, particular attention should be paid to the PT signals: pruritus, skin fibrosis, and excessive granulation tissue. Among these, pruritus showed a high reporting frequency (n = 932) and has been documented in both the drug label and studies. Research indicates pruritus is an established adverse effect of sevelamer,[24] with 3 potential underlying mechanisms: first, this may be associated with CKD-related pathological changes including accumulation of uremic toxins, peripheral neuropathy, immune dysregulation and alterations in endogenous opioid signaling pathways.[25] Second, this may be attributed to drug-induced allergic reactions, which involve activation of the immune system that subsequently leads to cutaneous pruritus and skin rash. third, this may result from local irritation induced by the drug, whereby sevelamer crystals may deposit in the gastrointestinal tract, triggering localized inflammation that subsequently affects systemic immune responses and ultimately leads to cutaneous pruritus. Although these observations support a causal link between sevelamer and pruritus, direct associations with skin fibrosis and excessive granulation tissue have not yet been established. We hypothesize these effects may occur through the following mechanisms: disruption of calcium–phosphate metabolism, soft tissue calcification and crystal deposition-induced mucosal injury triggering granulation tissue formation. Accordingly, during clinical administration of sevelamer, clinicians should monitor skin reactions, especially pruritus and rash. If severe symptoms occur, dose adjustment or switching to alternative medications should be considered.

4.5. Confounding by underlying diseases

A fundamental challenge in pharmacovigilance studies using spontaneous reporting databases is distinguishing AEs directly attributable to drug exposure from those resulting from the natural progression of underlying diseases. This confounding is particularly relevant to sevelamer, which is primarily indicated for patients with CKD or end-stage kidney disease complicated by calcium-phosphatemetabolism disorders, a population already characterized by profound immune dysregulation, systemic inflammation, and multi-organdysfunction.[26,27] Consequently, the interpretation of several AE signals identified in this study requires careful consideration of the confounding between disease and drug effects.

Infections serve as a typical example. Patients with advanced CKD exhibit an immunosuppressive state resulting from accumulated uremic toxins, impaired neutrophil and T-cell function, and compromised antigen presentation, all of which collectively increase susceptibility to bacterial infections.[27,28] Similarly, metabolic and nutritional disorders, including electrolyte imbalances and acid-base disturbances, are inherent features of progressive renal failure. Therefore, while the signals for infectious and metabolic AEs observed in this study are statistically significant, they may partially reflect the underlying immunosuppressive and metabolic milieu of the CKD population rather than being solely attributed to the pharmacological effects of sevelamer.

4.6. Summary and clinical recommendations

Given the above, there remains a significant knowledge gap regarding sevelamer-associated AEs, with current mechanistic explanations primarily based on theoretical hypotheses rather than conclusive evidence. To clarify definitive causal relationships, systematic studies integrating pharmacokinetic profiles and analysis of patient heterogeneity are required. Concurrently, clinicians should implement enhanced monitoring strategies and perform dynamic risk–benefit assessments during sevelamer treatment.

5. Limitations

This study has the following limitations: First, the FAERS database used is a passive surveillance system relying on spontaneous reports from worldwide sources, which introduces heterogeneity in reporting quality and completeness, potentially leading to selection bias and information bias. Notably, the high proportions of missing data for sex and age reflect a well-recognized limitation of spontaneous reporting systems such as FAERS, in which demographic information is often incompletely documented. Consequently, our descriptive findings regarding sex and age distributions should be interpreted with caution, as they are derived from a subset of reports with available data and may not be generalizable to the entire sevelamer-exposed population. Second, despite the large sample size, critical exposure information such as drug dosage, treatment duration, and concomitant medications is frequently missing, making precise incidence estimates infeasible. Finally, our risk signals are generated by data‐mining methods without long‐term follow‐up, so causal relationships between sevelamer and AEs cannot be definitively established. These limitations indicate that the current findings require further validation through prospective study designs.

6. Conclusion

By mining sevelamer AE reports in the FAERS database and applying multiple disproportionality algorithms at different levels of analysis, this study provides scientific evidence for the safety evaluation of sevelamer. The findings revealed that gastrointestinal disorders were particularly prevalent among sevelamer users, including constipation, gastric or intestinal ulcer/hemorrhage/perforation/necrosis/ischemia, colitis ischemic, erosive esophagitis/gastritis, abdominal pain, dysphagia, nausea, vomiting, peritonitis, and pruritus. Notably, we also identified several serious AEs not documented in the drug label, such as aorto-esophageal fistula, diabetic gastroparesis, large intestinal stenosis, short-bowel syndrome, bezoar, intestinal diverticulum, gastric polyps, large intestine polyp, pale feces, diverticulum, hemorrhagic intestinal diverticulum, skin fibrosis, and excessive granulation tissue. Although further research is required to establish definitive causal relationships, these findings provide significant value for future pharmacological studies and drug safety surveillance, while offering targeted guidance for clinical medication safety.
