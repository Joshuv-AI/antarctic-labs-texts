Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHow to cite this article: Attachaipanich T, Kaewboot K, Attachaipanich S. Author Response: Efficacy of Noninvasive Ventilation Compared with Invasive Mechanical Ventilation in Cardiogenic Shock: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis. Indian J Crit Care Med 2026;30(4):352–353.

Dear Editor,

We would like to thank Dr Yadav et al. for their thoughtful comments regarding our systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating the efficacy of noninvasive ventilation (NIV) compared with invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) in patients with cardiogenic shock.1 We appreciate their interest in our work and agree that careful interpretation of observational evidence in this complex population is essential. We address the concerns raised below.

First, we acknowledge that all six included studies were observational and, therefore, inherently subject to bias. We explicitly acknowledged the observational design and the potential for residual confounding in the manuscript. Our study aimed to synthesize the currently available evidence in a field where randomized controlled trials are lacking, thereby highlighting an important gap in the literature. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as demonstrating an association rather than a causal treatment effect, and further prospective studies are warranted.

Second, we acknowledge the potential for confounding by indication when evaluating ventilatory strategies in cardiogenic shock. In several included cohorts, patients treated with IMV were often more critically ill, with higher use of vasopressors, mechanical circulatory support, and coronary revascularization. These baseline differences likely reflect real-world clinical decision-making in which IMV is preferentially used in patients with more advanced shock or respiratory failure. To address this issue, we conducted sensitivity analyses restricted to studies using propensity-score adjustment, which demonstrated consistent associations between NIV use and lower in-hospital mortality. Nevertheless, residual confounding due to unmeasured differences in shock severity cannot be excluded.

The cardiogenic shock population is heterogeneous with respect to both etiology and severity, both of which significantly influence prognosis. Only two of the included studies reported baseline Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) cardiogenic shock classification, and approximately half of the patients in those studies were classified as SCAI stage C.2,3 Importantly, the limited availability of standardized shock staging across studies restricts the ability to fully account for differences in baseline shock severity. One study reported that NIV was more frequently used in patients with less severe cardiogenic shock compared with those treated with IMV. After stratification according to SCAI stage at 24 hours, multivariable-adjusted analysis demonstrated no significant difference in 60 days mortality between ventilation strategies across shock stages.3 In contrast, another propensity-score–matched study showed that NIV was associated with lower in-hospital mortality among patients with SCAI stage C shock but not among those with more advanced shock (SCAI stages D or E).2 These findings suggest that NIV may be applicable in carefully selected patients, particularly in earlier stages of cardiogenic shock. Nevertheless, because SCAI classification was not consistently reported across studies, residual confounding related to shock severity remains possible.

Third, we acknowledge the important concern regarding potential immortal time bias related to NIV failure and subsequent intubation. In the included studies, approximately 20–50% of patients initially treated with NIV eventually required escalation to IMV.2–4 These crossover patients were classified according to the definitions used in the original studies. We agree that time-varying exposure modeling would provide a more precise estimate of treatment effects. However, this requires individual patient-level data, which were not available in the included studies. We, therefore, acknowledge in the manuscript that pooled estimates may be influenced by this limitation and that future studies using time-dependent analytic approaches are warranted.

Fourth, we agree that statistical tests for funnel plot asymmetry have limited power when the number of included studies is small. In our analysis, funnel plots and Egger's test were performed primarily as exploratory assessments. Because only four to six studies were available for each outcome, the ability to reliably detect publication bias is limited. For this reason, we noted in the limitations section that these assessments should be interpreted cautiously.

Finally, we appreciate the authors' concern regarding the strength of the conclusions. We acknowledge that the available evidence is derived entirely from observational studies and, therefore, reflects associations rather than causal effects. In the manuscript, we emphasized that NIV may be most applicable in carefully selected patients, particularly those with earlier stages of cardiogenic shock, while recognizing that NIV failure and delayed intubation remain important clinical considerations. Accordingly, we concluded that further randomized trials or large prospective studies incorporating detailed risk stratification, including SCAI cardiogenic shock classification, are needed to better define the optimal ventilatory strategy in this population.

We appreciate the opportunity to clarify these methodological considerations. Our study was intended to summarize the currently available evidence and highlight the need for high-quality prospective research to determine the role of NIV in cardiogenic shock.
