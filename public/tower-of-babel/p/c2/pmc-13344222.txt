Co‐occurring opioid and stimulant use disorder and the relationship to severe maternal morbidity
Authors: Tucker E. Doiron, Noor Al‐Hammadi, Alexandra R. Schroeder, Niraj R. Chavan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo evaluate the impact of the use of opioids, stimulants, or both on the rates of various severe maternal morbidity (SMM) events during inpatient delivery‐related hospitalizations among patients with substance use disorder (SUD).MethodsWe performed a cross‐sectional analysis of inpatient pregnancy hospitalizations from the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) National Inpatient Sample (NIS) from 2016 to 2022. Hospital and core files were linked and cross‐referenced for demographic information. International classification of diseases (ICD) 10 codes were used to identify patients with SUD who had an SMM event during a delivery hospitalization. SMM was defined as a composite of individual maternal morbidity diagnoses, as characterized by the centers for disease control (CDC). Survey‐weighted multivariable logistic regression models estimated the adjusted odds of SMM among delivery hospitalizations of patients with opioid use disorder (OUD), stimulant use disorder (StUD), or co‐occurring OUD and StUD, using delivery hospitalizations of patients without any SUD as the reference group. Models adjusted for maternal age, race/ethnicity, primary insurance payer, household income quartile, hospital region, hospital location and teaching status, hospital bed size, cohort year, and Elixhauser comorbidity score. Prevalence of composite SMM and individual SMM events was compared across exposure groups using Rao–Scott chi‐square tests.ResultsOf the 4,804,219 delivery‐related inpatient pregnancy hospitalizations over the study period, 45,671 admissions were identified as having SUD with either opioids (28,464), stimulants (13,920), or both (3287). There was an increasing trend of StUD from 2016 to 2021 (p < 0.0001). Overall rates of SMM between 2016 and 2022 were significantly higher among pregnancy hospitalizations for those with co‐occurring SUD (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 5.12; 95% confidence interval [CI], 4.48–5.84), followed by StUD (aOR, 3.84; 95% CI, 3.578–4.13) and OUD (aOR, 2.23; 95% CI, 2.09–2.38), compared with delivery hospitalizations of patients without any SUD. There were significant differences between the rates of individual SMM events between those with StUD, OUD, and those with co‐occurring use disorder at all time points.ConclusionPregnant people with co‐occurring OUD and StUD are at a significantly increased risk of SMM overall, as well as risk of certain individual SMM events as compared to those with OUD or StUD alone.

INTRODUCTION

Severe maternal morbidity (SMM) events are defined by the CDC as unexpected outcomes during delivery hospitalizations that result in substantial consequences to maternal health, encompassing 21 indicators across cardiovascular complications, severe systemic complications, and morbidities requiring emergency procedures [1]. In the United States, the prevalence of SMM events has been increasing over the past several years, which has effects on both the individual patient and their family, along with the healthcare system and national healthcare costs [1]. Those without substance use disorder (SUD) have rates of SMM events around 1.99% versus 3.14% in those with SUD [2]. SMM events are often precursors or coincide with maternal mortality, and rates of maternal mortality have been steadily increasing in the United States [3].

A total of 16.5% of the population in the United States age 12 or older met criteria for having SUD in 2021 [4]. A national survey in the United States in 2020 revealed that approximately 8% of pregnant people reported using illicit drugs [5]. In addition, SUD in pregnancy has been associated with adverse outcomes for both pregnant people and their neonates [6]. Understanding the risk factors for SMM is crucial for decreasing the rates of these events among vulnerable patient populations, such as pregnant patients with SUD—an at‐risk cohort identified as having a higher risk for experiencing an SMM event [7].

Of note, women with opioid use disorder (OUD) have been shown to have higher rates of SMM events [8]. However, over the past decade, exclusive opioid use is becoming rarer and those with OUD are more likely to be concurrently using another substance illicitly such as stimulants (e.g., concurrent OUD and methamphetamine use disorder) [9]. Given the increasing prevalence of stimulant use in general as well as stimulant use in the perinatal context [5], elucidating the differential impact of opioid and stimulant use versus co‐occurring stimulant and opioid use on SMM is critical for risk stratification and patient counseling.

The objective of this study was to evaluate the association between OUD, stimulant use disorder (StUD), or both co‐occurring OUD and StUD on the rates of various SMM events during inpatient delivery‐related hospitalizations, using delivery hospitalizations of patients without any SUD as the comparator. We hypothesized that co‐occurring SUD would be associated with higher rates of SMM overall as well as higher rates for individual SMM events as compared to those with StUD or OUD alone among pregnant people during their inpatient delivery hospitalizations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We performed a cross‐sectional analysis of the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) National Inpatient Sample (NIS), the largest publicly available all‐payor database released yearly in the United States. The HCUP‐NIS provides national estimates of inpatient utilization, access, cost, quality, and outcomes with diagnosis and procedure codes with a weighted evaluation of 35 million hospitalizations annually [10]. This study used the 2016 through 2022 NIS datasets. The years of study were chosen after the transition from ICD‐9‐CM to ICD‐10‐CM and ICD‐10‐procedure coding system (PCS) in order to avoid inaccuracies in the conversion between coding standards. Hospital admissions for pregnant patients were identified using ICD‐10‐CM diagnosis and ICD‐10‐PCS procedure codes supplemented by Diagnosis Related Group (DRG) codes to define delivery hospitalizations (see the Appendix in the Supporting Information). Consistent with HCUP‐NIS data limitations, intensive care unit (ICU) admission was not used to define SMM or as a covariate. The study cohort was limited to pregnant patients who had a DRG code indicating delivery during that admission; therefore, nondelivery admissions were excluded. Delivery hospitalizations were categorized into three groups: those among patients with OUD, StUD, or co‐occurring OUD and StUD (see the Appendix in the Supporting Information). These codes were validated from both a technical aspect for format and structure as well as from a clinical aspect for medical accuracy. The core and hospital databases were linked based on the availability of data; therefore, patient data extracted from the core database were required to have corresponding data in the linked hospital database. ICD‐10‐CM, ICD‐10‐PCS, and DRG code lists used to define pregnancy and delivery hospitalizations, SUD exposure, and SMM outcomes were adapted from prior NIS‐based analyses and CDC surveillance algorithms; a complete code list with sources is provided in the Appendix in the Supporting Information.

Our primary outcome of interest was SMM—defined as a composite, comprising a list of 21 individual SMM events, as defined by the CDC [1]. A complete list of the 21 SMM indicators along with the ICD‐10‐CM and ICD‐10‐PCS codes pertaining to these can be found in the Appendix in the Supporting Information. SMM rates among delivery hospitalizations for the whole sample and within each exposure level were computed and analyzed by age, race, and ethnicity (White, Black, Hispanic, none of the above, and missing), insurance payer (government, private, other, and missing), median household income for patient's ZIP code, and year of the cohort. The “other” category for race included Asian or Pacific Islander, Native American, and none of the above. The “other” category for insurance payer included Workers' Compensation, TRICARE (formerly CHAMPUS), CHAMPVA, Title V, other government programs, self‐pay, and no charge.

Hospital characteristics, including hospital region, urban–rural location, bed size, and teaching status, were also considered in this analysis. The hospital region was defined by the US Census Bureau (Northeast, Midwest, South, and West) [11]. Hospitals in areas classified by Core Based Statistical Area (CBSA) as “metropolitan” (population ≥50,000) were considered urban, while hospitals in areas classified as “micropolitan” (population 10,000–49,999) or “rural” (<10,000) were classified as rural [10]. Hospital bed size was defined by the number of short‐term or acute beds in a hospital and classification into small, medium, or large was based on whether the hospital was rural, urban non‐teaching, or urban teaching [10]. Rural hospitals were considered small with 1–49 beds, medium with 50–99 beds, and large with 100 beds or greater. Urban non‐teaching hospitals were classified small with 1–99 beds, medium with 100–199 beds, and large with 200 or greater beds. Urban teaching hospitals were classified as small with 1–299 beds, medium with 300–499 beds, and large with 500 beds or greater [10]. The teaching status of hospitals was defined as having an AMA‐approved residency program or having a membership in the Council of Teaching Hospitals [10].

All analyses incorporated the complex survey design of the HCUP‐NIS, accounting for stratification (HOSP_NIS_STRATUM, with NIS_STRATUM used for years prior to 2018), clustering at the hospital level (HOSP_NIS), and discharge‐level weights (DISCWT, derived from the American Hospital Association universe), per HCUP guidance, to produce nationally representative estimates. Weighted frequencies and percentages of baseline characteristics by exposure group (Table S1) were estimated using PROC SURVEYFREQ, with Rao–Scott chi‐square tests used for comparisons. SMM rates by exposure group and year (Table S2) and by SMM indicator (Figure 3, Table S3) were unweighted and estimated using PROC SURVEYFREQ. Multivariable survey‐weighted logistic regression (PROC SURVEYLOGISTIC) was used to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for composite SMM, with delivery hospitalizations of patients without any SUD as the reference group, comparing other SUD, OUD only, StUD only, and co‐occurring OUD + StUD. Models adjusted for maternal age, race/ethnicity, primary insurance payer, household income quartile, and cohort year. In keeping with HCUP‐AHRQ analytic conventions, the CONSORT flow diagram (Figure S1) presents unweighted record counts to document analytic sample construction. All descriptive estimates (Table 1, Figures 1 and 3, Tables S2 and S3) and multivariable regression analyses (Table 2) incorporate the NIS complex survey design, including discharge weights (DISCWT), stratification (HOSP_NIS_STRATUM/NIS_STRATUM), and clustering (HOSP_NIS) at the hospital level, to produce nationally representative estimates. All analyses were performed in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.).

The NIS database is de‐identified and the study received nonhuman subjects research (non‐HSR) exemption by the institutional review board (IRB). Study statistician had access to the de‐identified database and coauthors had access to the aggregate data with Data User Agreements from the HCUP. Adjustment for established comorbidity indices (Elixhauser; the obstetric comorbidity index of Leonard [12]) was considered but not incorporated into the primary multivariable models. Many of the conditions captured by these indices (e.g., mental health disorders, hepatitis C, HIV, hypertension, cardiovascular and renal complications, anemia) are recognized sequelae or correlates of active SUD rather than purely independent pre‐existing comorbidities. In cross‐sectional administrative data, these conditions cannot be reliably distinguished as confounders versus mediators on the SUD—SMM causal pathway, and their inclusion as covariates would risk overadjustment bias and underestimation of the SUD–SMM association.

RESULTS

Of the 5,095,464 pregnant patients admitted between 2016 and 2022, 4,804,219 had DRG codes indicating delivery hospitalizations. Of these, 45,671 hospitalizations were identified as among patients with SUD involving opioids (28,464; 62.32%), stimulants (13,920; 30.48%), or both (3287; 7.20%). Baseline characteristics of the study sample can be seen in Table 1; our sample weighted to the population of the United States is shown in Table S1. Delivery hospitalizations of patients without any SUD (n = 4,515,482) served as the reference group for multivariable regression analyses, with hospitalizations involving other SUD diagnoses (n = 243,066) included as a separate comparison category (Table 2; Figure S1).

The majority of patients were 20–30 years old, white, and had government insurance. Inpatient delivery hospitalizations among patients with OUD were more likely to occur among individuals that self‐identified as White (p < 0.0001) and were located in the South (p < 0.0001). On the other hand, inpatient delivery hospitalizations among those with StUD were most likely to occur among individuals in the West (p < 0.0001). Most inpatient delivery hospitalizations across all 3 types of SUD occurred in urban teaching hospitals (p < 0.0001).

When SMM rates were investigated among patients with SUD by type of substance (Figure 1/Table S1), there were statistically significant differences in the rates of SMM between those who used stimulants, opioids, and both at all time periods evaluated. Most recently in 2022, those with co‐occurring use had SMM rates of 9.84% (58/589) versus rates of 8.95% (181/2022) and 4.47% (156/3493) in those with stimulants and OUD, respectively (p < 0.001).

Table 2 represents data from multivariable regression analyses comparing the likelihood of SMM based on the type of substance used. Compared with inpatient hospitalizations without any SUD, those with other SUD (besides opioids or stimulants) had higher odds of experiencing an SMM event (aOR, 1.21; 95% CI, 1.17–1.25). Inpatient hospitalizations for those with OUD alone had an aOR of 2.23 (95% CI, 2.09–2.38), while those with StUD had an aOR of 3.84 (95% CI, 3.58–4.13) of composite SMM events. The highest risk for SMM was noted among inpatient admissions from pregnancies with co‐occurring opioid and stimulant use (aOR, 5.12; 95% CI, 4.48–5.84) (Figure 2). From the standpoint of sociodemographic and clinical predictors of SMM in this unique cohort of those who use both stimulants and opioids—the greatest risk for a composite SMM event was noted among pregnancies occurring in patients greater than 40 years of age (aOR, 1.59; 95% CI, 1.52–1.65), those that self‐identified as non‐Hispanic or Black (aOR, 1.97; 95% CI, 1.93–2.01), with government insurance (aOR 1.19 95% CI: 1.17–1.21), and in the bottom quartile of income (aOR, 1.17; 95% CI, 1.15–1.20).

Severe maternal morbidity with substance use disorder (SUD) by substance type compared to those without SUD. aOR, adjusted odds ratio; CI, confidence interval.

Rates of SMM events were further analyzed by SMM event type (Figure 3/Table S3). SMM events with zero or near‐zero rates of occurrence were excluded (Amniotic fluid embolism, severe anesthesia complication, sickle cell disease with crisis, temporary tracheostomy, conversion of cardiac arrhythmia, aneurysm, acute myocardial infarction, heart failure/arrest during surgery). Patients with co‐occurring stimulant and OUD had a higher proportion of SMM overall, in comparison to pregnancies with stimulant or opioid use alone (9.85% vs. 8.95% vs. 4.47%, p < 0.0001) as well as higher proportion of individual SMM events including blood transfusion (6.11% vs. 5.64% vs. 2.58%, p < 0.0001), acute renal failure (2.72% vs. 1.38% vs. 0.66%, p < 0.0001), sepsis (1.70% vs. 0.69%, vs. 0.49%, p < 0.0001), acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) (1.36% vs. 1.04% vs. 0.57%, p < 0.0001), shock (1.02% vs. 0.25% vs. 0.29%, p < 0.0001), and ventilation (0.68% vs. 0.59% vs. 0.31%, p < 0.0001).

DISCUSSION

In this nationally representative study, we aimed to evaluate the rates of SMM as a composite as well as rates for individual SMM events among inpatient pregnancy hospitalizations in patients with OUD, StUD, and co‐occurring OUD and StUD. We found that co‐occurring SUD with both opioids and stimulants was associated with a statistically significant increase in the rates of SMM overall as well as for several individual SMM events, including blood transfusion, acute renal failure, sepsis, ARDS, shock, and ventilation when compared to rates of SMM with OUD or StUD alone. Between 2016 and 2021, there was a statistically significant increase in the overall prevalence of both patients with StUD and those with co‐occurring stimulant and OUD; however, between 2021 and 2022, there was a statistically significant drop in all three groups (Table 1). The year 2020, when compared to the other years evaluated, had the highest rates of composite SMM among all groups. This could possibly be attributed to the COVID‐19 pandemic, which had a known effect on maternal mortality rates and pregnancy outcomes [13].

This study and its implications are important because SMM events are closely linked to maternal mortality given their similar risk factors [1]. Between 2019 and 2021, maternal mortality rates in the United States increased despite an overall decrease in maternal mortality worldwide [14]. In 2021, the maternal mortality rate was 32.9 deaths per 100,000 live births, compared to 23.8 in 2020 and 20.1 in 2019 [15]. However, most recently in 2023, the maternal mortality rate was 18.6 deaths per 100,000 live births; although decreasing, the rate remains higher than in other high‐income countries [16]. Our study focused on SMM rather than maternal mortality because in‐hospital maternal death is a rare outcome with limited statistical power in delivery‐hospitalization analyses, and the HCUP‐NIS does not capture out‐of‐hospital maternal deaths or deaths during readmission, making maternal mortality estimates incomplete in this data source. However, since SMM events often precede maternal mortality, recognizing vulnerable groups for SMM events could play an important role in decreasing rates of maternal mortality in the United States.

The opioid epidemic in the United States and its impact on public health outcomes are well documented [9]. Outside of pregnancy, co‐occurring use with opioids and cocaine has been shown to have higher rates of overdose [17]. The focused efforts by policymakers and physicians on decreasing OUD have overlooked that most people with OUD also have poly‐SUD [9]. In fact, a survey of 15,741 patients entering treatment for OUD found that those with OUD were more likely to have poly‐SUD than OUD alone in their sample size with nearly the entire sample endorsing at least one other drug used in addition to opioids in the past month [9].

Approximately 8% of pregnant people in a 2020 national survey reported using illicit drugs [5]; the lower prevalence of SUD identified in our group (approximately 1%) likely reflects the difference between self‐reported drug use in a community survey versus clinically diagnosed SUD recorded via ICD‐10‐CM codes during a delivery hospitalization, the latter of which is recognized to underestimate true SUD prevalence due to under‐screening, stigma, and incomplete documentation. A previous evaluation of the NIS from 2004 to 2015 evaluated rates of amphetamine and opioid use at the time of delivery admissions and reported a disproportionate increase in rates of amphetamine and opioid use in pregnant people suggesting a worsening public health crisis [18]. A review of polysubstance use among pregnant patients with OUD showed that polysubstance use increased from 60.5% to 64.1% from 2007 to 2016 [19]. A subsequent review of the NIS from 1998 to 2011 revealed that deliveries associated with OUD alone had higher odds of a maternal death during hospitalization; however, it did not evaluate StUD or use disorder of both opioids and stimulants [20]. The aforementioned reviews provide valuable insight into the worsening rates of opioid, stimulant, and polysubstance use; however, they do not comment on the rates of adverse maternal outcomes specifically in relation to the type of substance use. Describing and identifying this emerging problem is an important first step in recognizing the complexities of complications that may arise in this patient population.

Due to the national trend of increasing prevalence of co‐occurring SUD in the pregnant population [9, 19], it is important to evaluate its impact on pregnant patients and peripartum outcomes as compared to those with opioid or stimulant use alone. A single study evaluating rates of SMM in the state of Utah reported that pregnant patients with co‐occurring opioid and methamphetamine use had an increased rate of SMM compared to individual substance use alone [21]. Our study adds to these findings on the influence of co‐occurring stimulant and opioid SUD on SMM events across the country by utilizing a national dataset rather than data from a single state. Therefore, our work offers critical information that can assist providers with patient counseling in regard to patient outcomes, as it pertains to SMM events in the setting of co‐occurring SUDs.

It is important to recognize the limitations of our study. Our sample population was taken from a pre‐existing national database. In a database such as this, data across time cannot be accounted for; therefore, it is possible that a patient may be accounted for more than once; however, given the large sample size, we anticipate the effect, if any, is small. We also cannot account for errors or inaccuracies regarding coding at the institutional level. We were also unable to confirm the presence, absence, or severity of SUD by substance type with records, urine toxicology, or clinical scenarios. Therefore, ICD‐10 codes limit our ability to distinguish between true SUD versus sporadic substance use. It is possible that SMM events were missed or not entered as a diagnosis code in both the SUD group and the non‐SUD group. The HCUP‐NIS also does not capture participation in substance use rehabilitation programs, medication for OUD, or other outpatient treatment exposures, which limits our ability to compare peripartum outcomes between treated and untreated patients with SUD. Since our data was limited to delivery hospitalizations, SMM events that occurred after discharge in both groups were not accounted for.

Adjustment for clinical comorbidity indices (e.g., Elixhauser; the obstetric comorbidity index of Leonard [12]) was considered but not incorporated into the primary models, as mentioned above. Nevertheless, residual confounding from unmeasured pre‐existing comorbidities, detailed clinical factors not captured in administrative data, and social and environmental determinants of both SUD and SMM remains possible. Several individual SMM indicators had small event counts (eclampsia, puerperal cerebrovascular disorder, cardiac arrest/ventricular fibrillation) (Table S2); estimates for these indicators should be interpreted with caution.

While our study has its limitations, there are several strengths that are important to recognize. Given that these data were taken from the NIS, the sample size is large and is likely representative of the population as a whole. Although these data are interpreted in the setting of inpatient hospitalizations and not patients themselves, we narrowed down our population to delivery hospitalizations in order to avoid capturing a patient's pregnancy more than once. There are little data evaluating co‐occurring SUD and its effects on SMM; therefore, we believe this study provides a unique perspective in the literature given that both of these issues—that is, OUD and StUD—occurring in isolation and combination, are becoming more prevalent in the United States.

Our study provides evidence that people with co‐occurring SUD of both opioids and stimulants during pregnancy are at an increased risk for SMM overall, along with an increased risk for several individual SMM events compared to those with OUD or StUD alone. SMM events are complex and multifactorial; however, they are critical to consider when discussing maternal mortality in an at‐risk population.

CONCLUSION

It is crucial for providers caring for pregnant people with SUD to recognize co‐occurring SUD, specifically with opioid and stimulants; as it is a significant risk factor for SMM, with a higher magnitude of risk as compared to OUD or StUD alone. Specific actions include: (a) universal validated screening for SUD, (b) prompt evidence‐based treatment, (c) coordinated multidisciplinary care, (d) attention to social determinants of health, and (e) tailored counseling that frames co‐occurring use disorder as a higher‐risk requiring intensified surveillance and individualized peripartum planning.

Our research has shown that pregnancies complicated by co‐occurring StUD and OUD are at a substantially greater risk for composite SMM events when accounting for relevant sociodemographic factors. Our analysis provides evidence to demonstrate that co‐occurring SUD is an independent risk factor for SMM, which presents a higher risk profile than individual SUD alone and calls for increased awareness among clinicians, improved screening, and recognition of the heightened risks that patients with poly‐SUD may face.
