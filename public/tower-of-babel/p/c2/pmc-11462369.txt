Predicting MHD mixed convection in a semicircular cavity with hybrid nanofluids using AI
Authors: Prosenjit Das, Mohammad Arif Hasan Mamun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a numerical analysis of magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) mixed convection in a semicircular enclosure containing a rotating inner cylinder and filled with nanofluids and hybrid nanofluids. The investigation explores the effects of Al2O3-TiO2-SWCNT-water hybrid nanofluids with varying nanoparticle compositions, as well as Al2O3-water, TiO2-water, and SWCNT-water nanofluids. The analysis includes the development of an artificial neural network (ANN) model to predict outcomes, achieving 97.34 % accuracy in training and 97.41 % in testing for the average Nusselt number. The study examines the impact of Reynolds number (Re), Richardson number (Ri), Hartmann number (Ha), cylinder rotation speed (Ω), cylinder size, and nanoparticle volume fraction (φ) on heat transfer and fluid flow. Key findings include a 6.98 % increase in heat transfer for SWCNT-water nanofluid from Ri = 1 to Ri = 10, a reduction in heat transfer with higher Hartmann numbers, and a significant 21.12 % enhancement when cylinder speed increases to Ω = 10 compared to a stationary cylinder. Larger cylinder sizes also improve convective heat transfer, with a 66.14 % increase for SWCNT-water nanofluid. Additionally, higher concentrations of SWCNT and Al2O3 in hybrid nanofluids enhance heat transfer performance.Keywords: Artificial neural network, MHD mixed convection, Semicircular cavity, Hybrid nanofluid

Introduction

In today's fast-evolving tech landscape, AI (Artificial Intelligence) is essential in modern engineering, enabling cross-field collaboration. Its applications span microbiology, IoT, robotics, computer vision, health monitoring, natural language understanding, and more [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Machine learning techniques like ANNs, deep learning, and reinforcement learning are advancing fluid dynamics by improving modeling, prediction, and optimization. These algorithms optimize configurations to meet objectives such as minimizing drag or enhancing heat transfer, automating design exploration and boosting performance in applications like heat exchangers, HVAC, and environmental monitoring [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. AI algorithms, especially ANN and PINN [11,12], accelerate traditional CFD simulations, integrating data with physics for efficient and cost-effective solutions, marking a significant leap forward in engineering.

Mixed convection is critical in engineering fields like coating, MEMS, nuclear reactor cooling, electronics cooling, natural gas storage, food storage, journal bearing lubrication, solar collectors, and more [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. Lid-driven systems, spinning cylinders, and magnetohydrodynamic forces exert external influences on heat transfer in these applications. Numerous scholars have explored the impact of diverse parameters on the heat transfer characteristics of this mixed convection phenomenon, aiming to enhance the heat transfer performance. Çolak et al. [22] used the Finite Volume Method to study mixed convection in a lid-driven cavity with a porous block serving as a heat source, finding that the Darcy number significantly influences vortex formation and overall heat transfer. Prakash et al. [23] investigated mixed convection with surface radiation in an air-filled enclosure, providing insights into temperature distributions and Nusselt numbers. Shahid et al. [24] analyzed mixed convection in a triangular cavity using the lattice Boltzmann method, revealing the impact of lid movement direction on heat transfer. Oudina et al. [25] studied mixed convection in a trapezoidal cavity with discrete heat sources, demonstrating that variations in the Richardson and Reynolds numbers can significantly enhance heat transfer.

In cases with electrically conductive fluids, the magnetic field becomes a vital external force, influencing both thermal conductivity and viscosity. The interplay between buoyancy-driven flow and electromagnetic forces is essential in engineering applications such as electrochemical generation of elemental bromine in porous electrodes [26], buoyant gas-liquid flows in packed beds [27], fire-safe intumescent paints [28], and reactive polymer flows in porous media [29]. Moreau's work on magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) provides a foundational understanding of these phenomena and their technological implications [30]. Recent research has focused on how Lorentz forces, induced by magnetic fields, affect heat transfer efficiency in cavity flows [31,32]. For example, Akram et al. [33] explored MHD mixed convection in a chamfered square enclosure filled with water, examining the effects of magnetic field orientation on flow and heat transfer. They found that changes in magnetic field strength and inclination angle significantly impacted heat transfer, with higher Hartmann numbers generally reducing Nusselt numbers. Maya et al. [34] studied a lid-driven cavity with a rectangular heat source under a magnetic field, highlighting the Hartmann number's role in modifying flow patterns and heat transfer rates. Xiong et al. [35] extended these findings by investigating MHD fluid behavior in triangular lid-driven cavities, examining the combined effects of Hartmann, Richardson, and Reynolds numbers on convective heat transfer. Nadeem et al. [36] analyzed MHD mixed convection in wavy square cavities with heated corrugated rods, revealing that variations in Hartmann number and undulation frequency significantly alter temperature and flow patterns. Additionally, Mehmood et al. [37,38] have explored the effect of Lorentz force on heat and mass transfer behavior.

Researchers are exploring nanofluids to enhance performance across various applications such as solar energy, machining, electronics cooling, nuclear systems, industrial cooling, transformer oil, transportation, heat exchangers, and refrigeration [[39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]]. Their high thermal conductivity and unique nanoparticle motion lead to superior heat transfer performance compared to traditional fluids. Research by Gibanov et al. [45] on Al2O3-water nanofluids in a cavity with a semicircular heat source highlighted the combined effects of nanoparticle addition and magnetic fields in enhancing energy removal. Hossain et al. [46] studied carbon nanotube nanofluids in cavities with semi-circular heaters, showing the stability and efficiency of oil-based nanofluids at elevated temperatures. Iftikhar et al. [47] examined entropy generation and energy transport in a non-Newtonian fluid within a square cavity, focusing on bi-viscosity effects. Santhosh et al. [48] developed a model for MHD mixed convection in a porous cavity with Al2O3-water and Cu-water nanofluids, finding increased velocity and heat transfer with higher nanoparticle volume fractions—6.96 % and 6.40 % higher heat transfer rates for Cu-water and Al2O3-water, respectively. Additionally, Mehmood et al. [49,50] and other researchers, including Rana et al. [51], Nayak et al. [52], Tabassum et al. [53], Reddy et al. [[54], [55], [56], [57]], and Meenakumari et al. [58], have investigated nanofluid behavior in various geometrical configurations.

Recently hybrid nanofluids which combine different types of nanoparticles, have shown further heat transfer enhancements. Hayat et al. [59] demonstrated that hybrid nanofluids outperform traditional nanofluids in thermal performance. Korei et al. [60] explored magneto-convection in cavities filled with Al2O3-Cu/water hybrid nanofluid, finding that specific compositions optimize heat transfer and entropy production. Job et al. [61] and Jiang et al. [62] extended these investigations to complex geometries, revealing that factors such as magnetic field orientation, nanoparticle size, and flow dynamics significantly affect heat transfer. Jamshed et al. [63] studied heat transfer and MHD convection in a porous medium with MWCNT/CMC nanofluid, noting that higher Richardson numbers and porosity increased Nusselt numbers. Mandal et al. [64] analyzed MHD convection in a W-shaped cavity with hybrid nanofluid, comparing various factors to a square enclosure. Hussain et al. [65] examined entropy generation and MHD convection in an elbow-shaped cavity with rotating blocks, finding enhanced heat transfer with increased power law index, clockwise rotation, and higher aspect ratios.

Researchers are increasingly using Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) to reduce costs in experiments and simulations. Nahak et al. [66] used ANN to validate simulations of mixed convection in a triangular enclosure. Cho et al. [67] built an ANN model to predict effects on 3D mixed convection, while Filali et al. [68] used ANN for thermal predictions in a lid-driven square enclosure. Cho et al. [69] applied ANN to heat transfer predictions with an inclined elliptical cylinder, and Hussain et al. [70] used ANN to analyze radiation effects on mixed convection in a circular cylinder.

Despite extensive research on mixed convection and MHD flows in various geometries, there is a notable gap in studies focusing on semi-circular cavities, particularly those containing rotating cylinders and influenced by magnetic fields. Most existing research has explored basic nanofluids, with limited investigations into hybrid nanofluids in semi-circular geometries [[71], [72], [73], [74]]. Additionally, the combined effects of MHD, mixed convection, and hybrid nanofluids in this particular geometry were not explored previously. Insipred by the gap in the existing literature, this research aims to investigate the influence of hybrid nanofluids, nanofluids, and magnetic field on heat transfer in a semi-circular cavity containing an inner rotating cylinder. In addition to numerical simulations, a novel ANN model is developed based on the obtained simulation data. This investigation explores the impact of various parameters such as Reynolds number, Hartmann number, Richardson number, angular rotational speed of the cylinder, and cylinder size on heat transfer within the geometric framework. Utilizing machine learning, precise predictions are achieved with minimal computational resources.

Problem formulation

In this analysis, we have explored a configuration featuring a semicircular cavity of diameter H filled with different types of nanofluid and hybrid nanofluid. The cavity contains a central cylindrical obstacle that is rotating in a clockwise direction. As depicted in Fig. 1, the upper flat wall has a length of H and is maintained at a higher temperature Th. Both the lower curved wall and the centrally positioned cylindrical obstacle are maintained at a lower temperature Tc. The cylinder is rotating at an angular rotational speed Ω. The diameter of the cylinder is D (D = 0.2H) and the radius of the cylinder is r (r = 0.1H). The point (0.5,0.25) is the center of the cylinder. Gravity exerts a downward force in this setup, with the upper flat wall considered stationary. The system involves 2D steady state laminar mixed convection flow and heat transfer, influenced by varying temperature gradients and the rotational motion of the cylinder wall. For this analysis, we're assuming the fluid's thermal properties are constant. However, we account for density changes due to temperature variations using the Boussinesq approximation within the buoyancy term. Additionally, we assume the presence of a uniform vertical magnetic field of strength B0, neglecting any induced magnetic field. Solution methodology

A commercial finite element solver “COMSOL Multiphysics 6.0” is utilized for solving the governing equations under predefined boundary conditions. The finite element method (FEM) is used to divide the computational domain into smaller non-overlapping areas. The finite element formulation based on the Galerkin procedure approximates each flow variable with interpolation functions. Zienkiewicz and Taylor [82] gave a comprehensive account detailing the execution of this methodology. Lagrange finite elements of different orders are employed to discretize velocity components, temperature, and concentration within the framework. Utilizing a non-linear parametric solution approach, the numerical solution for the governing equations is found. This approach will result in substantially faster convergence than alternative methods. A relative tolerance of 10−6 is adopted for the error criterion. The non-linear equations are iteratively solved employing Broyden's method using LU-decomposition technique. An LU-decomposition preconditioner is used for this purpose. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the mesh of the computational domain consists of both quadrilateral and triangular elements. Mesh resolution is finely tuned to eliminate the necessity to stabilize convective components in momentum equations.

Artificial neural network

Mathematical modeling of ANN

Unlike traditional approaches, ANNs do not rely on task-specific rules of programming; instead, they learn to perform tasks by using examples of a given system. ANN architecture consists of three main layers: the input layer, hidden layer(s), and output layer. These layers can be visualized as a network of interconnected neurons/nodes, depicted as balls in Fig. 6. The details of ANN structure and how it works were well documented in these works [[83], [84], [85], [86], [87], [88], [89], [90]]. In the context of this investigation, the input dataset contains eight distinct features (Re, Ri, Ha, Ω, ϕSWCNT, ϕTiO2, ϕAl2O3, and r/H) and is derived from numerical simulations. The training phase of the network involves predicting an output based on input data. The output is influenced by the biases, weights, and transfer functions. The initialization of the model involves setting values (often randomly) for parameters such as biases (bj) and weights (wj). At first, a set of input data is processed through the network to generate predictions. The next steps involve a comparison of the predicted values with the anticipated values and calculating the loss. This loss is then propagated backward through the network's parameters via back-propagation. Gradient descent is used to update the parameters using this propagated information. This is a mechanism that is aimed at minimizing the total loss and refining the model. These iterative steps, guided by learning algorithms continue until a satisfactory model is achieved. Throughout these stages, the Python programming language is used, utilizing the powerful Keras and Scikit-Learn libraries.

Data collection and processing for ANN training

We collected total 16318 combinations of datasets from numerical simulations. The datasets contains a total of 9 variables: Re, Ri, Ha, Ω, ϕAl2O3, ϕTiO2, ϕSWCNT, r/H (cylinder size) and Num). The first 8 variables are input parameters for training the ANN model, while the Num is the desired output. Based on these inputs and outputs the ANN model is trained. The datasets are subsequently preprocessed for use in developing, training, and post-processing the Artificial Neural Network (ANN) model. The preprocessing involves preparing separate input and output datasets to facilitate effective training. Throughout the development process, Python libraries such as Keras and Scikit-Learn are used for their robust functionalities. Prior to training, the input dataset undergoes scaling using a robust scaler from the Scikit-Learn library. This scaler, known as the robust scaler, operates by removing the median and scaling the data based on the interquartile range (IQR), which encompasses the range between the first quartile (25th percentile) and the third quartile (75th percentile). By employing this approach, outliers within the input dataset are effectively identified and eliminated, ensuring the reliability and robustness of the training process.

Results and discussion

In this current investigation, we have examined the influence of key parameters specifically, Reynolds number (Re), Richardson number (Ri), Hartman number (Ha), angular rotational speed of the cylinder (Ω), cylinder size (r/H), and nanofluid volume fractions (ϕSWCNT, ϕTiO2, and ϕAl2O3) on heat transfer for MHD mixed convection within a semicircular enclosure which contains a clockwise rotating cylinder. The analysis is conducted across various nanofluids (Al2O3-water, TiO2-water, and SWCNT-water) as well as hybrid nanofluids (Al2O3-TiO2-SWCNT-water). We have employed both numerical simulations and a machine-learning approach utilizing a novel ANN model. The outcomes are quantified in terms of the average Nusselt number (Num).

Effect of Reynolds number

The streamlines provide essential information about the complex fluid dynamics in a particular domain. In Fig. 7, the impact of different Reynold's numbers on the streamlines and isotherms inside the Al2O3-water nanofluid-filled semicircular cavity is illustrated for Ri = 1, Ha = 20, and Ω = 2. The streamlines exhibit similar patterns for Re = 50 and Re = 100, with a recirculation zone observed on the upper left side of the cavity in both cases, moving in an anticlockwise direction. However, at Re = 100, there is a slightly higher penetration of streamlines on the upper right side of the cavity. At Re = 300, two additional recirculation zones become visible above and below the cylinder, both exhibiting anticlockwise motion. The strength of the upper left recirculation zone appears to increase with higher Reynolds numbers. For all the Reynolds number values, the fluid velocity is comparatively higher closer to the rotating cylinder. Therefore, the flow is split into a low-velocity zone (near the top and bottom surfaces of the cavity) and a high-velocity zone (near the cylinder surface).

Effect of Reynolds number on isotherms and streamlines for Al2O3-water nanofluid (Ri = 1, Ha = 20, Ω = 2).

As depicted in Fig. 7, the isotherm patterns demonstrate significant similarity within the Reynolds number range of 50–300. A low-temperature region is established near the bottom of the cavity, while a hot fluid layer forms near the upper wall. Across all Re values, the penetration of the hot fluid consistently occurs on the right side of the cavity because of the clockwise rotation of the cylinder. Consequently, relatively low-temperature regions seem to form on the lower left side of the cavity. Notably, higher Reynold's numbers lead to reduced penetration of hot fluid towards the right side of the cavity.

In Fig. 8, the changes of Num with Reynolds number are illustrated for various types of hybrid nanofluids and nanofluids for Ri = 1, Ha = 20, Ω = 2. At higher Re, enhanced convective heat transfer is visible across all the studied fluids. Specifically, the SWCNT-Water nanofluid exhibits the highest Num for different values of Re, while the TiO2-Water nanofluid demonstrates the lowest Num for different Re. Because of the relatively higher thermal conductivity of SWCNT nanoparticles, SWCNT-water nanofluid has greater heat transfer capability than other nanofluids. For SWCNT-water nanofluid, an increase of 6.34 % in heat transfer is observed when comparing flow at Re = 400 to that at Re = 100. All the hybrid nanofluids perform better than Al2O3-water and TiO2-water nanofluids due to relatively higher thermal conductivity than these nanofluids. The convective heat transfer efficiency is slightly improved in hybrid nanofluids that contain a higher composition of SWCNT and Al2O3 nanoparticles, under the specified parameters. Among the studied hybrid nanofluids, HNF 03 shows slightly superior heat transfer capabilities due to a higher concentration of SWCNT nanoparticles. HNF 03 shows 2.48 % heat transfer enhancement compared to TiO2-water nanofluid at Re = 400. This observation highlights how the effectiveness of a nanofluid can be enhanced by adding a small percentage of higher thermal conductive nanoparticles, resulting in a more effective hybrid nanofluid. In Fig. 9, the comparison of the results obtained from finite element method (FEM) and ANN is depicted. From Fig. 9, it can be seen that there is a close alignment between the predicted value of ANN and FEM results. The comparative analysis of results between FEM and ANN highlights the robust performance of the ANN model and its capability to accurately predict Num across various Re for different kinds of fluids.

Effects of richardson number

In Fig. 10, the influence of the Richardson number for Al2O3-water nanofluid on both streamlines and isotherms is demonstrated for Re = 300, Ha = 20, Ω = 2. The Richardson number serves as a metric to measure the relative impact of natural convection in comparison to forced convection. When the Ri is below 0.01, it indicates minimal to negligible effects of natural convection. Conversely, values exceeding 10 signify little to no impact from forced convection.

Effect of Richardson number on isotherms and streamlines for Al2O3-water nanofluid (Re = 300, Ha = 20, Ω = 2).

As depicted in Fig. 10, when Ri is 0.01 an almost symmetrical velocity region is formed around the vertical mean position. This symmetry occurs due to strong forced convection effects. Additionally, a notably high-velocity flow encircles the cylinder because of the strong forced convection effects. At Ri = 1 both the impact of natural and forced convection can be visible. Recirculation zones are visible between the bottom wall and the upper wall of the cavity due to natural convection effects. A greater Ri signifies an increased impact of natural convection, leading to a higher buoyancy-induced flow rate. When Ri = 10, strong natural convection effects become apparent as indicated by strong recirculation zone formations between the heated top wall and the cooled bottom wall. These recirculation patterns arise due to the strong influence of natural convection, driven by temperature differences between the cold curved wall, the circular cylinder wall, and the upper heated wall. The penetration of fluid also increases quite significantly on the upper part of the cavity at Ri = 1.

From Fig. 10, the impact of the Ri on the isotherms can be seen. In the cases of strong forced convection, such as when Ri = 0.01, a distinct thermal pattern emerges. The clockwise motion of the rotating cylinder induces a high-temperature region on the right of the cylinder, while a relatively low-temperature region is visible on the bottom left side. Conversely, under conditions of strong natural convection (Ri = 10), the zero-temperature differences between the curved wall and the cylinder result in a relatively low-temperature region in the lower part of the cavity. Additionally, penetration of the hot fluid is comparatively higher on the cavity's right side at a lower Richardson number due to strong forced convection effects.

In Fig. 11, the changes of Num with Ri are illustrated for various types of studied fluids for Re = 300, Ha = 20, Ω = 2. Enhanced convective heat transfer is visible at higher Ri across all cases, as indicated by the increased Num with greater Ri. As shown in Fig. 11, it becomes evident that for each of the nanofluids and hybrid nanofluids, increasing Ri from 1 to 20 results in a consistent monotonic increment in Num. As mentioned earlier at higher Ri strong recirculation zones form inside the cavity and the penetration of fluid increases near the top hot wall. As a result, an increment in heat transfer occurs at the top wall, ultimately resulting in a rise of the Num with increasing Ri, as illustrated in Fig. 11. Among various nanofluids, the SWCNT-water nanofluid consistently exhibits the highest average Nusselt number for all the Ri values due to its relatively higher thermal conductivity. Effect of Hartman number

The Hartmann number is used to measure the strength of the magnetic field in a conducting fluid. It is a measure of the impact of magnetic forces relative to viscous forces. Fig. 13 illustrates the impact of the Hartmann number on isotherms and streamlines for Al2O3-water nanofluid under specific conditions (Ri = 1, Ω = 2, Re = 300). The introduction of magnetic forces significantly influences the streamline behavior as can be seen from Fig. 13. At Ha = 0, a strong clockwise recirculation zone is evident on the right side of the cavity. Additionally, two anticlockwise recirculation zones are observable at the top and left parts of the cavity. Upon increasing Ha to 20, the strength of the upper recirculation zone intensifies, while the recirculation zone on the right side of the cavity weakens slightly. Additionally, a new recirculation zone emerges in the lower part of the cavity. At Ha = 50, two anticlockwise recirculation zones become prominent in the upper part of the cavity. Notably, the upper and lower recirculation zones, visible at Ha = 20, appear to shift closer to the vertical mean of the cavity at Ha = 50 due to strong magnetic force effects. At Ha = 20, the magnetic forces are already influential, leading to the formation and positioning of recirculation zones within the cavity. However, as the Hartmann number increases to Ha = 50, the magnetic forces exert a stronger influence on the fluid motion, causing a more pronounced shift in the recirculation zones towards the vertical mean of the cavity. This shift occurs due to the magnetic forces altering the trajectory of fluid particles, particularly in regions where the fluid motion interacts with the magnetic field. As a vertical magnetic field is applied in our study, the recirculation zones tend to align more closely with the direction of the magnetic field lines, leading to their observed displacement towards the vertical mean of the cavity.

Effect of Hartmann number on isotherms and streamlines for Al2O3-water nanofluid (Ri = 1, Re = 300, Ω = 2).

The variation in the Hartmann number also influences the isotherms. With increasing Hartmann number, hot fluid moves towards the hot wall. Conversely, at lower values of Ha, hot fluid penetrates deeper into the right part of the cavity. As a result, a relatively low-temperature region is visible on the lower left side. The observed changes in streamlines and isotherms highlight the close relationship between the thermal characteristics of the fluid flow and the Hartmann number.

In Fig. 14, Num is presented for different Ha for various nanofluids and hybrid nanofluids (Ri = 1, Ω = 2, Re = 300). The figure reveals that the Num reduces with the rising Hartmann number for all the nanofluids and hybrid nanofluids as the magnetic field suppresses the convective recirculating flow within the cavity. Among various nanofluids, the SWCNT-water nanofluid consistently demonstrates the highest average Nusselt number for all the Ha values due to its excellent heat transfer capability. In contrast, the TiO2-water nanofluid demonstrates the lowest heat transfer. The Num value of different hybrid nanofluids is almost similar to that of SWCNT-water nanofluid. Among the various hybrid nanofluids considered, HNF 03 shows slightly higher average Nusselt number at higher Hartmann numbers. Also, higher heat transfer is observed for all the hybrid nanofluids compared to Al2O3-water and TiO2-water nanofluids across all the scenarios due to addition of SWCNT-water nanoparticles which have very high thermal conductivity. The comparison of Num calculated using both FEM and ANN is depicted in Fig. 15. Both the ANN model and FEM are used to determine Num and the comparison is shown in Fig. 15. From the comparison, it is evident that our ANN model has the capability to predict Num for various nanofluids and hybrid nanofluids with very good accuracy across various Ha values.

Effect of rotational speed of the cylinder

Rotational speed has a significant impact on the streamline and significantly affects the flow speed inside the cavity. In Fig. 16, the influence of the angular rotational speed (Ω) of the cylinder for Al2O3-water nanofluid on both isotherms and streamlines is demonstrated for Ri = 1, Re = 300, Ha = 20. At lower rotational speeds (Ω = 2), the streamlines appear more scattered, and distinct recirculation zones are visible inside the cavity. Conversely, with increasing rotational speed, the fluid flow becomes more symmetrical and tends to center around the cylinder. Moreover, higher rotational speeds result in significantly increased fluid velocity, leading to enhanced heat transfer.

Effect of angular rotational speed on isotherms and streamlines for Al2O3-water nanofluid (Ri = 1, Re = 300, Ha = 20).

Fig. 16 illustrates the significant influence of rotational speed on the distribution of isotherms within the cavity. The rotational speed notably affects the temperature distribution, leading to the formation of a hot fluid region on the right side of the cavity and a relatively cooler region on the lower left side. With higher rotational speeds, there is an increase in the penetration of hot fluid on the right side of the cavity due to the intensified clockwise rotation of the cylinder.

In Fig. 17, the variations of Num with Ω are illustrated for various kinds of studied fluids for Ri = 1, Re = 300, Ha = 20. Enhanced heat transfer is visible at higher rotational speed across all cases, as indicated by the increased Num with greater Ω. As shown in Fig. 17, it becomes evident that for each of the nanofluids and hybrid nanofluids, increasing the Ω from 0 to 10 results in a consistent increment in Num. As the rotational speed increases, the fluid velocity inside the cavity increases which causes enhanced heat transfer at the upper hot wall. Among various nanofluids, the SWCNT-water nanofluid consistently exhibits the highest average Nusselt number across all rotational speeds. The higher thermal conductivity of SWCNT nanoparticles is the reason for the high heat transfer capability of SWCNT-water nanofluid. A substantial enhancement of 21.12 % in the Num is attained for the SWCNT-water nanofluid at Ω = 10, in comparison to the scenario where the cylinder remains motionless at Ω = 0. The heat transfer performance of all the hybrid nanofluids is obviously higher than that of Al2O3-water and TiO2-water nanofluids due to containing SWCNT nanoparticles which have much higher thermal conductivity. At lower rotational speed, the Num is almost same for all the hybrid nanofluids. But at higher rotational speed the heat transfer disparity between the hybrid nanofluids becomes more visible and HNF 03 demonstrates higher Num than other two hybrid nanofluids. The comparision of Num calculated by using both FEM and ANN is illustrated in Fig. 18. Comparing the results from the FEM and the ANN shows that the ANN model works really well in accurately predicting Num for different values of Ω.

Effect of cylinder size

Fig. 19 presents the impact of the size of the cylinder on the isotherms and streamlines for Ri = 1, Re = 300, Ha = 20, and Ω = 2. With the increment in the cylinder size, the width between the top wall and the cylinder decreases, resulting in a change in flow behavior. At r/H = 0.05, two recirculation zones are observed on the left and right sides of the cavity. When the cylinder size is increased (r/H = 0.1), the strength of these recirculation zones decreases. Additionally, the flow velocity between the top wall and cylinder increases at r/H = 0.1. Furthermore, at r/H = 0.2, the strength of these recirculation zones decreases significantly, and the flow velocity between the top wall and the cylinder increases even further. The impact of the cylinder size on the isotherms is also depicted in Fig. 19. With an increase in the cylinder size, a concentrated region of hot fluid forms between the cylinder and the upper wall, indicating the formation of thin boundary layers with high normal temperature gradients along the top wall.

Effect of the cylinder size on isotherms and streamlines for Al2O3-water nanofluid (Ri = 1, Re = 300, Ha = 20, Ω = 2).

In Fig. 20, the influence of the cylinder size on the Num at the heated surface is demonstrated for various nanofluids and hybrid nanofluids, with parameters Ri = 1, Re = 300, Ha = 20, and Ω = 2. Noticeable improvements in convective heat transfer are observed for larger cylinder sizes across all scenarios, as indicated by the increased Nusselt number with increased r/H. As the cylinder size expands, there is a corresponding rise in fluid velocity between the top wall and the cylinder surface, leading to enhanced heat transfer at the upper hot wall. Specifically, a significant enhancement of 66.14 % in the Num is achieved for the SWCNT-water nanofluid at r/H = 0.2, in contrast to the case where r/H is 0.05. The hybrid nanofluids outperform Al2O3-water and TiO2-water nanofluids in terms of heat transfer. Specifically, a significant enhancement of 2.15 % and 0.47 % in heat transfer is observed for HNF 03 compared to TiO2-water and Al2O3-water nanofluids, respectively, at r/H = 0.2. Utilizing both FEM and ANN, the corresponding comparison of Num is illustrated in Fig. 21. The notable precision showcased by our ANN model in predicting Num across various cylinder sizes underscores its remarkable accuracy and highlights its capability to accurately predict the impact of cylinder size on heat transfer for different kinds of fluids.

In this section and previous sections, we have analyzed the effects of different parameters on the heat transfer capabilities of various types of nanofluids and hybrid nanofluids within this particular geometrical domain. Our study aimed to explore the potential benefits of hybrid nanofluids in enhancing heat transfer processes, and the results indicate that the improvement achieved compared to conventional nanofluids is less than 5 %. Although this improvement may seem very small, when we consider broader scenarios such as real-world applications like cooling nuclear reactors, electrical component cooling, and industrial cooling, this 5 % improvement in heat transfer can significantly reduce costs. Additionally, the heat transfer capabilities of different hybrid nanofluids are almost similar to those of SWCNT-water nanofluids, which, despite their higher thermal conductivity, are also costly. Therefore, studying different hybrid nanofluids can provide insights to engineers and researchers working on how to increase heat transfer efficiency cost-effectively in engineering applications.

Prediction of ANN model for new hybrid nanofluid

Our developed ANN model has shown very good accuracy while predicting the heat transfer of the fluids whose data are used to train and validate this model. In order to determine how effectively our ANN model performs in predicting the heat transfer of entirely new fluids, a new hybrid nanofluid, Al2O3-TiO2-water (HNF 04) is used to test the accuracy of our ANN model. This new hybrid nanofluid, which was not part of the training data consists of 1.5 % Al2O3 and 3.5 % TiO2 nanoparticles. We have found that our ANN model has shown 97.41 % accuracy in predicting convective heat transfer while tested with this new test dataset. The average Nusselt number for different parameters such as Richardson number, Reynolds number, Hartmann number, angular rotational speed of the cylinder, and cylinder size has been determined in this case using both FEM and our developed ANN model. From Fig. 22, we can see that there is a close alignment between the predictions generated by the ANN model and the results obtained through FEM simulations. Therefore, our developed ANN model can be used for predicting heat transfer in different kinds of fluids.

Comparison of the computational cost of FEM and ANN

This investigation, conducted on a readily available personal computer with a 12th Gen Intel® Core™ i5-12500H processor, 8 GB of RAM, an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 GPU, and Windows 11, highlights the remarkable efficiency of Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) compared to traditional methods like the Finite Element Method (FEM). While the initial FEM simulation demanded a staggering 54 h of computational power, our innovative ANN model trained remarkably fast, requiring only 1 h on the same machine. Following this brief training period, the ANN delivers accurate results within a matter of seconds, maintaining an accuracy level comparable to the much slower FEM analysis. This translates to a significant reduction in computational time and, consequently, cost. In a test case with HNF 04, FEM required 7 h to process and provide results, while the ANN accomplished the same feat in mere seconds with very high accuracy. This example showcases how powerful ANN can be, especially when it comes to speeding up those slow and computationally expensive calculations.
