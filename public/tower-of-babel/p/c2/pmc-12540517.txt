Beyond the Classical Triad: Atypical Presentations and Regulatory T Cell Phenotyping in a Cohort of IPEX Patients
Authors: Ismail Yaz, Sevil Oskay Halacli, Canberk Ipsir, Baris Ulum, Elif Soyak Aytekin, Hacer Neslihan Bildik, Melike Ocak, Hanife Avci, Fatma Visal Okur, Hayriye Hizarcioglu Gulsen, Hulya Demir, Ayse Metin, Alev Ozon, Baris Kuskonmaz, Ilhan Tezcan, Saliha Esenboga, Deniz Cagdas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune dysregulation, polyendocrinopathy, enteropathy, and X-linked(IPEX) syndrome caused by FOXP3 mutations is rare. FOXP3 is a transcription factor required for the regulatory T cell (Treg) development/function.AimWe aimed to characterize the clinical, immunologic, and genetic features of a single-center cohort of IPEX syndrome.Patients and MethodsWe present the clinical/immunological/genetic features of 12 patients with IPEX syndrome. We used whole exome and Sanger sequencing for the diagnosis/familial segregation. We performed immunophenotyping and measured Treg percentage and FOXP3 expression in peripheral blood by flow cytometric analysis.ResultsMedian age at diagnosis was 2.5 years (range: 0.3–22 years). Common clinical manifestations were infections (n = 9, 75%), allergies (n = 8, 67%), autoimmunity (n = 7, 58%), enteropathy (n = 7, 58%), and lymphoproliferation (n = 3, 25%). Atypical initial presentations included class IV lupus nephritis, a SCID-like immunophenotype (CD3+ T cells: 4% [100/µL]; CD4+ T cells: 3%, CD8+ T cells: 1%, CD19+ B cells: 81%, CD16/56+ NK cells: 13%), and isolated hypogammaglobulinemia persisting for years during follow-up. At the time of diagnosis, three (25%) patients had leukopenia, six (50%) had lymphopenia and two (17%) had neutropenia. Eosinophilia was observed in 42% of patients (25% mild, 17% moderate). Six different variants in FOXP3 were characterized in 12 patients from nine unrelated families. Four (33%) patients underwent hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). Overall, three (25%) patients died due to infections. One patient died due to HSCT-related catheter complications, one patient died in an accident. Among the transplanted patients, two are alive and well. Among the non-transplanted patients, five are alive and are being followed up at our center. Treg (CD4+CD127−/lowCD25+Foxp3+) percentage was low in eight patients compared to healthy controls (p < 0.001). FOXP3 expression was low in all the patients compared to healthy controls.ConclusionAtypical presentations make the diagnosis of IPEX syndrome challenging. This study expands the current knowledge of IPEX syndrome by describing a single-center cohort with certain atypical manifestations and by confirming previously reported rare phenotypes. Elucidating the genetic basis of immunodeficiency diseases contributes to improving diagnostic approaches and patient management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10875-025-01934-7.Keywords: FOXP3, IPEX, TREG, WES

Introduction

After the discovery of FOXP3 and its defect in Scurfy mice in 2001 [1], it became clear that the genetic defect in FOXP3 leads to autoimmune and inflammatory diseases in mice and humans by impacting CD4 + CD25 + regulatory T cell (Treg) development and function [2]. FOXP3 is a transcription factor essential in the thymus and secondary lymphoid organs, playing a crucial role in Treg development and function [3].

FOXP3 defect leading to `Immune dysregulation, polyendocrinopathy, enteropathy, X-linked (IPEX) syndrome` is rare, with a prevalence of < 1/1,000,000 [4, 5]. Hemizygous mutations in forkhead box P3 (FOXP3) gene encoding FOXP3 protein causes this X-linked syndrome [5]. The defective or deficient FOXP3 protein impairs self-tolerance maintenance and regulation of the peripheral T cell responses, leading to organ-specific autoimmune diseases [2, 6]. Clinical manifestations of IPEX syndrome are autoimmune enteropathy, early-onset diabetes, thyroiditis, hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and eczema. Elevated IgE and IgA and the absence or impaired function of Tregs are other characteristics [7].

The diagnosis of IPEX involves the Tregs immunophenotyping, as well as advanced functional and molecular tests [8]. As FOXP3 expression can remain preserved despite the presence of hemizygous missense mutations in FOXP3, flow cytometric detection of FOXP3 alone does not rule out IPEX syndrome [9]. Further functional studies such as suppression testing could be performed [10]. However, definite diagnosis requires molecular analyses to identify FOXP3 mutations [5, 8, 11].

One of the largest IPEX cohorts suggested that hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is the only curative treatment option, with a 73.2% overall survival rate, for patients with FOXP3 deficiency besides the supportive and immunosuppressive therapies included in the treatment and management [5].

Here, we report distinct clinical manifestations, immunological findings, medical approaches, and the outcome of 12 patients diagnosed with IPEX syndrome. Moreover, we highlight the importance of whole exome sequencing (WES) for establishing the genetic diagnosis essential for family studies, genetic counseling, early diagnosis, and treatment.

Patients and Methods

Twelve patients were diagnosed with IPEX syndrome after referral to two Turkish clinical centers: Section of Pediatric Immunology, Ihsan Dogramaci Children’s Hospital, Hacettepe University, Ankara (n = 11); Department of Pediatric Immunology and Allergy, Bilkent City Hospital, Ankara (n = 1). Demographic information (age, sex, age at diagnosis, consanguinity, family history, etc.) along with clinical and immunological findings, genetic analysis, and treatment approaches were gathered from the medical records. Comprehensive information regarding genetic analysis, flow cytometry and statistical methods is provided in the Supplementary Material. The research was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hacettepe University, and written informed consents were also obtained from all participants (KA-15069, GO 181682-27, GO-21/125).

The same hemizygous mutation, c.506G > A, was identified in four patients from four unrelated families. Demographic and Clinical Findings

The study included a total of 12 patients from nine unrelated families. All patients were male. Two families (F1 and F4) had more than one patient with IPEX syndrome. Figure 2 shows the pedigrees of these families. The median age at disease onset was 12 months (1 month – 180 months (mean 48 months)). The median age at diagnosis was 2.5 years (0.3–22 years (mean 8 years)). Seven (58%) patients had IPEX syndrome diagnosis in three years of age. Four (33%) patients had consanguineous parents.

Pedigrees of the two families (F1 and F4) with more than one patient with IPEX syndrome

The median diagnostic delay was 1.42 years (range: 0–16 years). No clear correlation was observed between diagnostic delay and severity of clinical manifestations. For example, two (P11 and P12) of the patients with the longest diagnostic delays (12 and 16 years) developed gingivitis, oral candidiasis, oral aphthae and gluten sensitive enteropathy and type I diabetes, whereas about half (P1, P4, P5, P9, P10) of the patients diagnosed at disease onset presented with significant complications including pneumonia, oral candidiasis, CMV retinitis, BCGitis, oral aphthae, mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis, class V lupus nephritis, hypothyroidism, autoimmune hepatitis, immune thrombocytopenic purpura, type I diabetes, chronic diarrhea, atopic dermatitis and/or lymphoproliferation.

Clinical findings of our patients during the follow-up are depicted in Fig. 3. Common clinical symptoms were infections (n = 9, 75%) and allergy (n = 8, 67%), followed by autoimmunity (n = 7, 58%), enteropathy (n = 7, 58%) and lymphoproliferation (n = 3, 25%).

Infections

Pneumonia was the most common infection, occurring in 6 (50%) cases, followed by oral aphthae (n = 3, 25%), upper respiratory tract infection (n = 2, 17%) and oral candidiasis (n = 2, 17%). Other infectious manifestations are shown in Fig. 3. Viral infections such as cytomegalovirus (CMV), severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), rhinovirus, bocavirus, parainfluenza and/or influenza were detected in 5 (42%) patients. Gram-positive bacteria (Streptococcus pneumoniae from respiratory viral panel and Staphylococcus aureus from blood culture) and gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli from urine culture) were isolated in cultures (n = 3, 25%). Candida albicans was isolated in the blood culture of one patient (8%).

Allergy

Allergic manifestations were present in 67% (n = 8) of the patients. Four patients (33%) developed food allergy. The main responsible food antigens were cow milk (n = 1, 8%) and egg white (n = 3, 25%) proteins. Atopic dermatitis and allergic rhinitis were observed in 33% (n = 4) and 17% (n = 2) of the patients, respectively.

Autoimmune Findings

Patients with IPEX syndrome were affected by a variety of autoimmune complications such as diabetes mellitus (n = 6, 50%), hypothyroidism (n = 3, 25%), immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) (n = 2, 17%), one of whom (P3) also presented with autoimmune hemolytic anemia, consistent with Evans Syndrome. Other rare autoimmune manifestations are shown in Fig. 3. Two out of 12 patients (17%) developed immune-mediated kidney disease. One patient was diagnosed with class V lupus nephritis. The second patient was diagnosed with mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis (Suppl. material).

Enteropathy

About half (58%) of the patients with IPEX syndrome presented with enteropathy such as chronic diarrhea (n = 6, 50%) and gluten sensitive enteropathy (n = 3, 25%) (Two patients had both manifestations). Intestinal biopsy was performed in two patients: one (P6) exhibited histological features consistent with celiac disease and a reduction in enteroendocrine cells in the colonic mucosa, while the other (P3) showed nonspecific findings. Anti-enterocyte antibody testing is not available in our center.

Lymphoproliferation

Lymphoproliferation, encompassing manifestations such as lymphadenopathy (n = 1, 8%), splenomegaly (n = 1, 8%), hepatomegaly (n = 1, 8%), or hepatosplenomegaly (n = 2, 17%), emerged as one of the less prevalent clinical features observed in individuals diagnosed with IPEX syndrome.

Atypical Presentations of FOXP3 Deficiency

Three patients (P4, P5 and P7) were classified as having atypical manifestations at initial presentation. P4 exhibited a SCID-like phenotype characterized by markedly reduced T cell subsets (CD3+: 4% (100/µL), CD4+: 3% (75/µL), CD8+: 1% (25/µL) with preserved B (CD19+: 81% (2033/µL)) and NK (CD16/56+: 13% (326/µL)) cells. P5 presented with class IV lupus nephritis. P7 was followed for years with isolated hypogammaglobulinemia without other major autoimmune or infectious complications (Suppl. material).

Laboratory Findings

Complete Blood Count Analysis

Lymphopenia, eosinophilia, leukopenia, and neutropenia were present in 50% (n = 6), 42% (n = 5), 25% (n = 3), and 17% (n = 2) of the patients, respectively; among those with eosinophilia, 25% (n = 3) had mild (500–1500/mm3) and 17% (n = 2) had moderate (1500–5000/mm3) eosinophilia.

FOXP3 Expression Analysis

In seven (58.3%) patients, FOXP3 expressions were evaluated in total PBMCs, ranging from 0.03 to 0.98% in patients, and 1.47–2.96% in healthy controls (Table 2). FOXP3 expression was low in 50% (n = 6) of the patients compared to the healthy controls analyzed on the same day. FOXP3 expression in PBMCs was comparable with the healthy control in P8 whereas P12 has lower FOXP3 expression than healthy control (Fig. S2).

Autoantibodies

A variety of autoantibodies have been detected in the patients. Antinuclear antibody was positive at 1:100 dilutions in five out of 8 (62.5%) patients and anti-thyroid peroxidase antibody titer was positive in three out of 8 (37.5%) patients. Direct Coombs test was positive in two (50%) out of four patients. In P6, anti-gliadin IgA and anti-gliadin IgG levels were positive. Anti-thyroglobulin antibody was positive in one (P3) out of 8 (12.5%) patients A detailed summary of the autoantibody profiles is provided in Table S1.

Treatment and Outcome

Nine patients received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy. Additional immunosuppressive therapy other than steroids and IVIG was required in six out of 12 patients (50%). Treatments included cyclophosphamide (n = 2), mycophenolate mofetil (n = 2), cyclosporine (n = 2), rapamycin (n = 2), azathioprine (n = 1), and hydroxychloroquine (n = 1). The treatments given to the patients and their indications are shown in Table 3. IVIG was administered at substitutive doses in patients with hypogammaglobulinemia and/or recurrent infections. Immunomodulatory doses were given in patients for autoimmune cytopenias, such as autoimmune hemolytic anemia (P3) and/or immune thrombocytopenic purpura (P3, P9).

Discussion

Immune dysregulation, polyendocrinopathy, enteropathy, X-linked syndrome generally presents with skin disorders, type I diabetes mellitus (T1DM), autoimmune cytopenia, hepatitis, and hypoparathyroidism [9]. Median age at diagnosis was 2.53 years, but may occur late in life [12, 13]. In another study, median age at onset was 0.1 years and median age at diagnosis was 4 years (IQR: 0.4–11) [14]. In the present IPEX cohort, the median symptomatic age was 12 months (1 month – 180 months (mean 48 months)) whereas the median age at diagnosis was 2.5 years (0.3–22 years (mean 8 years)). Although early diagnosis is generally emphasized in IPEX syndrome, our data suggest that disease severity does not consistently correlate with diagnostic delay. Heterogeneity of disease progression and clinical severity may be more closely related to factors such as environmental and epigenetic modifiers, rather than time to diagnosis alone.

Frequently encountered clinical manifestations were enteropathy (97.9%), skin manifestations (62.1%), endocrinopathies (53.3%), infections (40.0%), hematologic abnormalities (38.5%), renal involvement (16.4%) and other immune-related complications (22.1%) in a total of 195 patients with IPEX [13]. In the present study, infections (75%) and allergies (67%), followed by autoimmunity (58%), enteropathy (58%), and lymphoproliferation (25%) were prevalent.

The enteropathy frequency in the present cohort (58%) was lower than the frequency reported in the literature. Bacchetta and Roncarolo reported enteropathy and endocrinopathy ratios over 70% and 50% (mostly T1DM), respectively [15]. Barzaghi et al. described enteropathy in in 41% of patients (n = 39/96). They tested enteropathy-related autoantibodies in 42 patients with enteropathy and found positive in 79% of them (n = 33/42) [5]. Jamee et al. reported enteropathy ratio as (70%) [14]. The lower enteropathy ratio in the present cohort may be explained by the variable age of onset and progression of autoimmune features with age in IPEX syndrome, and the microbiota composition due to the dietary differences compared to other countries. Autoimmunity, including enteropathy, may develop later in the disease course with the triggering factors. Moreover, environmental (exposomal) factors are inherently heterogeneous and unpredictable. Finally, the presence of yet unidentified protective genetic variants may also play a role in modulating the clinical expression of enteropathy in some patients.

The proportion of patients with T1DM reported as 39% by Jamee et al. [14]. In this study, the ratio of patients with T1DM was 50%. Jamee et al. reported autoimmune cytopenia in 22% (68 out of 312) of the IPEX patients [14], whereas the ratio in our cohort was 17% (2/12), including one patient (P3) with Evans syndrome.

Local/systemic bacterial, viral, and fungal infections may occur in IPEX syndrome patients [13–15]. Pneumonia is common and reported in 47% of the IPEX syndrome patients [9]. In the present study, a similar pneumonia rate (50%) was present. In APECED, an IPEX-like disease, thymic tolerance is impaired due to AIRE gene defect and patients may develop autoimmune lung diseases such as pneumonitis [16–18]. Furthermore, autoimmune pneumonitis may develop in patients with IPEX syndrome due to Treg dysfunction [19, 20]. However, we detected microorganisms, such as CMV, SARS-CoV-2, S. pneumonia, and S. aureus in patients with pneumonia and did not demonstrate autoimmune pneumonitis.

As one of components of IPEX triad, atopic dermatitis typically ranges from mild to severe. The rash typically occurs in early infancy and may be concomitant with the onset of endocrinopathy and enteropathy. Other skin manifestations may include diffuse erythema, alopecia universalis, and pemphigoid nodularis [21]. Skin disorders including eczematous, atopic, or psoriasiform dermatitis as well as alopecia was observed in 66% of the IPEX syndrome patients [14]. Dermatitis was reported in 19% [14] and food allergies was reported in 36% of IPEX syndrome patients [9]. Food allergies was noted in about 14% (13/96) in another cohort [5]. In the present cohort, dermatitis and food allergy ratios were 33% each.

Lymphoproliferation was rarely reported in IPEX [5, 9, 22]. Lymphoproliferation was recorded in one fourth of the IPEX patients in the present study.

In the present IPEX cohort, three patients had intriguing and atypical characteristics similar to other reports [15, 23–27]. Consonni et al. noted that several cases of IPEX developed kidney involvement, which can infrequently be the first clinical manifestation [24]. Voarino et al. stated that IPEX syndrome may present with atypical findings including granulomatous inflammation, myelodysplasia and unique renal and neurological manifestations [28]. Consonni et al. reported that the CVID phenotype may be present in certain families and documented as an atypical finding [24]. In this study, one patient exhibited SCID phenotype. Another patient had a diagnosis of SLE and lupus nephritis, and one patient was followed up for probable transient hypogammaglobulinemia for years. Moreover, the patient with SCID phenotype (P4) and the others with hypogammaglobulinemia (P3, P7 and P8) had the same mutation. Unusual manifestations can be observed even in siblings with the same genetic mutation [11, 29], indicating the epigenetic modification effect on FOXP3 expression [30]. The significance of DNA methylation and chromatin accessibility is already underlined in complex diseases such as common variable immunodeficiency [31]. Yet, it needs to be elucidated whether these changes in epigenetic mechanisms are the cause or the result. Furthermore, it is crucial to evaluate the molecular mechanisms in which microRNAs play a role [32]. Similar to APECED syndrome, it is not possible to predict the symptom onset in IPEX syndrome [18]. Depending on the specific triggering exposomal effects encountered, various findings may arise at different periods of life. Within our cohort, three patients received a diagnosis within their second decade of life.

FOXP3 is expressed in humans as four isoforms: the full-length isoform (FOXP3FL), and three alternatively spliced isoforms: FOXP3Δ2 (lacking exon 2), FOXP3Δ7 (lacking exon 7), and FOXP3Δ2Δ7 (lacking both exon 2 and exon 7) [33, 34]. FOXP3Δ2 isoform, if present in an individual may be protective compared to the severe IPEX syndrome patients [35]. Therefore, the diagnosis may be delayed because of atypical presentation.

Hematological features may be present in 38% of the patients [13]. Our analysis revealed that lymphopenia, eosinophilia, leukopenia, and neutropenia are present in 50%, 42%, 25%, and 17% of the patients, respectively. Normal lymphocyte subsets [14], CD8 + T cell and NK cell count increases with normal or increased CD4/CD8 ratio [36] were reported in patients. The CD4/CD8 ratio range of 1.5 to 2.5 are typically regarded as normal [37]. In the present cohort, four patients had a CD4/CD8 ratio over 2.5, four had a ratio ranging from 0.6 to 1.5, and four had a normal range.

Defective Treg function alters the humoral response, leading to abnormal activation of B cells and changes in antibody production [38]. In studies, elevated serum IgA and IgE are present in more than half of the IPEX patients with normal IgG and IgM levels or hypogammaglobulinemia [7, 24, 39, 40]. In the present cohort, eight (%75) patients had IgM levels either lower or higher than age-related reference values. IgA levels were normal in more than half of the patients (%67) and 25% of the patients had IgA levels higher than reference values. Seven (58%) patients had IgE levels higher than 200IU/mL. Three patients had hypogammaglobulinemia similar to literature [24]. The patients (P3, P7 and P8) with hypogammaglobulinemia had the same hemizygous missense c.506G > A mutation with another patient (P4) without hypogammaglobulinemia.

FOXP3 mutations indirectly increase the frequency of autoreactive B cells, resulting in the production of autoantibodies specific to certain tissues [38]. In IPEX syndrome, prior studies have documented the presence of several autoantibodies target antigens in the stomach, kidney, skin, pancreas and thyroid [40]. Our data demonstrated a high incidence of autoantibodies in IPEX patients (75% (n = 8)).

There are a limited number of studies examining T and B cell subsets in IPEX syndrome patients. Costa-Carvalho et al. reported that naïve CD4 + and naïve CD8 + T cells were reduced in a patient with IPEX [41]. Huang et al. reported skew in peripheral T cells in three patients out of five IPEX patients. Memory and naïve B cell frequencies were high in two and transitional B cells were reduced in three patients [42]. Consistent with the previous reports, naïve CD4 + and CD8 + T cells were reduced in 30% and 20% in our cohort, respectively. Effector memory CD4 + and terminally differentiated CD4 + T cells were increased in 50% and 30% of the patients, respectively. Transitional B cells were decreased in 30%. Recent thymic emigrants were reduced in three (P4, P6 and P10) patients.

We have determined six previously reported variants in FOXP3 in 12 patients [43–47]. c.1040 G > A, c.1150 G > A, and c.751_753del are the most frequently reported pathogenic mutations of FOXP3 in patients with IPEX [5, 9, 13–15, 46]. In the present study, hemizygous c.506G > A mutation was found to be the most common mutation in four unrelated patients. Three of the mutations are in the fork-head domain (FHD) which is responsible for localization of the protein in the nucleus and its ability to bind to DNA [48]. The remaining two missense mutations were located in the repressor domain (RD) and leucine zipper domain (LZ), which play crucial roles in repressing IL2 transcription in Tregs and facilitating protein–protein interactions, respectively [48].

The cell surface markers used to characterize Tregs vary from study to study. Some studies characterize Tregs as CD4+CD25+Foxp3+ [9], and some characterize as CD4+CD25highCD127lowFoxp3high [49]. Yet, the two populations should not be considered identical [8]. Duclaux-Loras et al., analyzed Tregs in ten patients and found low in six of their patients (normal range: 5–10%) [46]. It is emphasized that there were variable FOXP3 expressions in patients with missense and splicing mutations and two patients with missense mutations (p.E323K, p.S390N) had markedly elevated percentages of FOXP3 expressing CD4 + cells in the same study [9]. We evaluated Treg ratio in eight out of 12 patients and it was ranging from 0.02 to 5.48%, highest ratio in P8 with c.506G > A mutation whose clinical features were food allergy, otitis media, tonsilitis and scalp abscess. Gambineri et al. reported significantly lower FOXP3 expression in CD4+ lymphocytes compared to controls [9]. In our study, Treg levels were significantly lower in patients compared to healthy controls. Tregs may remain unchanged in patients with IPEX syndrome. It is important to realize that mutations could disrupt the function of FOXP3, regardless of having an impact on its expression. Flow cytometric evaluation of FOXP3-expressing CD4 + CD25 + CD127low T cells in patients with IPEX and IPEX-like diseases, such as LRBA, CTLA4, DEF6, STAT1, and STAT3 defects, may also provide insight into immune reconstitution and chimerism after HSCT [14, 50] if the expression is low before HSCT.

While Treg frequency and FOXP3 expression measurement using flow cytometry are useful methodologies, they should not be considered conclusive screening tests for IPEX syndrome. Some patients with FOXP3 mutations may exhibit normal Treg frequencies and normal FOXP3 expression. Furthermore, FOXP3 expression levels are not associated with disease severity, as indicated by Gambineri et al. [9]. Consequently, genetic testing is the definitive method for diagnosing IPEX syndrome since expression may be normal in some cases.

In the comprehensive review of Bacchetta and Roncarolo, overall survival rate after HSCT is 73% [15]. In our study, overall survival of patients after HSCT is 75. P6 died during the HSCT process due to a catheter-related complication. Both thromboembolic and infectious etiologies are plausible, as catheter-related thrombosis and infection are known risks in immunocompromised patients [51].

Although HSCT is the only curative therapeutic approach for IPEX syndrome, some patients with atypical or milder phenotypes may achieve disease control with immunomodulatory therapies such as sirolimus or abatacept, as recently reported by Gerbaux et al. [52].

In conclusion, this study reports 12 additional genetically confirmed cases of IPEX syndrome, including patients with classical and atypical clinical presentations. Our findings expand the known phenotypic spectrum of this rare immune dysregulation disease. By providing detailed clinical, immunological, and genetic data, this study contributes to a better understanding of the variability of IPEX manifestations. Despite the clear definition of IPEX syndrome, diagnostic challenges persist due to the variability in the characteristic features. Consequently, the disease course often exhibits a high degree of variability, rendering it unpredictable. Individuals lacking the classical triad often necessitate genetic testing for accurate diagnosis. Moreover, our observations underscored the clinical variability among individuals even with the same genetic mutation highlighting the importance of genetic and functional analysis. By shedding light on the diverse clinical presentations and genetic underpinnings of the syndrome within this population, our findings contribute to a deeper understanding of IPEX syndrome and may aid in refining diagnostic approaches and optimizing patient management strategies.
