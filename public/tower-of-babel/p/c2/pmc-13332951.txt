Infrared thermography of the tongue in systemic lupus erythematosus: a pilot study of thermal response to cold stimulation
Authors: Karolina Jezierska, Lidia Szczucka, Maciej Brzosko, Piotr Skomro, Helena Gronwald, Danuta Lietz-Kijak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSystemic lupus erythematosus is a chronic autoimmune disease in which vascular and microcirculatory disorders can play a significant role in the clinical picture. The aim of this pilot study was to evaluate the dynamic thermal response of the tongue to a controlled cooling stimulus in patients, compared with that of healthy individuals. This observational study was conducted in 106 healthy individuals and 10 patients. Measurements were taken using a thermal imaging camera. After the baseline measurement, participants held cold water in their mouths for 60 s. Thermal images of the tongue were recorded immediately after the stimulus and after 2, 5, and 10 min. Mean temperature was analysed. Differences between groups were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test. No significant differences were found between groups in most absolute temperature values. In the unadjusted analysis, at 2 min after stimulation, patients with systemic lupus erythematosus had significantly higher temperatures than healthy controls in the apex and central region of the tongue. Significant differences were also observed at 10 min in the apex and central region. The most consistent between-group differences were observed in dynamic recovery parameters. The T2-T0 parameter differed significantly between groups in all analysed tongue regions. In the central region, additional significant differences were observed for T0-T, T5-T0, and T10-T0. After false discovery rate adjustment, significant differences remained for T2-T0 and T10-T0 in the central region of the tongue. Overall, patients with SLE demonstrated a tendency toward greater post-cooling temperature recovery, particularly in the central region and apex of the tongue. Dynamic thermography of the tongue following controlled cold stimulation may reveal differences in thermal response between SLE patients and healthy individuals that are not apparent from baseline temperature alone. Due to the pilot nature of the study, the results should be considered preliminary and require confirmation in larger, well-characterised cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00296-026-06229-7.Keywords: Lupus erythematosus, Systemic, Thermography, Skin temperature, Microcirculation, Rheumatic diseases

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic, multisystem autoimmune disease characterised by autoantibodies, immune complex deposition, and chronic inflammation affecting multiple organs, including the skin, joints, and kidneys [1]. Vascular and microcirculatory disorders are also significant components of SLE pathophysiology and may contribute to skin symptoms, changes in peripheral perfusion, and Raynaud’s phenomenon [2, 3].

Infrared thermography (IRT) is a non-invasive, non-contact, and passive imaging method that enables quantitative assessment of temperature distribution on the skin surface [4–6]. Because skin temperature may depend on factors such as local blood flow, metabolic activity, and inflammatory processes, IRT can be used to indirectly assess microcirculatory and vascular changes [3, 7].

The aim of the presented study is to compare the dynamic tongue response to thermal stimulation in healthy individuals and those with lupus, using thermographic measurements. In rheumatology, thermography has been analysed primarily in the context of Raynaud’s phenomenon, systemic sclerosis, inflammatory joint diseases, and monitoring vascular and joint involvement [8, 9]. However, the number of studies in patients with SLE remains limited. Previous studies have included, among others, thermographic assessment of Raynaud’s phenomenon in SLE patients and analysis of selected anatomical areas, such as the temporomandibular joint region [10, 11]. Therefore, comparing quantitative thermographic parameters between SLE patients and controls may provide additional information on potential differences in skin surface temperature distribution in this group.

Methods

The study was observational and conducted among 106 healthy individuals and 10 patients with systemic lupus erythematosus, aged 18 to 64. Adult participants of both sexes, aged 18–64 years, were eligible for inclusion in the study. All participants were required to be neurologically and mentally healthy, able to cooperate during the examination, and able to follow the study instructions. In addition, participants were required to have a clinically healthy tongue, with normal colouration and without visible pathological lesions. The systemic lupus erythematosus group consisted of patients with a documented diagnosis of systemic lupus erythematosus confirmed in their medical records. Healthy individuals without a documented systemic disease relevant to the study protocol served as the control group.

The exclusion criteria were pregnancy, elevated body temperature, active bacterial or viral inflammatory disease, active inflammatory lesions within the oral cavity, and current or previous neoplastic disease in the head and neck region. Participants were also excluded if they were unable to cooperate during the examination, unable to comply with the study protocol, or did not provide written informed consent.

The measurements were performed in a draft-free room with the air conditioning and heating systems turned off (01.05.2025–30.09.2025). Ambient temperature was maintained within the range of 20–24 °C. Before data acquisition, environmental conditions, including temperature and humidity, were monitored to ensure measurement repeatability. The environmental conditions and the method of recording and analysing thermal images were consistent with the recommendations of the international protocol for standardising thermographic examinations [7, 12].

Before inclusion in the study, each participant underwent an oral health assessment performed by a dentist. Active inflammation within the oral cavity constituted an exclusion criterion. Prior to the experiment, all participants were informed about the study’s purpose and procedures and provided written informed consent. Eligible participants were required to be neurologically and mentally healthy, cooperative, and to have a clinically healthy tongue, free from pathological lesions and with normal colouration. The local bioethics committee approved the project (Bioethics Committee of the Pomeranian Medical University in Szczecin, KB-006/98/2024).

Participants were instructed to refrain from eating or drinking for at least 60 min before the examination. Upon arrival, each participant underwent a 15-minute acclimatisation period under the conditions of the examination room. Before the measurements began, participants received detailed information about the study, signed the required consent forms, and completed the relevant documents and questionnaires. The researcher explained the procedure before each examination and answered any questions the participant raised.

Before stimulus application, preliminary thermal images were acquired. A Fluke Ti400 thermal imaging camera (Fluke Corporation, Everett, WA, USA) was positioned as perpendicular as possible to the tongue surface. The camera is equipped with an uncooled microbolometer detector with a resolution of 320 × 240 pixels and enables temperature measurements from − 20 °C to + 1200 °C, with a thermal sensitivity of ≤ 0.05 °C and an accuracy of ± 2 °C or ± 2%.

Although the camera’s absolute accuracy is ± 2 °C or ± 2%, the present analysis focused primarily on within-subject relative temperature changes and recovery indices, thereby limiting the impact of potential systematic measurement uncertainty. The device also features LaserSharp automatic focusing and emissivity compensation. The distance between the camera and the participant was standardised using the participant’s nose as an anatomical reference point; the upper edge of the thermal image was aligned with the nasal tip, corresponding to an approximate camera-to-subject distance of 35 cm.

The emissivity was set to 0.98, consistent with commonly reported emissivity values for biological tissues. As the camera system did not allow manual adjustment of the reflected apparent temperature, all measurements were performed under controlled, stable ambient conditions to minimise potential environmental influences. During image acquisition, the tongue remained protruded from the oral cavity.

Before the thermographic examination, participants were instructed to remain still, avoid speaking, and minimise tongue movement during image acquisition. Between consecutive measurements, participants were asked to keep their mouths closed and to refrain from unnecessary tongue movements. The participant then took 20 mL of water at 8 °C into the mouth and held it there for 60 s. The water temperature was selected to provide a sufficiently strong yet safe cold stimulus that would elicit a measurable thermovascular response without inducing discomfort or protective reflexes. This temperature corresponds to typical refrigeration conditions, supporting both reproducibility and ecological validity of the experimental protocol.

After 60 s, the participant swallowed the entire volume of water, and thermal images were acquired immediately after stimulus completion. Subsequent tongue temperature measurements were performed at 2, 5, and 10 min after completion of the thermal test.

The duration of image acquisition was not included in the predefined time intervals; timing was initiated only after each measurement had been completed. During and between measurements, participants kept their mouths closed and were instructed not to speak or move their tongues.

The mean temperature was analysed within three regions of interest (ROIs): the dorsum, central region, and apex of the tongue. This approach enabled assessment of both global temperature changes and local thermal responses. Quantitative analysis was performed using Fluke Connect software (Fluke Corporation, version 2.0.5.0, 2021).

Thermal image acquisition was performed by a dentist, whereas quantitative image analysis, including manual ROI delineation, was conducted by the same medical physicist. This approach ensured clinical and metrological consistency of the research procedure. Manual ROI delineation was selected because of the tongue’s complex and variable anatomy, which may limit the reliability of automated segmentation methods.

To reduce potential observer bias, ROIs were defined using standardised anatomical landmarks and delineated by the same trained examiner, following a consistent protocol across all participants and time points. To assess the repeatability of ROI placement, repeated measurements were performed in a subset of participants for selected representative regions of interest. The variability of repeated measurements was quantified using the coefficient of variation (CV). Low CV values indicated good intra-rater consistency of ROI delineation and temperature measurement. An example thermal image with the designated ROIs is presented in Fig. 1.

Thermal image of the tongue showing the designated regions of interest (ROIs): the dorsum, central region, and apex. These ROIs were used to quantitatively analyse thermal parameters in the Fluke Connect software

The following parameters were recorded:

T - baseline tongue temperature, before the thermal stimulus [°C].

Tn - tongue temperature measured at time point n after completion of the thermal stimulus, where n = 0, 2, 5, or 10 min. T₀ denotes the temperature measured immediately after the stimulus, whereas T₂, T₅, and T₁₀ denote the temperatures measured 2, 5, and 10 min after stimulus completion, respectively [°C].

Descriptive statistics were performed for all analysed variables. Due to the small sample size in the individual groups, differences in median values were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Statistical analyses were performed using Statistica version 13 (TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA). Because multiple comparisons were performed across thermal parameters within each tongue region, p-values were additionally adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. Both unadjusted and FDR-adjusted p-values are reported. Given the pilot and exploratory nature of the study, unadjusted findings were interpreted cautiously, and statistical significance was primarily assessed using FDR-adjusted p-values.

Results

All participants who were assessed for eligibility and met the inclusion criteria agreed to participate, completed the full thermographic protocol, and were included in the final analysis. No participants were excluded after enrolment, and no non-participation occurred at subsequent stages of the study. Discussion

This pilot study demonstrated that patients with systemic lupus erythematosus may differ from healthy individuals in their dynamic thermal response of the tongue to a controlled cooling stimulus. A greater amplitude of the thermal response was observed in the SLE group, including both a greater temperature drop immediately after cooling and a greater subsequent temperature increase relative to the T0 value.

The most pronounced differences were observed in the central part of the tongue and its apex. In the unadjusted analysis, patients with SLE showed higher temperatures in the central and apical regions of the tongue at T2 and T10, but these differences were not significant after FDR correction. Among the dynamic parameters, T2-T0 showed statistically significant differences across all analysed tongue regions; however, after FDR adjustment, statistically significant differences remained only for T2-T0 and T10-T0 in the central region of the tongue. Although the small SLE sample limits the precision of the estimates, the observed rank-biserial effect sizes suggest moderate-to-large between-group differences, particularly for T2-T0 and T10-T0 in the central region of the tongue. These findings support the potential relevance of dynamic recovery parameters, while requiring confirmation in larger cohorts. Several additional parameters showed nominal significance in the unadjusted analysis, including T2-T0 in the dorsum and apex and T0-T or T5-T0 in the central region; however, these findings did not remain significant after FDR correction and should therefore be interpreted as exploratory.

The obtained results suggest that in patients with SLE, the tongue’s thermal response to a cooling stimulus is altered primarily in dynamic parameters, not in baseline temperature. This may reflect altered vascular or thermoregulatory regulation of tongue tissues during SLE, but the mechanism underlying these changes cannot be clearly determined from the current study. These findings may be interpreted in the context of known microvascular involvement in SLE. To the best of our knowledge, dynamic tongue thermography after controlled oral cooling has not previously been specifically evaluated in patients with SLE. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted in relation to three partially overlapping areas of evidence: dynamic tongue thermography in systemic conditions, thermographic assessment of vascular reactivity after cold provocation, and microvascular involvement in SLE.

The tongue was selected as a target region because it is a highly vascularized, easily accessible oral structure that can be assessed noninvasively using infrared thermography. Unlike nailfold capillaroscopy, which provides direct morphological information on peripheral microvascular architecture, tongue thermography does not visualise capillaries or directly measure blood flow. Instead, it assesses surface temperature distribution and thermal recovery after a controlled stimulus, which may reflect the integrated effects of local perfusion, heat transfer, tissue composition, and autonomic or vascular regulation. Therefore, tongue thermography should not be regarded as a replacement for established techniques such as nailfold capillaroscopy, Doppler imaging, or endothelial biomarker assessment. Rather, it may provide complementary, functional information on thermal regulation in an oral mucosal region that is not routinely evaluated by standard microvascular assessment methods. Future studies combining tongue thermography with direct vascular techniques are needed to determine whether altered thermal recovery corresponds to measurable microvascular abnormalities in SLE. This potential screening role remains exploratory and requires validation in larger, independent cohorts with direct vascular reference standards.

The methodological rationale for analysing dynamic, rather than only static, tongue temperature is supported by Wziątek-Kuczmik et al., who introduced a cooling procedure into tongue thermography and evaluated the temperature distribution on the dorsal and apical surfaces of the tongue in patients with general diseases. Their work suggested that dynamic changes following oral cooling may provide additional information beyond a single resting-temperature measurement. In the present study, this concept was extended to SLE, where the most evident between-group differences were observed not at baseline but in recovery-related parameters [13].

The choice of the tongue and oral cavity as a target region is also justified by the recognised oral involvement in SLE. Oral manifestations, including ulcers, hyposalivation, glossodynia, cheilitis and tongue-related abnormalities, have been reported in patients with SLE, although these manifestations are usually assessed clinically rather than functionally or thermographically [14, 15]. The present study, therefore, adds a functional thermal perspective to a field that has so far been dominated by descriptive clinical assessment of oral lesions.

The interpretation of altered thermal recovery in SLE is biologically plausible given the disease’s known vascular involvement. Nailfold capillaroscopy studies have repeatedly demonstrated microvascular abnormalities in SLE, including nonspecific structural changes in the capillary bed, and endothelial dysfunction has been confirmed in meta-analytic data, even among patients with SLE without overt cardiovascular disease [16–20]. These observations support the hypothesis that altered post-cooling tongue temperature recovery may reflect abnormal microvascular or vasomotor regulation. However, because thermography provides only an indirect measure of surface temperature, it cannot identify the precise biological mechanism. The altered thermal recovery observed in patients with SLE may reflect differences in local vascular regulation or microvascular function; however, this interpretation remains speculative because no direct vascular assessments were performed in the present study. Thus, thermal imaging findings should be regarded as indirect indicators of altered physiological responses rather than direct evidence of microvascular dysfunction.

The emphasis on post-cooling recovery parameters is also consistent with thermographic studies of peripheral vascular reactivity. In Raynaud’s phenomenon, cold provocation is commonly used as a physiological stressor to reveal altered vascular responses, particularly during the rewarming phase, which may be less apparent from baseline temperature alone. Although these studies focus on the peripheral skin rather than the oral mucosa, they support the broader principle that dynamic thermal recovery may be more informative than static thermal assessment for investigating vascular reactivity [21]. However, extrapolation from peripheral skin thermography to the oral mucosa should be made with caution due to differences in tissue structure, moisture, saliva distribution, and local heat exchange.

It should be emphasised that this was a pilot study and that the SLE patient population was small, which limits the generalisability of the results. The impact of disease activity, duration, treatment, or the presence of vascular symptoms such as Raynaud’s phenomenon was also not analysed in detail. Manual delimitation of regions of interest and the lack of independent assessment of measurement repeatability were also potential limitations. Absolute temperature values should be interpreted with caution due to the accuracy of the thermal imaging camera used, although the analysis was primarily based on relative changes within the same participant. The analysis, therefore, focused mainly on within-subject relative recovery parameters. The lack of formal device calibration, repeated measurements, and assessment of intra- and inter-observer variability is a limitation of this pilot study. From a statistical perspective, the obtained results should be considered preliminary and exploratory. The small SLE sample size limits the study’s statistical power and increases the risk of unstable effect estimates. Although the control group was substantially larger, the limited size of the SLE cohort reduced the study’s statistical power and increased the risk of both type I and type II errors. Therefore, statistically significant findings should be interpreted with caution and regarded as preliminary. The observed differences may indicate potentially relevant associations, but they require confirmation in larger, adequately powered cohorts of patients with SLE. Consequently, the present results should not be overgeneralized and should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Furthermore, multiple comparisons were conducted across tongue regions and thermal parameters. Although the FDR correction was applied, the small SLE cohort and exploratory design mean that both corrected and uncorrected findings should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in larger cohorts.

The oral cooling protocol was standardised with respect to water volume, water temperature, stimulation duration, ambient conditions, and timing of post-stimulation measurements. However, individual factors such as saliva volume, mucosal moisture, tongue protrusion duration, mouth-open duration, and evaporative cooling were not directly measured or controlled. These factors may have influenced surface temperature measurements and should be considered in future protocol refinement. Another limitation is that the present study assessed surface temperature distribution and thermal recovery patterns, but did not include direct measures of microvascular structure or function. Therefore, altered thermal recovery cannot be interpreted as direct evidence of microvascular dysfunction. Although such an interpretation is biologically plausible in the context of SLE, it remains indirect and should be treated as a hypothesis-generating observation. Future studies should combine thermal imaging with dedicated vascular assessments, such as nailfold capillaroscopy, Doppler-based measurements, or circulating endothelial biomarkers, to better clarify the mechanisms underlying the observed thermal patterns.

Despite these limitations, the consistent pattern of differences in dynamic temperature recovery parameters, particularly T2-T0, and the changes observed in the central tongue region suggest that these variables may be promising for further evaluation. In summary, dynamic tongue thermography after controlled cold stimulation may be a potentially useful tool for investigating thermal response abnormalities in patients with SLE. However, its diagnostic or monitoring value requires confirmation in further studies involving a larger, well-characterised patient population, taking into account effect sizes, confidence intervals, and correction for multiple comparisons. Therefore, the present findings should be regarded as preliminary evidence of altered dynamic thermal recovery of the tongue in SLE.
