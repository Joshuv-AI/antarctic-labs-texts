Integrative methylation and miRNA dysregulation in dlPFC reveal distinct molecular signatures of suicide and non‐suicide subtypes in major depressive disorder
Authors: Aleena Francis, Yogesh Dwivedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is a leading cause of disability and carries a high risk of suicide. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are epigenetic regulators implicated in MDD and can be regulated by DNA methylation, potentially reshaping downstream gene networks. We investigated methylation‐linked miRNA dysregulation and explored whether these changes are specifically associated with suicide among MDD patients.MethodsGenome‐wide DNA methylation profiling of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex from 15 MDD patients who died by suicide (MDD+S), 17 MDD patients who died from causes other than suicide (MDD−S), and 16 controls (C) using the Illumina 850K MethylationEPIC array was integrated with small RNA sequencing‐based miRNA quantification to identify miRNA‐associated differentially methylated probes (DMPs), link methylation to miRNA expression, and infer downstream targets and pathways.ResultsDifferential methylation analysis (P ≤ 0.05) revealed 139 miRNA‐linked DMPs in C vs. MDD+/−S, 135 in C vs. MDD−S, 179 in C vs. MDD+S, and the highest in MDD+S vs. MDD−S (235). CpG‐miRNA pairing (within 1500kb promoter) followed by Spearman correlation identified inverse associations between CpG β values and miRNA expression, with the most consistent signals in suicide‐status‐stratified subsets, including cg06341821‐hsa‐miR‐574‐3p and cg25451306‐hsa‐miR‐2110 in MDD+S‐related contrasts, and cg06179179‐hsa‐miR‐595 in MDD−S‐related contrasts. High‐confidence target prediction and ClueGO enrichment indicated distinct biology: miR‐595 target genes in MDD−S were enriched for interferon/innate immune signaling, whereas miR‐2110 target genes in MDD+S were enriched for ligand‐gated ion channel activity and synaptic/receptor signaling.ConclusionThis integrated approach identifies methylation‐regulated miRNA pathways that may play key roles in the molecular pathogenesis of MDD and suicide.Keywords: depression, dlPFC, methylation, miRNA, suicide

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a leading cause of disability worldwide, imposing substantial personal, societal, and economic burdens. 1 This complex neuropsychiatric illness is characterized by pervasive low mood, anhedonia, cognitive impairments, and altered neurovegetative functions, significantly diminishing an individual's quality of life and social functioning. 2 The etiology of MDD is multifactorial, involving an intricate interplay among genetic vulnerabilities, environmental stressors, and neurobiological dysregulation. 3 MDD is linked to a higher risk of suicide; however, most people who have MDD do not attempt suicide. The neurobiological factors associated with suicide among MDD subjects remain unclear. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms is crucial for developing more effective diagnostic tools and targeted therapeutic interventions for depression and suicidal behavior among depressed patients.

Emerging evidence underscores the important role of epigenetic changes in mediating the complex interaction between an individual's genetic makeup and their environment, influencing their risk for psychiatric conditions such as MDD. 4 , 5 , 6 Epigenetics involves heritable modifications in gene activity that do not modify the DNA sequence itself. 7 Of these mechanisms, DNA methylation, adding a methyl group to cytosine bases, is extensively studied for its significant effect on gene expression and cell function. 8 Dysregulated DNA methylation patterns have been consistently observed in the brains and peripheral tissues of individuals with MDD, often affecting genes involved in stress response, neuroplasticity, and immune function. 4 , 9 For instance, hypermethylation of the SLC6A4 gene promoter has been linked to reduced serotonin availability, a hallmark of MDD, 10 , 11 whereas altered methylation of the BDNF gene is implicated in the impaired synaptic function observed in the disorder. 12 , 13 , 14

MicroRNAs (miRNAs), small non‐coding RNA molecules typically 19–25 nucleotides in length, represent another crucial layer of epigenetic regulation. They function primarily by binding to complementary sequences in messenger RNA (mRNA) molecules, leading to translational repression or mRNA degradation, and thus finely tuning gene expression. 15 The expression of miRNAs themselves can be regulated by DNA methylation, creating an intricate epigenetic network where methylation patterns directly influence miRNA levels, which, in turn, modulate the expression of numerous target genes. 16 Growing evidence suggests that aberrant miRNA expression and methylation patterns are associated with the pathophysiology of several psychiatric conditions, including MDD. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 While initial studies have characterized overall DNA methylation changes in MDD, the specific mechanisms by which these methylation alterations impact miRNA genes and their subsequent expression in MDD remain unknown.

This study aims to comprehensively investigate the relationship between DNA methylation and miRNA expression in MDD and suicide. Utilizing the high‐resolution Illumina 850K Infinium Methylation EPIC BeadChip array from the previous study, 21 a genome‐wide DNA methylation analysis was performed to identify differentially methylated CpG sites within or near miRNA genes in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) from MDD patients with and without suicide compared with non‐psychiatric controls. We focused on the miRNAs with promoter differential methylation and correlated methylation levels with gene expression to identify those whose levels are significantly altered in MDD. This integrated approach is designed to reveal novel, methylation‐driven miRNA pathways critically involved in the molecular pathogenesis of MDD, potentially serving as promising biomarkers for diagnosis, prognosis, and the development of targeted epigenetic therapies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Human postmortem brain samples

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Alabama at Birmingham. Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) samples (Brodmann area 9 (BA9)) were obtained from the Maryland Brain Collection at the Maryland Psychiatric Research Center in Baltimore, MD. The cohort comprised 48 brain samples: 15 from the MDD+S (MDD patients who died from suicide) group, 17 from the MDD−S (MDD patients who died from causes other than suicide) group, and 16 from the non‐psychiatric control group (C). Tissues were collected only after informed consent had been obtained from a family member, and all samples were evaluated for neuropathological features. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the subjects are summarized in Table 1. There were no statistically significant differences observed in PMI, brain pH, RIN, sex, race, or age between control subjects and those with MDD+/−S. Furthermore, no significant differences were identified when comparing the control group with the MDD+S and MDD−S groups individually, nor when comparing the MDD+S group with the MDD−S group. Psychiatric diagnoses were assigned through psychological autopsy, 22 using the Diagnostic Evaluation After Death (DEAD) 23 and the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM‐V (SCID). 24 The interviews were done by a trained psychiatric social worker. Two psychiatrists independently reviewed the write‐up from this interview, as well as the SCID that was completed from it, as part of their diagnostic assessment of the case. Diagnoses were made from the data obtained in this interview, medical records from the case, and records from the Medical Examiner's office. The two diagnoses were compared, and discrepancies were resolved through a consensus conference. Control subjects were verified as free from mental illnesses using these consensus diagnostic procedures. All tissues from controls and MDD subjects were screened for evidence of neuropathology and were excluded if they exhibited features of Alzheimer's disease, infarctions, demyelinating diseases, or atrophy (or clinical history of these disorders). Toxicology and the presence of antidepressants were examined by analysis of urine and blood samples from these subjects. Brain pH was measured as described previously. 25 Tissue dissection has been provided in our earlier publication. 26

DNA methylome assays with Illumina 850K EPIC array

Genomic DNA was isolated from the dlPFC using the QIAamp® genomic DNA extraction kit (Qiagen, Germany). The DNA concentration was determined using a Nanodrop (Thermo Scientific, USA), and the OD260/OD280 ratio was measured to assess for any residual contaminants in the DNA preparation. Isolated DNA samples were processed with bisulfite reagents according to the manufacturer's instructions, using a DNA methylation kit (Zymo Research, USA). After purification, the DNA samples were hybridized using the 850K Infinium Methylation EPIC array (Illumina Inc., USA), which analyzes over 850,000 methylation sites across CpG islands, genes, transcription factor binding sites, open chromatin regions, and enhancers at single‐nucleotide resolution. The experiment included both unmethylated and methylated CpGenome controls, with duplicates of a pooled DNA sample run on each array to evaluate inter‐array consistency.

Differential methylation analysis

Signal intensities and raw methylation β values were extracted from Illumina's Genome‐Studio software R package ChAMP (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/ChAMP.html). 27 The methylation β values were calculated from normalized signal intensities after subtracting background noise using negative control probes. Differential methylation analyses were conducted across four groups (C vs. MDD+/−S, C vs. MDD−S, C vs. MDD+S, MDD+S vs. MDD−S) using a threshold P < 0.05 and identified 32,958 DMPs in C vs. MDD+/−S comparison, 30858 in C vs. MDD−S, 41647 in C vs. MDD+S, and 49,848 DMPs in MDD+S vs. MDD−S comparison. 21 For this study, the annotated miRNA‐associated differential probes were selected from each comparison. The CpG probes are annotated in relation to UCSC CpG island as shore: 0‐2Kb from the island, shelf: 2‐4Kb from the island, N_shore/N_shelf: upstream of island and S_shore/S_shelf: downstream of island.

RNA isolation

Total RNA from the human brain was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Slight modifications were made in the standard centrifugation speed while separating the aqueous phase followed by addition of 1.5‐ul glycogens as coprecipitant for overnight incubation at −20°C with isopropanol. During the washing phases, the RNA pellet was washed twice with 70% alcohol at 13,000 RPM for 30 min. These few modifications were made to increase the final yield of RNA from human frozen brain tissue samples. Glycogen (20 μg) was added to the RNA precipitation step and incubated overnight at −20°C. RNA samples were quantified on a NanoDrop ND‐1000 instrument, and purity was assessed at 260/280 nm (cutoff ≥1.8). Agarose electrophoresis was used to check the integrity of RNA samples.

Small RNA Sequencing

Total RNA isolated from dlPFC was used for miRNA sequencing library preparation. Briefly, small RNA libraries were prepared using NEBNext Multiplex Small RNA Library Prep Set for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The procedure included: (i) 3′‐adapter ligation by T4 RNA ligase 2; (ii) 5′‐adapter ligation by T4 RNA ligase; (iii) cDNA synthesis by reverse transcription; (iv) low cycle PCR amplification of the library DNA; and (v) size selection by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis of 135 ~ 155 bp PCR amplified fragments (corresponding to ~15–35 nt small RNAs).

The quality and size distribution of the libraries were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Libraries were denatured with alkaline treatment (0.1 M NaOH) to generate single‐stranded DNA molecules, loaded onto Illumina flow cells, and amplified via in situ cluster generation. Sequencing was performed for 51 cycles on an Illumina NextSeq 500 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Raw sequencing reads passing Illumina quality filtering (Illumina NextSeq 500) were used for downstream analysis.

miRNA expression profiling and data processing

Small RNA‐seq reads were analyzed using miRDeep2 (v2.0.1.3). 28 Raw FASTQ files were quality‐checked, and adapter sequences were removed using cutadapt (v1.18). Trimmed reads were collapsed to unique sequences while retaining read counts and aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) with mapper.pl. (Bowtie‐based), producing genome‐mapped reads in ARF format. Known human miRNA annotations (mature and hairpin precursor sequences for Homo sapiens) were obtained from miRBase (https://www.mirbase.org/) and supplied to miRDeep2.pl. together with the reference genome and ARF alignments to identify known miRNAs.

Differential DNA methylation and association with miRNA expression

CpG‐miRNA pairs were defined by assigning CpG probes located within ±1500 kb of miRNA genes to capture promoter‐proximal and cis‐regulatory regions potentially involved in the regulation of miRNA expression. For each pair, Spearman's rank correlation was used to estimate the association between methylation level (β value) and miRNA expression (log counts per million; logCPM), and correlations were considered significant at P ≤ 0.05.

miRNA target gene prediction and pathway analysis

Putative miRNA target genes were identified by integrating two independent in silico resources: (i) a sequence‐matched target set derived from TargetScan, filtered to retain only predicted targets with a cumulative weighted context++ score ≤ −0.3; and (ii) miRDB. The resulting consensus target genes were imported into Cytoscape (v3.10.2) and functionally annotated using the ClueGO plugin (v2.5.9) to identify significantly enriched Gene Ontology terms and pathway categories, with P‐values adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini‐Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction with an FDR cutoff ≤0.05.

Tissue‐specific expression of miRNA target genes

Brain‐specific expression profiles of key miRNA target genes were examined using the GTEx database (https://www.gtexportal.org/home/), confirming their tissue‐specificity and relevance to brain function.

Estimation of neuronal and non‐neuronal cell‐type proportions

To account for cellular heterogeneity in bulk dlPFC tissue, neuronal (NeuN+) and non‐neuronal (NeuN‐) cell‐type proportions were estimated using a reference‐based deconvolution approach. Reference DNA methylation profiles were obtained from the FlowSorted.DLPFC.450k dataset (https://bioconductor.org/packages/FlowSorted.DLPFC.450k). To ensure compatibility between datasets, CpG probes common to both the study dataset and the reference dataset were identified and retained for downstream analysis. Cell‐type proportions in bulk samples were estimated using the robust partial correlation (RPC) method implemented in the epidish package (https://github.com/sjczheng/EpiDISH).

Association of CpG methylation with demographic, clinical, and cell‐type proportion covariates

Linear regression analyses were performed to assess associations between the selected differential probes and covariates (age, PMI, sex, race, group, and estimated neuronal proportion (NeuN+)).

RESULTS

Differential methylation of miRNAs

Across all comparisons, miRNA‐associated differentially methylated probes (DMPs) showed clear shifts in both burden and directionality (Fig. 1a–d). In the overall case–control comparison (C vs. MDD+/−S), 139 miRNA‐linked DMPs were detected, comprising 79 hypomethylated and 60 hypermethylated probes (Table S1). Stratifying MDD by suicide status indicated fewer DMPs in the non‐suicide comparison (C vs. MDD−S: 135 total; 85 hypo, 50 hyper, Table S2), but a larger and more balanced signal in the suicide comparison (C vs. MDD+S: 179 total; 86 hypo, 93 hyper, Table S3). The strongest divergence was observed directly between clinical subgroups, with MDD+S vs. MDD−S yielding 235 DMPs and a higher number of hypermethylated sites (90 hypo vs. 145 hyper) (Table S4). Genomic‐context annotation demonstrated that hypomethylated probes were predominantly located in OpenSea regions across all contrasts, whereas hypermethylated probes showed a relative shift toward CpG‐dense regions, with increased representation in CpG islands and flanking shores compared with hypomethylated probes (Fig. 1e–l). Annotation of the functional elements further indicated that DMPs were concentrated near transcription start sites, with TSS1500 consistently representing the largest fraction of both hypo‐ and hypermethylated probes, followed by TSS200 and gene body (Fig. 1m–t).

Impact of aberrant DNA methylation on miRNA expression

Aberrant DNA methylation at CpG sites proximal to miRNA loci was associated with changes in miRNA expression. The most consistent signals were observed in CpG‐miRNA pairs unique to the suicide‐status‐stratified comparisons (MDD+S and MDD−S). Using CpG‐miRNA pairs within ±1500 kb of each miRNA gene, Spearman correlation analysis (P ≤ 0.05) demonstrated significant inverse (negative ρ) correlations, suggesting increased methylation at the promoter region corresponded to decreased miRNA expression and vice versa (Fig. 3a). In the MDD+S‐unique subset (common between C vs. MDD+/−S and C vs. MDD+S but not in C vs. MDD−S), hypermethylated CpGs exhibited significant negative correlations with their associated miRNAs, including cg06341821‐hsa‐miR‐574‐3p (ρ = −0.38, P = 7.25 × 10−3) and cg25451306‐hsa‐miR‐2110 (ρ = −0.29, P = 4.90 × 10−2) (Fig. 3b,c). Similarly, in the MDD−S‐unique set (shared between C vs. MDD+/−S and C vs. MDD−S but not in C vs. MDD+S), hypermethylated CpGs showed inverse associations with miRNA expression, cg06179179‐hsa‐miR‐595 (ρ = −0.32, P = 2.89 × 10−2) (Fig. 3d). Methylation at cg06179179 showed a significant positive association with age (P = 0.038) and a significant negative association with neuronal proportion (NeuN+) (P = 0.044). The C vs. MDD+S comparisons also revealed significant inverse relationships involving hypomethylated probe, cg22645159, with miRNA hsa‐miR‐122‐5p (ρ = −0.40, P = 4.48 × 10−3) (Fig. 3e) and hypermethylated probe cg20610269 as identified in the C vs. MDD+/−S comparison with hsa‐miR‐191‐5p (ρ = −0.32, P = 2.47 × 10−2) (Fig. 3f). Notably, cg22645159 exhibited a significant positive association with estimated neuronal proportion (NeuN+) (P = 0.0030), suggesting that cellular composition may partially contribute to methylation variation at this locus. These findings suggest that methylation signatures specific to suicide status (MDD+S) and non‐suicide status (MDD−S) include distinct CpG‐miRNA regulatory interactions.

miRNA target genes and pathway analysis

To infer the biological processes potentially impacted by subgroup‐specific miRNA dysregulation, we defined high‐confidence target genes for the MDD−S‐unique miR‐595 (Table S5) and MDD+S‐unique miR‐2110 (Table S6) and evaluated their functional organization in the brain using expression‐based clustering and pathway enrichment networks (Fig. 4). In MDD−S‐unique miR‐595, the predicted target gene set formed a coherent, highly connected functional landscape with prominent enrichment for immune‐ and cytokine‐related programs, including type I interferon receptor binding and related antiviral/innate immune signaling nodes (Fig. 4A). Hierarchical clustering of target gene expression further revealed that most of the miR‐595 target genes are highly expressed in the brain tissues (Fig. 4b). In contrast, MDD+S‐unique miR‐2110 targets exhibited a broader and more diverse enrichment architecture (Fig. 4c), dominated by synaptic/neuronal and signaling‐related categories, including ligand‐gated ion channel activity, receptor‐mediated signaling, cellular stress/response programs, and additional immune/trafficking terms such as positive regulation of leukocyte proliferation and phagosome. Expression analysis of the miR‐2110 target genes also showed a high expression pattern in different brain tissues (Fig. 4d). Collectively, these analyses suggest that subgroup‐specific miRNA‐linked regulation may converge on distinct biological themes: miR‐595 targets in MDD−S are preferentially associated with interferon/innate immune signaling, whereas miR‐2110 targets in MDD+S map to a more heterogeneous network enriched for ion channel/receptor signaling and synaptic/neuronal processes, consistent with suicide‐status–associated molecular differentiation.

Discussion

In this study, we integrated genome‐wide DNA methylation profiling in dlPFC with small RNA sequencing to define miRNA‐associated epigenetic alterations in MDD and to determine whether these alterations relate to miRNA expression and downstream biological pathways. A major observation was the progressive increase in the number and directionality of miRNA‐associated DMPs when MDD was stratified by suicide status. While the overall case–control comparison identified 139 miRNA‐linked DMPs, the MDD+S comparison against controls increased to 179 DMPs, and the direct contrast between MDD+S and MDD−S yielded the largest signal (235 DMPs), with a notable enrichment of hypermethylation (145 hyper vs. 90 hypo). This pattern argues against a single ‘MDD epigenetic signature’ and instead supports heterogeneity in which suicide status captures a biologically distinct state. Particularly hypermethylated probes showed relatively higher localization to CpG‐dense contexts (islands and shores) compared with hypomethylated probes, which were more common in OpenSea. Although OpenSea methylation changes can be functionally meaningful, the shift of hypermethylation toward CpG‐rich regions may indicate a stronger impact on promoter‐like regulatory elements controlling miRNA transcription, miRNA host genes, or local chromatin accessibility. 29

The positional distribution of DMPs further strengthens the idea of regulatory involvement. Across comparisons, DMPs were concentrated near transcription start sites, with TSS1500 representing the dominant feature class. In dlPFC, miRNA promoter methylation is linked to transcriptional dysregulation or altered promoter usage, 30 and this enrichment suggests that the identified DMPs are not simply stochastic epigenetic drift but may influence transcription initiation of miRNA genes. Importantly, genome‐wide circos visualization showed broad chromosomal dispersion rather than hotspot clustering.

A central goal of this work was to connect miRNA promoter methylation differences to miRNA expression. The CpG‐miRNA pairing strategy (promoter methylation within ≤1500 kb of the miRNA gene) allowed capture of cis‐regulatory relationships. Within this framework, significant CpG‐miRNA correlations were predominantly inverse, supporting a model in which altered methylation contributes to miRNA dysregulation. Importantly, the most consistent correlations were observed among pairs unique to the suicide‐status‐stratified contrasts, again pointing to a distinct regulatory architecture in MDD+S and MDD−S. For MDD+S‐unique pairs, hypermethylated CpGs were inversely correlated with miRNA abundance (e.g., cg06341821‐hsa‐miR‐574‐3p and cg25451306‐hsa‐miR‐2110), suggesting that increased methylation near these loci lowers the miRNA expression. Both hsa‐miR‐574‐3p and hsa‐miR‐2110 are known for their potential role as a biomarker for MDD. 31 , 32 For MDD−S‐unique pairs, cg06179179‐hsa‐miR‐595 showed a similar inverse relationship. hsa‐miR‐595 has been identified as a nerve‐immunity‐related microRNA that is downregulated in the plasma of individuals with MDD. 33 Despite broad cis pairing, only a limited number of CpG‐miRNA associations reached significance, consistent with the expectation that true methylation‐linked regulation is confined to a small subset of functional regulatory CpGs and mature miRNA levels are strongly influenced by post‐transcriptional processing and stability. 34 , 35

Pathway analyses of subgroup‐specific miRNA targets provide functional hypotheses consistent with established MDD biology while also highlighting divergence between MDD+S and MDD−S. Targets of MDD−S‐unique miR‐595 were enriched for immune and cytokine programs, particularly type I interferon receptor binding and related innate immune/antiviral signaling. Immune dysregulation and inflammatory signaling have long been implicated in MDD, and interferon‐related signatures are known to affect mood and cognition, including depressive symptoms induced by interferon‐based therapies. 36 The enrichment observed here suggests that in non‐suicide MDD, miRNA‐linked regulation may converge on immune signaling pathways that shape microglial activity, cytokine responses, and neuroimmune crosstalk in dlPFC. In contrast, targets of MDD+S‐unique miR‐2110 mapped to a broader network that prominently included ligand‐gated ion channel activity, receptor‐mediated signaling, and synaptic/neuronal processes, along with immune/trafficking terms such as leukocyte proliferation and phagosome. This mixed synaptic‐immune architecture is notable because suicide risk has been repeatedly linked to altered synaptic function, excitatory/inhibitory balance, and stress‐responsive neural circuitry. 37 Enrichment for ion channel and receptor signaling suggests that epigenetic‐miRNA mechanisms in MDD+S may be more tightly coupled to neuronal excitability and synaptic transmission, offering a plausible molecular substrate for suicide‐related phenotypes beyond general depressive symptomatology.

Taken together, our findings support a model in which suicide status stratifies MDD into biologically distinct epigenetic states at miRNA loci, with downstream consequences for miRNA expression and target networks. MDD−S appears more aligned with immune/interferon‐associated miRNA targeting, whereas MDD+S shows a stronger signature of neuronal signaling and synaptic regulation (with accompanying immune/trafficking components). This framework fits the broader literature proposing that MDD involves both neuroimmune and synaptic dysregulation but suggests that the relative weighting of these mechanisms and the regulatory routes that engage them may differ between suicide and non‐suicide MDD.

Several limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. Initially, methylation and gene expression were quantified in bulk dlPFC tissue; consequently, observed differences may reflect alterations in cellular composition, such as variations in neurons, glia, or immune infiltrates, in addition to changes in regulatory mechanisms within cells. Furthermore, the CpG‐miRNA pairing window (±1500 kb) encompasses long‐range cis associations but may also include pairs that are spatially proximal yet functionally unrelated; integrating chromatin interaction maps or enhancer annotations would improve causal inference. Lastly, target predictions are based on computational databases; although concordance between TargetScan and miRDB enhances confidence, experimental validation remains essential to confirm direct regulatory relationships in brain tissue. We also acknowledge that the relatively small sample size represents a limitation of the study, which may affect statistical power and the generalizability of the findings. Despite these limitations, this study offers an integrated epigenetic and miRNA framework for MDD and highlights suicide status as a key factor in molecular heterogeneity. The discovery of suicide‐specific CpG‐miRNA connections and unique target‐pathway enrichments suggests possible biomarkers and treatment targets. Future research incorporating cell‐type‐specific methylation and miRNA profiles, longitudinal studies, and functional experiments will be essential to establish causality and determine whether these methylation‐driven miRNA networks can be used for risk assessment or targeted treatments in MDD, especially related to suicide risk.
