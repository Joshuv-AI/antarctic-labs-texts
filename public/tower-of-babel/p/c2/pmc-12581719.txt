Studies on the single-chain antibody (CRAb20) effectively protects chickens against very virulent infectious bursal disease virus
Authors: Yue Li, Zhiying Wei, Yulei Liu, Junyan Zhang, Xiaolong Fan, Han Zhou, Guojie Ding, Yijing Li, Xiaona Wang, Lijie Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfectious Bursal Disease Virus (IBDV) infection leads to the destruction of B lymphocytes in chicks, resulting in severe immunosuppression. In recent years, the prevalence of very virulent IBDV (vvIBDV) have significantly increased the complexity of preventing and controlling Infectious Bursal Disease (IBD). Anti-viral single chain antibody (scFv) has a small molecular weight, strong penetration, and can be efficiently expressed by genetic engineering. In this study, we utilized both a high-safety Lactobacillus expression system suitable for oral delivery and a high-expression Escherichia coli system to expressed scFv (CRAb20). Subsequently, we evaluated the in vitro neutralization effect of CRAb20 against vvIBDV and its protective efficacy in chicks. The results demonstrated that CRAb20 can significantly suppress the replication of IBDV in vitro. Importantly, oral administration of recombinant Lactobacillus expressing CRAb20 or intraperitoneal injection of recombinant CRAb20 can significantly mitigate the detrimental effects of vvIBDV on the bursa of Fabricius, improved the survival rate, and offered both prophylactic and therapeutic benefits against IBDV infection in chickens. These findings highlight the potential of scFv-based approaches as a promising strategy for controlling IBDV.Keywords: scFv, IBDV, Escherichia coli, Lactobacillus, Neutralization

Introduction

Infectious bursal disease (IBD), commonly known as Gumboro disease, poses a significant threat to the poultry industry due to its highly contagious nature (Myint et al., 2021) The causative agent, infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV), belongs to the Avivavirus genus within the Birnaviridae family (Müller et al., 1979; Lian et al., 2022). Characteristic clinical signs of IBD include bursal atrophy, growth retardation, immunosuppression, and increased mortality (Li et al., 2023). Vaccination is considered the most effective approach for the prevention and control of IBD. However, despite their extensive application, conventional IBD vaccines-such as live-attenuated and inactivated formulations-encounter several notable challenges (Okura et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2022). One major limitation is the interference caused by maternally derived antibodies (MDA), which have the potential to neutralize live vaccine viruses (Skeeles et al., 1979; Fan et al., 2020). This necessitates highly precise, yet often difficult to implement, vaccination schedules in young chicks, thereby leaving a critical period during which the chicken remain susceptible to infection. Furthermore, the continuous emergence of antigenically variant strains and very virulent IBDV (vvIBDV) can overcome the immunity conferred by some traditional vaccines, leading to suboptimal protection (Fan et al., 2019; Pikuła et al., 2020; Shirokov et al., 2018). These limitations underscore the urgent need for novel intervention strategies that can circumvent these issues. Recombinant neutralizing antibodies, particularly single-chain variable fragments (scFv), represent a promising alternative. Their mechanism of action-direct viral neutralization-is independent of the host's immune system, thus potentially bypassing MDA interference (Bird et al., 1988; Ahmad et al., 2012; Muñoz-López et al., 2022). Recombinant neutralizing antibodies have been developed for the diagnosis and treatment of various viral infections, including the HTNV, HBV, PEDV and HIV (van Dorsten et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2010). Australia's Dr. J. Ignjatovic Laboratory made a breakthrough discovery with the identification of CRAb20, a chicken scFv exhibiting remarkable properties against IBDV, as first demonstrated in vitro. However, its in vivo protective efficacy and delivery strategies remain entirely unexplored (Sapats et al., 2003).

ScFv can be expressed in prokaryotic or eukaryotic expression systems, The Escherichia coli expression system is particularly advantageous for the rapid and cost-effective production of recombinant antibodies, yielding high titers of functional protein suitable for parenteral administration (intramuscular or intraperitoneal injection). To circumvent the practical challenges of mass inoculation in poultry farming and to harness the benefits of mucosal immunity, oral delivery presents an attractive alternative. Engineered Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) can efficiently secrete functional scFv antibodies directly into the gut lumen (Wang et al., 2016; Bermúdez-Humarán et al., 2011). Oral delivery of antibody-secreting LAB thus represents a novel strategy for the non-invasive, mass application of biologics, potentially enabling direct neutralization of IBDV at the primary site of replication and offering a practical and welfare-friendly approach for flock-wide protection.

In this study, we engineered and expressed recombinant Escherichia coli and recombinant chicken Lactobacillus to secrete the single-chain antibody CRAb20, which is well-known for its neutralizing effect against IBDV. The resulting soluble antibody exhibited discernible binding and inhibitory properties against IBDV. Comprehensive in vitro and in vivo assessments were conducted to evaluate the efficacy in preventing and treating IBDV infection. The outcomes of our research provide a foundational framework for the development of scFv-based therapeutics for the prophylaxis and management of IBDV infections.

Expression and purification of recombinant proteins

Recombinant Escherichia coli (pCold-His-TF-CRAb20/BL21) were cultivated in LB medium supplemented with ampicillin (100 μg/mL), followed by 1 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) for 20 hours at 16°C. The bacterial precipitate was collected for ultrasonic fragmentation, while the supernatant was obtained through centrifugation. The recombinant protein (His-TF-CRAb20) was purified using a His-tag protein purification kit (Beyotime, China). Recombinant Lactobacillus (pPG-USP45-CRAb20-Flag/M11) were cultured for 18 hours at 37°C in MRS (Hopebol, China) medium supplemented with chloramphenicol (10 μg/mL), and the secreted CRAb20 protein (USP45-CRAb20-Flag) was harvested from the supernatant after concentration using the trichloroacetic acid (TCA) technique (Koontz, 2014, Sapats et al., 2003). Briefly, 5 mL of TCA was added to 50 mL of culture supernatant and on ice for at least 30 min to precipitate the proteins, then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 30 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 2 mL of cold acetone, stand at −20°C for 30 min, then centrifuged again at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and washed twice. After discarding the supernatant, the tube was inverted until the acetone evaporated completely, and the pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of PBS. Both purified proteins were stored at −80°C for future use.

Western blot

The protein samples were resolved using 10 % SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, Milford, MA, USA). The primary antibody used was a 1:200 dilution of antibodies (mouse anti-His for His-TF-CRAb2 and mouse anti-Flag for USP45-CRAb20-Flag), which was incubated at 37°C for one hour. This was followed by incubation with a 1:5000 dilution of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-Mouse IgG secondary antibody at 37°C for 30 minutes. The membranes were then washed three times with PBST (0.1 % Tween-20). Finally, the immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Solarbio, Beijing, China).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Sandwich ELISA was employed to evaluate the binding ability of CRAb20 to vvIBDV (LJ-5 strain, GenBank: MT133301, subsequently, it will be referred to as LJ-5). LJ-5 virus suspension were utilized as antigens for coating 96-well microplate (100 μL/well) overnight. Recombinant protein (USP45-CRAb20-Flag, His-TF-CRAb20) collected after purification as the primary antibody, rabbit negative serum and rabbit anti-LJ-5 polyclonal antibody were used as negative and positive control, respectively, then mouse anti-CRAb20 polyclonal antibody as the secondary antibodies, finally horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG was used at a dilution of 5000-fold to do the incubation. A TMB chromogenic solution was added, incubated, and the reaction was stopped. OD450 was measured to assess CRAb20 binding ability to LJ-5 strain (A sample with a P/N ratio of the OD values greater than 2.0 is considered positive). Indirect ELISA was employed to detect cytokines levels. Serum samples were collected and measured the levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10, IFN-γ, and TLR3 using ELISA assay kits (MEIMIAN, China).

Immunofluorescence analysis (IFA)

LJ-5 virus infected DT40 cells at a concentration of 102 ELD50 in 200 µL, with a cell density of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Uninfected cells served as negative controls. Cells were harvested at 48 hours post-infection, washed with PBS, and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde at room temperature. A portion of the cell suspension was then transferred to a glass slide and further fixed with absolute ethanol. After drying, the cells were permeabilized with a Triton X-100 solution and then blocked with BSA. The primary antibodies (His-TF-CRAb20, USP45-CRAb20-Flag, and rabbit anti-LJ-5 polyclonal antibody for the positive control group) were incubated with cells in a humidified chamber and further treated with mouse anti-CRAb20 polyclonal antibody, followed by FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (ZSGB-Bio, China). The positive control group received only FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ZSGB-Bio, China). Finally, all groups were stained with DAPI. Cells were mounted in a 30 % glycerol solution and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

Assessment neutralizing activity of CRAb20

The LJ-5 was mixed with the same volume of purified His-TF-CRAb20 protein and concentrated USP45-CRAb20-Flag protein, and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. After that, DT40 cells were infected with the LJ-5 viruses, virus-protein mixture, respectively. The cells were collected at 48 h post infection, and RT-qPCR was employed to quantify the copy number of the LJ-5 virus using the following primers F3/R3 (sequences shown in Table 1).

Effect of CRAb20 on chickens against vvIBDV infection

Forty-nine specific pathogen-free (SPF) white leghorn chickens were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center at the Harbin Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. The chickens were housed in negative-pressure poultry isolators (the temperature was maintained at 32°C initially and then reduced by 3°C weekly, and the relative humidity was kept at 50-60 %) under strictly controlled environmental conditions. They were randomly assigned to seven experimental groups (n = 7 per group, Table 2), including three treatment groups (Groups I, II, and III), two prophylactic groups (Groups IV and V), one challenge control group (Group VI), and one PBS control group (Group VII). At 19 days post-birth (dpb), chickens in Groups I and II received oral administration of recombinant Lactobacillus: pPG-USP45-CRAb20-Flag/M11 (hereinafter referred to as pPG-CRAb20/M11) and the empty vector control strain pPG/M11, respectively. The administration was performed over two consecutive days, followed by a one-day interval, and this cycle was repeated three times. In Group III, purified His-TF-CRAb20 (hereinafter referred to as CRAb20) was administered via intraperitoneal injection 24 hours prior to the challenge (at 27 dpb). For therapeutic intervention, Group IV received oral administration of pPG-CRAb20/M11, and Group V received intraperitoneal injection of His-TF-CRAb20, both starting 24 hours post-challenge and continuing daily for 7 consecutive days. At 28 dpb, Groups I to VI were challenged with vvIBDV-LJ-5 via ocular, intranasal, and oral routes. The control group (Group VII) received an equivalent dose of sterile PBS and was maintained under a standard feeding regimen. Following the challenge, a 7-day observation period was conducted, during which clinical symptoms, mortality rates, and body weight were recorded on a daily basis. The average daily gain (ADG) for each group was subsequently calculated. All surviving chickens were humanely euthanized 35 days post-challenge.

Viral load detection in tissues by qPCR

1.0 g samples of each organ (cysts, spleens, and thymuses) were weighed and transferred into 2 mL centrifuge tubes. Subsequently, 1 mL of sterile PBS and magnetic beads were added to each tube. The mixtures were thoroughly homogenized using a tissue homogenizer and then centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 minutes. RNA was extracted using Trizol that were reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and the viral load in each organ was quantified by qPCR using CFX96 real-time system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with iTag Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Table 1 (F3/R3) lists the primers information used.

Clinical signs and histopathological analysis

During the monitoring period, the body weights (BW) and weights of the bursa of Fabricius (BF) of the chickens in each group were recorded, and the bursal index (the bursa weight (mg) × 1000/body weight (BW; kg)) and bursa of Fabricius body index (BBIX: (BF/BWIBDV-infection group)/(BF/BWcontrol group) were calculated. At the end of monitoring period bursa of Fabricius samples were collected from the surviving chickens in the Group I, Group Ⅲ, Group Ⅳ, Group Ⅴ and the dead chickens in the Group Ⅵ, Group Ⅶ for pathological examination and recording of organ-specific changes. A suitable amount of organs were fixed in a 4 % paraformaldehyde solution for over 24 hours to prepare histopathological sections stained with HE dye.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 7.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The results are presented as mean ± SD of triplicate measurements. Tukey's multiple comparison test was employed to assess the differences between the control groups. Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05, indicating a significant difference, and**P < 0.01,***P < 0.001 indicating a highly significant difference.

Survival analysis of chickens

In the Group Ⅱ and Group Ⅵ, chickens began succumbing to the infection on the fourth day post-challenge, while in the recombinant bacteria treatment groups (Group I, Group Ⅲ, Group Ⅳ, Group Ⅴ), mortality started on the fifth day. Survival rates exhibited variability, with the Group I (Oral pPG-CRAb20/M11+LJ-5) showing a rate of 28.57 % (2/7) and the Group Ⅲ (CRAb20 injection+LJ-5) at 42.85 % (3/7). In the Group Ⅳ (LJ-5+Oral pPG-CRAb20/M11), the survival rate was 14.29 % (1/7), whereas in the Group Ⅳ (LJ-5+CRAb20 injection), it reached 28.57 % (2/7). All chickens in the Group Ⅶ survived, while none in the Group Ⅵ or the Group Ⅱ survived (Fig. 9). These findings suggest that CRAb20 produced by the recombinant bacteria provide some degree of therapeutic and preventive benefits against vvIBDV infection in chickens and the protective effect of the intraperitoneal injection of CRAb20 prevention Group (Group Ⅲ) was significant (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

IBDV remains a persistent challenge within the poultry industry, causing significant economic losses due to its highly contagious nature and detrimental impact on the immune systems of young chickens (Myint et al., 2021). This necessitates the development of effective preventive and therapeutic strategies. The control of IBD largely relies on vaccination using live-attenuated or inactivated vaccines (Shah et al., 2022; Fan et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2020). However, these conventional strategies are hampered by several significant limitations. First, the presence of MDA in young chicks can neutralize live vaccine viruses, leading to inadequate immune activation (Skeeles et al., 1979; Fan et al., 2020). Second, the emergence of antigenic variant strains and vvIBDV challenges the efficacy of existing vaccines (Fan et al., 2019; Pikuła et al., 2020; Shirokov et al., 2018). The development of recombinant neutralizing antibodies, particularly scFv, presents a promising alternative.

This study evaluated the protective efficacy of CRAb20 (Sapats et al., 2003; Ignjatovic et al., 2006), a IBDV-neutralizing scFv identified through phage display technology, against IBDV infection in chickens. We move beyond a single expression system to evaluate a novel dual-platform strategy for scFv delivery: E. coli for high-yield, cost-effective production of scFv (Lou et al., 2025); and Lactobacillus as a safe, oral delivery vehicle to secrete functional scFv directly into the gut lumen (Saeed et al., 2025). The injected CRAb20 ensures immediate bioavailability of a precise, high dose but is impractical for large-scale flock application due to cost and animal stress. Oral delivery is scalable and low-cost but faces significant hurdles in achieving a sufficient effective dose due to gastrointestinal barriers. In conclusion, the incomplete protection observed in this study is a direct result of the fundamental trade-offs between the two delivery strategies. The timing of CRAb20 administration, relative to viral challenge, played a significant role in its protective efficacy, regardless of the expression system used. Prophylactic administration, where CRAb20 was introduced before viral challenge, consistently resulted in better protection compared to therapeutic administration, where CRAb20 was administered after the establishment of infection. This underscores the importance of early intervention, ideally before viral exposure, to maximize the protective benefits of CRAb20.

Interestingly, while both systems successfully produced functional CRAb20, the Escherichia coli-expressed His-TF-CRAb20 demonstrated slightly superior protection in chickens, with a higher survival rate and less severe bursal damage compared to the recombinant Lactobacillus group. On the premise of ensuring the same antibody dosage in vitro, the oral Lactobacillus delivery therapy itself also faces significant biological barriers. The secreted CRAb20 protein is prone to degradation in the low-gastric-acid pH environment and by intestinal proteases. Moreover, Lactobacillus must survive under these harsh conditions, colonize in the intestine, and continuously produce and secrete a sufficient quantity of active proteins to exert their effects. Our research findings emphasize the prospects and challenges of using Lactobacillus for oral antibody delivery. Future research will focus on optimizing the oral delivery system, such as employing microencapsulation technology to protect Lactobacillus from gastric acid damage, or attempting the bacterial surface display system to optimize the protein display mode and stability.

Our study demonstrates that both parenterally administered (intraperitoneal injection of CRAb20) and orally delivered (feed recombinant Lactobacillus) can provide partial protection against IBDV challenge. The efficacy rates in our results are indeed lower than those typically reported for commercial live-attenuated or inactivated vaccines in chicken (Shah et al., 2022; Fan et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2020). However, the value of our approach lies not in directly competing with these established vaccines in ideal scenarios, but in addressing their inherent limitations. Firstly, our recombinant antibody-based therapy functions through direct viral neutralization, independent of the host's immune system. This mechanism has the potential to circumvent maternal antibody interference-a major hurdle that complicates vaccination schedules and often leads to immunization failures in the field. Secondly, the production of antibodies in heterologous systems like Escherichia coli and Lactobacillus offers a defined and highly safe biologics platform, free from the risks of pathogen reversion, adventitious agents, or immunosuppression associated with some live vaccines. The partial protection in our result is not an endpoint but a starting point that reveals clear opportunities for optimization through synergistic combinations. The modest efficacy likely stems from insufficient bioactive antibody (CRAb20) concentration at the site of infection, which could be dramatically improved by later research strategies. First, the co-administration of mucosal adjuvants could potentiate the local gut immune response, enhancing antigen uptake and creating a more robust immunomodulatory environment (Li et al., 2024). Second, a combinatorial therapy approach holds great promise. Administering our oral platform alongside a low-dose conventional vaccine could create a synergistic effect: the scFv provides immediate passive immunity to bridge the critical early window, while the vaccine primes a sustained active immune response. Finally, technological advancements in delivery, such as microencapsulation of the recombinant bacteria to enhance gastric survival and target release in the intestine, could significantly increase the effective dose delivered (Brittain and Finbloom, 2025; Jiménez-Gastelum et al., 2025). Exploring the synergistic combinations of scFvs will be the main focus of our subsequent experiments, aiming to transform this innovative platform into a robust and practical intervention strategy for the development of novel formulations against IBDV.

Previous research has indicated that CRAb20 can specifically bind to the VP3 protein of IBDV (Sapats et al., 2003; Ignjatovic et al., 2006). We hypothesize that the mechanism by which CRAb20 inhibits IBDV may be that its specific binding to the VP3 protein of IBDV prevents the formation of a complex between the VP3 and VP1 proteins. This affects the replication and assembly processes of the virus, thereby suppressing the proliferation of IBDV. In this study, we detected the viral loads in the bursa of Fabricius, spleen, and thymus of chickens in each group after virus challenge. We found that both the CRAb20 treatment group and the prevention group could significantly reduce the viral loads in tissues, which indicates that CRAb20 inhibits the replication of IBDV and mitigates the damage to the organism. Cytokines are a diverse class of small protein molecules that play pivotal roles in regulating hematological function, inflammatory responses, and immune processes in the body (Liu et al., 2021). Our findings demonstrated that IBDV infection induced a significant increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines, indicating a strong inflammatory response, and CRAb20 effectively alleviates this cytokine storm. Although the exact cellular mechanism was not fully elucidated in this study, we assume that the primary mechanism by which CRAb20 reduces inflammation is through the reduction of the viral load of vvIBDV, thus cutting off the excessive inflammatory response (cytokine storm) at its source. Additionally, study has reported that IBDV can infect macrophages and trigger a strong inflammatory response (Huang et al., 2025). A limitation of this study is that we did not directly measure the activity of immune cells (such as macrophages). Therefore, investigating the effects of CRAb20 on the polarization and function of macrophages will be the focus of our future work.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study successfully demonstrates the potential of scFv-based approaches for controlling IBDV infection. The expression of CRAb20 in both Escherichia coli and Lactobacillus yielded functional antibodies capable of neutralizing IBDV and protecting chickens from bursal damage and mortality. While Escherichia coli-expressed His-TF-CRAb20 exhibited slightly better protection in this study, further optimization of expression systems, delivery methods, and dosage regimens could enhance the efficacy of scFv-based strategies against IBDV and pave the way for their potential application in the poultry industry. Lactobacillus expressing CRAb20 shows promise as a potential oral prophylactic against IBDV. Further optimization of expression levels or delivery methods could enhance its protective effect.
