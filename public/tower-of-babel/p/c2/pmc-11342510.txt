Association of serum calcium, vitamin D, and C-reactive protein with all-cause and cause-specific mortality in an osteoarthritis population in the UK: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Kai Fu, Qianying Cai, Xinzhong Jin, Lingxiao Chen, Win Min Oo, Vicky Duong, Guangyi Li, Zhaohua Zhu, Changhai Ding, Changqing Zhang, Youshui Gao, David J Hunter
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoarthritis is a prevalent musculoskeletal condition, but the role of specific serum biomarkers, such as calcium, vitamin D, and C-reactive protein (CRP), in predicting mortality among individuals with osteoarthritis remains unclear.MethodsThis observational study analyzed longitudinal data from over 500,000 participants in the UK Biobank, identifying those with osteoarthritis using ICD-9/10 codes or self-reported history. We performed multivariable cox-regression and flexible parametric survival model (FPSM) for survival analysis, with adjustments made through the inverse probability of treatment weight (IPTW) for baseline covariates identified by directed acyclic graphs (DAGs).ResultsOf the 49,082 osteoarthritis population, the average age was 60.69 years, with 58.7% being female. During the follow-up period exceeding 15 years, a total of 5,522 people with osteoarthritis died. High serum calcium levels, compared to normal serum calcium levels, were significantly associated with all-cause mortality (hazard ratio (HR) 1.33, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.11, 1.59), cardiovascular diseases (CVD)-related deaths (HR 1.55, 95% CI 1.05, 2.29), and other deaths (HR 1.59, 95% CI 1.20, 2.11). Low serum calcium levels, compared to normal serum calcium levels, was linked with CVD-related deaths (HR 2.06, 95% CI 1.02, 4.14). Vitamin D insufficiency, compared to sufficient vitamin D levels, was correlated with all-cause mortality (HR 1.22, 95% CI 1.13, 1.33), CVD-related deaths (HR 1.43, 95% CI 1.20, 1.72), and other deaths (HR 1.26, 95% CI 1.09, 1.45) but not with cancer-related deaths. High serum CRP levels, compared to normal CRP levels, were associated with all outcomes (all-cause mortality: HR 1.22, 95% CI 1.12, 1.33; CVD-related death: HR 1.24, 95%CI 1.03, 1.49; cancer-related death: HR 1.23, 95% CI 1.09, 1.40; other deaths: HR 1.19, 95%CI 1.03, 1.38).ConclusionsBoth high and low serum calcium levels, elevated CRP, and vitamin D insufficiency are potential predictors of increased mortality risk in the osteoarthritis population. These findings emphasize the importance of monitoring and possibly addressing these serum biomarkers in osteoarthritis populations to improve long-term outcomes. Further studies are needed to understand the underlying mechanisms and to propose therapeutic interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-19825-8.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Calcium, Vitamin D, C-Reactive protein, Mortality, Cohort studies, Biobanks

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (osteoarthritis) is a disorder involving movable joints and stands as a prevalent and incapacitating ailment that constitutes a significant and escalating healthcare challenge [1]. Not only is osteoarthritis associated with an array of comorbidities and chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease (CVD) and diabetes, but recent years have also marked a growing recognition of its linkage to increased mortality rates [2–4]. The severity of osteoarthritis-associated pain and disability, particularly those affecting mobility, can substantially predict all-cause mortality [5]. Reduction in physical activity, a common trait among osteoarthritis patients, could compromise defenses against CVD. Concurrently, continuous tissue damage might lead to chronic inflammation, potentially instigating long-standing conditions such as cardiovascular and neurodegenerative diseases, diabetes, and cancer [6]. At present, the mechanisms underlying the increased mortality in osteoarthritis remain unclear.

Biomarkers often serve as insightful indicators in disease diagnosis, prognosis, and management. In this context, we selected serum calcium, vitamin D, and C-reactive protein (CRP) for scrutiny due to their respective significance and potential associations with osteoarthritis outcomes. Serum calcium, integral for both bone health and muscle contractions, can present a health paradox which underscores the complex role of calcium in human health, where its benefits in one aspect can pose risks in another. Elevated serum calcium levels have been linked to lifespan reduction and an increased risk of other health conditions, including CVD, kidney disease, and chronic pancreatitis [7, 8]. Interestingly, research indicates that genetically higher serum calcium levels could be associated with a lower risk of osteoarthritis, yet increased risk of hypertension and coronary atherosclerotic diseases, etc. [9]. Vitamin D is a significant micronutrient with multiple roles, including calcium absorption and immunomodulation [10]. It has been shown to influence various immune processes, which could help mitigate the progression of chronic diseases such as osteoarthritis through its immunomodulatory effects [11]. Vitamin D deficiency is linked with the risks of many chronic diseases, including osteoarthritis, where its role in modulating immune responses may be particularly relevant [12]. However, the specific role and impact of vitamin D within the osteoarthritis context are yet to be unequivocally determined, as prior epidemiological studies have produced varied outcomes [13]. Similarly, the levels of CRP, an inflammatory marker, could potentially be elevated in individuals with osteoarthritis, but the evidence remains conflicting [14]. While some studies suggest that elevated CRP is associated with increased severity of osteoarthritis symptoms and progression, other research finds no significant correlation, highlighting the complexity of CRP’s role in osteoarthritis pathophysiology [15]. Further insights into the roles of CRP underscore its significance not only in systemic inflammation but also in potentially elevating cardiovascular risks, which are crucial considerations in the context of osteoarthritis mortality [16]. The choice was further influenced by the constraints of the UK Biobank dataset, which does not encompass all serum markers, such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) [17]. This omission could affect our study’s ability to fully evaluate the role of inflammation in osteoarthritis mortality, as ESR is a key indicator of inflammation.

Against this backdrop, this study delves into the long-term effects of serum calcium levels, vitamin D, and CRP levels on mortality outcomes in individuals with osteoarthritis. The primary hypothesis is that elevated or reduced levels of these serum markers are associated with increased all-cause and cause-specific mortality in this population. Using the longitudinal data from the UK Biobank study, we aim to examine the associations of these serum markers with all-cause and cause-specific mortality in individuals with osteoarthritis. Our research rigorously adjusts for multiple confounding factors, thereby providing robust insights essential for understanding osteoarthritis management and improving its prognosis.

Methods

Study design and participants

We completed a prospective, population-based cohort study using data from 502,412 participants registered in the UK Biobank (http://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk) [18]. Every participant (age range: 40–69 years) granted informed consent and underwent baseline evaluations between 2006 and 2010 at various assessment centers in Scotland, England, and Wales. A comprehensive collection of data, including sociodemographic factors, lifestyle, and medical history, was amassed via questionnaires. These questionnaires were either delivered through touchscreen interfaces or through face-to-face interviews. In conjunction, physical metrics and biological samples were obtained [18, 19].

Participants with a follow-up duration of less than 24 months were excluded (n = 2,639) from the study as our focus was primarily on investigating long-term associations, and abnormal serum markers may be a manifestation of an existing illness [20]. This duration minimizes the effect of acute biomarker fluctuations due to recent illnesses, ensuring the focus remains on chronic osteoarthritis conditions. Additionally, matching the follow-up period with similar studies ensures consistency and strengthens our conclusions. Consequently, our cohort comprised 499,773 qualifying participants. Of whom, we identified 49,082 individuals with osteoarthritis to be included in this study, and the serum samples were collected after the osteoarthritis diagnosis. The designated osteoarthritis cases included participants who (1) had an osteoarthritis diagnosis before 2010 or (2) mentioned an osteoarthritis history during the preliminary assessment and were subsequently diagnosed within the follow-up window. More details can be found in supplementary materials (appendix 1, page 3).

Exposures: serum calcium, vitamin D, and CRP

During the initial assessment, bio-samples were subjected to routine serum biomarker analyses.

Bio-samples in the initial UK Biobank assessment were analyzed using advanced instruments and methods, complying with the ISO 17025:2005 standards for precise biomarker quantification. Biomarkers were measured at baseline (2006–2010) and during a follow-up in 2012/2013, allowing analysis of their stability over time for robust longitudinal studies. Quality controls and external quality assurance schemes ensured consistent assay performance and accuracy. We primarily focused on calcium, vitamin D, and CRP as our exposures of interest. To account for potential bias in serum calcium measurement caused by albumin levels, we utilized albumin-adjusted serum calcium values. For the association analyses, we stratified the three exposures into specific categories based on the established reference cut-off values from the general UK population, and these thresholds are based on clinical guidelines and epidemiological studies that correlate these levels with health outcomes [21–23]. Specifically, serum calcium levels were segmented into three categories: low (< 2.19mmol/l), medium (2.19-2.56mmol/l), and high (> 2.56mmol/l) [21]. Serum vitamin D (25-hydroxy-vitamin D) was classified as either insufficient (< 50 nmol/l) or sufficient [22], and serum CRP was determined as indicating inflammation (> 3 mg/l) or within the normal range (0 to 3 mg/l) [23] (Table 1).

Outcome

Participant data were linked with the national mortality records available within the UK Biobank. Our analysis continued until 12th November 2021, with a median follow-up duration of 12 years (range: 2 to 15.7 years). The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. We also examined cause-specific mortalities, emphasizing CVD-related and cancer-related deaths due to their recognized significance in mortality patterns, especially in cohorts with osteoarthritis conditions [2]. It’s crucial to note that the cause of death classification was derived from available ICD-10 codes within the UK Biobank, rather than direct cause-of-death information. Specifically, CVD deaths were signified by ICD-10 codes starting with ‘I’, while cancer deaths were marked by codes initiating with ‘C’ [24, 25].

Identification of confounding variables using DAGs

We extracted covariates from the baseline assessment data, including sociodemographic details, lifestyle habits, and health indicators as described below (Refer to Supplemental file for details). Ethnicity was dichotomized into “white” and “others”. Lifestyle factors including smoking status (current smoker, former smoker and non-smoker), alcohol consumption (categorized by frequency: never, occasional, moderate, frequent), sleep duration(≥ 7 h/day and ≤ 9 h/day or < 7 h/day or > 9 h/day), physical activity (measured using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ)) and supplemental calcium/vitamin D (yes or not) were collected using a touch-screen self-report questionnaire. The Townsend deprivation index (TDI) was used to represent socioeconomic status, circumventing the direct use of individual education, employment, and income data [26].

Key health status markers were evaluated using specific criteria. Hypertension was identified based on ICD-9/10 codes, blood pressure measurements, self-reported history, or use of antihypertensive medications [27]. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) [28] was diagnosed by an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 ml/min/1.73m2 or urine albumin creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥ 3 mg/mmol (detailed in appendix 1, page 6). The Charlson comorbidity index (CCI), used as an indicator for general health status, was computed from ICD-9/10 codes before 2010 (appendix 1, page 4–5). However, it is noteworthy to emphasize that while the CCI includes conditions like CKD and hypertension, our study adopted a more nuanced criterion for these conditions, given their strong link to serum exposures, particularly serum calcium levels. Discrepancies in the baseline prevalence of these conditions, when determined via ICD codes versus our detailed criteria, were conspicuous.

We employed directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) to elucidate potential confounders and mitigate bias related to conditioning on a collider [29–31]. Using DAGs as a guide, we identified potential confounding variables and the minimal sufficient adjusted sets (MSAS) for our multivariable analyses (appendix 1, pages 7–9). We specified the variables included in the MSAS based on their relevance to osteoarthritis outcomes and their potential to confound the relationships between serum markers and health outcomes (e.g. mortality). The MSAS encompassed sociodemographic elements (baseline age, ethnicity, gender, TDI), lifestyle factors (alcohol consumption, smoking status, physical activity IPAQ scores, sleep duration), baseline health metrics (body mass index (BMI), CCI, CKD, hypertension), serum cholesterol and the other two serum biomarkers.

Statistical methods

Descriptive analysis

Baseline and subsequent follow-ups were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were displayed as either means ± standard deviations (SD) or medians with respective ranges if the data were skewed. In contrast, categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. We detailed incidence rates alongside estimated cumulative incidences for both primary (all-cause mortality) and secondary outcomes (cause-specific mortality).

Inference analysis

Initially, a five-knot (positioned at the 5th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 95th percentiles) age-sex adjusted restricted cubic spline (RCS) was employed to scrutinize the relationship between exposures and primary outcomes, ensuring exposure categorization’s validity. Using the RCS analysis, we reviewed the rationale behind the established abnormal cut-off values in UK populations, which relate to their impact on all-cause mortality and the corresponding hazard ratios (HRs) (appendix 2, page 5). Given the disproportional hazard assumptions observed in variables such as baseline age and CCI (identified through log-minus log-survival curves and Schoenfeld residuals), we opted for flexible parametric survival models (FPSM). These models harness natural splines for the log-cumulative hazard, facilitating the direct calculation of hazard and time-varying effects [32]. Their application is widespread in survival analyses to address non-proportional effects [33–35]. Consequently, HRs and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were derived from both the Cox proportional hazard regression and FPSM. We optimized FPSM based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) criteria, encompassing the baseline log-cumulative hazard with 4 degrees of freedom and time-varying coefficients for age, BMI and CCI.

We conducted inverse probability of treatment weight (IPTW) based analyses to fully adjust measured confounding [36]. Effects stemming from IPTW analysis for serum vitamin D and CRP were reported once probability scores proved apt and covariate balances between groups (standard mean differences between treat and control groups) were confirmed. However, due to probability score distribution concerns for IPTW (the markedly variation in probability score distribution across serum calcium levels groups), serum calcium levels’ effects were detailed using Cox proportional hazard regression if the estimated effects were found to be similar to those with FPSM.

Taking into account potential influences of calcium and vitamin D levels based on supplemental history (vitamin D and calcium supplementation), initial CKD, and baseline age and gender, we stratified the osteoarthritis group for subgroup analyses. Specifically, we defined four subgroups: (1) Baseline calcium supplemental history, (2) Baseline vitamin D supplemental history,, (3) Baseline CKD, and (4) based on baseline age (below 60 years or not) and gender. No statistical interactions were found between stratified factors and the primary exposed biomarkers, as well as baseline age and gender (P for interaction > 0.05). Within these subgroups, associations between exposures and all-cause mortality were ascertained using Cox proportional hazard regression models. Both this and FPSM regression analyses were adjusted for the confounding variables in the MSAS.

Sensitivity analysis

To ensure the robustness of our results, we conducted several sensitivity analyses. Albumin-adjusted calcium’s alternative cut-off values were established through (1) 99% reference interval values for osteoarthritis participants and (2) age and sex-specific values extracted from a preceding study based on UK Biobank data [21]. Notably, as around 25% of baseline data (specifically IPAQ) was missing, we re-performed association analyses applying multiple imputations via chained equations (MICE) for the missing value of each covariate. We imputed 20 data sets and ultimately pooled their estimates from multivariable Cox regression models according to Rubin’s rules [37] (refer to appendix 1, page 10 for baseline variables used in multiple imputation model). Additionally, given the potential impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on mortality patterns, we added a sensitivity analysis by delineating the timeline before the onset of the pandemic (up to 31 January 2020: the date of the first recorded UK COVID-19 case). This allowed us to assess the impact of serum biomarkers on mortality independent of the confounding effects related to COVID-19.

Computational tools

Analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute) and R (version 4.3.1, R Core Team). DAGitty 3.0 software was employed to craft and inspect DAGs [38]. For FPSM and IPTW, we utilized the “rstpm2” and “twang” (DOI: 10.32614/CRAN.package.twang) packages, respectively [39]. The “referenceIntervals” package (DOI: 10.32614/CRAN.package.referenceIntervals) established reference bands for albumin-adjusted calcium in osteoarthritis subjects, while the “mice” package was used to conduct multiple imputations for missing data [40]. Statistical significance was affirmed when the 95% CIs for HRs lacked overlap with the value of 1.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The flow chart illustrates the participation of individuals in this study (Fig. 1). A total of 49,082 participants were included in the analyses. Of these, 28,827 (58.7%) were female, and the average age was 60.69 ± 6.54 years. The majority of participants, specifically 92.8%, were white. The baseline characteristics between survival outcomes are displayed in Table 1. In general, people who survived during the follow-up tend to be younger, more physically active and have a higher household income. Compared to the general population, the osteoarthritis group were predominantly female, older, more likely to be former smokers, and less physically active. Additionally, they had a higher prevalence of hypertension (67.5%), an increased BMI (29.51 ± 5.41), and reported lower household income. Moreover, a significant portion (33.6%) of the osteoarthritis population reported sleep durations of either < 7 h/day or > 9 h/day (appendix 2, pages 3–4).

Serum markers and outcome

The median follow-up times were 12 years (interquartile range: 11.9–13.3 years). Over the follow-up, there were 5,522 recorded deaths, comprised of 1,128 CVD-related, 2,373 cancer-related, and 2,021 from other causes.

Using RCS analysis adjusted for baseline age and sex, we identified a non-linear relationship between serum calcium levels and all-cause mortality (appendix 2, page 5). Further details, including estimated event rates of serum calcium levels, vitamin D and CRP, are provided in the supplemental materials (appendix 2, page 6). Table 2 elucidates the relationships between serum calcium levels and the outcomes (all-cause mortality and cause-specific mortality) in the osteoarthritis population. After adjusting for MSAS, high serum calcium levels were significantly associated with all-cause mortality (HR 1.33, 95% CI 1.11, 1.59), CVD-related deaths (HR 1.55, 95% CI 1.05, 2.29), and other deaths (HR 1.59, 95% CI 1.20, 2.11) across all models. Conversely, low serum calcium levels were linked with CVD-related deaths (HR 2.06, 95% CI 1.02, 4.14) in both Cox-PH and FPSM model, and showed a stronger hazard ratio compared to high serum calcium levels. No relationship between serum calcium levels and cancer-related deaths was identified.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

We stratified participants based on their calcium or vitamin D supplementation history and CKD history to conduct subgroup analyses for exposure and all-cause mortality. Results revealed no association between low serum calcium levels and all-cause mortality across all subgroups. However, high serum calcium levels (HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.01, 2.01; HR 1.28, 95% CI 1.03, 1.58; HR 1.28, 95%CI 1.04, 1.57; HR 1.33, 95%CI 1.10, 1.61) and vitamin D insufficiency (HR 1.24, 95% CI 1.03, 1.49; HR 1.18, 95% CI 1.09, 1.28; HR 1.22, 95% CI 1.12, 1.32; HR 1.19, 95% CI 1.10, 1.28) were linked with all-cause mortality in both CKD subgroups and participants without a history of calcium or vitamin D supplementation. CRP levels remained consistent across all subgroups (HR 1.44, 95% CI 1.21, 1.71; HR 1.28, 95% CI 1.17, 1.39; HR 1.50, 95% CI 1.18, 1.92; HR 1.29, 95% CI 1.19, 1.40; HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.21, 1.41) (appendix 2, page 7).

We observed significant gender- and age-specific associations between abnormal serum calcium levels and all-cause mortality. Elevated serum calcium levels were positively associated in only two subgroups: females under 60 years and males aged 60 years and above. The effect was particularly pronounced in females under 60 years, where high calcium levels had a substantial impact (HR 2.08, 95% CI 1.21, 3.58) (see appendix 2, page 8).

No significant changes were observed in sensitivity analyses following the imputation of missing baseline covariate data, nor when alternative cut-off values for serum calcium levels were analyzed. In a supplementary sensitivity analysis accounting for the potential influence of the COVID-19 pandemic on mortality patterns, by specifically examining the timeline before the onset of the pandemic, our findings remained relatively consistent (details and associated data are provided in appendix 2, pages 9–12).

Discussion

Our study of the osteoarthritis population found that high serum calcium levels and vitamin D insufficiency significantly correlated with an increased rate of all-cause mortality and CVD-related deaths. However, there was no significant association with cancer-related deaths. Conversely, a low serum calcium level was linked to CVD-related deaths. Notably, the observed associations were influenced by calcium or vitamin D supplementation. Furthermore, elevated serum CRP levels in the osteoarthritis population were significantly associated with all-cause mortality, CVD, and cancer-related deaths. Our findings underline the potential role of serum markers, particularly serum calcium levels and vitamin D, in predicting the mortality outcomes of people with osteoarthritis. The relationships we observed between serum biomarkers and mortality risk are congruent with the hypothesis that systemic factors may influence the overall health and longevity of the osteoarthritis population.

High serum calcium levels and vitamin D insufficiency, their combined impact on all-cause mortality and CVD-related deaths in osteoarthritis patients is noteworthy. Hypercalcemia has been linked to cardiovascular complications, renal dysfunction, and increased inflammatory processes [41–43]. Elevated calcium levels could lead to vascular calcification, a known factor for cardiovascular events [44]. Similarly, vitamin D insufficiency has previously been linked to several adverse health outcomes, including increased all-cause mortality and CVD-related deaths [45]. Vitamin D plays a vital role in bone health, immune function modulation, and inflammation regulation [46–48]. Its deficiency might exacerbate osteoarthritis’ inflammatory and degenerative processes, thereby leading to poorer overall outcomes [49, 50]. Moreover, whether this calcium-vitamin D interplay is exclusive to the osteoarthritis demographic is unclear. Such patterns might echo broader metabolic dynamics beyond osteoarthritis, calling for comparative analyses across populations. The influence of calcium or vitamin D supplementation and a history of CKD on the observed associations offers another dimension to this complexity. CKD itself is a risk factor for CVD, and alterations in calcium and phosphorus metabolism are frequent findings in CKD patients [51]. These disturbances, coupled with vitamin D insufficiency, can culminate in vascular calcifications and other cardiovascular complications.

On the other hand, the lack of association between serum calcium levels and cancer-related deaths is intriguing. Previous studies have shown mixed results about the relationship between calcium, vitamin D, and cancer risk. While some studies have suggested a protective role, particularly in colorectal cancer, others have not identified significant associations [52, 53]. Our study reaffirms the need for a more comprehensive investigation into the dynamics between calcium, vitamin D, and cancer.

The significant associations observed with elevated serum CRP levels are consistent with the existing literature that identifies CRP as an indicator of inflammation and as a potential prognostic marker in various conditions. Delving deeper into the mechanism, chronic inflammation, as evidenced by raised CRP levels, plays a multifaceted role in the body [54]. At its core, chronic inflammation is a persistent immune response, often exacerbated by factors like obesity, sedentary lifestyles, or certain dietary habits [55]. Over time, these sustained inflammatory reactions can damage tissues and organs. Within the context of osteoarthritis, chronic inflammation has been recognized as more than just a byproduct of joint wear and tear. It is, in fact, a driving factor that exacerbates joint structural destruction, reduces the synovial fluid quality, and results in pain and stiffness characteristic of osteoarthritis, independent of BMI [56]. Furthermore, the effects of chronic inflammation are not localized solely to the joints. Systemically, it can lead to endothelial dysfunction, atherogenesis, and plaque instability, all critical processes in the pathogenesis of CVD [57, 58]. This pervasive inflammatory milieu can also create a conducive environment for DNA mutations, impair the body’s ability to recognize and repair genetic anomalies, and promote tumor growth and progression, thereby increasing cancer risk [59].

But, what does all this mean in practical terms? The implications of these findings are manifold. Recognizing such associations offers clinicians a predictive tool to identify osteoarthritis patients potentially at heightened risk of mortality. By monitoring serum calcium and vitamin D levels, interventions can be tailored to mitigate risks, such as recommending dietary modifications, vitamin D supplementation, or other therapeutic strategies. Addressing underlying inflammation, possibly via targeted anti-inflammatory treatments, could further bolster patient outcomes.

Moreover, there is a broader public health message. By understanding and publicizing these findings, it could prompt more widespread health screenings, more robust patient education about the importance of balanced serum levels, and potential dietary and lifestyle changes that might reduce associated risks.

In conclusion, our study underscores the complex interplay of serum markers with mortality in osteoarthritis patients. Armed with this knowledge, the onus is on healthcare systems to utilize it in meaningful ways, from enhancing clinical monitoring to shaping public health initiatives.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of our study include its foundation on a sizable and well-delineated cohort from the UK Biobank, which ensures robust statistical power and permits adjustment for a myriad of potential confounders. Moreover, the observational design of our study, combined with an extended follow-up duration, anchors our ability to determine temporality and evaluate long-term associations. Advanced statistical techniques, such as FPSM and IPTW, further strengthen the depth of our analysis. However, our study is not without limitations. The UK Biobank specifically targeted participants in the 37–69 age range from the UK, necessitating prudence in extrapolating these findings to broader age demographics or different geographical regions. While we made extensive efforts to adjust for potential confounders, the spectre of residual confounding cannot be thoroughly dismissed. The UK Biobank dataset does not include detailed dietary information, limiting our ability to directly assess the impact of dietary habits on serum calcium and vitamin D levels, and their potential effects on mortality risk. Additionally, the overrepresentation of white individuals in our cohort might circumscribe the broader applicability of our findings. We also acknowledge the absence of certain serum markers, such as serum parathyroid hormone and magnesium, which could offer additional insights, though the potential calcium-inflammatory pathway exists. The reliance on self-reported data for variables like calcium or vitamin D supplementation or physical activity could introduce elements of recall bias, and the singular measurement point for serum biomarker levels does not capture potential fluctuations or long-term changes. The possibility of reverse causality in our study exists, as deteriorating health could influence serum biomarker levels. Despite measures to minimize this, such as adjusting for covariates and excluding early follow-up, our observational design cannot entirely eliminate this concern. Future research should further explore this issue to confirm the causal relationships suggested by our findings.

Conclusions

Our study highlights the potential utility of serum calcium, vitamin D, and CRP levels as prognostic indicators in individuals with osteoarthritis. Clinicians should consider monitoring these parameters to identify patients at an elevated mortality risk. Future research should aim to elucidate the underlying mechanisms behind these associations and explore potential therapeutic interventions to modify these risk factors in the osteoarthritis population.
