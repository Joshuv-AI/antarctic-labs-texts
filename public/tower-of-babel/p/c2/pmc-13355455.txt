Multimodal imaging features of primary hepatic malignant perivascular epithelioid cell tumor: A case report
Authors: Guangliu Wu, Rujiao Yin, Xiaoyan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerivascular epithelioid cell tumors (PEComas) of the liver are rare mesenchymal neoplasms with heterogeneous biological behavior, and malignant hepatic PEComas are particularly uncommon, aggressive, and associated with an unpredictable prognosis. Preoperative diagnosis remains challenging due to nonspecific clinical manifestations and laboratory findings, and comprehensive analyses of multimodality imaging features are limited. We report a case of a 56-year-old woman without liver cirrhosis who was incidentally found to have a hepatic mass during routine physical examination. Ultrasound, contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), CT, and MRI revealed a large cystic–solid lesion in the right hepatic lobe with intratumoral hemorrhage, characterized by a fluid–fluid level on T2-weighted imaging. Arterial-phase imaging demonstrated abnormally thickened intratumoral vessels, while the solid components showed persistent delayed enhancement with pseudocapsule formation. CEUS revealed slow washout in the delayed phase, with subtle differences compared with CT and MRI enhancement patterns. Based on multimodality imaging findings, a hepatic mesenchymal tumor with high malignant potential was suspected, and right hepatic lobectomy was performed. Histopathological and immunohistochemical examination confirmed primary malignant hepatic PEComa. No metastasis or recurrence was observed during follow-up. This case highlights that multimodality imaging may provide important clues for assessing malignant potential and should prompt consideration of malignant PEComa in the differential diagnosis of hepatic cystic–solid lesions.Keywords: Primary hepatic PEComa, Malignant PEComa, Multimodality imaging features, Preoperative diagnosis, Case report

Introduction

Liver PEComa is a rare mesenchymal tumor. This tumor exhibits significant biological heterogeneity, presenting as benign, potentially malignant, or malignant. Hepatic PEComa is rare, but case reports have increased in recent years. However, owing to the lack of specificity in clinical symptoms and laboratory tests, preoperative diagnosis using imaging is difficult.

Currently, there is no consensus on the imaging features that indicate malignancy potential. The role of multimodal imaging in the diagnosis and clinical decision-making for malignant hepatic PEComas requires further investigation. Previous studies have primarily relied on postoperative pathological results for definitive diagnoses. Few studies have systematically analyzed the imaging features of hepatic PEComas using multiple modalities, including CEUS, CT, and MRI. This study reports a case of primary malignant hepatic PEComa. By retrospectively analyzing multimodal imaging features, including CEUS, CT, and MRI, and combining these with postoperative pathological results, this study evaluated the potential role of these imaging features in preoperative assessment and surgical planning.

Case report

A 56-year-old woman presented to the hospital 6 days after a liver mass was discovered during a routine physical examination. The patient did not report any abdominal pain, nausea, or vomiting. On December 4, 2024, the patient was admitted to the Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University for further evaluation of the liver mass. Physical examination upon admission revealed no abdominal tenderness or rebound tenderness. Murphy's sign was negative. No obvious mass was palpated in the abdomen. The liver and spleen were not palpable below the costal margins. Shifting dullness was negative, and serologic markers for hepatitis B were negative. Tumor markers alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), and carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) were within the normal range.

Routine abdominal ultrasound was performed using a Philips ultrasound system with SonoVue (Bracco, Milan, Italy) as the contrast agent for CEUS. Ultrasound revealed a predominantly cystic-solid mass in the right lobe of the liver. The mass was irregularly shaped and had multiple internal septations. The mass had relatively clear boundaries and measured approximately 7.9 cm × 7.4 cm in size. Color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI) showed minimal blood supply within the mass, with only a small amount of blood flow signal visible in the periphery. CEUS showed isoenhancement of the cyst wall and septations in the arterial phase, while the solid components demonstrated mild hypoenhancement. Thickened intratumoral vessels were also identified (Fig. 1A). In the portal venous phase, the cyst wall and solid components showed iso-enhancement. In the delayed phase, they demonstrated mild hypoenhancement relative to the surrounding liver parenchyma, with unenhanced fluid areas observed within the lesion. (Fig. 1B).

Ultrasound and CEUS findings of primary malignant hepatic PEComa. (A) CEUS in the arterial phase demonstrated thickened intratumoral vessels (red arrow) and (B) CEUS in the delayed phase demonstrates mild hypoenhancement of the cyst wall and septa relative to the surrounding liver parenchyma (red arrow).

CT was performed using a 320-detector-row scanner (Aquilion ONE Ace, Canon Medical Systems, Japan) with iopamidol as the contrast agent. CT demonstrated a mixed slightly low-density mass in the right lobe of the liver. Dynamic enhanced scanning revealed progressive uneven enhancement of the mass. In the arterial phase, the cyst wall, septations, and mural nodules showed significant enhancement (Fig. 2A). In the delayed phase, the enhancement of the cyst wall, septations, and mural nodules decreased (Fig. 2B). MRI was performed on a 3.0-T system (Achieva, Philips Healthcare, The Netherlands) using gadobutrol as the contrast agent. MRI revealed low signal intensity on T1-weighted images (T1WI). No significant signal reduction was observed in the T1 out-of-phase images. T2-weighted images (T2WI) showed mixed high and low signal intensities, with visible septations and fluid-fluid levels (Fig. 2C). Dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI revealed mild heterogeneous enhancement during the arterial phase, followed by heterogeneous progressive enhancement during the portal venous and delayed phases.

Contrast-enhanced CT and MRI features of primary malignant hepatic PEComa. (A) Contrast-enhanced CT in the arterial phase shows marked enhancement of the mural nodules (red arrow). (B) Contrast-enhanced CT in the delayed phase shows decreased enhancement of the mural nodules (red arrow) and (C) T2-weighted MRI shows heterogeneous signal intensity with a fluid–fluid level (red arrow).

Several imaging features raised concern for malignancy prior to surgery, including the large tumor size, cystic–solid morphology, intratumoral hemorrhage manifested as a fluid–fluid level on MRI, abnormal intratumoral vessels in the arterial phase, and persistent enhancement of the solid components on delayed imaging. Based on these comprehensive imaging findings, a hepatic mesenchymal tumor with high malignant potential was highly suspected. Given the large tumor size, the indeterminate imaging findings with malignancy not excluded, and the absence of evidence of distant metastasis on conventional imaging, surgical resection was considered the most appropriate treatment strategy. Preoperative core needle biopsy was not performed because of the heterogeneous cystic–solid nature of the lesion and the potential risk of sampling error. In addition, the biopsy result was unlikely to alter the planned surgical management. PET/CT was not performed because CT and MRI showed no evidence of extrahepatic disease or distant metastasis. Accordingly, the patient underwent surgical resection of the right hepatic lobe tumor. Postoperative pathological examination confirmed a malignant hepatic PEComa. Grossly, the tumor capsule was incomplete, with focal infiltration into the adjacent liver parenchyma, although no vascular invasion was observed. Pathological examination revealed epithelioid and spindle-shaped tumor cells (Fig. 3A). Significant nuclear atypia was also observed. The cytoplasm was abundant and showed eosinophilic or clear changes. Tumor necrosis was also noted. Pathological mitotic figures were active, exceeding 2 per 10 high-power fields (HPF). Immunohistochemical staining results (Fig. 3B) showed that the melanoma-associated markers HMB45 and Melan-A were positive. The myogenic markers SMA and Desmin were positive to varying degrees. The epithelial markers broad-spectrum CK broad-spectrum and CK7 were negative. The hepatocellular markers Hepa and GS were negative. The Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 30% in hot-spot areas.

Histopathological and immunohistochemical characteristics of malignant hepatic PEComa. (A) HE staining (×100) reveals epithelioid tumor cells with marked nuclear atypia and (B) Immunohistochemical staining (×100) shows tumor cells are positive for HMB45 and Melan-A.

Given the pathological diagnosis of malignant PEComa, a whole-body CT scan was performed postoperatively. The results showed no definite distant metastasis. The surgical margins were also negative. Therefore, no adjuvant therapy was administered. The patient recovered well postoperatively and was successfully discharged. Close follow-up is recommended. Follow-up imaging examinations were performed at 1 and 6 months after surgery. No evidence of local recurrence or distant metastasis was detected on follow-up imaging (Fig. 4). The patient remains under regular clinical and radiological surveillance.

Noncontrast CT obtained 6 months after surgery showed no evidence of tumor recurrence or metastasis (red arrow).

Discussion

Perivascular epithelioid cells (PECs) exhibit dual differentiation toward melanocytic and smooth muscle lineages [1,2]. According to the 5th edition of the WHO Classification of Tumors of Soft Tissue and Bone, tumors derived from PECs are classified into the PEComa family of tumors [1]. This family includes renal angiomyolipoma (AML), pulmonary clear cell “sugar” tumor (CCST), lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM), conventional PEComa (PEComa-NOS), malignant PEComa, and TFE3 rearrangement-associated PEComa. PEComas are more common in women and can occur in almost all organs of the body. Common sites of occurrence include the kidneys and liver [3]. Hepatic PEComas are rare tumors. It can occur at any age but is more common in middle-aged women. Previous studies have suggested that hepatic PEComas lack a clear association with viral hepatitis and cirrhosis. Serum tumor marker levels are usually negative. These features often lead to misdiagnosis as other hepatic tumors in clinical practice. Among these, primary malignant hepatic PEComa is particularly rare [4].

Pathologically, tumor cells exhibit PEC differentiation. When tumors are predominantly composed of epithelioid cells, they usually express melanocyte-related markers. When a tumor is predominantly composed of spindle cells, it mostly expresses myogenic cell markers. Among immunohistochemical markers, HMB-45 is one of the most sensitive indicators for the diagnosis of PEComa [1,4]. Folpe et al. [2] first proposed the pathological diagnostic criteria for malignant PEComa. A diagnosis of malignant PEComa can be considered when 2 or more of the following criteria are met: tumor diameter ≥5 cm, mitotic count >1/50 HPF, marked nuclear atypia, significant nuclear pleomorphism, tumor necrosis, infiltrative growth pattern, and vascular invasion. Further studies indicated that a tumor diameter ≥5 cm and a mitotic rate >1/50 HPF are significantly associated with an increased risk of postoperative recurrence [5]. Subsequently, Shi et al. [6] proposed diagnostic criteria specifically for malignant hepatic PEComas. These criteria include the following key points: the tumor is confined to the liver, recurrence or metastasis is clearly observed after surgery, atypical epithelioid or myoid tumor cells are present, and immunohistochemistry of the tumor cells shows positive staining for HMB45 and/or Melan-A and SMA. In the present case, the mitotic count exceeded 2 per 10 HPF (equivalent to >10 mitoses per 50 HPF), which clearly exceeds the malignancy threshold proposed by Folpe et al. [5]. Thus, this patient met the diagnostic criteria for malignant PEComa. The risk of postoperative recurrence was high. Although no recurrence or metastasis was observed during the 2 follow-up visits, long-term close follow-up is still necessary.

Despite well-established pathological criteria, preoperative imaging diagnosis of hepatic PEComas remains challenging. Previous studies have reported that the accuracy of preoperative diagnosis based solely on CT or MRI is less than 20% [3,4]. In terms of imaging features, hepatic PEComa usually lacks a background of cirrhosis. Pseudocapsule formation has been observed in some cases. Abnormally thickened blood vessels within or around the tumor are considered diagnostically relevant [4]. On unenhanced CT scans, malignant PEComas often present as slightly low-density masses. In unenhanced MRI scans, T1-weighted images usually show a low signal intensity, whereas T2-weighted images usually show a high signal intensity. Enhanced CT and MRI may show a "fast-in, fast-out" or "fast-in, slow-out" enhancement pattern, respectively. When the tumor stroma has abundant vascular components, the contrast agent washout is rapid, showing rapid enhancement and washout. When the tumor stroma has larger blood vessels, the contrast agent retention time is prolonged, which may manifest as persistent or progressive enhancements.

Ultrasound and CEUS have supplementary value in the evaluation of hepatic PEComa. Conventional ultrasound usually shows a relatively well-defined, round, or oval solid mass with heterogeneous internal echoes. CEUS typically shows rapid enhancement during the arterial phase [7]. The delayed phase may show a slow washout or persistent enhancement. This may be attributable to the abundant capillary network within the tumor, leading to prolonged contrast agent retention time [8]. In this case, the patient exhibited the following characteristics on multimodal imaging examinations: no background of liver cirrhosis and a large tumor volume.

T2-weighted images showed a fluid-fluid level, suggesting internal tumor hemorrhage. Abnormally thickened blood vessels were visible in the arterial phase, and the solid components of the tumor showed persistent enhancement in the delayed phase. There were some differences in the enhancement patterns in the delayed phase across different imaging modalities. In the present case, CT demonstrated decreased enhancement of the cyst wall, septations, and mural nodules during the delayed phase, whereas MRI showed progressive enhancement of the solid components. This apparent discrepancy may be related to differences in imaging techniques, contrast agents, and the heterogeneous composition of the tumor. CT primarily reflected washout within the more vascularized mural nodules, while MRI was more sensitive to delayed contrast retention within the tumor stroma and vascular channels. Indeed, such different enhancement patterns have been reported in hepatic PEComas and may reflect variations in the vascular and stromal components of the tumor. In the present case, the combination of a large tumor size, intratumoral hemorrhage, abnormal intratumoral vessels, and delayed enhancement across multimodal imaging suggested a high malignant potential. These imaging findings supported the adoption of an aggressive surgical treatment strategy.

Conclusions

Primary hepatic malignant PEComa is extremely rare, but should be included in the differential diagnosis of cystic and solid liver lesions in patients without underlying liver disease. A comprehensive analysis of multimodal imaging can aid in identifying the potential malignant characteristics of tumors, which may inform preoperative risk assessment and surgical treatment decisions. Herein, we report a case of hepatic malignant PEComa with comprehensive imaging data. By analyzing its multimodal imaging features, this study aims to improve the understanding of the clinical and radiological characteristics of this rare tumor type.
