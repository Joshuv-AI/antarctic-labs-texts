Accessibility, safety, and early effect of the malaria vaccine in routine immunisation practice among children under 36 months in the Soa Health District, Cameroon
Authors: Cavin Epie Bekolo, Gladys Aimée Simo Zekeng, Paul Onambele, Benjamin Signe, Shalom Tchokfe Ndoula, Jérôme Ateudjieu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria remains a leading cause of child mortality in sub-Saharan Africa. Cameroon introduced the RTS,S/AS01 malaria vaccine in 2024, but real-world evidence on its early effects, coverage, and safety remains limited. This study evaluated these parameters among children under 36 months in the high-transmission Soa Health District.MethodsA community-based analytical cross-sectional study was conducted from February to May 2025. Households were selected by stratified cluster sampling (62 clusters, 622 participants). Children aged < 36 months who had resided in the district for ≥ 3 months were eligible. Vaccination status and malaria episodes (confirmed by rapid diagnostic test or microscopy) during the preceding six months were abstracted from cards. Primary exposure was ≥ 2 doses of RTS,S/AS01; primary outcome was documented malaria episodes. Multivariable modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors was used to estimate risk ratios (RR) adjusted for age, breastfeeding, insecticide-treated net (ITN) use, and intermittent preventive treatment in infancy (IPTi). The effect of the vaccine was measured as (1–adjusted RR) × 100. Coverage and adverse events were also assessed.ResultsAmong 622 children (mean age 17.9 months, 51.4% female), documented coverage of dose 1 was 56·8% (95% CI 53.5–61.7), dropping to 46·6% for dose 2 and 39·0% for dose 3. Perceived free access (aRR 1.97, 95% CI 1.47–2.64) and knowledge of a nearby vaccination facility (aRR 1.86, 95% CI 1.41–2.45) were strongly associated with uptake. In the cross-sectional analysis, children who received ≥ 2 doses were associated with a 50% lower occurrence of malaria episodes (aRR 0.50, 95% CI 0.34–0.73, p = 0.001) compared with those receiving ≤ 1 dose. Minor adverse events occurred in 27% (fever 93%, injection site swelling 10%).ConclusionsIn this high-burden Cameroonian setting, two or more doses of RTS,S/AS01 were associated with a lower occurrence of malaria episodes favourable safety profile. Coverage remains suboptimal, and dropout is high. Ensuring free access and strengthening community awareness are critical for maximising vaccine uptake.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-026-01024-3.Keywords: Malaria vaccine; RTS,S/AS01; Vaccine coverage; Vaccine safety; Children under five; Cameroon

Background

Malaria is a febrile, haemolytic erythrocytopathy caused by protozoan parasites of the genus Plasmodium, transmitted to humans through the bite of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes [1]. The disease remains a major global public health problem, with an estimated 282 million cases and 610,000 deaths in 2024 [2]. Sub-Saharan Africa bears the heaviest burden, accounting for 95% of cases and 95% of deaths. Children under five years are particularly vulnerable, representing approximately 75% of all malaria-related deaths [2].

Cameroon is among the 15 most affected countries worldwide and ranks third in Central Africa. In 2024, the country reported approximately 3 million malaria cases and 2,016 deaths [3, 4]. The National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP) classifies 21% of health districts as having very high transmission, 31% as high, 27% as moderate, and 21% as low [5]. The Soa Health District in the neighbourhood of the capital city of Yaounde falls into the high-transmission category. Between 2015 and 2018, malaria incidence in children under five increased by 16% (from 188 to 218 cases per 1,000 children), and mortality rose by 16·5% [5].

Despite the implementation of multiple control strategies, the decline in malaria burden has stalled in many high-transmission areas [6]. This situation prompted the development and introduction of the RTS,S/AS01 malaria vaccine (Mosquirix), the first vaccine recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) for routine use against a human parasitic disease [7].

RTS,S/AS01 is a recombinant protein vaccine that targets the circumsporozoite protein (CSP) of Plasmodium falciparum, the most deadly and prevalent malaria parasite in Africa [8]. Phase III clinical trials conducted across seven African countries (Burkina Faso, Gabon, Ghana, Kenya, Malawi, Mozambique, Tanzania) involving approximately 15,000 young children demonstrated a 39% reduction in uncomplicated malaria and a 29% reduction in severe malaria over four years of follow-up among children aged 5–17 months who received four doses [9]. The vaccine has an acceptable safety profile, with the most common adverse events being transient, mild-to-moderate injection site reactions and fever [10, 11].

Following successful pilot implementation programmes in Ghana, Kenya, and Malawi, where nearly 2 million children received the vaccine with demonstrated feasibility and a 30% reduction in severe malaria hospitalisations [12], the WHO recommended widespread use of RTS,S/AS01 in October 2021 for children aged 6–24 months living in moderate-to-high transmission settings[7]. Cameroon received its first shipment of 331,200 doses in November 2023 and became the first country to integrate the vaccine into the Expanded Programme on Immunisation (EPI) schedule in January 2024, with doses administered at 6, 7, 9, and 24 months of age. The Soa Health District was one of the 42 most-at-risk districts selected for the introduction of the malaria vaccine in Cameroon [13].

However, vaccine effectiveness in real-world routine settings may differ from clinical trial efficacy due to incomplete dosing, programme leakage, variations in transmission intensity, and logistical barriers [14]. Furthermore, vaccination coverage in Cameroon for new vaccines often falls below the 80% target recommended by the WHO [15]. For example, during the 2023 polio outbreak, coverage with the oral polio vaccine was suboptimal in several districts [16]. There is also evidence that parental knowledge, perceived cost, distance to health facilities, and satisfaction with services influence vaccine uptake [17, 18]. No published study has yet evaluated the post-introduction performance of RTS,S/AS01 in Cameroon. Therefore, we aimed to: (1) measure documented vaccination coverage of RTS,S/AS01 among children under 36 months in the Soa Health District; (2) determine factors associated with vaccine uptake (accessibility); (3) assess the cross‑sectional association between receipt of at least two doses of the vaccine and the occurrence of documented malaria episodes; and (4) describe the frequency and distribution of adverse events following immunisation.

Methods

Study design and period

This was a community‑based analytical cross‑sectional study. Data were collected from February to May 2025. The study included a descriptive component (coverage, accessibility, adverse events) and an analytical component (association between vaccination status and malaria episodes). The overall study spanned 11 months from September 2024 to July 2025, including protocol development, ethical approvals, training, and analysis.

Study setting

The study took place in the Soa Health District, located in the Mefou-et-Afamba division of the Centre Region, Cameroon (Fig. 1). The district covers an area of 326 km2 with an estimated population of 51,228 inhabitants in 2024 (density 212 inhabitants/km2). It comprises six functional health areas: two urban (Soa, Ntouessong), one semi-urban (Ebang), and three rural (Koulou, Ngali 2, Ting-Melen). There are approximately 100 health facilities, with 41 registered in the District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2). Malaria transmission is perennial and intense (estimated entomological inoculation rate > 300 infective bites/person/year). The district is bordered by Awae, Obala, Djoungolo, and Mvog-Ada health districts.

Participants and eligibility

The target population comprised all children under 36 months of age who had resided in the Soa Health District for at least 3 months before the survey, with the source population comprising all residents of the district. For the cross‑sectional component, children were included if their parent/guardian was available to answer questions and provided written informed consent. For the analytical component, children in the cross‑sectional sample were further required to have a vaccination card (to minimise recall bias) and to have consultation records available for the six months preceding the interview. Within this cohort, the exposed group was defined as children who had received at least two documented doses of the RTS,S/AS01 malaria vaccine. The unexposed (reference) group comprised children who had received at most one documented dose.

Sample size estimation

Cross-sectional component:

Using the Cochran formula: n = Zα2 × P(1–P)/d2, with Zα = 1.96 (95% confidence), P = 0.24 (malaria prevalence in children under five from the 2018 Cameroon Demographic and Health Survey [19]), and d = 0.05 (margin of error), the initial sample was 280. Accounting for a 90% response rate and a design effect of 2 (cluster sampling), the final sample was 622. This sample also sufficed for the analytical cross‑sectional association analysis (393 children with complete card data).

Sampling procedure

We used a stratified cluster sampling design. The sampling frame consisted of all villages/localities within the six health areas of the Soa Health District. First, we determined the required number of clusters (62) based on the sample size calculation (622 participants, 10 households per cluster). Second, we allocated these 62 clusters proportionally to the target population of children aged 0–59 months in each health area (Table 1). Third, within each health area, we listed all constituent villages/localities and randomly selected the required number of clusters using simple random sampling without replacement (e.g., drawing lots). Fourth, in each selected village/locality, the first household was identified by the “spinning pen” method: the enumerator stood at a central point (e.g., the village chief’s compound or the market square), spun a pen on the ground, and walked in the direction indicated by the pen’s tip; the first household encountered was the starting point. Fifth, within that household, all eligible children (aged < 36 months, resident for ≥ 3 months) were enrolled after obtaining informed consent. Sixth, the enumerator then moved to the nearest household to the right (or the next nearest dwelling) and repeated the process, continuing until 10 households had been surveyed or until no further eligible children were found. If a selected household had no eligible child, the enumerator moved to the next household. All eligible children in each selected household were included. This approach ensured random selection of clusters, proportional representation of urban and rural areas, and systematic household selection within clusters, minimising selection bias.

All questionnaires and card data were double-entered and cross-checked for consistency. Interviews were conducted on both weekdays and weekends to include working parents, each lasting approximately 30‑45 min. Vaccination and consultation cards were photographed for verification.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM-SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were calculated as frequencies and proportions for categorical variables and means with standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables. Vaccination coverage was defined as the proportion of children with at least one documented dose of RTS,S/AS01 and was reported with 95% confidence intervals (Wilson method).

Accessibility analysis (factors associated with having received ≥ 1 documented dose): We performed bivariate modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors (cluster‑robust at the village level) to obtain crude risk ratios (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for each potential predictor: knowledge of a nearby vaccination facility, travel time, ownership of transport, perceived vaccine cost, satisfaction with services, and service quality. Variables with p < 0.20 in bivariate analysis were entered into a multivariable modified Poisson regression model to calculate adjusted risk ratios (aRR) and 95% CI. Health area was included as a fixed effect to adjust for stratum‑specific variation. The model's goodness‑of‑fit was assessed using the generalised Hosmer‑Lemeshow test for generalised linear models.

Cross‑sectional association analysis (association between ≥ 2 documented doses and malaria episodes): Among children with complete card data (n = 393), we used modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors to estimate the aRR for malaria episodes (any episode in the preceding 6 months) comparing children with ≥ 2 doses versus ≤ 1 dose. Covariates included the child's age (categorical: 0–11, 12–23, 24–36 months), sex, breastfeeding type (exclusive/mixed/artificial), ITN use (always/sometimes/never), and IPTi (yes/no). Health area was included as a fixed effect. Because exact dates of individual malaria episodes were not collected, we used a binary outcome (any episode vs none) over the fixed 6‑month recall window. The vaccine effectiveness was measured as (1–aRR) × 100% and is presented as an association measure only. All tests were two‑tailed, with α = 0·05.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee for Research on Humans for the Centre Region (Reference No. 007713/CRERSHC/2025). Administrative authorisation was obtained from the Soa Health District. Written informed consent was obtained from each parent/guardian. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality was ensured through anonymisation. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the National Ethics Committee for Research on Humans in Cameroon and with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study followed the STROBE guidelines for observational research.

Participant characteristics

The distribution of participants across health areas was: Soa, 269 (43.3%); Ebang, 161 (25.9%); Ntouessong, 122 (19.6%); Ngali 2, 30 (4.8%); Koulou, 20 (3.2%); and Ting‑Melen, 20 (3.2%). Among the 622 children, 393 (63·2%) had a vaccination card and complete consultation records, forming the analytical cohort for the association analysis.

Parents were predominantly female (96·5%), with a mean age of 27·9 ± 6 years; 59·6% were aged 25‑34 years. Most had secondary education (63·2%). Children had a mean age of 17·9 ± 8 months; 58·7% were aged 12‑23 months. Slightly more than half were female (51·4%). Exclusive breastfeeding before 6 months was practised by 58·2%. The majority (96·5%) were born in a health facility (Table 2).

Vaccination coverage

Documented coverage of the first dose of RTS,S/AS01 was 56·83% (95% CI 53·5–61.7%). Coverage declined to 46.63% for the second dose and 38.97% for the third dose (Fig. 3). When maternal recall (unverified) was included, overall coverage rose to 76.49%, 64.35%, and 49.26%, respectively. The documented dropout rate between dose 1 and dose 3 was 31·3 percentage points (i.e., 38.2% relative dropout). Dropout varied by health area, from 15.2% in Ebang to 61.5% in Koulou. The median interval between dose 1 and dose 2 was 28 days (IQR 26–35), between dose 2 and dose 3 was 56 days (IQR 49–63), and between dose 3 and dose 4 was 441 days (IQR 420–455).

Factors associated with vaccine uptake (accessibility)

In the adjusted model (Table 3), knowledge of a nearby vaccination facility was strongly associated with uptake (aRR = 1.86, 95% CI 1.41–2.45, p < 0.001). Perceiving the vaccine as free (vs any cost) increased the risk of vaccination by 97% (aRR = 1.97, 95% CI 1.47–2.64, p < 0.001). Satisfaction with vaccination services was also significant (aRR = 1.26, 95% CI 1·01–1.56, p = 0.039). Travel time of < 30 min (compared with > 60 min) was associated with lower risk of vaccination (aRR = 0.42, 95% CI 0.24–0.73, p = 0.002). Having personal transport was not significant after adjustment (aRR = 1.12, 95% CI 0.94–1.33, p = 0.200). Service quality (very good, good, average vs poor) was not independently associated with uptake in the adjusted model.

Association between vaccination and malaria episodes

Among the 393 children retained for the analytic component, 214 (54·5%) had received ≥ 2 documented doses (exposed) and 179 (45·5%) had received ≤ 1 dose. Statistically significant differences were observed for age group, maternal education, socioeconomic proxy, IPTi use, perceived vaccine cost, and prior healthcare utilisation (p < 0.05), while sex, health area, ITN use, and travel time were similarly distributed between the two groups (Table 4).

During the 6 months preceding the survey, malaria episodes occurred in 28 exposed children (13·1%) and 48 non‑exposed children (26·8%). After adjusting for age, sex, breastfeeding, ITN use, and IPTi, children who received ≥ 2 doses were associated with a 50% lower occurrence of malaria episodes compared with those who received ≤ 1 dose (aRR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.34–0.73, p = 0·001). Neither ITN use nor IPTi was independently associated with malaria episodes in the adjusted model (Table 5).

The complete‑case analysis (excluding children with any missing covariate data, n = 373) gave an aRR of 0.51 (95% CI 0.35–0.76), corresponding to a reduction of frequency of malaria episodes by 49%.

Adverse events following immunisation

Among the 359 children who received at least one documented or reported dose, 97 (27.0%) experienced at least one adverse event (Table 6). Fever was the most common (93/97, 95.9%), followed by injection site swelling (10/97, 10.3%), cough (5/97, 5.2%), coryza (5/97, 5·2%), and headache (4/97, 4.1%). All events were mild and transient, with the majority occurring within 24 h of vaccination. Only 15 children (15.5%) with adverse events were taken to a health facility; none required hospitalisation. No serious adverse events (e.g., convulsions, anaphylaxis) were reported.

Discussion

This study provides some of the first real‑world evidence on the effectiveness, accessibility, and safety of the RTS,S/AS01 malaria vaccine in Cameroon's routine immunisation programme. We found that receipt of at least two documented doses was associated with a 50% lower risk of malaria (aRR 0.50, 95% CI 0.34–0.73) among children under 36 months in a high‑transmission district, a level of protection comparable to clinical trial efficacy and pilot implementation results from other African countries [9, 12]. The vaccine was well tolerated, with minor, transient adverse events in 27% of recipients, consistent with the known safety profile [10, 11]. However, documented coverage of the first dose was only 56·8%, and dropout from dose 1 to dose 3 exceeded 30%, falling short of the 80% target needed for population impact [15].

Vaccination coverage and dropout

The documented coverage of 56.8% for dose 1 is higher than the 32.2% reported for any dose in the Tiko Health District, Southwest Cameroon [20], but lower than the 60–70% achieved during the pilot MVIP programme in Ghana, Kenya, and Malawi [12]. The wide variation in coverage across health areas (18·5% in Koulou to 80·6% in Ebang) highlights the importance of local context. Rural areas may face greater challenges in access, awareness, and supply chain reliability. The high dropout rate (38·2% relative dropout from dose 1 to dose 3) is concerning because our effectiveness analysis clearly shows that protection may require at least two doses. Similar dropouts have been observed for other vaccines in Cameroon, often linked to caregiver forgetfulness, perceived adverse events, and competing priorities [16].

Factors influencing vaccine uptake

The strong association between knowledge of a nearby vaccination facility and uptake (aRR = 1.86) underscores the critical role of information dissemination. This aligns with studies from Nigeria and Ghana, where a lack of awareness was a major barrier [17, 18]. Community-based health education, use of community health workers, traditional and religious leaders, and mass media campaigns could improve awareness.

The counter-intuitive finding that shorter travel time (< 30 min) was associated with lower vaccination odds (aRR = 0.42) compared with longer travel (> 60 min) is striking but has been observed elsewhere [21, 22]. One explanation is that parents living very close to a facility may postpone vaccination (“I can go anytime”), leading to delays and missed opportunities. In contrast, families in remote areas may be more motivated or be specifically targeted by mobile outreach teams. In Soa, outreach vaccination sessions are organised monthly in hard-to-reach villages, which may effectively buffer distance. This finding implies that geographical proximity alone does not guarantee timely vaccination; demand-generation and reminder systems are needed even in well-served areas.

The perception that the vaccine is free was the strongest predictor of uptake (aRR = 1.97). Despite official free vaccination policies, some parents reported paying informal fees (for cards, syringes, weighing, or transport). This is consistent with evidence from Burkina Faso and the DRC showing that even small out-of-pocket costs deter vaccination [23, 24]. Health authorities must enforce zero-cost policies and communicate clearly that all vaccine-related services are free.

Satisfaction with previous vaccination contacts remained significant after adjustment (aRR = 1.26). This reinforces the importance of respectful, efficient, and well-organised services. Reducing waiting times, improving health worker attitudes, and providing clear information about the vaccination schedule could improve retention.

Vaccine effect on malaria occurrence

The 50% lower occurrence of malaria episodes associated with ≥ 2 doses is consistent with the 39–63% efficacy reported in phase III trials [9] and with the 56% effectiveness reported from Ghana’s pilot programme [25]. The lack of independent association for ITN use and IPTi in the adjusted model does not imply that these interventions are ineffective; rather, it may reflect low statistical power or collinearity (vaccinated children were also more likely to use ITNs and IPTi). In fact, the combination of vaccines and ITNs has been shown to have a synergistic effect in reducing malaria burden [26].

Our VE estimate is slightly higher than the 39% efficacy reported over four years in the phase III trial [9]. Because of the cross‑sectional design and potential residual confounding (healthy vaccinee bias), these findings should be interpreted as associations; causality cannot be inferred. Possible reasons include the shorter follow-up period (6 months) in our study, during which vaccine-induced immunity is still strong, and the fact that we used documented malaria (symptomatic episodes seeking care), whereas trials often used active case detection. Additionally, we could not adjust for household-level transmission intensity, which may have differed between exposed and non-exposed groups.

Safety

The frequency of adverse events (27%) and the predominance of mild, transient fever are almost identical to clinical trial findings [9, 10, 12]. The low rate of health facility consultation (15·5%) and the absence of severe events confirm the vaccine’s favourable safety profile in routine use. Nonetheless, we noted that some parents discontinued vaccination because of fear of fever. Pre-vaccination counselling should include guidance on fever management with paracetamol and reassurance about its benign, transient nature.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include the community-based design with a large sample size, verification of vaccination and malaria status from cards (reducing recall bias), the use of multivariable adjustment for key confounders, and sensitivity analyses that confirmed the robustness of the findings.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. Because of the cross‑sectional design and potential residual confounding (healthy vaccinee bias), these findings should be interpreted as associations; causality cannot be inferred. First, residual confounding may be present; we could not adjust for household wealth, maternal education beyond secondary level, or household‑level transmission intensity, raising the possibility of healthy vaccinee bias—children who received ≥ 2 doses might have come from families with better health‑seeking behaviour and lower baseline malaria exposure, which could lead to overestimation of the association despite adjustment for ITN use, IPTi, and breastfeeding. Second, the 6‑month recall for malaria may miss some episodes, and asymptomatic parasitaemia was not measured; however, non‑differential misclassification would likely bias towards the null, making our estimate conservative. The absence of exact malaria episode dates precluded a time‑to‑event analysis, and residual temporal bias cannot be completely excluded. Third, the study was conducted in a single district, albeit one representative of high‑transmission Cameroon; generalisability to low‑transmission settings requires further research. Fourth, the cross‑sectional assessment of accessibility factors precludes causal inference, though the strong associations are plausible. Fifth, we did not systematically collect data on reasons for vaccine refusal, which limited our understanding of hesitancy drivers. Sixth, we relied on parental reports for adverse events, which may under‑report mild reactions. Seventh, a substantial proportion of children (229/622, 36·8%) were excluded from the analytical cohort because they lacked vaccination or consultation cards, a common challenge in low‑resource settings. This exclusion could introduce selection bias if non‑cardholders differ systematically from those included, but the direction of any bias is uncertain—it could overestimate or underestimate the association. Our estimate, therefore, applies to children with documented vaccination histories, which is the standard approach in routine programme evaluations. Future studies should improve card retention (e.g., digital records) and, where feasible, conduct sensitivity analyses to assess the impact of missing data. Finally, the original analyses were performed using health‑area fixed effects but did not explicitly adjust for clustering at the primary sampling unit (village) level. However, given the fact that health‑area fixed effects absorb most geographic variation, any residual clustering bias is likely negligible, and the point estimates would remain unchanged under cluster‑robust standard errors.

Implications for policy and practice

From a programmatic perspective, several actions are urgent: (1) intensify community sensitisation about the vaccine’s benefits, schedule, and safety, using local languages and trusted messengers; (2) eliminate all informal fees associated with vaccination and clearly communicate that services are free; (3) strengthen outreach services for remote populations while simultaneously improving demand generation in near-facility communities (e.g., reminder SMS, appointment tracking); (4) monitor dropout rates in real time and implement default tracking systems; and (5) integrate pre-vaccination counselling on fever management to reduce vaccine hesitancy due to fear of adverse events.

Future research should include multi-district evaluations with longer follow-up to assess waning of protection, cost-effectiveness analyses, and studies on the impact of the vaccine on severe malaria and all-cause child mortality.

Conclusions

In a high‑transmission area of Cameroon, two or more doses of the RTS,S/AS01 malaria vaccine were associated with approximately 50% lower occurrence of malaria episodes compared with ≤ 1 dose, with a favourable safety profile. However, coverage is suboptimal, and dropout is high. Achieving high and equitable coverage requires guaranteed free access, stronger community engagement, and tailored strategies that address both distance and complacency.
