Rabbit Skull Shape: Three‐Dimensional Geometric Morphometric Analysis
Authors: İsmail Demircioğlu, Yasin Demiraslan, Ali Koçyiğit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe objective of the present study was to analyse the three‐dimensional shape of the cranium models of New Zealand rabbits and to determine the effects of sex on shape. To this end, a total of 12 rabbit craniums were used (six female and six male). After maceration, the craniums were scanned using a three‐dimensional surface scanner to create models. The models were then marked with 23 landmarks (nine median line, 14 bilateral) using Stratovan Checkpoint software for shape analysis. Procrustes superimposition and principal component analysis were performed to determine shape variations, while Procrustes ANOVA was performed to determine dimorphic differences in size and shape. Discriminant function analysis was performed for the effect of gender on shape. The study's analysis revealed that PC1 accounted for 25.47% of the variation in principal component analysis, with this variation predominantly concentrated in the temporal region. These variations were followed by medial eye angle and incisive bone. While statistical differences in cranium shape were observed between the sexes, no significant differences in size were detected. Consequently, it was determined that gender exerts an influence on shape differences in rabbit cranium. It is hypothesised that the three‐dimensional (3D) geometric morphometry method can be utilised for sex determination in rabbit craniums.

Introduction

In mammals, the skeleton is of significant importance for the purposes of phylogenetic and taxonomic classifications (Tresguerres et al. 2002; Rai and Ranade 2007). The rabbit cranium is frequently utilised in experimental craniofacial research due to the anatomical characteristics and size of the specimen in question (Rai and Ranade 2007). In addition, within the skeletal system, the cranium and pelvis are the bones with the highest accuracy rate in determining gender dimorphic structures (Bruzek 2002; Rosas and Bastir 2002). While there exist studies that have utilised craniometric and two‐dimensional shape analysis to reveal sexual dimorphisms (Dayan et al. 2023; Gündemir et al. 2023; Koçyiğit and Demircioğlu 2024), the application of three‐dimensional (3D) geometric morphometry analysis in this context remains limited. In the analysis of intricate shapes, 3D models are employed to obtain a standard image. 3D model technology is of particular importance in imaging the cranium and surrounding anatomical structures, and in monitoring changes in these structures (Ohlerth and Scharf 2007; Moselhy and Mahdy 2019).

3D models can be obtained by medical imaging techniques as well as by various scanners and photometric methods (Sarıtaş 2015; Südor 2021; Uslu and Uysal 2017). Model studies using a 3D scanner are preferred in craniotomy and cranioplasty experimental studies (Ozdol 2015; Alaca 2014).

Geometric morphometry is a methodological framework that facilitates the analysis of object shape in two or three dimensions according to the Cartesian coordinates of anatomical points (Bookstein 1997; Zelditch et al., 2004; Aytek 2017). This analysis method has been frequently employed in recent years to determine shape differences and similarities on different biological materials (Gündemir et al. 2023b, 2023a; Manuta et al. 2023; Demiraslan et al. 2023; Karaavci et al. 2024; Demircioğlu et al. 2024). The utilisation of 3D image analysis has been demonstrated to yield more precise outcomes, facilitating the identification of the standard image and the accurate location of landmarks on the objects under scrutiny (Gündemir et al. 2023b, 2023a; Manuta et al. 2023; Demiraslan et al. 2023; Karaavci et al. 2024). The study aims to use 3D image analysis to explore the 3D morphology of the skull models created by scanning the skull of the New Zealand rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus L.) using a 3D scanner, and to explain the effect of gender on shape variations.

Materials and Methods

Samples

A total of 12 skulls from 1‐year‐old New Zealand rabbits were used in the study; six were female and six were male skulls. The study was approved by Harran University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee (Decision no: 2024/008/07). Prior to analysis, the skulls underwent standard maceration procedures (Tasbas and Tecirlioglu 1996).

Three‐Dimensional Modelling

The 3D scanning process was conducted using the Shining 3D EinScan Pro 2X (2020) device, accompanied by the Shining 3D EinScan Colour Pack and Industrial Pack. The subsequent scanning and 3D modelling of the obtained data was facilitated by the EXScan Pro v.3.6 software. Prior to scanning, the scanner device underwent a calibration procedure that addressed colour and sensitivity parameters. This process was concluded with measurement deviations of 0.01 mm and colour deviations of 0.02 pixels. The fixed scanning option from the software interface was utilised, with the rotary table setting configured to execute 24 scans per cycle. Subsequent to each scan, the position of the heads on the table was altered, and a total of three distinct scans were conducted in different positions for each head. Thereafter, 3D cranium models were generated on the software interface (Koçyiğit and Demircioğlu 2024).

In order to facilitate the analysis of the skull shape, landmarks were marked using the Stratovan Checkpoint programme. The landmarks included x, y and z coordinates, with a total of 23 landmarks marked in total. Nine of these landmarks were marked on the median line, and a further 14 were marked bilaterally. Statistical Analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed following procrustes superimposition to determine the shape variation in the rabbit skull. Thus, groups and individuals were positioned in the PCA plot. Procrustes ANOVA was performed for the statistical difference between centroid size and shape in female and male individuals. Also, discriminant function analysis was performed for the effect of sex on shape. In this way, the grouping of the specimens was determined according to the cross‐validation scores. MorphoJ (version 1.07a, Klingenberg 2011) was used for all these analyses.

A total of 11 PCs were calculated as a result of PCA (Table 2). The shape variation graph is shown in Figure 4, and the distribution graph of the individuals is shown in Figure 5. According to PC1 (25.47%), shape variation was concentrated in the temporal region (LM18‐25). This was followed by landmarks at the medial eye angle level (LM8‐11). There were also significant shape variations in LM4 and LM5 representing the incisive bone and LM12 representing the face compared to PC1. The shape variation for PC2 (21.73%) was mostly consistent with PC1. According to the scatterplot of PC1 and PC2, females clustered more sharply than males.

Discussion

Geometric morphometry is a method of two‐dimensional or 3D shape analysis of biological structures, which is of great importance for taxonomy, phylogenetic and especially archaeological studies. Geometric morphometry is a practical and cost‐effective method of revealing sex and race differences in biological materials without damaging excavated specimens (Işcan 2001; Mooder et al. 2005; Kaestle and Horsburg 2002). In our study, we employed a 3D surface scanner to scan rabbit craniums and conducted geometric morphometric analysis to assess its efficacy in dimorphic discrimination.

It has been documented that 3D models can be obtained by three‐dimensional surface scanners other than medical imaging systems, and it has been suggested that the data obtained after scanning and modelling can be utilised as a substitute for real values (Koçyiğit and Demircioğlu 2024). Consequently, the analyses conducted on the models obtained using the surface scanner in our study are unrestricted. Upon examination of the study data, it was observed that the most dimorphic difference in terms of shape analysis of the craniums was observed in the temporal region, followed by the medial eye angle and incisive bone. In contrast, Yong et al. (2018) reported that there was no dimorphic difference between genders in 3D geometric morphometric analysis of human premolar teeth. In a separate study, Zhang and Schepart (2021) reported dimorphic differences in 3D shape analysis of the human occipital region.

In their study on the cranium of New Zealand rabbits, Akkaya and Gürbüz (2024) reported that the effects of cranium on shape were not significant. The study determined that there were statistically significant dimorphic differences but no significant statistical difference in centroid size. The PCA revealed that PC1 accounted for 34.81% of the total shape variation, with the first four PCs contributing to 74.80% of the variation. The PCA identified the caudal region of the proc. nasalis of the os incisivun as the area of the greatest variation. In a subsequent study, Böhmer and Böhmer (2017) analysed the shape of the craniums of European rabbits and domestic rabbits, reporting that the first three PCs (PC1+PC2+PC3) explained 75% of the shape differences. The highest variance was observed in the craniomedial part of the os nasale, followed by the caudal part of the os occipitale. In a subsequent study, Önel and Temizer (2019) conducted a shape analysis of wild rabbit craniums, reporting that no differences were observed between groups or sexes. They further noted that in the PCA, variation was confined to the ventral part of the cranium, with no differences detected in the dorsal region. In a subsequent investigation (Bruzek 2002), the effects of domestication on cranial shape were examined using geometric morphometrics. The study involved the analysis of crania from both wild and domestic rabbits, and it was reported that the shapes of the first two basic components in the lateral direction differed by 61.6%. The potential causes of this variation were hypothesised to be related to differences in feeding and breeding styles. The study determined that PC1 accounted for 25.47% of the shape variation, with the first four PCs explaining 71.28%. The differing results of the shape analyses on the same species are hypothesised to be due to 3D analyses providing more detailed results than two‐dimensional analyses.

Linear measurements do not provide sufficient information on shape when analysing irregular bones such as the cranium. For this reason, the geometric morphometric method provides an undisputed advantage in order to comment on the shape of such objects (Rohlf and Marcus 1993). Since the size of the objects to be analysed is ignored in this analysis, comparisons are possible without standardisation of the animal material. This is particularly advantageous for zooarchaeology (O'Higgins 2000).

As a result, in this study, it was aimed to reveal the shape differences between sexes by performing 3D shape analysis of rabbit craniums. For this purpose, it was statistically determined that there were shape differences between sexes in rabbit cranium. It is thought that 3D geometric morphometry method of rabbit craniums can be used as an analysis method for sex determination.
