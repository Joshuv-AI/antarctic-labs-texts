Microtensile bond strength and dentin–adhesive interface of universal adhesives before and after artificial aging: an in vitro study
Authors: Flor Santander-Rengifo, Carmen Martin Carreras-Presas, César Cayo-Rojas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUniversal adhesives (UAs) are widely used for resin composite restorations; however, the durability of the dentin–adhesive interface after aging remains a key clinical concern, as interfacial degradation may compromise bond stability over time. Therefore, this study evaluated the microtensile bond strength (µTBS) and dentin–adhesive interface of four universal adhesives before and after artificial aging. Occlusal thirds of human third molars were sectioned and randomly assigned to four groups: A, Palfique Universal Bond; B, Single Bond Universal; C, All-Bond Universal; D, One Coat 7 Universal. All UAs were used in self-etch mode. Palfique Universal Bond was not light-cured (chemical activation); All-Bond Universal was applied in two coats; the others in one. The total sample comprised 144 sticks. Of these, 120 were tested for µTBS (30/group) at 1 mm/min and subdivided with/without artificial aging (n = 15/subgroup; 10,000 thermocycles between 5 ± 2 °C and 55 ± 2 °C). Failure mode was examined under a stereomicroscope. Adhesive-interface thickness was measured on 24 sticks (n = 6/group) by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Data were analysed by two-way ANOVA for µTBS, followed by simple comparisons when the interaction was significant. For adhesive-interface thickness, comparisons among adhesives within each aging condition and between aging conditions within each adhesive were evaluated using one-way ANOVA with Scheffé post hoc tests (p < 0.05). Two-way ANOVA showed significant effects of adhesive type, aging condition, and their interaction on µTBS. Group A showed significantly lower µTBS than Groups B–D (all p < 0.001; η2 = 65.9% without aging; η2 = 85.4% with aging); B, C and D did not differ (p > 0.05). Aging reduced µTBS for A and D (p < 0.001 and p = 0.008, respectively) but not for B or C. In the non-aged condition, C and D did not differ (p > 0.05), while D had a thicker interface than A and B (p < 0.05; η2 = 82.5%). After aging, A presented a thinner interface than B–D (p < 0.05; η2 = 93.5%) and was the only UA showing a reduction versus its non-aged counterpart (p = 0.003). Failure modes were predominantly adhesive for A and B, and mainly mixed for C and D (p < 0.001). After artificial aging, Palfique Universal Bond showed lower microtensile bond strength and a thinner adhesive interface, whereas Single Bond Universal and All-Bond Universal maintained comparable bond strength with no reduction in adhesive-interface thickness; One Coat 7 Universal showed a significant decrease in bond strength without a reduction in adhesive-interface thickness. Within the limitations of this in vitro study, the 10-MDP–containing adhesive systems evaluated showed greater interfacial stability after aging than the 3D-SR–containing system tested; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because the materials also differed in overall formulation and application protocol.Keywords: Universal adhesives, Microtensile bond strength, Dentin, Adhesive interface, Self-etchSubject terms: Health care, Materials science, Medical research

Introduction

The longevity of resin composite restorations bonded with adhesive systems may be compromised over time owing to hydrolytic degradation of the adhesive interface and hybrid layer after the etch-and-rinse process, leading to incomplete monomer infiltration into etched dentin and thereby impairing adhesive performance1,2. Modern dental adhesive systems—so-called universal adhesives were developed to provide reliable bonding to dental substrates through simplified clinical protocols, particularly when applied to dentin in self-etch mode3–9. In this approach, acidic functional monomers infiltrate simultaneously, generating a dual bonding mechanism based mainly on ionic interaction and micromechanical interlocking through dentin wetting, allowing the formation of resin tags1,2,10–12.

Among UA components, the acidic monomer appears to be pivotal, as formulations may include one or more hydrophilic functional monomers such as carboxylic acids or methacrylate phosphate acids13. These partially demineralize the smear layer and can bond ionically with the calcium in dentinal hydroxyapatite (HAp)14,15. Functional monomers feature a polymerizable group and a polar functional group that together promote wetting, demineralization, and ionic bonding to tooth tissue6,8,13,16.

The acidic functional monomer 10-methacryloyloxydecyl dihydrogen phosphate (10-MDP) is currently among the most effective with respect to chemical interaction and clinical longevity, forming stable calcium-phosphate complexes and regular layers on the HAp surface16. The long, relatively hydrophobic spacer chain of 10-MDP separating the methacrylate moiety from the phosphate group1,13 confers hydrolytic stability and low solubility to the 10-MDP–Ca calcium salt formed on HAp, yielding an effective and durable bond to dentin6–8,17.

Other functional monomers, such as three-dimensional self-reinforcing acid (3D-SR), contain multiple functional groups per molecule, enabling multiple point interactions with dentinal calcium6–15, which promotes strong adhesion to tooth structure and three-dimensional cross-linking via reaction with calcium. Such tridimensional cross-links play an important role by enabling copolymerization reactions that form a uniform, thin, highly adhesive layer. According to the manufacturer, stabilizing the adhesive system at pH 2 reduces monomer hydrolysis during storage6,18.

Additional factors associated with improved UA bonding to dentin include the use of very mild acidic functional monomers; alternative, less hydrophilic polymeric components19; and the choice and optimization of solvent systems with higher vapor pressure and wettability, which facilitate monomer diffusion and the evaporation of dentinal fluid during bonding procedures3,19.

Chen et al. (2015)20, Dönmez et al. (2019)21, Hass et al. (2019)22, Santander et al. (2024)23, and Wagner et al. (2014)7 reported comparable bond-strength outcomes when they compared Single Bond Universal and All-Bond Universal. Siqueira et al. (2018)24 also evaluated One Coat 7 Universal against All-Bond Universal and Single Bond Universal and reported favorable bond-strength performance, consistent with the pattern observed in the present study. In addition, Wagner et al. (2014)7 assessed bond strength and adhesive-interface thickness and found similarly strong bonding for Single Bond Universal and All-Bond Universal, which supports the findings of the present investigation.

The composition of universal adhesive systems can markedly influence their interaction with dentin structure; however, few studies characterize the interaction of existing functional monomers with dentin and its effects on adhesion6. Therefore, this study evaluated the microtensile bond strength and the dentin–adhesive interface of four universal adhesives before and after artificial aging. Based on the available evidence on functional-monomer chemistry and durability, this study tested the following research hypotheses: (1) the 10-MDP–containing universal adhesives differ from the 3D-SR–containing adhesive in microtensile bond strength, both before and after artificial aging; (2) artificial aging reduces microtensile bond strength, and the magnitude of this reduction differs among universal adhesives; and (3) artificial aging reduces adhesive-interface thickness, and the magnitude of this reduction differs among universal adhesives.

Methods

Ethical considerations

This research was reviewed and approved by the Ethics and Research Committee of Universidad Europea de Madrid (internal code CIPI/22.331). The teeth used in the present study were voluntarily donated for research purposes by patients treated at the Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery Service of Universidad Peruana Cayetano Heredia, with informed consent and with participants’ confidentiality safeguarded, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki25.

Study design

This was an experimental in vitro study. It was conducted at the Oral Health Research Laboratory (LISO), Faculty of Stomatology, Universidad Peruana Cayetano Heredia, and at an ISO/IEC 17,025–accredited High Technology Laboratory in Peru, from December 2022 to April 2023. The study adhered to the CRIS Guidelines (Checklist for Reporting In-vitro Studies)26.

Sample size calculation and selection

The total sample comprised 144 sticks obtained from 48 sound human third molars randomly allocated to four adhesive groups (12 teeth per group). Microtensile bond strength served as the primary outcome. The tooth was the original biological source of the specimens; however, the final analytical dataset for µTBS was organized at the stick level after random selection from the pool obtained within each adhesive group. For comparison of adhesive bond strength, the final analyzed sample comprised 120 sticks, corresponding to 30 sticks per adhesive and 15 sticks per adhesive-by-aging condition. To ensure balanced sampling, we selected sticks at random from the pool obtained within each group. For µTBS, sample size was estimated using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) for F tests, ANOVA: fixed effects, special, main effects and interactions, considering a two-factor design with 8 groups (4 adhesives × 2 aging conditions), α = 0.05, power (1 − β) = 0.80, and numerator df = 3. The effect size for the interaction term of the factorial model was obtained from a pilot study including 10 sticks per subgroup (ηp2 = 0.086; Cohen’s f = 0.307), yielding a required total sample size of 120 sticks. Additional sticks were generated a priori to preserve the target sample size if exclusions became necessary. When a tested stick exhibited cohesive failure, it was excluded from the final interfacial µTBS dataset, and another available stick from the same experimental subgroup was selected to maintain the planned sample size. In addition, 24 specimens were used to compare adhesive-interface thickness, corresponding to 6 specimens per adhesive and 3 per adhesive-by-aging condition. Adhesive-interface thickness served as a secondary outcome and was quantified on specimens not subjected to µTBS testing. For adhesive-interface thickness, sample size was estimated using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) for F tests, ANOVA: fixed effects, special, main effects and interactions, considering a two-factor design with 8 groups (4 adhesives × 2 aging conditions), α = 0.05, power (1 − β) = 0.80, and numerator df = 3. The effect size was derived from the interaction term of the factorial model (ηp2 = 0.621; Cohen’s f = 1.28), which yielded a required total sample size of approximately 14 specimens. Therefore, the final sample of 24 specimens was considered sufficient for the factorial analysis of adhesive-interface thickness (Fig. 1).

Sample characteristics and specimen preparation

Forty-eight sound human third molars were extracted three months prior to the experiment14. Residual soft tissue and plaque were removed using an ultrasonic scaler (DTE D5 LED, Woodpecker, Guilin, Guangxi, China). Teeth were then stored in 70% ethanol for two weeks at 4 °C14, and subsequently kept in deionized distilled water until use. This storage medium was used as a disinfecting condition before specimen preparation, following a previously published protocol14,27. Because storage media may influence dentin permeability and bond-strength outcomes, this factor should be considered when interpreting the results. Storage water was refreshed every three days.

Each tooth was positioned individually in polyvinyl chloride (PVC) tubes (25 × 15 mm), fixed with sticky wax (Koriwax, Lima, Peru), and embedded with self-curing acrylic resin (Reliance Dental Mfg., Worth, IL, USA), ensuring the long axis of the tooth was parallel to the tube walls. The occlusal third of the crown was removed using a precision sectioning machine (OCP 100, ODEME, São Carlos, Brazil) with a diamond disc (#7020, KG Sorensen, Barueri, SP, Brazil) at 300 rpm under continuous water cooling (Fig. 2A). Flat mid-coronal dentin surfaces were prepared with a #600-grit silicon-carbide abrasive paper under running water for 60 s to create a standardized smear layer (Fig. 2B). The tubes were labelled and randomly assigned to the four universal-adhesive groups. The allocated adhesive system was applied immediately and actively with a microbrush applicator (Microbrush Plus and Tube Series, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Fig. 2C).

(A) Sectioning of the occlusal third of molars; (B) polishing of the dentin surface; (C) application of the universal adhesive; (D) placement of resin composite in 2 mm increments; (E) measurement of the final composite increment with a digital caliper; (F) light-curing of the resin composite.

Two 2-mm increments of Filtek Z350 XT resin composite, shade A1 (3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA), were then placed to obtain a total composite height of 4 mm (Fig. 2D), verified with a digital caliper with 0.01 mm resolution (Digimatic Caliper, Mitutoyo, Tokyo, Japan) (Fig. 2E). Each increment was light-cured for 20 s using an LED curing unit (Valo, Ultradent, South Jordan, UT, USA) at 1000 mW/cm2 (Fig. 2F)28,29. After 24 h, half of the specimens were subjected to artificial aging by thermocycling (10,000 cycles; 30 s dwell; 3 s transfer; 5–55 °C) before microtensile bond strength (µTBS) testing; the remaining specimens underwent µTBS testing without aging9,20.

Microtensile bond strength (µTBS) test

Each restored tooth was sectioned with a high-precision digital cutting machine (OCP 100, ODEME, São Carlos, Brazil) first in the buccopalatal direction across the restoration–tooth interface and then mesiodistally to obtain dentin–resin sticks with a cross-sectional area of approximately 1 ± 0.2 mm230, randomly selecting 36 sticks per group. In total, 144 sticks were obtained, each ~ 1 mm wide and 8–9 mm long (Fig. 3A). Stick thickness was measured with a digital caliper. Subsequently, 120 sticks were mounted to an aluminum jig using a cyanoacrylate-based adhesive (Triz, Soldimix, Lima, Peru) by bonding the resin and dentin ends, for µTBS testing in a universal testing machine (OM 100, ODEME, São Carlos, Brazil) (Fig. 3B and C). Tests were performed at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min until failure at the dentin–resin adhesive interface. Bond strength (MPa) was calculated by dividing the applied load (N) by the bonded area (mm2), i.e., N/mm2 = MPa.

(A) Preparation of dentin–resin sticks; (B) sticks mounted on metal bases; (C) universal testing machine for microtensile testing.

Failure mode

The failure modes were assessed on the sticks tested for µTBS using a stereomicroscope (Leica S8 APO; LAS v3.4) at 40× magnification. Specimens were classified as: adhesive (failure at the adhesive interface), mixed (failure at the adhesive interface with residual adhesive or resin on the dentin surface), or cohesive (failure within dentin or within the resin composite)20. Specimens showing cohesive failure were excluded from the final analytical dataset according to the predefined interfacial analytical criterion. Accordingly, the reported failure-mode analysis was based on the included non-cohesive sample only. Failure modes were assessed using these predefined morphological criteria under stereomicroscopy.

Adhesive-interface thickness

Six sticks per group (total n = 24), with and without artificial aging, were randomly selected and not subjected to µTBS; instead, they were prepared and examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to evaluate and measure adhesive-interface thickness. Preparation involved polishing with #1500, #2000, and #2500 silicon-carbide papers, rinsing with distilled water, and placing the specimens in an ultrasonic bath (Gnatus, Brazil) for 30 s. For demineralization, specimens were immersed in 6 N hydrochloric acid (HCl; ~6 mol/L) for 30 s and rinsed with distilled water for 1 min. For deproteinization, 1% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) was applied for 10 min, followed by a 5-min rinse with distilled water. Specimens were then dehydrated in graded ethanol: 25% (20 min), 50% (20 min), 75% (20 min), 95% (30 min), and 100% (60 min). Finally, they were dried in a desiccator with silica at 37 °C for 24 h. The prepared specimens were examined under SEM at 2000× and 5000× magnifications, and thickness was measured at four equidistant points along the adhesive interface31,32. The mean of these four measurements was used as the specimen-level value for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed in IBM SPSS Statistics, version 28.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). We summarized continuous variables as mean and standard deviation (SD) and reported 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Prior to hypothesis testing, we assessed normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and homoscedasticity using Levene’s test. For µTBS, we performed a two-way ANOVA with adhesive type and aging condition as fixed factors, including their interaction. Because the interaction was significant, the comparisons presented were treated as follow-up simple comparisons to describe differences among adhesives within each aging condition and differences between aging conditions within each adhesive. According to the predefined analytical criterion of this study, cohesive failures were excluded from the final interfacial µTBS dataset; therefore, the reported µTBS and failure-mode analyses were based on the included non-cohesive sample. Because the final analytical dataset was organized at the stick level and tooth-level identifiers were not retained, clustering within teeth could not be modeled explicitly; therefore, pseudoreplication cannot be fully excluded and this issue was considered when interpreting the findings. For adhesive-interface thickness, comparisons among adhesives within each aging condition and between aging conditions within each adhesive were interpreted as follow-up simple comparisons using one-way ANOVA with Scheffé post hoc tests, as appropriate. For the two-way ANOVA, effect sizes were expressed as partial eta-squared (ηp2). We estimated effect sizes using eta-squared (η2) to quantify the magnitude of between-adhesive differences in the one-way models. For failure mode, we evaluated the association between adhesive type and failure category using Pearson’s chi-square test, followed by pairwise comparisons using Z tests with Bonferroni correction when applicable. We set statistical significance at p < 0.05.

Results

Adhesive type showed the largest effect on µTBS (ηp2 = 0.700) (p < 0.001), indicating that bond strength was influenced mainly by the adhesive system used. Aging condition also had a significant effect (ηp2 = 0.219) (p < 0.001), consistent with an overall reduction in µTBS after aging. Although smaller in magnitude, the adhesive type × aging interaction was also significant (ηp2 = 0.083) (p = 0.021), indicating that the effect of aging differed across adhesives (Table 1).

Discussion

Universal adhesive systems containing functional monomers such as 10-MDP and 3D-SR interact with dentin in distinct ways, thereby influencing bond performance1. In the present study, the 10-MDP-containing systems Single Bond Universal, All-Bond Universal, and One Coat 7 Universal achieved higher dentin bond strength than Palfique Universal Bond, which contains 3D-SR, both with and without thermocycling. Although this pattern is consistent with the recognized properties of 10-MDP, the tested adhesives differed not only in functional monomer but also in pH, solvent system, bottle architecture, and application protocol; therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as differences among commercial adhesive systems rather than as a direct monomer-to-monomer comparison. This pattern may be related to the ability of 10-MDP to form stable chemical interactions with hydroxyapatite, which has been associated with durable adhesion3,13. However, because the present study did not directly evaluate chemical bonding mechanisms, this interpretation should remain cautious. Overall, the results indicate that the tested adhesive systems differed in µTBS and in their response to artificial aging.

Dentin is a hydrated, collagen-rich biocomposite whose tubules are perfused by fluid under outward pulpal pressure; its intrinsic wetness and compositional dynamics make resin–dentin bonding markedly less predictable than enamel7. These substrate characteristics help contextualize the variability in µTBS and interface morphology observed across systems.

The stability and longevity of bonding at the resin–dentin adhesive interface are critical to the clinical performance of direct resin composite restorations. Adhesive strategies include etch-and-rinse, self-etch, and selective-etch approaches using one- or two-step universal adhesives. Regardless of the strategy, the goal is to form an adhesive and hybrid layer via adequate adhesive infiltration and to achieve durable bonding—a goal that remains challenging19.

Thermocycling is widely used to simulate aging because it imposes cyclic expansion–contraction stresses along the resin–dentin interface7. In this study, specimens underwent 10,000 cycles between 5 and 55 °C, a condition used in previous in vitro studies as an approximate laboratory simulation of one year of clinical aging; however, this equivalence should be interpreted cautiously because it is not universally established9,23. In addition, immersion in warm water may facilitate extraction and elution of incompletely polymerized adhesive resin components and accelerate degradation of unprotected collagen fibrils, helping to explain the reduced bond strength after artificial aging compared with non-aged specimens. Consistent with Wagner et al.7, Maciel et al.3, and Chen et al.20, evaluations at 5,000–10,000 thermocycles with Single Bond Universal and All-Bond Universal have shown no significant reduction in bond strength relative to non-aged controls. This accords with the present findings: although all four adhesives showed some reduction, only Palfique Universal Bond and One Coat 7 Universal exhibited a significant decrease when aged versus non-aged. Such differences may reflect acidity, composition, presentation (e.g. one- vs. two-bottle), application protocol, etching strategy, and other factors6,7,16,20.

Building on the preceding overview, we examined how formulation—particularly acidity—relates to performance. In our study, the ultra-mild systems (One Coat 7 Universal, All-Bond Universal, and Single Bond Universal; pH 2.8, 3.2, and 2.7, respectively) achieved higher µTBS than the mild Palfique Universal Bond (pH ≈ 2). Within the adhesion–decalcification framework, greater acidity increases the depth of dentin demineralization, but excessive demineralization can outpace monomer infiltration, leaving water-rich collagen susceptible to hydrolysis and compromising the hybrid layer13. However, pH alone is not determinative of interfacial morphology or performance16. This pattern is consistent with our interface findings: after thermocycling, Palfique Universal Bond exhibited the thinnest adhesive interface and was the only system showing a significant within-adhesive reduction in interface thickness, alongside a significant drop in µTBS, whereas the ultra-mild 10-MDP–containing systems maintained comparable interface thicknesses and µTBS stability overall (with only One Coat 7 Universal showing a modest but significant decrease).Thus, the effect of aging on adhesive-interface thickness should be interpreted cautiously and should not be generalized to all adhesives tested, as a significant reduction was observed only for Palfique Universal Bond.

Monomer chemistry likely contributed. The 10-MDP–containing systems (Single Bond Universal, All-Bond Universal, One Coat 7 Universal) benefit from a relatively hydrophobic spacer that separates the methacrylate from the phosphate group, enabling stable ionic interaction with hydroxyapatite and formation of a water-insoluble 10-MDP–Ca salt—features linked to hydrolytic stability and durable bonding3,7,13. By contrast, 3D-SR (in Palfique Universal Bond) provides multiple potential interaction sites and three-dimensional cross-linking, but its overall formulation and acidity may favour deeper demineralization without proportionally improving infiltration, which could explain the greater aging-related losses observed here. Protocol-level variables, including coating strategy, bottle architecture, solvent behavior, and application dynamics, may also have contributed to the differences observed among the tested systems3,7,16,20.

Although the thickness of the adhesive interface may not be critical to the efficacy of self-etch universal adhesives and can be thin or even absent7, mild and ultra-mild systems incompletely demineralize dentin, leaving hydroxyapatite partially attached to collagen and available for chemical interaction and protective nanolayering7,13. By contrast, stronger acids dissolve apatite crystals and preclude chemical bonding between functional adhesive monomers and apatite20. Solvent type and evaporation time may also influence performance26. In our study, however, the ethanol/water-based systems (All-Bond Universal, One Coat 7 Universal, Single Bond Universal) produced higher µTBS than the acetone-containing Palfique Universal Bond, both with and without thermocycling, suggesting that monomer chemistry and overall formulation may outweigh solvent volatility under the present protocol9,20.

The more favorable performance observed for the 10-MDP–containing systems in this study may be related to the recognized chemical behavior of this functional monomer, including stable ionic interaction with hydroxyapatite, formation of water-insoluble calcium salts, and self-assembly of stabilizing nanolayers, all of which have been associated with hydrolytic durability1,12,13. However, these findings cannot be attributed exclusively to 10-MDP, because the tested adhesives also differed in formulation, acidity, solvent system, and application protocol. This is particularly relevant for Single Bond Universal, which also contains a polyalkenoic acid copolymer (Vitrebond) capable of chelating calcium, although its high molecular weight may also hinder monomer diffusion and compete with 10-MDP for binding sites6,7,9,16,18,20. By contrast, 3D-SR has been proposed to provide multisite ionic interaction and three-dimensional cross-linking8, and the two-bottle design of Palfique Universal Bond has been claimed to improve polymerization18; nevertheless, in the present study and in previous reports, its bond-strength performance was lower than that of the 10-MDP–containing systems6,23. This discrepancy may reflect differences in overall formulation and in the acid-dissociation behavior of the functional monomer, particularly because pKa values for 3D-SR remain unavailable. Overall, the present findings support a formulation-dependent interpretation of bond performance, consistent with the large effect sizes observed and with previous evidence showing that in vitro outcomes remain sensitive to protocol and sample characteristics22,33,34.

Regarding failure modes, previous studies associated adhesive failures with lower bond strength and mixed failures with higher bond strength, indicating a relationship between bond strength and failure pattern9. Our findings align with this pattern: All-Bond Universal and One Coat 7 Universal showed a higher proportion of mixed failures and higher µTBS, whereas Palfique Universal Bond exhibited predominantly adhesive failures and lower µTBS. Accordingly, the predominance of mixed failures in the higher-performing adhesives may reflect more favorable interfacial behavior, whereas the predominance of adhesive failures in Palfique Universal Bond is consistent with its lower bond-strength values.

With respect to the adhesive interface and hybrid-layer thickness, and in agreement with Wagner et al.7, Cengiz et al.9, and Feitosa et al.1, our SEM analysis showed that none of the universal adhesives removed the smear layer or penetrated dentinal tubules (i.e., few or no resin tags), a finding consistent with their mild or ultra-mild acidity. Consequently, the hybrid layer appeared very thin or indistinct, consistent with previous reports on self-etch universal adhesive systems1,2,7,9.

For direct restorations, the mild and ultra-mild 10-MDP–containing adhesives evaluated in this study showed more favorable bond-strength performance than Palfique Universal Bond. However, because this was an in vitro study, these findings should not be overinterpreted as direct evidence of clinical superiority. A strength of this study is that effect sizes were reported, which may be useful for planning future clinical studies33,34. The study design, including sample-size planning, thermocycling, and controlled experimental procedures, helped reduce measurement bias; nevertheless, as an in vitro study, clinical extrapolation remains limited. The present findings should therefore be interpreted as laboratory-based evidence under controlled conditions rather than as direct evidence for clinical recommendation or superiority. Another limitation was that the adhesives were applied according to manufacturer-specific protocols, which differed in application time, number of coats, and curing scheme. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting the performance of complete adhesive systems under recommended use rather than a protocol-standardized comparison. In addition, specimens showing cohesive failure were excluded from the final interfacial µTBS dataset according to the predefined analytical criterion. Because cohesive failures are a relevant methodological consideration in µTBS studies and may reflect the mechanical properties of the substrate or restorative material rather than interfacial bond strength, the reported µTBS and failure-mode analyses should be interpreted as reflecting the included non-cohesive analytical sample rather than the complete fracture profile of all tested sticks7,35. The teeth were also stored in 70% ethanol before specimen preparation; although this condition was applied uniformly to all teeth, storage media may influence dentin permeability and bond-strength measurements and should therefore be considered when interpreting the findings. In addition, because multiple sticks could originate from the same tooth and the final analytical dataset did not retain tooth-level identifiers, within-tooth clustering could not be modeled explicitly; therefore, pseudoreplication cannot be fully excluded and the findings should be interpreted with caution. Although the final sample size for adhesive-interface thickness exceeded the minimum estimated requirement for the factorial model, the number of specimens per adhesive-by-aging condition remained limited; therefore, the SEM findings should be interpreted with caution. In addition, formal examiner-calibration statistics for failure-mode assessment were not reported, which may limit reproducibility and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Future studies should compare universal adhesives across pH ranges, application modes and times, and functional monomers, and should corroborate bond-strength findings with SEM characterization of the interface.

Conclusion

After artificial aging, Palfique Universal Bond showed lower microtensile bond strength and a thinner adhesive interface, whereas Single Bond Universal and All-Bond Universal maintained comparable microtensile bond strength with no reduction in adhesive-interface thickness; One Coat 7 Universal exhibited a significant decrease in microtensile bond strength without a decrease in adhesive-interface thickness. Within the limitations of this in vitro study, the 10-MDP–containing adhesive systems evaluated showed greater interfacial stability after aging than the 3D-SR–containing system tested; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because the materials differed in overall formulation and application protocol.
