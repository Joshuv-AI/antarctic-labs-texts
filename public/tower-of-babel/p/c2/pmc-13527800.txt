Prostate Cancer Management in Nigeria: A mini-review of up-to-date treatment options
Authors: Emmanuel O. Oisakede
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prostate cancer remains a major public health challenge in Nigeria, being the most commonly diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among men. Despite recent improvements in cancer policies, registries, and selected diagnostic and treatment investments, access to timely, equitable, and guideline-concordant care remains limited. Current evidence indicates persistent late presentation, delays in diagnosis and staging, high out-of-pocket costs, and significant disparities in access to specialist services. Although curative treatments such as radical prostatectomy, radiotherapy, and brachytherapy are available in selected centres, many patients present with advanced disease or experience delays that limit their effectiveness. Consequently, androgen-deprivation therapy remains the predominant treatment modality. Improving outcomes requires strengthening the entire care pathway through early risk-based detection, efficient referral systems, faster pathology services, expanded financial protection, multidisciplinary management, and robust monitoring of treatment outcomes.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is now one of the central cancer-control challenges facing men's health in Nigeria. Globally, it accounted for about 1.47 million new cases and 397,430 deaths in 2022, making it the second most frequently diagnosed cancer in men [1]. The burden is expected to rise further. The 2024 Lancet Commission projected that annual new cases worldwide will increase from 1.4 million in 2020 to 2.9 million by 2040, with much of that growth occurring in low- and middle-income settings [2].

Nigeria already carries a substantial share of this burden. GLOBOCAN 2022 estimated 18,019 new prostate cancer cases and 11,443 deaths in the country [3]. Current IARC data identify prostate cancer as the most common cancer in Nigerian men and the leading cause of male cancer death, underlining its importance as both a clinical and public health priority [4]. Yet the problem is not defined by incidence alone. It is shaped by the conditions under which men encounter the health system. For example, out-of-pocket spending still makes up 71.9% of total health expenditure in Nigeria [5], while the country's reported UHC service coverage index is 47, both of which have direct implications for timely diagnosis and continuity of care [6].

Recent Nigerian evidence suggests that late diagnosis remains common. In one 2025 study from Abeokuta, men reported low perceived risk, poor screening uptake, embarrassment, fear of pain, financial barriers, and lack of physician recommendation as important reasons for delayed presentation [7]. In a separate Nigerian cohort, the median interval from referral to treatment was 103 days, illustrating how delays may continue even after men enter specialist care [8]. Against this background, an update on prostate cancer management in Nigeria is timely. The issue is no longer simply what treatments are available, but who can access them, how early they are reached, and whether care is delivered in a way that can realistically improve survival. This mini-narrative review therefore reviews the current state of prostate cancer management in Nigeria, highlighting recent progress while drawing attention to the structural gaps that still shape outcomes.

Methods

Search strategy

This mini narrative review was informed by a targeted literature search of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Google Scholar, and African Journals Online (AJOL). The search covered publications from January 2010 to December 2025, with additional policy documents and cancer-control reports included where they provided current national or regional context. Search terms were used alone and in combination and included: "prostate cancer," "Nigeria," "prostate cancer management," "prostate cancer screening," "early diagnosis," "PSA testing," "radical prostatectomy," "radiotherapy," "brachytherapy," "androgen-deprivation therapy," "metastatic prostate cancer," "castration-resistant prostate cancer," "cancer care delivery," "health systems," "cancer registry," "National Cancer Control Plan," and "Nigeria cancer policy." Reference lists of relevant articles and reports were also screened to identify additional sources.

Selection criteria

Sources were included if they addressed prostate cancer burden, diagnosis, screening, treatment, outcomes, service delivery, financing, workforce, infrastructure, registries, or policy in Nigeria. Relevant Nigerian studies, reports, policy documents, and selected international guidelines were considered for contextual interpretation. Sources lacking relevance to prostate cancer management, Nigeria or comparable settings, methodological detail, or substantive evidence were excluded. Opinion pieces and non-substantive reports were generally excluded unless they described important recent service developments.

Study types and evidence sources

The review considered a broad range of evidence sources, reflecting the health-system focus of the article. These included observational studies, hospital-based case series, cancer-registry reports, qualitative and mixed-methods studies, policy documents, national cancer-control plans, international agency reports,

clinical guidelines, and selected narrative or systematic reviews. This approach was chosen because prostate cancer management in Nigeria is shaped not only by clinical evidence but also by financing, workforce capacity, diagnostic infrastructure, radiotherapy access, referral pathways, and policy implementation.

Synthesis method

Findings were synthesized thematically rather than statistically. Evidence was organized around four major themes: disease burden and epidemiologic context; diagnosis and early detection; contemporary treatment pathways; and health-system barriers affecting access, timeliness, and quality of care. Additional themes included emerging progress, persistent implementation gaps, and priority actions for improving prostate cancer outcomes in Nigeria. Because this was a mini narrative review, no formal meta-analysis, risk-of-bias assessment, or pooled estimate was undertaken. Instead, emphasis was placed on identifying recurring patterns, recent developments, and practical implications for policy, clinical care, and cancer-service delivery.

Burden and epidemiologic context in Nigeria

Prostate cancer represents a major component of Nigeria's male cancer burden. GLOBOCAN 2022 estimated 18,019 new cases and 11,443 deaths, making it the most common cancer among Nigerian men, accounting for 37.5% of male cancer diagnoses. Registry data, including findings from the Abuja Cancer Registry, similarly demonstrate a high disease burden. However, national estimates remain limited by incomplete cancer surveillance and reliance on modelling due to insufficient registry coverage. Beyond incidence, the major challenge is late diagnosis. Nigerian clinical studies show that many men present with prolonged symptoms, minimal screening detection, advanced disease, and poorly differentiated tumours at diagnosis. This pattern reflects broader challenges in low- and middle-income countries, where delayed presentation remains common. Thus, Nigeria's prostate cancer burden is driven not only by disease frequency but also by gaps in early detection, timely diagnosis, and access to effective care pathways. Strengthening surveillance and early diagnosis strategies is essential to improving outcomes.

Current diagnostic and screening landscape in Nigeria

The diagnostic pathway for prostate cancer in Nigeria remains shaped more by opportunistic case-finding than by a mature, population-based early detection system. Although Nigeria's National Strategic Cancer Control Plan 2023–2027 includes an objective to establish nationwide routine screening programmes, more recent field evidence still describes screening as centralized and opportunistic rather than systematic [12]. In practice, men are often investigated only after urinary symptoms, bone pain, or a chance PSA result brings them into specialist care. This matters because the central problem is not merely low awareness; it is the absence of a dependable pathway that moves men from suspicion to diagnosis before disease becomes advanced. Community-level data support this interpretation. In Ido-Ekiti, 57.9% of surveyed men aged 40 years and older reported awareness of prostate cancer, yet 74.4% had poor knowledge of the disease and screening tests, and only 18.2% had ever undergone screening [13]. In Abeokuta, late diagnosis was linked to low perceived susceptibility, fear, embarrassment, cost, and lack of physician recommendation [7]. These studies suggest that awareness campaigns alone will not be enough. Men may have heard of prostate cancer, but many still do not see themselves as candidates for testing, do not know where to go, or are discouraged by the financial and psychological costs of entering care.

At the clinical level, the usual entry points remain PSA testing and digital rectal examination, followed by biopsy and histopathology when suspicion is high. However, the weak point in Nigeria is not the existence of PSA as a test; it is the fragility of the diagnostic chain that follows an abnormal result. In one Nigerian cohort, the median interval from presentation to biopsy was 30 days, from biopsy to histology review 24 days, and the median overall interval from referral to treatment was 103 days [8]. A later ten-year experience from another Nigerian teaching hospital also reported prolonged delays, with diagnosis taking an average of 6.53 months and treatment starting after 9.71 months [14]. These findings make an important analytical point: diagnostic delay in Nigeria is cumulative. Time is lost not at one single step, but across referral, biopsy scheduling, pathology processing, treatment planning, and financial preparation.

Imaging illustrates a similar pattern of uneven progress. Contemporary prostate cancer care increasingly links PSA-based early detection with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and the 2024 Lancet Commission argues that in low- and middle-income countries, the immediate priority should be to expand diagnostic capability, raise awareness, and use targeted case-finding in high-risk groups rather than assume that Western-style mass screening can simply be transplanted [2]. In Nigeria, selected tertiary centres now use pelvic or multiparametric MRI, and a 2025 study from southeast Nigeria reaffirmed its value for staging and characterization [15]. Yet the same study also concluded that patients in that setting still commonly presented with advanced disease. The implication is clear: new diagnostic technologies are appearing, but their impact is constrained when access is urban-centred, biopsy pathways are slow, and most men still enter care late.

The most useful way to address the reality of the Nigerian screening and diagnostic landscape is therefore not as a binary of “available” versus “unavailable.” PSA exists. Biopsy exists. Histopathology exists. MRI exists in some centres. The more pressing question is whether these services are connected well enough to produce timely, stage-shifting diagnoses at scale. At present, the evidence suggests they are not. Contemporary treatment pathways in Nigeria

The most important feature of prostate cancer treatment in Nigeria is that stage at presentation largely determines what treatment is realistically delivered. Curative options are available in some tertiary centres, including radical prostatectomy, external-beam radiotherapy with linear accelerator or cobalt-60, brachytherapy, and androgen-deprivation therapy with GnRH agonists [16]. However, the same studies describing this capacity also emphasize that care is delivered largely through a fee-for-service model, with weak referral systems, long waiting times, and uneven access across regions. As a result, the treatment landscape is better understood not as an absence of modalities, but as a system in which potentially effective treatments are available to some patients, while many others arrive too late or progress too slowly through care to benefit from them.

Localised Disease

For clinically localized disease, the formal treatment menu in Nigeria broadly mirrors international practice: active surveillance for selected low-risk disease, radical prostatectomy, definitive radiotherapy, and, in selected settings, brachytherapy. Yet the key analytical issue is that availability does not translate into routine guideline-concordant care. In a 2025 multicentre Nigerian study of 110 men with clinically localized prostate cancer, only 18% had guideline-concordant staging imaging, 61% received any treatment or active surveillance, and just 15% received guideline-concordant definitive treatment with either radical prostatectomy or radical radiotherapy. Retention in care was 42%, and three-year all-cause survival was 41%. [17]. It also took an average of 68 days from time of diagnosis to initiation of first treatment for these men. These figures suggest that the bottleneck in localized disease is no longer simply whether a hospital can name a treatment option, but whether patients can move from diagnosis to timely, complete, and appropriate definitive care.

Radical prostatectomy is performed in Nigerian centres, and recent work from private and tertiary settings confirms that surgery remains a meaningful component of localized treatment. Even so, the published literature still reflects relatively small institutional experiences rather than broad national scale-up [16,18]. That pattern itself is revealing: surgery is feasible, but it remains concentrated in centres with specialist urologic capacity, operating theatre access, perioperative support, and patients able to finance care. In other words, the main limitation is less technical impossibility than restricted reach.

Radiotherapy occupies a similarly mixed position. It is indispensable for definitive treatment of localized disease and for palliation in advanced disease [19], yet access remains uneven. A recent international assessment reported that cancer services in Nigeria are still concentrated around larger cities and are not easily accessible for many patients outside major urban centres; Nigeria has therefore prioritized expansion of radiotherapy capacity, including brachytherapy [20]. This is important for prostate cancer because external-beam radiotherapy may be available on paper, but delays, machine shortages or downtime, and travel burdens can still convert a theoretically curative pathway into a practically inaccessible one.

Brachytherapy is one of the clearest signs of recent progress. At University College Hospital, Ibadan, high-dose-rate prostate brachytherapy became available, and a 2024 report described 73 patients treated between 2020 and 2023. Most had stage I or II disease (64.4%), 76.7% were high-risk, and 75.3% received androgen-deprivation therapy before brachytherapy [21]. This is an encouraging development because it shows that Nigeria is no longer limited to a purely conventional treatment model. At the same time, the profile of these patients also shows how selective access remains: they were treated at a pioneer centre, many had relatively early-stage disease, and a substantial proportion were detected through screening. Brachytherapy therefore represents real progress, but not yet a national solution.

The recent appearance of high-intensity focussed ultrasound (HIFU) in Nigeria deserves attention, but not exaggeration. Reports from late 2025 suggest that a Lagos centre has introduced prostate HIFU, marking a notable technological milestone [22]. HIFU is a minimally invasive treatment option for localized

prostate cancer that uses high-frequency sound waves to heat and destroy cancer cells while sparing surrounding healthy tissue [23]. It is designed for carefully selected localised disease, and current international guidance remains cautious: NICE considers efficacy evidence limited and recommends special governance and audit, while the EAU recommends focal HIFU only within prospective registries [24,25]. For Nigeria, this means that HIFU may expand options for a small subset of men with organ-confined or possibly locally recurrent non-metastatic disease, but it does not alter the broader treatment reality that most men still present with disease requiring systemic therapy and palliation rather than focal ablation.

Locally advanced and metastatic disease

Once disease is locally advanced or metastatic, treatment pathways in Nigeria shift decisively toward androgen-deprivation therapy (ADT). This is partly because many men present with advanced disease, and partly because ADT is more feasible than multimodal curative therapy in a resource-constrained setting. Nigerian hospital series continue to show the dominance of surgical castration in routine practice: bilateral total orchidectomy was reported in 59.8% of patients in one Jos series and 62.6% in a Lokoja cohort, while other Nigerian reports note that bilateral orchidectomy remains the commonest option despite increasing use of medical castration [26,27,28]. The significance of this pattern is not merely clinical but reflects the political economy of cancer care. In a system dominated by out-of-pocket payment, the cheapest durable therapy often becomes the default standard.

Medical castration and newer systemic options are available in some centres, but affordability remains a major limiting factor. The qualitative study of localized care in Lagos and Zaria identified low insurance coverage and the exclusion of much oncology-directed care from popular public insurance schemes as major barriers to treatment [16]. More recently, a 2025 survey focused on Nigeria reported that abiraterone and enzalutamide improve survival and quality of life for men with advanced prostate cancer, yet access remains restricted because patients often pay out of pocket and insurance coverage is limited [29]. The practical result is a treatment hierarchy in which modern agents exist, but many patients reach them late, intermittently, or not at all.

Chemotherapy is part of the treatment pathway for advanced and castration-resistant disease, but again the problem is selective access rather than formal absence. A Nigerian single-centre study of castration-resistant prostate cancer described poor access to active treatments and poor survival overall but also found that men who received docetaxel survived significantly longer than those who did not [30]. That is analytically important because it shows that therapeutic benefit is not fundamentally different in Nigerian patients; what differs is the probability of receiving treatment early enough, consistently enough, and for long enough to change outcomes.

The treatment reality can therefore be summarized as a stage-distorted pathway where men with localized disease often struggle to complete definitive care, while men with advanced disease are channelled toward ADT, symptom control, and selective use of chemotherapy or newer agents [16,17]. This is why prostate cancer treatment options in Nigeria must move beyond listing modalities. The more important question is whether the system can shift patients from late, predominantly palliative care toward earlier, coordinated, and affordable definitive treatment. Health system realities affecting management

The central limitation in prostate cancer management in Nigeria is no longer the simple absence of treatment options; it is the health-system environment in which those options must operate. Men move through a pathway marked by fragmented referral networks, uneven specialist distribution, recurrent infrastructure failures, and heavy reliance on direct payment. In that setting, even technically available interventions do not reliably translate into timely care. Nigeria's 2024 imPACT review described a system in which about 60% of healthcare services are provided privately and out-of-pocket spending accounts for more than three quarters (76%) of current health expenditure [10]. For prostate cancer, this financing structure has practical consequences. It delays diagnosis, narrows treatment choice, and increases the risk that men start care but do not complete it.

Workforce constraints remain a major structural weakness. A recent national analysis described Nigeria as having only about 70 clinical oncologists providing care for roughly 124,815 patients with cancer in a population of 213 million, while the same study warned that emigration is further shrinking the workforce [31]. The radiotherapy sector shows a similar deficit. The 2024 imPACT review reported 62 trained medical physicists against an estimated need for 150 and noted the need for additional staff alongside stronger retention strategies [10]. However, another study identified 31 medical physicists unevenly distributed in Nigeria's tertiary centres [32]. In practical terms, this means that specialist cancer care is not only scarce; it is also fragile, because service continuity depends on a small and overstretched workforce.

Radiotherapy capacity illustrates the wider problem of infrastructure without dependable throughput. The 2024 imPACT review reported 15 functional external-beam machines across eight states for a population of 223 million, with three centres that previously offered radiotherapy not functional at the time of the in-country mission [10]. An earlier nationwide assessment conducted in 2020–2021 found that only 4 of 9 surveyed radiotherapy centres had at least one functional piece of equipment, and that one-third of centres

were located in the southwest [32]. These are not abstract deficits. In northern Nigeria, the median interval from diagnosis to first radiation consultation was 40 days overall, but for prostate cancer it was 305 days, the longest among the cancers studied; the median interval from first radiation oncology consultation to first treatment was 130.5 days [33]. Such delays help explain why radiotherapy in Nigeria often functions as a bottleneck rather than a timely component of definitive treatment.

There are, however, signs that systems can improve when governance and maintenance are addressed directly. The same imPACT review noted that public-private partnership (PPP) arrangements in Enugu, Sokoto, Ebonyi, and Lagos had increased radiotherapy machine uptime to more than 95% and reduced treatment-initiation waiting times to less than one month in those sites [10]. The analytical lesson is important: Nigeria's problem is not simply low baseline capacity, but inconsistent operational reliability. Where service contracts, clear workflows, and maintenance systems are in place, performance improves. Where they are absent, breakdowns in electricity supply, lack of maintenance contracts, and equipment downtime continue to undermine care.

Diagnostic support services face similar urban concentration and affordability problems. According to the imPACT review, pathology services are available across secondary and tertiary facilities, but molecular biology and genetic marker testing are not readily available, often requiring samples to be sent abroad or to specialized private centres. Human resources for pathology are concentrated in urban areas, and low salaries, weak infrastructure, and migration continue to limit workforce availability [10]. For prostate cancer, this means that the pathway from biopsy to full characterization of disease remains uneven, especially outside major teaching hospitals. Access to imaging is also constrained. Nigeria's equipment density for CT and MRI remains below expected sub-Saharan benchmarks, reinforcing the gap between theoretical diagnostic capacity and what most patients can actually obtain.

Another underappreciated constraint is the weakness of multidisciplinary cancer care as routine practice. The imPACT review stated plainly that surgical oncology care in Nigeria is often not performed in a multidisciplinary setting [10]. A 2025 national survey of oncology professionals, although focused on breast cancer, gives a useful system-level indication of this problem: only 38% of respondents reported regular multidisciplinary tumour board discussions, and only 34% were aware of the resource-adapted harmonized guidelines endorsed by the Federal Ministry of Health [34]. This matters for prostate cancer because management increasingly depends on coordinated decision-making among urologists, radiation/clinical oncologists, radiologists, pathologists, and palliative-care teams. Where multidisciplinary structures are weak, treatment becomes more provider-dependent, less standardized, and more vulnerable to delay.

Cancer data systems are improving, but still not strong enough to fully guide practice and policy. The 2024 National Cancer Registry regulations established a National Cancer Registry of Nigeria with 26 population-based and hospital-based registries and made cancer a notifiable disease. Yet only five population-based registries currently contribute to Nigeria's GLOBOCAN estimates; none has contributed recently to Cancer Incidence in Five Continents, and the imPACT review highlighted weak funding and outdated census data as continuing barriers [10]. For prostate cancer management, this means that service planning still relies too heavily on partial surveillance, institutional case series, and modeled national estimates, rather than robust routine outcome data.

Palliative care deserves more attention in the Nigerian prostate cancer pathway than it often receives in policy discussion. This is because many men still present with advanced or metastatic disease, symptom control, psychosocial support, and end-of-life care, which are not peripheral services but central components of management. Nigeria launched a National Hospice and Palliative Care Policy in December 2021, but the imPACT review found that palliative-care coverage remains less than 1%, with limited insurance reimbursement, shortage of trained professionals, and weak community or home-based services [10]. For advanced prostate cancer, this means that men may receive hormonal treatment or palliative radiotherapy without adequate parallel support for pain, function, and family burden.

Overall, the treatment gap is therefore not only therapeutic, but also organizational. Curative and palliative modalities both exist, but their impact is filtered through workforce shortages, unstable infrastructure, weak multidisciplinary practice, and incomplete financial protection. Any serious update on prostate cancer management in Nigeria must therefore treat health-system reform not as a background issue, but as part of the treatment strategy itself.

Emerging progress and areas of improvement

An updated account of prostate cancer management in Nigeria should not read as if the system is static. There has been measurable institutional progress over the last few years, particularly in governance, service organization, cancer data systems, and selected treatment platforms. The more careful interpretation, however, is that progress has been selective rather than system-wide. Nigeria is building the architecture of modern cancer control, but the reach of that architecture remains uneven across states and across levels of care.

One of the clearest changes has been at the level of national coordination and policy. The 2024 WHO-IAEA imPACT review described cancer control governance as substantially stronger than in the past, citing the operationalization of the National Institute for Cancer Research and Treatment (NICRAT) in 2023 and the implementation of the National Strategic Cancer Control Plan (NSCCP) 2023–2027 [10]. The plan identifies eight strategic goals spanning prevention, diagnosis and treatment, supply chain management, palliative care, advocacy, data and research, governance and finance, and survivorship. That matters for prostate cancer because it shifts the discussion from isolated hospital responses to a more coherent national framework. The caveat is equally important: the same review noted that awareness and impact of the NSCCP remain underdeveloped at the state level, so the policy framework is ahead of implementation in many parts of the country.

A second area of progress is financial and institutional recognition of cancer care as a public responsibility, even if coverage remains incomplete. The imPACT review reported that NICRAT is implementing the National Cancer Health Fund, which supports chemotherapy and radiotherapy for patients with breast, cervical, and prostate cancers in six public comprehensive cancer centres [10]. For prostate cancer, this is important not because it has solved affordability, but because it signals a move away from the earlier assumption that men must finance nearly the whole pathway privately. The analytical significance is that Nigeria is beginning to create targeted public financing instruments for cancer, even though these have not yet translated into broad financial protection across the care continuum.

Progress is also visible in cancer surveillance and data governance. Under the 2024 National Cancer Registry Regulations, the National Cancer Registry of Nigeria was established with 26 population-based and hospital-based registries, and cancer is now a notifiable disease. Five population-based registries currently contribute data to Nigeria's GLOBOCAN estimates and participate in the African Cancer Registry Network [10]. This is a meaningful shift because better reporting and standardized data collection are prerequisites for more rational service planning, including for prostate cancer. Even so, the same review emphasized that registry funding remains weak and that no Nigerian registry has recently contributed to Cancer Incidence in Five Continents. The progress here is therefore foundational as data governance is being formalized, but registry maturity still lags behind policy ambition.

A more concrete service gain has occurred in radiotherapy operations. The imPACT review found that PPP arrangements in Enugu, Sokoto, Ebonyi, and Lagos improved machine uptime to more than 95% and reduced estimated waiting times for treatment initiation to less than one month in those centres [10]. This is one of the strongest examples in the current Nigerian cancer landscape of a systems intervention

producing visible operational benefit. For prostate cancer, it matters because radiotherapy is needed both for definitive treatment and for palliation. The wider lesson is that Nigeria's cancer-control problem is not only about buying equipment; it is also about keeping services functional through maintenance contracts, defined workflows, and managerial accountability.

There are also signs of progress in the specialized treatment repertoire available to Nigerian men with prostate cancer. High-dose-rate prostate brachytherapy, once unavailable in the country, is now being delivered at University College Hospital, Ibadan. A 2024 report described 73 patients treated between 2020 and 2023, showing that Nigeria has moved beyond a purely open-surgery-or-ADT model for localized disease [21]. A follow-up 2025 report from the same pioneer centre further indicates that local teams are now evaluating treatment-plan quality rather than merely establishing service availability [35]. That shift is noteworthy. It suggests movement from initial access toward questions of optimization and quality assurance, which is what mature oncology programmes eventually need to address. At the same time, because this experience remains concentrated in a pioneer centre, it should be understood as proof of feasibility rather than evidence of national scale.

Workforce development, while still insufficient, is another area where the trend is more positive than a purely deficit-based account would suggest. The imPACT review reported 74 practicing clinical oncologists, six training programmes, and 70 registrars in training as of 2024, alongside active efforts to assess national capacity-building needs in oncology, pathology, and palliative care [10]. The IAEA's January 2025 report similarly noted that Nigeria is increasing resource allocation and building its oncology workforce and services [20]. This does not mean the workforce gap is close to being solved. It does mean that workforce expansion is now a visible part of national strategy rather than an incidental by-product of individual institutions.

Another encouraging development is the shift toward decentralization and broader partnership-building. The IAEA reported that Nigeria is working to establish comprehensive cancer treatment and diagnostic facilities across all six geopolitical zones, while also aligning expansion efforts with the Rays of Hope initiative and other international partnerships [20]. A 2024 Milken Institute roundtable similarly framed cancer control in Nigeria around coordinated solutions involving government, academia, clinicians, civil society, and external partners [36]. Analytically, this matters because prostate cancer outcomes in Nigeria are heavily shaped by geography. Any great improvement in management will depend not only on improving flagship centres, but on reducing the present dependence on a small number of urban institutions.

The overall picture, then, is not one of simple stagnation. Nigeria has made progress in policy coherence, registry regulation, radiotherapy recovery, targeted financing, and specialized prostate cancer treatment capacity. Yet these gains are still best described as platform-building rather than full system transformation. They show that improvement is possible and, in some centres, already visible. What they do not yet show is that most Nigerian men with prostate cancer can consistently access timely, affordable, and stage-appropriate care regardless of where they live. That distinction is important because it keeps the commentary from swinging between pessimism and overstatement. The progress is real, but the scale problem remains. Emerging progress in prostate cancer management in Nigeria and what it means

Persistent challenges

The most persistent problem is that Nigeria has not yet achieved a meaningful stage shift at diagnosis. Clinical series still show that many men enter care with symptomatic, biologically aggressive, or already

advanced disease rather than with screen-detected localized cancer. In one south-eastern Nigerian series, only 1.8% of cases were screen-detected, 72.0% of men had symptoms for more than 3 months before presentation, and 62.2% had poorly differentiated tumours with Gleason score ≥ 7 (4+3) [11]. That local pattern mirrors the broader concern raised by the 2024 Lancet Commission: in low- and middle-income countries, more than half of men are diagnosed with advanced prostate cancer [2]. The practical implication is that improvements in treatment capacity will have limited population impact unless the diagnostic pathway begins to intercept disease earlier than it currently does.

A second challenge is the implementation gap between policy ambition and everyday practice. Nigeria now has a national cancer-control plan and an explicit policy interest in expanding screening, but the underlying early-detection system remains thin. The 2024 WHO-IAEA imPACT review states that national programmes for breast, cervical, prostate, and colorectal cancer screening have not yet been fully established [10]. Qualitative work from Nigeria adds an important explanation for why this gap persists: barriers are not only institutional but also cognitive and social, including limited knowledge, misperceptions among patients and non-specialist providers, and dependence on centralized opportunistic screening rather than a reliable, decentralized pathway [16]. In Abeokuta, embarrassment, fear of pain, low perceived susceptibility, and lack of physician recommendation were all linked to delayed diagnosis [7]. In other words, Nigeria's problem is not just insufficient screening volume; it is an unstable chain from risk recognition to referral to diagnostic confirmation.

A third persistent barrier is financial toxicity, which continues to shape who gets diagnosed, what treatment is chosen, and whether care is completed. The imPACT review reports that out-of-pocket spending still accounts for more than 76% of current health expenditure in Nigeria [10]. Although NICRAT's National Cancer Health Fund now supports chemotherapy and radiotherapy for prostate cancer in six public centres, the same review notes that the Basic Health Care Provision Fund currently includes only self-breast examination as its cancer-care component. For prostate cancer, this means that public commitment has improved, but financial protection across the full pathway, i.e., from PSA testing and biopsy to imaging, surgery, radiotherapy, systemic treatment, and palliation, remains incomplete. This helps explain why less expensive and more immediately accessible options, especially surgical castration, still dominate treatment for advanced disease even when more contemporary therapies exist.

The fourth challenge is capacity without throughput. Nigeria now has more cancer infrastructure than it did a decade ago, but service volume and timeliness still lag far behind need. The 2024 imPACT review documented 15 functional external-beam machines across eight states for a population of 223 million, against an estimated requirement of more than 120 machines [10]. It also reported 74 practicing clinical oncologists and 62 trained medical physicists, below the estimated workforce need. These shortages translate directly into delays. In northern Nigeria, prostate cancer had the longest median interval from diagnosis to first radiation consultation of about 305 days [33]. Even among men with clinically localized disease, the 2025 Nigerian quality-of-care study reported median times from diagnosis to first treatment of 104 days for radical prostatectomy and 117 days for radical radiotherapy [17]. These figures show that the major problem is no longer only whether a modality exists, but whether it can be delivered quickly enough to preserve curative intent.

A fifth challenge is that quality and continuity of care remain inconsistent even after diagnosis is made. The 2025 multicentre Nigerian study of clinically localized prostate cancer is particularly revealing here: only 18% of patients underwent guideline-concordant staging imaging, 61% received any treatment or active surveillance, only 15% received guideline-concordant definitive treatment, retention in care was 42%, and three-year all-cause survival was 41% [17]. These are not merely technical deficits; they suggest a broader systems problem in which attrition occurs between diagnosis, staging, treatment selection, and treatment completion. In that sense, the persistent challenge is not only under-capacity but also weak care

coordination. A health system can possess surgery, radiotherapy, and hormonal therapy yet still produce poor outcomes if patients move through it slowly, unevenly, or incompletely.

Finally, there is a lingering evidence and accountability gap. Nigeria's cancer registry architecture is improving, and cancer is now a notifiable disease, but only five population-based registries currently contribute to GLOBOCAN estimates, and none has recently contributed to Cancer Incidence in Five Continents. Funding for registry operations remains weak. The consequence is that prostate cancer policy is still guided too often by modeled estimates, isolated hospital series, and short-term programme reports rather than robust national outcome tracking. That matters because persistent challenges are harder to correct when they are not measured consistently. Without better survival, stage-distribution, and treatment-completion data, it becomes difficult to distinguish between local success stories and true population-level improvement. Priority actions for Nigeria

Taken the evidence reviewed so far seriously, the priority for Nigeria is not simply to add more prostate cancer services, but to re-engineer the pathway through which men move from risk recognition to diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up. The central failure of the current system is attrition, explained by men entering late, waiting too long, or do not complete appropriate care. Priority actions should therefore focus less on isolated interventions and more on removing the specific points where patients are lost.

Shift from opportunistic screening to risk-adapted early diagnosis

Nigeria needs a clearer early-detection strategy for prostate cancer, but not a simple importation of indiscriminate mass PSA screening. The 2024 Lancet Commission argues that, in low- and middle-income countries, the more realistic priority is to improve awareness, diagnostic access, and targeted case-finding among higher-risk men, rather than rely on broad screening programmes modeled on high-income settings [2]. That position is consistent with the Nigerian context, where current detection remains opportunistic, poorly integrated into primary care, and heavily dependent on outreach campaigns or urban specialist access. A more effective approach would be to develop national risk-adapted early-diagnosis guidance for men at higher risk, especially older men, those with suggestive symptoms, and those with relevant family history, while also giving clear advice against unstructured routine PSA testing with no referral or follow-up pathway behind it.

Build a functional referral-and-diagnosis pathway, not just more testing

The diagnostic weakness in Nigeria is not only low uptake of PSA; it is the absence of a dependable system that links abnormal findings to biopsy, histopathology, staging, and treatment planning within a clinically useful time frame. The imPACT review explicitly recommends stronger early-diagnosis initiatives, community engagement, and well-defined referral pathways [10]. For prostate cancer, that means creating standardized referral rules between primary care, general hospitals, and tertiary urology or oncology services; shortening biopsy and pathology turnaround times; and protecting access to essential imaging in regional hubs. Without those changes, more awareness or more PSA testing will mainly generate anxiety and delay rather than earlier cure.

Protect curative treatment pathways for localized disease

One of the most striking recent findings from Nigeria is that men with clinically localized prostate cancer often do not receive timely, guideline-concordant definitive care. In the 2025 multicentre study, only 15% received guideline-concordant definitive treatment, and retention in care was just 42% [37]. That points to a clear policy priority. Localized disease should be treated as a time-sensitive opportunity for cure, not as a diagnosis that can wait indefinitely while financing, staging, or scheduling problems accumulate. In practical terms, tertiary centres should begin tracking simple quality indicators such as time from diagnosis to staging, time from diagnosis to definitive treatment, treatment completion, and loss to follow-up. These measures are inexpensive to collect and would make it easier to identify where curative pathways are breaking down.

Expand financing in ways that change treatment choices

Financial reform is essential because cost is not a background inconvenience in Nigeria, but rather one of the main forces shaping treatment itself. Out-of-pocket spending remains extremely high, and recent Nigerian survey data show that most clinicians believe fewer than one in five patients can afford abiraterone, while affordability of enzalutamide is even worse. The National Cancer Health Fund is an important start, but its reach remains too narrow to transform the full pathway [10]. Nigeria therefore needs to move from selective support for treatment episodes toward broader financial protection across diagnosis, imaging, surgery, radiotherapy, systemic therapy, and palliative care. In parallel, the 2025 Nigerian survey on advanced prostate cancer suggests that cost-saving approaches such as evidence-based dose optimization for selected medicines deserve serious local discussion, because affordability is now directly linked to adherence and continuity of care [29].

Decentralize service access while standardizing quality

Cancer services in Nigeria remain too geographically concentrated for a disease as common as prostate cancer. National efforts to establish comprehensive cancer treatment and diagnostic facilities across the six geopolitical zones are therefore well directed. But decentralization should not mean simple duplication of incomplete services. What is needed is a hub-and-spoke model in which regional centres provide biopsy,

pathology, imaging, surgery, radiotherapy, and medical oncology links under shared protocols, while lower-level facilities handle awareness, initial assessment, follow-up, and supportive care. The evidence from PPP radiotherapy sites is especially useful here: where operational systems are strong, machine uptime and waiting times improve markedly [20]. Nigeria should apply that lesson more broadly by linking decentralization to maintenance contracts, workforce planning, referral standards, and minimum service benchmarks.

Make multidisciplinary care routine rather than exceptional

Modern prostate cancer management increasingly depends on coordination between urologists, medical and radiation oncologists, medical physicists, radiologists, pathologists, palliative-care teams, and specialist nurses. Yet the imPACT review suggests that multidisciplinary practice remains inconsistent in Nigeria [10]. That is not a soft organizational issue; it directly affects staging quality, treatment selection, sequencing of therapy, and management of complications. A realistic national priority would be to require regular multidisciplinary tumour board review for newly diagnosed high-risk, locally advanced, metastatic, and recurrent prostate cancer at tertiary and regional referral centres, including virtual participation where workforce is thin. This would do more than improve decision-making. It would also reduce provider-to-provider variation and make prostate cancer care less dependent on where a man happens to present.

Treat palliative care and outcome measurement as core parts of management

Because many Nigerian men still present with advanced disease, palliative care should not be treated as an afterthought. Earlier symptom control, psychosocial support, and community-based follow-up are central to quality of care in advanced prostate cancer, especially where cure is no longer possible. At the same time, Nigeria needs better measurement of what its system is actually achieving. The new cancer registry framework is a major step, but registry funding and data completeness remain weak. For prostate cancer, the next phase should include routine reporting of stage at diagnosis, treatment initiation intervals, definitive treatment rates, metastatic presentations, and short-term survival across major centres. Without these outcome measures, policy will continue to be driven more by institutional anecdotes than by system performance.

Conclusion

Prostate cancer remains the most diagnosed cancer in Nigerian men and the leading cause of male cancer death in the country, making it both a clinical and health-system priority. Yet the central message of this update is not simply that the burden is high. It is that outcomes in Nigeria are still shaped by a familiar sequence of late presentation, weak early-diagnosis pathways, limited financial protection, and uneven access to specialist treatment.

There are real signs of progress. Nigeria now has a stronger national cancer-control framework, a more formal registry structure, expanding radiotherapy and brachytherapy capacity, and growing institutional support through NICRAT and related initiatives. But these gains have not yet produced a consistent population-level shift toward earlier diagnosis and timely, guideline-concordant care.

The next phase of prostate cancer management in Nigeria should therefore focus less on isolated technological advances and more on making the care pathway work end to end. This entails early risk-adapted diagnosis, faster referral and pathology turnaround, better protection of curative treatment opportunities, broader financial coverage, stronger multidisciplinary care, and more reliable outcome measurement. That is where the largest gains in survival and quality of life are now likely to be made.
