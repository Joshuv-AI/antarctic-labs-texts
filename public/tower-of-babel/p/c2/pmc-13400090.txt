Left Atrial Appendage Occlusion Device–Associated Infective Endocarditis: Analysis of the FDA-MAUDE Database
Authors: Anthony Mufarreh, Vivek Bhat, Adeeb Al-Quthami, Cynthia C Taub, Jorge E Romero, Ankur Kalra
Published: 2026

Introduction

Percutaneous left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) has become an established strategy for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation, particularly among patients at increased risk of bleeding.1 These devices have demonstrated favorable safety profiles, with most reported adverse events restricted to the periprocedural period, including pericardial effusion, stroke, device embolization, and device-related thrombosis.2

Infective endocarditis (IE) involving intracardiac devices is a feared complication. While their procedural characteristics differ significantly from LAAO, IE has been reported in 1% to 3% of non-LAAO cardiac implantable devices.3 Descriptions of LAAO-related IE, however, are limited almost exclusively to case reports,4 limiting understanding of characteristics and outcomes. Postmarketing surveillance databases have the potential to capture rare or late-occurring adverse events that are not well represented in published literature. We aimed to describe reports of LAAO-associated IE within the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience (MAUDE) database.5

Methods

The MAUDE database is a passive postmarketing surveillance system that includes mandatory reports of serious adverse events from manufacturers, importers, and device user facilities, as well as voluntary reports from health care professionals, patients, and consumers.

We queried the MAUDE database for reports submitted between January 2015 and October 2025 using FDA product code NGV (“System, Appendage Closure, Left Atria”). Reports were screened further using the keyword “endocarditis.” All identified reports were reviewed manually to exclude duplicate entries and non-IE events. Reports describing multiple distinct LAAO-related IE events were treated as separate entries.

We extracted time to event for included events, representing the duration (in days) between device deployment and reported diagnosis of LAAO-associated IE. Events were classified according to their MAUDE reports as either injury or death. Descriptive analyses were performed with stratification by device type using RStudio (Posit Software, PBC). Institutional review board approval was not required as this is an analysis of publicly available anonymized data.

Results

A total of 78 reports were extracted, of which 16 were excluded, including 8 duplicates, 3 reports of pericardial effusion, and 5 reports of device/prosthetic valve-related thrombus without evidence of infection. After separation of reports into individual events, we included 107 reported events of LAAO-associated IE.

Watchman FLX (Boston Scientific) had the most reported events (n = 85), followed by Watchman 2.5 (Boston Scientific) (n = 10), Amulet (Abbott Laboratories) and Watchman FLX Pro (Boston Scientific) (n = 6 each). Age was reported for 52 events, ranging from 45 to 89 years, and sex was reported for 62 events, of which 20 (32.3%) involved women.

Time-to-event data were available for 92 events, ranging from 12 to 2252 days. Overall median (IQR) time to event was 181 (116-291) days. This varied across device types, being shortest for Watchman FLX-PRO at 63 (30-139) days. Overall, 20 (18.7%) events were classified as deaths (Table 1).

Discussion

In our analysis of the FDA-MAUDE database, we identified a substantially larger number of LAAO-associated IE events than has been described in published literature to date. Our study also provides additional insight into characteristics of this complication beyond isolated case reports.

Left atrial appendage occlusion–related IE is thought to arise from the formation of thrombotic material on the device surface, followed by colonization during transient bacteremia.4 We found that IE was reported across a broad temporal window, with a median time to event of 6 months. This pattern of delayed presentation is consistent with prior case reports, most of which described LAAO-related IE occurring several months after implantation. In a contemporary analysis of the National Cardiovascular Data Registry LAAO Registry, infection was not reported as an in-hospital adverse event.6 Taken together, this highlights the potential for delayed presentation of LAAO-related IE, extending well beyond the peri-procedural period. Time to event varied across devices, although this was likely influenced by reporting practices. A significant proportion of events were classified as death, highlighting the potentially fatal nature of LAAO-related IE. Overall, these findings support continued clinical vigilance and systematic follow-up following LAAO. While current guidelines offer conditional recommendations for postprocedural imaging for detection of peridevice leaks and device-related thrombi,1 the role of longer-term surveillance with imaging may merit further consideration.

Our study has limitations. The MAUDE database is subject to substantial underreporting, incomplete clinical detail, and ascertainment bias.7,8 Most reports are submitted by manufacturers, introducing variability in reporting practices. Reports typically are submitted as unstructured free text and often lack key details, including demographic information. Mortality classification reflects report designation, rather than adjudicated outcomes.7 Given that they are derived from retrospective reporting that may be delayed and lacks consistent documentation,8 time-to-event estimates warrant cautious interpretation. The database lacks information required to estimate incidence, perform comparative analyses, or establish causality. Identified events could represent IE in high-risk populations, rather than true LAAO-associated infection. Therefore, our findings remain descriptive.

Our study highlights a significant number of LAAO-associated IE in the US, occurring across a wide time frame, and associated with potentially poor outcomes. Large prospective registry-based studies are needed to elucidate the long-term incidence, risk factors, and outcomes of LAAO-associated IE.
