Managing Vitality, Sustainable Performance and Fatigue in Nursing Home Staff: A Multi‐Location Cross‐Sectional Survey Study: Empirical Research Quantitative
Authors: Jan de Jonge, Ramona Backhaus, Jan P. H. Hamers
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study examines the moderating role of specific job resources in the association between job demands, vigour, sustainable performance and fatigue in nursing home staff.DesignA multi‐location cross‐sectional survey study in line with the STROBE guidelines.MethodsOnline self‐completion questionnaires were distributed in the Summer of 2022. A total of 667 healthcare employees from 24 nursing home locations in the Netherlands participated in this study. Data were analysed using hierarchical multiple regression analysis.ResultsJob resources were able to moderate the relation between job demands on the one hand, and vigour, sustainable performance and fatigue on the other. Regression analyses revealed that high cognitive job resources moderated (i.e., strengthened) the positive association between cognitive job demands and successively cognitive liveliness, physical strength and sustainable performance. Furthermore, high emotional job resources moderated (i.e., buffered) the positive relation between emotional job demands and emotional and cognitive fatigue, respectively.ConclusionsIt can be concluded that job resources are crucial for nursing home staff to deal with their high job demands. Maintaining a healthy balance between job demands and sufficient, matching, job resources to promote vigour and sustainable performance, as well as to prevent fatigue of staff, seems to be important.Implications for the ProfessionThere is an urgent need to train nursing home staff to cope better with increasing job demands. Because job demands cannot be reduced in many situations, this study revealed that the idea of boosting job resources instead is appealing to both staff and managers.Patient or Public ContributionDutch healthcare staff from 24 nursing home locations were involved in this study by responding to the online survey.

Introduction

Societal and demographic developments have influenced working conditions in nursing home care to a large extent (cf. Korbus et al. 2023). Examples of such developments include a progressive ageing of the population (e.g., Otto et al. 2021), the COVID‐19 pandemic (e.g., Hoedl, Thonhofer, and Schoberer 2022; Van Dijk et al. 2022), and labour and budget shortages in nursing home care (e.g., Otto et al. 2021). Throughout the European Union, there are strong indications of an increase in healthcare staff leaving the profession (cf. European Labour Authority 2022). At the same time, there is an increase of care complexity in nursing homes due to ageing in‐place policies in most Western countries. As a consequence, nursing home staff is increasingly affected by work stress due to high job demands, which can be described as work‐related tasks that require instant or enduring effort (cf. Van Dijk et al. 2022; Korbus et al. 2023). So, job demands encompass a wide range of factors that staff must handle as part of their daily work routines. Due to these high job demands, staff is at high risk for burnout, physical complaints, poor performance and sickness absenteeism (e.g., Van Dijk et al. 2022; Korbus et al. 2023). For example, a recent empirical study showed that depressive symptoms and burnout complaints have increased among nursing home staff during the COVID‐19 pandemic, which was associated with high sickness absenteeism (Van Dijk et al. 2022). These adverse health problems could affect the quality of care and organisational outcomes, such as client safety, organisational commitment and work performance.

Because it is rather difficult to reduce job demands of nursing home staff, research has searched for means to allow staff to withstand high job demands in order to avoid poor health and to promote work performance. It is therefore attractive to tackle the adverse effects of job demands with means such as job resources (cf. De Jonge, Demerouti, and Dormann 2024). Job resources can be described as energy reservoirs at work that employees could use to regulate demanding aspects at work (Van den Tooren and De Jonge 2010). In fact, they are all means in the work environment that can help staff to approach a desired state or to avoid an undesirable state. Examples of job resources are workplace social support, job control and technical devices. Empirical research has shown that job resources can moderate the association between (1) job demands and (2) health, well‐being or performance outcomes (cf. De Jonge, Demerouti, and Dormann 2024). For instance, a cross‐sectional survey study among more than 400 nursing home employees found a buffering effect of job control in the association between job demands and the burnout‐component ‘emotional exhaustion’. This finding was cross‐validated in a comparable sample (Van Vegchel, De Jonge, and Landsbergis 2005). In other words, occupational burnout expressed in ‘emotional exhaustion’ seems to be an adverse health reaction to high job demands that will be reduced in the case energy reservoirs at work such as job control are largely present.

Background

The important consequences of job demands and the compensating role of job resources to promote health and performance in nursing home staff should be studied in more depth. A work stress model that explains the precise role of job resources in the relation between job demands and employee outcomes, is the Demand‐Induced Strain Compensation (DISC) Model developed by De Jonge and Dormann (2003, 2006). This model proposes that compensating effects of job resources are dependent on the issue of ‘match’ or ‘fit’ between distinct types of job demands and job resources. The model is especially constructed for human service occupations such as healthcare, retail and education staff.

In more detail, the DISC Model entails two core principles; that is, the principle of multidimensionality and the triple‐match principle (cf. De Jonge and Dormann 2006). The principle of multidimensionality proposes that work‐related demands and resources basically consist of cognitive, emotional and/or physical components. The same is proposed for health, well‐being and performance outcomes. More specifically, job demands could be either cognitive (e.g., making complex decisions), emotional (e.g., emotion‐handling due to an aggressive client) or physical (e.g., having to lift a client). In a similar vein, job resources can be primarily cognitive (e.g., information from the internet, job control), emotional (e.g., emotional guidance from a supervisor) or physical (e.g., ergonomic aids). Finally, the model distinguishes health, well‐being and performance outcomes of cognitive, emotional and/or physical kind, which can be negative or positive. Examples are employee creativity, sustainable work performance, emotional exhaustion and physical health complaints.

The triple‐match principle claims that compensating effects of job resources will largely take place if particular kinds of job resources are matched to particular kinds of job demands. Or, to put it differently, when employees are faced with a particular demand at work, they will primarily opt for using a corresponding type of job resource. For instance, a nurse who has to lift a heavy client would have more profit from a bed lift than from a colleague who offers a listening ear. Finally, the triple‐match principle proposes also that job demands and job resources should match health, well‐being and performance outcomes. For instance, a complex task may induce employee creativity in the case of sufficient job control.

Two subprinciples of the DISC Model are (1) the compensation mechanism and (2) the balance mechanism. The first mechanism proposes that poor health effects of high job demands can be tackled if employees have ample job resources to deal with these demands. For instance, an emotionally demanding task may result in less emotional exhaustion in the case of high emotional job resources. The second mechanism proposes that high job demands engage positive employee outcomes in the case employees have abundant matching job resources. In the case employees possess relatively high job resources, they can investigate several ways of dealing with job demands, which could lead to positive employee outcomes such as higher vigour and work performance. For example, a cognitively challenging task may stimulate cognitive vigour and work performance only in the case of high cognitive job resources.

Generally, the DISC Model proposes that employees will first handle their job demands using available, accessible and relevant matching job resources. In the case these matching job resources are not available anymore, employees will search for other job resources that are less fitting. For example, when emotional problems with clients arise, the presence of emotional support from a close colleague is likely to be of help. If such a close colleague is not available, other types of job resources can be helpful, for example, job control to deal with the awkward client. In the case those less matching resources are also not present anymore, employees will finally apply resources at work that do not match at all to the emotionally demanding task (e.g., ignoring the awkward client and leaving accordingly). So, in addition to triple‐matches, the DISC Model distinguishes two types of so‐called double‐matches which are less strong in terms of fit. In that case, two out of three constructs fit. Double‐matches are assumed to take place less often than triple‐matches. For instance, cross‐sectional survey research among more than 800 healthcare employees (De Jonge et al. 2008) showed a double‐match between high emotional demands and high emotional resources predicting worker's creativity as a cognitive outcome. This type of match is called a ‘double‐match of common kind’ (DMc). In an identical fashion, a double‐match between job demands and employee outcomes might exist when job resources are different in nature. Or, alternatively, a double‐match between job resources and employee outcomes might exist when job demands are different in nature. For example, the relation between emotional demands and emotional fatigue is moderated by physical resources. This kind of double‐match is called a ‘double‐match of extended kind’ (DMe). Finally, a non‐match (NM) between demands, resources and outcomes implies that the compensating role of job resources is least likely to take place. For example, when job demands, job resources and employee outcomes are all different in nature). Two review studies among different occupations from several countries concluded that the triple‐match principle received empirical support, and revealed that the assumed triple‐matches were more often detected than double‐matches or even non‐matches (Van den Tooren, De Jonge, and Dormann 2011; Van de Ven 2011). To conclude, the triple‐match principle assumes that the chance of detecting compensating (i.e., moderating) effects of job resources increases in the case of a closer match between job demands, job resources and employee health, well‐being and performance outcomes.

The Study

Aim and Hypotheses

The present study conducts an investigation into the compensating role of specific job resources in the association between specific job demands, employee health and performance in nursing home care. The triple‐match principle will be tested using different employee outcomes: vigour, sustainable performance and fatigue. Firstly, employee vigour refers to one's affective experience of being energetic physically, mentally and interpersonally (Shirom 2011). Vigour is a multidimensional construct that comprises feelings of physical strength, cognitive liveliness and emotional energy. Secondly, employee sustainable performance has been defined as a sustainable process of self‐regulatory behaviour in which an employee tries to reach desirable, longer‐term, work goals together with a decent level of well‐being (Ji et al. 2021). This process is merely cognitive in nature, and consists of a steady regeneration and safeguarding of job resources in the longer run. Lastly, employee fatigue is defined as continuing tiredness caused by work that could be expressed in different dimensions such as physical, emotional and cognitive exhaustion (Stein et al. 2004). In line with previous DISC studies (e.g., De Jonge, Peeters, and Taris 2019; De Jonge and Huter 2021), the following hypotheses are formulated accordingly:Hypothesis 1 Positive associations between job demands and employee vigour will be moderated by matching job resources, in a way that these associations will be stronger for nursing home staff with high job resources than for staff with low job resources. More explicitly, high cognitive job resources will strengthen the positive association between cognitive job demands and cognitive liveliness (Hypothesis 1a); high emotional job resources will strengthen the positive association between emotional job demands and emotional energy (Hypothesis 1b); and high physical job resources will strengthen the positive association between physical job demands and physical strength (Hypothesis 1c). Hypothesis 2 Positive relations between job demands and employee sustainable performance will be moderated by matching job resources, such that these relations will be stronger for staff with high job resources than for staff with low job resources. More directly, high cognitive job resources will strengthen the positive association between cognitive job demands and employee sustainable performance. Hypothesis 3 Positive associations between job demands and employee fatigue will be moderated by matching job resources, such that these associations will be weaker for nursing home staff with high job resources than for staff with low job resources. More explicitly, high cognitive job resources will buffer the positive association between cognitive job demands and cognitive fatigue (Hypothesis 3a); high emotional job resources will buffer the positive association between emotional job demands and emotional fatigue (Hypothesis 3b); and high physical job resources will buffer the positive association between physical job demands and physical fatigue (Hypothesis 3c).

Methods

Design and Procedure

This research used a cross‐sectional design with the Qualtrics platform embracing an online survey questionnaire with closed, structured answers. Primary data were collected in the Summer of 2022.

Sample

Healthcare staff (i.e., registered nurses, nurse assistants, housekeeping, nutritional assistants and activity supervisors) employed in 24 nursing home locations in the Netherlands filled out the survey questionnaire. Informed consent was provided before participants entered the study. The final sample consisted of 667 respondents with completely filled out questionnaires. Distribution of participants among the 24 locations was in line with location size. As we do not know the total number of staff employed, the response rate could not be calculated.

Instruments

The entire survey questionnaire contained a battery of four well‐validated instruments, addressing both the predictor measures (job demands and job resources) and the outcome measures (vigour, sustainable performance and fatigue).

Job Demands and Job Resources

The predictor measures ‘job demands’ and ‘job resources’ were assessed with a short version of the DISC Questionnaire (DISQ‐S 3.1; De Jonge et al. 2018) that was specifically developed and validated for testing the DISC Model. The DISQ‐S consists of three scales for cognitive, emotional and physical demands and resources, respectively. The scales consist of three items each (except for cognitive resources that consists of four items), and were scored on a 5‐point frequency scale ranging from (1) ‘never or very rarely’ to (5) ‘very often or always’. As far as job demands are concerned, example items are ‘I need to display high levels of concentration and precision at work’ (cognitive demands; Cronbach's alpha in the current study is 0.81), ‘I have to do a lot of emotionally draining work’ (emotional demands; Cronbach's alpha = 0.75) and ‘I have to perform a lot of physically strenuous tasks to carry out my job’ (physical demands; Cronbach's alpha = 0.86). Regarding job resources, example items are ‘I have the opportunity to determine my own work method’ (cognitive resources; Cronbach's alpha = 0.69), ‘In my job, I get emotional support from others (e.g., colleagues or supervisors) when a threatening situation at work occurs’ (emotional resources; Cronbach's alpha = 0.91) and ‘I am able to use adequate technical equipment to accomplish physically strenuous tasks’ (physical resources; Cronbach's alpha = 0.71).

Employee Vigour

The Shirom–Melamed Vigor Measure (S‐MVM; Shirom 2011) was applied to measure employees' vigour. The S‐MVM entails three different dimensions of vigour; that is, cognitive liveliness (three items), emotional energy (four items) and physical strength (five items). Staff had to indicate how often they expressed feelings of vigour during a 1‐month period. All twelve items were rated on a 7‐point Likert scale, ranging from (1) ‘almost never’ to (7) ‘almost always’. For example, ‘I feel able to be creative’ (cognitive liveliness; Cronbach's alpha = 0.85); ‘I feel capable of investing emotionally in colleagues or clients’ (emotional energy; Cronbach's alpha = 0.93); ‘I feel energetic’ (physical energy; Cronbach alpha = 0.96).

Employee Sustainable Performance

Employee sustainable performance was measured with a 10‐item instrument called E‐SuPer which was empirically justified by Ji et al. (2021). As sustainable work performance is about work performance in the longer run (i.e., during an employee's entire career), this notion was integrated into this instrument. Example items are ‘I expect to be able to continuously achieve the objectives of my job’ and ‘I expect to be permanently competent in all areas of my job’ with a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from (1) ‘strongly disagree’ to (5) ‘strongly agree’. Cronbach's alpha of this scale was 0.93.

Employee Fatigue

The Multidimensional Fatigue Symptom Inventory–Short Form (MFSI‐SF) was chosen to assess employees' fatigue (Stein et al. 2004). The MFSI‐SF reflects cognitive, emotional and physical fatigue consisting of six items per dimension (18 items in total). Employees had to indicate whether or not they agree with the statements during a 1‐month period. All 18 items were rated on a 5‐point Likert scale that ranged from (1) ‘not at all’ to (5) ‘extremely’. For instance, ‘I have trouble paying attention’ (cognitive fatigue; Cronbach's alpha = 0.88); ‘I feel tense’ (emotional fatigue; Cronbach's alpha = 0.92); ‘My legs feel weak’ (physical fatigue; Cronbach's alpha = 0.90).

Demographic Characteristics

We included several demographic characteristics such as gender (indicated as 0 = female and 1 = male), age (years), education level (ranging from 1 = basic level to 6 = highly advanced level) and working hours per week. They acted as confounders as they have proven to be important in other research among healthcare employees (e.g., De Jonge, Gevers, and Dollard 2014), in previous DISC studies (e.g., De Jonge, Peeters, and Taris 2019) as well as in the current research (see Tables 1 and 2).

Data Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS for Windows, version 29. First, zero‐order Pearson correlations were calculated, next to the means and standard deviations. Second, we performed series of hierarchical multiple regression analyses (HMRAs) to investigate the assumed relations between job demands and job resources on the one hand, and vigour, sustainable performance and fatigue on the other. Initial screening revealed no significant violations of linear regression assumptions. Two hierarchical regression models were tested: demographic characteristics as well as standardised main terms of demands and resources were entered in the first model. In the second model, moderating effects of job resources were examined by adding interaction terms of standardised demands and resources into the analyses (i.e., demands × resources; Dawson and Richter 2006). Two different series of HMRAs (14 in total) were performed as the number of possible interaction effects in one single analysis would be too large. In line with the recommendations of De Jonge and Dormann (2006), we break apart the regression analyses into (1) matching interaction testing and (2) less−/non‐matching interaction testing. This implies that the first series of seven HMRAs tested seven triple‐matches and 14 double‐matches of common kind for all seven outcome measures. The second series of seven HMRAs tested 28 double‐matches of extended kind and 14 non‐matches for all seven outcome measures. Following the suggestions of Dawson and Richter (2006), moderating effects of job resources were graphically represented using a MS Excel template. Moreover, slope significance tests of the simple (subgroup) regression lines were calculated.

Ethical Considerations

Our research was carried out in line with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki (WMA) and the American Psychological Association (APA). Ethical approval for this study was acquired from the ethics committee of Zuyderland Medical Center and Zuyd University of Applied Sciences, the Netherlands (no. METCZ 20220028).

Results

Sample Characteristics

The majority of the employees was female (90.0%). Healthcare staff's mean age was almost 50 years (M = 49.5, SD = 11.4), and their age ranged from 17 to 67 years. Median education of the sample was secondary vocational education. Mean working hours per week were 25.9 h (SD = 6.6). Finally, 70.3% of the staff was involved in shiftwork.

Preliminary Analyses: Means and Zero‐Order Correlations

Mean scores for job demands (Table 1) reveal a relatively high score for cognitive demands (M = 3.85, SD = 0.77), an average score for physical demands (M = 3.22, SD = 1.02), and a relatively low score for emotional demands (M = 2.62, SD = 0.78; cf. Bova, De Jonge and Guglielmi 2015). As far as job resources are concerned, mean score for emotional resources was the highest (M = 3.66, SD = 1.00), mean score for physical resources was average (M = 3.40, SD = 0.87) and mean score for cognitive resources was the lowest (M = 3.05, SD = 0.73; cf. Bova, De Jonge and Guglielmi 2015). Mean scores for employee vigour were relatively high (cf. Shirom 2011), ranging from M = 4.71 (SD = 0.96) to M = 5.74 (SD = 0.90). Mean score for employee sustainable performance was average (M = 3.78, SD = 0.61; cf. Ji et al. 2021). Furthermore, mean scores for fatigue were relatively low (cf. Stein et al. 2004), ranging from M = 1.51 (SD = 0.63) to M = 1.89 (SD = 0.88). Finally, the Pearson zero‐order correlations in Table 1 show that both job demands and job resources were statistically significantly related to the outcome measures as initially assumed. For instance, cognitive demands and resources were positively associated with both cognitive liveliness and employee sustainable performance.

Predictors of Vigour and Sustainable Performance

We tested three job demands, three job resources and the first set of matching interactions as predictors of vigour and sustainable performance, controlling for demographic characteristics.

Table 2 shows the corresponding HMRAs for the tests of triple‐matches and double‐matches of common kind interactions between demands and resources. With regard to employees' vigour, two interaction models (M2) and one main effect model (M1) were statistically significant. The first interaction model concerns a triple‐match for cognitive liveliness, which is graphically shown in an interaction graph (Figure 1A). To be more specific, a statistically significant interaction of cognitive demands and cognitive resources predicting the vigour‐scale ‘cognitive liveliness’ was detected (b = 0.09, p = 0.008). Looking into the two simple slope tests revealed a significant slope for high cognitive resources, but not for low cognitive resources. The triple‐match interaction graph indicates that an increase in cognitive demands was associated with more cognitive liveliness when cognitive resources were high (+1 SD; t(666) = 5.11, p = 0.0001). In contrast, there was no statistically significant relation between cognitive demands and cognitive liveliness when cognitive resources were low (−1 SD; t(666) = 1.83, p = 0.068).

The second interaction model (M2) concerns a double‐match of common kind for ‘physical strength’ as vigour‐scale. This interaction is visualised in Figure 1B. More directly, a statistically significant interaction of cognitive demands and cognitive resources predicting physical strength was found (b = 0.10, p = 0.009). Again, the two simple slope tests showed a significant slope for high cognitive resources, but not for low cognitive resources. The double‐match interaction graph reveals that an increase in cognitive demands was related to more physical strength when cognitive resources were high (+1 SD; t(666) = 3.83, p = 0.0001). Contrariwise, no statistically significant relation between cognitive demands and physical strength was detected when cognitive resources were low (−1 SD; t(666) = 0.18, p = 0.857).

The remaining outcome of vigour, emotional energy, showed a main‐effect model (M1) to be significant. Please be aware of the fact that we do not display interaction coefficients in the main‐effect models. In model M1, both emotional resources (b = 0.26, p = 0.0001) and cognitive demands (b = 0.10, p = 0.011) were positively related to emotional energy. Generally, the explained variance of the best fitting models expressed in R 2 was 16% for cognitive liveliness, 14% for emotional energy and 16% for physical strength.

As far as employee sustainable performance is concerned, the interactive model M2 appeared to be statistically significant, which was caused by the triple‐match interaction of cognitive demands and cognitive resources in the prediction of performance (b = 0.06, p = 0.007; see also Figure 1C). Simple slope tests revealed that an increase in cognitive demands was related to more sustainable performance when cognitive resources were high (+1 SD; t(666) = 3.35, p = 0.0001). In contrast, there was no statistically significant relation between cognitive demands and sustainable performance when cognitive resources were low (−1 SD; t(666) = 0.67, p = 0.503). The R 2 for the best fitting regression model M2 was 7%.

Predictors of Fatigue

Similar to the former analyses, demands, resources and their matching interactions were used as predictors of fatigue, again using demographics as confounders. Table 2 shows that two out of three interaction models were statistically significant.

The first interaction model entails a triple‐match interaction of emotional demands and emotional resources in the prediction of emotional fatigue (b = −0.10, p = 0.0001). Figure 1D shows the interaction graph, in which both simple slopes (for low and high emotional resources) were statistically significant. More explicitly, an increase in emotional demands was related to more emotional fatigue not only when emotional resources were low (−1 SD; t(666) = 6.64, p = 0.0001), but also when emotional resources were high (+1 SD; t(666) = 2.85, p = 0.005). Note that the positive relation between emotional demands and emotional fatigue was stronger at low levels of emotional resources, compared to high levels of emotional resources.

Table 2 reveals also a statistically significant double‐match of common kind interaction for cognitive fatigue; that is, the interaction of emotional demands with emotional resources (b = −0.06, p = 0.012). Looking into the two simple slope tests depicted a statistically significant slope for low and high emotional resources. Figure 1E reveals that an increase in emotional demands was associated with more cognitive fatigue, particularly in the case of low emotional resources (−1 SD; t(666) = 7.38, p = 0.0001). When emotional resources were high (+1 SD; t(666) = 2.91, p = 0.004), the positive relation between emotional demands and cognitive fatigue was less strong.

Furthermore, we found a main‐effects model (M1) for physical fatigue. Both higher physical demands (b = 0.24, p = 0.0001) and lower physical resources (b = −0.10, p = 0.020) were related to higher physical fatigue. In addition, emotional demands were also positively associated with physical fatigue (b = 0.14, p = 0.0001).

Generally, the R 2s of the best fitting models were 9%, 16% and 19% for cognitive, emotional and physical fatigue, respectively.

Double‐Match of Extended Kind and Non‐Match Interactions

De Jonge and Dormann (2006) suggested to test all remaining double‐match of extended kind and non‐match interactions to perform a complete test of the DISC Model. We followed this suggestion in our final analytical steps, as depicted in Table 3. The corresponding seven HMRAs showed no statistically significant interaction models for all outcomes involved. This means that there were neither significant double‐match of extended kind interactions nor significant non‐match interactions between job demands, job resources and employee health and performance.

Discussion

The current study performed an investigation into the compensating role of job resources in the association between (1) job demands and (2) health and performance in nursing home care. We tested the DISC Model's triple‐match principle among nursing home staff working in 24 locations to investigate if a match between specific job resources, specific job demands and health/performance outcomes exists. Generally speaking, four out of our eight hypotheses were confirmed. Our findings showed that matching resources at work do have a compensating role in the association between specific job demands on the one hand, and health and performance outcomes on the other.

Theoretical Implications

Regarding employee vigour, regression analyses showed empirical confirmation for Hypothesis 1a. Higher cognitive demands were related to more cognitive liveliness (i.e., vigour) when cognitive resources were high, which is in agreement with the triple‐match principle and the balance mechanism. It also aligns with former research among nursing home staff (e.g., De Jonge, Gevers, and Dollard 2014) and academic staff (e.g., De Jonge and Huter 2021). Findings imply that nursing home staff is likely to report higher cognitive liveliness if they have adequate and sufficient cognitive job resources to deal with cognitively demanding tasks. In contrast, no empirical confirmation for Hypotheses 1b and 1c regarding the two other components of vigour (i.e., emotional energy and physical strength) was found. The main‐effect model for emotional energy, however, showed one predictor in line with the matching principle (i.e., a positive association with emotional job resources), next to a positive relation of cognitive demands with emotional energy. Furthermore, a double‐match interaction of common kind (i.e., cognitive demands and cognitive resources) in the prediction of physical strength was empirically justified. This finding reveals that a double‐match interaction which stems from another, cognitive, domain could be functional for increasing physical strength of staff, too (see also Van den Tooren, De Jonge, and Dormann 2011). The positive valence of cognitive demands could also be explained by the fact that it is largely considered a ‘challenge demand’, as described in the challenge–hindrance demands framework of Lepine, Podsakoff, and Lepine (2005). Challenge demands can be appraised as promoting active learning, creativity and growth, and hence to increase positive cognitive outcomes such as cognitive vigour and sustainable performance.

As far as employee sustainable performance is concerned, we detected a triple‐match interaction in agreement with the triple‐match principle and the balance mechanism. Higher cognitive demands were only related to higher sustainable performance when cognitive job resources were high. Hypothesis 2 was confirmed accordingly. Within this point of view, Dorenbosch (2014) considered employee sustainable performance as a shared function of (1) high levels of job resources and (2) the allocation of job resources. This function allows for regeneration of job resources in the longer run as well. So, in order to deal with job demands, staff need access to sufficient, specific and relevant job resources. In the case of sustainable performance, this largely involves self‐regulatory, cognitive, processes to maintain a state of balance between job resources and (challenging) job demands (cf. Ji et al. 2021).

With respect to employee fatigue, we detected a triple‐match for emotional fatigue and a double‐match of common kind for cognitive fatigue. No interaction effects were found for physical fatigue. The triple‐match interaction reveals that higher emotional demands were particularly related to more emotional fatigue in the case emotional resources were absent. Moreover, nursing home staff is likely to report less emotional fatigue if they experience ample emotional job resources (e.g., emotional support from a close colleague) to combat emotionally demanding tasks. This triple‐match finding confirms Hypothesis 3b and the DISC Model's stress‐buffering mechanism. It is also in agreement with other empirical studies in healthcare that call attention to the stress‐buffering role of emotional resources (e.g., De Jonge and Dormann 2006; De Jonge et al. 2008). Longitudinal studies into occupational burnout have revealed that emotional job demands may cause emotional exhaustion (e.g., Schaufeli and Salanova 2024). It could be questioned how this negative effect of emotional job demands can be effectively dealt with. Staff burnout can be considered a general response to emotion work, which can be dealt with when matching emotional resources at work are directly available.

We could not find empirical confirmation for both Hypothesis 3a (cognitive fatigue) and Hypothesis 3c (physical fatigue). Nevertheless, we detected a double‐match interaction of common kind between emotional job demands and emotional job resources predicting cognitive fatigue. Again, a double‐match interaction that stems from another, emotional, domain could also be helpful for diminishing cognitive fatigue. So, it seems that a decent balance between emotional job demands and emotional job resources might act as a properly working mechanism for both emotional and cognitive fatigue (cf. De Jonge, Gevers, and Dollard 2014; De Jonge and Huter 2021). This is in line with empirical research about the important role of emotional support from colleagues and supervisors for both mental and physical health (e.g., Taylor 2011). Finally, main effects with regard to predicting physical fatigue indicate that its key predictors are in agreement with the triple‐match principle, too. More explicitly, higher physical demands as well as lower physical resources were related to higher physical fatigue of nursing home staff.

The findings depicted in Table 4 indicate that the ratio of statistically significant interaction effects versus interactions tested was progressively and positively related to the degree of match between job demands, job resources, and employee outcomes. This means that Hypothesis 4 is empirically justified in agreement with previous DISC research (e.g., De Jonge and Dormann 2006; De Jonge and Huter 2021). Processes of match or fit could be clarified by functional self‐regulation theory that is shifted to work (De Jonge, Demerouti, and Dormann 2024). This theory proposes that staff in general apply functional self‐regulation strategies in their work, which key coping function is to restore the demand‐resource imbalance prompted by high demands at work. The DISC Model suggests that employees show functional self‐regulatory behaviour in combating job demands. More specifically, nursing home staff who faces a specific type of job demands (e.g., emotional demands) are generally inclined to search for and to use functional matching job resources such as emotional social support to tackle these demanding aspects at work.

Finally, the explained variances expressed in R 2s for the seven outcome variables ranged between 7% for employee sustainable performance and 19% for physical fatigue, which is satisfying as well as practically significant given the multi‐causal aetiology of these outcome variables. They are also largely in agreement with earlier DISC research in both healthcare and other work domains (e.g., see De Jonge and Dormann 2006; Van den Tooren, De Jonge, and Dormann 2011; De Jonge and Huter 2021; Ji et al. 2021).

Strengths, Limitations and Future Research

Strengths of the current study are the large sample size as well as the number of nursing home locations involved. Despite these strong points, several limitations should be addressed. The cross‐sectional survey design and the use of self‐report measures are a first limitation. Generally, we assumed a causal flow of the study variables (i.e., from job demands to employee vigour and sustainable performance), but one should keep in mind that reversed causal directions are possible, too. For instance, healthcare staff who are not very healthy may experience more job demands and less job resources, which could imply that they report more emotional demands and less emotional support than was actually available to them. Research studies with longitudinal designs (e.g., panel or diary studies) are highly recommended in this respect. A second limitation is that method variance could be partly responsible for the current associations between the variables. However, several research studies have revealed that this effect is not as high as commonly believed (e.g., Spector 2006). As recommended by Podsakoff, MacKenzie, and Podsakoff (2012), we strove for minimising method variance by measuring the key variables with distinctive response scale formats and different ranges. Notwithstanding this, studies using multiple sources (e.g., employees, supervisors, peers, observers) and/or multiple methods (e.g., surveys, dialy diary studies, interviews) are highly recommended to combat method variance. Finally, the sample has been derived from 24 nursing home locations in a single Western country, which implies that further generalisation of the current findings in other countries and cultures is recommended.

Implications for Nursing and Nursing Management

Despite its limitations, this study suggests important implications for contemporary nursing home practice. There is an urgent need to train nursing home staff to cope better with increasing job demands. Because these demands cannot be reduced in many situations, the idea of boosting job resources instead is appealing to both staff and managers. The main focus should be on work‐related interventions aimed at increasing job resources—preferably those resources that match particular job demands. First, our findings reveal that cognitive job resources such as job autonomy and smooth access to useful (client) information are primarily important to deal with cognitively demanding tasks to improve both cognitive vigour and physical vigour as well as sustainable performance. To increase the presence and use of cognitive job resources enables nursing home staff to adequately meet their high cognitively demanding tasks. Nowadays, job crafting is a powerful job redesign strategy through which nursing home employees can configure their own job resources (Demerouti and Bakker 2024). Job crafting can be considered pro‐active employee behaviour which merely consists of resource‐seeking and challenge‐seeking. It is important that managers realise that an individual employee knows his or her job best, and can judge how the job fits better with him or her. Second, our results also show that emotional resources such as emotional support from colleagues and supervisors are primarily important to tackle emotional demands to reduce emotional and cognitive fatigue of nursing home staff. Work‐related interventions to enhance emotional support should not only centre on the actual presence of those job resources, but also on ways to trigger staff to receive compassion and comradeship from their closest network (cf. De Jonge and Huter 2021). Giving and receiving support can also increase positive emotions and decrease negative emotions. Third, findings reveal that nursing home staff who is often faced with high physical job demands and at the same time reports low physical job resources are exposed to physical fatigue. Increasing physical job resources, such as ergonomic aids or instrumental support from colleagues, together with further skills training, enables staff to deal with their high physical job demands. Finally, employees with relatively high scores on sustainable performance experience not only higher levels of vigour and seem to perform better than their colleagues, they are also eager to create a sustainable future for themselves and for the organisation as a whole (cf. Ji et al. 2021).

Conclusion

This empirical study provides insight into the compensating, preferably matching, role of job resources in the association between (1) job demands and (2) health and performance of nursing home staff. Moreover, this study contributes to the DISC Model's empirical evidence. In addition, it promotes sustainable work environments in nursing home care that are about preserving a decent balance between specific job demands and corresponding job resources to promote vigour and sustainable performance, as well as to prevent fatigue of staff. Nursing home staff who faces a specific type of job demands are in general inclined to search for and to use functional matching job resources to combat job demands. The ideal organisational goal should be to have nursing home staff consisting of vital employees who are presumed to be highly productive employees in the longer run as well.
