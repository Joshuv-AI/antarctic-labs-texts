The include network: Advancing cross‐linguistic equity in brain health research
Authors: Adolfo M García, Jessica de Leon, Suvarna Alladi, Petronilla Battista, Agustina Birba, Stefano F Cappa, Bárbara Costa Beber, Jessica Jiang, David Kemmerer, Maxime Montembeault, Juan Rafael Orozco‐Arroyave, Maria Luisa Gorno‐Tempini, Boon Lead Tee, the Include Network
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpeech and language measures are increasingly recognized as sensitive, scalable, non‐invasive markers of diverse brain disorders. Yet, current research is overwhelmingly English‐centric, neglecting the world's vast linguistic diversity and undermining these markers’ global applicability. Here we present the International Network for Cross‐Linguistic Research on Brain Health (Include), a multicentric initiative created to address this gap (https://include‐network.com/). First, we review the network's origins, mission, and organizational structure. Then, we summarize key milestones achieved during its first 4 years, spanning cross‐linguistic empirical studies, harmonized data‐governance frameworks, and global dissemination efforts. We further highlight flagship projects demonstrating how linguistic typology and culture shape speech‐language markers of neurodegeneration. Finally, we outline critical challenges and opportunities for the field, focusing on language underrepresentation, tool harmonization, regulatory heterogeneity, and expansion beyond neurodegeneration. Together, these efforts position Include as a strategic platform for fostering equitable, globally relevant brain‐health research.Keywords: brain health, cross‐linguistic research, global equity, neurodegeneration, speech and language markers

BACKGROUND

Contemporary knowledge about brain health and disease is fueled by speech and language research. 1 Assessments of phonetic, phonological, lexico‐semantic, morphosyntactic, and pragmatic/discursive skills (Box 1) illuminate our understanding of language‐dominant syndromes (e.g., post‐stroke aphasia [PSA] or primary progressive aphasia [PPA] variants) and non‐language‐dominant disorders (e.g., Alzheimer's disease [AD], Parkinson's disease [PD], and behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia [bvFTD]). 2 , 3 In PSA and PPA, speech and/or language disturbances are core diagnostic symptoms, mapping on syndrome‐specific brain damage patterns and representing a leading cause of functional disability. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 In AD, 1 , 8 PD, 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 and bvFTD, 13 , 14 specific speech and/or language alterations constitute early and even prodromal markers, sensitive to severity, progression, and neural anomalies. Emerging evidence further points to their role as predictors of pathological and genetic abnormalities, reinforcing their relevance to modern‐day neurology. 1 , 13

Now boosted by digital technologies, speech and language tests tackle critical limitations of other (e.g., neural, biofluid) measures, as they are non‐invasive (avoiding discomfort and risks), cost‐effective (increasing accessibility), time‐efficient (offering real‐time results), and remotely deployable (overcoming geographical barriers). Such features strengthen their utility for multiple clinical aims, including patient screening, detection, diagnosis, phenotyping, monitoring, and treatment efficiency assessment. Unsurprisingly, speech and language measures are gaining momentum in brain health research, 3 with growing presence in leading journals, 1 , 15 , 16 , 17 research challenges, 18 patient‐facing apps, 19 , 20 and clinical trials. 21

Yet, the field's potential for equitable global impact has been undermined by a core caveat: the neglect of language diversity. 3 , 22 Most of the world's 7,000 languages are under‐represented or absent in the literature, including those from regions with the greatest burden of brain disorders. Although 83% of the world does not speak English, nearly 70% of studies target English‐speaking cohorts, whose deficits may be attenuated, exacerbated or reversed in other language groups. 3 Little attention has been paid to culturally patterned norms that shape speech and language behavior (e.g., response timing, word use, communication styles), prompting dubious assumptions of universality across language measures. 23 Despite their potential for scalable global assessments, digital speech markers are mainly developed in high‐income countries. 1 Worldwide inequities are also present in the training of speech‐language pathologists, extending imbalances to treatment options. 24

Additional challenges pervade research on language as a culture‐sensitive driver of wellbeing, particularly for Indigenous populations. 25 The same is true of multilingualism, a brain‐protective, 26 syndrome‐modifying 27 factor present in a vast proportion of patients. Indeed, as multilinguals outnumber monolinguals worldwide, 28 limited knowledge on the former's distinct speech and language manifestations undermine the generalizability of existing clinical frameworks. 29 Compounding these issues is the lack of multicentric efforts for harmonized cross‐linguistic research, precluding the discernment of universal and language‐specific manifestations in each disorder. 30 This gap largely stems from the high infrastructural and resource demands of multilingual data collection, making siloed efforts unsustainable and underscoring the need for coordinated, collaborative actions.

Against this background, we created the International Network for Cross‐Linguistic Research on Brain Health (Include), a global initiative for equitable language‐based research on neural disorders. Four years after its launch, we review the network's history, goals, and organization; synthesize its major scientific, operational, and dissemination achievements; highlight site‐specific initiatives; and identify critical challenges and opportunities for growth. This way, we seek to raise awareness of cross‐linguistic brain health research, in general, and of Include, in particular, as a strategic space to foster the field.

AIMS AND HISTORY

Include was conceived by its Executive Team in 2021, aiming to advance equitable neurolinguistic markers across diverse languages. Its mission was structured around four core aims (Figure 1), namely: (i) fostering cross‐linguistic research on brain disorders to reveal language‐specific and typologically generalizable markers, (ii) promoting language diversity in brain health studies, (iii) supporting the creation of cross‐linguistically and cross‐culturally valid tools for research and clinical purposes, and (iv) amplifying the field's visibility among diverse audiences via targeted dissemination efforts. Thus, the network is programmatic in addressing recent calls for global equity. 3

Include was conceived to pursue for main aims: (i) promoting cross‐linguistic research on brain disorders, (ii) increasing language diversity in brain health studies, (iii) developing cross‐linguistically and cross‐culturally valid tools, and (iv) strengthening dissemination and visibility across diverse audiences.

Include’s achievements to date (Figure 2) reflect efforts across 4 years. Formal operations began in March 2022, supported by initial funds from the Global Brain Health Institute, the Alzheimer's Association, and the Alzheimer's Society, through their joint Pilot Awards for Global Brain Health Leaders program. Initial guidelines were created alongside the network's multilingual logo and website.a Sites were recruited through targeted outreach efforts, including e‐mails, in‐person meetings, and video‐calls. By September 2022, the network had members from nearly 40 sites across 20 countries.

The inaugural online meeting took place in November 2022, with 60 attendees spanning world‐renowned leaders, early‐career researchers, and post‐graduate students. Four committees were then established to guide and support the network's operations (Section 3). Since February 2023, sites began contributing pre‐existing data for network‐wide projects, followed by open calls for cross‐linguistic studies leveraging such data (Section 4.1). In‐person events began in March 2023, with successive yearly meetings in Amsterdam, Philadelphia, Toronto, and London (hosted by the Alzheimer's Association), and presentations in academic events across Spain, the United Kingdom, the United Sates, Argentina, and Japan. A flagship position paper was published in 2023 followed by an initial cross‐linguistic study (Section 4.1). Further scientific progress was enabled by developments in data governance (Section 4.2). Our webinar series, titled “Language diversity and brain health,” was launched in 2023 in collaboration with the Alzheimer's Association (Section 4.3). Milestones in 2025 include the creation of a working group on multilingual speech and language testing, the launch of two special issues, and the release of cross‐cultural awareness‐raising video (Section 4.3).

By February 2026, Include comprised 186 members from 109 sites spanning 35 countries (Figure 3; Supplementary material 1). Members encompass neuroscientists, speech‐language pathologists, neuropsychologists, engineers, and data scientists at diverse career stages, who have partaken in over 80 online meetings, 10 in‐person events, and five webinars. Collectively, sites provide expertise in 37 languages, contributing initial pre‐existing data from over 5000 participants across AD, PD, bvFTD, PPA, PSA, and healthy aging cohorts. These resources are currently being harnessed in four cross‐linguistic multicentric projects.

Overall, Include’s rapid growth and productivity stemmed from the convergence of established sites committed to language equity, enabling a shared scientific vision across regions and disciplines. Collectively, these elements allowed Include to overcome traditional infrastructural and resource constraints, accelerating breakthroughs beyond the possibilities of isolated efforts. Relevant actions are detailed below.

STRUCTURE

Include’s operations are supported by four interacting bodies: a Coordinating Committee, a Research Committee, a Data Governance Committee, and an Outreach Committee. Each one comprises two leaders and three to eight members from diverse disciplines and geographical regions. All committees meet online once per quarter, except for the Coordinating Committee, whose Executive Team meets weekly and its full membership meets once annually at year's end.

The Coordinating Committee is Include’s primary governing body, responsible for fostering synergy and inclusiveness across institutions and thematic groups to ensure cohesive operation. It provides strategic direction, facilitates communication among committees, and coordinates network‐wide meetings for shared planning and evaluation. It also oversees membership governance, including reviewing and approving applications to safeguard the network's scientific scope and integrity. Additionally, it supports long‐term sustainability by identifying relevant grant opportunities and assesses reports from other committees to keep activities aligned with network priorities. Day‐to‐day operations are led by the Executive Team, composed of founding members within this committee.

The Research Committee advances the network's scientific mission. Its core responsibilities include maintaining and updating multicentric databases to facilitate productive collaborations. Its members work to develop cross‐linguistic research protocols that enable harmonized data collection and comparability across languages and populations. They also evaluate and approve concept sheets for new studies, ensuring that projects meet scientific and ethical standards. Beyond project initiation, the committee offers guidance on methodological or organizational challenges faced by ongoing projects.

The Data Governance Committee enables efficient, secure, and responsible data sharing by organizing and curating databases so datasets are well documented, interoperable, and accessible to authorized users. It guides data harmonization across sites to support meaningful comparison and integration, and facilitates bilateral and multilateral data transfer by addressing technical, legal, and ethical barriers. By establishing best practices aligned with local regulations, the committee promotes transparency, reproducibility, and reuse of research outputs.

The Outreach Committee focuses on communication, visibility, and community building. It produces a periodic newsletter to disseminate updates on network activities, research highlights, and opportunities for collaboration. The committee actively identifies funding opportunities as well as relevant conferences and events. A central goal is to increase the network's visibility, both within the scientific community and to external stakeholders, by promoting the network's mission and achievements. The committee also coordinates educational activities, such as webinars and workshops, to support capacity building among members.

KEY ACHIEVEMENTS

Include has accomplished several achievements in terms of research, data governance, and dissemination. Below, we highlight some such contributions.

Research

A key research output was the publication of a perspective on cross‐linguistic equity in neurodegeneration research. 3 English speakers are shown to account for over two thirds of speech and language research on PPA, AD, PD, and bvFTD. This is noted to misrepresent the nature of deficits in each disorder, since their manifestation in Anglophone patients may not be mirrored in other language groups. For example, English speakers with AD overuse pronouns 31 , 32 but Bengali‐speaking patients underuse them. 33 Across PSA cases, agrammatism is common in English and other analytic languages but less so in agglutinative (e.g., Indian) languages with flexible word order. 34 In PPA, difficulties with noun classifiers are systematic in languages whose grammar renders them obligatory (e.g., Chinese) but much less noticeable in languages where they prove optional or less central, like English. 20 Among Brazilians with logopenic variant PPA, repetition deficits are not systematic in early stages, contrasting with descriptions of English‐speaking cohorts. 35 Cross‐linguistic discrepancies also depend on orthographic systems, with evidence from Chinese‐speaking PPA patients revealing writing impairments that are not accounted for by English‐based models. 36 Proposed strategies to counter this scenario include expanding cross‐linguistic experiments with public and proprietary datasets, fostering multinational collaborations, and designing harmonized data‐collection protocols across countries. These efforts are essential for generating widely applicable neurocognitive models of language disorders, ensuring that theoretical and clinical advancements do not create yet new forms of worldwide inequity in behavioral neurology.

Another Include paper notes that, while more than half the world's population is multilingual, most clinical and research tools are built on monolingual norms, leading to widespread misdiagnosis and inequitable care. 37 Multilingualism is shown to fundamentally reshape (native and non‐native) language processing, brain organization, cognitive reserve, and symptom expression across brain disorders—all under diverse influences of second‐language proficiency, age of acquisition, and modality (spoken vs. signed) as well as between‐language distance, dialect, education, and socioeconomic context. Accordingly, speech and language performance in multilinguals should not be assessed with the same benchmarks employed for monolinguals—especially those coming from over‐represented Western, highly educated, industrialized, wealthy, and democratic regions. 38 , 39 An actionable agenda is advanced, focused on building multilingual norms, collecting detailed multilingual histories, redesigning tests for functional equivalence, integrating AI cautiously to support multilingual assessment, and adopting open‐science practices to share data and tools. Overall, a call is raised to develop culturally sensitive, theory‐driven, multilingual‐first models of assessment and care.

As regards empirical contributions, a pioneering study evaluated whether digital speech and language biomarkers of AD generalize across typologically different languages. 40 Speech was elicited from participants using a standardized picture description task, then preprocessed and transcribed to derive two types of features: speech timing (e.g., pause durations, pace) and lexico‐semantic measures (e.g., lexical ratios, semantic variability). Machine learning models were trained on English‐speaking patients and healthy controls, then tested on Spanish speakers. Timing features afforded reasonable classification (area under the curve [AUC] ≈ 0.75) and predicted cognitive scores in the Spanish‐speaking testing set, whereas lexico‐semantic and combined models performed poorly. This suggests that speech timing features, likely indexing word retrieval effort, generalize better than those capturing vocabulary usage, at least as regards this particular language pair.

Another study examined how culture and language influence semantic fluency performance by comparing Korean‐ and English‐speaking AD patients. 41 Thirty‐six participants with AD completed an animal‐naming task. Their responses were analyzed using two approaches: a standard, culture‐neutral framework based on Troyer's clustering and switching taxonomy, and a culture‐specific framework that additionally captured culturally salient categories such as East Asian zodiac animals. Measures included overall output, early responses, cluster size, switching behavior, and associations with global cognitive status. Results showed that Korean‐speaking individuals frequently produced zodiac animals early and grouped them into distinct clusters, accompanied by higher levels of switching. These effects were not observed in the English‐speaking group and were invisible when only culture‐agnostic analyses were applied. Moreover, only the Korean group showed significant positive links between fluency measures and Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores. The authors conclude that culturally grounded knowledge structures shape semantic fluency patterns in AD and argue that neuropsychological assessment should incorporate culture‐specific norms for valid cross‐linguistic interpretation.

Combined data across sites has been further leveraged in four network‐wide projects. The first integrates picture naming responses across dementia syndromes to establish the prevalence of multiple error types across 10 languages. A second one applies an automated word property framework 8 , 9 to data from 2,000 dementia patients to establish shared and differential vocabulary search patterns in eight languages. A third project uses federated learning to test whether a single multilingual natural language processing model yields robust classification of Dutch‐, Spanish‐, and Italian‐speaking AD patients relative to same‐language controls. The final one examines whether digit span varies across 12 languages depending on sublexical and semantic properties of number words. Together, these projects showcase diverse strategies to tackle Include’s core mission.

Network members have also led special issues in high‐impact journals. The issue “Multilingualism and literacy: Resilience and risk factors for Alzheimer's disease and related dementia,” hosted by Alzheimer's & Dementia: Diagnosis, Assessment, & Disease Monitoring, examines how multilingualism and literacy influence cognitive outcomes across languages, covering cognitive and neural mechanisms, risk and resilience, assessment, sociocultural factors, and biological correlates. Another issue, “Language disorders in understudied languages,” published in Cortex, promotes language diversity in brain health research, with contributions on underrepresented languages, cross‐linguistic comparisons, bilingual populations, and language‐specific assessment tools.

Data governance

Include has established formal data‐management protocols, with workflows governing how studies are proposed, reviewed, executed, and reported. Investigators submit standardized Concept Sheets detailing hypotheses, variables, analytic plans, roles, and authorship. These are reviewed by the Coordinating Committee for merit, feasibility, overlap, and compliance with prior agreements Thereupon, they can be accepted, returned for revision, or rejected, with revisions undergoing full re‐review. After approval, investigators sign project‐specific Data Management Agreements and receive only cleaned, periodically “frozen,” de‐identified datasets to ensure traceable analyses. Quality control relies on harmonized collection protocols, training sessions, and clear requirements for data type, volume, timing, de‐identification, and storage. Security measures include stripping identifiers, encrypted transfers, password protection, and restricted re‐identification codes. Fair use is enforced through limits to approved analyses, permission for changes, a 6‐month embargo on returned results, mandatory reporting of negative findings within 1 year, and bans on third‐party sharing without approval.

Formal guidelines have also been established for inter‐institutional data sharing.b Sites are instructed to amend consent forms with standardized language permitting upload of anonymized data to open repositories (e.g., Open Science Framework) for scientific reuse, aligned with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), in Europe; and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), in the United States. Privacy is operationalized through irreversible de‐identification workflows requiring removal or encryption of all personal identifiers and prohibiting any re‐identification. For raw or high‐risk data, investigators must seek case‐specific guidance based on standard privacy‐rule methods. To protect analytic reliability, data providers must clean and validate data before transfer, removing inconsistencies and duplicates and supplying documentation on collection methods, biases, and limitations. Secure exchange is ensured by mandating encrypted channels such as virtual private networks or secure file transfer protocols, with tailored technical support for choosing appropriate repositories and transfer mechanisms after project approval.

Guidelines have also been formalized for federated learning, a decentralized machine‐learning paradigm that enables multiple institutions to collaboratively train a shared model without exchanging raw data. Each site keeps its data locally, trains the model on its own infrastructure, and sends only updates (e.g., parameter weights or gradients) to a coordinating server, which aggregates them (often by averaging) and redistributes the improved global model for subsequent rounds. Through repeated cycles, the model benefits from the diversity and scale of all datasets while data remain at their source. This approach supports effective cross‐site learning under heterogeneous privacy laws and regulatory systems, enabling knowledge sharing and improved performance without violating legal or ethical constraints.

This regulatory corpus is freely available online. Include members must abide by it and non‐members may use it reference for their own multicentric projects. Members may further contact the Data Governance Committee for ad hoc advice on country‐ or region‐specific regulations. Through such milestones, Include ensures that its scientific agenda can advance while meeting the diverse legal constraints of its participating sites.

Dissemination

Include’s flagship dissemination effort is the “Language diversity and brain health” webinar series, co‐organized with the Alzheimer's Association's Bilingualism, Literacy and Language Special Interest Group of its Diversity and Disparities Professional Interest Area. Five webinars have been held since August 2024, titled “Brain dysfunctions across the world's languages” (examining typological variations and their implications for worldwide brain‐disorder research), “Alzheimer's disease across languages” (exploring how linguistic deficits in AD vary cross‐linguistically and related test development challenges), “Speech errors across languages” (discussing the definition and generalizability of speech error types), “Cognitive testing across language and culture: Generalizability vs specificity?” (debating trade‐offs between language‐specific and language‐generalizable tasks), and “Language‐specific phenomena in primary progressive aphasia” (addressing language‐driven symptom variations). Together, these offerings attracted 290 unique attendees and 25 panelists from 28 countries across Europe, the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Oceania.

Another highlight is the cross‐cultural audiovisual piece “Language, your ally for brain health.”c This video was designed to elevate global awareness of the relationship between language and cognitive well‐being while addressing long‐standing inequities in English‐centric health communication. It highlights (i) language changes as potential signatures of neurodegenerative disease, (ii) sustained linguistic activity as a scalable neuroprotective habit, and (iii) language itself as a vector for disseminating and normalizing brain‐health knowledge. Its creative team brought together expertise in neurolinguistics, visual communication, filmmaking and racial equity. The material strategically leaned on culture‐neutral visuals and narrated audios—eschewing text, portraits, and localized imagery—to facilitate translation and reuse across diverse settings. It is distributed with English and Spanish voiceovers, with subtitles available in Arabic, Chinese, French, Italian, and Portuguese.

These actions are complemented with periodic newsletters, disseminating collaboration opportunities, scientific milestones, membership updates, calls for papers, webinar recaps, and conference calendars. In addition, press releases, science blog posts, and media appearances further strengthen the network's public outreach mission. Overall, Include has become a strategic hub for raising awareness of language as a driver of global equity around brain health.

Outstanding initiatives from low/middle‐income target AD, MCI, PPA, PSA, and healthy aging in speakers of Spanish (in Argentina, Colombia, and Peru), Quechua (in Peru), Portuguese (in Brazil), Arabic (in Egypt), and Thai (in Thailand). Some of these concentrate on the development or cultural–linguistic adaptation of standardized assessment instruments, such as regional versions of language examinations for dementia. Others emphasize the creation of novel speech and language datasets in underrepresented languages, enabling automated detection of cognitive decline. Additional work investigates neural mechanisms of recovery and disease progression using electroencephalography, neuroimaging, and behavioral measures, with one project further evaluating language‐based cognitive interventions delivered through telehealth. Collectively, these projects prioritize cultural validity, dialectal sensitivity, and feasibility in resource‐constrained clinical environments. Projects focused on high‐income countries are concerned with AD, MCI, bvFTD, PPA, PSA, and healthy aging. These examining language performance in English, French, Italian, Greek, Korean, Portuguese, and selected Asian languages, targeting cohorts in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, France, Italy, Greece, Switzerland, and the Republic of Korea. These efforts emphasize fine‐grained linguistic and cognitive analyses, including micro‐ and macrostructural discourse measures, verbal fluency, grammatical processing, and bilingualism. Specific projects aim to refine diagnostic classification through either novel digital language testing batteries or multivariate modeling of speech and language data, while others investigate cognitive reserve and aging through speech connectivity or assess the neural and behavioral effects of structured language‐based cognitive training. Together, these studies contribute high‐resolution linguistic biomarkers and normative frameworks that advance theoretical understanding and clinical classification.

Finally, other projects bring together researchers, clinicians, and participants from low/middle‐ and high‐income countries across Latin America, North America, Europe, and Asia. These collaborative efforts target AD, MCI, bvFTD, PPA, PSA, and healthy aging and seek to compare diverse languages, including Spanish, English, French, Korean, Quechua, and Portuguese. Rooted in cross‐linguistic and cross‐cultural design, these initiatives aim to identify language markers of cognitive decline that are robust across languages while remaining sensitive to sociolinguistic and cultural variation. Methodologically, they combine harmonized speech tasks, machine learning, neuroimaging, electroencephalography, and behavioral assessments to test cross‐language generalizability, improve comparability of clinical measures, and distinguish universal from language‐specific patterns. These integrative initiatives function as a bridge between resource‐rich and resource‐limited settings, supporting scalable and inclusive approaches with a (g)local philosophy.

CHALLENGES AND WAYS FORWARD

Cross‐linguistic research on brain health is fraught with challenges and opportunities for progress. Include is a strategic forum to face the former and foster the latter. Future efforts should revolve around increasing language coverage, disentangling between universal and language‐specific markers, harmonizing tools and data, integrating multimodal data sources, handling regulatory discrepancies across countries, expanding beyond geriatric neurology, tackling publication challenges, fostering cross‐regional speech/language therapy training, and raising awareness of language as a scalable window into brain health.

Persistent underrepresentation of languages remains the most visible challenge. Fewer than 20 languages dominate the language marker literature, while thousands spoken in regions with the highest dementia burden remain unstudied. 3 This skews theoretical models and perpetuates inequity in diagnosis and care. While full coverage of all languages is likely unattainable, strategic prioritization offers a feasible way forward. Initial efforts should target understudied yet widely spoken languages in low/middle‐income countries (e.g., Hindi, Bengali, Swahili, Hausa, Vietnamese), which offer potentially large cohorts, low costs, and high translational impact. Ideally, diagnostic and characterization efforts should explicitly account for differences across language families and writing systems when evaluating individuals with the same cognitive disorders. Starting with the 37 languages it currently covers, Include can act as an incubator by pairing emerging sites with established ones, supporting small seed projects, and creating shared repositories of multilingual stimuli and scripts. Such targeted expansion would rapidly increase global coverage while building sustainable local capacity.

The field also needs to navigate the balance between universal and language‐specific assessment criteria. Widely used instruments are first developed for English and often adapted under the assumption that English‐relevant markers (e.g., pronoun overuse, agrammatism, surface dysgraphia) are generalizable across languages. Yet, accruing evidence shows that these phenomena may be attenuated, reversed, or entirely absent in other languages. 3 This creates diagnostic blind spots and threatens construct validity. Assessment tools could be fruitfully reconceptualized as dual‐layer systems: a universal core probing shared cognitive mechanisms (e.g., speech timing, vocabulary complexity) and language‐specific modules targeting typological features (e.g., classifiers in Chinese, rich inflection in Italian). Paths forward include (i) coordinated adaptation protocols that go beyond back‐translation, (ii) systematic validation of tools such as the Mini‐Linguistic State Examination 53 and the Comprehensive Aphasia Test, 54 (iii) machine‐learning transfer studies to test which features generalize and which require local recalibration, 40 and (iv) theoretically driven research frameworks that explicitly incorporate linguistic typology and language‐specific feature selection.

Dialectal variation must also be grappled with. In inferential statistical models, comparisons can be adjusted for dialect (e.g., via covariate inclusion or mixed‐effects structures), enabling the separation of disorder‐related effects from geographically patterned variation. In machine‐learning settings, one strategy is to stratify the overall sample by dialect and replicate core analyses within each subset, to distinguish between stable and dialect‐sensitive features. Generalizability could be further tested via a leave‐one‐dialect‐out approach, that is, training on all but one dialect and testing on the held‐out group, iteratively across dialects. Finally, dialectal differences can be residualized from input features (e.g., by regressing out dialect effects prior to modeling), so that models can privilege speech/language information that proves agnostic to such variance. Together, these approaches render dialect‐sensitive modeling both feasible and essential for equitable research.

Another challenge lies in harmonization. Speech and language datasets across sites differ in tasks, transcription conventions, acoustic preprocessing, and clinical labels, limiting cross‐linguistic comparability. This is problematic because even subtle discrepancies in picture‐description prompts, word‐frequency norms, or pause‐detection thresholds, for example, can produce artifactual “language effects,” obscuring genuine typological patterns. Layered harmonization pipelines should be developed that retain local sensitivity while enabling global synthesis. Concrete paths forward include (i) defining a minimal common dataset (e.g., shared elicitation tasks and metadata fields) required for new data collection efforts; (ii) adopting shared multilingual resources for stimulus construction (e.g., Lexibank, MULTIMAP), 55 , 56 audio processing (e.g., NeuroSpeech, Phonet), 12 , 57 , 58 and integrated data acquisition and analysis (e.g., the TELL app); 19 , 20 and (iii) implementing statistical harmonization techniques and federated learning, which allow models to be trained across heterogeneous datasets without raw‐data sharing. These steps would preserve ecological validity while enabling robust multilingual inference.

Few studies have explored multimodal data integration. Cross‐linguistic research would benefit from combining speech and language measures with other biosignals to map clinically meaningful associations across data sources and improve disease detection and progression monitoring. Consensus groups should define best practices for data collection and analysis, including sensor selection, reducing signal heterogeneity, removing confounds, and synchronizing multimodal data streams (e.g., synchronous vs. asynchronous acquisition).

Cross‐linguistic research is further hindered by regulatory differences across countries, spanning ethics approvals, data‐protection laws, consent language, and transfer agreements. In practice, projects often stall when data‐sharing varies across sites, for example, one can share audio, another only transcripts, and another none outside national borders. This fragmentation disproportionately affects low‐ and middle‐income regions, where regulatory and legal frameworks for research reuse are often underdeveloped. A way forward is to institutionalize modular governance frameworks, including template consent aligned with GDPR and HIPAA, standardized data‐management agreements, and clear risk‐tiering of data types (e.g., raw audio vs. derived features). In parallel, federated learning (where algorithms travel to the data) offers a practical way to bypass cross‐border transfer barriers while preserving privacy and scientific rigor.

Moreover, Include’s current emphasis on acquired and neurodegenerative disorders should be amplified to encompass psychiatric and neurodevelopmental conditions. Otherwise, the network may miss valuable opportunities to establish shared mechanisms of language vulnerability and resilience across the lifespan. The challenge lies in scalability: extending protocols, governance models, and harmonization strategies to new disorders without diluting focus. A pragmatic way forward is phased expansion: piloting cross‐linguistic paradigms in one additional condition at a time, leveraging existing bilingual and understudied‐language cohorts, and fostering cross‐network alliances (e.g., aphasia trialists, literacy consortia). This expansion would position Include as a general platform for equitable language‐brain research, irrespective of etiology.

Publication challenges must also be acknowledged. Manuscripts targeting understudied languages often face prolonged review timelines due to difficulties in identifying reviewers with appropriate linguistic expertise. Also, they may encounter English‐centric editorial policies, risking the loss of critical language‐specific information. Explicitly recognizing these issues may help the field better appreciate the structural and logistical barriers inherent in cross‐linguistic and multilingual research, while promoting equitable actions from publishers and editorial teams.

Cross‐linguistic inequities must also be tackled in speech and language therapy. Disparities abound in intervention‐related training, knowledge, and resources. For example, marked discrepancies exist among world regions in PPA‐specific educational curricula. 24 Recent work highlights the importance of aligning rehabilitation approaches with patients’ linguistic histories, sociocultural identities, and communicative environments, as well as the need for targeted training to support health professionals working in diverse settings. 24 Addressing these gaps is essential to avoid exacerbating inequities in functional recovery, participation, and quality of life for older adults the world over.

Finally, the import of language assessments should be made more visible for non‐language experts. Despite accruing conflicting evidence, many neurologists and trialists still regard language as negligible or peripheral in disorders other than aphasia or dyslexia. This operation model limits the integration of linguistic endpoints into medical workflows, large‐scale studies, and clinical trials, all while producing scientific results that cannot be fully generalized to real‐world populations. 59 Advocacy initiatives are needed to revert this scenario. Key actions include embedding language modules into neurology training curricula, disseminating evidence through webinars and audiovisual campaigns, and producing clinician‐friendly toolkits that link linguistic markers to disease mechanisms and outcomes. By lowering conceptual and practical barriers, Include can foster broader uptake of speech‐language metrics across disciplines.

CONCLUSION

Research on speech and language as markers of brain health stands at a pivotal juncture. While relevant assessments are more scalable and clinically actionable than ever, the field remains constrained by English‐centric paradigms, fragmented methodologies, and regulatory barriers that limit global impact. Growing evidence shows that language structure, culture, and multilingual experience profoundly shape how brain diseases manifest, making cross‐linguistic approaches indispensable for widely generalizable theory, diagnosis, and care. The path forward requires harmonized protocols, privacy‐preserving analytic frameworks, and sustained investment in underrepresented languages and regions. In this evolving landscape, the Include Network illustrates how coordinated international collaboration can convert these challenges into opportunities. By adopting and extending such models, the field can move toward a future in which language‐based brain‐health research is both scientifically rigorous and genuinely global.
