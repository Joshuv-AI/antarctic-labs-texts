Rejuvenation of human mesenchymal stem cells using a nonintegrative and conditionally removable Sendai virus vector
Authors: Mitsuo Oshimura, Toshiaki Tabata, Narumi Uno, Shuta Takata, Genki Hichiwa, Iori Kanazawa, Takeshi Endo, Kazuhisa Honma, Yayan Wang, Kanako Kazuki, Haochen Tu, Yuichi Iida, Satoshi Abe, Yasuhiro Kazuki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) with extended lifespan and differentiation potential that can recapitulate in vivo characteristics could significantly contribute to basic research, drug development, and cell therapy. Specifically, they could ensure a stable supply of specific cellular resources, and possibly extracellular vesicles. Here, we established a technology for extending the lifespan while maintaining differentiation potential, termed “rejuvenation,” of hMSCs (rej-hMSCs) using nonintegrative and conditionally removable temperature-sensitive Sendai virus (SeV) vectors. Various immortalizing factors (i.e., Bmi-1, hTERT, SV40T, and/or HPV E6/E7) were first introduced by the SeV vector into the cells. A combination of three SeVs with Bmi-1, hTERT, or SV40T conferred markedly improved cell proliferation and cloning ability while maintaining differentiation potential and a normal karyotype. An extended lifespan was also demonstrated in other cell types. The rejuvenation of long-passaged or aged hMSCs was also confirmed. SeV vectors were rapidly removed as a function of cell doubling by increasing the temperature from 35 °C to 37 °C or higher, while proliferative ability was maintained. Following FACS sorting, the complete removal of SeV vectors was confirmed by qPCR analyses. Therefore, our cell rejuvenation technology could contribute to research and clinical applications by enabling the supply of modified cells without damaging host chromosomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74757-y.

Introduction

Cultured cells capable of differentiation are essential materials in biomedical, pharmaceutical, and clinical fields. As bioengineering technologies have developed, cells capable of recapitulating in vivo conditions can facilitate the obtainment of new knowledge and the creation of elaborately modified designer cells1. However, the limited lifespans of primary cultured cells capable of continued cell division make it a challenge to maintain sufficient cell numbers while preserving important cellular characteristics2,3. The preparation of large numbers of homogeneous cells from heterogeneous cell populations from in vivo sources also remains difficult4.

To overcome these hurdles, various attempts have been made to introduce immortalizing genes, alone or in combination, that can extend cellular lifespan or make infinite growth possible5–8. Well-known immortalizing genes include telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) genes9, oncogenes (e.g., c-myc and Bmi-1)10–12, cell cycle regulation genes (e.g., mutant CDK4 and CCND1)13,14, and viral genes (e.g., SV40T, HPV E6/E7, and EBV)15–17.

In previous studies, plasmid DNA and lentiviral, retroviral, and adenoviral vectors have been used to introduce immortalizing genes into cells. Plasmid and adenoviral vectors often achieve this via integration into host chromosomes18. Lentiviral and retroviral vectors can also integrate into chromosomes to ensure long-term expression of the transgene19. However, the fact that immortalization vectors generally work by introducing foreign DNA into a host chromosome confers a risk of genomic damage, which can lead to a loss of cellular integrity.

Sendai virus (SeV) vectors are RNA viral vectors that have high gene transfer efficiency and can induce gene expression at high levels in various mammalian species, including humans. They are also nonintegrating viral vectors the transgenes of which are expressed in the cytoplasm of the host cell, which means that entry into the nucleus and integration into a host chromosome do not occur20. The level of expression of genes in SeV vectors can typically be tightly controlled and multiple genes can be simultaneously expressed by changing the position at which the gene(s) is inserted in the vector. These advantages have led to these vectors being widely used for the induction of pluripotent stem cells21. As SeV vectors present no risk of integration into chromosomes, genes can be expressed without damaging host chromosomes and the normal properties of the host cells can be maintained. It has also been found that the replication of mutant SeV vectors can be arrested by changing the temperature, when gene expression is no longer required21. Therefore, SeV vectors are expected to prolong the lifespans of most cell types, which would facilitate the massive proliferation of cells and enable control of the expression of vector-encoded gene(s) under certain conditions. However, because long-term expression using SeV vectors has yet to be established, the cell proliferation characteristics of human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) and other cells transfected with these vectors have not been investigated. Herein, we examine the rejuvenation of hMSCs using the SeV vector. This method can help to overcome shortcomings associated with conventional methods to extend cellular lifespans, and enable high cellular differentiation without integrating genes into the host genome.

Results

Temperature-sensitive SeV vectors encoding immortalization factors and infection conditions

Temperature-sensitive SeV vectors21 each carrying one candidate immortalization factor were prepared to determine the optimal combination for immortalization (Fig. S1a). The efficiency of infection of SeV vectors for gene transfer may vary depending on the cell type and the damage that such excessive infection causes. Therefore, we first examined the multiplicity of infection (MOI) for hMSCs. Under various conditions, no cytotoxicity was observed in all wells 9 days after SeV vector infection, and no abnormalities such as morphological changes were observed at an MOI of 20 (maximum MOI: 80, data not shown). Fluorescent dye expression was observed in most cells at an MOI of 20 upon infection with a single vector, and similar results were obtained upon co-infection with two vectors, one encoding Bmi-1 and the other hTERT (Fig. S1b, c). We considered that gene transfer efficiency was approximately 100% at an MOI of 20 upon co-infection with three vectors (Fig. S1d). Based on this finding, we subsequently used MOI of 40 for co-infections of hMSCs with three different vectors.

Selection of immortalization factors for future studies

hMSCs were infected with temperature-sensitive SeV vectors encoding four immortalizing factors (Bmi-1, hTERT, SV40T, and HPV E6/E7) and cultured to select the factors necessary for an extended lifespan. Cell proliferation rates were measured 80 days after gene transfer (number of cells at transfer: 3 × 104) (Table S1). Cell conditions were observed on day 59, when the uninfected parental line had apparently ceased to divide (Table S1, Fig. S2 No. 16). Notably, all vector combinations that did not include the hTERT-encoding vector induced morphological changes in the host cells (Table S1, Fig. S2), suggesting that hTERT is essential for cells to exhibit an extended lifespan without developing major abnormalities. The results obtained upon comparing transfection with SeV(Bmi-1, hTERT, and SV40T) or SeV(Bmi-1, hTERT, SV40T, and HPV E6/E7) showed that the addition of HPV E6/E7 had minimal effect on cell proliferation. Upon transfection with vectors encoding different combinations of immortalizing factors that include hTERT, telomere elongation was observed only in the presence of Bmi-1 and hTERT (Fig. S3a). Given these findings, the combination of Bmi-1, hTERT, and SV40T was judged to be suitable for extending the lifespan of host cells (Fig. S3b).

Analyses of hMSCs treated with Bmi-1, hTERT, and SV40T

The proliferative ability of SeV(Bmi-1, hTERT, and SV40T)-infected hMSCs and uninfected hMSCs was evaluated in long-term culture. After 75 days of culture, the treated hMSCs continued to proliferate, whereas the proliferation of the parental cells decreased (Fig. 1a). To examine the consequence of removing the SeV vector as a function of cell doubling by changing the temperature, hMSCs with a prolonged lifespan were maintained at 35 °C or shifted to a temperature of 37 °C on day 75. Cells cultured at 37 °C showed a decrease in proliferation because of the removal of the SeV vector, whereas cells cultured at 35 °C continued to proliferate beyond 130 days after gene transfer (Fig. 1a). Further validation of fluorescent protein expression 10 days after the temperature change revealed that the fluorescence of orange fluorescent protein (OFP) and green fluorescent protein (GFP) (co-expressed with Bmi-1 and hTERT, respectively) was visible in cells at 35 °C but barely visible at 37 °C (Fig. 1b, c). Thus, the introduction of Bmi-1, hTERT, and SV40T prolonged the capacity of the host cells to proliferate, with a constant growth speed, while a change of temperature to remove these three factors canceled this effect.

Evaluation of the relationship between temperature change and telomere length showed marked shortening of telomeres in long-term culture after removal of the temperature-sensitive SeVs as a function of cell doubling. Although telomere shortening was also observed when the temperature was maintained (35 °C), this was gradual, with telomeres in the final sample on day 98 remaining longer than the telomeres of parental cells at the start of the experiment (Fig. 1d). Chromosome analysis of the cells during long-term culture confirmed that the karyotype was normal and similar to that of the parental cells (Fig. 1e).

To determine whether the extended lifespan affects the differentiation capacity of SeV-hMSCs, we performed differentiation induction experiments on SeV-hMSCs cultured for 120 days after gene transfer. Parental hMSCs cultured for 14 days were used as positive controls for differentiation. Fat droplets were used as an indicator of adipocyte differentiation, with them being observed in SeV-MSCs (Fig. 2a). Moreover, alkaline phosphatase staining was used as an indicator of osteoblast differentiation, which was observed in both SeV-MSCs and the controls (Fig. 2b), with SeV-MSCs having a higher osteoblast differentiation rate than the positive controls. Fluorescence as an indicator of neuronal differentiation was observed in almost all cells in both SeV-MSCs and positive controls, indicating successful differentiation into neurons (Fig. 2c). Finally, alcian blue staining was used as an indicator of chondrocyte differentiation, revealing similar differentiation rates in SeV-MSCs and controls (Fig. 2d). Therefore, SeV-hMSCs with an extended lifespan could also maintain their differentiation capacity after long-term culture, with the phenotype being referred to as the “rejuvenation” of hMSCs (rej-hMSCs).

Cloning analysis

Cloning experiments were performed to examine whether cloning from parental hMSCs and SeV-hMSCs was possible. Parental MSCs and SeV-hMSCs cultured for 14 and 120 days, respectively, were used for the cloning experiments. Only 2 of 24 wells in a 32-cell per well compartment formed colonies from the parental hMSCs in the early stage of proliferation. In contrast, SeV-MSCs formed colonies in 9 of 24 wells at a low density of four cells per well at 14 days after infection and in 16 of 24 wells at a low density of four cells per well at 120 days after infection with multiple colonies in some wells. This revealed that even hMSCs, which are difficult to clone, could be cloned efficiently when treated with SeV, regardless of the time elapsed (Fig. 3a).

Analysis of the cloned SeV-hMSCs

Chromosome analysis of the 10 clones isolated at 2 weeks after SeV infection revealed that eight of them had a normal karyotype (Fig. 3b). Five clones selected from those eight clones were cultured at 35 °C and 37 °C after 60 days of culture. Three of the five clones showed a decrease in proliferation at 37 °C with enhanced growth inhibition (Fig. 3c). Five of the cloned SeV-hMSCs were examined for differentiation into adipocytes, osteoblasts, and neurons with all of them showing multipotent differentiation potential (Fig. 3d).

Rejuvenation of hMSCs at different cell ages

hMSCs isolated from the human body contain a mixture of cells that have existed for various time periods, ranging from young to aged. Propagating hMSCs at specific time points is difficult and extending the lifespan and being able to clone hMSCs at any time point would greatly expand the range of applications. Therefore, we tested the feasibility of rejuvenation and single-cell cloning of hMSCs from young, middle-aged, and senescent cell populations (days 21, 36, and 61) (Fig. 4a). There were marked phenotypic differences between the untreated and treated cells 8 days after infection (Fig. 4b). Compared with uninfected cells in early culture, treated cells were more easily single-cloned, regardless of the degree of senescent-like features (Table 1). A group of mitotic cells indicated that the proliferative ability was maintained, and that single-cell cloning was enabled by introducing the three immortalization factors via an SeV vector, even for nearly senescent cells (Fig. 4c; Table 1).

Extended lifespan of other cell types

We examined the effect of the SeV vector on adipose- and bone marrow-derived hMSCs in serum-free culture considering their potential use in a clinical context. We confirmed that extended lifespans of adipose- and bone marrow-derived hMSCs (hMSC-AT/hMSC-BM) could be achieved in the same way as for umbilical cord-derived hMSCs (Fig. S4). We also performed SeV infection of human fibroblasts (HFL-1), human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), and rat MSCs and confirmed that similar effects, namely, extension of the lifespan, could be conferred on these various cell types (Figs. S5–S7).

Removal of SeV from SeV-infected hMSCs

To obtain only hMSCs from which SeV had been removed as a function of cell doubling by changing the culture temperature, SeV vectors (Bmi-1, hTERT, and SV40T) were co-expressed with genes expressing fluorescent proteins, namely, OFP, GFP, and blue fluorescent protein (BFP), respectively. After infection, three fluorescent colors were observed (Fig. S8a). The fluorescence was almost eliminated after incubation at a higher temperature for SeV removal. No fluorescence was observed in the cell population (rej-hMSCs) following additional fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) from the fluorescence-negative population (Fig. S8b). The residual level of SeV in the sorted cells was confirmed via qPCR. Some residual SeV remained in cells treated at 37 °C, whereas no SeV was detected in those treated at 39 °C (Fig. 5a). Flow cytometry (FCM) analysis confirmed similar hMSC surface marker expression among the parental hMSCs, SeV-hMSCs, and rej-hMSCs (Fig. S9). However, the CD105 expression level was slightly reduced in SeV-hMSCs and rej-hMSCs compared with that in parental hMSCs. The proportion of apoptotic cells was comparable between parental hMSCs and rej-hMSCs (Fig. S10). The proportion of senescent cells was low and comparable among young parental hMSCs, SeV-hMSCs, and young rej-hMSCs, while it was increased in parental hMSCs and rej-hMSCs after long-term culture (Fig. S11). Conversely, control cells increased to the 106 range, and rej-hMSCs increased to at least the 1017 range until growth-arrested through the cancelation of immortalization; the cells increased to the 1011 range after SeV removal (Fig. 5b). The karyotype of the cells after SeV removal remained normal (Fig. 5c). SeV infection significantly increased the telomere length, whereas the telomeres gradually shortened after the removal of SeV and culture for several passages (Fig. 5d). These results suggest that SeV could be removed from SeV-infected cells to abolish the expression of immortalizing factors; more importantly, cell proliferation increased more than that in the original parental line when immortalization genes were expressed.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the possibility of rejuvenating cells using the SeV vector, a nonintegrating RNA virus. The optimal gene combination for such rejuvenation comprised three factors: Bmi-1, hTERT, and SV40T. Each immortalization gene was individually inserted into a temperature-sensitive SeV vector: Bmi-1 was placed upstream of the NP gene, hTERT was placed between the P and M genes, and SV40T was placed upstream of the NP gene because of the SeV production and control of expression level of the onboard genes (Fig. S1a). Each gene was introduced individually into a single vector as we sought to examine the best combination of immortalized genes and optimal balance of gene expression to induce rejuvenation in this study. However, similar efficiency may be achieved by loading three tandemly connected factors in a single SeV vector.

Combined treatment with hTERT and SV40T via a lentiviral vector was previously shown to enhance proliferation without senescence markers. However, it could also cause chromosomal aberrations in human adipose tissue-derived stromal cells. By contrast, hTERT and Bmi-1 were reported to be associated with no chromosomal aberrations and a low risk of tumorigenic transformation5. Notably, SV40T in a nonintegrated SeV vector may not induce chromosome aberration. The three factors selected in this study may be a well-balanced combination capable of guaranteeing cell proliferation without inducing the negative consequences of chromosomal aberrations (Fig. 1e). In addition, SeV vectors containing hTERT and Bmi-1 but lacking SV40T may exert similar effects depending on the cell type, although additional analyses are required to confirm this. Furthermore, several systems capable of inducing conditional immortalization in various types of cells, including temperature-sensitive SV40T in an integrated vector, Tet, and Cre-loxP systems, have been reported22. This work provides the advance of using nonintegrative SeV vectors as conditional immortalization technologies while avoiding the damage to the host chromosome previously associated with introducing genes. Another advance provided by this work is that the extended lifespan of cells induced by introducing the three immortalization factors using temperature-sensitive mutant SeV vectors allows the unlimited proliferation of hMSCs, but this can be abolished by changing the temperature if required.

Performing single-cell cloning is essential for isolating homogeneous cells from heterogeneous cell populations obtained from living organisms. Here, extending the lifespan of replicatively limited hMSCs significantly increased the efficiency of single-cell cloning and the possession of proliferative ability by cells could be prolonged at any cellular age, even at the phase of growth arrest (Fig. 4). The rejuvenation of older cells increased the efficiency of single-cell cloning, indicating that a more proliferative population was created among cells at an advanced age (Table 1). Thus, this technique using SeV vectors with three immortalization factors may be effective even in cases where cells from a living organism are highly heterogeneous or only aged cell populations can be obtained.

To generate SeV-free cells with high proliferative potential, the most critical issue is the timing of SeV removal after rejuvenation. After SeV infection, SeV is rapidly, if not completely, eliminated from most cells as a function of cell doubling by incubation at 37–39 °C because of the temperature sensitivity of its replication (Fig. 1a, S8a, b). The culture of rejuvenated cells with SeV for longer periods was associated with rapid cellular senescence after SeV removal (Fig. 1a). Cellular senescence had already begun because SeVs were gradually lost with subsequent growth; this was shown by the gradual shortening of the telomeres, even upon culture at 35 °C (Fig. 1d). Furthermore, SeV-retaining cells tend to live longer than SeV-free cells if cells are cultured for long periods because of the SeV-encoded immortalization factors that increase cell viability. It is challenging to obtain SeV-free cells due to positive selection. Therefore, if SeV is removed early after infection, for example, when telomeres reach their peak length, it is expected that SeV-free cells would proliferate for long periods because some cells with long-term proliferative potential can be obtained after that stage. In this case, 1011-fold more hMSCs were obtained through SeV infection and removal than that in uninfected (control) cells (Fig. 5b). Thus, the timing of SeV removal is key to obtaining large numbers of valuable cells.

Conditional rejuvenation using SeV vectors is helpful for efficiently immortalizing living cells without losing their properties or damaging host chromosomes. This technique is expected to contribute to basic research and clinical applications as a tool for generating various cell lines, including normal and cancer cells with slow proliferation. This technique is also expected to help mechanize and automate the process of cell generation and enable the industrialization of regenerative medicine by enabling the mass production of standardized cells with additional functions.

For clinical use, there is still a need to evaluate various factors to ensure the efficacy and safety of rejuvenated hMSCs for various applications. For example, there is a need to carefully assess the maintenance of differentiation potential and immunomodulatory functions in rejuvenated hMSCs to ensure their efficacy, by quantitatively analyzing the expression of marker genes for multi-lineage differentiation, profiling secreted factors, and assaying the proliferation of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells23,24. Furthermore, the detailed analysis of genomic integrity, such as via comparative genomic hybridization or next-generation sequencing, and tumorigenicity testing of rejuvenated hMSCs are important for the secure genetic engineering of hMSCs and to ensure their safety for therapeutic purposes25,26. In conclusion, our study indicates that the combination of hTERT-mediated telomere elongation, Bmi-1-mediated delay in senescence, and SV40T-mediated proliferative potential produced a long-lived cell population with high cell proliferative potential. Furthermore, the use of temperature-sensitive SeVs allows the timing of SeV removal to be precisely set. In particular, removing SeVs from cells with desirably elongated telomeres may allow the selection of cells with particularly strong replicative capacity after SeV removal, producing a greater number of cells. This would allow the acquisition of a greater number of homogeneous cells and their extracellular vesicles by cloning MSCs and other cells for regenerative medicine. This technique would also enable designer cells to be created by chromosome transfer or gene transfer.

Methods

Temperature-sensitive SeV vectors encoding immortalization factors and infection conditions

Here, we selected the common immortalization factors Bmi-111, hTERT9, SV40T15, and HPV-E6/E7 16 for the creation of SeV vectors each encoding an immortalization gene. OFP, GFP, and BFP genes were also co-expressed with Bmi-1, hTERT, and HPV E6/E7, respectively, to facilitate monitoring of the presence of temperature-sensitive SeV vectors (Fig. S1a). The positions of the immortalized genes within the SeV vectors were as follows: Bmi-1 and SV40T were placed at the most upstream position of the vector genome as their high expression (associated with more upstream positions) results in long-term expression. By contrast, hTERT and HPV E6/E7 were located downstream as their high expression would decrease vector production efficiency. The SeV vector used in this study was a temperature-sensitive mutant TS15ΔF-type vector that was modified so that it was rapidly eliminated from cells as a function of cell doubling when the culture temperature was changed from 35 °C to 37 °C or higher21. Immortalization genes were then cloned into SeV vectors that had been manufactured to GMP grade by ID Pharma, Inc. (Ibaraki, Japan).

The efficiency of SeV infection was examined at each MOI. Infection efficiency differs depending on the cell type, and experiments were conducted to identify the MOI that can produce almost 100% infection efficiency. Once these conditions were determined, infection was performed at an MOI of 40, and infected cells were cultured at 35 °C. The temperature of the culture was changed from 35 °C to 37 °C or higher at the desired time point of each experiment.

Cells and culture

The following commercially available cultured cell lines were obtained: bone marrow-derived MSCs [hMSC-BM; product name: Ultrapure Human Mesenchymal Stem Cells (REC), PuREC Corporation] for evaluating the status of infection; cord blood-derived MSCs (product name: Umbilical Cord-Derived Mesenchymal Stem Cells; normal, human, ATCC PCS-500-010) for determination of immortalizing factor combination, infected mass and cloned cell culture analysis, cloning of cells of different ages, and SeV removal experiments; and adipose-derived hMSCs (hMSC-AT; PromoCell; C-12977) and hMSC-BM (PromoCell; C-12974) for serum-free culture.

The serum-containing medium for culturing hMSCs was D-MEM (low glucose), 20% FBS, HEPES (0.01 mol/L), penicillin (100 units/mL), streptomycin (100 µg/mL), and bFGF (20 ng/mL) (Thermo Fisher Cultures), with culture occurring in a 5% CO2 incubator. In addition, the StemFit for Mesenchymal Stem Cell (AJINOMOTO; A3) medium was used as a serum-free medium. Culture dishes were coated with iMatrix-511 silk as culture substrate (Matrixome; 892 091).

Rat subcutaneous fat-derived MSCs (rMSCs, MSA01C; Cosmo Bio Co., Ltd.) were cultured in collagen-coated dishes using rat subcutaneous fat-derived mesenchymal hepatocyte growth medium (MSA-GM; Cosmo Bio Co., Ltd.). Human fibroblasts (HFL1 cells, RCB0521; RIKEN) were cultured in Ham-F12 medium supplemented with 15% heat-inactivated FBS and penicillin–streptomycin (100 units/mL). In addition, HUVECs (C-12205; Promocell) were cultured in the medium used for endothelial cells (1001; ScienCell).

Telomere length analysis

Relative quantification of telomere length was performed via real-time PCR using genomic DNA as a template. Genomic DNA extraction from cells was performed using the Gentra Puregene Kit (Qiagen), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The primers used for real-time PCR were the telomere primer set and the single-copy reference primer set provided with the Relative Human Telomere Length Quantification qPCR Assay Kit (ScienCell Research Laboratories). PCR reagents were 2× GoldNStart TaqGreen qPCR master mix included in the kit. PCR reaction conditions followed the manufacturer’s recommendations. PCR reactions and data acquisition were performed using StepOnePlus (Life Technologies Japan). Data analysis was performed with StepOne Software v2.3.

Cloning method

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates divided into compartments with a determined cell number per well and cultured in a 5% CO2 incubator at 35 °C until single cells formed colonies. Half of the medium was changed every 3 days to avoid disturbing colony formation.

Differentiation ability

Commercially available differentiation kits (Promo Cell) were used for MSC differentiation and included the following: Mesenchymal Stem Cell Adipogenic Differentiation Medium 2 (C-28016) for adipocyte differentiation, Mesenchymal Stem Cell Osteogenic Differentiation Medium (product code C-28013) for osteoblast differentiation, Mesenchymal Stem Cell Neurogenic Differentiation Medium (product code C-28015) for neuronal cell differentiation, and Mesenchymal Stem Cell Chondrogenic Differentiation Medium (product code C-28012) for chondrogenic differentiation. Adipocyte differentiation was confirmed using Lipi-Green (product code LD02; Doujin Chemical), a fluorescent dye that exclusively stains fat droplets, an indicator of adipocyte differentiation. Osteoblast differentiation was confirmed by alkaline phosphatase staining. Neuronal cell differentiation was confirmed using NeuroFluor NeuO (product code ST-01801; VERITAS), a fluorescent dye that exclusively stains neurons. Chondrocyte differentiation was confirmed by Alcian blue staining.

Chromosome analysis

Chromosome analysis was performed using the quinacrine–Hoechst staining method. First, chromosome slides were immersed in 50 mL of McIlvaine solution (280 mL of 0.1 M citric acid and 220 mL of 0.2 M disodium hydrogen phosphate were mixed and autoclaved). Then, Hoechst 33,258 (10 ng/mL, cat. No. B-2883-25MG; Sigma) was added to 50 mL of McIlvaine solution for 30 min. After washing the chromosome specimen slides, they were immersed in McIlvaine solution for 5 min and then covered and sealed with cover glass using McIlvaine inclusion solution (1:1 mixture of McIlvaine solution and glycerol). Finally, karyotyping was performed using a microscope (AxioImager Z2; ZEISS) and software (Ikaros V5.7.4 CM/V5.4.12; Metasystems).

Isolation of SeV-eliminated hMSCs and SeV detection

The SeV vector containing SV40T was modified to include BFP, and this vector was used for the removal experiment. SeV-infected cells were cultured under two temperatures before cell sorting. Cells were cultured at 37 °C for 9 days. To remove SeV, cells were cultured at 39 °C for 1 day and then at 37 °C for 8 days.

The isolation of SeV-depleted cells and subsequent flow cytometric analysis were performed using a FACS Aria Fusion (BD Biosciences). Harvested cells were cultured at 37 °C for 20 days, and RNA was collected. SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to prepare cDNA. Total RNA (1 µg) was used for cDNA synthesis. cDNA was diluted to a theoretical total RNA concentration (4 ng/µL), assuming 100% conversion of RNA to cDNA. TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for qPCR detection. TaqMan Assay [20×] Mr04269880_mr (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as the primer and probe for Sendai virus detection. TaqMan Assay [20×] Hs01060665_g1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as the primer and probe for ACTB detection as an internal control. The qPCR reaction was performed using the StepOne Real-Time PCR System.

FCM analysis

The parental MSCs, SeV-MSCs, and rej-MSCs were stained with allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated anti-human CD90 antibody (5E10), anti-human CD105 antibody (43A3), anti-human CD73 antibody (AD2;), anti-human CD34 antibody (581), anti-human CD31 antibody (WM59), anti-human CD45 antibody (2D1), anti-human HLA-DR antibody (L243), anti-human CD14 antibody (M5E2), and isotype control antibody (MOPC-173) (all from BioLegend) in PBS containing 2% FBS. All antibodies were used in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Two independent FACS experiments were performed. All FACS experiments were performed on CytoFLEX SRT (Beckman Coulter) and the results were analyzed by Kaluza software v2.1.

Apoptosis analysis

Apoptosis was evaluated using the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (15342-54; Nacalai Tesque), which includes an Annexin V-FITC conjugate and propidium iodide (PI), in accordance with the protocol provided by the manufacturer, and was further analyzed using FCM.

Senescence analysis

Senescence was assessed by β-gal staining. β-gal senescence assay was performed using Senescence Cells Histochemical Staining Kit (CS0030; Sigma), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, with the staining of cells for 8 h. The staining was visualized and captured using an optical microscope. In five randomly selected fields of each dish, stained and unstained cells were counted to analyze the proportion of SA-b-gal-positive cells.
