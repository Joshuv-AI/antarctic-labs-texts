Cardiac Undifferentiated Pleomorphic Sarcoma with Hemorrhagic Changes, Mimicking Benign Myxoma: A Case Report
Authors: Da-Kyong Lee, Hwan-Seok Yong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
When diagnosing cardiac tumors, it is important to consider factors such as the patient’s age, tumor location, radiologic features, differentiation between benign and malignant tumors and to provide the surgeon with appropriate information. In this report, we present a case of two cardiac masses originating in the left atrium that were diagnosed as undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcomas through surgical resection. Imaging studies showed two masses attached to the posterior wall of the left atrium and mitral valve; hemorrhagic changes were suspected in these masses. Irregular thickening of the mitral valve suggests tumor invasion; however, the possibility of a mural thrombus should be excluded. Multiplicity, large size, and irregular mitral valve thickening suggest a malignancy. However, given the much higher incidence of myxomas, well-defined margins, and the fact that hemorrhagic changes can occur in large myxomas, the possibility of a benign tumor cannot be ruled out.

INTRODUCTION

Primary cardiac masses are rare and challenging to diagnose, with nearly 75% of them classified as benign (1). The incidence of primary cardiac tumors in autopsy studies is very low, ranging from 0.001% to 0.03%, and approximately 25% of these are malignant, with sarcomas being the predominant histological subtype (23). Unlike secondary tumors, which account for most cardiac masses, primary cardiac tumors present unique diagnostic challenges. When faced with a cardiac mass, factors beyond the rarity of the tumor must be considered, with emphasis on location, patient age, and radiological features.

Here, we present a case of two cardiac masses originating in the left atrium that were diagnosed as undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcomas through surgical resection to relieve heart failure. On imaging, the two masses were attached to the posterior wall of the left atrium and mitral valve, and hemorrhagic changes were suspected inside the masses. Although the imaging findings suggested malignant tumors such as sarcoma, differentiation from myxoma was necessary.

CASE REPORT

A 51-year-old female patient visited our hospital’s emergency room because of shortness of breath that had started a month prior and had gradually worsened over the past 10 days. She also had a history of hypertension. Upon arrival, the blood pressure was 149/97 mmHg, the heart rate was 71 beats/min, and the oxygen saturation was 97% on room air. In particular, laboratory results showed elevated creatine kinase myocardial band levels at 29.50 ng/mL (normal range: up to 3.51 ng/mL) and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide levels at 1,200 pg/mL (normal range: up to 121 pg/mL).

The initial chest radiography revealed an enlarged heart with increased pulmonary vascular markings. Echocardiography revealed two amorphous masses in the left atrium, resulting in severe mitral stenosis and left atrial dilatation (Fig. 1A). One appeared as a heterogeneous hypoechoic mass within the left atrium, whereas the other appeared as a heterogeneous echogenic mass attached to the mitral valve. Cardiac CT and MRI were performed to further evaluate the left atrial masses. Cardiac CT revealed two well-defined low-density masses in the left atrium (Fig. 1B). In the vertical long-axis view, one mass was located posteriorly within the left atrium and attached to the atrial wall, and the other was located anteriorly and attached to the mitral valve, extending into the atrial orifice and left atrial appendage. Cardiac MRI revealed tissue characteristics of these cardiac masses. On the vertical long-axis cine image, the posteriorly located mass within the left atrium appeared as a large cystic mass with irregular wall thickness, whereas the anteriorly located mass attached to the mitral valve showed slightly higher signal intensity than the myocardium (Fig. 1C). On the short-axis T1-weighted image, the inside of the cystic mass showed a high signal intensity, which was thought to be a hemorrhagic change (Fig. 1D). In a short-axis delayed enhancement study performed 10 minutes after contrast injection, each mass showed enhancement mainly in the area attached to the atrial wall (Fig. 1E). These masses appeared separate from each other and one was attached to the atrial wall and the other to the mitral valve. Irregular thickening of the anterior leaflet of the mitral valve suggested tumor invasion; however, the possibility of mural thrombi should also be excluded. The main radiological differential diagnosis was multiple malignant cardiac tumors, such as angiosarcoma with hemorrhagic changes; however, the possibility of a benign tumor, such as myxoma, could not be ruled out.

The masses caused mitral valve obstruction and worsening heart failure symptoms. Therefore, surgical resection was performed to relieve the symptoms. On surgical examination, the hemorrhagic mass was widely attached to the posterior inferior wall of the left atrium, and another mass appeared to have invaded the anterolateral commissure and leaflets of the mitral valve. Each mass was 5 cm in size and appeared as two lumps stacked on a single thin membrane. Gross specimens showed internal hemorrhagic changes consistent with the imaging findings (Fig. 1F). These masses were histopathologically confirmed as undifferentiated cardiac pleomorphic sarcomas without definite cellular differentiation (Fig. 1G).

According to our institutional policy, ethical approval was not required for this case report.

DISCUSSION

Most primary cardiac tumors originating from the pericardium or myocardium are benign, and nearly half of the benign tumors are myxomas (4). Among malignant tumors, which account for approximately 25% of primary cardiac tumors, 95% are sarcomas, and the most common primary cardiac malignancies are soft tissue sarcomas, including angiosarcoma, undifferentiated sarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, and fibrosarcoma (5).

To suggest a radiological diagnosis, it is essential to consider various characteristics such as multiplicity and location. Benign tumors such as myxomas and rhabdomyomas may be multiple in number. Cardiac tumors show a preference for specific locations. Myxomas, some sarcomas, and thrombi commonly occur within the left atrium, as observed in our case. Cardiac sarcoma is a rare malignant tumor arising from the mesenchymal tissue of the heart. Radiological features can vary, and a combination of imaging modalities is often required for an accurate diagnosis. Cardiac sarcomas can occur in any part of the heart and appear as infiltrative masses with irregular borders on imaging.

Compared with CT, MRI better demonstrates the tissue characteristics of cardiac masses and is therefore useful for differentiating cardiac tumors. Undifferentiated cardiac sarcomas typically show moderate enhancement, non-specific signal intensity on T1-weighted images, and bright signal intensity on T2-weighted images (4). However, similar findings may be observed in other primary cardiac malignancies, making a specific differential diagnosis difficult. A recent study showed that approximately 24% of primary cardiac malignancies are represented by undifferentiated sarcomas (6). Several imaging findings suggested cardiac undifferentiated sarcoma. These tumors may appear as discrete masses or as infiltrative and irregular lesions, often accompanied by necrosis and hemorrhage (7). In addition, previously reported features distinguishing left atrial sarcomas from benign myxomas include: 1) nonseptal origin, 2) extension into the pulmonary vein, 3) multiple masses, 4) broad attachment to the left atrial wall, and 5) semisolid consistency (8).

In our case, multiplicity, nonseptal origin, broad attachment to the left atrial wall, and possible mitral valve invasion suggested malignancy. However, the possibility of a myxoma with hemorrhagic change could not be excluded because 1) myxomas are far more common, 2) the masses are relatively well-circumscribed, 3) mural thrombus involving the mitral valve cannot be ruled out, and 4) internal hemorrhagic changes may also occur in large myxomas. Although imaging studies suggested possible invasion of the mitral valve and the adjacent left atrial wall, the full extent of tumor infiltration was underestimated preoperatively. Surgical findings demonstrated extensive infiltration involving a wide area of the left atrial wall and mitral valve, beyond that observed on imaging. A PET scan might have been useful for further evaluation of malignancy; however, the patient’s symptoms rapidly worsened, preventing additional investigations.

In summary, we describe a case of left atrial undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma with hemorrhagic changes mimicking benign myxoma. Although several imaging findings, including multiplicity, broad left atrial attachment, and possible mitral valve invasion, suggest malignancy, its differentiation from myxoma remains challenging because of its relatively well-circumscribed appearance and associated hemorrhagic changes. This case highlights that even when imaging demonstrates suspicious malignant features, undifferentiated cardiac pleomorphic sarcoma may closely mimic benign myxoma, and the extent of tumor infiltration may be underestimated preoperatively. Therefore, careful assessment of subtle invasive imaging features and surgical planning based on the possibility of extensive infiltration are important when evaluating large left atrial masses.
