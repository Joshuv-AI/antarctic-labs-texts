Effect of intravitreal injection on optic nerve in infants with retinopathy of prematurity: a long-term follow-up study
Authors: Jianbo Mao, Xinyi Deng, Yijing Chen, Shian Zhang, Hanxiao Li, Xiaoya Zhang, Ziyi Xiang, Jiajun Zhu, Yiqi Chen, Li-Jun Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo investigate the effect of intravitreal injection (IVI) on the optic nerve in infants with retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) during long-term follow-up.MethodsThe preterm infants included were divided into control, untreated and treated groups. The treatment group was further divided into the plus disease group and the no plus disease group. The vertical cup-to-disc ratio (C/D) was measured and compared in fundus photography at 36 weeks to 6 months of postmenstrual age (PMA) in the three groups of infants, respectively.ResultsA total of 381 eyes were included in this study. As the PMA passed from 36 weeks to 6 months, the C/Ds of the infants in all three groups increased to varying degrees, in which those of the treated group were significantly larger than those of the untreated group and the control group at all follow-up periods except at 36 weeks of PMA. In addition, in the treatment group, the C/D was significantly lower in the plus disease group than in the no-plus disease group, except at 6 months of PMA.ConclusionsThe present study found that IVI for ROP would result in an enlargement of the C/D in prematurity. The presence of plus disease was associated with an underestimation of the C/D. And the C/D in preterm infants continued to increase to varying degrees in at least 6 months of PMA.

Introduction

In recent years, with the improvement of preterm neonate care techniques, an increasing number of very preterm and very low-birth-weight infants have survived. The incidence and severity of retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) are also elevated. ROP is the leading cause of preventable blindness in children in China. A 12-year study in northwest China revealed that the prevalence of ROP and severe ROP had reached 16.16% and 5.63%, respectively.1

The treatment of ROP has evolved from cryotherapy to laser photocoagulation and has proven to be safe and effective. The pathologic process of ROP proceeds through the first phase of relatively hyperoxic vascular obliteration (postnatal to approximately 30 weeks of postmenstrual age (PMA)) and a second phase of vaso-proliferation in a relatively hypoxic environment (30 to 42 weeks of PMA). Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is downregulated during the first phase and over-regulated during the second phase, resulting in stagnation of vasculogenesis within the normal retina and, conversely, the generation of pathologic neovascularisation at the junction of the avascular zone and the vascular retina.2 Therefore, intravitreal injection (IVI) of anti-VEGF has become an effective treatment for ROP in recent years.3 4 Compared with laser treatment, anti-VEGF therapy has a lower risk of causing myopia and reduces visual field loss.5 However, VEGF serves as an important regulator during organogenesis in preterm infants, so the long-term adverse effects of ocular anti-VEGF therapy on growth and development should be cautiously evaluated. In addition, IVI has been observed to result in increased intraocular pressure (IOP) in infants.6 However, whether the persistent IOP elevation in infants associated with IVI may further lead to glaucomatous optic nerve damage in the long term has not been studied.

The cup-to-disc ratio (C/D) is generally acknowledged to be an important indicator for assessing optic nerve damage. An increased C/D is usually associated with retinal ganglion cell loss and the thinning of the lamina cribrosa.7 During embryonic optic nerve development, degeneration and atrophy of the glial cells as well as the vitreous arteries form the physiologic depression. The degree of atrophy determines the depth and size of the physiologic depression of the optic cup. Anatomical studies of the optic nerve in human embryos of different gestational ages (GA) found that the development of the optic nerve was exponential, with 50% of the optic nerve completed at 20 weeks of gestation, 75% at birth, 95% by 1 year of age8 and 70% of the axons having degenerated by 33 weeks of gestation.9 However, interference with physiologic ganglion cell axonal regression by prematurity leads to excessive axonal clearance, therefore increased optic cup depression and optic cup area.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the growth and developmental trends of postnatal C/D in preterm infants, as well as the changes in C/D in ROP and ROP infants who underwent IVI, hoping to provide insights for subsequent ROP clinical management and treatment.

Materials and methods

Participants

Preterm infants who attended Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital and the Women’s Hospital School of Medicine Zhejiang University from January 2022 to August 2023 were included in this study. For infants with bilateral ROP, the more severely affected eye was uniformly included in the study. The gender, GA, birth weight (BW) and oxygen inhalation history of the infants were recorded. The groups were divided into treated, untreated and control groups according to whether they had ROP and whether they received IVI. Infants who received only one IVI of medication were included in this study. The control group were infants born prematurely and in whom ROP was not detected during routine infant fundus examination. Based on the presence or absence of plus disease, the treated group was further divided into the no plus disease group and the plus group. Plus diseases were distinguished by senior paediatric ophthalmologists with favourable reproducibility and preplus was incorporated into the group of no plus disease.

Exclusion criteria were vitreous haemorrhage, history of retinal laser photocoagulation, or any opacity of the refractive media that may affect the image quality. Preterm babies with serious life-threatening comorbidities were also excluded. Other associated comorbidities were not recorded in the preterm infants who participated in the study.

Fundus imaging and optic nerve head analysis

All the infants received the first standard contact wide-angle fundus imaging after pupil dilatation. Infants in the treatment group received their first fundus screen within 3 days before receiving an IVI. Fundus screening results in the three groups were collected at 36 weeks, 40 weeks, 44 weeks, 48 weeks, 52 weeks and 6 months of PMA. Although no ROP was detected, the preterm infants in the control group were born at a small GA and their vascular development was not yet complete. In order to monitor the process of retinal vascular development and the development of ROP (if any), follow-up examinations were performed until the retina is fully vascularised, usually at 45 weeks of PMA. Due to the large number of preterm infants we were screening, some infants who were still not fully vascularised were followed up until 6 months PMA.

The fundus imaging size was 1600×1200 pixels with a 130-degree fundus angle. Posterior fundus images with clear optic disc were collected in the analysis (representative images were shown in figure 1). The vertical lengths of the optic cups and discs, which were more frequently evaluated clinically for glaucoma and less affected by optic nerve tilt,10 were measured independently by two physicians who were blinded to group assignment three times using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA), and the mean C/D was then calculated. Intravitreal injection

Infants in the treatment group underwent the standard surgical procedure of IVI. Surface anaesthesia was first administered using proparacaine hydrochloride eye drops to the operating eye. Next, the skin around the eye was sterilised using 10% povidone-iodine. Next, the eyelid is held open with an eye speculum, and the conjunctival sac is flushed with 0.1% povidone-iodine, followed by 0.9% saline. A 30-gauge needle was used for injection. The dose of injection was 0.25 mg/0.025 mL. Injections were administered 1.5 mm posterior to the corneal limbus. After the injection, ofloxacin eye ointment was used three times a day for 3 days to prevent postoperative infection. All IVI procedures were performed by the same ophthalmologist.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (V.26.0, SPSS Inc, Chicago, Illinois, USA). All data were presented in the form of means±SD. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants’ demographic and baseline characteristics

A total of 381 eyes were recruited in this study (201 male and 180 female). The mean GA was 28.8±2.5 weeks (range from 24 to 36 weeks). The mean BW was 1158.49±337.53 g (range from 500 to 2170 g). GA and BW were both significantly different among the three groups (both p<0.001). GA and BW were significantly lower in the treated group than in the untreated and control groups. The proportion of oxygen inhalation was significantly higher in the ROP group than in the control group (table 1). In the treated group, there were 16 eyes with zone 1 stage 2 (20.25%), 10 eyes with zone 1 stage 3 (12.66%) and 53 eyes with zone 2 stage 3 (67.09%). In the untreated group, there were 34 eyes with zone 2 stage 1 (20.73%), 103 eyes with zone 2 stage 2 (12.66%) and 24 eyes with zone 3 stage 1 (67.09%).

Discussion

By analysing wide-field fundus images of prematurity at different times of PMA over a 6-month period, this study found that among all three groups of preterm infants, the C/D was significantly larger in the IVI treatment group. The C/D was enlarged in varying degrees from 36 weeks to 6 months of PMA in three groups. In the treatment group, the C/D was lower in the plus disease group compared with the no plus disease group. The baseline C/D did not correlate with GA and BW.

First, ROP infants treated with IVIs had a larger C/D. In adult IVI, the elevated IOP after injection is one of the common complications. Even transient postoperative elevation of IOP may cause damage to the optic nerve. Shah et al reported a case of a patient who underwent monthly IVIs in the single eye, in which the treated eye presented with a significant enlargement of the C/D, with a thinning of the Retinal Nerve Fiber Layer (RNFL) inferiorly and a corresponding decrease in the visual field superiorly 9 months after the last injection.11 It was considered to be a cumulative effect secondary to transient IOP elevation after IVI. However, infants have a lower IOP and are thus less tolerant of elevated IOP than adults. In addition, infants with ROP often have a combination of abnormal anterior segment anatomy and inadequate development of the trabecular meshwork and are incapable of tolerating acute IOP elevation.12 In our clinical practice with infants who underwent IVI for the treatment of ROP, it is possible to observe haze of the cornea due to oedema after the injection. Although IOP was not measured in the children after the IVI, bulbous palpation IOP commonly reached T+2 or even +3. Gao et al evaluated the effect of IVI on IOP in infants, and they found that IOP increased dramatically to nearly 50 mmHg within 2 min after injection, decreased to less than 30 mmHg an hour later and returned to baseline levels on the first postoperative day.6 In the present study, the ROP infants in the treatment group had significantly larger C/Ds, possibly derived from the effect of IVI-induced IOP elevation.

A variety of factors have an impact on the IOP after injection. Elevated IOP was correlated with the number of injections, the amount of medication injected and the pressure of the injection.13 Infant eyes receiving IVIs may have a lower tolerance for elevated IOP. There is a linear relationship between the biological rigidity of the eye and the injection volume.14 Studies have been performed to compare the therapeutic effects of different doses of anti-VEGF IVI on ROP and postoperative IOP elevation. Separate studies comparing doses of 0.0125 and 0.025 mL, and 0.025 and 0.03 mL, respectively, confirmed that ROP recurrence rates were reduced in the high-dose treatment group,15 but IOP elevation was more pronounced in the high-dose group.16 Furthermore, the pressure at which the injection is administered, as well as the cumulative number of injections, could contribute to postoperative IOP elevation.13 Therefore, to alleviate the enlargement of the C/D and the optic nerve damage associated with the elevated IOP after injection, we recommend the use of low-dose, high-concentration anti-VEGF drug formulations for the treatment of ROP.

Second, all three groups of infants showed different increases in C/Ds through time. The results of previous studies of C/D in preterm infants have presented conflicting conclusions. Samarawickrama and colleagues analysed optical coherence tomography (OCT) optic disc scans from over 2 000 12-year-olds and concluded that lower BW was associated with a larger C/D.17 Fieß et al retrospectively evaluated fundus photography in adults aged from 18 to 52 years and suggested that extremely low GA, untreated ROP infants have an enlarged C/D, whereas retinal laser treatment would lead to a reduction of the C/D.18 Comparing OCT scans of preterm versus term children between the ages from 4 to 14 years, Park et al found that preterm children had a reduced thickness of the RNFL and that ROP severity was correlated with reduced nasal RNFL.19 The difference in findings may be due to the fact that all the above-mentioned studies were retrospective and conducted on adults and children participants of different ages. Consistent with the present study, fundus photography studies of preterm infants at 36 weeks PMA revealed that optic disc area, disc rim area, vertical C/D and cup-to-disc area ratio were associated with GA and BW.20 For perinatal histopathologic studies, excessive apoptosis of axons in preterm infants leads to an increase in the optic cup area. Analysis of C/Ds in preterm infants quantified by OCT scan indicated an increase in vertical optic cup diameter as well as vertical C/D in preterm infants compared with term infants, and an increase in vertical optic cup diameter in preterm infants from 31 to 36 weeks to 37–42 weeks of PMA.21

Next, in our study, BW and GA were lower in the ROP group than in the control group, and the proportion of oxygen inhalation was higher than in the control group. BW, GA and fluctuations in oxygen saturation during the neonatal period are well-recognised risk factors for ROP.22 However, in several neonatal care centres where preterm nursing care is more technologically advanced, strict oxygen management has contributed to decreasing the severity and incidence of ROP.23 24 In this study, GA and BW were not related to the baseline C/D at 36 weeks of PMA. Several retrospective studies in children and adults have shown that low GA and BW may lead to an increased C/D.17 18 With regard to the composition of severity and stages between the two groups of ROP, although no specific epidemiological data on the zone and staging of ROP were queried, a 12-year screening study from China showed that stage 2 accounted for about 70% of total ROP. In contrast, stage 3 predominates in severe ROP.1 This is consistent with the composition ratio of our study.

Finally, in our study, ROP infants with plus disease had a smaller C/D, which could be the result of the tortuosity and dilatation of retinal vasculature in the optic disc region masking and underestimating the C/D.

The limitation of this study is that the evaluation of the C/D in fundus photography may result in measurement inaccuracy. Second, infants who underwent multiple IVIs were not included in this study due to sample size limitations. Further studies comparing the difference in impact of multiple versus single injections should be conducted in follow-up. In addition, the brain developmental status and lesions of preterm infants were not taken into account, although it has been suggested that hypoxic-ischaemic brain injury can lead to an increase in the C/D.25 Nevertheless, there has been no systematic study of the effect of comorbidities related to prematurity on the development of C/D. This is not only a limitation of this study but also a direction for our future research.

In conclusion, by comparing the C/D in fundus photography in infants with ROP who were treated with IVI with untreated ROP and infants free of ROP, this study suggests that IVI may cause an enlargement of the C/D in infants with ROP that persists at least until 6 months of PMA. The effects of different doses of IVI on IOP, C/D and optic nerve should be further investigated in follow-up.
