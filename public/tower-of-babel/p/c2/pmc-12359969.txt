Global, regional, and national burden of colorectal cancer associated with diet high in red meat, 1990 to 2021: an analysis for the global burden of disease study and prediction to 2036
Authors: Huijun Lei, Miaomiao Chen, Haoyu Qu, Zuomei He, Hui Zhong, Liang Li, Mengzhou Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the global, regional, and national burden of colorectal cancer (CRC) attributable to diets high in red meat from 1990 to 2021, project trends to 2036, and stratify findings by age, sex, and Sociodemographic Index (SDI).MethodsData on CRC burden associated with diet high in red meat were extracted from the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx). Age-standardized rates (ASR) and estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) were calculated to assess temporal trends. Inequality slope and concentration indices quantified health disparities, while decomposition analyses identified drivers of burden changes. A Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model projected future trends through 2036.ResultsFrom 1990 to 2021, age-standardized mortality rates (EAPC = − 0.84%, 95% CI: − 0.87 to − 0.81) and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) (EAPC = − 0.85, 95% CI: − 0.88 to − 0.81) for CRC attributable to high red meat intake declined globally. The reduction in DALYs was driven by decreasing years of life lost (YLLs; EAPC = − 0.9, 95% CI: − 0.93 to − 0.86), contrasting with rising years lived with disability (YLDs; EAPC = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.41–0.48). Males and older populations bore a disproportionate burden. Global population growth accounted for 99.73% of increased deaths, 112.77% of DALYs, 62.42% of YLDs, and 116.5% of YLLs. Inequality slope indices for mortality remained stable, while concentration indices for DALYs declined (deaths: 0.31 [95% CI: 0.28–0.34] to 0.26 [0.23–0.29]). Projections indicate declining age-standardized mortality and DALY rates by 2036, but absolute deaths, DALYs, and YLLs are expected to rise.ConclusionDespite declining age-standardized rates, the absolute burden of CRC associated with diet high in red meat persists, with marked disparities across regions and demographics. Population growth remains a critical driver, underscoring the need for targeted dietary interventions and equity-focused policies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-23712-1.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Red meat, Global burden of disease, Disability-adjusted life years, Health inequality, Decomposition

Introduction

Globally, colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most common malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality across both sexes, with approximately 1.93 million new cases and 903,859 deaths recorded in 2022 [1]. Studies have shown that 90% of CRCs are adenocarcinomas, which are malignant tumours that develop from adenepithelial cells of the colon and rectum. Other rare types include squamous cell carcinomas, adenosquamous carcinomas, spindle cell carcinomas, and undifferentiated carcinomas [2]. The disease typically progresses through a prolonged carcinogenic sequence, evolving from precancerous lesions to invasive carcinoma over 5–10 years, often remaining asymptomatic until advanced stages [3]. Established risk stratification incorporates both non-modifiable factors (age, male gender, hereditary syndromes) and modifiable determinants (obesity, physical inactivity, alcohol consumption, smoking) [2].

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) defines unprocessed mammalian muscle, including beef, veal, pork, lamb, horse or goat meat, as red meat [4]. Diet high in red meat is defined as intake above an average of 100 g per day (95% UI 0–200) of unprocessed red meat [5], which is strongly associated with CRC risk, supported by epidemiological [6, 7] and prospective studies [8, 9]. The findings indicate that elevated levels of economic development and the adoption of a Western lifestyle may be associated with an increased incidence of CRC [10]. Additionally, the Western diet, which is characterised by a high intake of heme-rich red meat, may increase the risk of CRC [11, 12]. Religious dietary practices and intensive livestock farming jointly drive differential red meat diets, exacerbating CRC burdens. Given that the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classification of red meat as “probably carcinogenic to humans” (group 2A) [4], the reduction of CRC risk through dietary and lifestyle modifications has become an accepted preventive strategy [13].

Upon synthesizing the latest research advances in CRC, we observe that most studies utilizing the GBD database focus primarily on comprehensive analyses of multiple risk factors [14, 15] or investigate CRC relationships with individual risk factors beyond high red meat consumption [16–18]. Other research concentrates on CRC within specific regions, countries, or age groups [19–24]. While existing investigations into diet high in red meat and CRC emphazise cross-sectional carcinogenic analyses [25–28], a gap persists in examining 30-year disease burden evolution and future projections.

This study systematically tracks global trends from 1990 to 2021 using GBD data, extends projections to 2036 based on existing CRC DALYs estimates linked to diets high in red meat [29], and reveals the emergent'scissors gap' phenomenon characterized by declining age-standardized rates alongside rising absolute burdens. This establishes baseline data for evaluating dietary intervention efficacy over time.Contrary to prior studies simplistically attributing CRC incidence to red meat intake, our Das Gupta decomposition model quantifies differential mortality trend contributors. Results identify population growth as the predominant driver, aligning with the 2025 Global Cancer Report's conclusions on East Asian ageing exacerbating CRC burdens [18]. Current assessments frequently neglect socioeconomic gradients in diet-related CRC disparities. Applying SII and RCI indices demonstrates divergent trends: decreasing burdens in high-SDI regions versus increasing burdens in low-SDI regions [29]. This research innovatively analyses red meat-associated CRC burden by incorporating both YLDs and YLLs to holistically evaluate prevention strategies. The dual reduction of YLLs and YLDs could enhance CRC control comprehensively, advancing the "prioritising prevention over treatment" public health goal.A comprehensive 2021 GBD analysis evaluates temporal trends in red meat-related CRC mortality, DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs globally, regionally, and nationally, stratified by gender, age, and SDI. Findings provide actionable insights for researchers.

Materials and methods

Data source and frameworks

This study derive estimates of CRC attributable to diet high in red meat from the Global Health Data Exchange tool( http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool). In terms of data inclusion, we first selected'high red meat intake' from the list of risk factors and then'colorectal cancer (CRC)' from the list of causes. GBD 2021,which is a database of studies designed to quantify age- and sex-specific health losses for 371 diseases and injuries and 88 risk factors in 204 countries and territories [30]. DALYs were estimated from 100,983 data sources (including 19,189 new sources used in 2021) [31]. And according to similar geographic location, epidemiology, the 204 countries and territories, GBD 2021 are further classified into 21 regions and 7 super-regions. The SDI is a comprehensive indicator used to measure the development status of a country or region [32]. Based on the SDI, all countries and territories were stratifies into five quintiles, including Low SDI (≤ 0.4658), Low-middle SDI (0.4658–0.6188), Middle SDI (0.6188–0.7120), High-middle SDI (0.7120–0.8103), and High SDI (> 0.8103) [33]. In order to visualise the 2021 SDI-ASR relationships across regions and countries, scatter plots were constructed [34]. The'geom_smooth' function from the'ggplot2' package in R was utilised to fit a locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) trend line, with the method parameter set to'loess' [35]. In addition, all patients were stratified by sex into male and female categories and by age into 15 groups (5-year intervals, between the ages of 25 and ≥ 95 years).In GBD 2021, the International Classification of Diseases-10 (ICD-10) codes for CRC are C18–C21 [1].

Definitions

Death estimates were obtained from vital disease registries, verbal autopsies, surveys, censuses, surveillance systems, cancer registries, police records, etc. [36]. During data processing, any data points that were significantly higher than expected, inconsistent with the time series, or unable to be effectively decomposed were identified as outliers and treated accordingly. Briefly, we estimated the number of deaths from CRC associated with diet high in red meat using the Cause-of-Death Integrated Model (CODEm) [37]. This study was conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Reporting of Health Estimates (GATHER) [38].

The DALY is a comprehensive metric used to quantify health loss from premature mortality and disability, serving as a key indicator for evaluating disease burden. One DALY represents the loss of one year of healthy life [39]. This metric reflects the potential reduction in disease burden that could occur if population-level exposure to a specific risk factor is modified.

YLDs measure the number of years individuals live with disabilities caused by conditions such as cancer, calculated through microsimulation modeling [40]. Conversely, YLLs quantify the years lost due to premature death from a disease. DALYs are calculated as the sum of YLDs and YLLs [41].

ASR is defined as the rate calculated using the age composition of a standard population [42]. To adjust for confounding from demographic shifts and variations in age distribution, all rates are expressed as age-standardized rates (per 100,000 population) based on the GBD reference population. This approach enables comparisons of CRC burden across sex, age groups, years, locations, and SDI.

The absolute numbers, ASRs, and percentage changes (with 95% uncertainty intervals, UIs) of CRC burden attributable to diet high in red meat were used to assess current trends and status. UIs were generated for every metric using the 25th and 975th ordered 1000 draw values of the posterior distribution.The GBD database employs DisMod-MR 2.1 (a Bayesian meta-regression tool) to generate over 1,000 estimates [43]. In all analyses, statistical significance was defined as a 95% UI excluding zero.

Health inequality

To assess health inequalities associated with socioeconomic development, we used Lorenz curves to calculate the slope index of inequality (SII) and relative concentration index (RCI) for deaths, DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs of CRC attributable to high red meat diets across SDI groups from 1990 to 2021. These indices measure absolute and relative health inequalities, respectively. The SII quantifies the absolute difference in burden between the highest and lowest SDI groups, while the RCI evaluates whether the CRC burden is disproportionately concentrated in countries with high or low financial development levels. An RCI of 0 indicates no inequality; a positive value signifies concentration in high-financial-development countries, and a negative value indicates concentration in low-financial-development countries [44, 45].

Decomposition analyses

The relative contribution of changes in age structure, population size, and epidemiology to the global burden of CRC associated with diet high in red meat from 1990 to 2021 was quantified using Das Gupta’s decomposition [46]. Changes in age structure primarily reflect population ageing, changes in population size are associated with changes in population size, and epidemiological changes are associated with age- and population-standardized morbidity and mortality rates [47].

Prediction

The BAPC model was used to predict the ASR of CRC burden associated with a diet high in red for both sexes from 2021 to 2036 [48]. Given its demonstrated accuracy in forecasting disease burden, we applied the BAPC model via integrated nested Laplace approximation (using R packages BAPC) to project ASRs of deaths, DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs [49].

Statistical analyses

The estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) in ASR of CRC associated with diet high in red meat was calculated as EAPC = 100 × (exp(β)-1) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) was also obtained from the linear regression model. The EAPC describes the trend in ASR over a given time interval. When both the EAPC and the lower limit of the 95% CI are positive, the ASR is observed to increase. When the EAPC and the lower limit of the 95% CI are negative, the ASR is observed to decrease [50]. Otherwise, the burden of CRC caused by diet high in red meat is regarded as stable. Analyses were completed using R 4.3.3 and STATA 18.0 with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 (two-sided).

CRC burden by regions and countries associated with diet high in red meat

We assessed the burden of CRC associated with a diet high in red meat across 7 GBD super regions. In 2021, Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia were the regions with the highest ASR of deaths (2.87 [95% UI = -0.0009 to 5.69] per 100,000), ASDR (65.82 [95% UI = -0.02 to 129.88] per 100,000), and ASSR (63.63 [95% UI = -0.02 to 125.61] per 100,000) for CRC burden attributable to diet high in red meat. High-income regions had the highest ASYR (3.52 [95% UI = -0.0016 to 7.62] per 100,000), while South Asia had the lowest ASR across all burden metrics (Supplementary Fig. 1.B; Tab S4). In terms of the number of CRC burden associated with a diet high in red meat, Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia had the highest number of deaths and YLDs, whereas Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania had the highest number of DALYs and YLLs. The lowest numbers of deaths, DALYs, and YLLs were observed in South Asia, while the lowest YLDs were recorded in Sub-Saharan Africa (Supplementary Fig. 1.A; Tab S4) (Figure 1).

CRC burden by sex and age associated with diet high in red meat

Globally, men were more affected by CRC than women in 2021 associated with diet high in red meat (Supplementary Fig. 2). By age group, both the number of deaths and DALYs globally increased with age, peaking in the 70–74 age group (21,867.24 [95% UI = -6.96 to 44,918.95]), after which the number of deaths declined. For DALYs, the 65–69 age group exhibited the highest global burden (500,321.92 [95% UI = -210.05 to 1,009,752.17] per 100,000) (Supplementary Fig. 3.A, Tab S5). Globally, the ASR of deaths, DALYs, YLDs, and YLLs for CRC burden associated with diet high in red meat increased with age, particularly in groups aged 65 years and older, with the decline in ASDR primarily attributed to ASSR (Supplementary Fig. 3.B).

Between 1990 and 2021, the global number of CRC deaths associated with diet high in red meat grew significantly among individuals aged 55 to 89 years. In contrast, DALYs increased markedly in the 50–84 age group; the trend of YLLs across all ages aligned closely with DALYs. YLDs rose sharply in the 65–74 age group, while the most stable burden counts were observed in the 25–29 age group (Fig. 3A). The ASR trend for CRC burden attributable to diet high in red meat shifted from stable to increasing with age, with the most variable changes and highest ASRs occurring in the 75 + age group (Fig. 3B).

Trends by SDI in the burden of CRC associated with diet high in red meat

Between 1990 and 2021, the number of deaths, DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs of CRC attributed to diet high in red meat increased annually in four of the five SDI regions, except for the low SDI region where the burden stabilized. In the low SDI region, YLLs attribute to a higher proportion of DALYs. The rate of CRC burden increase significantly greater in middle and low SDI regions compared to high and high-middle SDI regions (Fig. 4A). ASR of deaths, DALYs, YLDs, and YLLs showed decreasing trends in high and high-middle SDI regions (Fig. 4B). Notably, the ASR of deaths (ASDR) declined more sharply in high SDI regions (EAPC = -1.29; 95% CI: -1.32 to -1.25) than in high-middle SDI regions (EAPC = -0.71; 95% CI: -0.76 to -0.66). This decline in ASDR was primarily attributed to reduction in the high SDI region (EAPC = -1.36; 95% CI: -1.39 to -1.33), while the ASR of ASYR remained stable (EAPC = 0.02; 95% CI: -0.08 to 0.11). In contrast, low-middle SDI region experienced annual increases in ASR of deaths, DALYs, and YLLs, whereas middle SDI region showed a flattened trend in overall CRC burdens attributed to diet high in red meat (Fig. 4B). By 2021, high SDI region recorded the highest absolute numbers of deaths (52,511 [95% UI: -20 to 106,389]) and YLDs (69,642 [95% UI: -29 to 149,098]), while high-middle SDI region had the highest DALYs (1,111,311 [95% UI: -437 to 2,256,530] per 100,000) and YLLs (1,059,051 [95% UI: -418 to 2,160,430] per 100,000). The lowest CRC burden metrics were consistently observed in low SDI region. However, the highest ASR for CRC burden attributed to diet high in red meat were concentrated in high-middle SDI region, except for the highest ASDR, which occurred in high SDI regions (Tables 1 and 2; Supplementary Tab S1, S2).

Decomposition analyses

Globally, the overall number of deaths, DALYs, YLDs and YLLs is increasing.For YLLs, the overall difference was greatest in the middle SDI region, followed by East Asia. Globally, population growth was the largest contributor to the increase in burden, accounting for 99.73%, 112.77%, 62.42% and 116.5% of the overall difference in deaths, DALYs, YLDs and YLLs, respectively. Among the five SDI regions, the population with YLDs values in the High SDI region accounted for the largest proportion of the overall-difference, 57.57%; epidemiological changes contributed the most to the reduction in deaths, DALYs and YLLs values in the High SDI region, accounting for -126.98%, -177.89% and -210.05% of the overall-difference, respectively.

In the remaining four SDI regions, population is the largest contributor to the overall difference in the values of DALYs, YLDs and YLLs, respectively. The largest increase in the overall difference among the 21 of these regions is in the East Asia. The main driver of the changes in the number of deaths, DALYs and YLLs in the East Asia is also the change in population size, which accounts for 57.27%, 79.57% and 72.63% of the overall difference. On the other hand, epidemiological changes were the main driver of YLDs in the region, accounting for 45.37% (Fig. 8, Tab S10,11,12,13).

Prediction

Figure 9 shows the changes in the number of CRC deaths, DALYs, YLDs and YLLs and their ASRs over the next 25 years associated with diet high in red meat. The projections show a convergence of temporal trends for males and females, but the burden of CRC and its ASR are higher for males than for females. For males, only the ASR for YLDs is trending upward, from 4.44 (95% UI = 3.16 to 5.72) to 4.37 (95% UI = 4.34 to 4.39) in 2036, whereas the ASR for all other burdens is expected to decline annually, with the reduction in DALYs largely associated with the decline in YLLs (Fig. 9, Tab S14).

Discussion

Our study analyzed the global burden of CRC associated with diet high in red meat from 1990 to 2021, stratified by time, age, sex, region, and SDI. The results show that global CRC ASR associated with diet high in red meat (including: deaths, DALYs and YLLs) has decreased, with a more pronounced downward trend compared to the GBD 2019 [29, 51]. Concurrently, the reduction in DALYs was mainly attributable to the declining trajectory of YLLs. These observations indicate that the diminished burden of premature mortality from CRC linked to diet high in red meat may signify early-stage efficacy of globally coordinated policy interventions. However, the global burden of CRC associated with diet high in red meat remains high and is projected to increase annually until 2036. These paradoxical situations can be explained by the following reasons.

The IARC classifies processed meat as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1), while categorizing red meat as probably carcinogenic (Group 2A) [4]. Potential mechanisms underlying this classification involve two distinct pathways: genotoxic effects from cooking-related compounds like heterocyclic amines (HCAs), nitroso compounds (NOCs), and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PACs), combined with metabolic interactions driven by nutrients naturally abundant in red meat such as heme iron, sulfur compounds, and choline derivatives [52–54]. To clarify the specific contribution of diet high in red meat to CRC risk, future studies should prioritize quantifying exposure thresholds through long-term dietary monitoring combined with biomarker validation.

Regional differences in dietary habits lead to differences in red meat intake, which in turn lead to an increase in the global burden of CRC associated with diet high in red meat. In 2021, Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia had the highest CRC ASR for deaths, DALYs, and YLLs, while Australasia had the highest ASYR. These regions follow a predominantly westernised dietary pattern with high red meat intake. The lowest ASR of CRC burden in South Asia is attributable to lower red meat consumption. Sri Lanka and Bangladesh exemplify this through minimal meat consumption rooted in nonkilling principles, effectively reducing disease risk [55]. Conversely, pastoral regions such as Uruguay and the Netherlands, where optimal grazing ecosystems support intensive red meat production systems, exhibit dietary patterns conducive to CRC risk [56].

Undoubtedly, sex and age heterogeneity with respect to red meat-rich diets also has an important influence on the increased burden of CRC. Our analyses suggest that the burden of CRC attributable to a diet high in red meat is greater in men than in women. Furthermore, projections show that the burden for both sexes will increase annually over the next 15 years, and that the burden for men will be higher than that for women. There may be several reasons for this. First, men consume more red meat than women, but the difference in the gender association does not seem to be associated with higher intake in men. It is therefore hypothesised that differences in obesity, smoking, hypertension and other risk factors between men and women contribute to higher male mortality, and that these factors further confound the association between red meat intake and disease [57, 58]. Secondly, gender differences may be influenced by differences in hormone levels, with women more susceptible to proximal tumours and men more susceptible to distal and rectal tumours [59]. Moreover, studies have indicated that estrogen exerts a protective effect against microsatellite instability (MSI), while estrogen deficiency in postmenopausal women heightens susceptibility to MSI-high colorectal malignancies. This emphasises the critical need for optimised diagnostic protocols and personalised therapeutic strategies for the management of colorectal cancer in ageing female populations [60]. The age distribution shows that the global burden of CRC associated with a diet high in red meat peaks between the ages of 65 and 70 years, and the age-standardized rates increase with age. However, some surveys have shown that older people have better dietary patterns than the young, mainly reflected in relatively lower consumption of unhealthy foods (e.g. unprocessed red meat, sugary drinks and trans fats). Paradoxically, the increased burden of CRC persists in older adults, possibly associated with age-related comorbidities and frailty-related amplification of mortality [42].This necessitates dual strategies: (1) integrating geriatric nutritional optimization with comorbidity management, and (2) enhancing CRC screening protocols within age-specific healthcare frameworks. Study also shows that in regions with longer life expectancies, diseases may place a greater burden on middle-aged and older populations, potentially shifting the age-related disease profile and masking the impact on younger groups. This dynamic highlights the role of life expectancy in understanding the distribution of CRC burden. Additionally, early-onset CRC mortality exhibits an upward trend and is possibly associated with dietary changes in adolescence characterised by excessive consumption of red meat and inadequate fibre intake [22, 61, 62]. These findings call for policy interventions targeting adolescent through evidence-based dietary guidelines and regulation of red meat consumption. Research indicates that most early-onset CRC patients are the primary breadwinners of their families, thus the disease imposes substantial psychological impacts on both patients and their households. A more critical observation is that, in comparison with traditional CRC patients, those with early-onset CRC typically exhibit a poorer prognosis and higher recurrence rates. This phenomenon may be ascribed to the under-diagnosis of early-onset CRC, given that CRC screening programmes in certain European countries typically commence at the age of 50. Consequently, this results in reduced early diagnosis rates for early-onset CRC in comparison to the overall rate of CRC diagnoses [14, 63]. Concurrently, comprehensive interventions are imperative: governments should not only focus on dietary improvements, such as increasing whole grain intake and reducing red meat consumption, but also prioritise effective prevention and management of hyperglycaemia and obesity. Future prevention strategies should emphasise synergistic effects among these risk factors.Meanwhile,the increase in early-onset CRC mortality and DALYs were less prominent than the increase incidence among highly developed countries and territories, which is possibly due to increased screening and surveillance in adults at risk of CRC and advancement in CRC management [64]. Countries with economic disadvantage tend to have suboptimal CRC outcomes due to constrained access to adequate healthcare infrastructure.Thus,international collaborations to address high case-fatality and underlying poor healthcare conditions in these countries are crucial.

With the exception of the SII for death, which remained unchanged, all of the SII and RCI magnitudes in 2021 were lower than those in 1990 for CRC attributed to diet high in red meat. This suggests that the global burden of CRC attributed to diet high in red meat in 2021 was concentrated in countries with a low SDI, comparing to 1990, although health inequalities among countries decreased to some extent.

Nearly all SII and RCI for childhood cancers attributable to high red meat consumption in 2021 were lower than in 1990. This suggests that health inequalities between countries had partially decreased by 2021 compared to 1990. However, exceptions included the SII for deaths, which remained unchanged, and the SII for YLDs, which increased. This suggests that, despite a global decline in the age-standardized burden of CRC, population growth and ageing have driven an increase in the absolute burden. The global CRC burden attributable to high red meat consumption remains concentrated in countries with a lower SDI. Specifically, low- and middle-SDI countries experienced rising absolute CRC burdens, while high-SDI countries saw declines in ASRs. This reflects a complex pattern of'persistent absolute disparities and localised improvements in relative disparities'.This phenomenon aligns with research on racial disparities in U.S. healthcare resources. Non-Hispanic Black (NHB) Americans have a higher incidence of CRC and worse survival rates than Non-Hispanic White (NHW) Americans. However, in integrated healthcare systems such as the Veterans Affairs (VA) system, the survival disadvantage experienced by Black Americans with CRC narrows considerably, highlighting that inequities in screening and access to care are central to racial survival disparities [65]. The stable SII for CRC-related YLDs highlights entrenched health inequalities and the need for stratified policies, data-driven interventions and global collaboration to reduce the disability burden gap between socioeconomic groups and achieve health equity and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Looking ahead, dietary interventions must be integrated with SDI-tailored policies, with a particular focus on addressing transition risks in middle-SDI countries and strengthening the prevention and control of early-onset CRC. Moreover, many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) face severe shortages of oncologists and diagnostic tools, delaying care for patients. Training healthcare providers in oncology and expanding diagnostic and treatment facilities, such as pathology laboratories and radiotherapy units, are critical to improving cancer health inequities [66].

The impact of the differential distribution of the SDI on the global burden of CRC associated with a diet high in red meat is extensive.

Firstly, the decrease in the ASR of deaths, DALYs for the global burden is mainly associated with the fact that the ASR of this burden in high SDI region has decreased sharply, and the downward trend of the ASR is more significant compared to the results of previous studies [29]. Our study also shows that the rapid decline in ASR in high SDI region is largely associated with the downward trend in ASSR. This suggests a reduction in the loss of life expectancy associated with premature death. This may be related to the fact that countries with a higher level of economic development are quicker to recognize the negative impact of red meat on CRC and to set targets to reduce red meat consumption for the whole population [56], by promoting CRC screening and improving diagnostic techniques [67]. The United States has been insured for CRC screening since 1998 and has a specialized Multi–Social Task Force (MSTF) for CRC [68]. Research indicates a potential decline in the risk of CRC in the United States over the next 15 years.In Australia, Australia’s National Bowel Screening Program (NBCSP) was launched in 2006, implemented and gradually improved [69], and study shows a significant reduction in risk in Australia in the last decade [70]. However, the level of insurance coverage and treatment costs exhibits significant disparities across nations, influenced by variations in economic development. Therefore, advocacy for enhanced health aid from high-income nations to low- and middle-income countries is imperative.Although high-SDI regions demonstrate relatively advanced awareness of CRC screening, diagnosis, and treatment, significant disparities remain within these regions. The existence of marked cancer health inequities in these areas is attributable to a number of factors, including socioeconomic status, healthcare accessibility, individual behaviours and treatment availability. This is especially pertinent for high-income nations, where addressing these disparities is critical to tackling systemic inequality [71]. For instance, governments should prioritise the reduction of income inequality through the implementation of effective policies, whilst ensuring equitable access to health insurance and medical services for ethnic minorities and vulnerable populations. The development of telemedicine infrastructure, including online cancer consultation platforms, has the potential to further enhance accessibility for rural and marginalised communities [72].

Secondly, the increases in global burden of deaths, DALYs and YLLs are explained by the fact that the middle SDI region has experienced the greatest increase in CRC burden of deaths, DALYs and YLLs, consistent with previous findings [73]. The rapid economic growth and industrialization in middle SDI countries after 2000 have led to lifestyle changes, including adoption of Western diets characterized by diet high in red meat [74], which are associated with increased CRC risk [75]. While the Mediterranean diet has protective effects against CRC, global implementation faces challenges [76]. Public awareness of unhealthy dietary patterns remains limited, necessitating stricter policies on red meat consumption. Health authorities should translate evidence from dietary studies into layman-friendly guidelines to facilitate public education and intervention. Enhanced awareness would enable effective health policy implementation [77]. In concrete terms, policy-makers could implement a comprehensive strategy involving tiered taxation on red meat, applying ecological surcharges of 20–35% to processed products and 5–10% to fresh cuts. The generated revenue could fund nutrition research and subsidies for plant-based proteins. Although the burden in the middle SDI region remains lower than that in the high and high middle SDI regions, the upward trend is growing faster than high middle SDI region, and population growth is the main reason for this, as shown by the results of the decomposition analysis.To address CRC challenges driven by population expansion, it is recommended that policy-makers should expand public health system coverage, optimize healthcare resource allocation, and establish comprehensive medical support systems through primary prevention strategies targeting diet high in red meat. However,screening uptake remains suboptimal, particularly in low-income countries [78]. In the Philippines, colonoscopy screening is only available in urban areas and is often prohibitively expensive for the average citizen [79]. Population-based screening programs cannot be overlooked. Policy-makers are advised to implement low-cost screening methods like fecal occult blood tests in high-incidence areas to increase early diagnosis rates [80].

Thirdly, in region with SDI < 0.4, the ASR of CRC associated with diet high in red meat exhibit significant inverse correlations with SDI. Despite the plateauing of CRC burden and ASR trends in low SDI region, its ASR of CRC burden remains lower than that in middle, high-middle and high SDI regions. Economic constraints and subsistence priorities drive reliance on red meat as a critical nutrient source, posing dual public health challenges. Red meat provides heme iron (reducing anemia in high-risk subgroups like women and children), zinc (essential for immune and cognitive function), and vitamin B12, with protein promoting satiety [81]. However, the increased CRC burden associated with excessive red meat consumption should not be underestimated [29], suggesting adherence to WHO limits (< 500 g/week) [82]. While plant-based diets are associated with reduced gastrointestinal cancers, they risk compromising iron/zinc status, which is associated with poor non-heme iron absorption and inadequate fortification of meat alternatives [81, 83]. Policy frameworks could balance these trade-offs through tiered interventions: combining fiscal incentives for nutrient-dense plant foods and social media-driven education on balanced red meat intake, such as holding social media campaigns with 90-s videos could educate people about symptoms, creating a multi-tiered public health network.A recent study based on PURE study found that the healthiest dietary pattern included an average of 54.5 g per day (0.5 servings) of red meat [84]. Context implementation, aligned with local food security and dietary cultures, could synergize CRC prevention with sustainable development goals for malnutrition and non-communicable disease control.

The GBD study, led by the University of Washington's Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), is the world's largest public health project.It has the following advantages and innovative features: ①Comprehensive and Comparable: Covers 369 diseases and 87 risk factors, supports comparisons across regions and time dimensions, and provides a reference for global health policy. ②Dynamic adaptability: integrating influencing factors of emerging health threats and updating model parameters in real time. ③Uncertainty Quantification: Generate 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) through simulation to distinguish between parameter and model structural uncertainty, thereby enhancing the robustness of the results.④Interdisciplinary Integration: Combining epidemiological, statistical, and health economics methods to optimize resource allocation decisions.

Our study used data from GBD 2021, which has some limitations in terms of data quality and quantity. Firstly, some underdeveloped regions lack sufficient cancer registries, so estimates are based on predicted covariates or trends from neighbouring countries, which may bias the results [16]. To address estimation inaccuracies and improve reliability, advanced modeling techniques could fill data gaps and compensate for insufficient high-quality data in low-resource areas, while incorporating reference and alternative case definitions during bias correction guide future data collection to improve consistency [85]. The lack of CRC subgroup and individual-level data in the GBD database limited the analytical depth of our study. Therefore, expanding the data pool will require identifying and incorporating additional unpublished original research, such as survey reports, to fill these gaps [86]. Meanwhile, the presence of multiple dietary risk factors made it difficult to definitively attribute the burden of CRC solely to a diet high in red meat. We also did not consider the effect of racial and urban–rural differences on the burden of CRC associated with high red meat consumption. Therefore, these limitations may affect our assessment of the burden of CRC, and future studies should be careful to overcome these shortcomings.

Conclusion

This study used a systematic method to assess the spatial and temporal changes in the global burden of CRC associated with high red meat intake worldwide from 1990 to 2021 and observed large regional and national differences in burden. Although global ASR of deaths, DALYs, YLDs and YLLs for colorectal cancer associated with diet high in red meat have declined year by year, the absolute numbers of deaths and DALYs have increased substantially with population growth. Diets high in red meat continue to be a significant dietary risk factor for the global burden of colorectal cancer, particularly among men, older adults, and those located in middle SDI region. Although global health inequalities in CRC associated with diet high in red meat have moderated, the burden of the disease is gradually shifting to regions with lower levels of economic development. Decomposition analyses revealed that population growth is the main influence on the increasing burden. We found that changes in CRC burden associated with a diet high in red meat were similar in China and globally. In addition, The ASR of deaths、DALYs and YLLs were significantly higher in China than globally and declined at a slower rate. In the future, policy makers should develop flexible, integrated, gender-, age- and region-specific policies and programs to reduce red meat intake.
