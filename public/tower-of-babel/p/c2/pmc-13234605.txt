A sulfated chitosan-driven immune-ECM reprogramming strategy to break the vicious cycle of aged wound healing
Authors: Xiaohui Zhang, Siyi Wang, Yongqing Liu, Jing Wang, Changsheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic wounds in the elderly are characterized by persistent inflammation and excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, which form a self-perpetuating vicious cycle that impedes healing and increases morbidity. Here, we identify sulfated chitosan (SCS) as an a synthetic glycosaminoglycan capable of disrupting this pathological "ECM degradation-immune imbalance" cycle and promoting robust skin wound repair in aged mice. SCS activates JAK2-STAT3/STAT6 signaling to rebalance M1/M2 macrophage polarization, while simultaneously enhancing phosphoadenosine phosphosulfate (PAPS) production and sulfonate-group trafficking. These coordinated actions collectively improved collagen deposition, angiogenesis, and glycosaminoglycan (GAG) content within the wound microenvironment. Using Sulf2 knockdown and overexpression models, we further show that chitosan-anchored sulfonate groups are required for driving macrophages toward an M2 phenotype, whereas free sulfonate groups facilitate glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis. These coordinated immune and matrix effects lead to enhanced collagen organization, angiogenesis, and glycosaminoglycan deposition within the aged wound microenvironment.Together, this work establishes SCS as a materials-driven immune-ECM reprogramming strategy and highlights its translational potential for the treatment of age-associated chronic wounds.

Introduction

Chronic wounds in the elderly represent a major clinical challenge, with delayed healing leading to increased morbidity and healthcare costs [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Aging skin undergoes progressive extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, including an imbalanced type I/III collagen ratio, elastic fiber fragmentation, and reduced glycosaminoglycan content, which together compromise mechanical integrity and wound repair [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. These changes compromise mechanical integrity and impair wound healing. Aged ECM and chronic inflammation form a vicious cycle [10]. Senescent cells (SnCs) release matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) via the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), accelerating ECM breakdown [11,12]. Degradation products, such as elastin fragments, further activate immune responses [13,14].

Current ECM-based biomaterials provide mechanical support but do not correct immune dysregulation, leading to inflammatory degradation and foreign body responses [[15], [16], [17]]. Anti-inflammatory therapies reduce inflammation yet fail to restore ECM function owing to poor specificity and systemic toxicity [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. MMP inhibitors conceptually offer a means to preserve ECM integrity and remodel the tissue microenvironment; however, their clinical translation is limited by the functional diversity and temporal dependence of MMP activity, making selective inhibition difficult without impairing normal tissue remodeling or promoting fibrosis.

As key regulators of skin homeostasis, macrophages coordinate senescent cell clearance and inflammatory control during aging [23,24]. In aged skin, macrophages skew toward a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, characterized by elevated IL-1β and TNF-α and reduced VEGF, FGF, and platelet-derived growth factor, thereby suppressing collagen synthesis and angiogenesis [25,26]. Targeting macrophages as a central hub can break the ECM degradation-immune dysregulation vicious cycle by reprogramming M1 macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory, pro-repair state, reducing MMP secretion at its source while restoring the immune microenvironment [[27], [28], [29]]. Notably, diabetic and age-related chronic wounds share key pathological features, including persistent inflammation, impaired angiogenesis, ECM dysregulation, and macrophage dysfunction. Recent advances in multifunctional bioactive wound dressings have demonstrated promising therapeutic efficacy in diabetic wound healing, largely through macrophage modulation, particularly by promoting the transition from pro-inflammatory to pro-regenerative states [[30], [31], [32]]. Although primarily focused on diabetic wounds, these strategies may be broadly applicable to aging-associated chronic wounds, shifting therapy from passive inflammation suppression toward active restoration of tissue homeostasis.

Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) are extracellular matrix polysaccharides composed of repeating disaccharide units containing an amino hexose and modified by sulfate or carboxyl groups, which maintain tissue structure and act as cell-surface signal recognition molecules to regulate diverse cellular and inflammatory processes [33,34]. However, the high chemical heterogeneity and source-dependent variability of natural GAGs result in significant side effects, greatly limiting their clinical application [35,36]. To address these challenges, increasing efforts have focused on the development of structurally controllable GAG-mimetic materials [37,38].

Sulfonated chitosan (SCS) derived from natural chitosan is a heparin like glycosaminoglycan. We have previously demonstrated that SCS can modulate vascularization and inflammation during tissue repair by altering macrophage phenotype [39,40]. However, it remains uncertain whether SCS can improve the function of ECM in the aged healing wounds. Here, we demonstrated that SCS exerts a dual therapeutic role by restoring immune balance and ECM integrity in aged wounds. Sulfonation drives macrophage polarization from M1 to M2, reducing inflammation, and provides sulfate donors to promote glycosaminoglycan and glycoprotein synthesis, restoring ECM structure and function (Scheme 1). This study investigates whether SCS can break the “ECM degradation-immune dysregulation” loop, offering a promising material-based strategy for chronic wound management in the elderly.Scheme 1Schematic of SCS as a biomaterial capable of disrupting this pathological “ECM degradation-immune imbalance” feedback loop, thereby accelerating wound closure and restoring tissue integrity in aged mice.

Results

Systemic effects of aging on skin structure and repair in mice

Aging skin exhibits progressive structural and functional decline, driven by intrinsic senescence and extrinsic insults such as UV exposure and chronic inflammation [41,42]. These changes degrade the extracellular matrix (ECM), reduce vascular density, and impair epidermal turnover, contributing to delayed wound healing [43,44].

In aged mice, H&E staining revealed marked thinning of the epidermis and dermis (Fig. S1A), while immunofluorescence showed reduced expression of fibronectin (Fig. S1B), indicating compromised structural integrity. Functionally, wound models demonstrated markedly delayed healing in aged mice (Fig. 1A). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) indicated that key pathways involved in epidermal regeneration, cell adhesion, cytokine transcription, and angiogenesis were significantly downregulated in aged skin wounds (Fig. 1B). Loricrin is a well-established terminal differentiation marker of keratinocytes in the suprabasal layers. Aged mice showed significantly lower Loricrin expression in wound tissue compared to young controls (Fig. 1C–E), suggesting impaired re-epithelialization. Consistently, the neoepidermis in aged wounds exhibited a diminished Krt14 signal (Fig. S2), likely due to impaired local proliferation and migration of Krt14+ keratinocytes during re-epithelialization [45,46]. Together, these results indicate that aging impairs skin repair by disrupting tissue architecture and altering the expression of genes associated with regenerative processes, providing mechanistic insight into delayed wound healing in the elderly.

SCS modulates macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype

The biological activity of polysaccharides is highly dependent on their degree of sulfation, which governs their interactions with cellular receptors and downstream signaling pathways [47,48]. To explore this, we compared sulfated chitosan (SCS) with chitosan (CS). FT-IR spectra results indicated that compared to CS, the sulfonated modified SCS exhibited two stretching vibration peaks at 1240 cm−1 and 815 cm−1, corresponding to the O=S=O group and the C-O-S group, respectively (Fig. 2A), demonstrating the successful grafting of sulfonic acid groups onto CS. In comparison to CS, the proton signals exhibited shifts following the grafting of sulfonic acid groups (-OSO3-) (Fig. 2B). Additionally, XPS spectra reveal the appearance of new-NH-SO3 and S2p peaks in the SCS spectrum (Fig. 2C), confirming the successful sulfonation modification of chitosan [[49], [50], [51]]. To evaluate the effect of sulfonation on the bioactivity of chitosan, we first assessed the viability of RAW 264.7 cells treated with various concentrations of SCS or CS (Fig. 2E). Within 1-50 μg/mL, SCS induced peak viability at 20 μg/mL, after which viability declined. In contrast, CS led to a modest, concentration-dependent increase in viability. Based on cellular responses and potential dose-related risks, 20 μg/mL SCS was selected for subsequent experiments.

To further investigate the role of SCS in macrophage polarization, we established an inflammatory model using LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells. Neither SCS nor CS affected the expression of the M1 marker gene iNOS (Fig. 2F). However, SCS significantly upregulated the M2 marker gene Arg-1, while CS showed no effect (Fig. 2G). Immunofluorescence analysis ((Fig. 2H–I)) confirmed these trends: SCS reduced the fluorescence signal of the M1 marker CCR7 (CD197) and enhanced that of the M2 marker CD206, whereas CS altered neither CCR7 nor CD206 expression.

Flow cytometry further confirmed that SCS facilitated macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 state toward the reparative M2 phenotype (Fig. 2J–K). SCS exhibited markedly enhanced activity in regulating macrophage function than CS. The WB results revealed that SCS promoted phosphorylation of STAT3 and STAT6 while suppressing STAT1 phosphorylation (Fig. 3A), a signaling profile consistent with anti-inflammatory and tissue-repair phenotypes [[52], [53], [54]]. We also detected the phosphorylation level of JAK2. The JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway is crucial in mediating the polarization and activation of M2 macrophages [[55], [56], [57]]. The sulfation of glycosaminoglycans enables them to regulate growth factor signaling bidirectionally, playing key roles in development and disease [58,59]. This study analyzes the mechanism by which 26SCS enhances the biological effects of JAK2 at the molecular level through dynamic simulation (Fig. S5). In silico docking studies revealed that SCS engaged in hydrogen bonding with a greater number of amino acid residues in JAK2 than CS (Fig. 3B–C). Specifically, SCS forms a hydrogen bond network with eight amino acid residues of JAK2 (Arg847, Asp873, Pro870, Leu871, Thr875, Gly876, Glu987, Gln955 and His950). However, CS only forms intermolecular hydrogen bonds with five amino acid residues (Gln1072, Gln1070, Val1075, Asp1068, and Arg1063) of JAK2. SCS exhibits lower root mean square deviation (RMSD) and potential energy (EPOT), indicating that it is more likely to form a stable structure in aqueous solution (Fig. 3D–E and Fig. S6). Sulfonate group mediates SCS-induced macrophage polarization and glycan sulfation

To investigate the molecular basis for the differential effects of SCS and CS on macrophage function, Astral DIA quantitative proteomics was performed. KEGG analysis showed that, compared with CS, SCS significantly enriched multiple glycosylation pathways, including N-glycan biosynthesis (mmu00510), keratan sulfate biosynthesis (mmu00533), and various N-glycan pathways (mmu00513) (Fig. 4A). The enhanced biosynthesis of keratan sulfate, an important glycosaminoglycan, points to possible changes in extracellular matrix remodeling and ligand-receptor interactions. These findings suggest that the sulfonate group of SCS may modulate glycan sulfation [60,61]. Sulf2, a macrophage-enriched extracellular sulfatase that removes 6-O-sulfate groups from glycosaminoglycans [62,63], was markedly upregulated following SCS treatment (Fig. S8 and Fig. 4B–C). Glycan sulfation requires 3′-phosphoadenosine 5′-phosphosulfate (PAPS) as the sulfate donor, synthesized by Papss2 and transported to the Golgi via Slc35b3 [64,65]. To examine the role of glycan sulfation in macrophage polarization, Sulf2 was selectively knocked down (Fig. S9), overexpressed (Fig. S10), or inhibited with the small-molecule inhibitor OKN-007 [66]. Functional analyses revealed that Sulf2 knockdown or inhibition enhanced SCS-induced M1-to-M2 polarization, whereas Sulf2 overexpression suppressed this transition (Fig. 4E–F), highlighting the central role of glycan sulfation in regulating macrophage phenotype. Immunofluorescence further revealed elevated chondroitin sulfate levels in macrophages (Fig. 4G), indicating enhanced glycan synthesis. Sulf2 knockdown or inhibition reduced chondroitin sulfate in macrophages, whereas Sulf2 overexpression enhanced it, confirming free sulfonate groups played a pivotal role in SCS-mediated Glycan sulfation and intercellular regulation.

To evaluate the effect of SCS on senescent cell function, an in vitro model of senescent cells was established in this study (Fig. S12). As shown in Fig. 5B, Ki67 was markedly reduced in H2O2-treated cells compared to controls, indicating impaired proliferation [67]. In co-culture systems (Fig. 5A), SCS enhanced senescent cells proliferation and migration (Fig. 5B and Fig. S13). Senescent endothelial cells (ECs) exposed to SCS-treated macrophages displayed enhanced tube formation on Matrigel, indicating partial restoration of angiogenic potential (Fig. 5B–C). Moreover, SCS treatment upregulated fibronectin expression in senescent fibroblasts, contributing to extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and maintenance of tissue homeostasis (Fig. 5B and D). In vitro degradation in PBS (pH 7.4, 37 °C) showed the slow initial degradation (1-6 d) preserves structural integrity, while accelerated degradation by day 12 facilitates tissue remodeling (Fig. 6G). SEM revealed a porous fibrous network (Fig. 6H) suitable for cell adhesion and molecule loading. EDS confirmed successful SCS incorporation, with sulfur contents of 0.15% (HA), 0.29% (HA/CS), and 6.3% (HA/SCS) (Fig. 6H).

In vivo fluorescence imaging (Fig. 6I) revealed strong retention of FITC-labeled hydrogels at wound sites from 0 to 4 h. Signals persisted to day 1; by day 3, signals remained detectable in the SCS group but had already diminished in the CS group, and both groups faded by day 6. Thus, while the HA hydrogel enables sustained release, SCS exhibits longer wound retention and a prolonged bioactivity window than CS.

To assess macrophage polarization, HA hydrogel (500 μg) was loaded with 1, 20, or 50 μg of CS or SCS. HA alone reduced the M1 marker CCR7, with no further difference between CS and SCS (Fig. 6J–K). In contrast, the M2 marker CD206 increased dose-dependently (Fig. 6J–K), and SCS induced significantly higher expression than CS. Similarly, CS-56 (chondroitin sulfate) expression also increased dose-dependently and was higher with SCS than with CS (Fig. 6L–M).

Collectively, these properties indicate that the HA/SCS composite hydrogel is a promising immunomodulatory tissue engineering scaffold and local delivery system for wound repair and tissue regeneration.

SCS hydrogel modulates macrophages to improve the wound microenvironment in aged mice

The regulatory effect of SCS on the inflammatory microenvironment and immune cell phenotypes during wound repair was investigated by collecting wound skin samples on day 6 (Fig. 7A). Functionally, SCS-loaded hydrogels modulated macrophage polarization in vivo. Flow cytometry showed a significant reduction in M1-like macrophages (CD86+CD206-) and a marked increase in M2-like macrophages (CD86+CD206+) in SCS-treated wounds compared with control and CS groups (Fig. 7B–C). Immunohistochemistry confirmed the predominance of CD206+ M2-like macrophages and decreased CD197+ M1-like macrophages (Fig. 7D). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of whole-wound tissue further revealed upregulation of M2-associated genes, including Arg1, TGFβ, Pdgfb, Mrc1, and CD163, as well as upstream signaling molecules JAK1, JAK2, STAT3, and STAT6. Given that macrophages are a major source and target of JAK-STAT signaling in the wound microenvironment, these findings are consistent with enhanced JAK-STAT pathway activation through IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, and IL-13 receptor signaling to promote M2 polarization (Fig. 7E). Immunofluorescence showed that SCS significantly upregulated the M2 marker Arg-1 (Fig. 7F–G). ELISA on day 6 (Fig. 7H) revealed that both CS and SCS reduced IL-1β and TNF-α levels compared to HA, confirming their anti-inflammatory properties. SCS hydrogel remodels the extracellular matrix to promote wound healing in aged mice

Macroscopic and molecular analyses revealed that SCS-treated wounds healed significantly faster than control and CS groups (Fig. 8A). By day 14 post-injury, SCS-treated wounds were nearly fully closed, with an average unhealed area of 10.9%, compared with 19.7% in the CS group and 21.7% in controls. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) indicated upregulation of pathways associated with extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, collagen deposition, and angiogenesis (Fig. 8B–D). H&E staining showed shortened wound edges by day 12, reflecting accelerated re-epithelialization (Fig. 8E and G). In SCS-treated wounds, Krt14 expression was increased in the neoepidermis, likely due to enhanced proliferation and migration of Krt14+ keratinocytes [71]. Histological analyses further confirmed enhanced fibronectin deposition, CD31-positive vessel-like structures suggestive of neovascularization, and collagen synthesis in SCS-treated wounds (Fig. 8F and H). Discussion

This study suggests the dual mechanisms through which SCS (synthetic GAG) accelerates wound healing in aged mice by coordinately regulating macrophage polarization and glycosaminoglycan sulfation. Chronic wounds in the elderly, characterized by a self-perpetuating vicious cycle of persistent inflammation and excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, represent a significant clinical challenge. Here, we demonstrate that SCS effectively interrupts this cycle, not merely by providing a passive physical barrier or acting as a simple growth factor delivery vehicle, but by actively remodeling the dysregulated aging wound microenvironment.

Sulfated polysaccharides have potent anti-inflammatory and pro-repair activities, which are attributed to their specific sulfate group configurations [[75], [76], [77], [78]]. Building upon this foundation, our study further elucidates the critical role of the sulfonate groups in SCS-mediated regulation of macrophage immunity. Compared to CS, SCS significantly activates the JAK-STAT3/STAT6 pathway in macrophages and promotes their polarization towards the M2 phenotype. By knocking down or overexpressing Sulf2 to alter the sulfonate groups content in SCS, we found that this group positively regulates the SCS-induced shift from M1 to M2 polarization, confirming its central role in macrophage polarization. We hypothesize that the sulfonate groups on SCS may act more directly and persistently on downstream signaling targets, thereby amplifying the polarization effect. This interpretation aligns with emerging evidence suggesting that sulfate groups can directly modulate Toll-like receptor signaling pathways [79], underscoring the pivotal role of sulfation in innate immune regulation.

On a metabolic level, our study delineates a coherent pathway from SCS internalization to the synthesis of functional sulfated glycosaminoglycans (GAGs). SCS activates the PAPS synthase Papss2, thereby enhancing the cellular pool and Golgi translocation of the universal sulfate donor, PAPS. This culminates in the GlcNAc6ST-catalyzed sulfation of glycosaminoglycan chains. This series of metabolic reprogramming events intersects with, yet is distinct from, the canonical chondroitin sulfate biosynthesis pathway [80]. Immunofluorescence analyses confirmed that SCS not only increased the chondroitin sulfate content within macrophages but also, crucially, remotely initiated its synthesis program in fibroblasts and keratinocytes via macrophage crosstalk. This intercellular metabolic "relay" is key to breaking the local impasse of ECM degradation. Through Sulf2 knocking down or overexpressing studies, we provide evidence that the sulfonate groups on the chitosan backbone act as key "signaling units" driving this cascade.

Sulfation density is a key determinant of the bioactivity of sulfated polysaccharides. Our SCS preparation has a sulfur content of 12.8% (DS), and although we only compared it with non-sulfated CS, in vitro concentration–response experiments indicate that increasing SCS levels within the tested range enhances M2 macrophage polarization and glycosaminoglycan synthesis. These findings suggest that DS likely modulates bioactivity by altering charge density, JAK2 binding, and downstream signaling, thereby potentially influencing macrophage polarization and ECM remodeling, and warrant further studies to systematically explore the structure–activity relationship of SCS variants with varying DS.

Recent multifunctional bioactive wound dressings, including smart responsive hydrogels, conductive/electronic dressings, controlled drug delivery systems, and biomimetic ECM scaffolds, have been widely investigated [[81], [82], [83]]. Here, the HA-based hydrogel loaded with SCS integrates biomimetic ECM features with controlled SCS delivery capability, while also exhibiting good injectability and suitable degradability for wound environments, thereby expanding the applications of bioactive wound dressings.

Regarding functional outcomes, the dual mechanisms of SCS converge to produce synergistic effects. On the immune front, driving macrophages toward an M2 phenotype directly addresses the core issue of persistent inflammation in aged wounds. This aligns with strategies utilizing IL-4 or IL10 to induce M2 polarization for improved healing [84,85], but SCS offers a unique implementation path leveraging its biomaterial properties. On the ECM front, the enrichment of sulfated GAGs like chondroitin sulfate provides structural support and, critically, functions as co-receptors for various growth factors (e.g., FGF-2, VEGF). This enhances collagen deposition density and organization and fosters a pro-angiogenic microenvironment [86,87]. Collectively, these actions shift the molecular network of the aging wound from a pathologically steady state of "degradation-inflammation" to a physiological state of "synthesis-repair," ultimately accelerating wound closure and improving tissue quality. The application value of HA/SCS was evaluated against several commercially available conventional wound dressings. As illustrated in Fig. S16, HA/SCS achieved a superior therapeutic outcome relative to the commercial products tested (Hydrocolloid Thin Dressing, Alginate, and Tegaderm™).

Although this study focuses on sulfated chitosan (SCS), the mechanisms identified here likely may provide a basis for understanding related synthetic GAG mimetics, pending further investigation with tunable backbone chemistry and sulfation patterns. By functioning as active regulators at the immune-matrix interface rather than passive ECM substitutes, SCS may offer a potential strategy for restoring immune-ECM homeostasis in aging and other chronic degenerative conditions under the tested conditions.

Conclusion

The present study establishes sulfated chitosan (SCS) as a potent therapeutic agent for aged chronic wounds. SCS effectively breaks the pathological "ECM degradation-immune imbalance" cycle by simultaneously reprogramming macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype and restoring sulfated glycosaminoglycan levels in the extracellular matrix. Mechanistically, the covalently bound sulfonate groups on the chitosan backbone are essential for driving immunomodulation, while the hydrolyzed free sulfonate groups facilitate metabolic synthesis. These findings establish SCS as a promising candidate for geriatric wound care and suggest that synthetic glycosaminoglycan mimetics warrant further investigation as a mechanism-driven strategy for restoring immune-matrix homeostasis in age-associated degenerative conditions, pending validation in broader experimental models.

Experimental section

Chemicals and reagents

Sulfated chitosan (SCS) was prepared as previously described previously [88]. The raw materials for chitosan (CS) were highly deacetylated chitosan oligosaccharide (COS). Briefly, dialysis of COS with a 3000 molecular weight dialysis bag for 3 days followed by freeze-drying. COS was procured from Shenzhen Zhongfayuan Biological Technology Co. Ltd. (Shenzhen, China).

FITC-labeled SCS (FITC-SCS) was prepared as follows. FITC dissolved in sodium bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.8) was added dropwise to SCS solution, reacted at room temperature in the dark, dialyzed against distilled water in the dark with daily medium changes to remove unreacted FITC, and stored frozen in the dark.

Characterization of SCS

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were measured within the 4000-400 cm−1 range utilizing a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS10 FT-IR spectrometer. Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy (400MHz/AVANCE Ⅲ 400, Bruker Corporation, Switzerland) was employed to confirm the chemical structures of synthesized compounds, with deuterium oxide (D2O) as solvent, and data were processed and analyzed using MestReNova software (version12.0.0). Molecular weights were measured by gel permeation chromatography (P230, Elite, Dalian), and surface compositions of CS and SCS were analyzed by XPS (ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Scientific, USA). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements of CS and SCS samples were collected on a SmartLab-SE X-ray powder diffractometer from Nippon Paint.

Preparation and characterization of hydrogel scaffold materials

HA (Mw ≈ 1.3 × 105 Da) hydrogels were formed via chemical crosslinking with BDDE in 1% NaOH, stirred at 35 °C for 2 h, soaked in water for 18 h, pulverized, and sieved (80-100 mesh). HA hydrogel was freeze-dried for 2 days to obtain HA dry gel. SCS or CS was dissolved in PBS at 10 mg/mL, then mixed with the HA dry gel at the appropriate ratio to obtain HA/SCS or HA/CS hydrogel.

The rheological properties of the prepared microgels were characterized using a rotational rheometer at (25 ± 2) °C, including steady-state shear scanning (shear rate: 0.001–1000 s−1), frequency sweep (0.01–100 Hz) for G′ and G″, hysteresis loop method (shear rate ramped from 0 to 100 s−1 and then reversed to 0 s−1), and alternating shear mode (cycling between 10 Pa and 100 s−1). Rheology was assessed on a Mars60 rheometer (0.001-1000 s−1; 0.01-100 Hz).

The in vitro degradation of HA hydrogels was evaluated by gravimetric analysis. Crosslinked HA xerogels were accurately weighed, placed in dialysis bags, and immersed in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C. At predetermined time points (days 1, 3, 6, and 12), three parallel samples per time point were collected, dehydrated with anhydrous ethanol, centrifuged, and vacuum-dried at 80 °C to constant weight. The weight retention rate was then calculated.

The microstructure was examined by SEM (JSM-IT800HL, JEOL, Japan), combined with EDS analysis to map the distribution of C, N, S and other elements.

Sulf2 cell lines

Sulf2 knockdown and overexpression lines were generated by lentiviral transduction or plasmid transfection, respectively, in 60-70% confluent RAW264.7 cells (Stem Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences). Transduction/transfection efficiency was confirmed by green fluorescence, and puromycin selection yielded stable lines.

Cell culture and treatment

RAW264.7 cells were seeded at 5 × 104 cells/mL and treated with 200 ng mL−1 LPS for 24 h; LPS + SCS + OKN-007 groups received 20 μg/mL SCS and 50 μM OKN-007. L929 and HUVEC cells were seeded at 2 × 104 cells/mL in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Senescence cells were induced with 500 μM H2O2 for 2 h, and then cultured in normal medium for 24 h.

Cells in 6-well plates (90%-100% confluence) were scratched with a 200 μL pipette tip, washed three times with PBS, and cultured in serum-free medium. Scratch images were captured at 0 and 48 h post-scratching, and wound closure area was quantified to assess cell migration.

Matrigel (Corning, USA) was thawed at 4 °C overnight, coated onto pre-chilled 48-well plates (200 μL/well), and polymerized at 37 °C for 30 min. HUVECs were seeded at 5 × 104 cells/well and incubated for 6 h (37 °C, 5% CO2). Cells were stained with FITC-phalloidin and imaged by confocal microscopy. Total capillary length and branch points per field were quantified using ImageJ to assess angiogenesis.

RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured overnight. Four groups were tested: blank control, HA gel, CS/HA gel, and SCS/HA gel, with the latter two containing 1, 20, or 50 μg of cargo and 0.5 mg/well HA. Materials were placed in Transwell inserts (0.4 μm pore size, Corning, USA), and co-culture was established with target cells in the lower chamber. After 24 h of incubation (37 °C, 5% CO2), macrophage functional regulation was assessed.

Animal models and wound healing

Male C57BL/6 mice (2 and 18 months) were used (ECUST-2022-052). Full-thickness dorsal wounds (10 mm) were created under isoflurane anesthesia. Young mice received PBS (Young); aged mice were assigned to PBS (Aged), Aged (Aged + HA), Aged + CS (Aged + HA + CS), or Aged + SCS (Aged + HA + SCS). Hydrogels were applied every 6 days. Wounds were photographed on days 3, 6, 9, and 12.

qPCR and western blot

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol and quantified by NanoDrop 2000. cDNA was synthesized using iScript Kit (Bio-Rad), and qPCR was performed with iTaq SYBR Green (CFX96, Bio-Rad). Primer sequences are listed in Table S3.

Proteins were extracted with RIPA buffer + PMSF, quantified by BCA, separated on SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes. Primary antibodies included JAK, p-JAK, STAT1, p-STAT1, STAT3, p-STAT3, STAT6, p-STAT6 (1:1000; Abways), and Actin (1:5000; Abways). For phosphorylation assays, cells were subjected to serum starvation with 2% FBS for 2 h, followed by stimulation for 30 min.

Molecular docking

The three-dimensional structures of chitosan (CS) and sulfated chitosan (SCS) were constructed using the LigPrep module within the Schrodinger Suite. Each molecule was initially aligned with the mass center of the JAK2 protein (PDB: 7RN6) and subsequently translated 18 Å along the Z-axis to define the starting position for the molecular dynamics (MD) simulation. The system was solvated in an orthorhombic box with an 8 Å buffer of SPC water molecules and neutralized with 0.15 M NaCl. All MD simulations were performed using Desmond for a duration of 100 ns.

Flow cytometry analysis

RAW264.7 cells were digested to obtain a single-cell suspension, blocked with CD16/32 on ice, and stained with CCR7 (APC) and CD206 (FITC or PE) for flow cytometry.

For flow cytometric analysis of macrophages in wounds in vivo, Single-cell suspension of skin was obtained by digestion with collagenase I, dispase, and DNase I. Cells were stained with CD45 (BioLegend; 1:200), F4/80 (BioLegend; 1:200), CD86 (BioLegend; 1:200), CD11b (BioLegend; 1:200) and CD206 (BioLegend; 1:200), and dead cells were excluded using a Live/Dead Fixable Far Red kit (Thermo Fisher).

Data were acquired on a flow cytometer (Beckman Cytoflex, USA) and analyzed with FlowJo.

Histomorphology analysis and immunofluorescence staining

Macrophages were fixed, permeabilized, and stained with anti-ki67 (Affinity; 1:200), anti-fibronectin (Affinity; 1:200), anti-Sulf2 (Affinity; 1:200) and anti-CS-56 (Sigma-Aldrich; 1:200), followed by Alexa Fluor-647 secondary. Tissues were fixed, paraffin-embedded, sectioned (7 μm), and stained with H&E, Picrosirius Red, and HID-AB (Solarbio). Immunofluorescence for CD197 (Affinity; 1:200), CD206 (Affinity; 1:200), CD31 (Affinity; 1:200), cytokeratin 14 (Affinity; 1:200), and fibronectin used appropriate Alexa Fluor secondary antibodies.

Statistical analyses

Analysis of the results was performed using GraphPad Prism software (version10.0; GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA). Data are shown as means ± SD. The N values indicate biological replicates from experiments carried out a minimum of three times, unless stated differently. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using unpaired, two-tailed Student's t-tests. For comparisons involving multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized, as specified.
