Macrophage-derived exosomes carrying miR-30a-5p alleviates IL-13/IL4-induced epithelial-mesenchymal transition in mouse bronchial epithelial cells via Runx1
Authors: Bin Wu, Danhong Li, Yu Wang, Jieru Xu, Lihong Li, Mengjie Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAllergic asthma (AA) has an increasing incidence rate. Macrophage-derived exosomes (EXO) carrying microRNAs (miRNAs) regulate epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in AA, which is worth further exploration. MiR-30a-5p is a small molecule with the potential to inhibit EMT.MethodsTo explore miR-30a-5p’s effects on EMT, this study established OVA-induced AA mice, evaluating the model through airway hyperresponsiveness, immune cell counts in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, Masson staining, and Western blot. Using quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction and fluorescence in situ hybridization assays observed gene levels. Then mouse alveolar macrophages (MH-S) were co-cultured with primary mouse bronchial epithelial cells (BECs), incubated with IL-13 (10 ng/mL), IL-4 (10 ng/mL) and GW4869 (10 µM) (an EXO synthesis inhibitor) for 48 h. Cell transfection assessed RUNX1 pathway effects.ResultsMiR-30a-5p was decreased in AA mice and IL-13/IL-4 treated-BECs. Overexpressing miR-30a-5p in MH-S cells inhibited EMT in BECs, including decreasing E-cadherin and increasing α-SMA and Vimentin, while GW4869 reversed it. Moreover, EXO carrying miR-30a-5p derived from MH-S cells also inhibited EMT in BECs. Especially, the protein expressions of RUNX1 and TGF-β1 were decreased by overexpressing miR-30a-5p, Runx1 and Tgf-β1 overexpression offset the inhibition impact on EMT in BECs by EXO carrying miR-30a-5p. Result of dual luciferase report confirmed that the targeted inhibition of Runx1 by miR-30a-5p. Additionally, silencing RUNX1 inhibited EMT in BECs.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that macrophage-derived EXO regulates EMT in AA. Overexpression of miR-30a-5p in macrophages can regulate the EMT of BECs by EXO pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03960-y.Keywords: Allergic asthma, Exosome, Macrophage, Bronchial epithelial cells, MicroRNA

Introduction

In 2021, there were 4758 cases of asthma per 100,000 people worldwide [1]. Despite the optimization of treatment strategies and the decreasing age standardized rate of asthma, it still poses a huge health burden for the world [1]. The increase of urbanization, the time spent indoors and the use of antibiotics have led to the increase of the global prevalence of allergic diseases, and the incidence rate of allergic asthma (AA) is also increasing [2]. AA is one of the main subtypes of asthma [3]. Swedish scholars’ reports [4] indicated that the incidence of AA has been increasing from 1996 to 2016. Research in China has shown that after the COVID-19 pandemic, hospitalization rate for children with exacerbations asthma and allergic diseases increased [5].

AA is primarily a type 2 inflammation-driven disease triggered when allergens breach the epithelial cell barrier, initiating a cascade of immune responses. This process activates T-helper 2 cells (Th2), which secrete cytokines that induce bronchoconstriction, inflammation, and structural remodeling in the airways [6]. The pathological manifestations of AA include airway inflammation, variable airflow obstruction, excessive mucus production, airway hyperresponsiveness, and airway remodeling [7]. Modern research supports that one of the hallmarks of allergic asthma is epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in the airways, which contributes to airway remodeling and chronic inflammation. EMT is a reversible process of epithelial traits losing, including decreased E-cadherin [8]. The ovalbumin (OVA)-induced AA mouse model exhibits significant lung tissue damage, airway remodeling, and EMT, accompanied by the expression of cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 [9, 10]. Thus, identifying strategies to block or reverse EMT in lung epithelial cells remains a key focus of modern research.

Exosomes (EXO) are small extracellular vesicles secreted by cells, including B lymphocytes, T lymphocytes, dendritic cells, mast cells, epithelial cells, and macrophages [11]. They are loaded with various bioactive molecules, such as microRNAs (miRNAs), proteins, and nucleic acids [12]. MiRNAs are small endogenous non-coding RNAs, typically measuring around 22 nucleotides in length, functioning to regulate gene expression at the post-transcriptional level [13]. Both EXO and miRNAs are considered potential tools for treating AA and have received attention from researchers in recent years.

Macrophage is involved in damage of epithelial cell barrier and the occurrence of EMT in AA [14]. Macrophages are highly susceptible to environment that drive their phenotypic differentiation, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis and progression of AA [15]. Contemporary studies indicate that Th2-type inflammatory cytokines such as IL-13 and IL-4 induce M2 polarization of macrophages, which in turn exacerbates bronchial epithelial cell damage [16]. Furthermore, EXO derived from M2-polarized macrophages have been shown to induce EMT in human umbilical vein endothelial cells under IL-4/IL-13 stimulation [17]. In the context of allergic asthma, the unique cargo carried by macrophage-derived EXO can be harnessed for therapeutic purposes. Modern research suggests that macrophage EXO delivery of miRNA has the potential to be developed for the treatment of inflammatory diseases. Bouchareychas et al. [18] found that miRNA derived by macrophage EXO alleviated inflammation in atherosclerosis. EXO carrying miRNA targeting to macrophage reduced airway inflammation in mice [19]. Macrophage EXO loaded with different miRNAs may be able to regulate the occurrence and development of AA, which is worth further exploration.

MiR-30a-5p is a small molecule with the potential to inhibit EMT [20]. Therefore, this study observed the expression of miR-30a-5p and its association with airway remodeling in an OVA-induced AA model. Furthermore, to investigate the role of EXO carrying miR-30a-5p in EMT, MH-S cells were co-cultured with primary mouse bronchial epithelial cells (BECs). This study aims to provide experimental evidence supporting the potential of macrophage-derived EXO as a therapeutic agent in AA treatment.

Methods

Allergic asthma animal modeling

Female mice experience more airway remodeling compared to male mice [21]. Therefore, 30 of female BALB/c mice (6 weeks) were divided into two groups: Sham and OVA mice. All mice were adapted fed for 1 week. Then, on Day 0, 7, and 14, mice in group OVA were sensitized using 200 µL PBS containing 50 µg OVA and 2 mg aluminum peroxide adjuvant. After a week (on Day 21), mice in group OVA were challenged with 1% OVA (100 µg) nebulized inhalation for 30 min for three consecutive days. Equal volume of PBS was used for mice in group sham for sensitization and challenge. All animals were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd., China. All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by Laboratory animal management and ethics committee of Hangzhou Hunter Testing Biotechnology Co., Ltd. [approve number: IACUC/HTYJ-8201-81]

Detection of airway hyper-responsiveness in AA mice

On day 24, mice were placed into a barometric chamber for 3-min baseline readings. Then, methacholine chloride (0, 6.25, 12.5, 25, and 50 mg/mL) were used for nebulized inhalation and to test enhanced pause (Penh) index values with 5-min reading.

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and immune cell count

After airway hyper-responsiveness detection, mice were euthanized, followed by exposing the trachea. Mouse lungs were perfused with 0.8 mL of cold PBS using tracheal cannulations, three times [16]. Then, samples were collected and centrifuged. Cell sediment is used for preparation of Cytosin slides, followed by staining the slide. Cell classification counting was performed to determine the cell counts of eosinophils, neutrophils, macrophages, lymphocyte, and total cells.

Masson staining

The lungs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 72 h, followed by dehydration and embedding. Then, paraffin blocks were cut into 5 μm sections. The lung tissue section was dewaxed and hydrated. Weigert’s hematoxylin (HT10-79, Sigma, USA) was used to stain sections for 10 min. After washing, 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol was used to differentiate sections for 3 min, followed by Ponceau 2R/acid fuchsin solution staining (71019360/71033761, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, China) for 5 min. The sections were then stained with phosphomolybdic acid (XW514297441, Vokai Biotechnology, Beijing) for 10 min and aniline blue (71003644, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent) for 5 min and rinsed with 1% glacial acetic acid. Finally, the sections were rendered transparent and sealed. The sections were observed under the microscope and taken photos. ImageJ (NIH) was used to analyze collagen volume fraction (%).

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

RNA FISH kit (designed by GenePharma, China) was used to observe the localization and expression of miR-30a-5p on paraffin sections. After dewaxing, the lung sections were incubated with Proteinase K for 20 min, followed by incubating with pre-warmed denaturation solution. Then, RNA FISH probe was used to incubate sections for 12 h. After washing, DAPI was used to stain nucleus. The lung sections were observed under fluorescence microscope. ImageJ (National Institutes of Health) was used to analyze the area of red fluorescence.

Isolation of primary mouse BECs

According to Zhang et al. reported [22], the bronchial were separated and were incubated with 3 mL protease at 4 °C overnight. The culture medium of BECs (DMEM and 10% fetal bovine serum; Gibco, USA) was added to terminate digestion. After centrifugating, cells were cultured in 6-well plates with a fibroblast complete medium for 4 h. Suspended cells were collected and seeded in 6-well plates with BEC complete culture medium (iCell-a008-002 m, iCell, China). BECs at third passage were used for experiments. According to previous research [23], 10 ng/mL IL-13 and 10 ng/mL IL-4 were used to induce EMT by incubating BECs for 48 h.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

IF was used to identify BEC purity. BECs at 5 × 104 cells/well were seeded to slice in 24-well plates for 12 h. 4% paraformaldehyde was used to fix cells, followed by incubating with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 2 min. Cells were washed with PBS and blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min. Then, primary antibodies were used to incubate cells at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubating secondary antibodies for 1 h in the dark. After staining with DAPI for 1 min, cell slices were sealed with Fluoromount-G anti-fluorescence quenching sealing tablets (36307ES08, Yeasen, China). Nikon fluorescence inverted microscope (Ts2-FL, Nikon, Japan) was used to observe fluorescence intensity. Antibody information is shown in Table 1.

Cell transfection

Mouse alveolar macrophages MH-S cells were cultured according to Yan et al. reported [24] and seeded into 24-well plates. Cell transfection was referenced Wang et al. reported [25]. In brief, using Lipofectamine 2000 (11668-019, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA), miR-30a-5p mimics (miR10000128-1–5, RiBoBio, China), scrambled miRNA mimics (mimics-NC) (miR1N0000001-1–5, RiBoBio), small interfering RNA (siRNA) against Runx1 (siG1241991618-1–5, RiBoBio, China) and its negative control (siN0000002-1–5) were transfected into MH-S cells.

Co-culturing

MiR-30a-5p mimics/mimics-NC-treated MH-S cells and IL-13/IL-4-exposed BECs were co-cultured to observe effects of macrophages with miR-30a-5p overexpression on BECs. Transwell (3470, Corning, USA) was used for co-culture, BECs at 4 × 105 cells/well were seeded into upper chambers for 12 h, and then, MH-S cells at 8 × 106 cells/well were seeded into down chambers to observe effects of MH-S on BECs according to Li et al. reported [26]. GW4869 (≥96%; S81516, Yuanye, China) at 10 µM was used to inhibit EXO synthesis [27]. IL-13 (HY-P70460, MCE, USA) of 10 ng/mL and IL-4 (HY-P70653, MCE) of 10 ng/mL was used to induce EMT. Cells were co-cultured for 48 h.

Isolation and identification of EXO

MH-S cells (iCell-m078, iCell, China) were treated with IL-13/IL-4 for 48 h. According to Xiang et al. reported [28], the supernatant was collected and was subjected to gradient centrifugation in the following order: 300×g 10 min, 2000×g 10 min, and 10,000×g 30 min. Then, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 120,000×g for 70 min, after washing, this centrifugation was repeated once. Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA) used to identify EXO.

Western blot (WB)

WB was used to detect E-cadherin, α-SMA, Vimentin, RUNX1, TGF-β1, and GAPDH protein expressions. Mouse lungs or BECs were homogenized or lysed with RIPA lysates (P0013B, Beyotime, China), followed by centrifugation. Then, supernatant was determined the protein concentration using BCA kits (P0012, Beyotime, China) and diluted with lysates. After mixing with loading buffer and boiling for 5 min, 20 µg protein samples and 2 µL color pre-stained protein Marker (Monad Biotech, China) were mixed and were added into SDS-PAGE gel to separate the proteins, followed by transferring protein from gel to a PVDF membrane (GE Healthcare Life, USA). After blocking members, members were sequentially incubated with primary and secondary antibodies (Table 1). An electrochemiluminescence substrate developer was used to develop protein blots under a Qingxiang 610020-9Q chemiluminescence (Shanghai, China). ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA) was used to exposure imaging analyzing relative expression of GAPDH.

Quantitative Real-Time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

qPCR was used to detect Runx1, Tgf-β1, and miR-30a-5p gene levels. Total RNAs were collected from mouse lung or BECs using total RNA extraction kit (B618583-0250, BBI, China) and synthesized cDNA using reverse transcription reagent (PC7002, Aidlab, China) or miRNA RT-qPCR Primer (R10031.8, RiboBio, China) for first strand cDNA synthesis of mRNA or miRNA. The mRNA levels of Runx1, Tgf-β1, and miR-30a-5p were detected using a SYBP Green qPCR mix kit (PC6202, Aidlab, China) or miRNA RT-qPCR Starter kit (R11067.2, RiboBio, China). The 2−ΔΔCT method was used for statistical analysis compared to Gapdh or U6 mRNA levels. All primer information is shown in Table 2.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using Graph Prism v8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA) or SPSS 20.0 (SPSS, USA). The independent sample t-test was used for comparison between sham group and OVA group. Additionally, a one-way analysis of variance was applied to evaluate differences across multiple groups. Statistical significance was determined at a threshold of p < 0.05. The data was expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

Results

Establishing AA mouse model

As Supplementary Fig. 1 shows, we sensitized mice three times and challenged them with OVA for three consecutive days to establish AA models. Before euthanasia on day 24, we assessed airway hyper-responsiveness in Sham and OVA mice. Figure 1A shows that Penh index values of OVA mice were significantly lower than Sham mice. After euthanasia, BALF was collected to count the number of immune cells. Figure 1B shows that cell counts of total cells, eosinophils, neutrophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes all increased in OVA mice compared to Sham mice. Additionally, Masson staining of lungs showed that OVA Sensitization and challenge significantly increased the collagen volume fraction of mouse lungs (Fig. 1C, D). The above results confirm that we have successfully established a mouse model of AA.

MiR-30a-5p decreased and EMT was activated in the lungs of AA mice

We used RT-qPCR to detect miR-30a-5p levels. As shown in Fig. 2A, miR-30a-5p levels decreased in OVA mice compared to the sham, while Runx1 and Tgf-β1 mRNA increased. Furthermore, Western blot was used to measure the levels of EMT biomarkers (E-cadherin, α-SMA, and Vimentin), RUNX1, and TGF-β1 proteins. Results were shown in Fig. 2B, expressions of α-SMA, Vimentin, and RUNX1 all increased by nearly 150% of them in OVA mice, while E-cadherin decreased by 41.8%. The EMT biomarker gene levels were detected, and results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, which indicates that the EMT gene levels in OVA mice are consistent with those of proteins. Especially, we found that Tgf-β1 mRNA did not significantly change in OVA mice, but its protein expression increased by 1.87 times (Fig. 2A, B). Additionally, we used FISH to observe miR-30a-5p and found that miR-30a-5p (red) not mainly co-localized in the nucleus (blue), and the positive region area size was significantly reduced in OVA mice (Fig. 2C). These results suggest that miR-30a-5p downregulation may be related to pulmonary fibrosis and EMT activation in AA mice.

Primary mouse BECs identification

We isolated and cultured mouse BECs from mouse main bronchi. Under the microscope, the primary BECs of mice were cobblestone-shaped with smooth cell edges and nuclei located in the center of the cell. The nucleus was clearly seen under the microscope (Supplementary Fig. 3 A). Additionally, IF of PCK, CK5, and E-cadherin was used to identify BEC purity (Supplementary Fig. 3B, C). PCK, CK5, and E-cadherin positive cell rates were 97.47%, 97.83%, and 98.11% (Supplementary Fig. 3B, C).

Macrophages with miR-30a-5p overexpression antagonized IL-4/IL-13-induced EMT activation in mouse BECs

Macrophages are involved in the damage of epithelial cell barrier and the occurrence of EMT in AA [14]. Macrophages are highly susceptible to environment that drive their phenotypic differentiation, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis and progression of AA [15]. MiR-30a-5p mimics/mimics-NC-treated MH-S cells and BECs were co-cultured to observe effects of macrophages with miR-30a-5p overexpression on BECs. The crosstalk between macrophages and MH-S cells is a necessary step for the role of macrophage-derived EXO. IL-13 and IL-4 were used to establish in vitro AA models by incubating MH-S cells and BECs for 48 h [16]. RT-qPCR results showed that IL-13/IL-4 exposure decreased miR-30a-5p levels in BECs co-cultured with mimics-NC-transfected MH-S cells (Fig. 3A). Compared to the co-culture system of mimics-NC-treated MH-S cells and BECs, miR-30a-5p levels were hugely increased in BECs co-cultured with miR-30a-5p mimics-transfected MH-S cells and BECs. Additionally, IL-13/IL-4 exposure also decreased miR-30a-5p levels in co-culture system of miR-30a-5p mimics-transfected MH-S cells and BECs. It suggests that macrophages with miR-30a-5p overexpression can upregulate the expression of miR-30a-5p in BECs, while IL-13 and IL-4 can inhibit miR-30a-5p levels. To investigate whether EXO are involved in the transmission of miR-30a-5p, GW4869, an inhibitor of EXO synthesis, was used [27]. GW4869 intervention significantly decreased miR-30a-5p levels in IL-13/IL-4-exposed BECs co-cultured with miR-30a-5p mimics-transfected MH-S cells (Fig. 3A), suggesting that EXO are important mediators for the transmission of miR-30a-5p in MH-S cells and BECs.

Furthermore, Western blot was used to detect EMT biomarker expressions. IL-13/IL-4 exposure decreased E-cadherin in BECs co-cultured with mimics-NC/miR-30a-5p mimics-transfected MH-S cells, while α-SMA and Vimentin were increased (Fig. 3B, C). MiR-30a-5p-overexpressed macrophages-derived EXO inhibited the EMT of mouse BECs

TEM, NTA and Western blot assays were used to identify EXO (Fig. 4A-C). TEM and NTA observed cup-shaped vesicles with a mean particle size of 85.1 nm (Fig. 4A, B). Western blot results showed that EXO only expressed CD81 and ALIX proteins, while supernatant of lysed MH-S cells expressed CD81, ALIX and Grp94 proteins (Fig. 4C).

Subsequently, EXO that isolated from mimics-NC/miR-30a-5p mimics-transfected MH-S cells were used to incubate IL-13/IL-4-exposed BECs for 48 h. MiR-30a-5p mimics transfection significantly upregulated miR-30a-5p levels in EXO (Fig. 5A). Additionally, EXO derived from miR-30a-5p mimics-transfected MH-S cells increased miR-30a-5p levels in BECs (Fig. 5B) (Please refer to the supplementary files for methods of EXO treatment). Compared to the mimics-NC-EXO group, BECs treated with miR-30a-5p mimics-EXO and exhibited a tendency towards being rounder and more clustered (Fig. 5C). IF and PKH67 probe staining were used to mark EXO (the method shown in the supplementary Method) and results showed that the amount of EXO ingested by BECs was not affected by miR-30a-5p overexpression (Fig. 5D), confirming no statistically significant difference (Supplementary Fig. 4). Furthermore, Western blot assay was used to detect expressions of EMT biomarkers. EXO that was derived from miR-30a-5p mimics-transfected MH-S cells increased E-cadherin by nearly 50%, while α-SMA and Vimentin were significantly decreased by 63.1% and 84.4% (Fig. 5E). Additionally, RUNX1 and TGF-β1 expressions significantly decreased by 60.9% and 53.3% (Fig. 5D).

Overexpressing RUNX1 or TGF-β1 reversed the inhibition of miR-30a-5p overexpression on EMT in BECs

To explore the effects of RUNX1 and TGF-β1, plasmids carrying Runx1 or Tgf-β1 cDNA were transfected into BECs, as well as their empty vectors. As the results of Fig. 6A show that compared to oe-NC group, Runx1 mRNA increased by over 2 times in oe-RUNX1 group, as well as Tgf-β1 mRNA increased by over 4 times in oe-TGF-β1 group, suggesting the successful establishment of RUNX1 and TGF-β1-overexpressed BECs. Additionally, overexpressing RUNX1 or TGF-β1 did not affect the miR-30a-5p levels (Fig. 6B). The PKH67 staining results also indicated that BECs in oe-RUNX1 or oe-TGF-β1 group normally engulf EXO (Fig. 6C).

Silencing RUNX1 inhibited the EMT of mouse BECs exposed to IL-13/IL-4

Mouse BECs exposed to IL-13/IL-4 to induce EMT (Fig. 8A). Furthermore, to clarify the important role of RUNX1 in EMT activation, the siRNAs, siRUNX1#1/#2/#3, were used to silencing RUNX1. They significantly decreased RUNX1 by 48%, 75%, and 65% (Fig. 8B). Therefore, siRUNX1#2 were choice to explore the expression of EMT biomarkers. Discussion

Airway remodeling is a key clinical feature of AA, and studies suggest that EMT promotes airway remodeling [29]. Intranasal administration of house dust mites induced airway remodeling in mice and significantly activated EMT of mouse airway epithelium, as characterized by increased α-SMA and Vimentin expression, and decreased E-cadherin [30]. Both clinical and animal studies support the observation of significant EMT in AA [31]. OVA induced AA in mice, meanwhile, EMT was activated in the lung [10]. Pereira et al. [32] reported that N-cadherin was highly expressed in patients with AA. EMT is a critical driver of airway fibrosis and remodeling in asthma, contributing to chronic airway obstruction and tissue dysfunction. EMT is a reversible biological process of epithelial traits losing, characterized by decreased E-cadherin [8]. Multiple signaling pathways centered around TGF-β1 can induce epithelial cell dysfunction and their transformation into mesenchymal cells [33, 34]. In our AA mouse model, OVA challenge induced robust EMT activation, marked by decreased E-cadherin and increased α-SMA and Vimentin, along with collagen deposition in lung tissue. These changes align with the pathological hallmark of airway remodeling, where epithelial cells lose polarity and acquire mesenchymal traits, promoting fibrotic tissue accumulation.

IL-13 and IL-4 are commonly used to establish in vitro AA models [16]. Burgess et al. [35] reported that IL-13 treatment increased EMT biomarkers in human lung epithelial cells, including increased α-SMA and fibronectin, and decreased E-cadherin. Cheng et al. [36] reported that E-cadherin decreased and Vimentin increased in IL-4-exposed human nasal epithelial cells. Yu and co-workers [37] also found that IL-13 treatment increased vimentin and decreased E-cadherin in human bronchial epithelial cells. This study found that IL-13 and IL-4 induced EMT in BECs; after exposure to IL-13/IL-4, BEC morphology tended to be spindle-shaped and arranged relatively loosely and showed downregulated E-cadherin alongside upregulated α-SMA and Vimentin, which supports previous research.

miRNAs represent key epigenetic modulators in asthma, with dysregulated miRNA expression linked to immune dysfunction and epithelial injury [38]. Wang et al. [39] reported that miR-30a inhibited EMT in triple‑negative breast cancer. Zhang et al. [20] also found that miR-30a-5p significantly inhibited EMT in human nasal epithelial cells. Additionally, miR-30a-3p mimetics improved airway injury and inflammatory cell infiltration in OVA mice [40]. This study found that miR-30a-5p levels were downregulated in both OVA mice and IL-13/IL-4-exposed BECs. This study provides preliminary evidence that overexpressing miR-30a-5p in MH-S cells inhibits EMT in IL-13/IL-4-exposed BECs, shedding light on novel mechanisms linking miRNAs, EMT, and macrophages in AA. By demonstrating that miR-30a-5p overexpression in macrophages attenuates IL-4/IL-13-induced EMT in BECs—both in co-culture systems and via exosome-mediated transfer—our findings indicate that miR-30a overexpression may be a tractable target for interrupting asthma progression.

Furthermore, a central revelation of this study is the indispensable role of macrophage-derived EXO as nanoscale messengers in transmitting miR-30a-5p to bronchial epithelial cells, thereby modulating EMT. EXO, extracellular vesicles (30–150 nm) loaded with functional miRNAs, proteins, and nucleic acids, exhibit exceptional stability and tissue tropism [41], enabling intercellular communication in the asthmatic microenvironment. Mesenchymal stem cell-derived EXO inhibited EMT in rat tracheal epithelial cells [42]. Li et al. [43] found that macrophage-derived EXO promoted EMT in airway epithelial cells of OVA rats. Tu et al. [19] found that mannose-decorated exosomes carrying miR-511-3p alleviated asthma in mice. Collectively, EXO as delivery carriers for miRNA have promising development prospects in the treatment of asthma. Our findings demonstrated that miR-30a-5p overexpression in macrophages (MH-S cells) led to increased miR-30a-5p content within EXO, which were internalized by BECs in an EXO-dependent manner, as confirmed by PKH67 labeling and GW4869 inhibition experiments. Critically, blocking EXO biogenesis abrogated the protective effects of miR-30a-5p on EMT, highlighting that EXOs are not merely bystanders but essential vectors for miR-30a-5p delivery. Our study also highlights the translational potential of using macrophage-derived EXO as a natural nanocarrier for miRNA therapeutics.

Moreover, this study identified RUNX1 as a direct target of miR-30a-5p, providing a pivotal mechanistic link in asthma-associated EMT. TGF-β1 is a key cytokine that induces EMT via the Smad pathway [44, 45]. RUNX1, one of transcription factors in the Runx family, exerts its pro-EMT effects by promoting TGF-β pathway [46]. This study found that silencing RUNX1 inhibited EMT in BECs. Additionally, TGF-β1 also promotes RUNX1 expression in T cells [47]. This study revealed that in IL-13/IL-4-exposed BECs treated with macrophage-derived EXO carrying miR-30a-5p, overexpressing RUNX1 regulated TGF-β1 protein expression, while overexpressing TGF-β1 did not increase RUNX1 expression. This finding indicates a targeting effect of Runx1 on Tgf-β1. Additionally, this study found that RUNX1 overexpression in BECs reversed the protective effects of miR-30a-5p, increasing α-SMA, Vimentin, and TGF-β1 while decreasing E-cadherin. This suggests that miR-30a-5p exerts its anti-EMT effects by suppressing Runx1, thereby inhibiting downstream Tgf-β1 signaling.

This study has several limitations. While this study provides preliminary evidence for the roles of miR-30a-5p and EXO in AA-associated EMT and the reverse effects of RUNX1 or TGF-β1 overexpression on it. Additional experimental and clinical evidence is needed before these findings can be transformed into clinical applications. Future studies will address RUNX1 knockdown in human primary cells to directly assess its requirement for EMT activation and miR-30a-5p-mediated pathways in human samples, thereby clarifying mechanistic dependencies and advancing translational relevance.

Collectively, targeting this axis via EXO-delivered miR-30a-5p could disrupt the vicious cycle of epithelial injury and mesenchymal transformation, offering a precise approach to combat airway remodeling in asthma.

Conclusions

This study establishes that macrophage-derived exosomes carrying miR-30a-5p mitigate EMT in asthmatic BECs by directly targeting RUNX1 to suppress TGF-β1-mediated fibrogenesis. These findings not only deepen our understanding of miRNA-exosome crosstalk in airway epithelial remodeling but also provide basic data for novel therapeutic strategy to harness exosomal miR-30a-5p delivery to inhibit EMT, potentially addressing an unmet need for managing severe, remodeling-prone asthma.
