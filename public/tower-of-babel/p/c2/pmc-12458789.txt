New insights into the role of IFN-α/β and TLR7/8/9 in cancer immunotherapy and systemic autoimmunity
Authors: Arthur M Krieg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe role of type I interferon (IFN) in mediating tumor immunosurveillance, in situ vaccination, and response to other cancer immunotherapies is well established. The biologic and species-specific differences between type I IFN family members have not been sufficiently addressed. Recombinant IFN-α2 has been approved for the treatment of chronic viral infections (hepatitis B virus, hepatitis C virus) and some forms of cancer. IFN-β was approved to treat patients with multiple sclerosis but failed in the treatment of chronic viral infections or cancer. These distinct therapeutic applications most likely reflect distinct biologic roles of the individual IFNs that unfortunately remain poorly understood. IFN-β can be secreted by essentially all nucleated cells activated through most pattern recognition receptors (PRR). By contrast, IFN-α is predominantly secreted by the P1 subset of plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), which mediate viral defense and successful cancer immunotherapy following their activation by toll-like receptor (TLR)7 or TLR9 agonists. The biologic effects of TLR7 and TLR9 agonists depend critically on their structure and their ability to be processed by endosomal nucleases into fragments capable of binding two distinct agonist binding sites. The clinical activity of TLR7 and TLR9 agonists in cancer immunotherapy is associated with their induction of IFN-α secretion and P1 pDC differentiation.CD8+ T cells likely evolved for the primary purpose of killing viral/retroviral-infected cells. Immune cells detect viral/retroviral infected cells using TLR7 and TLR8 to distinguish viral GU-nucleotide-rich RNA from self-RNA modified with pseudouridine, and TLR9 to distinguish viral unmethylated CpG DNA from self-DNA modified with 5-methylcytosine. Recent studies have defined antagonist pockets in TLR7, TLR8 and TLR9 that bind degradation products of endogenous modified RNA and DNA to inhibit responses to host nucleic acids. These findings point to a role for TLR7/8/9 acting cooperatively to enable innate immunity to distinguish retroviral particles from apoptotic blebs, with important implications for optimising in situ vaccination and other cancer immunotherapy strategies, and treating systemic autoimmune diseases mediated by the inappropriate activation of TLR7/8/9 by apoptotic debris.Keywords: interferon, innate, dendritic, toll-like receptor - TLR, pathogen-associated molecular pattern - PAMP

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionised cancer immunotherapy, especially for treating ‘inflamed’ tumors with interferon (IFN) signatures associated with the presence of effective tumor-specific CD8+ T cells.1 2 The low response rate to ICI treatment of uninflamed tumors has inspired efforts to convert these into ‘inflamed’ tumors by inducing the production of type I IFN using innate immune activators to activate, expand, and/or induce antitumor CD8+ T cells.1 2 Type I IFN is critical for tumor immunosurveillance and for induction of antitumor CD8+ T cells in humans and in mouse tumor models.3 4 Unfortunately, cancer immunotherapy drug development in the decade since the first approvals of programmed death-1 (PD-1) pathway inhibitors has primarily been characterized by repeated human clinical failures of type I IFN inducing agents. This is in spite of uniformly positive results in mouse tumor models thought to support clinical development. To resume progress in the development of cancer immunotherapies, there is an urgent need for a deeper understanding of the association between type I IFN and CD8+ T cell induction in humans, which this review will attempt to provide.

In humans, IFN-α drives CD8+ T cells; IFN-β drives regulatory T cells

Humans have 17 functional type I IFNs, including 13 IFN-α genes and one each encoding IFN-β, IFN-ε, IFN-κ, and IFN-ω.5,7 Interestingly, these all act through a common heterodimeric receptor, the IFN-α/β receptor (IFNAR)1/2, composed of the two transmembrane IFNAR1 and IFNAR2 proteins with strikingly divergent binding affinities to type I IFNs (table 1).8 IFNAR is expressed on essentially all nucleated cells, but the effects of receptor binding are pleiotropic. Briefly, IFNAR binding causes phosphorylation of the constitutively associated tyrosine kinases Tyk2 and Jak1.5 6 These phosphorylate STAT proteins that dimerize or complex with DNA-binding proteins that translocate to the nucleus, promoting the expression of IFN-inducible genes.5 6 Although only a few of the IFN-α subtypes and IFN-β have been studied in any detail, they stimulate subtype-specific intracellular signaling pathways and induce distinct transcriptional profiles in IFNAR1/2 expressing cells such as T lymphocytes and dendritic cells (DCs).5 6 9 10 This is perhaps due to their unique binding affinities for the receptors, divergence in their respective complex lifetimes, and/or timing of synthesis and secretion by immune cell subsets.56 8,10 IFN-β binds IFNAR1/2 with 10–100× greater affinity than IFN-α211 and therefore may dominate biologically when present at equimolar concentrations (table 1). The large number of type I IFN subspecies that signal through a common receptor makes it likely that they have been evolutionarily retained for selective functions, although their individual mechanistic contributions to host defense remain obscure.5 6 In general, IFN-α directly stimulates CD8+ T cells, promoting rapid clonal expansion and memory formation, with sustained exposure resulting in T cell exhaustion.6 CD8+ T cell responses are also promoted indirectly by type I IFN, through the recruitment and activation of DC populations and enhanced antigen processing and presentation. IFNAR signaling induces increased IFNAR expression in many immune cells, including plasmacytoid DCs that are the major source of IFN-α during infection, in a positive feedback loop that further increases IFN-α secretion6 (see also below). In summary, in vitro studies show clear biologic differences between IFN-α2 and IFN-β, but the other IFN-α isotypes have scarcely been studied. All 13 human isotypes of IFN-α have been conserved through evolution, which is believed to be due to providing survival benefit by enhancing CD8+ T cell induction in response to retroviral/viral pathogens.5 6

IFN-α2 and IFN-β recombinant proteins and gene therapies have been evaluated in human clinical trials in a wide range of conditions, demonstrating strikingly divergent clinical effects. IFN-α2 is approved for the treatment of chronic infection by hepatitis B and hepatitis C viruses, as well as for some forms of human cancer, but failed to gain approval in the treatment of patients with multiple sclerosis (MS) (table 1).12 13 In marked contrast, the human clinical efficacy of IFN-β in recombinant or gene therapy form was insufficient to support Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for any oncology or infectious indications. IFN-β, instead, was approved by the FDA for the treatment of patients with MS, in which IFN-β promotes immunomodulatory responses including induction of interleukin (IL)-10 and regulatory T (Treg) cells.14

Therapy with IFN-α2 has been associated with induction of autoimmune diseases including systemic lupus erythematosus, psoriasis, rheumatoid arthritis, thyroiditis, vitiligo, hepatitis, immune thrombocytopenia, and diabetes.15 16 In contrast, therapy with IFN-β does not induce autoimmune disease, but rather reduces rheumatoid arthritis disease activity.16 Human gain-of-function mutations and diseases provide a separate line of evidence, demonstrating a significant biological difference between IFN-α and IFN-β.17,23

To summarize their distinct human biology, IFN-α drives CD8+ T cells while IFN-β drives Treg cells. In marked contrast to their human biology, both IFN-α and IFN-β promote strong CD8+ T cell responses that mediate tumor rejection in murine tumor models, with the efficacy of IFN-β being slightly superior.24,26 Furthermore, human and mouse IFN-β gene therapy exhibits different antitumor mechanisms in mouse models.26 This distinct biology of IFN-α/β in rodents versus primates may also be relevant to the interpretation of experiments in which strong inducers of IFN-β secretion, such as GMP-AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) agonists (table 1), cause tumor regression in mice. Considering that recombinant IFN-β causes tumor regression in mouse models but not in humans, inducers of predominately IFN-β secretion should not be expected to translate from efficacy in mice to efficacy in humans. In addition to other limitations described for mouse tumor models,27 the species-specific differences in type I IFN biology would seem to invalidate the use of mouse models for investigating therapeutics designed to induce type I IFN expression.

The primary source of IFN-α is the P1 subset of plasmacytoid dendritic cells activated through TLR7 or TLR9, which mediate host defense against viral or retroviral infection by inducing CD8+ T cells

Plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) are the predominant source of IFN-α in response to viral infections (table 1).28 29 pDC secretion of IFN-α is induced by viral GU-rich RNA via TLR7, and by viral unmethylated CpG DNA via TLR9. These two nucleic acid immune sensing receptors evolved as a part of nucleic acid immunity to protect genome integrity against viral and retroviral infection (table 2).30 31 Pseudouridine modification of host RNA prevents TLR7 stimulation.32 CpG methylation and suppression in host DNA prevents TLR9 stimulation,17 33 enabling discrimination of viral and self-nucleic acids. Viral nucleic acids are processed by endosomal DNase II to generate the CpG DNA fragments that bind TLR9 at two sites, and by RNAse T2 and T6 to generate the GU RNA fragments that bind the two agonist sites in TLR7 (and TLR8).34,38 These nucleases also process endogenous host nucleic acids, generating fragments that bind antagonist sites in TLR7/8/9, inhibiting the response to agonist binding and thus providing further discrimination between self and non-self nucleic acids (table 2, figure 1E).39,42

The three flavors of danger. The innate immune system evolved pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that function cooperatively to distinguish three major classes of perturbation from normal homeostasis: intracellular infection (A), extracellular infection (B), and sterile tissue damage (C). Intracellular infection by DNA viruses and retroviruses able to integrate into host cell genomes endangers genome integrity. To protect genome integrity, higher eukaryotes evolved PRRs able to detect retroviral replicative complexes. These include toll-like receptor (TLR)7 and TLR8, which are activated by viral guanine-uracil (GU))-rich RNA, but not by endogenous pseudouridine-modified RNA, and TLR9, which is activated by unmethylated CpG motifs in viral genomes, but which are methylated and suppressed in the host genome. TLR7/8/9 are differentially expressed in various dendritic cell (DC) and myeloid populations, with variable expression across species. When activated, TLR7/8/9 induce interferon (IFN)-α secretion, activate DC and myeloid cells to present antigen in a T helper (Th)1-like cytokine milieu, and promote rapid expansion of antigen-specific CD8+ T cell responses able to eradicate viral-infected cells. In addition, B cells are activated to secrete protective antibodies that slow or stop viral infection of new cells (A). Extracellular pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) such as lipopolysaccharides and peptidoglycans are detected via a panoply of TLR2/4/5/6, NLRs, ALRs, and other PRRs, triggering inflammatory responses with recruitment of neutrophils and other cell populations specialized for clearing extracellular infection, without notable induction of IFN-α secretion (B). In damaged or stressed cells, damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as self-DNA and HMGB1 activate GMP-AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) and other pathways promoting IFN-β secretion as the dominant type I IFN (C). Stressed and apoptotic cells release membrane blebs containing endogenous nucleic acids and other DAMPs and induce macrophage expression of lipid uptake receptors such as CD36 and macrophage receptor with collagenous domains (MARCO). A key challenge for innate immunity must be distinguishing retroviral/viral particles from the structurally similar endogenous apoptotic blebs and exosomes (D). Viral/Retroviral particles contain GU-rich RNA and unmethylated CpG double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), which bind and activate two agonist sites in TLR7/8 (RNA) and TLR9 (DNA), respectively. Conversely, host RNA in apoptotic blebs or exosomes contains multiple modifications including pseudouridine, 2’-O-methylated nucleosides, N6-methyladenosine, inosine, and N7-methylguanosine (in the 5’-terminal cap), while host DNA is predominantly CpG-methylated and contains telomere structures. As reviewed in the text, recent structural studies have defined antagonist sites in TLR7/8 binding 2’-O-Me-guanosine RNA degradation products38 in addition to the previously defined antagonist site in TLR9 binding guanosine-rich DNA sequences identified in telomere structures.39,41 Thus, TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 appear to be the key PRRs able to discriminate retroviral particles from apoptotic blebs or exosomes, based on differences in self and non-selfmodification of RNA and DNA (E). In addition to preventing degradation products of self-nucleic acids from stimulating the agonist sites in TLR7/8/9, these modifications appear to bind the antagonist binding sites in TLR7/8/9, improving self:non-self discrimination and potentially reducing the risk of developing autoimmune disease (shown for TLR7/9; E). cDC, conventional dendritic cell; MP, mononuclear phagocyte; PMN, polymorphonuclear leucocyte.

Activation of pDC by viruses or synthetic TLR7 or TLR9 agonists induces their differentiation into three distinct subpopulations: P1, P2, and P3. These are defined based on surface expression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and cluster of differentiation 80 (CD80).30 Although their biologic roles remain to be fully elucidated, these three pDC subsets have distinct transcriptional profiles and functional properties. P1 pDCs (PD-L1+/CD80−) account for most of the IFN-α secretion. The biologic role(s) of P2 pDCs (PD-L1+/CD80+) is unknown at present, but P3 pDCs (PD-L1−/CD80+) promote CD4+ T cell proliferation and TH2 responses.30

The secretion of IFN-α and P1 pDC differentiation depends critically on the structure of the TLR7 or TLR9 agonist evaluated (table 3; data for P1 pDC frequency induced by different agents extracted from figure 8a in the study by Alculumbre et al).30 Two main subsets of TLR7 agonists have been defined, including small molecule agonists such as R848 and GU-rich RNA agonists. Small molecules bind TLR7 (and/or TLR8) through one of the two agonist binding sites, causing an allosteric shift that activates the nuclear factor kappa B pathway.35 36 42 43 GU-rich RNA binds TLR7 at both of the defined agonist binding sites, inducing a distinct allosteric shift and a higher percentage of P1 pDC differentiation and IFN-α secretion when compared with R848.3536 43,46 Three main classes of synthetic CpG DNA TLR9 agonists have been described, which differ primarily in their backbone chemistry and presence or absence of polyG sequence motifs that form G-quadruplex structures similar to those present in retroviral and viral genomes.47 48 CpG-A DNA TLR9 agonists have a native phosphodiester backbone and form G-quadruplex structures, while CpG-B and CpG-C DNAs are synthesized with nuclease-resistant phosphorothioate backbones and do not form G-quadruplex structures.47 49 Of all agents tested, only the CpG-A DNA TLR9 agonist induces >50% of resting pDCs to differentiate into the P1 subset (table 3).30 This is consistent with reports that CpG-A TLR9 agonists induce higher levels of IFN-α expression than other immune activators.47 49 These CpG-A TLR9 agonists induce relatively weak B cell activation compared with CpG-B and CpG-C TLR9 agonists with a nuclease-resistant phosphorothioate backbone.33 47 CpG-B TLR9 agonists have gained regulatory approvals as adjuvants in two vaccines with excellent safety records, HeplisavB and Cyfendus. They are, however, relatively weak inducers of IFN-α secretion and P1 pDC differentiation (table 3).30 33 47 49

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) respond to the RNA virus SARS-CoV-2 via TLR7/8-dependent signaling with P1 pDC differentiation, IFN-α secretion, and induction of antiviral CD4+ and CD8+ T cells.44 45 50 51 Asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2-infected individuals demonstrate higher proportions of circulating P1-like pDCs when compared with patients hospitalized with severe COVID-19 infection. Patients with severe COVID-19 had impaired pDC-mediated control of SARS-CoV-2.44 45 52 Some patients presenting with life-threatening COVID-19 or influenza virus infection have genetic defects in TLR7 or other pathways mediating pDC activation and IFN-α secretion.53,55 These data suggest a role for P1 pDCs in defense against viral infection. TLR8 is structurally related to TLR7 and is also activated by GU-rich RNA contributing to defense against viral infection in ways that are incompletely understood.56 57

Host defense against DNA viruses such as herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1), HSV-2, and murine cytomegalovirus likewise relies at least in part on TLR9 activation of P1 pDCs.58,60 Gene therapy using adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors results in TLR9-mediated pDC activation and anti-AAV CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses.61,63 Engineering AAV vectors to evade TLR9 activation by reducing CpG content reduces the adaptive immune response, improving the duration of vector expression.64 65 Considering the TLR9-mediated suppression of AAV expression, great evolutionary pressure must have been present on DNA viruses and retroviruses infecting vertebrates. Consistent with this, retroviruses and all small DNA viruses show marked suppression of genomic CpG content down to <20% of the expected frequency if base usage were random.66 The remaining CpG dinucleotides in the HIV genome are preferentially located in the long terminal repeats, comprising the binding sites of obligatory transcription factors.67 There is no known biologic process that could account for such CpG suppression, indicating an extraordinary level of selective pressure against CpG retention in retroviral genomes.

Mice express TLR9 in essentially all myeloid cell populations, as opposed to its selective expression in human B cells and pDC. Mouse TLR8 does not recognize agonists stimulating human immune cells,31 providing further reason to avoid the use of mouse models for developing human TLR therapeutics.

To summarize, IFN-α secretion and host defense against viral or retroviral infection depend in part on P1 pDC activated through TLR7 or TLR9, and promoting CD8+ T cell responses, with the strongest reported induction by CpG-A DNA TLR9 agonists.

Induction of IFN-α secretion and differentiation of P1 pDC is associated with the human clinical antitumor efficacy of innate immune activators

Evidence that TLR7 and TLR9 agonists can induce IFN-α and promote CD8+ T cell differentiation spurred the clinical evaluation of several classes of TLR7 and TLR9 agonists, with generally low rates of tumor regression as monotherapies (table 3). To determine whether the in vitro induction of P1 pDC by a panel of diverse TLR7 and TLR9 agonists may be associated with therapeutic outcomes for related agonists in vivo, I examined published data from Alculumbre et al together with published human clinical trial data using structurally similar agonists (data extracted and summarized in table 3).30 The CpG-A TLR9 agonist tested by Alculumbre et al, 2216, is commonly used as an experimental reagent for inducing IFN-α expression. It is comparable to vidutolimod, the only CpG-A in human clinical development, which uses a virus-like particle for delivery and induces higher levels of IFN-α secretion from human PBMC than any other tested TLR agonist.49 In human clinical development, only the CpG-A TLR9 agonist in vidutolimod showed substantial monotherapy efficacy by intratumoral injection in patients with PD-1 blockade-resistant melanoma. RECIST V.1.1 responses were observed in 8/40 patients, with one additional postprogression responder, including four systemic responses (regression of liver, lung, and visceral soft tissue metastases).68 Two of these responders had prior progressive disease during therapy using talimogene laherparepvec in combination with PD-1 blockade, suggesting that CpG-A agonists have biologically distinct effects from the oncolytic virus. Intratumoral injection of vidutolimod demonstrated encouraging evidence of clinical activity in combination with pembrolizumab in patients with PD-1 blockade-resistant melanoma, with overall response rate per RECIST V.1.1 of 23.5% (23/98). This included seven patients with complete response and 16 patients with partial response with a median duration of response of 25.2 months.68 An additional four patients had postprogression responses. Notably, these responses were systemic, with similar degrees of tumor shrinkage in injected and uninjected lesions, including regression of uninjected metastases in the lungs, spleen, central nervous system, bone, and liver, which respond less well to cancer immunotherapies. Patients who responded to vidutolimod had non-inflamed tumors at baseline and induction of an IFNγ gene signature following treatment, as well as increased systemic expression of the IFN-inducible chemokine CXCL10.49 Intratumoral vidutolimod in combination with systemic nivolumab induced a major pathologic response (MPR) in 57% (17/31) of patients with high-risk resectable melanoma,69 which compares favorably with the 59% MPR to systemic ipilimumab and nivolumab combination reported by Blank et al in a similar population.70 A notable feature of the vidutolimod combination with PD-1 blockade is the relative safety, with grade 3+ treatment-related adverse events reported in 21% of patients,69 compared with 39% of patients in the combination with ipilimumab.70 Induction of MPR by vidutolimod was associated with increased CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and pDCs within the tumor microenvironment as well as gene signatures for tertiary lymphoid structures, pDCs, and macrophage activation.69

CpG-B and CpG-C DNAs with phosphorothioate backbones induce much lower P1 pDC differentiation and IFN-α secretion when compared with CpG-A TLR9 agonists with native phosphodiester backbones and G-quadruplex structures47 49 and have shown little clinical benefit in the treatment of patients with advanced melanoma—either as monotherapy or in combination with standard of care chemotherapy or ipilimumab—including three failed phase III clinical trials (table 3).71,75 These findings demonstrate that even among TLR9 agonists, efficacy in cancer immunotherapy is associated with the magnitude of in vitro P1 pDC induction and IFN-α secretion. Clinical development of other P1 pDC inducers similar to agents shown in table 3 has revealed only occasional clinical responses. Live influenza virus was second only to CpG-A in P1 pDC induction, and might be functionally similar to talimogene laherparepvec, the first approved oncolytic virus for cancer immunotherapy. Intratumoral injection of talimogene laherparepvec can induce local tumor response but a low rate of systemic response in uninjected lesions. This is in contrast to the relatively high rate of systemic response seen with vidutolimod, the only CpG-A in clinical development. These findings raise the intriguing possibility that the magnitude of P1 pDC differentiation, and by extension, secretion of all 13 IFN-α gene products, may govern the induction of systemic versus local CD8+ T cell responses.

The level of P1 pDCs in the blood of immunotherapy-naïve patients with melanoma is associated with improved survival, while the transcriptionally distinct P3 pDC subset that does not secrete type I IFN is associated with reduced survival.76 Studies performed prior to the discovery of pDC subsets generally showed that tumor-infiltrating pDCs are immune-suppressive and associated with a poor prognosis.77,82 To be clear, pDCs are generally considered to be only modestly effective antigen-presenting cells, if at all. TLR9 stimulation induces tumor-associated pDCs to secrete tumor neoantigens-laden exosomes that are taken up and cross-presented by conventional DCs.83 Further studies are needed to characterize human tumor-associated pDC subsets and their potential modulation using TLR7 and TLR9 agonists.

IFN-α evolved to protect genome integrity against retroviruses and other intracellular pathogens

The type I IFN system evolved approximately 250 million years ago as a part of the innate immune defenses against viruses and retroviruses,5,7 specifically as a key mediator of what has been termed ‘nucleic acid immunity’ in higher eukaryotes.31 Nucleic acid immunity starts from the premise that all forms of life must protect their genome integrity against exogenous threats, such as bacteriophage in bacteria, and retroviruses in eukaryotes. The earliest forms of nucleic acid immunity evolved in archaebacteria about 1.7 billion years ago, protecting their genome integrity against bacteriophage with exquisitely sequence-specific defenses to destroy foreign nucleic acids. These included CRISPR-Cas and DNA restriction enzymes.31 Higher eukaryotes evolved nucleic acid immune sensing receptors, some of which can differentiate retroviral and viral nucleic acids from self based on highly conserved structural differences discussed earlier (table 2). Nucleic acid immune sensing receptors function as subsets of the broader families of pattern recognition receptors (PRR), which evolved the ability to distinguish at least three broad classes of ‘danger’: (i) intracellular infection; (ii) extracellular infection; and (iii) sterile cell damage (figure 1).84,86

In considering the evolution of the defenses against intracellular infections, this discussion will focus on retroviruses as the simplest threat to genome integrity. Their relatively short single-stranded RNA genome, packaged within particles derived from host cell membranes, makes them difficult to detect. About half of our genome comprises reverse-transcribed sequences suggesting an ongoing need for nucleic acid immunity to prevent further retroviral infection. TLR7/8/9 appear to be the key PRRs able to detect viral replicative complexes. We can infer that the expression of TLR7/8/9 in various populations of DCs, myeloid cells, and B cells evolved at least in part to promote effector CD8+ T cells, notwithstanding the existence of alternative pathways for CD8+ T cell activation (figure 1A), and many other TLR7/8/9-induced T-cell independent effects contributing to the induction of an ‘antiviral state’. The P1 pDC plays a unique role in secreting high local concentrations of IFN-α that activate IFNAR on nearby innate immune cells, which together induce CD8+ T cells specific for whatever novel antigens are present. Immune tolerance mechanisms normally prevent induction of autoimmune T cells. The cells induced are by default selected for specificity to viral antigens presented by viral-infected cells. The effectiveness of CpG detection via TLR9 in retroviral defense may be inferred by the previously noted suppression of CpG content to be <20% of that expected in all vertebrate retroviral genomes.66 67

In comparison to retroviral nucleic acids, it is relatively easy to detect the presence of extracellular infections using PRRs specific for a wide array of bacterial and fungal products such as lipopeptides, peptidoglycans, beta-glucans, lipopolysaccharides, and flagellin (figure 1B). Bacterial RNA and DNA can also stimulate TLR7 and TLR9 in pDCs. pDC IFN-α secretion is also suppressed by TGF-β, IL-10, and prostaglandin E2, which are induced by many bacterial and fungal PRR ligands.87 88 Combinatorial stimulation of various PRR sets ‘informs’ the immune system of the pathogen class, reducing the risk of inappropriate CD8+ T cell induction in the setting of extracellular pathogens.84,86

Sterile cell damage is the third form of danger that must be distinguished by innate immunity, which we can infer evolved to restore host homeostasis as swiftly as possible, reducing inflammation and inducing angiogenesis and other wound repair mechanisms (figure 1C).85 89 Since tissue damage through evolution generally would have been accompanied by infection, the innate immune system must initially ‘assume’ that any stressed or damaged tissue is infected. A wide range of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) are released by stressed or dying cells, as soluble factors or within apoptotic debris and membrane blebs.85 89 These are bound and cleared by the complement protein C1Q or taken up by immune cells via lipid uptake receptors such as CD36 and macrophage receptor with collagenous domains.90,92 The DNA damage response, chromosomal instability, and other cell stresses trigger release of nuclear DNA into the cytoplasm, including endogenous retroviruses and other retroelements that entered the genome many millions of years ago.93 94 These induce cGAS-STING pathway,95,97 triggering IFN-β secretion, as well as the activated in melanoma 2 inflammasome, causing IL-1β secretion.98,100 By inducing the secretion of chemokines and the expression of adhesion molecules, these inflammatory mediators recruit a wide range of immune cells expressing diverse pathogen-selective PRRs into damaged tissues, thereby increasing the sensitivity for detecting and classifying infection. Release of high mobility group proteins (HMGBs) such as HMGB1 promotes responses to many PAMPs and activates the receptor of advanced glycation end-products.85 89 HMGB proteins bind immune stimulatory RNA and DNA, promoting activation of nucleic acid immune sensing receptors,101,103 and cGAS contributes to antiviral defense.104

In the absence of any concurrent PAMPs to indicate intracellular or extracellular infection, innate immunity must ‘conclude’ that HMGB1 and cGAS-STING signaling alone reflect sterile cell immune inflammation. Local immune cells now suppress other immune defenses, especially CD8+ T cells that risk autoimmunity, and instead promote angiogenesis and tissue repair. Indeed, STING signaling impairs CD8+ T cell memory,105 suppresses the pDC response to TLR9 agonists,106 107 and promotes vascular normalization.108 In the absence of infection, physiologic cGAS-STING also suppresses organ-specific and systemic autoimmunity in mouse models.109,111 Tumors co-opt these mechanisms, releasing DAMPs, triggering the damage/death response, which promotes a prometastatic tumor microenvironment. This supports the concept that tumors are ‘addicted to death’.85 89 95 112 From our deeper understanding of the functional differences between IFN-α and IFN-β, the clinical failures of STING agonists113,115 should be unsurprising since they induce an IFN-β dominant type I IFN response, promoting Treg and IL-10 responses in humans. The murine efficacy of STING agonists can be accounted for by the species-specific induction of effector CD8+ T cells in response to any type I IFN.24,26

A major challenge for the innate immune system must be distinguishing retroviral particles from the membrane blebs and other debris released by apoptotic cells (figure 1D). In both settings, the membrane particles have been generated from host cell membranes, with surface phosphatidylserine promoting efferocytosis. To protect genome integrity, innate immunity must induce CD8+ T cell responses to the antigens associated with retroviral particles. To avoid causing autoimmune diseases, innate immunity must not induce adaptive immune responses to sterile apoptotic blebs or exosomes. TLR7/8/9 are the only PRRs able to distinguish these particles. They must have been under intense evolutionary pressure to function coordinately with a high level of precision. As reviewed above (table 2), structural and functional studies have recently revealed that all three of these receptors contain two agonist sites with specificity for degradation products of viral RNA (TLR7/8) or DNA (TLR9) and a single antagonist binding site with specificity for degradation products of endogenous nucleic acids (figure 1E). Genetic deficiency of a single factor that assists with clearance of apoptotic debris, C1Q, results in systemic lupus erythematosus in >90% of humans. Gain-of-function mutations or inappropriate activation of TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 have been associated with systemic autoimmune diseases mediated by autoimmune CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and by autoreactive B cells secreting diverse autoantibodies contributing to autoimmune disease through complex and incompletely understood mechanisms.18 19 42 116 More than 5% of humans will develop an organ-specific or systemic autoimmune disease, often following viral illness, suggesting that evolution has set the immune rheostat at a level that accepts a certain background risk of autoimmunity as the price of protecting the genome and generally defending against viral infections.

Conclusion

Despite extensive evidence that type I IFN mediates antitumor immunity for in situ vaccination and other cancer immunotherapy strategies, clinical trials of innate immune agonists that induce type I IFN expression rarely benefit patients with advanced cancer. These failures point to a need to dive more deeply into type I IFN biology, starting with the distinct human clinical activities of IFN-α and IFN-β, their production by individual cell types, and their induction by various PRRs (table 1). The present manuscript summarizes experimental data including results of human clinical trials supporting three conclusions with therapeutic implications: (i) IFN-α drives predominantly CD8+ T cells while IFN-β drives Treg; (ii) the P1 subset of pDC is the major natural IFN-α secreting cell type; and (iii) P1 pDC and IFN-α secretion can be variably induced by TLR7 or TLR9 ligands (tables1 2). These findings suggest new directions for cancer immunotherapy, including the development of TLR7/8/9 RNA/DNA agonists as synthetic mimics of retroviral replicative complexes, containing the minimal signals that evolved for CD8+ T cell induction to protect genome integrity and limit viral replication. Development of lipid delivery systems for such agonists builds on the successful use of such systems for delivery of other nucleic acid therapeutics into liver immune cells and systemically, to tumor-associated lymph nodes via lipid uptake receptors.117 This avoids the induction of neutralizing antibodies to virus-like particles and oncolytic viruses.118

These findings also suggest a new approach to developing surrogate assays for screening new type I IFN-inducing drug candidates for clinical development. Mouse tumor models have been commonly used to select innate immune activators for clinical development and to ‘optimize’ in situ vaccination and other cancer immunotherapies. Murine models are unsatisfactory for this purpose because of their species-specific differences in IFN-β biology and differences in TLR expression and function. It is tempting to speculate that innate immunity and the type I IFN system evolved to function so differently in humans and mice because of lifespan differences as well as maintaining the balance between autoimmunity and viral defense. Humans reach reproductive maturity after more than a decade, by which time they have experienced a large number of viral infections that need to be cleared. Traumatic sterile or infected injuries must be healed without induction of autoimmune disease. In contrast, mice reach reproductive maturity in a few weeks, and as a prey species, significant trauma is likely to be rapidly lethal, so there may be relatively less risk from induction of CD8+ T cell responses in settings of sterile tissue damage.

Instead of using such murine tumor models, I propose that the most promising surrogate assay to use during drug development is induction of IFN-α secretion in human immune cells. For initial screening, such assays can be performed in human PBMC. Promising agents could then be screened in human tumor-associated immune cells, as reported previously with vidutolimod.119 Since IFN-α is predominantly secreted by the P1 subset of pDC, evaluation of pDC subsets may be an informative addition to measuring IFN-α secretion. To date, there is insufficient published literature to substitute such assays for the direct measure of IFN-α protein and/or RNA expression in heterogeneous populations of immune cells. Alternative surrogate assays for type I IFN responses such as induction of PD-L1 or various IFN gene transcriptional signatures do not distinguish between IFN-β and IFN-α induction.

An important challenge in understanding type I IFN biology is the paucity of experimental data addressing the unique roles of the 13 IFN-α genes that have been maintained throughout human evolution, or the species-specific differences in their evolution and function.5,7 Only a few studies have attempted to determine how differences in binding IFNAR translate into distinct biologic effects of the individual type I IFN family members. One could use methods such as CRISPR to knockout these genes, individually and in combinations, to elucidate their function. In the absence of such findings, teleological deduction is another scientific tool that has been employed by generations of immunologists, with variable success. Paul Ehrlich’s concept of horror autotoxicus famously denied the possibility of autoimmune disease using the argument that “It would be dysteleologic in the highest degree…”, which delayed the acceptance of the reality of autoimmune diseases among immunologists for many years.120 On the other hand, Janeway’s hypothesis of PRRs relied essentially on his teleologic deduction that “self-non-self discrimination arose long before the development of specific recognition, and that even today, many immune defense mechanisms are based on such non-antigen specific responses”.86 In the decades since Janeway’s prophetic prediction, immunologists have discovered several families of PRR as reviewed earlier, but we still lack a comprehensive understanding of how they function, and how they can be most effectively harnessed for therapeutic applications. Teleologic deduction may provide a useful tool, at the least to guide further experimental investigations. PRR evolution should have endowed the system of innate immunity with the ability to distinguish three fundamental types of danger: (i) intracellular infection; (ii) extracellular infection; and (iii) sterile tissue damage (figure 1). Likewise, CD8+ T cells clearly have evolved for the purpose of eliminating host cells that have been infected by retroviral or other intracellular pathogens. We can deduce that the retroviral replicative complex must contain the minimal set of PRR agonists required to stimulate the diverse DC and other myeloid cell populations that coordinately regulate effective CD8+ T cell induction. The only PRRs known to be able to distinguish retroviral particles from self are TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9, as detailed above. TLR7/9 are the sole PRRs able to induce maximal IFN-α secretion and P1 pDC differentiation, while the antiviral role of TLR8, which is expressed in monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, myeloid DCs and Treg cells, has been partially elucidated.56 57

Many questions remain to be explored, such as the biologic effects of concurrent signaling induced by viral nucleic acid mimics in the diverse immune cell subsets expressing TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9, as the most fundamental and minimal signals able to induce CD8+ T cells able to identify and lyse infected host cells in the absence of other inflammatory signals. Further studies need to be performed to evaluate the possibility that TLR7/8/9 evolved for the purpose of enabling innate immunity to distinguish between retroviral particles and apoptotic blebs and other debris from uninfected cells, based on their differences in nucleic acid content (figure 1E). Such studies should also address the possibility that TLR7/8/9 expression in distinct populations of innate immune cells may cooperatively regulate the magnitude of CD8+ T cell induction via previously described concepts such as ‘crowd wisdom’ and quorum sensing, as proposed for other innate immune responses.121

Oncolytic DNA viruses such as talimogene laherparepvec would be expected to costimulate TLR7/8/9, and to generate antagonist nucleic acids from lysis of host cells (figure 1E). Viruses also use multiple mechanisms to suppress IFN-mediated immune defenses. They induce the production of neutralizing antibodies that impair DC and CD8+ T cell responses.122 Combined with the logistic challenges of administering infectious viruses in clinical care settings, it seems preferable to perform in situ vaccination with a synthetic mimic of the infectious virus. I propose that a mimic of a retroviral replicative complex activating all six TLR7/8/9 agonist sites and none of the antagonist sites could provide superior efficacy for in situ vaccination strategies and as an adjuvant for promoting CD8+ T cell responses.
