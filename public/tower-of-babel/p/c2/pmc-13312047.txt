Raptor and Drp1 Function Synthetically to Control Hypoxic Death and the Mitochondrial Network in Caenorhabditis elegans
Authors: Julien Goldstick, Diego Compte, Chun‐Ling Sun, Alison E Ritter, C Michael Crowder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHypoxia induces mitochondrial fragmentation. Whether this fragmentation promotes or prevents cell death and whether the mitochondrial dynamics machinery plays a role are unresolved. To address these questions, we measured the effect of hypoxia on mitochondrial morphology in a Caenorhabditis elegans Raptor mutant resistant to hypoxic death and in mutants with disrupted mitochondrial fission and fusion. The Raptor loss‐of‐function mutant reduced hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation and death. However, forcing mitochondrial fragmentation prior to hypoxia by combining the Raptor mutation with a loss‐of‐function mutation in mitofusin did not increase hypoxic death. A loss‐of‐function mutation in drp‐1, which is required for mitochondrial fission, did not block hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation nor enhance Raptor hypoxia resistance; rather, drp‐1(lf) was surprisingly mildly hypoxia resistant and partially suppressed the high‐level hypoxia resistance of the Raptor mutant. Likewise, loss of DRP‐1 function interacted synthetically with the Raptor(lf) mutant to produce tangled mitochondria, demonstrating a role of Raptor in maintenance of the mitochondrial network. Vitamin B12 supplementation and feeding with a bacterial strain replete in vitamin B12 mitigated hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation. Our results demonstrate that fragmented mitochondria do not necessarily promote hypoxic cell death, and hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation is mechanistically distinct from physiological mitochondrial fission.

Introduction

Mitochondria are the primary consumers of cellular oxygen and as such are the first organelle affected when the cell is deprived of oxygen. Lack of oxygen blocks ATP production by the mitochondrial electron transport chain, resulting in a cascade of effects throughout the cell ultimately producing cell death. Pharmacological mitigation of mitochondrial dysfunction or its consequences in therapies against hypoxic‐injury diseases such as stroke or myocardial infarction has unfortunately failed to produce significant improvements in patient outcomes [1, 2]. However, hypoxia results in multiple mitochondrial pathologic features, many of which have not been specifically targeted by therapies. One such pathology is hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation [3, 4, 5].

Mitochondria undergo a continuous process of fission and fusion (called mitochondrial dynamics) that shapes the mitochondrial network and contributes to mitochondrial health [6, 7]. Under normal physiological conditions, mitochondrial fusion dilutes dysfunctional mitochondria and results in a net improvement in mitochondrial function. Fission, on the other hand, produces small spherical mitochondria that allow severely damaged or non‐functional mitochondria to be removed by mitophagy. Fusion is mediated by mitofusins, which control mitochondrial outer membrane fusion, and by optic atrophy 1 (Opa1), which controls inner membrane fusion. Mitochondrial fission is mediated primarily by dynamin‐related protein 1 (Drp1) in concert with other adapter proteins that recruit and localize Drp1 to the mitochondrial fission site. Mitochondrial stress, including hypoxic stress, results in fragmented mitochondria that resemble those produced by physiological fission [5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Whether hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation is mechanistically similar to physiological fission and mediated by Drp1 is unresolved [5, 14], also whether it promotes or protects from hypoxic cell death is also not established [15, 16].

We have recently reported the isolation of a missense reduction‐of‐function mutation (I1033K) in the Caenorhabditis elegans Regulatory‐associated protein of mTorC1 (Raptor) gene daf‐15. The daf‐15(I1033K) mutation confers temperature‐sensitive phenotypes where mutant animals raised at 20° appear wild type whereas those raised at 22° are highly resistant to hypoxic death and have an extended lifespan [17]. daf‐15(I1033K) animals raised at 25° fail to develop to adults, arresting as third stage larvae, the daf‐15 null phenotype [17, 18]. Raptor is a core subunit of mTorC1, which is a master regulator of eukaryotic anabolic metabolism [19, 20]. When activated by nutrients and growth factors, mTorC1 promotes protein synthesis while inhibiting autophagy. mTorC1 has also been implicated in the promotion of mitochondrial biogenesis and respiration [21, 22]. Additionally, pharmacological inhibition of mTorC1 results in increased mitochondrial fusion by reducing recruitment of DRP1 to the mitochondria in mammalian cells, and blocking of this hyper‐fusion resulted in increased markers of apoptosis, suggesting that mitochondrial fusion produced by mTorC1 inhibition is cytoprotective [23]. Together, these findings suggest the hypothesis that reduced Raptor function protects from hypoxic death by blocking hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation. We tested this hypothesis using unique genetic reagents in C. elegans .

Materials and Methods

C. elegans Strains and Culture Methods

Unless otherwise indicated, animals were maintained at 20°C on nematode growth media (NGM) plates seeded with Escherichia coli OP50 [24]. The N2 (Bristol) strain is the standard wild type strain [25]. All strains are listed in Table S1 and genotyping information is shown in Table S2. Some strains including N2 were provided by the Caenorhabditis Genetics Center (CGC), which is funded by NIH Office of Research Infrastructure Programs (P40 OD010440). Compound mutants were constructed using standard genetic techniques [26, 27]. Outcrossing was performed against N2 unless otherwise stated. Genotypes were confirmed by PCR amplification of mutated segments, coupled, if necessary, with informative restriction digests of amplicons and/or scoring of unambiguous phenotypes (Primer sets are listed in Table S2). Standard C. elegans nomenclature is used throughout [28] except for the abbreviation of daf‐15(bgg95[I1033K]) as either daf‐15[I1033K] or daf‐15(rf).

Hypoxic Incubations and Hypoxic Death Scoring

Synchronized young adult worms were subjected to hypoxia as previously described [29]. For hypoxic death, each plate of worms was washed into one 1.5 mL tube with 1 mL of M9 buffer (22 mM KH2PO4, 22 mM Na2HPO4, 85 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgSO4). Worms were allowed to settle by gravity, and 900 μL of M9 was removed. The tubes were then placed in the anaerobic chamber (Coy Vinyl Anaerobic Chamber) at 27°C for 24 h for hypoxic death assays. Oxygen tension was always ≤ 0.3%. Following the hypoxic insult unless otherwise noted, worms were transferred using glass Pasteur pipettes onto NGM plates spotted with OP50 bacteria and recovered at 22°C for 24 h. Animals without spontaneous or touched‐evoked movement and/or pumping behavior were scored as dead as previously described [30]. We have previously shown that animals recover to their full extent within the 24‐h recovery period [30, 31]. Typically for each strain in a trial, three plates of worms with approximately 50 worms/plate (each plate is treated as a technical replicate) were tested. A non‐hypoxia‐resistant negative control, N2, and hypoxia‐resistant positive control, daf‐15(I1033K), were present in all trials for all hypoxic death experiments. Normoxic incubations were otherwise identical except performed in a 27°C room air incubator.

Vitamin B12 Supplementation

Adult hermaphrodites of a given genotype laid eggs onto plates containing a three day old lawn of OP50 E. coli supplemented with 12.7 nM (final concentration in agar) adenosylcobalamin (Sigma Inc., St. Louis, MO, Cat#:C0884) or with an equal volume of H2O buffer as a control or onto plates containing a three day old lawn of HT115 E. coli bacteria with no additive. The adults were removed and eggs allowed to develop at 22° to young adults (one day post L4 stage). The young adults were then exposed to 12 h of hypoxia or normoxia, remaining on the agar plates, then returned to room air and mounted for confocal imaging as described below.

Mitochondrial Morphology Image Acquisition

Confocal microscopy was performed using a Leica TPS SP8 (Leica Microsystems, Deerfield, IL, USA) as previously described [32]. Before imaging, age synchronized adult C. elegans containing the muscle mitochondria outer membrane marker transgene syIs243[Pmyo‐3::TOM20::mRFP] or the muscle mitochondrial inner membrane/matrix marker transgene gcIs46[pT05G5.1::UCR‐11::GFP + pha‐1(+) + rol‐6(su1006)], were subjected to hypoxia in the anaerobic chamber (Coy Vinyl Anaerobic Chamber) at 27°C or normoxia at 27°C for 12 h. Directly after incubation, worms were transferred into a solution of 2 μL of 200 mM levamisole (Sigma‐Aldrich Corp., St. Louis, MO, USA) and 2 μL of 30 mM sodium azide dissolved in M9 on glass slides with 2% agarose pads. Confocal images were acquired from 5 to 10 body wall muscles of randomly selected worms using a 63× objective with 8× zoom producing a 23.07 × 23.07 μm image defined as one high‐power field (HPF). The wavelength of the laser to capture the signal for TOM20 signals was 590–691 nm. All images were acquired as a ten‐slice Z‐stack with scan speed of 800–1800 Hz and flattened as a maximum intensity projection prior to analysis. To ensure a random sample was obtained, only one HPF per individual worm was analyzed.

Analysis of Mitochondria Morphology

Mitochondrial morphology was analyzed both qualitatively and quantitatively to maximize the sensitivity to detect alterations in shape and size. For qualitative analysis, images were scored by an observer blinded to condition and genotype. Images were preprocessed in ImageJ [33] first by the smooth function, the filenames were encrypted using the NIH ImageJ Blind Analysis tools to ensure scoring was done blinded to condition and genotype [34]. Each high‐power field was scored as previously described [35] either as tubular (most mitochondria had an elongated morphology), as fragmented (most mitochondria had a spherical morphology), or as intermediate (mitochondria were about equally tubular and fragmented) (Figure S1) [35]. Each image was scored independently three times. At least two of the three scores had to be concordantly fragmented or tubular for the final determination to be assigned as such. A final determination of intermediate was obtained by either a minimum of two of the three scores as intermediate or one score in each category (i.e., one fragmented, one intermediate, and one tubular).

Mitochondrial Morphometry

Several mitochondrial morphometry tools were considered, including mitochondrial analyzer [36], Mitograph [37], and MINA [35, 38]. Ultimately, ImageJ “Analyze Particles” was selected due to its wide availability, accuracy, reproducibility, and ease of use [34]. Raw images were preprocessed and mitochondria outlined and measured using three ImageJ macros (Figure S2). Raw images were preprocessed by converting to 8 bit and a binary mask created with the following settings: Subtract Background = 48.2, pixels Gaussian blur = 4 pixels, Gamma = 0.5, Threshold method: Otsu, Remove outliers: radius = 4 pixels and threshold = 10, and overlayed onto the original image with “Image Calculator” (BatchMNI ImageJ macro). The overlayed preprocessed images were segmented into individual mitochondria with the ImageJ “Find Maxima” tool with the following settings: Prominence Value = 20, output = [Segmented Particles]. A prominence value of “20” was empirically determined to produce the most accurate separation of mitochondria as assessed by the greatest difference between a test set of control and hypoxic images alongside visual inspection of separation accuracy. The segmented image was then overlayed onto the overlayed preprocessed image (BatchSC ImageJ macro). Morphometry of the segmented overlayed preprocessed image was acquired using the ImageJ Analyze Particles function. (“Analyze Particles…”, “size = 5‐Infinity pixel show = Outlines display”) (BatchAP ImageJ macro). Aspect ratio (AR), circularity, and area were measured.

Statistical Analysis

For hypoxic death assays, a minimum of three technical replicates with at least 30 animals/replicate were pooled for each biological replicate. The number of biological replicates is noted in the figure legends or shown as individual datapoints. Summary data for biological replicates were expressed as mean ± SD in all figures. Statistical comparisons were by t‐test for hypoxic death assays with significance set at p < 0.05. For morphometry, t‐tests were performed and Bonferroni‐corrected for multiple comparisons where appropriate and p < 0.05 (after Bonferroni correction of 3 for the three measurements—AR, circularity, and area) was set as the significance threshold. For categorical comparison of qualitative scoring, Fishers exact test was performed. Graphpad Prism version 10 was used for all statistical tests except for morphometry where RStudio was used [39].

Results

Hypoxia‐Induced Mitochondrial Fragmentation Reduced by Raptor Loss‐of‐Function

We first determined whether severe hypoxia (O2 < 0.3%) results in fragmented mitochondria in C. elegans body wall muscle as has been demonstrated in other experimental models [3, 4, 5]. We have previously established that hypoxic death in C. elegans is temperature and time dependent [30]. Higher temperatures and longer incubations during hypoxic incubations increase hypoxic death of animals and cells. Hypoxic incubations of 24 h at 27° produce nearly 100% death of wild type C. elegans whereas twelve‐hour incubations produce < 10% animal death. Incubations longer than four hours result in mitochondrial pathology including protein aggregates in most mitochondria and distinct RNA‐containing stress granules [32, 40, 41, 42]. Using an integrated transgene, syIs243, expressing a fluorescently‐tagged TOMM20 outer mitochondrial membrane protein [35], we found here that twelve hours of hypoxic incubation at 27° produces a highly penetrant and reproducible disruption of mitochondrial size and shape in the otherwise wild type syIs243 animals (Figure 1A–C, Videos S1 and S2). Qualitative scoring of mitochondrial morphology (Figure S1) found that hypoxia produced > 80% fragmented mitochondria compared to < 6% under control incubations at 27° in room air; mitochondria in well‐fed worms mounted directly from agar plates remaining at 22° (baseline condition) had no fragmentation (Figure 1A,F). Quantitation of parameters of mitochondrial shape and size confirmed hypoxia produced smaller and more rounded mitochondria in wild type animals (Figure 1G–I) and that the alterations in shape were due primarily to the hypoxic stress as opposed to thermal stress of 27° (Figure S3). Combining the qualitative and quantitative analyses showed that qualitative scoring of mitochondrial morphology segregated with quantitative mitochondrial morphometry values (Figure S4A,C,E). The distributions of morphometry values were highly significantly different for mitochondria scored as fragmented, intermediate, or tubular (Figure S4B,D,F), and principal component analyses combining aspect ratio and circularity showed separation of fragmented, intermediate, and tubular HPF in both control and hypoxic conditions (Figure S4G,H). Thus, both qualitative scoring and quantitative morphometry demonstrate that hypoxia produces mitochondrial fragmentation in C. elegans .

Next, we asked whether reduced Raptor function, which protects C. elegans from hypoxic death, also protects from hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation. We used the temperature‐sensitive daf‐15(bgg95 [I1033K]) mutant, hereafter referred to as daf‐15(I1033K) or I1033K, raised at 22° a temperature where the mutant is highly resistant to hypoxic death, combining I1033K with syIs243[TOMM20]. daf‐15(I1033K) markedly reduced hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation as assessed qualitatively and by morphometry compared to the wild type strain (Figure 1D–I, Videos S3 and S4). Using a different mitochondrial localized GFP‐tagged protein reporter [41], we confirmed hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation and its mitigation by the I1033K mutation, thereby ruling out an artifact specific to mislocalization of the TOMM20 reporter (Figure S5).

Hypoxia‐Induced Mitochondrial Fragmentation Does Not Require an Intact Mitochondrial Fission/Fusion Machinery

While hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation is well‐documented in mammalian cells and now in C. elegans , the mechanism that produces it is not fully established. One possibility is that hypoxia induces the mitochondrial fission machinery or alternatively inhibits the fusion machinery. fzo‐1 encodes a C. elegans mitofusin and is required for mitochondrial fusion [43]. fzo‐1(tm1133), a deletion mutation resulting in complete loss of function [44], produced a 100% penetrant fragmented mitochondrial morphology, visualized by combining with syIs243[TOMM20], after both control and hypoxic incubations (Figure 2A,B,E, Videos S5 and S6). While qualitative scoring was unable to distinguish control from hypoxia‐treated mitochondria in the fzo‐1(lf) mutant, morphometry showed that hypoxia significantly increased the circularity of the mitochondria in the fzo‐1 mutant (Figure 2G,H). Thus, morphometry argues that at least some aspects of hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation do not rely on inhibition of mitofusin. Mitochondrial fission requires the dynamin‐related protein DRP1, which in C. elegans is encoded by the drp‐1 gene [43]. Under baseline conditions (culture at 22°, no hypoxic or 27° normoxic incubation), mitochondria in the drp‐1(tm1108);syIs243 mutant, carrying a drp‐1 deletion mutation resulting in complete loss of function [44], had a larger area as expected for fission defective mitochondria (Figure S2). But under control conditions (culture at 22°, normoxic incubation at 27°), drp‐1(lf) mitochondria were less tubular with more intermediate morphology than the wild type strain by qualitative scoring (p < 0.0001) (Figure 2C–E). Likewise, the drp‐1 mutant mitochondria had a significantly decreased mean aspect ratio (syIs243 = 3.15 vs. drp‐1(tm1108) = 2.53, p < 0.0001), increased mean circularity (syIs243 = 0.416 vs. drp‐1(tm1108) = 0.493, p < 0.00001), and decreased area (syIs243 = 7.7 vs. drp‐1(tm1108) = 4.5, p < 0.001) (Figures 1B,F–I and 2C,E–H, Video S7). Hypoxia resulted in a high prevalence of qualitative fragmented morphology in the drp‐1(lf) mutant significantly greater than that after control incubations and similar to the level of fragmentation in wild type (Figures 1C,F and 2D,E, Video S8). Morphometry showed a significant decrease in mitochondrial aspect ratio and increase in circularity in the hypoxic‐treated drp‐1(lf) mutant (Figure 2F,G) with mean AR (syIs243 = 1.913 vs. drp‐1(tm1108) = 1.903, p = 0.86), mean circularity (syIs243 = 0.602 vs. drp‐1(tm1108) = 0.575, p = 0.083), and mean area (syIs243 = 3.702 vs. drp‐1(tm1108) = 4.643, p = 0.099) not significantly different than hypoxia‐treated wild type. Thus, our data show that hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation does not require the function of DRP‐1 and is mechanistically distinct from mitochondrial fission.

As described above, inhibition of mTorC1 function results in hyper‐fused mitochondria in mouse embryonic fibroblast cultures [23]. Additionally, pharmacological mTorC1 inhibition or genetic ablation of Raptor in these cells reduced Drp1 recruitment to mitochondria. Thus, a reasonable hypothesis is that the reduced hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation seen in the daf‐15(I1033K) Raptor mutant is due to reduced DRP‐1 recruitment to mitochondria and loss of homeostasis between FZO‐1‐driven fusion and DRP‐1‐driven fission. To test this hypothesis, we combined the Raptor mutation with the drp‐1(tm1108) or fzo‐1(tm1133) deletion mutations along with the syIs243(TOMM20) reporter and measured hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation in these double mutant strains. Again, the highly penetrant hyper‐fission phenotype of fzo‐1(lf) precluded qualitative assessment of the effect of Raptor(lf) on hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation in the fzo‐1(lf);daf‐15(I1033K) double mutant (Figure 3A,B,E,G). However, morphometry showed that the Raptor mutation did mitigate increased circularity and decreased area in the fzo‐1(lf) background with hypoxia treatment (Figure 3I,J). In the case of drp‐1 loss‐of‐function, daf‐15(I1033K) significantly reduced hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation qualitatively compared to the drp‐1(lf) single mutant without daf‐15(I1033K) (Figure 3C,D,F,G, Videos S9 and S10). However, morphometry found no significant difference between drp‐1(lf) and drp‐1(lf) daf‐15(I1033K) mitochondria in any of the three metrics whereas daf‐15(I1033K) did significantly differ from drp‐1(lf) daf‐15(I1033K) for AR and circularity (Figure 3H–J). Thus, our qualitative data indicates that amelioration of hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation by reduced Raptor function does not depend on regulation of DRP‐1; however, morphometry failed to capture the differences observed qualitatively.

Fragmented Mitochondria Do Not Promote Hypoxic Death

Fragmented mitochondria could promote or protect from cell death. To assess the role of fragmentation in hypoxic cell death, we measured animal survival following prolonged 24‐h hypoxic incubation in mitochondrial dynamic mutants with and without the hypoxia protective Raptor mutation. If increased fission promoted hypoxic cell death, the fzo‐1(tm1133 lf) mutant should have decreased survival after hypoxia but it did not (Figure 3K). Likewise, fzo‐1(lf) did not reduce the hypoxia resistance produced by daf‐15(I1033K). On the other hand, the drp‐1(tm1108 lf) mutant was mildly but significantly hypoxia resistant, arguing that disabled fission does provide some hypoxia protection. Surprisingly, the combination of drp‐1(lf) with daf‐15(I1033K) had an intermediate hypoxia resistance, more than drp‐1(lf) but less than daf‐15(I1033K) single mutants. These results argue that one of the mechanisms of hypoxia protection by Raptor(lf) depends on DRP‐1 function.

DRP‐1 and Raptor Synthetically Regulate Mitochondrial Distribution

We noticed in the drp‐1(lf) mutant an alteration in the distribution of mitochondria within C. elegans muscle in a small fraction of animals (Figure 4A–C). The mitochondria were concentrated into a tangled distribution (Videos S9 and S10). The tangled distribution was seen at a similar frequency in drp‐1(lf) animals after either control or hypoxic incubations (Figure 4F). Although no tangles were observed in daf‐15(I1033K) animals, the combination of drp‐1(lf) with daf‐15(I1033K) markedly increased the prevalence of tangled mitochondria after both control and hypoxic incubations (Figure 4D–F). Thus, mitochondrial distribution is co‐regulated by the activities of DRP‐1 and Raptor.

Vitamin B12 Mitigates Hypoxia‐Induced Mitochondrial Fragmentation

The OP50 E. coli strain is the standard bacterial food for routine C. elegans culture [45] and is used as the standard food here. OP50 has been shown to be deficient in vitamin B12 compared to the HT115 strain of E. coli , another common C. elegans food source. Vitamin B12 (adenosylcobalamin) supplementation of OP50 cultures or growth on HT115 bacterial food was shown to increase mitochondrial length in both wild type and drp‐1(tm1138) worms and increased mitochondrial tangles in the drp‐1 mutant [35]. The increased mitochondrial length and increased tangles seen in vitamin B12 replete conditions were attributed to reduced mitochondrial fission. Given that this effect of vitamin B12 occurred both in wild type and the drp‐1(lf) mutant, the reduction in mitochondrial fission must not require DRP‐1, thus sharing this mechanistic feature with C. elegans hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation. We hypothesized that vitamin B12 might likewise inhibit hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation and increase tangles. We observed no significant effect of B12 supplementation or the B12‐replete HT115 food source on the prevalence of mitochondrial tangles in hypoxia‐treated drp‐1(lf) or drp‐1(lf) daf‐15(I1033K) animals (Figure 4G). However, both vitamin B12 supplementation of the agar with adenosylcobalamin and HT115 bacteria significantly reduced hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation compared to H2O solvent control, scored qualitatively and quantitatively by a reduction in circularity in syIs243 mitochondria (Figure 5A,C). Notably, neither the aspect ratio nor mitochondrial area was consistently reduced in both the B12 and HT115 conditions (Figure 5B,D). Hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation mitigation by B12 or HT115 was not observed in the daf‐15(I1033K) mutant (Figure 6) and was not consistently observed in the drp‐1(lf) mutant (Figure 7) or in the drp‐1(lf) daf‐15(I1033K) double mutant background (Figure 8). Overall, the data support the conclusion that vitamin B12 mitigates hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation, but the effect is suppressed in the Raptor and drp‐1(lf) mutant backgrounds.

Discussion

Our primary hypotheses were: (1) Hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation requires an intact mitochondrial fission machinery; (2) Hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation promotes hypoxic death; (3) Raptor activity controls hypoxic death by regulating hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation. Our data lead to a rejection of the first hypothesis and provides little support for the second or the third. Below we will review our data in the context of the previous work that led to these hypotheses.

Is hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation mediated by the mitochondrial fission machinery and does hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation promote cell death? Considerable published evidence generated in other model systems supports the hypothesis that hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation is mechanistically similar to physiological fission and contributes to hypoxic cell death. Transfection of HL‐1 cardiac‐derived immortalized cells with a construct expressing a dominant‐negative Drp1 mutation reduced mitochondrial fragmentation produced by simulated ischemia/reperfusion [11]. Likewise, the mitochondrial fission inhibitor mDivi‐1 also reduced hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation and both the Drp1 dominant negative mutation and mDivi‐1 treatment reduced cell death from simulated ischemia/reperfusion. However, the method these authors used to score hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation did not discern whether hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation was abolished or just reduced by manipulation of Drp1 activity. mDivi‐1 treatment failed to protect mouse or pig hearts from infarction in vivo [11, 46], and was found to not protect H9C2 myocytes against peroxide‐mediated cell death [15] and actually worsened cerebral infarct size in a mouse middle cerebral artery occlusion model [47]. A different inhibitor, antagonizing the interaction between Drp1 and Fis1, which recruits Drp1 to mitochondria [48], has been shown to reduce but not abolish hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation, mitochondrial dysfunction, or cell death in both cell culture and in vivo models of ischemia/reperfusion in rat cardiac cells [49]. A Drp1 dominant negative mutation fully blocked hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation in mouse fibroblast, and shRNA knockdown of Drp1 in rat cardiac myocyte‐derived H9C2 cells reduced hypoxia‐induced cell death [12]. Interestingly, these authors showed that Drp1 activity was regulated by hypoxia through an oxygen‐regulated ubiquitin ligase in a HIF‐1‐independent manner, thus, providing a potential mechanism whereby hypoxia promotes Drp1‐mediated mitochondrial fragmentation. Reduced Drp1 activity also mitigated hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation and was cytoprotective in primary mouse cortical neurons and immortalized neuroblastoma cells [16, 50]. Overall, the weight of the evidence in mammalian in vitro and in vivo models indicates that Drp1, at least in part, promotes hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation but its role in cell death is less clear. In C. elegans , one previous study demonstrated that anoxia resulted in reduced mitochondrial length in neurons in a DRP‐1‐dependent manner [51]. However, standard, not confocal, fluorescent imaging was used in this study, and the resolution appeared to be inadequate to assess individual mitochondrial morphology, leaving open the possibility that hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation may in fact be present in the drp‐1 mutant.

Our data shows unequivocally that Drp1 is not required for hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation in C. elegans . Hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation is not only present in the drp‐1 deletion mutant; it is not even significantly reduced, either qualitatively or in morphometry analysis. We do not have an explanation why the requirement for Drp1 for hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation appears to differ between C. elegans and mammalian cells. We speculate that the more severe hypoxia in the C. elegans model (< 0.3% O2) compared to some of the other models where the [O2] was considerably higher or not measured [11, 12, 49] might produce Drp1‐independent hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation whereas higher oxygen could allow for Drp1‐mediated fragmentation. Alternatively, partial but not complete loss of Drp1 activity or off target effects produced by inhibitors or shRNA could reduce Drp1‐mediated physiological fission that offsets that produced by other unknown hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation effectors. On the other hand, consistent with some of the mammalian studies, our data shows that hypoxic death is significantly, albeit modestly, reduced in the drp‐1 mutant. The hypoxia protective effect of the loss of DRP‐1 activity could be due to some effect unrelated to hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation, or alternatively, the drp‐1 mutant has a subtle effect on fragmentation not detected by our qualitative scoring or morphometry.

Does Raptor protect from hypoxic death by reducing hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation? Multiple lines of evidence link mTorC1 with mitochondrial function [52, 53]. Loss of TOR in yeast was shown to upregulate the synthesis of mitochondria‐encoded respiratory chain subunits through translational upregulation of mitochondrial transcripts [54]. In mammalian cells, however, mTorC1 inhibition has been found to decrease mitochondrial oxygen consumption and oxidative capacity, and this was controlled in part by a nuclear transcription factor, directly regulated by mTorC1 [22, 55] and by selective reduction in translation of nuclear‐encoded transcript pools enriched in mitochondrial proteins, including electron transport chain complex subunits [21, 56]. The same group later showed that besides mitochondrial oxygen consumption, mTorC1 also controlled mitochondrial dynamics by promoting translation of MTFP1 [23], an inner mitochondrial membrane protein that inhibits inner membrane fusion coordinating with Drp1‐mediated fission [57, 58, 59]. The authors demonstrated that mTorC1 inhibitors as well as a Raptor deletion mutation resulted in hyperfused and branched mitochondria in mouse embryonic fibroblasts and that this effect was associated with reduced mitochondrial localization of Drp1 and reduced overall levels of MTFP1 [23]. On the other hand, cells with constitutive activation of mTorC1 had increased mitochondrial fragmentation [23]. Finally, these authors showed that mTorC1 inhibitors reduced cell proliferation and increased caspase activity and that this effect was markedly enhanced when MTFP1 was overexpressed [23]. Consistent with the findings in MEF cells, Raptor overexpression in Drosophila flight muscle produced smaller mitochondria, suggesting increased fission, whereas Raptor RNAi produced larger mitochondria [60].

Our data shows that in C. elegans reduced Raptor activity markedly mitigates hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation. Further, the mitigation of qualitative hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation by the Raptor reduction‐of‐function mutation occurs in the complete absence of Drp1. As MTFP1 is thought to require Drp1 for its role in fission [59], the effect of Raptor activity on hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation is unlikely to be mediated by the MTFP1‐regulated fission mechanism observed in mammalian cells [57, 59]. We speculate that hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation might be produced not by promoting DRP1‐mediated fission but by inhibiting fusion. Indeed, mitochondrial morphology after hypoxia is remarkably similar to that produced by loss of mitofusin function (fzo‐1(lf)). However, hypoxia did produce significantly increased circularity in the fzo‐1(lf) mutants, indicating that mitochondria can be further fragmented without mitofusin. Notably, a double mutant strain combining drp‐1(tm1108) and fzo‐1(tm1133) has been reported to have a non‐fragmented mitochondrial size and morphology [61], indicating that disruption of both fusion and fission machinery does not necessarily result in a fragmented morphology. Thus, while DRP‐1 is not required for hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation, simply inhibiting FZO‐1 in the absence of other effects does not seem likely to produce hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation either. Other than a role of vitamin B12, the mechanism responsible for hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation must be more complex and at this point remains obscure.

Our results show that vitamin B12 supplementation mitigates hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation. As discussed above, Wei and Ruvkun demonstrated that a relative B12 deficiency in C. elegans feeding on OP50 bacteria results in an increase in mitochondrial fragmentation in both a wild type and drp‐1(lf) background and that supplementation of OP50 with B12 adenosylcobalamin reduced fragmentation [35]. While Wei and Ruvkun demonstrated B12 deficiency could induce mitochondrial fragmentation even in the absence of DRP‐1, B12 supplementation did not fully restore mitochondrial length to wild type levels in the drp‐1(lf) mutant [35]. Thus, while mitochondrial fragmentation induced by B12 deficiency can occur in the absence of DRP‐1, the effects of B12 supplementation may still depend on DRP‐1 function. Indeed, we found that the reduction of hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation by B12 was suppressed in drp‐1(lf) mutants. In any case, the molecular mediators for the DRP‐1 independent mitochondrial fragmentation whether induced by B12‐deficiency or hypoxia remain unclear. A clue to the mechanism is that RNAi knockdown of the methylmalonyl CoA mutase, a vitamin B12 requiring enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of homocysteine to methionine, increased mitochondrial fragmentation and other B12 deficiency phenotypes and that methionine supplementation reduced mitochondrial fragmentation [35]. Further, knockdown of the C. elegans methionine adenosyltransferase, which converts methionine to S‐adenosylmethionine, similarly augmented mitochondrial fragmentation [35]. S‐adenosylmethionine serves a methyl donor for numerous methyltransferase reactions, one or more of which could regulate mitochondrial fragmentation from hypoxia and vitamin B12 deficiency.

As for whether the mitigation of hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation by the Raptor mutant is responsible for at least a portion of its protection from hypoxic cell death, the high level of fission produced by the fzo‐1(lf) mutation did not reduce the hypoxia resistance of daf‐15(I1033K). Thus, fragmented mitochondria, at least that produced by lack of FZO‐1, in and of itself does not suppress the resistance to hypoxic death phenotype of daf‐15(lf). On the other hand, drp‐1(lf) had a non‐additive interaction with the daf‐15(I1033K) mutation with a hypoxia resistance between that of each single mutant. This result is inconsistent with the hypothesis that inhibition of DRP‐1‐mediated fission is entirely responsible for the hypoxia resistance of daf‐15(I1033K). However, the data indicate that at least a portion of the hypoxia protection by daf‐15(I1033K) is dependent on DRP‐1.

Finally, we observed an unusual synergistic interaction between drp‐1(lf) and daf‐15(I1033K) on the mitochondrial network, producing a large tangle of mitochondria in the majority of C. elegans muscle cells. This tangled mitochondrial network was not exacerbated by hypoxic incubation. Wei and Ruvkun have previously described in the same drp‐1(tm1108) mutant an abnormal mitochondrial network in C. elegans muscle that they also referred to as “tangled” [35]. The appearance of the tangles in their publication is consistent with the tangles that we observe. The 4.5‐fold increase in tangles when the drp‐1(lf) mutation is combined with the Raptor mutation argues that Raptor has a role in the mitochondrial network that involves DRP‐1.

Limitations of the Study

The primary limitation of this study is the generalizability of the findings to human hypoxic injury. Hypoxic cellular injury in humans is often due to focal ischemia in the heart or brain. Our model is global hypoxia in C. elegans and certainly cannot fully model all aspects of focal cardiac or neuronal ischemia. In particular, the role of signaling from immune cells and ischemic penumbral cells is not modeled here. Here we imaged body wall muscle mitochondria that may better model cardiac muscle mitochondria rather than those in neurons. Finally, while our data do definitively show that mitochondrial fragmentation produced by the loss of FZO‐1 mitofusin does not increase hypoxic death, our findings do not rule out that hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation itself promotes hypoxic death. Indeed, our data show that C. elegans hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation is mechanistically distinct from physiological mitochondrial fission and therefore could promote hypoxic death due to an aspect of the hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation mechanism that does not involve mitofusin or DRP‐1 and is distinct from fragmented mitochondria themselves.

In summary, we have shown that hypoxia induces mitochondrial fragmentation in C. elegans and that it does not require the essential mitochondrial fission protein DRP‐1 in C. elegans . Although we find that reduced Raptor function mitigates hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation, our data does not clearly support that hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation promotes hypoxic cell death nor that Raptor loss‐of‐function protects from hypoxic cell death by mitigating fragmentation. However, our data does support a role of DRP‐1 in the hypoxia protective effect produced by reduced Raptor activity. We discovered incidentally that the combination of daf‐15(I1033K) and drp‐1(lf) synthetically alters the mitochondrial network, irrespective of hypoxic exposure, producing a tangled mitochondrial network. Finally, we found that vitamin B12 supplementation mitigates hypoxia‐induced mitochondrial fragmentation. Thus, future studies should address whether vitamin B12 supplementation might prevent or treat other hypoxia‐induced pathologies, including cell death.
