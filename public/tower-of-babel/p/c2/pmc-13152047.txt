HDL and ASCVD: is high HDL-C protective, harmful, or simply misleading?
Authors: Paolo Parini, Matteo Pedrelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of reviewThis review examines whether high high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) is protective, harmful, or simply misleading in relation to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), with emphasis on recent mechanistic, epidemiologic, genetic, and trial evidence.Recent findingsHDL is biologically important and multifunctional, but HDL-C is an imperfect surrogate for HDL function. INTRODUCTION

High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) is often called the “good cholesterol” because low HDL-C is associated with higher rates of coronary disease in classic and modern cohorts. The present dispute is that the relationship is not unequivocal, since risk reduction plateaus, and very high HDL-C can associate with higher mortality and sometimes higher cardiovascular risk. Meanwhile, therapies that raise HDL-C have not reliably reduced atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) events, and Mendelian randomization (MR) studies have not provided a clean causal signal for HDL-C as an ASCVD-protective factor. Together, these findings have understandably shifted the field from considering “HDL quantity” to “HDL quality”, emphasizing particle functions (e.g., cholesterol efflux and anti-inflammatory signaling) and their heterogeneity over the cholesterol cargo routinely measured in real-life clinical settings. This review synthesizes key mechanistic concepts, observational findings, genetic evidence, and trial data from roughly the last five years, with a pragmatic focus on how to interpret high HDL-C in clinic practice and how to frame the next generation of HDL research.

HIGH-DENSITY LIPOPROTEIN FROM AN EVOLUTIONARY PERSPECTIVE

HDL clearly did not evolve “to prevent plaque”! HDL-like particles appear early in evolution as versatile lipid-transport systems and are thought to represent the principal lipoprotein class in invertebrates (Fig. 1), whereas ApoB-containing lipoproteins, including the VLDL/LDL system, emerged later as a vertebrate specialization [1]. This frames HDL as a multifunctional platform whose original selection pressure likely extended far beyond ASCVD, helping to explain why the same particle family can be beneficial in one physiological context but neutral, or even detrimental, in another [1]. Further, HDL's “reusability” represented a clear evolutionary advantage, since small particles that accept and redistribute lipids can prevent toxicity, while supporting membrane homeostasis across tissues. HDL also binds and helps neutralize bioactive lipids such as bacterial lipopolysaccharide, linking HDL to innate immunity. In infections and systemic inflammation, HDL composition shifts rapidly, which may help explain why HDL-related traits are associated with outcomes beyond ASCVD (e.g., sepsis severity) and why “HDL biology” cannot be reduced to HDL number or steady-state plasma HDL-C levels alone [1].

MULTIPLE HIGH-DENSITY LIPOPROTEIN FUNCTIONS IN ATHEROGENESIS

If neither the number of HDL particles nor HDL-C serves as a reliable proxy for atheroprotection, the key question becomes which HDL functions map to causal pathways in atherosclerosis, and which biomarkers capture them (Fig. 1). The canonical anti-atherogenic mechanism attributed to HDL is reverse cholesterol transport (RCT), a concept introduced by Glomset nearly sixty years ago [2]. RCT begins with the efflux of cholesterol from arterial wall macrophages to lipid-poor apolipoprotein A-I (ApoAI) and HDL particles, thereby promoting cholesterol removal from the vessel wall. Cholesterol efflux capacity (CEC) assays quantify this initial step, and a 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis confirmed that higher CEC was associated with lower odds of coronary artery disease (CAD) across observational studies, supporting the view that function-based metrics may be more informative than HDL-C alone [3].

Beyond cholesterol transport, HDL particles carry enzymes, apolipoproteins, and bioactive lipids that modulate endothelial nitric oxide signaling, leukocyte adhesion, oxidative stress, and thrombosis. These pleiotropic effects are exerted by the particle as an integrated functional unit rather than by its cholesterol cargo. As a result, HDL-C can be elevated because HDL particles are cholesterol-enriched, yet those same particles may carry altered protein cargo and/or exhibit impaired signaling capacity. This distinction is especially relevant to cholesteryl ester transfer protein (CETP) inhibition, which raises HDL-C primarily by blocking the transfer of cholesteryl esters from HDL to ApoB-containing lipoproteins. As discussed in 2003 with my former mentor and close friend Professor Larry Rudel (1941–2019), the accumulation of cholesteryl ester-enriched HDL under CETP inhibition seems not automatically beneficial, because the role of CETP in lipoprotein metabolism and atherogenesis is highly complex and reflects the interaction of this protein with multiple factors [4▪]. Because low HDL-C levels are associated with increased coronary heart disease risk, high HDL-C levels would theoretically be expected to be protective; however, this association appears to be independent of CETP status [5]. A further layer of complexity arises from the apolipoprotein composition of CETP-deficient HDL. ApoE levels in HDL are positively correlated with plasma HDL-C, and this correlation becomes dramatically amplified under CETP deficiency or pharmacological CETP inhibition, as initially reported by Yamashita et al. [6]. Several lines of evidence suggest that ApoE enrichment may shift HDL toward a compositionally distinct and, in some contexts, dysfunctional phenotype. In a proteomic analysis of five inbred mouse strains, Pamir and colleagues showed that HDL-associated ApoE acts as a negative regulator of ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux and that HDL isolated from ApoE-deficient mice displays greater ABCA1-specific efflux than HDL from wild-type mice [7]. In line with this particle-centered view, we previously also reported that HDL from ApoE-deficient mice promoted greater ABCA1-mediated efflux than HDL from wild-type mice, being HDL from ApoE-deficient mice indeed enriched in ApoA-I-rich pre-beta particles at the expense of larger alpha-migrating species [8]. Consistent with these observations, large ApoE-enriched HDL particles carrying the dysfunctional ApoA-I L159R (FIN) mutation were found to be explicitly proatherogenic, paradoxically conferring greater atherosclerotic burden than the complete absence of HDL [9].

These observations raise the possibility that, in subjects with very high HDL-C but low plasma triglycerides, plasma ApoE may serve as an additional clinical biomarker of enrichment in large ApoE-rich HDL particles, a phenotype that may be associated with impaired HDL function and increased cardiovascular risk. Importantly, these observations derive largely from mouse studies, as do many of the early findings underpinning the original hypothesis that high HDL-C is a reliable proxy for atheroprotection. Their clinical translatability remains uncertain because major species differences exist between mice and humans, particularly in hepatic metabolism and lipoprotein biology [10].

Nevertheless, all these findings together with the epidemiological clinical observation converge on the broader principle that HDL particles are highly heterogeneous, varying in size, protein cargo, and lipid composition. Also, under conditions of chronic inflammation, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or acute infection, HDL can undergo extensive remodeling, with alterations in both proteome and lipidome, that may blunt cholesterol efflux capacity or shift the particle toward pro-inflammatory signaling. This “dysfunctional HDL” framework provides a mechanistic bridge to epidemiology: high HDL-C can coexist with impaired HDL function, and very high HDL-C may sometimes reflect abnormal remodeling or impaired hepatic clearance rather than beneficial atheroprotection [1].

EPIDEMIOLOGIC OBSERVATIONAL STUDIES SUGGEST THAT HIGH HDL-C IS NOT UNIFORMLY PROTECTIVE

The main recent epidemiologic insight is not that low HDL-C has ceased to be associated with increased risk, but that very high HDL-C is increasingly recognized as a marker of HDL heterogeneity rather than universal protection (Fig. 2).

In the Copenhagen City Heart Study and the Copenhagen General Population Study, two prospective population-based cohorts including 52 268 men and 64 240 women, Madsen et al. reported a U-shaped association between HDL-C and all-cause mortality, with the lowest mortality at approximately 73 mg/dl (54–77) in men and 93 mg/dl (69–97) in women, while higher mortality was observed at very high HDL-C levels, beginning around 97 mg/dl in men and 116 mg/dl in women [11].

In ChinaHEART (3.3 million adults), HDL-C showed U-shaped associations with all-cause, cardiovascular, and cancer mortality; both very low and very high HDL-C (e.g., >90 mg/dl) were associated with higher hazards compared with intermediate ranges [12]. In NHANES 1999–2018, extremely high HDL-C (≥ 80 mg/dl) was similarly associated with higher all-cause, cardiovascular, and non-cardiovascular mortality compared with mid-range HDL-C [13▪].

Among patients with established coronary artery disease, Liu and colleagues reported that very high HDL-C (>80 mg/dl) was paradoxically associated with higher all-cause and cardiovascular mortality compared with HDL-C 40–60 mg/dl across two biobanks (UK Biobank and Emory), despite extensive adjustment [14]. These signals may vary by sex and comorbidities, but they are clinically relevant because they directly challenge the automatic assumption that high HDL-C is protective also in secondary prevention.

Inevitably, confounding and reverse causation linger as plausible contributors (e.g., alcohol use, liver disease, frailty, inflammatory illness, medication patterns), and it is possible that the “very high HDL-C” is the shared peripheral phenotype resulting from multiple endophenotypes. Yet the repeated observation of nonlinearity across settings suggests that extreme values often reflect a distinct biology: altered HDL turnover, cholesterol-enriched particles, or composition changes that blunt function. Clinically, the safest interpretation is that very high HDL-C should not be used for reassurance or to discount causal risk factors such as ApoB-containing lipoproteins [1,12,13▪,14].

GENETIC AND MENDELIAN RANDOMIZATION STUDIES: MIXED FINDINGS, NO CLEAR PROTECTIVE EFFECT

Genetic analyses ask whether lifelong differences in HDL-C (or related traits) causally alter ASCVD risk. Overall, results have not supported HDL-C as causal driver of ASCVD, in contrast to ApoB-containing lipoproteins (i.e., VLDL/LDL and chylomicron remnants).

In a systematic review and meta-analysis of MR studies, causal associations with CAD were strong for LDL and ApoB, and for lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)], whereas HDL-C did not show an equivalently robust protective causal effect in pooled analyses [15]. This pattern is consistent with the trial record: changing HDL-C without improving causal pathways (or without lowering ApoB-containing particles) does not reliably reduce events [1,4▪,15]. MR can be affected by pleiotropy as many HDL-C-associated genetic variants also influence triglycerides, LDL-C, insulin resistance, and/or inflammation. Also, some variants can raise HDL-C while impairing particle flux through key pathways (e.g., altered receptor-mediated uptake or remodeling enzymes), potentially yielding high HDL-C but reduced atheroprotection. The steady-state plasma HDL-C levels are the result of production, remodeling, and clearance of the HDL particles; changing any of these can move HDL-C in either direction, but the ASCVD consequence depends on which step is perturbed and whether ApoB particle burden changes in parallel. This pathway view helps reconcile why “HDL-raising” can be neutral while certain HDL functions remain plausibly protective [1,4▪]

INTERVENTIONAL TRIALS: OFTEN DISAPPOINTING EFFECTS OF RAISING HDL-C

Randomized trials are the clearest test of whether HDL-centered strategies prevent ASCVD events. The overall lesson is that HDL-C is not a reliable surrogate endpoint, and that mechanism and patient selection prominently matter.

The AEGIS-II study tested repeated infusions of CSL112 (plasma-derived ApoA-I) soon after myocardial infarction to enhance cholesterol efflux capacity during the high-risk early post-MI period. Four weekly infusions did not reduce cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction (MI), or stroke through 90 days compared with placebo, despite robust mechanistic rationale [16]. The implication is not that the cholesterol efflux is irrelevant, but that the intervention, timing, population risk profile, or competing event drivers may be more relevant and have limited the ability to translate a protective biologic process into clinical benefit.

CETP inhibitors raise HDL-C, but clinical benefit has been clearly inconsistent. Long-term follow-up in REVEAL suggests that the net benefit of anacetrapib is best explained by lowering of non-HDL/ApoB-related lipoproteins rather than by HDL-C increases [1,17]. The dal-GenE trial attempted a genotype-guided CETP strategy; the primary endpoint was not significantly reduced, again underscoring that HDL-C movement is not a valid surrogate for outcomes [18].

NEW CETP INHIBITOR OBICETRAPIB AS AN APOB-LOWERING THERAPY WITH MARKED HDL-C INCREASES

Modern CETP inhibitors such as obicetrapib can lower LDL-C reductions while also producing marked increases in HDL-C. In a 2025 NEJM trial in high-risk patients, obicetrapib met lipid efficacy endpoints and was generally well tolerated, but outcomes benefits were not tested [19▪▪]. A HeFH trial The BROOKLYN trial on heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia patients similarly demonstrated strong lipid effects and large HDL-C rises [20▪]. These agents reframe CETP inhibition as an ApoB-lowering approach, raising a practical question for HDL biology: if outcomes benefits are eventually demonstrated, are they fully explained by ApoB reduction, or is there an additional function-based contribution from HDL remodeling?

A PRACTICAL INTERPRETATION FOR CLINICIANS

Moderately higher HDL-C within common physiological ranges often tracks with favorable metabolic features and may correlate with lower risk, but at the extreme high end (commonly ≥80–90 mg/dl, depending on sex and population) it may reflect distinct biology, including genetic variants, altered remodeling, or secondary causes, and is not reliably protective in modern cohorts [11,12,13▪,14]. Accordingly, high HDL-C should not be used to downplay LDL-C/ApoB, Lp(a), blood pressure, or other proven causal factors. Instead, when very high HDL-C is encountered, it is reasonable to review secondary drivers such as alcohol use patterns, liver disease, thyroid status, and medications, while focusing risk reduction on established causal targets. As mentioned above, this limitation of HDL-C has driven interest in alternative metrics, including HDL particle number, CEC, and panels of HDL-associated proteins or lipids, including plasma ApoE measurement when triglycerides are low and HDL-C is greatly elevated to indirectly evaluate the possible presence of ApoE-rich HDL particles. Although these are not routine clinical tests, they are increasingly used in mechanistic studies because HDL-C does not capture RCT efficiency or other HDL functions. Their translation into clinical practice will require assay standardization, robust reference ranges, careful pre-analytic handling, and evidence that functional HDL metrics improve prediction or change management beyond existing risk models [1].

CONCLUSION

The field has moved beyond the assumption that higher HDL-C is inherently protective. The evidence now supports a clearer and more demanding view: HDL biology is important, but HDL-C is an inadequate surrogate and a poor therapeutic target (Fig. 2). A broad consensus has emerged that HDL is multifunctional, that HDL-C does not reflect the functions most likely to matter biologically, and that raising HDL-C per se is not an evidence-based strategy for ASCVD prevention. [1,15,16]. Indeed, observational studies increasingly reveal nonlinear associations, with very high HDL-C not consistently protective and sometimes linked to higher mortality, whereas MR studies and randomized trials have failed to validate HDL-C elevation as a causal strategy. What remains unresolved is not the relevance of HDL biology, but which components of that biology are actionable. As argued in 2003 [4▪], the strategic rationale for CETP inhibition was not simply HDL-C elevation, but the possibility that, in the appropriate metabolic context, modifying CETP activity could reduce the burden of ApoB-containing lipoproteins and thereby alter atherogenic risk. In the current therapeutic era, however, that question must be reconsidered against the proven efficacy of statins, ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors, and more recently bempedoic acid; the relevant issue is no longer whether CETP inhibitors can raise HDL-C, but whether they can provide incremental, mechanism-specific benefit beyond established ApoB-lowering therapies. Future work should therefore abandon HDL-C as the central organizing endpoint and concentrate on functional and mechanistic precision: scalable efflux and particle assays, pathway-specific genetic instruments, deep phenotyping of very-high-HDL-C states, and outcomes trials that directly test whether improving HDL function rather than HDL-C concentration can reduce ASCVD risk.
