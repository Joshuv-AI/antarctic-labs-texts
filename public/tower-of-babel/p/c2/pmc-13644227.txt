Compassion fatigue and its associated factors among clinical nurses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Juan Wang, Rong Su, Shouhua Peng, Ling Chen, Yan Sun, Qinqin Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo investigate the prevalence of compassion fatigue among clinical nurses in Xinjiang, China, and to identify factors associated with CF, thereby providing scientific evidence for developing targeted interventions to safeguard nurses’ occupational health.DesignA cross-sectional study conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.SettingTertiary, secondary and primary hospitals located in Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, China.ParticipantsA total of 544 registered clinical nurses were enrolled using convenience sampling between September 2025 and October 2025.Main outcome measuresThe level of compassion fatigue was measured using the Chinese version of the Compassion Fatigue Scale-Short Scale (C-CFSS). Introduction

Compassion fatigue (CF) refers to a gradual decline in empathic ability, accompanied by secondary traumatic stress and job burnout, which results from caregivers’ prolonged exposure to patients’ pain, trauma, and death [1]. This phenomenon is particularly prominent among nurses and has emerged as a major occupational health issue in the global nursing workforce [1]. Recent cross-sectional studies indicate that the prevalence of compassion fatigue among nurses is generally at moderate to high levels. For instance, a multicenter study in Spain reported that among oncology nurses, 18.2% had low compassion satisfaction, while 20.2% and 37.4% experienced high burnout and high secondary traumatic stress, respectively [2]. Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that the prevalence of CF among nurses may be increasing over time. A four-year longitudinal study found that personal burnout scores among nurses peaked increase gradually, with long-term overtime work being a significant predictor of worsening burnout [3]. The adverse consequences of CF are multidimensional, affecting individual nurses, patient care quality, and the healthcare system [4]. At the individual level, CF not only leads to anxiety and depression but also contributes to physical symptoms and reduced professional well-being [5]. At the patient level, diminished empathic capacity may erode the nurse-patient trust relationship, increasing the risk of nursing errors and reducing patient satisfaction [6]. Moreover, CF significantly elevates nurses’ turnover intention, leading to workforce instability and a shortage of experienced nursing staff [7]. Given these negative impacts, timely assessment of CF prevalence and identification of factors associated with CF are essential for effective nursing management. Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, located in western China, faces unique challenges in healthcare delivery, including geographic dispersion, multi-ethnic patient populations, and varying levels of medical resource allocation across urban and rural areas. These contextual factors may influence nurses’ compassion fatigue experiences differently compared to other regions in China.

Regarding high-risk populations, previous studies have identified several characteristics associated with higher CF levels. Nurses with less clinical experience, weaker workplace support, and heavier family responsibilities have been reported to experience lower compassion satisfaction and higher CF levels [8]. Younger, male, and less experienced emergency nurses—particularly those with higher education levels—appear to be especially vulnerable [9]. These findings highlight the need to examine how demographic and work-related factors contribute to CF among general clinical nurses. Although compassion fatigue has garnered increasing academic attention, most existing studies have focused on nurses in specific specialties, limiting the generalizability of their findings to general clinical nurses. Furthermore, while cultural differences in the understanding of compassion fatigue have been noted [10], evidence regarding the demographic and occupational factors associated with compassion fatigue among general clinical nurses remains limited. A clearer understanding of these associated factors may help inform the development of early identification and targeted support strategies for nurses at risk of compassion fatigue. Accordingly, this cross-sectional study aimed to: (1) assess the current status of compassion fatigue among general clinical nurses; (2) identify key demographic and work-related factors associated with CF.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional survey employed convenience sampling to recruit participants from tertiary, secondary, and primary hospitals across Xinjiang, China. The study was conducted between September 2025 and October 2025. Data were collected using self-administered questionnaires, and the study was reported following the STROBE guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

Study population

A convenience sample of clinical nurses working in various departments was recruited from tertiary, secondary, and primary hospitals in Xinjiang between September 2025 and October 2025. Data were collected from tertiary, secondary, and primary hospitals across Xinjiang. Inclusion criteria: (1) Registered nurse; (2) Currently working as a clinical nurse in a hospital setting; (3) Voluntary participation with signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria: (1) Trainee or intern nurses; (2) Participants on leave or who had resigned during the survey period; (3) Participants who submitted incomplete questionnaires.

Research tools

General information questionnaire

Demographic characteristics were collected, including gender, age, ethnicity, and highest level of education attained. Occupational characteristics comprised the hospital and department of employment, position held, professional title, years of nursing experience, years working in the current department, and whether specialised training in communication skills had been received.

Compassion fatigue assessment scale

This study used the Chinese version of the Compassion Fatigue Scale-Short Scale (C-CFSS), originally revised by Adams et al. [11], and adapted for Chinese use by Lou Baona [12]. This scale comprises two dimensions and 13 items. Each item employs a 10-point Likert scale, scored from 1 (Never) to 10 (Very Frequently), yielding a total score ranging from 13 to 130. Higher scores indicate greater perceived severity of compassion fatigue. Based on previous studies [13], the degree of compassion fatigue was categorized into three levels: low (scores between 13 and 51), medium (scores between 52 and 91), and high (scores between 92 and 130). The scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.954 [2]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale was 0.923.

Data collection

Questionnaires were administered online using the Wenjuan Xing platform (a widely used online survey platform in China). The electronic questionnaire link was distributed through nursing department work groups of the participating hospitals. A convenience sampling method was adopted, and we attempted to enhance sample heterogeneity by recruiting participants from hospitals of different levels (tertiary, secondary, and primary) and from multiple departments, which may partially mitigate, though not eliminate, selection bias. In addition, anonymous responses were implemented to reduce social desirability bias, and duplicate submissions were prevented by restricting entries from the same IP address. The informed consent form was embedded in the electronic questionnaire, and participants were required to carefully read the consent information before proceeding to the questionnaire section. A total of 560 questionnaires were distributed online, and 544 valid questionnaires were collected, yielding an effective response rate of 97.14%. The exact number of participating hospitals at each level was not recorded during the data collection process.

Statistical analysis

Data processing and analysis were conducted using SPSS 26.0 statistical software. Continuous variables were described as means ± standard deviations, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Univariate analyses including independent-samples t tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were conducted to examine the associations between compassion fatigue and participants’ general characteristics. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were subsequently entered as independent variables into a multivariate linear regression model, with compassion fatigue as the dependent variable, to identify factors associated with compassion fatigue. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a threshold of VIF < 5 indicating no significant multicollinearity [2]. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. An a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1) to determine the minimum required sample size for the multiple linear regression analysis [14]. With a total of 9 predictor variables, a medium effect size (f² = 0.15), a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a desired power (1-β) of 0.80, the calculated minimum required sample size was 114. The final sample of 544 participants in this study exceeded this requirement, indicating sufficient statistical power.

Results

General characteristics of study participants

Among these participants, 96 (17.6%) were from primary hospitals, 183 (33.6%) from secondary hospitals, and 265 (48.7%) from tertiary hospitals. The predominant age group was 25–34 years (46.5%). Most participants were female (96.9%) and of Han ethnicity (63.0%). Regarding educational background, the majority held a bachelor’s degree (61.3%). In terms of departmental distribution, oncology nurses accounted for the largest proportion of the sample (59.7%). Most participants were employed in general nursing positions (69.0%), and the most common professional title was Senior Nurse (43.1%). Nurses with ≥ 15 years of clinical experience represented 36.5% of the sample. In addition, the majority of participants (74.8%) reported that they had not received relevant communication skills training (Table 1).

Compassion fatigue scores among nurses

The overall compassion fatigue score for 544 nurses was 56.43 ± 25.12, with an average item score of (4.34 ± 1.93) points. Based on the predefined cutoff values [13], 251 nurses (46.1%) had low level of compassion fatigue (scores 13–51), 239 nurses (43.9%) had medium level (scores 52–91), and 54 nurses (9.9%) had high level (scores 92–130). Regarding dimensions, professional burnout scored (35.23 ± 15.66), with an item mean of (4.40 ± 1.96); the secondary trauma dimension scored (21.20 ± 10.33), with an item mean of (4.24 ± 2.07) (see Table 3).

Multivariate linear regression

A multivariate linear regression analysis was performed with compassion fatigue scores as the dependent variable and variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis (department type, professional title, years of service in the current department, and receipt of relevant training) as independent variables. Department type was entered as dummy variables with internal medicine as the reference category. The results showed that compared with nurses in internal medicine departments, those in intensive care units (B = 46.833, 95%CI: [37.329, 56.337], P < 0.001) and oncology departments (B = 20.569, 95%CI: [14.618, 26.521], P < 0.001) had significantly higher compassion fatigue scores. Nurses in surgery (B=-3.066, 95%CI: [-9.902, 3.771], P = 0.379) and palliative care (B=-2.892, 95%CI: [-12.312, 6.527], P = 0.547) did not differ significantly from those in internal medicine. Years of service in the current department (B = 2.590, 95%CI: [0.790, 4.391], P = 0.005) and lack of relevant training (B = 11.468, 95%CI: [7.403, 15.534], P < 0.001) were also significantly associated with higher compassion fatigue scores. Professional title was not significantly associated with compassion fatigue (B = 0.644, 95%CI: [-1.699, 2.987], P = 0.435). Regarding position, compared with nurses, specialist nurses had marginally higher compassion fatigue scores (B = 4.264, 95%CI: [-0.444, 8.971], P = 0.076), while head nurses did not differ significantly (B=-0.100, 95%CI: [-6.644, 6.444], P = 0.976). These variables together explained 32.1% of the total variance in compassion fatigue (R²=0.321, adjusted R²=0.310, F = 28.048, P < 0.001). Discussion

This study investigated the prevalence of compassion fatigue and its associated factors among clinical nurses, which provide empirical evidence for identifying nurses at higher risk of compassion fatigue. Although this study was conducted in Xinjiang, China, compassion fatigue is a global issue that affects nurses across different healthcare systems and cultural contexts. By providing evidence from an underrepresented area of China, this study enriches the global understanding of compassion fatigue and highlights the need for contextually tailored yet universally relevant interventions. The findings may help nurse managers, educators, and policymakers in different countries develop targeted strategies to support nurses’ psychological well-being.

The findings showed that the mean compassion fatigue score among clinical nurses was 56.43 ± 25.12, indicating a moderate level overall, with 53.8% of participants experiencing moderate to high levels of compassion fatigue. This prevalence is consistent with the 52.55% reported in a recent meta-analysis [15] and is generally comparable to findings from previous studies [16, 17]. The relatively high prevalence observed in this study may be related to the demanding nature of clinical nursing work. Nurses are frequently exposed to patients’ suffering, critical illness, family distress, and emotionally challenging care situations. Prolonged emotional involvement, combined with continuous occupational stress, may gradually deplete emotional resources and increase vulnerability to compassion fatigue. These findings highlight the considerable psychological burden experienced by clinical nurses and suggest the potential importance of organizational support for mental health in healthcare settings. Consistent with previous regional studies conducted in high-intensity clinical environments [18], our results suggest that nursing managers and healthcare institutions may consider strengthening early identification and support for nurses at risk of compassion fatigue. Potential strategies may include incorporating routine psychological assessment into occupational health management, providing communication and stress-management training, and establishing accessible support systems such as peer support programs and psychological counseling services.

Beyond the overall score, examining the two dimensions of compassion fatigue—burnout and secondary trauma—provides a more nuanced understanding of how nurses experience emotional distress. The mean item scores for the two dimensions—burnout and secondary trauma—were similar, suggesting that compassion fatigue in this population is not dominated by a single dimension but reflects the coexistence of both depletion patterns. This may be explained by nurses being simultaneously exposed to two types of stressors: sustained high-intensity work depletes emotional resources (burnout), while frequent exposure to patients’ traumatic experiences triggers secondary stress reactions (secondary trauma) [19]. These findings suggest that interventions for this population may need to address both workload optimization and the development of coping skills for trauma exposure.

Multivariate linear regression analysis revealed that department type was the strongest predictor of compassion fatigue. Compared with nurses in internal medicine departments, those in intensive care units (B = 46.833, 95% CI: [37.329, 56.337], P < 0.001) and oncology departments (B = 20.569, 95% CI: [14.618, 26.521], P < 0.001) had significantly higher compassion fatigue scores. This distribution is consistent with findings from multiple studies [20]. For instance, Wu et al. [13] reported that ICU nurses face high mortality rates and intense negative emotions from families, making them highly susceptible to emotional exhaustion. A recent systematic review confirmed that oncology nurses are at high risk for compassion fatigue [21]. A domestic study of oncology nurses further reported that approximately 72% of nurses experienced moderate to severe compassion fatigue [22]. In this study, ICU nurses had a significantly higher compassion fatigue score (90.93 ± 30.42) compared with other departments. This finding is consistent with previous studies [23], which have reported that ICU nurses frequently face patient deaths and family distress in high-intensity critical care settings. Such an environment demands sustained emotional engagement, which may contribute to compassion fatigue. In contrast, nurses in palliative care departments did not demonstrate significantly different compassion fatigue scores compared to internal medicine. Although palliative care nurses also care for terminally ill patients, their work incorporates emotional regulation into routine practice through multidisciplinary collaboration and systematic psychological interventions, which may be associated with lower levels of compassion fatigue [24]. Nurses with 10–14 years of service showed the highest compassion fatigue scores (63.31 ± 27.21), reflecting a double-peak burnout pattern characteristic of mid-career professionals. Mid-career nurses are often considered the core workforce—serving as clinical leaders while also managing teaching responsibilities and promotion pressures. Balancing demanding clinical duties with managerial roles frequently depletes their emotion regulation resources [25]. Nurses with 15 or more years of service had slightly lower scores, possibly due to promotion or role changes that reduced direct patient contact, or because they developed more mature emotion regulation strategies through prolonged professional experience [26].

Receiving empathy communication training was associated with lower compassion fatigue scores. Nurses who had not received such training had significantly higher compassion fatigue scores (58.91 ± 26.09) than those who had (49.14 ± 20.70), suggesting a potential protective association of professional competence with lower levels of compassion fatigue (B = 11.468, 95% CI: [7.403, 15.534], P < 0.001). This finding aligns with a study of palliative care professionals during the COVID-19 pandemic [27]. Training not only imparts communication skills but also may help enable nurses to balance support for others with self-protection [28]. However, only 25.2% of nurses in this study had received such training, highlighting a significant gap in professional development. Therefore, healthcare institutions may consider mandating empathy-based communication and end-of-life conversation skills as part of continuing education for all nursing staff, particularly for those working in high-risk departments. Consistent with Guo et al. [26], this study found an association between professional title and compassion fatigue in univariate analysis, but title did not emerge as an independent factor in the multivariate regression analysis, likely due to its strong correlation with department type and years of service; department attributes may be more strongly associated. The finding that nurses with 10–14 years of service had the highest compassion fatigue scores highlights the dual pressures faced by mid-career nurses: serving as a core workforce while being at high risk for burnout. This expands existing knowledge on the impact of years of service and suggests that managers should pay particular attention to nurses at specific career stages.

Based on these findings, targeted intervention strategies are warranted. First, hospital administrators should consider implementing emotional support in high-risk departments such as ICUs and oncology units—for example, by adjusting shift schedules, extending rest periods, or increasing rotation opportunities to reduce sustained emotional depletion. Second, empathy-based communication training should be incorporated into mandatory continuing education programs [29]. Burnout prevention workshops should be offered for core nursing staff with 10–14 years of service to help them identify early signs of burnout and take preventive action [30]. Third, managerial support is associated with lower compassion fatigue [2]. Hospitals should consider establishing mental health support systems that provide professional psychological counseling and emotional guidance for nurses [1]. Compassion fatigue is not a personal failure but rather a consequence of imbalance between occupational demands and individual resources. Addressing compassion fatigue requires both nurses’ development of emotion regulation skills and managers’ efforts to create a supportive work environment [31]. Such a dual approach—individual empowerment through training and organizational support through structural changes—may be helpful in addressing the high prevalence of compassion fatigue and in safeguarding nurses’ professional well-being.

These findings from Xinjiang—a region with distinct cultural characteristics and resource constraints—offer insights for understanding compassion fatigue in similar multicultural or resource-limited settings globally. While the specific context is unique, the core issues of high emotional load, mid-career burnout, and the protective association of communication training are likely transferable to clinical nurses in other regions facing comparable challenges.

From an international perspective, this study provides empirical evidence that compassion fatigue is not solely determined by national wealth or healthcare system maturity, but rather by common occupational stressors such as high emotional load, prolonged patient exposure, and insufficient training support. Therefore, the findings may inform nursing practice and policy in other developing countries or multicultural societies where nurses face similar working conditions, even if their healthcare systems differ. The protective role of communication training identified in this study is a low-cost, feasible intervention that could be implemented across various cultural contexts to mitigate compassion fatigue.

Limitations

Despite the strengths of this study, including its large sample size and coverage of hospitals at three levels across Xinjiang, several methodological considerations should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design allows us to identify associations but not to establish causal relationships between variables. Second, although we recruited participants from tertiary, secondary, and primary hospitals to enhance sample heterogeneity, convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, and the overrepresentation of oncology nurses (approximately 60%) limits the representativeness of the findings. Future studies should recruit more balanced samples across different departments to improve generalizability. Third, the variable-selection strategy (entering only variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis into the multivariate regression model) may have excluded potentially important confounding variables and increased the risk of residual confounding and model instability [32]. Although this approach is commonly used in exploratory cross-sectional studies to avoid overfitting, it remains a methodological limitation. Future studies are encouraged to adopt theory-driven variable selection methods based on prior evidence and conceptual frameworks. Consequently, our findings should be generalized to the broader population of clinical nurses with caution. Future studies should consider employing random sampling or larger multicenter designs, incorporating additional potential variables, and adopting longitudinal designs to examine the dynamic changes in compassion fatigue and the effectiveness of occupational interventions. Qualitative research could also explore nurses’ lived experiences and support needs in greater depth, particularly in multicultural and resource-limited settings such as Xinjiang.

Conclusion

Compassion fatigue levels among the participating clinical nurses were moderate. Nurses in intensive care and oncology departments exhibited a higher risk of compassion fatigue, and those lacking relevant training or having longer tenure in their current department experienced more pronounced fatigue. A dual approach of individual empowerment and organizational support could be considered as a potential strategy to address compassion fatigue, with the potential to contribute to improving the quality of nursing care and supporting nurses’ professional well-being.
