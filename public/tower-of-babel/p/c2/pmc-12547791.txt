3D-Printed Shellac-Based Delivery Systems: A Biopolymer Platform for Intestinal Targeting of Biologically Active Compounds
Authors: Siraprapa Chansatidkosol, Chutima Limmatvapirat, Pornsak Sriamornsak, Prasert Akkaramongkolporn, Kasitpong Thanawuth, Wantanwa Krongrawa, Sontaya Limmatvapirat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study presents the development of 3D-printed shellac-based devices designed for site-specific intestinal delivery of bioactive compounds. The devices were fabricated using fused deposition modeling (FDM) 3D printing technology, employing a shellac filament plasticized with 20% w/w poly­(ethylene glycol) 10000 (PEG 10000). The influence of structural parametersspecifically base thickness (0.5–2 mm) and lateral wall thickness (1–2 mm)on mechanical performance, disintegration behavior, and release kinetics was systematically evaluated using propranolol hydrochloride as a model bioactive compound. Characterization via scanning electron microscopy (SEM), mechanical testing, and thermal analysis confirmed the successful fabrication of devices with a consistent morphology and robust mechanical properties. Increased thickness notably enhanced the mechanical strength and modulated the release profiles. Disintegration and dissolution testing demonstrated that thicker devices resisted acidic degradation while enabling timely release under simulated intestinal conditions. These results underscore the versatility of shellac-based 3D-printed platforms for precise, delayed, and site-specific intestinal releasenot only for pharmaceutical agents but also for sensitive macromolecules, probiotics, enzymes, and nutraceuticals requiring protection from gastric degradation.

Introduction

In recent years, three-dimensional (3D) printing technology has emerged as a transformative tool across various sectors of healthcare and biotechnology, including pharmaceuticals, nutraceuticals, and functional food systems. − The advantages of 3D printing include cost-effectiveness, solvent-free processing, ease of use, and the ability to produce precisely controlled structures with complex geometries while maintaining excellent reproducibility. − Various 3D printing techniques have been explored in drug development, with fused deposition modeling (FDM) emerging as one of the most widely utilized methods, particularly for designing 3D-printed dosage forms. FDM operates by melting a thermoplastic material and extruding it through a high-temperature nozzle. The material is then deposited layer by layer onto a platform until the printing process is complete. To be suitable for FDM printing, filament materials must exhibit both thermoplastic behavior and favorable mechanical properties. , However, the broader application of FDM for oral delivery systemsincluding those for drugs, biologically active macromolecules, probiotics, enzymes, and other sensitive agentsremains limited by the availability of biocompatible and gastroprotective filament materials suitable for this technology. ,

While several thermoplastic polymers have been considered for FDM printing, , many are unsuitable for pharmaceutical applications. For example, commonly used polymers such as acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) and methacrylic acid copolymers are not ideal due to their nonbiocompatibility. Additionally, many synthetic polymers have relatively high melting points, making them unsuitable for drug formulations containing heat-sensitive compounds. To overcome these limitations, hot-melt extrusion (HME), an environmentally friendly process for developing solid dispersions without the use of solvents, has been explored as an alternative method for fabricating FDM-compatible pharmaceutical filaments. Among potential pharmaceutical-grade polymers, shellac stands out as a promising candidate due to its suitable thermal properties and its abundant availability as a natural resource in India, China, and Thailand.

Shellac, a natural resin secreted by lac insects (Kerria lacca or Laccifer lacca), has been widely used as a protective coating in the food, cosmetics, and agricultural industries. − In pharmaceuticals and food industries, shellac is valued for its excellent moisture barrier properties, strong film-forming capability, and fiber-forming ability. , It has been extensively applied in drug formulations for sealing, glossing, enteric coating, and drug delivery systems. In our previous study, shellac-based filaments were successfully fabricated at low temperatures using the HME process. However, their inherent brittleness posed a challenge, which was later mitigated by incorporating polyethylene glycol 10000 (PEG 10000) as a plasticizer. Despite these advancements, the potential of shellac-based filaments as an FDM-compatible material, particularly for targeted intestinal drug delivery, remains largely unexplored.

Targeted delivery to the intestine represents a significant challenge in the design of oral delivery systems, particularly for biologically active compounds that are unstable or ineffective in the gastric environment. Many active ingredientsincluding small-molecule drugs, peptides, proteins, probiotics, and enzymesare susceptible to degradation under acidic conditions, exhibit poor gastric absorption, or cause gastrointestinal irritation. These issues necessitate protective delivery platforms capable of withstanding the stomach and enabling controlled release in the intestinal tract. − For certain therapeutic applications, precise release in the distal small intestine or colon is essentialnot only for enhancing systemic absorption but also for achieving local treatment effects or incorporating lag time for chronotherapeutic interventions. Such approaches are especially beneficial for managing diseases with circadian patterns, including asthma, cardiovascular disorders, arthritis, and diabetes. For instance, hypertension often presents with an early morning surge in blood pressure, and time-specific intestinal release of antihypertensive agents can help synchronize drug absorption with this biological rhythm, potentially improving efficacy and minimizing side effects. , Therefore, advanced intestinal-targeted delivery systems offer broad utility across pharmaceutical, nutraceutical, and biotechnological domains.

In this study, propranolol hydrochloride was employed as a model compound to evaluate intestinal targeting. We developed 3D-printed shellac-based oral delivery devices designed to provide programmable release within the intestinal tract. By systematically varying design parameterssuch as base thickness and lateral wall thicknesswe investigated their influence on the devices’ physical characteristics, disintegration behavior, and release kinetics. This design-driven approach enabled fine-tuning of release profiles to achieve targeted delivery to different regions of the intestine. The findings may support the potential of this platform as a versatile and customizable system for the oral administration of drugs, macromolecules, probiotics, enzymes, or other bioactive components that are unstable in gastric environments.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of Core Tablets and 3D-Printed Shellac-Based Devices

Preparation of Core Tablets

Core tablets, each weighing 300 mg, were formulated with 20 mg of propranolol hydrochloride (6.7% w/w), along with excipients, including microcrystalline cellulose (57.8% w/w), lactose (30% w/w), sodium starch glycolate (4% w/w), magnesium stearate (1% w/w), and colloidal silicon dioxide (0.5% w/w). The tablets were manufactured by using a single-punch press equipped with 9.54 mm round flat punches. To ensure uniform compression, the hardness of the tablets was controlled within the range of 8–10 kg. The prepared core tablets were subsequently evaluated for their properties, as outlined in Section

Fabrication of 3D-Printed Shellac-Based Devices and Assembly with Core Tablets

Plasticized shellac filament was fabricated using a previously developed HME technique, as depicted in Figure . In brief, shellac and polyethylene glycol (PEG 10000) were blended using a mortar and pestle to ensure uniformity before extrusion. The resulting blend was then processed through a single-screw extruder (model: Wellzoom Desktop Extruder Line II, Shenzhen Mistar Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). The extrusion was conducted at a screw speed of 14 rpm, with the inlet and outlet temperatures maintained at 85 and 80 °C, respectively. Upon extrusion, the filament was rapidly cooled by using an air blower and subsequently stored at 4 °C to preserve its material properties. Before using 3D printing applications, the filament was conditioned at room temperature to stabilize its thermal and mechanical characteristics.

Plasticized shellac filament fabrication through the hot-melt extrusion process.

The 3D-printed shellac-based device was designed as a two-part system consisting of a body and a cap with structural variations in base and lateral thicknesses to regulate drug release intervals, as shown in Figure . To assess the impact of base thickness, the base dimensions of both the body and cap were varied between 0.5 and 2 mm, while the lateral thickness remained constant at 1 mm. These formulations were denoted as B0.5L1, B0.75L1, B1L1, B1.25L1, B1.5L1, B1.75L1, and B2L1. Similarly, to evaluate the influence of lateral thickness, the lateral dimensions were adjusted from 1 mm to 2 mm while keeping the base thickness constant at 2 mm. These formulations were labeled B2L1, B2L1.25, B2L1.5, and B2L2. These variations provided a systematic framework for analyzing the effect of geometric parameters on drug release performance. The details of the design are illustrated in Figure S1, Supporting Information.

Flowchart representing the fabrication process and evaluation of 3D-printed shellac-based devices.

The design of 3D-printed shellac-based devices was created using Fusion 360 (Autodesk Inc., USA) and exported in stereolithography (.stl) format for slicing with FlashPrint software (v.3.23.1, Flash Forge, USA). The devices were fabricated using a FlashForge Creator Pro FDM 3D printer (Zhejiang FlashForge 3D Technology Co., Ltd., China). The printing material consisted of a plasticized shellac filament, as described above. The printing parameters were optimized as follows: nozzle diameter: 0.4 mm, nozzle temperature: 125 °C, build plate temperature: 30 °C, printing speed: 20 mm/s, feed rate: 70 mm/s, layer height: ∼0.12 mm; each cap and body combination (without the core tablet) was assembled and evaluated for morphology and mechanical properties, as detailed in Section .

To examine the intestinal-targeting capability of the shellac-based devices, core tablets were inserted into the body component and sealed with the cap. The assembled devices were subsequently subjected to disintegration tests and drug release studies to evaluate their effectiveness in modulating drug release behavior.

Evaluation of Core Tablets

Weight and Size of Tablets

A set of ten tablets was accurately weighed using an analytical balance (CP224S, Sartorius, Germany). The diameter and thickness of each tablet were measured using electronic digital display vernier calipers (01407A, Louisware, China). The mean values and standard deviations for the weight, diameter, and thickness were then calculated.

Hardness

The hardness of ten tablets was measured using a tablet hardness tester (THB 225TD, Erweka GmbH, Heusenstamm, Germany).

Friability

The friability of core tablets was assessed by using a friability tester (D-61350, Erweka GmbH, Heusenstamm, Germany). A sample of tablets, weighing approximately 6.5 g (W 1), was placed in a drum and rotated at 25 rpm for 4 min. After the test, any dust was removed, and the tablets were reweighed (W 2). The friability (%) was then calculated using the following equation: Friability(%)=W1−W2W1×100

Disintegration

The disintegration time of six tablets was determined by using the USP disintegration apparatus (ZT323, Erweka GmbH, Heusenstamm, Germany). The test was conducted in 0.1 N HCl (pH 1.2) at a controlled temperature of 37 ± 0.5 °C. The disintegration time was recorded from the moment that each tablet fully disintegrated and passed through the mesh of the apparatus.

Drug Content

Tablets equivalent to 80 mg of propranolol hydrochloride were randomly selected and dissolved in 60 mL of ethanol within a 100 mL volumetric flask. The mixture was agitated for 2 h and subjected to ultrasonic treatment for 30 min. The volume was then adjusted to 100 mL in ethanol to prepare the stock solution. A 1.25 mL aliquot was further diluted in a 50 mL volumetric flask using a water-to-acetonitrile mixture (13:7) to yield a final concentration of 20 μg/mL. The solution was filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter prior to analysis. The quantification was performed using an Agilent 1220 Infinity II HPLC system with a UV detector and a C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm, Inertsil ODS-3). The mobile phase, comprising acetonitrile and 6.8 mg/mL monobasic potassium phosphate buffer (30:70), was delivered at a rate of 1 mL/min. A 20 μL injection volume was used, and the analysis was conducted at 25 ± 2 °C. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Drug Release

The drug release profiles of propranolol hydrochloride tablets were assessed using a USP dissolution apparatus I (AT Xtend, Sotax, Switzerland) following the USP-NF 2021 monograph for Propranolol Hydrochloride Extended-Release Capsules. Dissolution testing was conducted in 900 mL of 0.1 N HCl (pH 1.2) maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C, with a stirring speed of 100 rpm. At predefined time intervals, 3 mL samples were withdrawn and immediately replaced with fresh dissolution medium. The concentration of dissolved propranolol hydrochloride was determined using the HPLC-UV method, as detailed in Section . All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the drug release data were plotted over time to evaluate the dissolution profile.

Evaluation of 3D-Printed Shellac-Based Devices and Their Assembled 3D-Printed Devices

Morphological Characterization of 3D-Printed Shellac-Based Devices

The physical appearance of the 3D-printed shellac-based devices was assessed through visual inspection and digital camera imaging (AM4113T, Dino-Lite, Taiwan) to evaluate their surface integrity, uniformity, and overall structural consistency. For a more detailed morphological analysis, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was utilized to examine both the top and side views of the devices. Imaging was performed using a Mira3 SEM (TESCAN, Brno, Czech Republic) at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. Prior to imaging, the samples were mounted onto metal stubs and coated with a thin layer of gold under vacuum conditions to enhance the conductivity and image resolution.

Size and Weight of 3D-Printed Shellac-based Devices

A set of caps, bodies, and assembled devices were accurately weighed using an analytical balance (CP224S, Sartorius, Germany). The diameter and thickness of each assembled device were measured using electronic digital vernier calipers (01407A, Louisware, China)

Mechanical Properties of 3D-Printed Shellac-Based Devices

The mechanical properties of the assembled devices (without a core tablet) were evaluated using a texture analyzer (TA.XT Plus, Stable Micro Systems, United Kingdom) in compression mode. Each assembled device was positioned vertically between the base and the compression probe (Part Code P/0.5, Stable Micro Systems, United Kingdom). During compression, the force (N) and deformation (mm) were recorded as the top probe descended at a rate of 0.5 mm/s until the device fractured. The evaluated parameters included tensile strength (MPa) and tensile strain (%), which were calculated using equations below. Each device configuration was tested using five samples, and the mean and standard deviation were calculated. Tensilestrength(MPa)=2FπDH where F is the maximum applied force expressed as Newton, D is the diameter of the tablet (mm), and H is the thickness of the tablet (mm). Tensilestrain(%)=δL0×100 where δ represents the extension length of the tablet, defined as δ = L 1 – L 0, with L 0 denoting the initial length and L 1 indicating the expanded length (mm).

Physicochemical Characterization of Filament Components and 3D-Printed Shellac-Based Devices

To evaluate the molecular interactions, crystallinity, and thermal behavior of the materials used in the development of plasticized shellac filaments and 3D-printed shellac-based devices, a combination of analytical techniquesFourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD), and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)was employed.

FTIR was performed using a Nicolet 4700 spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) to investigate possible interactions between shellac and other formulation components. The samples analyzed included individual components, physical mixtures, plasticized shellac filaments, and 3D-printed devices. The KBr disc method was used, and spectra were recorded over a wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm– 1, with 32 scans per sample at a resolution of 4 cm– 1. Spectral acquisition and interpretation were carried out using OMNIC software (Thermo Scientific, USA).

The crystalline or amorphous nature of the same sample sets was examined by using PXRD (MiniFlex II, Rigaku, Japan) with a Cu Kα radiation source and a Ni filter. Diffraction patterns were collected over a 2θ range of 5–50° at a scanning speed of 4°/min.

Thermal properties were evaluated by using DSC. Approximately 3–5 mg of each sample was sealed in standard aluminum pans and analyzed under a nitrogen purge at a flow rate of 20 mL/min. Heating was conducted from 20 to 200 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min. An empty sealed pan served as the reference. Thermograms were obtained and analyzed using Pyris software (PerkinElmer, USA).

Disintegration of Assembled Devices

The disintegration time of assembled devices containing core tablets was evaluated using a USP disintegration apparatus (ZT323, Erweka GmbH, Heusenstamm, Germany) at 37 ± 1.0 °C. The assessment was conducted in three different media: 0.1 N HCl (pH 1.2) for 2 h, phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 3 h, and phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) for 7 h. The disintegration time was recorded as the point at which the device fractured and the core tablet fully disintegrated (n = 3).

Drug Release of Assembled Devices

The in vitro dissolution study was conducted in three phases to evaluate the release profile of propranolol hydrochloride from the assembled devices. The dissolution test was performed according to USP-NF 2021, using a USP dissolution apparatus I (basket apparatus) AT Xtend (RD-DS-10, Sotax, Switzerland). The test was carried out in 900 mL of dissolution medium at a stirring speed of 100 rpm and a controlled temperature of 37 ± 0.5 °C. To mimic the drug release in the gastrointestinal tract, the dissolution study was conducted in three consecutive media, as previously reported. , The testing conditions were as follows: 0.1 N HCl (pH 1.2) for 2 h (simulating gastric retention time), phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 3 h (simulating small intestinal transit time), and phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) for 7 h (simulating colonic condition). At predetermined time intervals, 3 mL samples were withdrawn and immediately replaced with fresh dissolution medium. The concentration of dissolved propranolol hydrochloride was analyzed using the HPLC-UV method, as detailed in Section . All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the drug release data were plotted against time.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 17.0. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to evaluate the effects of independent variables on the measured parameters. For post hoc comparisons, the least significant difference (LSD) test was utilized. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results and Discussion

This study investigated the fabrication of assembled 3D-printed drug delivery devices by integrating HME with FDM. The resulting systems were applied for targeted oral delivery in tablet form within the gastrointestinal tract. Filaments for 3D printing were prepared using a formulation composed of shellac (80% w/w) and polyethylene glycol 10000 (PEG 10000, 20% w/w), a composition previously identified as optimal based on prior research. The drug-loaded core tablet, containing propranolol hydrochloride, was manufactured via direct compression. The 3D-printed structures were produced with variable geometries: base and cap thicknesses ranged from 0.5 to 2 mm, while lateral wall thickness varied between 1 and 2 mm. Postfabrication, the devices underwent systematic evaluation, as outlined in the following sections.

Characterization of Core Tablets Containing Propranolol Hydrochloride

The evaluation of the physical and chemical properties of the core tablets demonstrated uniformity in the key parameters. The tablets exhibited consistent weight (304.1 ± 1.66 mg), with a diameter of 9.53 ± 0.01 mm, a thickness of 3.41 ± 0.01 mm, and a hardness of 106 ± 8.59 N. The drug content in the sampled tablets was determined to be 99.94%, ensuring high formulation accuracy. Additionally, the disintegration time was recorded at under 1 min, indicating rapid tablet breakdown (Table S1, Supporting Information).

Dissolution studies were performed in a simulated gastric environment (pH 1.2) to assess the drug release profile. The results revealed that over 90% of the drug was released within the first 5 min, with complete dissolution achieved within 15 min (Figure S2, Supporting Information). These findings confirm the high dissolution efficiency of the core tablets, making them well-suited for incorporation as model core tablets into the 3D-printed shellac-based device.

Fabrication and Characterization of 3D-Printed Shellac-Based Devices

Shellac was investigated as a base material for 3D printing through the preparation of filaments, which were subsequently processed by using an FDM 3D printer. Filaments composed of 80:20 shellac to PEG 10000 (w/w) were found to be suitable for printing when their diameter was maintained within the range of 1.6–1.75 mm. Although the feeding process was less continuous and slower compared to that of commercially available polylactic acid (PLA) filamentsdue to the relatively lower mechanical strength of the shellac-based filamentsmanual adjustments enabled successful completion of the printing process.

Printer settings were systematically optimized to establish suitable printing conditions. The following parameters were set: a nozzle diameter of 0.4 mm, a filament feed rate of 70 mm/s, and a build plate temperature of 30 °C. The nozzle temperature was varied to determine the optimal extrusion condition. Temperatures below 110 °C resulted in inadequate flow and filament dripping, while temperatures between 110 and 120 °C led to inconsistent extrusion and incomplete prints. The optimal extrusion temperature was identified as 125 °C, enabling continuous flow and reliable print quality. This optimized setting was used to fabricate both the body and cap components of the 3D-printed shellac-based drug delivery devices designed for intestinal targeting. The printed devices were subsequently evaluated for their physical and physicochemical properties and functional performance, both before and after incorporation of core tablets.

Mechanical Properties of 3D-Printed Shellac-Based Devices

To evaluate the robustness of the 3D-printed devices and the influence of structural thickness on their mechanical performance, key mechanical propertiesincluding toughness, tensile strength, and tensile strainwere assessed by using a texture analyzer in compression mode, as shown in Figure . Toughness exhibited a clear increasing trend with both increasing base and lateral wall thicknesses (Figure a). A similar pattern was observed for the tensile strength (Figure b), indicating an enhanced ability of the devices to maintain structural integrity during handling, packaging, and administration. The flexibility of the devices, assessed by tensile strain (%), is shown in Figure c. Increasing the base thickness from 0.5 to 2 mm while maintaining a constant lateral thickness of 1 mm led to improved flexibility. However, when the base thickness was set at 2 mm and the lateral thickness exceeded 1.25 mm, a significant reduction in tensile strain was observed, indicating decreased flexibility. These findings suggested that both base and lateral wall thicknesses played critical roles in defining the mechanical performance of 3D-printed shellac-based devices. Specifically, layer thickness had a significant impact on the balance between rigidity and flexibility, which was vital for ensuring mechanical resilience in practical applications.

Physicochemical Properties of 3D-Printed Shellac-Based Devices

A combination of analytical techniquesFTIR, PXRD, and DSCwas utilized to investigate the molecular interactions, solid-state characteristics, and thermal properties of the materials involved in the formulation of plasticized shellac filaments and 3D-printed shellac-based devices, as shown in Figure .

FTIR spectra (a), PXRD diffractograms (b), and DSC thermograms (c) of shellac (SHL), PEG 10000, the physical mixture of shellac and PEG 10000, plasticized shellac filament, and the 3D-printed shellac-based device.

FTIR spectroscopy is a technique used for identifying functional groups and assessing potential molecular interactions. The FTIR spectra of shellac powder, PEG 10000, their physical mixtures, plasticized filaments, and 3D-printed devices are presented in Figure a. Shellac and PEG 10000 exhibited broad absorption bands in the range of 3400–3500 cm– 1, corresponding to O–H stretching vibrations from hydroxyl groups in carboxylic acid moieties and free hydroxyl groups within the shellac and PEG structures, respectively. A distinct peak observed at 2900 cm– 1 in both shellac and PEG 10000 spectra is attributed to C–H stretching in aliphatic chains. Shellac also exhibited a strong absorption band around 1700 cm– 1, characteristic of CO stretching in the carboxyl group (−COOH), and a band at 1255 cm– 1 corresponding to C–O–C stretching in ether linkages. These findings are consistent with previously reported FTIR data for shellac. PEG 10000 showed a characteristic peak at approximately 1100 cm– 1, indicative of ether (C–O–C) functional groups.

The FTIR spectra of the physical mixture and the filamentcomprising shellac and PEG 10000displayed overlapping peaks from both components with a slight change at the O–H stretching band around 3400–3500 cm–1, suggesting the absence of any significant chemical interaction or bond formation between them. Similarly, the spectrum of the 3D-printed devices was nearly identical to that of the filament, confirming that the thermal processing temperature (up to 120 °C) used during printing did not alter the structural integrity or chemical stability of the shellac matrix.

PXRD is a widely used analytical technique for evaluating the crystalline or amorphous nature of materials. The PXRD diffractograms of shellac, PEG 10000, their physical mixture, the plasticized filament, and the 3D-printed device are presented in Figure b. The diffractogram of shellac displayed a broad halo pattern, characteristic of an amorphous structure. In contrast, PEG 10000 exhibited a well-defined crystalline diffraction profile, with prominent sharp peaks observed at 2θ angles of approximately 19° and 23°, confirming its crystalline nature.

In the physical mixture, the crystalline peaks of PEG 10000 remained visible. However, in the filament, a significant decrease in the intensity of these characteristic crystalline peaks was observed, indicating that PEG 10000 was likely molecularly dispersed within the shellac matrix during the HME process. The PXRD pattern of the 3D-printed device closely resembled that of the filament, further confirming that the printing processdespite involving elevated temperaturesdid not significantly alter the solid-state characteristics of the formulation. This suggested good preservation of material integrity and supported the physical stability of the system postprinting.

DSC was employed to investigate the potential interactions between shellac and PEG 10000, as well as to assess the influence of filament extrusion and 3D printing on the thermal properties of the materials. Figure c presents the DSC thermograms of shellac, PEG 10000, their physical mixture, the plasticized filament, and the 3D-printed device. Shellac exhibited a broad endothermic peak ranging from 50 to 70 °C, indicating a gradual melting process. This is consistent with findings reported by AQBairik and colleagues, who observed a shellac melting point at around 69 °C. In contrast, PEG 10000 showed a distinct and sharp melting transition, with the onset at 60 °C, a peak at 64 °C, and complete melting at approximately 65 °C. Although the endothermic peaks of shellac and PEG 10000 appeared in a similar temperature range, PEG 10000 exhibited a higher enthalpy, reflecting its greater crystallinity compared with the amorphous nature of shellac.

After the incorporation of PEG 10000 into the shellac matrix through filament fabrication, both the melting peak temperature and enthalpy were notably reduced compared to those of the physical mixture. These results are consistent with findings from PXRD and FTIR analyses, indicating that PEG 10000 was molecularly dispersed within the shellac polymeric matrix. Furthermore, the melting temperature and enthalpy of the 3D-printed device showed no significant differences from those of the filament, confirming that the thermal properties of the material remained stable even after exposure to elevated temperatures during the 3D printing process.

Characterization of Assembled Devices

Friability

To ensure the structural integrity of the devices following the incorporation of the core tablet, the assembled units were subjected to a friability test. The percent friability of all assembled devices was relatively low, ranging from 0.003 to 0.026% (Supporting Information, Table S2), indicating minimal surface erosion or mechanical damage during testing. In addition, the core tablets were securely accommodated within the internal cavities of the devices, showing no visible signs of cracking, deformation, or misalignment. These results suggested that the mechanical strength and structural design of the 3D-printed shellac-based devices were sufficient to protect the embedded core during handling and potential physiological stresses, thereby supporting their suitability for oral drug delivery applications.

Notably, the device design may be further adapted to accommodate other types or sizes of core tablets by modifying parameters, such as internal diameter and lateral wall height, enabling broader applicability for customized or patient-specific oral dosage forms.

All devicesexcept for B0.5L1, which had the thinnest wallssuccessfully resisted disintegration in 0.1 N HCl, simulating gastric conditions. This indicates that shellac, known for its acid-resistant properties, maintained its integrity in acidic media when printed with sufficient thickness. In phosphate buffer at pH 7.4, which simulates the intestinal environment, the disintegration time increased with both base and lateral thickness. A statistically significant delay in disintegration was observed as base thickness increased from 1.0 to 2.0 mm, suggesting a strong correlation between wall mass and matrix erosion rate (p < 0.05). Similarly, lateral thickness was shown to influence the disintegration time. Increasing the lateral thickness beyond 1.5 mm extended the disintegration time up to approximately 5 h, indicating that the device could potentially reach the distal small intestine or even early colonic regions before releasing its drug content. These results suggested that device geometry can be effectively tailored to achieve site-specific drug delivery along the gastrointestinal tract by modulating structural parameters such as wall thickness. This hypothesis would be further evaluated through drug release studies, as described in the following section.

Drug Release

Dissolution testing was conducted in consecutive simulated gastrointestinal media to evaluate the drug release profile of propranolol hydrochloride from the assembled 3D-printed shellac-based devices. The study was designed to investigate how variations in base and lateral wall thicknesses influenced the release behavior across different regions of the GI tract.

Figure a illustrates the drug release profiles of assembled 3D-printed devices with base thicknesses ranging from 0.5 to 2 mm while maintaining a constant lateral thickness of 1 mm. All formulations exhibited no drug release during the initial 2 h in acidic medium (0.1 N HCl, pH 1.2), confirming the acid-resistant nature of shellac. It was noted that the device with a base thickness of 0.5 mm and lateral thickness of 1 mm (B0.5L1) disintegrated during disintegration testing in acidic medium; however, no drug release was observed under the same conditions during the dissolution study. This discrepancy may be attributed to the greater mechanical force applied in the disintegration test, which does not fully replicate the hydrodynamic conditions of the dissolution apparatus. Upon transition to phosphate buffer (pH 7.4)mimicking the intestinal environmentdrug release was initiated. Devices with thinner bases (0.5 and 0.75 mm) exhibited rapid release, reaching nearly 100% cumulative release within 3 h. In contrast, devices with base thicknesses ≥ 1 mm showed a delayed release profile, with only 24–28% of the drug released by the fourth hour, which correlates well with disintegration results, indicating shellac matrix erosion beyond 3 h. Subsequent exposure to phosphate buffer (pH 6.8)simulating later stages of gastrointestinal transitfurther promoted drug release, with >95% cumulative release achieved within 12 h for all formulations.

The influence of lateral wall thickness on drug release was investigated using devices with a fixed base thickness of 2 mm and lateral thicknesses ranging from 1 to 2 mm (Figure b). Devices with lateral thicknesses of 1.0 and 1.25 mm initiated drug release earlier, starting around the third hour and achieving 24–25% cumulative release by the fourth hour. However, as the lateral thickness increased to 1.5 mm and above, a notable delay in drug release was observed. Devices with a 1.5 mm lateral thickness began releasing the drug at the fifth hour, reaching approximately 53% release by the sixth hour. Devices with a 2 mm lateral thickness exhibited the most delayed onset, with minimal release until the sixth hour, followed by a burst release phase, ultimately achieving over 98% cumulative release within 12 h.

The results revealed that modulating base and lateral wall thicknesses effectively influenced the lag time and release kinetics of the 3D-printed shellac-based devices. Thicker structures delayed drug release, demonstrating the potential of this approach as a tunable platform for site-specific drug delivery along the gastrointestinal tractfrom the duodenum to the distal ileum or proximal colon. A similar study by Okwuosa et al. investigated 3D-printed colon-targeting devices using pH-responsive polymers, such as Eudragit FS100 and polylactic acid, and confirmed that increasing shell thickness significantly prolonged the lag time before drug release. These findings reinforce the concept that structural design plays a critical role in defining drug release profiles.

In the present study, the delayed release behavior observed in thicker shellac-based devices was consistent with an erosion-controlled mechanism in alkaline environments. The increased wall thickness maintained the matrix integrity for a longer duration, thereby slowing drug dissolution and prolonging the release period.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated the development of personalized, intestine-targeted drug delivery systems using 3D-printed shellac-based devices fabricated via FDM. The incorporation of 20% w/w PEG 10000 into shellac produced plasticized filaments with suitable mechanical and thermal properties for FDM-based processing. By systematically adjusting the base and lateral wall thicknesses, we showed that the geometry of the printed devices significantly influences critical attributes such as mechanical strength, disintegration, and drug release. The 3D-printed devices exhibited excellent mechanical robustness and acid resistance, effectively preventing drug release in the gastric environment. Notably, disintegration and dissolution studies in simulated intestinal media confirmed that the lag time and release profiles could be precisely modulated through geometric design. Thinner configurations facilitated rapid release in the upper intestine, whereas thicker designs achieved delayed release, enabling potential drug delivery to the distal ileum or colon. These findings, therefore, underscore the potential of shellac-based 3D printing as a versatile and tunable platform for site-specific and personalized oral drug delivery. However, further research is needed to explore the influence of the shellac matrix composition, the scalability of this approach, and its applicability to a broader range of therapeutic agents and clinical use cases.
