Exploring Canadian Registered Dietitians' Confidence, Facilitators, Barriers, and Resource Needs in Father Engagement
Authors: Hillary WH Lo, Alyssa Chapleau, Paulina Morelli, Jess Haines, Geoff DC Ball, Brian K Lo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionRegistered dietitians (RDs) play a critical role in delivering family‐centered care by engaging both fathers and mothers to support children's optimal long‐term growth and development. Although fathers use distinct feeding practices and play an important role in shaping children's diet and growth, existing evidence suggests that they may be less engaged in pediatric nutrition care and services than mothers. Given that RDs are uniquely positioned to promote caregiver alignment in feeding practices and strengthen children's dietary and health outcomes, this study aimed to explore Canadian RDs' perceived confidence, facilitators, barriers, and resource needs related to father engagement in pediatric nutrition care and services.MethodsA cross‐sectional online survey was distributed to RDs across Canada between November 2024 and July 2025. The survey included six 5‐point Likert scale items assessing RDs' perceived confidence in engaging fathers in pediatric nutrition care and services, informed by the Connect, Attend, Participate, Enact (CAPE) model of parental engagement, as well as closed‐ended questions on resource and training needs. Additionally, open‐text questions assessed RDs' perceived facilitators and barriers to father engagement. Quantitative data were analyzed descriptively, while qualitative data were examined using an inductive content analysis approach to identify the major facilitator and barrier themes.ResultsA total of 129 RDs (96.9% female; 91.2% white; 69.0% ≥ 6 years of pediatric nutrition experience) participated in this study. While most RDs reported they felt very confident in inviting fathers to appointments (85.3%), encouraging their participation (83.7%), and contribution to care planning work (81.4%), fewer felt confident in retaining fathers in ongoing pediatric nutrition care and services (67.5%). Based on qualitative content analysis of open‐text responses, the most commonly reported facilitators to father engagement included fathers' presence and availability (40.4%), interest and openness (35.1%), and high caregiving involvement (23.7%), while the most commonly reported barriers included fathers' absence at appointments (33.3%), limited caregiving roles (33.3%), fathers' limited availability due to work schedule (23.3%), and traditional gender norms undermining father engagement (16.7%). Only 3.1% RDs had received prior training in father‐engagement practices. The top three selected resource needs were webinars (92.1%), websites with practice guidelines (66.7%), and self‐assessment tools (44.4%).ConclusionsWhile RDs reported confidence in various aspects of father engagement, few had received formal training in this area. Study findings highlighted opportunities for targeted professional development resources within dietetics to enhance facilitators and address barriers to engaging fathers in pediatric nutrition care and services.Keywords: dietetics, dietitian, family‐centered care, father engagement, fathers, pediatric nutrition

Introduction

Family‐centered care (FCC) is widely recommended in pediatric health care, emphasizing collaborative partnerships and shared decision‐making between families and healthcare providers [1]. Compared with only involving one parent, involving both fathers and mothers in care planning, implementation, and evaluation optimizes children's health outcomes, including improved treatment adherence, psychological adjustment, physical well‐being, and quality of care during hospitalization [2, 3, 4]. Fathers and mothers both contribute meaningfully to children's early growth, development, and well‐being. While mothers are often central to breastfeeding and early responsive caregiving [5], fathers are also directly involved in feeding (e.g., bottle‐feeding and complementary feeding) and broader caregiving interactions [6]. Research indicates that paternal involvement is associated with children's behavioral regulation, resilience, and socioemotional development [4, 7]. These complementary roles highlight the importance of meaningfully involving fathers alongside mothers in pediatric health care. In the context of feeding specifically, fathers' feeding practices are associated with children's eating behaviors and weight‐related outcomes [6], and inconsistent or unresponsive feeding practices across caregivers may undermine efforts to establish healthy eating patterns [6]. Together, these findings underscore the importance of meaningfully engaging fathers alongside mothers in pediatric health care.

Despite the benefits, fathers continue to have low participation in pediatric health care visits and services. For example, in a recent US survey of 1142 parents of children aged 0–17 years old examining responsibility for scheduling child healthcare appointments and attending well‐child and sick visits, only 40.0% of fathers reported handling all three tasks, compared to 82.9% of mothers [8]. Likewise, in a study conducted in Portugal examining parental involvement in the care of hospitalized children, Melo et al. [9] reported that fathers only comprised 17% of the caregiver sample (n = 660). Although no study has directly examined fathers' involvement in pediatric nutrition care and services, Markowitz et al. [10] conducted a retrospective electronic health record review of 1771 patients aged 0–29 years old in a pediatric diabetes clinic in US and found that fathers attended only 18% of registered dietitians (RDs) visits, which was lower than attendance for physicians (24%), nurse practitioners (23%), and nurses or certified diabetes educators (21%) [10]. Fathers' lower engagement with RDs may reflect barriers at different levels. At the individual level, fathers may perceive low competence and responsibility in child feeding [11, 12, 13, 14, 15] or have had prior negative experiences with child services [16, 17]. Interpersonally, maternal gatekeeping may limit involvement [11, 12]. Organizationally, limited professional development in father engagement may constrain practice [13, 16, 17]. At the broader societal level, persistent gender norms positioning mothers as primary caregivers may further restrict fathers' participation in pediatric nutrition services [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17].

Engaging fathers in pediatric nutrition care and services is crucial to reduce the nutrition care burden often placed on mothers and promote inter‐parental consistency in supporting sustainable dietary behavior change in children [18, 19]. While mothers remain the primary parent responsible for cooking and feeding in many heterosexual households [12], recent data from various regions, including North America [20, 21], Europe [22], Oceania [23], Africa [24], and Asia [25, 26, 27] have shown that fathers' involvement in family meal preparation and child feeding is increasing. Beyond supporting fathers in day‐to‐day nutrition‐related tasks, it is also essential to equip them with the skills and confidence to take on more complex responsibilities alongside mothers when a child is managing a diet‐related illness. Congruent and positive feeding strategies (e.g., structure‐based and autonomy supportive‐based practices) between fathers and mothers are associated with healthier dietary intakes and growth in children [28, 29].

Recognizing that fathers often have low participation in pediatric health care visits and services, and that their important role is not consistently emphasized within FCC models, several health organizations, including the World Health Organization [30], Public Health Agency of Canada [31], American Academy of Pediatrics [4], and the Victorian Department of Health from Australia [32], released recommendations to promote father engagement. However, their applicability to dietetics practice remains unclear due to the unique complexities of child feeding and nutrition counseling, such as exploring families' feeding co‐parenting unity and gendered perceptions of feeding roles. The lack of RD‐specific resources may be due to the lack of knowledge about RDs' perceptions and experiences in this area. To address this critical knowledge gap, this study aimed to explore Canadian RDs' perceived confidence, facilitators, barriers, and resource needs related to father engagement in pediatric nutrition care and services. Identifying areas where RDs feel less confident can inform targeted training priorities and continuing professional development, while high levels of confidence may reflect established competencies that can be reinforced within father‐inclusive practice models. Similarly, understanding perceived facilitators enables existing strengths to be leveraged, scaled, and embedded in future resource development, whereas identifying barriers informs strategies to address challenges and improve father engagement in practice. Lastly, identifying RDs' top resource needs helps prioritize the development of targeted, practical, and evidence‐informed tools or training materials that align with their needs in father engagement practice.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

A cross‐sectional online survey was conducted among Canadian RDs as part of a larger study exploring their experiences engaging fathers. Implementation and reporting of this study adhered to the Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E‐Surveys [33]. The University of Guelph Research Ethics Board approved this study (REB # 24‐05‐003).

Survey Development and Validation

The initial survey was drafted by the senior author (BKL), covering six broad topics, including RDs' father engagement experiences, father engagement confidence, father engagement facilitators and barriers, father engagement resource needs, practice profile, and demographic characteristics. It was later validated for face and content by an expert panel of five RDs working in pediatric nutrition and three nutrition scientists (GDCB, JH, and an external researcher). The expert panel provided feedback on the comprehensiveness, relevance, and clarity of the survey contents. Once survey feedback was incorporated, the survey was pilot tested online via QualtricsXM (Provo, UT, USA) by the lead author (HWHL), the senior author (BKL), and a co‐author (PM) to ensure acceptability of the online format of the survey and the usability of the captured data. For the present study, we used questions related to RDs' father engagement confidence, father engagement facilitators and barriers, father engagement resources, practice profile, and demographic characteristics (Supporting File 1).

Father engagement confidence was measured by six items developed by the research team based on the Conceptual Model of Parental Engagement in Programmes for Child Mental Health‐Connect, Attend, Participate, Enact (CAPE) [34]. The CAPE Model highlights the different areas where dietitians can engage fathers in pediatric nutrition care and services. Specifically, we asked RDs to rate their perceived confidence to (1) invite fathers to attend pediatric nutrition care and services (Connect), (2) retain (attend follow‐up sessions/appointments, either alone or with others) fathers in pediatric nutrition care and services (Attend), (3) encourage fathers to participate in session activities during sessions (Participate), (4) encourage fathers to contribute to care planning work (Participate), (5) support fathers' success in implementing suggested strategies (Enact), and (6) support fathers' success in sustaining suggested strategies (Enact). Each item was rated on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = not at all confident to 5 = very confident).

Father engagement facilitators and barriers were queried using two open‐text questions for participants to share factors that make it easy (i.e., facilitators) and challenging (i.e., barriers) to engage fathers in pediatric nutrition care and services.

Father engagement resources were measured by asking RDs to select the top three resources that they believe would be useful to support their competence in engaging fathers in pediatric nutrition care and services. Resource options included webinars, websites with practice guidelines, hands‐on workshops, university‐course resources, and self‐assessment tools identifying practice strengths and/or weaknesses. An “other resources (please specify)” option was also given. The response options were informed by discussions with the expert panel to ensure their relevance and applicability to dietetics practice.

Practice profile was assessed using questions that described participants' professional backgrounds and their scope of practice in pediatric nutrition. Variables included international training status, years of experience in providing pediatric nutrition care and services, region of practice, pediatric age group(s) served, practice setting(s), specialities, and average weekly hours spent providing pediatric nutrition care and services.

Demographic characteristics included participants' age, gender, race and ethnicity, highest level of education attained, and parental status.

Study Participants and Recruitment

To be eligible, participants needed to be (1) Canadian RDs who self‐identified as regularly (at least one client/patient in a month) providing direct pediatric nutrition care and services to children and their families in Canada and (2) able to complete the survey in English. Because there is no comprehensive contact list of all RDs in Canada for research, we used convenience sampling for recruitment by circulating a survey invitation, posting three study flyers on social media platforms, and direct emailing. Participants were also asked to share the survey link after survey completion to maximize snowball sampling. Specifically, we contacted various national and regional dietetic‐related professional organizations to share our survey invitation to their members, including Dietitians of Canada, Ontario Dietitians in Public Health, Primary Care Dietitian Association, Saskatchewan College of Dietitians, Nova Scotia College of Dietitians and Nutritionists, The College of Dietitians of Prince Edward Island, and Alberta Health Services. To encourage participation from RDs serving remote communities in Northern Territories, we also sent our survey invitation to nutrition services and programs in Yukon, Northwest Territories, and Nunavut. We also sent our survey invitation to nine children's hospitals across Canada and other independently operated email listservs (e.g., Gerry's List). Three study flyers (two in English and one in French) were posted on LinkedIn (e.g., BKL's personal page and Canadian Foundation for Dietetic Research), Instagram (e.g., Canadian Foundation for Dietetic Research), and Facebook professional groups (e.g., Internationally Educated Dietitians Network of Canada, Private Practice Dietitians in Canada, BC Dietitians Network, Alberta Private Practice Dietitians, Dietitians of Newfoundland and Labrador, Dietitian Alliance/Alliance des Diététistes (Ottawa & Area), and Weight Inclusive Dietitians in Canada).

We also emailed our survey invitation directly to RDs using the “Find a Dietitian” feature of the Dietitians of Canada (DC) website. To determine which RDs would be contacted, the keywords “Neonatal ICU,” “Prenatal,” and “Prenatal, postnatal, and obstetrics” were selected in the “Health Concern” search bar on the DC website. Each eligible participant was emailed a survey invitation using their publicly available contact information.

Survey Platform and Consent

The survey was disseminated and implemented online using QualtricsXM (Provo, UT, USA) and was open from November 2024 to July 2025. Study information was provided on the first page of the online survey, and participants indicated their implied informed consent by starting the online survey questions. Participants did not receive any incentive for completing the online survey.

Data Analysis

A total of 201 survey responses were collected. We excluded 51 incomplete responses, defined as those not marked as submitted. An additional 21 participants were excluded for ineligibility (18 reported not providing direct pediatric nutrition care and services; three indicated they had previously completed the survey). Data were reviewed and flagged for data integrity following the recommended procedures by Wang et al. [35], including identifying inconsistent response patterns, implausible information, straightlining, and unrealistically short completion times. We also checked for potential fraudulent or duplicate entries using the built‐in Qualtrics functions [36]. All flagged cases were found to be legitimate upon inspection and the final analytic sample consisted of 129 participants.

Descriptive statistics (percentages and frequencies) were used to characterize quantitative data. All quantitative analyses were conducted using R (version 4.1.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) within RStudio (version 2022.2.2.485, Boston, MA, USA). For qualitative data, open‐ended survey responses on dietitians' perceived facilitators and barriers to engaging fathers in pediatric nutrition care and services were analyzed using an inductive content analysis approach, following the preparation, organizing, and reporting phases outlined by Elo and Kyngäs [37]. This method is suitable for the exploratory nature of the study and the limited literature addressing father engagement within dietetics practice [38]. Prior to coding, blank or irrelevant entries (e.g., “N/A,” “unsure,” or “can't think of any”) were excluded, resulting in a total of 114 facilitator and 120 barrier responses for analysis, each representing one unique participant.

During the preparation phase, two researchers (HWHL and AC) independently familiarized themselves with the data and observed that the emergent ideas reflected facilitators and barriers operating at multiple levels (e.g., individual, interpersonal, institutional/organizational, and societal); thus, the socio‐ecological model was used as an organizing framework to develop an initial codebook [39]. In the organizing phase, three authors (HWHL, AC, and BKL) independently applied the initial codebook to a sample of 35 facilitator responses. The three authors then met to compare coding decisions, discuss discrepancies, and revise code definitions. Following initial coding, codes were examined for conceptual overlap and patterned meaning. Related codes were collapsed and organized into higher‐order themes through iterative discussion among the research team. Once themes were defined and refined, they informed the final coding framework, which was applied consistently to the full dataset. The final codebook can be found in Supporting File 2 (Tables S1 and S2). The barrier responses were analyzed separately following the same procedure. In the reporting phase, we summarized theme frequencies based on the number of unique participants mentioning each theme and maintained an audit trail documenting all codebook revisions to enhance transparency and credibility [37]. All qualitative analyses were conducted using Microsoft Word.

Results

Demographic Characteristics and Practice Profile

Briefly, of the 129 RDs included in the study, most were female (96.9%) and identified as white (91.2%), with a mean (SD) age of 40.5 (10.3) years (range 23–63 years). About half of them obtained a bachelor's degree (56.6%), while 40.3% of participants had also completed post‐graduate training. Most were Canadian‐trained (96.9%), and 69.0% of participants had at least 6 years of experience providing pediatric nutrition care and services. Confidence Level in Father Engagement

Overall, participants reported high levels of confidence in engaging fathers across various aspects of father engagement (Table 2). Most indicated being either “confident” or “very confident” in inviting fathers to attend appointments (85.3%), encouraging fathers' participation during sessions (83.7%), and encouraging fathers to contribute to care planning work (81.4%). The proportion of participants who indicated being either “confident” or “very confident” was slightly lower but still high in the areas of supporting fathers' success in strategy implementations (77.5%) and sustaining strategies over time (75.2%). While participants' confidence in retaining fathers in ongoing pediatric nutrition care and services also remains relatively high, the proportion of participants indicating “confident” or “very confident” was the lowest (67.5%).

Overall, we identified seven key facilitators, three emerged at the individual level related to fathers, two at the individual level related to RDs, one at the interpersonal level related to families, and one at the institutional/organization level related to dietitians' workplaces (Table 3). Among the 114 participants who provided valid comments for facilitators, the most frequently reported facilitators were at the individual level related to fathers, with fathers' presence and availability (40.4%), fathers' interest and openness (35.1%), and fathers' caregiving involvement and confidence (23.7%) being the most common. At the individual level related to RDs, participants noted that engagement is easier when they recognized and treated fathers as equal caregivers (16.7%) and employed father‐inclusive engagement strategies (11.4%). At the interpersonal level, participants identified collaborative co‐parenting (9.6%) as an important facilitator within the family. At the institutional level, father‐inclusive service design (11.4%) was identified as a structural facilitator within the workplace.

We also identified seven key barriers, four at the individual level related to fathers, one at each interpersonal, institutional/organization level related to fathers' workplace, and the societal level (Table 4). Among the 120 participants who provided valid comments for barriers, the most commonly reported barriers were also at the individual level. The two most prominent challenges were fathers' absence at appointments (33.3%) and their secondary role in caregiving and feeding (33.3%), followed by fathers' lack of interest in nutrition (10.0%) and their disengagement during sessions (10.0%). At the interpersonal level, relational barriers were described, including lack of co‐parenting unity (8.3%). At the institutional level, the most frequently cited barrier was fathers' rigid work schedules (23.3%). At the societal level, participants identified traditional gender norms undermining father engagement (16.7%) as barriers that continue to hinder fathers' involvement in pediatric nutrition care and services.

Father Engagement Resource Needs

Only four participants (3.1%) had received prior formal training in father engagement. Among these participants, training received was either free (2.3%) or paid for by their employer (1.6%). For their perceived needs, participants prioritized webinars offering practical tools, methods, and evidence‐based strategies (92.1%), websites with best practice guidelines (66.7%), and self‐assessment tools that identify practice strengths and/or weaknesses (44.4%) (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, most RDs reported high confidence across various aspects of father engagement. Perceived facilitators and barriers were identified across the individual, interpersonal, institutional/organizational, and societal levels, with many rooted at the individual level related to fathers. RDs' limited prior training and identified resource needs related to father engagement highlight opportunities to develop targeted, practice‐based resources.

RDs' perceived high level of confidence in inviting fathers to pediatric nutrition care and services may be explained by the nature of our sample that many RDs practiced in hospital settings and worked predominantly with infants and preschool‐aged children, contexts in which fathers may be more present due to the immediacy or complexity of care needs. Prior studies have found that fathers have a stronger sense of responsibility and influence in child health decision‐making in their children's early diagnostic or developmentally sensitive periods [41, 42]. When comparing fathers' involvement in various pediatric care contexts, studies have consistently found that fathers attend more regularly for prenatal appointments [41], childbirth [42], and acute care appointments (e.g., surgery) [43]. Garfield and Isacco [43] noted that some fathers demonstrate “situational flexibility,” rearranging work schedules to attend pediatric care visits when their child's health requires early attention [43]. In addition, RDs' high confidence in recruiting fathers may also be due to the relatively low level of specialized skill required and the routine nature of this task in RDs' practice.

RDs' high confidence in engaging fathers during sessions (e.g., encouraging fathers' participation and contributions during sessions) may be supported by father‐level facilitators reported by RDs and their counseling experience. In written responses, many RDs reported that fathers' physical presence and availability made engagement easier, as their physical presence naturally creates more opportunities for direct interaction [16]. Coupled with RDs' perceived facilitator attributed by fathers' interest and openness during sessions, it likely reinforces RDs' confidence in fostering fathers' active engagement and their contribution to strategy formulation. Notably, more than two‐thirds of participants had at least six years of pediatric nutrition experience. Given that years of experience are positively associated with greater perceived competence and self‐efficacy in counseling across nutrition and other health professions [44, 45], the accumulated experience in nutrition counseling and care planning development with all caregivers among RDs in our sample may contribute to their confidence in facilitating fathers' engagement during sessions.

In contrast, RDs expressed relatively lower confidence in their ability to support fathers in implementing and sustaining strategies, with the least proportion of RDs feeling confident in retaining fathers across ongoing visits. These findings align with several father‐level barriers identified in our study, including fathers' absence at appointments, their secondary role in feeding or caregiving, rigid work schedules, and low interest in nutrition. Although fathers are increasingly involved in child care, mothers are still responsible for most food‐related responsibilities in many households [12]. As participants described, when fathers have limited involvement in day‐to‐day feeding, they find it challenging to explore children's dietary behaviors with fathers and to develop nutrition plans that incorporate fathers' roles. This limits opportunities for RDs to create “buy‐ins” for fathers to implement the suggested changes and attend follow‐ups. Fathers' rigid work schedules, particularly among fathers of infants, who typically have shorter parental leave periods [46], further exacerbate difficulties in retaining fathers across ongoing care.

Interestingly, the perceived facilitators and barriers to father engagement reported by RDs are primarily centered on father‐related factors and the gendered division of labor within households. The most commonly reported factors are fathers' presence and availability, and their secondary role in feeding, aligning with findings from studies examining fathers' participation in child health and parenting intervention programs [16, 47, 48]. While these factors are uniquely individualized within families and are not easily modified, others have suggested that fathers may feel more competent and motivated to participate in child health services when they are seen as equal caregivers by practitioners [16]. Existing resources from organizations across multiple countries, such as the Father Engagement Training & Professional Development for Staff module at Dad Central (Canada) [49], the Training for Professionals module at Fatherhood Institute (UK) [50], the Partnering with Dads to Enhance Pediatric Care module at Center for Health Care Strategies (US) [51], and the Father‐Inclusive Practice Resource at LEAD Professional Development Association Inc. (Australia) [52], suggest that strategies such as encouraging practitioners to challenge their own biases or assumptions about fathers' caregiving roles, explicitly inviting fathers to appointments, and using father‐inclusive language and imagery in clinical materials may help enhance fathers' engagement in pediatric services. Although the open‐text questions in our survey were not specifically designed to elicit detailed descriptions of father‐inclusive engagement strategies or service models, some RDs nevertheless described examples of such practices in their responses. Several of these examples overlap with strategies highlighted in existing non‐dietetics‐focused resources, including directly addressing fathers during consultations [49, 50, 51, 52], encouraging their participation in discussions [49, 50, 51, 52], acknowledging their caregiving roles [50, 51, 52], explicitly inviting fathers to attend appointments or programs [49, 50, 51, 52], and offering flexible service delivery options (e.g., evening or weekend appointments or virtual communication) to facilitate their participation [51].

As Wynter et al. [16] emphasized, without appropriate formal training, practitioners rarely shift away from maternal‐centric models of care despite recognizing the importance of involving fathers in decision‐making. Given that father engagement is an emerging area within dietetics [12, 53], RDs may require additional guidance to integrate father‐inclusive approaches into everyday practice. Our survey findings provide preliminary insights into RDs' perceived facilitators, barriers, and preferred formats for father‐engagement resources. These insights may help inform the initial development of training materials and guidance documents aimed at supporting father‐inclusive pediatric nutrition care and services. However, further research, such as interviews and focus groups with RDs and fathers, is needed to more comprehensively explore the specific content, strategies, and contextual considerations required to develop these resources. Future work should also adopt collaborative approaches that engage both RDs and fathers to ensure that resource development is responsive to practice and family needs. Such resources may be especially useful for pediatric nutrition conditions requiring long‐term parental involvement and feeding strategies, such as types 1 and 2 diabetes [54], pediatric obesity [55], and eating or feeding disorders [56]. These resources can be in flexible, low‐cost formats such as webinars and websites with best‐practice guidelines as suggested by RDs in our study.

While this study provides novel information on RDs' father engagement experiences, several limitations should be considered. First, although RDs reported high confidence across many aspects of father engagement, our survey only captured perceived confidence rather than observed practice. We do not know what and how often father engagement practices are used or whether fathers perceive them as effective. Second, we were unable to estimate a response rate or evaluate the representativeness of our sample relative to RDs working in pediatrics across Canada, as no publicly available data exist regarding the number, demographic characteristics, or practice profiles of RDs working in pediatrics nationally. Third, our sample may be subject to self‐selection bias, as RDs who are more interested or confident in engaging fathers may have been more likely to participate. Fourth, because our study materials were only available in English, RDs who exclusively speak French were not represented. Together with the small size consisted of a majority of RDs being female (96.9%), white (91.2%), and only two RDs (1.6%) practicing in the Northern Territories, this may limit the generalizability of our findings. Despite employing multiple recruitment strategies, small samples are not uncommon in studies involving Canadian RDs [57, 58, 59].

Conclusion

Overall, this study highlighted that RDs reported high confidence in various aspects of father engagement, but few received formal training in this area. Confidence was notably lower for retaining fathers across ongoing visits, and fathers' presence and availability were identified as key factors needed for father engagement. Opportunities exist to strengthen practice through webinars, guidelines, and self‐assessment tools that support RDs in delivering more equitable, family‐centered pediatric nutrition care and services, including recognizing fathers as equal caregivers and adopting father engagement strategies that build fathers' competence and sustained involvement. Future research should examine actual practice behaviors and incorporate fathers' perspectives when working with RDs.
