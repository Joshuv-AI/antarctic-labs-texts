Golgi-derived COPI vesicles fuel the biogenesis of coronavirus replication organelles
Authors: Zhaohuan Wang, Xinyue Su, Kaoyi Gong, Zixiao Yang, Shu-Rui Liu, Bi-Rong Zheng, Keda Shi, Yu Ye, Xinrong Zhou, Jian Pan, Deyin Guo, Panpan Hou, Chun-Mei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPositive-strand RNA viruses remodel host endomembranes to form replication organelles (ROs). However, the membrane sources for coronavirus double-membrane vesicle (DMV) biogenesis remain unclear. Here, we used proteomic, genetic, pharmacological, and imaging approaches to identify the central membrane supply route for DMV formation. APEX2-based proximity proteomics first identified candidate pathways, followed by functional validation using siRNA knockdown, CRISPR/Cas9 editing, and Golgicide A (GCA) inhibition. DMV formation and viral RNA synthesis were assessed by electron microscopy, immunofluorescence, and qRT-PCR. Golgi–DMV association was analyzed by sucrose gradient fractionation and confocal imaging. In vivo relevance was evaluated in C57BL/6J mice infected with mouse hepatitis virus (MHV) and treated with GCA (intraperitoneal, 50 mg/kg; n = 4–5 per group). We showed that coronaviruses hijack the host GBF1-ARF1-COPI machinery to redirect Golgi-derived membranes to viral replication sites for DMV formation and expansion. Disruption of this pathway markedly impairs viral RNA synthesis, DMV biogenesis, and replication of multiple coronaviruses, including severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). Pharmacological inhibition of Golgi-specific Brefeldin A-resistance factor 1 (GBF1) suppressed viral replication and pathology in vivo. Together, our findings reveal a previously unrecognized Golgi-derived membrane supply route for coronavirus ROs and highlight the GBF1-ARF1-COPI pathway as a potential host-directed target for broad-spectrum antiviral therapies.Keywords: replication organelles (ROs), double-membrane vesicles (DMVs), GBF1-ARF1-COPI, coatomer protein I (COPI) vesicle, coronavirus

Introduction

Positive-strand RNA viruses extensively remodel the cellular endomembrane system to form specialized viral replication organelles (ROs) [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Coronaviruses generate double-membrane vesicles (DMVs) that function as platforms for viral RNA (vRNA) synthesis and protect vRNA from detection by the host innate immune system [5,6]. Coronaviral nonstructural proteins (nsp) nsp3 and nsp4 form the core structural scaffold of DMVs, while nsp6 facilitates their subsequent growth and expansion [[7], [8], [9]]. Despite the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) being widely considered the primary membrane source for coronavirus DMVs, increasing evidence suggests that viral replication organelles may draw membranes from multiple cellular compartments [5]. However, whether Golgi-derived trafficking systems actively contribute to DMV formation and how these pathways are mechanistically hijacked by coronaviruses remain unclear.

Viruses universally exploit and subvert host intracellular membrane systems and cellular machinery to facilitate their replication [10,11], as exemplified by the hijacking of autophagy [12], the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) [13], and lipid droplets [14]. A central regulator of Golgi membrane dynamics is the coatomer protein I (COPI) vesicular transport machinery. Composed of a multi-subunit coatomer complex and the small GTPase ADP-ribosylation factor 1 (ARF1), COPI vesicles mediate retrograde transport from the Golgi apparatus to the ER as well as intra-Golgi trafficking [15]. ARF1, a highly conserved eukaryotic class I ARF protein, is primarily activated at the Golgi membrane by the guanine nucleotide exchange factor Golgi-specific Brefeldin A-resistance factor 1 (GBF1) [16]. Thus, the GBF1-ARF1-COPI axis serves as a master regulator of Golgi structure and secretory trafficking. Beyond its canonical role in vesicle formation, emerging evidence underscores the involvement of this axis in diverse non-canonical cellular processes, including cytokinesis, autophagy, and lipid droplet homeostasis, all of which are frequently manipulated by viruses [15]. The GBF1-ARF1-COPI pathway, originally characterized for its role in Golgi-ER retrograde transport, has emerged as a broadly exploited host dependency factor for many positive-sense RNA viruses, including enteroviruses and flaviviruses [[17], [18], [19]]. Notably, previous functional genomic screens have identified several host factors in the early secretory pathway, especially GBF1 and its effector ARF1, as critical for coronavirus replication [19,20]. Nevertheless, fundamental questions remain unresolved. It remains unclear whether coronaviruses hijack individual components or the entire GBF1-ARF1-COPI trafficking machinery, and how this pathway is functionally linked to key steps of the viral life cycle, such as viral RNA synthesis and the biogenesis of DMVs that support coronavirus replication. Addressing these questions is critical for understanding virus‒host interactions at the membrane level and for identifying potential host-directed antiviral targets.

In this study, to comprehensively map the host–virus interactome at coronaviral DMVs, we employed an engineered ascorbate peroxidase 2 (APEX2)-mediated proximity labeling strategy targeted to nsp3. This approach identified the Golgi-resident GBF1 as a key host interactor of nsp3. We further demonstrate that coronaviruses hijack the conserved host GBF1-ARF1-COPI transport machinery to redirect Golgi-derived membranes for DMV assembly. Through a combination of chemical inhibition, genetic knockdown, and high-resolution imaging, we show that disruption of this pathway severely impairs viral RNA synthesis, reduces DMV size and abundance, and diminishes Golgi-DMV membrane colocalization. Importantly, disruption of the GBF1-ARF1-COPI complex drastically reduced viral replication and virion production in several coronaviruses, including severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). Therapeutically, pharmacological inhibition of GBF1 with the small-molecule inhibitor Golgicide A (GCA) robustly suppressed mouse hepatitis coronavirus (MHV) replication in vivo, significantly reducing both viral loads and pathology in infected mice. Collectively, our findings establish that coronaviruses co-opt COPI vesicles to exploit the Golgi apparatus as a critical membrane source for DMV biogenesis. These results provide fundamental insights into the viral reprogramming of host membrane systems and identify the host GBF1-ARF1-COPI as a promising target for the development of broad-spectrum anti-coronaviral therapeutics.

Results

The GBF1-ARF1-COPI Pathway is Identified for Coronavirus DMV Formation

Given that nsp3 and nsp4 constitute the minimal viral components required for DMV formation [7,8,21], and the N-terminal domain of nsp3 is predominantly exposed to the cytoplasm, these domains likely facilitate extensive interactions with host proteins. The APEX2 peroxidase catalyzes the generation of reactive biotin-phenoxyl radicals in the presence of H2O2 and biotin-phenol, which covalently label electron-rich amino acid residues in neighboring proteins, a strategy successfully applied to map interactomes in eukaryotic cells [22,23]. To exploit this property for DMV study, we constructed a pcDNA3.1-GFP-APEX2-nsp3-nsp4-HA fusion expression plasmid that efficiently induces DMV formation and employed it for proximity-dependent proteomic profiling (Figure S1A–S1C). Subsequently, HEK293T cells were transfected with the APEX2-nsp3-nsp4 plasmid. Biotinylation was conducted at 30 h post transfection, and biotinylated proteins were isolated using streptavidin-conjugated beads. Cells treated with H2O2 alone or biotin-phenol alone served as controls. Purified biotinylated proteins were processed for mass spectrometry (MS)-based identification and quantification. MS analysis identified 263 proteins that were significantly enriched compared to controls (Figure 1A). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis indicated that the identified proteins were predominantly involved in vesicle-mediated transport and organelle organization processes, especially those related to the ER and Golgi apparatus (Figure 1B). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays further showed that, in HEK293T cells co-expressing nsp3 and nsp4, nsp3 interacted with calnexin (an ER marker), COPA (a COPI vesicle marker), and SEC31A (a COPII vesicle marker), supporting the reliability of our MS-based identification (Figure S1D). This result suggests a potential link between host vesicular trafficking machinery and coronavirus DMV formation.

Among the candidate proteins, the GBF1-ARF1-COPI pathway drew our attention due to its strong enrichment in these biological processes (Figure S1E) and the identification of multiple COPI vesicle-associated proteins in our MS dataset (Figure S1F). The COPI complex assembles at the ER-Golgi interface under the direction of ARF1 and its guanine nucleotide exchange factor GBF1. Activated ARF1-GTP subsequently recruits the COPI vesicle coat, which mediates retrograde vesicular transport between the Golgi apparatus and the ER, as well as intra-Golgi trafficking [24,25]. Co-IP assays confirmed an interaction between endogenous GBF1 and nsp3 in cells co-expressing nsp3 and nsp4 (Figure 1C). Correlative imaging in cells co-expressing nsp3, nsp4, and Flag-GBF1 further demonstrated colocalization of overexpressed Flag-GBF1 with nsp3-labeled foci (Figure 1D). To verify the association of DMV and COPI machinery in viral infection conditions, we used a recombinant mouse hepatitis coronavirus (MHV-A59 strain expressing GFP-tagged nsp3) [7]. Following infection with MHV-GFP-nsp3 (a recombinant MHV-A59 expressing GFP-tagged nsp3), we observed that viral nsp3 interacted with endogenous GBF1, ARF1, and COPA (Figure 1E). Moreover, infection triggered the redistribution of these host factors, resulting in their perinuclear accumulation and colocalization with nsp3-marked DMVs (Figure 1F–1H). Together, these data suggest a potential involvement of the GBF1-ARF1-COPI pathway in coronavirus DMV biogenesis.

GBF1 Serves as an Essential Host Factor for Coronavirus Replication

To determine the role of individual COPI vesicle components in coronavirus replication, we transfected 17Cl-1 cells with control or Gbf1-targeting small interfering RNA (siRNA). Gbf1 knockdown significantly reduced MHV replication and progeny virion production (Figures 2A and S1G). We also designed two independent single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) (sgGbf1) to target Gbf1 in 17Cl-1 cells (Figure 2B). Infection of these cells with recombinant MHV-GFPΔNS5a [26] demonstrated impaired viral replication, as quantified by reduced GFP fluorescence intensity (Figure 2C). We next assessed highly pathogenic SARS-CoV-2 infection using GBF1-depleted Caco-2-N cells, a line stably expressing the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein that supports the full viral life cycle when infected with recombinant SARS-CoV-2 virus-like particles (SARS-CoV-2 GFP/ΔN trVLPs) [27]. In these cells, GBF1 knockdown reduced both SARS-CoV-2 replication and progeny virus production (Figure 2D). To determine whether GBF1 directly affects viral replication independently of entry, we employed a SARS-CoV-2 replicon system carrying a firefly luciferase reporter in HEK293T cells. Knockdown of GBF1 significantly reduced replicon luciferase activity (Figure 2E), indicating that GBF1 is required for the replication phase of the SARS-CoV-2 life cycle, independent of viral entry. Similarly, GBF1 knockdown in Huh7.5.1 cells markedly inhibited human coronavirus 229E (HCoV-229E) replication (Figure 2F). Additionally, we evaluated the effect of the specific GBF1 inhibitor GCA on coronavirus replication. GCA potently suppressed the replication of MHV, SARS-CoV-2, and HCoV-229E, with half-maximal effective concentrations (EC50) of 2.554 μM, 1.479 μM, and 1.531 μM, respectively, without inducing obvious cytotoxicity (Figure 2G–2H). Together, these results establish GBF1 as a critical host factor supporting the replication of diverse coronaviruses.

The GBF1-ARF1-COPI Pathway is Essential for Coronavirus RNA Synthesis

Given the involvement of nsp3 in double-membrane vesicle formation, which is essential for coronavirus RNA synthesis, and its interaction with the GBF1-ARF1-COPI pathway, we propose that this pathway is critical for coronavirus DMV formation and RNA synthesis. First, to exclude the effects of viral entry, we transfected HeLa cells with the GFP-nsp3-nsp4-HA plasmid and examined the impact of GBF1 or COPA knockdown on DMV formation. Immunofluorescence results showed that knockdown of either GBF1 (Figures 3A and S1H) or COPA (Figures 3B and S1I) significantly reduced the number of intracellular DMV puncta under conditions of nsp3 and nsp4 co-expression. Additionally, we found that the double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) levels following MHV infection were markedly reduced in 17Cl-1 cells expressing Gbf1-targeting sgRNA, as evidenced by viral dsRNA staining and imaging (Figure 3C). This result was further confirmed in cells treated with the GBF1 inhibitor GCA (Figure 3D). To investigate whether other components of COPI-vesicle trafficking contribute to coronavirus RNA synthesis, we disrupted this pathway by siRNA-mediated knockdown of ARF1 or COPA in 17Cl-1, Huh7.5.1, and Caco-2-N cells, respectively. Either ARF1 or COPA depletion significantly suppressed early viral RNA (vRNA) synthesis of MHV in 17Cl-1 (Figure 3E–3F), SARS-CoV-2 in Caco-2-N cells (Figure 3G–3H), and HCoV-229E in Huh7.5.1 (Figure 3I–3J), indicating that the requirement for intact COPI machinery is conserved across diverse coronaviruses. This consistent phenotype across multiple viruses and two distinct nodes of the pathway underscores that the entire GBF1-ARF1-COPI pathway is a critical host dependency factor for coronavirus replication.

The GBF1-ARF1-COPI Pathway is Required for Golgi-derived Membrane Supply to DMVs

Given these findings and the known function of the GBF1-ARF1-COPI axis in vesicle transport and lipid exchange, we hypothesized that this pathway may play a critical role in coronaviral DMV formation. To test this, we used transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to examine DMV biogenesis in sgGbf1 cells, which were generated in Figure 2B. While abundant typical DMVs were present in infected control cells, DMV formation was strongly impaired in sgGbf1 cells (Figure 4A). Statistical analysis of DMV sizes from randomly selected fields also revealed that DMVs in knockdown cells were significantly smaller than those in controls (Figure 4A). To examine another COPI component, we knocked down Copa in 17Cl-1 cells and performed TEM analysis of MHV DMVs. Consistent with the results above, Copa deficiency substantially impaired DMV biogenesis and reduced DMV size (Figure 4B). We then used GBF1 inhibitor GCA to block this pathway and found that GCA treatment reduced both the number and diameter of MHV DMVs compared to DMSO-treated controls (Figure 4C). To test the broad anti-coronaviral activity, we also evaluated the effect of GCA on DMV formation and morphology in cells infected with SARS-CoV-2 or HCoV-229E. Similarly, GCA-treated cells showed defective DMV biogenesis and altered DMV morphology relative to DMSO-treated controls (Figure 4D–4E). To exclude effects of viral entry and assess the role of GBF1 or COPA in DMV formation, HeLa cells with knockdown of GBF1 or COPA were transfected with the GFP-nsp3-nsp4-HA plasmid. DMV formation was examined by TEM. The results showed that under conditions of nsp3 and nsp4 co-expression, knockdown of either GBF1 (Figure S2A) or COPA (Figure S2B) significantly reduced both the number and the size of DMVs. Together, these results demonstrate that the GBF1-ARF1-COPI pathway is indispensable for coronavirus DMV formation.

Based on these findings, we propose that coronaviruses exploit the GBF1-ARF1-COPI pathway to redirect Golgi-derived membranes for DMV formation, thereby providing essential membrane scaffolds for viral RNA replication. To test this hypothesis, we performed sucrose density gradient fractionation. Briefly, HeLa-mCEACAM1 cells were either mock-infected or infected with MHV-GFP-nsp3 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1. At 12 h post infection, cells were collected, lysed, and the post-nuclear supernatant was loaded onto a discontinuous 20%–60% sucrose gradient. Following centrifugation at 100,000 × g for 3 h at 4 °C, fractions were collected and analyzed by immunoblotting. The results confirmed the redistribution of Golgi membranes and the COPI vesicle-associated proteins GM130, TGN46, and COPA (Figure 5A). Quantitative analysis from three independent replicates demonstrated that, upon viral infection, GM130, TGN46, and COPA shifted toward DMV-containing density fractions, with a pattern similar to that of ancient ubiquitous protein 1 (AUP1), an ER- and lipid droplet (LD)-shuttling protein previously shown to relocalize to DMV fractions in density gradient centrifugation. The quantification of fraction 24 (enriched in DMVs) is presented in Figure 5A, while the protein levels across all 24 fractions are shown in Figure S3A. To further trace the fate of Golgi-derived COPI vesicles, we monitored Golgi membrane dynamics during MHV infection. First, using a Golgi-Tracker Red fluorescent probe, we observed that from 4 to 12 h post infection, the increase in DMVs correlated with progressively enhanced perinuclear colocalization between Golgi membranes and DMVs (Figure S3B). Second, we employed mCherry-tagged VAMP-associated protein A (VAP-A), a well-established ER-localized marker [28], as a positive control. Under the same infection conditions, the perinuclear colocalization between GM130 (a Golgi marker) and DMVs similarly increased over time, following a trend comparable to that of VAP-A (Figure 5B). Together, these results indicate that Golgi membranes are actively recruited to DMV formation sites and contribute to DMV biogenesis.

To further determine whether coronaviruses utilize the GBF1-ARF1-COPI pathway to redirect Golgi membranes toward DMV formation, we treated MHV-infected cells with the GBF1 inhibitor GCA. GCA treatment substantially reduced the colocalization of GM130 with DMVs (Figure 5C). Similarly, COPA knockdown also significantly suppressed MHV-induced Golgi remodeling and diminished DMV-Golgi association (Figure 5D). These results indicate that disruption of the GBF1-ARF1-COPI pathway impairs the recruitment of Golgi membranes for DMV biogenesis, leading to reduced DMV size and abundance. Taken together, these data demonstrate that coronaviruses require the GBF1-ARF1-COPI pathway to redirect Golgi-derived membranes to DMV formation sites, and that this membrane supply is essential for DMV growth and expansion.

Targeting GBF1-ARF1-COPI Pathway Alleviates Coronaviral Pathogenesis

To evaluate the physiological role of the GBF1-ARF1-COPI pathway in coronavirus infection and replication, we assessed the antiviral efficacy of GBF1 inhibition in vivo. C57BL/6J mice were pretreated via intraperitoneal injection with GCA or the vehicle control one day before infection with MHV, which was administered either intranasally or intrahepatically. Daily administration of the drug was continued thereafter (Figure 6A). In the intranasal infection model, MHV genomic RNA (gRNA) and subgenomic RNA levels were quantified in lung homogenates; in the intrahepatic model, viral RNA loads were measured in liver tissues. Vehicle-treated groups showed high levels of MHV gRNA and subgenomic RNA in lungs (intranasal model) and liver (intrahepatic model), confirming robust infection in both models compared to mock-infected controls. In contrast, GCA treatment significantly reduced both gRNA and subgenomic RNA levels of MHV relative to vehicle-treated littermates, demonstrating in vivo antiviral activity through GBF1 inhibition; GCA treatment also lowered MHV viral titers in lung and liver tissues compared to the vehicle control (Figure 6B–6C). These results were further corroborated by immunoblot detection of the viral nucleocapsid (N) protein (Figure 6D–6E). The suppression of viral replication consequently alleviated tissue pathology (as shown by hematoxylin and eosin [HE] staining, Figure 6F–6H) and attenuated the virus-induced type I interferon response, as well as reduced inflammation (Figure 6I–6J). Together, these data establish that the GBF1 inhibitor GCA exerts potent anti-coronavirus activity both in vitro and in vivo, underscoring the GBF1-ARF1-COPI pathway as a promising host-directed therapeutic target against diverse coronaviruses.

Discussion

Positive-strand RNA viruses extensively remodel the host endomembrane system to form specialized ROs, which establish a microenvironment conducive to viral RNA synthesis and shield dsRNA intermediates from innate immune recognition [29]. Coronaviruses, including SARS-CoV-2, HCoV-229E, and MHV, generate DMVs to support viral replication. In cells infected with coronaviruses, DMVs are frequently observed to be continuous with the ER, supporting that the ER serves as a primary membrane source for ROs [6]. Studies on other viruses that also form ROs, such as poliovirus [30], coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3) [31], and flock house virus [3], suggest that besides the ER, organelles like the Golgi apparatus and mitochondria may also contribute membrane lipids to viral RO biogenesis [32]. However, direct experimental evidence supporting the hypothesis that the Golgi apparatus supplies membrane for coronavirus DMV biogenesis remains limited. In this study, we identify the host GBF1-ARF1-COPI secretory pathway as a key membrane supply route for coronavirus replication organelles. Disruption of this pathway markedly reduced viral RNA synthesis, impaired DMV formation, and diminished the spatial association between Golgi membranes and DMVs. These findings indicate that Golgi-derived membranes, delivered via the COPI trafficking pathway, contribute to DMV expansion. Our results therefore expand the prevailing ER-centric model of coronavirus replication organelle biogenesis and support a multi-organelle membrane supply mechanism in which Golgi-derived trafficking pathways participate in the formation of viral replication compartments.

Consistent with this, our GO analysis also showed enrichment of ER-related proteins among the top pathways, which is expected given the established role of the ER as a primary membrane source for DMVs. Nevertheless, our experimental evidence—including the entry-independent nsp3-nsp4 expression system showing that GBF1/COPA knockdown reduces DMV number, and the specific redistribution of Golgi markers to DMV-containing fractions in sucrose gradients—supports a model in which this pathway contributes at least partially through direct membrane supply. Our data collectively demonstrate that this pathway is an essential host dependency factor for coronavirus replication. Upon disruption of the GBF1-ARF1-COPI pathway, DMV biogenesis is a relatively early affected step. Knockdown of GBF1 or COPA directly reduces DMV number and size in cells co-expressing nsp3 and nsp4 (Figures 3 and S2A–S2B), demonstrating a direct requirement for DMV formation. The subsequent impairment of viral RNA synthesis and progeny virion production (Figures 2–3) can be attributed to defective DMVs, as they are the essential platform for viral RNA replication. Therefore, whether this pathway also affects viral RNP assembly or particle release requires further investigation, and our data support that DMV biogenesis is a relatively major and early affected process.

It should be noted that, while our data demonstrate that the GBF1-ARF1-COPI pathway is critical for DMV formation, we cannot fully exclude the possibility that this pathway indirectly influences DMV biogenesis through other organelles. For instance, the GBF1-ARF1-COPI axis regulates lipid homeostasis and membrane flux between the Golgi and the ER [33], and the ER is the primary membrane source for DMVs [1]. Thus, disruption of this pathway could alter ER lipid composition or membrane curvature, indirectly affecting DMV budding. Moreover, GBF1 and ARF1 are also involved in autophagy and lipid droplet homeostasis [15]. Given that coronavirus nsp6 hijacks the ER-associated protein degradation (ERAD) machinery to promote lipolysis and supply membrane components from lipid droplets [7], it is conceivable that perturbation of the GBF1-ARF1-COPI pathway could indirectly modulate DMV formation by affecting lipid droplet metabolism or autophagic flux. These possibilities represent limitations of the current study and warrant further investigation using more refined genetic tools.

Although multiple host factors have been implicated in the formation of DMVs [34], their roles vary in mechanism and context. For example, mammalian lethal with sec-13 protein 8 (mLST8) modulates DMV biogenesis through the mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling pathway [35], transmembrane protein 41B (TMEM41B) participates in ER membrane remodeling [36], the ER-shaping proteins reticulon-3 (RTN3) and RTN4 help regulate membrane curvature [37], and fragile X-related (FXR) family proteins (FXR1/FXR2/FMR1) promote SARS-CoV-2 DMV clustering via liquid–liquid phase separation to enhance viral RNA translation [38]. Transmembrane protein 198 (TMEM198) directly interacts with the C-terminal domains of nsp3 and nsp4 to facilitate DMV formation during infection by transmissible gastroenteritis virus (TGEV) and MHV [39]. Several studies have suggested that Golgi remodeling is critical for SARS-CoV-2 assembly and secretion [40], and COPI vesicles support classical swine fever virus proliferation by transporting fatty acid synthase [41]. Central to this membrane trafficking system are the ARF GTPases. ARF1 interacts with the viral M protein to promote its accumulation at the ERGIC, thereby enhancing virion assembly [42]. Similarly, its family member ARF4 facilitates the directed transport and release of viral particles in multiple RNA viruses, including Zika virus, influenza virus, and SARS-CoV-2; inhibition of ARF4 redirects virions to the lysosomal degradation pathway [43]. Building upon previous studies highlighting the involvement of individual host factors in viral replication, our findings reveal a higher-order mechanism of host exploitation: coronaviruses systematically hijack the entire GBF1-ARF1-COPI vesicular transport machinery to construct their replication organelles. Although Verheije et al. previously reported the requirement of GBF1 for MHV RNA replication [19], our Figure 6 provides in vivo validation of pharmacological GBF1 inhibition and offers mechanistic evidence linking this pathway to DMV membrane supply, thereby extending prior findings. This work not only elucidates a coordinated membrane-remodeling pathway essential for DMV biogenesis but also establishes a paradigm for how viruses repurpose whole cellular trafficking systems to support their replication. Moreover, pharmacological inhibition of GBF1 with GCA significantly reduced viral loads and pathology in MHV-infected mice, providing direct in vivo validation. These findings underscore that coronavirus replication is critically dependent on the GBF1-ARF1-COPI pathway. By uncovering the central role of conserved host machinery in infection across diverse coronaviruses, including MHV, HCoV-229E, and SARS-CoV-2, we identify the GBF1-ARF1-COPI axis as a promising broad-spectrum host-directed target for antiviral intervention.

In summary, we demonstrate that coronaviruses hijack the host GBF1-ARF1-COPI trafficking machinery to redirect Golgi-derived membrane for DMV biogenesis. Mechanistically, viral nsp3 recruits GBF1 to activate ARF1-dependent COPI vesicle formation, thereby rerouting Golgi trafficking toward viral replication sites. This strategy enables coronaviruses to efficiently assemble replication organelles using pre-existing host membranes. Our findings expand the prevailing ER-centric model of coronavirus replication organelles by revealing a Golgi-derived membrane supply pathway, providing new insight into how viruses reprogram host membrane trafficking and highlighting the GBF1-ARF1-COPI axis as a potential target for host-directed pan-coronavirus antiviral strategies.

Materials and methods

Animal Experiment and Ethics

For in vivo infection studies, 6–8 w female C57BL/6J mice (total n = 27) were used. Mice were obtained from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China) and were specific pathogen-free, with no genetic modifications or previous procedures. The mice were housed in a standard environment with a 12/12-h light/dark cycle, temperatures ranging from 20 to 24 °C, and continuous access to a standard diet and water. The experimental unit was a single mouse in all in vivo experiments. Group sizes were based on our previous experience with mouse models infected with MHV-A59 via intranasal or intrahepatic injection and published studies, and were considered sufficient to detect biologically meaningful differences while minimizing animal use. For the intranasal infection model, the Mock, Vehicle, and GCA groups each contained 5 mice, for a total of 15 mice. For the intrahepatic injection model, the Mock, Vehicle, and GCA groups each contained 4 mice, for a total of 12 mice. Mice were randomly assigned to groups using a computer-generated random number sequence (https://www.graphpad.com/quickcalcs/randomize1/). Mice were monitored daily, and predefined humane endpoints (e.g., > 20% body-weight loss, severe respiratory distress, or moribund state) were used as exclusion criteria. All animals that completed the experimental protocol were included in the final analysis, and no animals were excluded. Investigators performing histological scoring and data analysis were blinded to group allocation. Potential confounders, including the order of treatments and measurements, as well as animal/cage location, were not controlled in this study. Mice were infected either intranasally with MHV-A59 (2 × 105 plaque-forming unit [PFU]) to establish pulmonary infection or via direct intrahepatic injection with MHV-A59 (1 × 104 PFU) to model liver-specific infection. GCA (MCE, New Jersey, USA; Cat#HY-100540) was first dissolved in 5% DMSO and then diluted in normal saline containing 30% PEG300 and 20% HP-β-CD (2-hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin) to prepare the working solution. The compound was administered daily via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection at a dose of 50 mg/kg. For euthanasia, mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane, followed by cervical dislocation to minimize pain and distress. Blood was collected via cardiac puncture, and lung and liver tissues were carefully dissected. The tissues were either fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological analysis or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent molecular analyses.

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines and the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publications No. 8023, revised 1978). The animal use protocol has been reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), Sun Yat-sen University (approval number SYSU-IACUC-MED-2023-B009).

Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay

Luciferase assays were performed according to the manufacturer's protocol (Promega Corporation, Wisconsin, USA; Cat#E1910). Briefly, HEK293T cells seeded in 12-well plates were first transfected with the indicated siRNAs (targeting GBF1 or control siRNA). All siRNA constructs were designed and synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China); the siRNA sequences are provided in Table S1. After 24 h, the cells were co-transfected with 500 ng of pBAC-nCoV-Replicon-Luciferase plasmid and 20 ng of pRL-TK (Renilla luciferase) plasmid. At 30 h post transfection, cells were lysed in 200 μL of 1× Passive Lysis Buffer. Then, 10 μL of each lysate was transferred to a 96-well white plate and mixed sequentially with 20 μL of Luciferase Assay Reagent II and 20 μL of Stop & Glo solution. Firefly and Renilla luminescence were measured using the luminescence detector of a Synergy H1 Hybrid Multi-Mode Reader (BioTek, Vermont, USA).

APEX2 Proteomics and MS Analysis

HEK293T cells were transfected with the pcDNA3.1-GFP-APEX2-nsp3-nsp4-HA plasmids. At 30 min before the indicated times post transfection, the incubation medium was replaced with DMEM containing 500 μM biotin-4-aminophenol (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; Cat#SH1948) or DMSO, followed by incubation for 30 min. The cells were then treated with PBS containing 1 mM H2O2 (Millipore Sigma, Massachusetts, USA; Cat#H1009) for 1 min at 37 °C to initiate the biotinylation reaction. After 1 min, the reaction was quenched by washing the cells three times with a quenching solution (phosphate-buffered saline [PBS], 10 mM sodium azide, 5 mM Trolox, and 10 mM ascorbate). The cells were lysed in RIPA buffer with a protease inhibitor cocktail, followed by sonication. Protein lysate from each condition was saved for confirmatory immunoblotting. The remaining cell lysates were combined with the streptavidin magnetic beads and incubated on a rotator for 2 h at room temperature. The beads were collected and washed three times using a magnetic rack.

Following immunoprecipitation, protein samples were analyzed by mass spectrometry at the Proteomics and Metabolomics Core Facility of Guangzhou National Laboratory. Briefly, peptide samples were separated on a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) and analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) using an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) operating in data-dependent acquisition mode. GO enrichment analysis was performed using Metascape against the Gene Ontology (biological process [BP], cellular component [CC], molecular function [MF]) and KEGG databases [44]. Differentially expressed proteins were identified using an adjusted P value (FDR) < 0.05 and an absolute fold change > 1.5. Significantly enriched terms were selected as the top 10 terms with FDR < 0.05 and the highest enrichment scores.

Plasmid Construction

The plasmid encoding GFP-nsp3 was generously gifted by Professor Hong Zhang (Institute of Biophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China). The HA-tagged nsp4 plasmid was a generous gift from Professor Xian-Cai Ma (Guangzhou National Laboratory, China). The complementary DNA (cDNA) encoding Gbf1 (NM_178930.3) was cloned into the pCE-puro-3×Flag vector. The codon-optimized nsp3-nsp4-HA sequence was cloned into the pCDNA3.1-GFP vector using overlap-extension polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to construct the pCDNA3.1-GFP-nsp3-nsp4-HA plasmid. The expression of nsp3 and nsp4 from this plasmid, along with the induction of DMVs, has been confirmed in previous studies [45]. SARS-CoV-2 nsp3 and nsp4 are minimal constituents of a pore-spanning replication organelle. The APEX2 sequence (Addgene, Massachusetts, USA; Cat#79055) was cloned into the pCDNA3.1-GFP-nsp3-nsp4-HA using overlap-extension PCR to construct the pCDNA3.1-GFP-APEX2-nsp3-nsp4-HA plasmid. The cDNA encoding VAP-A (NM_194434.3) was cloned into the pcDNA3.1-mCherry vector.

Cell Culture and Transfection

HEK293T (ATCC, Virginia, USA; Cat#ATCC-CRL-3216), HeLa (ATCC, Virginia, USA; Cat#ATCC-CRM-CCL-2), and Huh7.5.1 (generously gifted by Yi-Ping Li, Sun Yat-sen University, China) were used. Caco-2-N, rat L2, HeLa-mCEACAM1, and murine 17Cl-1 cells were used as previously described [7]. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, Corning, New York, USA; Cat#10-013-CV) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Massachusetts, USA; Cat#10099141C) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Plasmid DNA transfection was performed using ExFect transfection reagent (Vazyme Biotech, Jiangsu, China; Cat#T101-02) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated Gene Editing

Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) technology was employed to generate sgGbf1 17Cl-1 cells. The specific targeting sequences for Mus musculus Gbf1 were AATGGACGAAGGAATACATT and TGACTTCCAGAAATGGACGA. The design and construction of the sgRNAs were described in detail previously [46]. In brief, the lentiviruses carrying lentiCRISPR-Gbf1 were produced in HEK293T cells. The cell supernatants containing lentivirus were collected and passed through a 0.45 μm filter. 17Cl-1 cells were transduced with lentiviruses for 4 h. At 48 h post infection, sgGbf1 17Cl-1 cells were selected in DMEM containing puromycin.

Sucrose Gradient Fractionation

The procedure was carried out as previously described [47]. Approximately 4 × 107 HeLa-mCEACAM1 cells were infected with MHV-GFP-nsp3 at an MOI of 1, with uninfected cells serving as controls. At 12 h post infection, the cells were collected and washed three times with PBS to remove residual medium components. Subsequently, the cells were washed three times with HB buffer (250 mM sucrose, 3 mM imidazole) to equilibrate osmotic pressure and remove PBS. The cells were then centrifuged at 900 × g for 5 min at 4 °C and resuspended in 1 mL of HB buffer containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail, followed by incubation on ice for 10 min to inhibit proteolysis. Cell disruption was achieved using a 22-gauge needle and syringe by performing 24 up-and-down strokes. Unlysed cells and nuclei were removed by centrifugation at 510 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, yielding the post-nuclear supernatant (PNS). The PNS was diluted 3-fold with HB buffer containing the protease inhibitor cocktail and then loaded onto a discontinuous sucrose gradient consisting of 2 mL layers of 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, and 60% sucrose. Samples were centrifuged in a SW-41 rotor (Beckman Coulter, California, USA) at 100,000 × g for 3 h at 4 °C to separate fractions based on density. The gradients were carefully collected, and proteins of interest were identified by Western blot using specific primary antibodies. Proteins were isolated from the appropriate layers, diluted with 2× SDS loading buffer, and subjected to immunoblot analysis to detect the presence and distribution of the proteins of interest.

siRNA Transfection and qRT-PCR

siRNA transfections were performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Invitrogen, California, USA; Cat#13778150) according to the manufacturer's instructions. One day before transfection, cells were seeded into 24-well plates (or 12-well plates) to reach 60%–80% confluence at the time of transfection. For transfection, siRNA was diluted in Opti-MEM reduced serum medium (Gibco, Massachusetts, USA; Cat#31985070) to a final concentration of 10 nM (e.g., 5 pmol per well for a 24-well plate), and Lipofectamine RNAiMAX was separately diluted in Opti-MEM (1.5 μL per well for a 24-well plate). The diluted siRNA and diluted transfection reagent were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and incubated for 5 min at room temperature to form RNAi-lipid complexes. The complexes were then added to the cells and gently mixed. Cells were incubated for 24–72 h before downstream assays. All siRNA constructs were designed and synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China); the siRNA sequences are provided in Table S1. Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol Reagent (CWBIO, Jiangsu, China; Cat#CW0580S) following the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA. GAPDH was used as an endogenous control. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed under the following cycling protocol: 94 °C for 3 min (1 cycle), followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. The primers for qPCR are listed in Table S2.

Western Blot Analysis

Cells were lysed in a buffer composed of 50 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 2 mM EDTA, and a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Baden-Württemberg, Germany; Cat#4693132001). The lysates were sonicated, mixed with a loading buffer containing 2% SDS, and subsequently separated by SDS-PAGE without boiling. The proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Massachusetts, USA) and blocked with 5% nonfat milk. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies, followed by secondary antibodies. The membranes were incubated with Immobilon Western chemiluminescent HRP substrate (EMD Millipore, Massachusetts, USA; Cat#34580), and protein signals were visualized. A complete list of antibodies is provided in Table S3. The band intensities were quantified by ImageJ software (v1.47; National Institutes of Health, Maryland, USA). For relative protein level quantification, Western blot images were opened in ImageJ and converted to 8-bit grayscale. The rectangular selection tool was used to outline each target protein band, and the same selection was applied to the corresponding loading control band (e.g., GAPDH). The integrated density of each band was recorded using the “Measure” function. Background subtraction was performed by measuring the integrated density of an empty area adjacent to each band and subtracting this value from the band density. The normalized protein level was calculated as the ratio of the integrated density of the target protein band to that of the loading control band. The relative expression level was then obtained by dividing the normalized value of each experimental sample by that of the control sample. For the sucrose density gradient fractionation experiments, the relative protein level in each fraction was calculated as the ratio of the integrated density of the target protein band in that fraction to the sum of the integrated densities of the target protein bands across all 24 fractions.

CCK-8 Cell Viability Assay

Seed cells (3 × 104) in 96-well plates and incubate overnight at 37 °C under 5% CO2 to allow cell attachment. Treat cells with desired concentrations of test compounds or controls. Include blank wells (medium without cells) for background subtraction. Incubate for the required treatment duration. Add 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent directly to each well containing 100 μL medium. Incubate the plate at 37 °C for 1 h, protected from light; absorbance readings at 450 nm and 590 nm were taken to evaluate cytotoxicity (Multiskan; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). All reagents and chemicals used in this study are listed in Table S4.

Antiviral Activity Evaluation of Compounds

17Cl-1, Caco-2-N, and Huh7.5.1 cells were seeded (5 × 104) in 48-well plates and cultured for 24 h to reach approximately 80% confluence. 17Cl-1 cells were infected with MHV-A59, Caco-2-N cells were infected with SARS-CoV-2 (both at 37 °C), and Huh7.5.1 cells were infected with HCoV-229E (at 33 °C), each for 2 h. Compounds or DMSO (as vehicle control) were added at concentrations ranging from 0.1 μM to 10 μM. Infected cells were then cultured for an additional 0.5–2 d, depending on the specific virus. Cells were harvested, and viral loads were quantified by qRT-PCR. The inhibition rate of each analog was calculated based on viral copy number, and the EC50 was determined using GraphPad Prism software (v8.0; GraphPad Software, California, USA).

Virus Infection and Propagation

MHV-A59 was propagated in 17Cl-1 cells (MOI = 0.5, 2 h adsorption, 24 h culture at 37 °C). SARS-CoV-2 GFP/ΔN trVLP was propagated in Caco-2-N cells (MOI = 1, 2 h, 48 h at 37 °C). HCoV-229E was propagated in Huh7.5.1 cells (MOI = 1, 2 h, 48 h at 33 °C). Supernatants were cleared by centrifugation and stored at −80 °C. Cells were infected at the indicated MOI for 2 h (37 °C; 33 °C for HCoV-229E), washed once with PBS, and cultured in medium containing 2% FBS. MOI was calculated as (virus titer × volume)/cell number. Specific MOIs are given in the figure legends. MHV-A59 virus was used as previously described [48]. HCoV-229E virus was kindly provided by Professor Wenjie Tan (Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, China). The SARS-CoV-2 GFP/ΔN trVLP was gifted by Professor Qiang Ding (Tsinghua University, China). All of the above viruses were handled in a biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) laboratory in accordance with relevant regulations.

Virus Titration

MHV-A59 titers were determined by plaque assay on L2 cells. Briefly, cells were infected with serially diluted MHV-A59 for 2 h at 37 °C. After removal of the inoculum, the cells were overlaid with 0.8% methylcellulose in complete medium and cultured for 48–72 h to allow plaque formation. Plaques were fixed with 0.5% crystal violet for 4 h at room temperature and then counted. HCoV-229E and SARS-CoV-2 GFP/ΔN trVLP titers were determined by fluorescent focus unit (FFU) assay. For HCoV-229E, serial dilutions were inoculated onto Huh7.5.1 cells (96-well, 33 °C, 2 h), cultured for 48 h, fixed, permeabilized, and stained with anti-nucleocapsid antibody plus fluorescent secondary antibody. For SARS-CoV-2 GFP/ΔN trVLP, serial dilutions were inoculated onto Caco-2-N cells (37 °C, 2 h), cultured for 48 h, and GFP-positive foci were directly counted. Titers are expressed as FFU/mL.

Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining

Lung or liver tissues were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4-μm thick sections. The sections were then subjected to HE staining to show lesions. All photomicrographs were automatically digitized using the Aperio CS2 slide scanner (Leica Biosystems Imaging Inc., Hesse, Germany), and image files were generated using the NDP.view2 Viewing software (U12388-01, Hamamatsu Photonics, Shizuoka, Japan). For assessing the severity of alveolitis on HE-stained sections, a 0–3 scale was used: 0, no alveolitis; 1+ (mild), thickening of the alveolar septum by a mononuclear cell infiltrate, involving focal, pleural-based lesions occupying less than 20% of the lung, with good preservation of alveolar architecture; 2+ (moderate), more widespread alveolitis involving 20%–50% of the lung, still predominantly pleural-based; 3+ (severe), diffuse alveolitis involving more than 50% of the lung, with occasional consolidation of air spaces by intra-alveolar mononuclear cells and some hemorrhagic areas.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and Co-IP

Cells were harvested and lysed in NP-40 buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 2 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 3% glycerol, and protease inhibitor cocktail tablets). The lysates were sonicated using 60% ultrasound intensity with six 10 s pulses. Subsequently, the lysates were incubated with anti-GFP agarose beads (Abclonal, Hubei, China; Cat#AE074) for 4 h at 4 °C with constant rotation. After incubation, the immunoprecipitated complexes were washed six times (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 3% glycerol, 1% NP-40) [49] to remove nonspecifically bound proteins and eluted in 2× SDS-PAGE sample buffer. Immunoprecipitated proteins were detected by Western blot.

Confocal Microscopy

HeLa or 17Cl-1 cells (6 × 104) on coverslips were subjected to transfection with plasmids or infection with MHV-GFP-nsp3. Then, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 (5 min), and blocked with PBS containing 1% BSA (1 h). The cells were then incubated with specific primary antibodies and corresponding fluorescent-dye-conjugated secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 800 fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Thuringia, Germany). Colocalization analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Live-cell Golgi Staining with Golgi-Tracker Red

The experiment was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions, briefly comprising the following steps: Golgi-Tracker Red stock solution was diluted 1:100 in Golgi-Tracker Red dilution buffer (Beyotime, Beijing, China; Cat#C1043). Culture medium was removed, and cells grown on coverslips were washed with an appropriate buffer, such as Hank's balanced salt solution (HBSS) containing Ca2+ and Mg2+. After removing the wash buffer, the prepared Golgi-Tracker Red working solution was added, and cells were incubated at 4 °C for 30 min. The Golgi-Tracker Red working solution was collected for reuse. Cells were washed approximately three times with pre-chilled culture medium and then incubated in fresh medium at 37 °C for 30 min. Cells were washed once more with fresh medium and observed under the confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 800).

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism. Results are representative of at least three independent experiments and are presented as mean ± SD, as detailed in the figure legends. Statistical significance was determined using an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test for comparisons between two groups, or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett's test for comparisons among three or more groups.
