The Implications of Private Equity Investment in Emergency Medicine
Authors: Monisha Dilip, Arthur R. Derse, James H. Paxton, Daniel R. Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Private equity (PE) investment in emergency medicine (EM) has expanded rapidly, with PE firms acquiring physician staffing groups and hospital-based services. This concept and policy review summarizes current evidence on PE involvement in EM, outlines ethical and operational implications, and identifies key gaps requiring further empirical study. The profit-driven model raises substantial ethical concerns for patient care and physician autonomy. Although these concerns are common to many for-profit models, we think that an examination of PE practices offers valuable insights into the ways in which the profit motive common to US modern health care and exacerbated by PE can conflict with the provision of high-quality patient care. PE-backed emergency departments (EDs) often implement cost-cutting measures, which may compromise care quality, increase costs, and heighten clinician moral distress. At the same time, proponents argue that PE investment can bring new capital, management expertise, and support growth strategies. The duty of PE firms to maximize investor returns inherently conflicts with the ethical obligations of emergency physicians to prioritize beneficence and nonmaleficence. The rise of PE investment in EM underscores fundamental tensions between profit motives and the ethical practice of EM. Safeguarding patient welfare will require enhanced regulatory oversight, physician advocacy, and professional organization support for physician-led practice models. Nonetheless, PE ownership may also provide opportunities for innovation, improved efficiency, and financial stability when aligned with patient-centered goals. Given limited EM-specific empirical data, we propose focused avenues for future research.

Background

Private equity (PE) firms have increasingly invested in emergency medicine (EM) services by acquiring physician staffing companies and emergency department (ED) management groups. Proponents argue that PE firms can provide needed capital and management expertise that may improve efficiency and care delivery, which are particularly important in the current time of increased ED volumes and increased boarding. However, their profit-driven model introduces many ethical concerns relating to the potential impact of cost-cutting measures and other profit-maximizing strategies on the quality of patient care.

In his landmark treatise, “The Biology of Emergency Medicine” (1979),1 Peter Rosen asserted that the specialty of EM was born from the societal need for a better way to treat patients following the “the disappearance of primary physicians, the demise of the housecall, the growth of urban populations, the increased expectations of the public, the initial willingness of third-party carriers to pay for ED visits but not office calls, and last, but not least, the captive presence of a physician in a predictable geographical area when the patient wanted to be seen.” This framing underscores EM’s origins as a patient-centered safety net and highlights why shifts toward investor-driven ownership raise concern about preserving the specialty’s core mission.

Increasing investment by PE firms within the EM space has already led to a critical tension between the profit motives of PE firm owners and the ethical obligations of emergency physicians. The American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP) has expressed concern that profit-driven decisions may adversely influence health care delivery.2 For example, recent research suggests that PE-owned EDs may reduce physician staffing levels through increased reliance upon less costly advanced practice providers (APPs).3,4 Emergency physicians working in such environments must navigate the myriad of ethical dilemmas, balancing patient welfare with corporate pressures.

These trends occur against a backdrop of declining reimbursement, rising operational costs, and financial pressures on EM groups. Some physician groups have pursued PE investment to obtain capital, stabilize operations, or facilitate succession planning when senior partners retire or become less able or willing to sustain day-to-day practice management responsibilities.5

In this article, we aimed to (1) describe the growth and business model of PE in EM; (2) synthesize available evidence regarding clinical, operational, and workforce impacts; (3) examine ethical concerns in the context of broader corporatization; and (4) propose policy implications and a prioritized research agenda to address key knowledge gaps.

PE in Emergency Medicine: Background and Definitions

Modern EM physicians face increasing challenges, including rising ED volumes, excessive patient boarding, staffing shortages, and financial pressures from health system owners. Many hospitals already emphasize operational efficiency, typically measured in relative value units (RVUs) per hour, potentially at the expense of patient safety. Such operational efficiency with increased RVUs per hour might decrease waiting room burden, though high-complexity patients demanding more time per patient could be at risk for patient safety events. The introduction of metrics that are not clearly aligned with patient-centered outcomes to increase profit by reducing costs and resource availability can jeopardize the quality of patient care.

Hospitals in the United States generally fall into several ownership categories, including federal systems, state and county hospitals, among others.6 Federal and many state and county hospitals are tax-exempt and funded at least in part by tax revenues, with requirements to provide charity care, although they may provide uncompensated care or community benefits for strategic or tax-related reasons. Although one might expect that public and nonprofit hospitals would provide more charity care than for-profit (FP) hospitals, this is not always the case. FP hospitals are driven by profit motives, but still are required to provide care to all patients.7

PE firms are business management firms that exist to provide investment opportunities to large organizations (eg, universities, pension funds, endowments, insurance companies, nonprofits, etc.) and high-net-worth individuals.8 The PE business model is designed to generate maximal profit for investors over a finite period of time (eg, 3-10 years) with high annual returns (often > 20%). They achieve these profits by acquiring partial or complete ownership of a business and employing tactics to reduce costs and increase revenue.8 Although most PE acquisitions are by private businesses (ie, not subject to the same regulatory scrutiny as public companies), PE firms may also take over public companies with the goal of taking them private. When these acquired companies become unprofitable—often due to a combination of depleted assets, heavy PE-initiated debt (ie, leveraging), and loss of market share—the PE firm will typically sell (“exit”) its ownership of the company to another company, often another PE firm. In many cases, the acquired company faces bankruptcy and/or a long road to recovery after its PE firm owners exit.

Across all markets (including health care), the PE investment model has expanded aggressively over the last 2 decades. Although PE (and other private funds) managed <$1 trillion in assets in 2004, this number has recently ballooned to >$13 trillion.9 Within the health care market, PE investment totaled $115 billion globally in 2024, compared with $83 billion in 2021,10 as the rise in PE in health care is no longer just an American phenomenon.11 In the United States, approximately 460 hospitals are currently owned by PE firms, constituting 8% of all private hospitals and 22% of all FP hospitals.12 Of these, 26% serve rural populations, and 22.5% are psychiatric hospitals, suggesting that PE firms appear to disproportionately target specific types of hospitals for acquisition.12

Proponents of PE investment in health care suggest that there are potential benefits of PE firm management, including greater efficiency, decreased waste, increased access to capital for technology investments, management expertise, less administrative burden, increased negotiating power, and greater facilitation for rapid growth.8 However, critics highlight potential negative impacts, including understaffing, higher patient bills, and increased financial instability, contributing to hospital closures. A recent 171-page US Senate Budget Committee bipartisan staff report from January 2025 concluded with the assessment that PE firms value “profits over patients.”9 This report was released in response to egregious reported outcomes from a PE-acquired FP hospital, citing examples of understaffing, quality-of-care declines, and worsening financial status, all to the “detriment of patients and hospital operations.” Recent studies comparing PE-acquired hospitals to matched non-profit hospitals showed that the patient care experience worsened after PE firm acquisition,13 and PE acquisition was associated with higher hospital–acquired adverse events, such as increased falls and central line-associated bloodstream infections.14 In fact, PE–backed FP hospitals can suffer extreme financial difficulties, leading to closure while PE investors still profit significantly from their investments.

Additional potential advantages described by professional and industry sources include quality improvement programs and clinical standardization, enhanced governance and compliance infrastructure, and support for value-based care capabilities, such as analytics, care coordination, and digital tools.15, 16, 17

Mixed evidence exists on the effects of PE investment on physician salaries. Although PE firms usually finance up to 80% of the practice, this does still allow for some degree of physician ownership. Some studies indicate increased compensation for physicians under PE ownership. For example, in one study, dollars paid to anesthesia practitioners were higher after acquisition by a physician management company (PMC) and even higher if the PMC was supported by PE.18 Another study demonstrated financial improvements for the hospital, as “private equity hospitals reduced ED salary expenditures by 18.2%.”19 However, research on dermatology, ophthalmology, and gastroenterology practices has shown higher physician turnover and increased rates of APP employment in PE-owned practices.20, 21, 22 In cardiology, PE acquisitions have been linked to price increases, understaffing, and restrictive employment contracts.23

Although empirical data on PE-owned EDs remain limited, PE has seen rapid expansion in the world of EM. Between 2009 and 2019, PE ownership of EM physician groups nearly tripled,24 with current estimates suggesting that 25% of EDs are staffed by PE-supported firms.3 These firms tend to employ fewer physicians and more APPs, contributing to increased patient loads per physician, longer wait times, and higher bills.25 One study found that PE-backed hospitals had reduced ED salary expenditures, employing fewer physicians in the entire hospital. It was also noted that the rate of ED deaths in PE-backed hospitals increased by 7 deaths per 10,000 visits and a 4.2% increase in transfers after acquisition.19 Physicians in PE-backed practices report reduced time with patients and greater productivity pressures. Further, physicians in PE-backed practices appear to have less agency in the group decision making, putting the operational and throughput decisions in the hands of firms with little or no medical expertise.

The objectives of PE in EM, as with PE elsewhere, are to pool investments from select private investors to use leveraged (ie, financed by loans) or other buyout strategies to purchase controlling stakes in companies to increase profits rapidly through aggressive generation of revenue and drastic reduction of costs before selling the investment, often after just a few years of ownership.7 Across these examples, the unifying feature of the model is a focus on maximizing returns for investors over a defined time horizon, which may not align with the long-term needs of patients, clinicians, or communities.

Ethical Considerations and Patient Care

Some concerns commonly attributed to PE, such as productivity pressure, workforce substitution, and constrained resources, are also documented in nonprofit, governmental, and large corporate health systems. In this sense, PE may represent an intensified form of broader corporatization rather than a wholly distinct phenomenon while still raising distinctive questions related to leveraged buyouts and short investment horizons.

Iserson et al26 outlined 4 responses to PE influence in EM: (1) prioritizing patient care over profit, (2) strengthening legal protections, (3) enhancing clinical education, and (4) advocating for policy changes. These strategies underscore the need for regulatory oversight to safeguard physician decision making and patient interests.26 Prioritizing patient care requires that emergency physicians resist inappropriate cost-cutting when it threatens safety. Strengthening legal protections may include enforcing corporate practice of medicine prohibitions and whistleblower safeguards. Enhancing clinical education can prepare trainees to recognize and navigate business pressures, and advocacy for policy changes can help align payment, ownership, and accountability structures with patient-centered goals.

Emergency physicians have an ethical obligation to prioritize patient welfare (ie, beneficence) and avoid harm (ie, nonmaleficence).27 Furthermore, as physicians are under greater pressure to minimize time with individual patients, informed decision making and respect for autonomy are at risk. Physicians also have a fiduciary duty to act in patients’ best interests,28 a legal responsibility that is not shared by PE firms, whose primary obligation is to their investors. This fundamental misalignment raises ethical concerns regarding clinical autonomy, cost–driven decision making, and patient outcomes. The increased involvement of PE in EM contributes to market consolidation and threatens to increase costs through strategies such as upcoding and overutilization. Patients bear the increased cost, which results in higher medical costs, either through insurance premiums or direct out-of-pocket costs. Such economic harm to patients is contrary to the ethical principle of nonmaleficence. This increases financial distress and total medical debt.

Common PE cost-cutting strategies include reducing physician-patient time, limiting testing, and increasing patient load per clinician. This reduced physician-patient time may result in compromising patients’ ability to make informed decisions about their care, thus affecting the respect for autonomy.29 These measures may be associated with higher error rates, restricted access to care, and financial burdens for patients. PE ownership can also introduce restrictive employment terms, further eroding physician autonomy and job security. Furthermore, excessive restrictions on care that limit physician autonomy in clinical practice threaten to result in lower quality of emergency care, as well as a concomitant increase in moral distress and clinician burnout.26

Empirical data specific to PE-owned EDs remain limited. Many hypothesized effects, such as changes in RVUs per hour, patients seen per clinician, APP utilization patterns, quality-of-care metrics, medical board complaints, malpractice claim rates, patient billing, or job satisfaction, have not been thoroughly measured in emergency care settings. Carefully designed observational and mixed-methods studies are needed to distinguish perceived from demonstrated effects and to clarify which outcomes are attributable to PE ownership vs broader system-level forces. To this end, it is also possible that some of these outcomes and effects are not unique to PE ownership itself.

Impact on EM Culture and Physician Workforce

The modern ED has evolved to become a safety net for vulnerable patients, and our specialty’s culture is known to emphasize patient-centered care, teamwork, and rapid decision making.30, 31, 32 However, increasing corporate influences may threaten this culture.

An increased focus on profit-centered metrics intended to reduce cost and avoid “waste” ultimately risks eclipsing patient-centered metrics, not only compromising patient care but also potentially reducing clinician job satisfaction. Misaligned performance metrics, such as prioritizing financial efficiency over patient care, could undermine EM’s core values. Prior work suggests that FP hospitals are more likely to close low-margin EDs, exacerbating workforce instability and limiting access to care for those who need it most.33

The evolving paradigm shift from patient-centered care to profit-centered care generally includes financial incentives to clinicians aimed at reducing utilization of expensive but potentially value-added tests and treatments in the ED. Although mature emergency physicians may be relatively immune to this form of coercion, EM trainees run the risk of being acculturated into a field that rewards cost savings at the expense of patient outcomes. As PE firms become increasingly involved in the provision of emergency care, physicians are likely to enjoy less job security, diminished investment in their local community, reduced equity ownership in their practices, and increased susceptibility to third-party manipulation of medical care. After all, when profitability declines, PE firms may exit their investments rather abruptly,34 leaving clinicians and their patients vulnerable.

Compensation structures and workforce composition also shift under PE ownership. Some specialties have seen increased practitioner payments after acquisition by physician management companies supported by PE, whereas others have reported higher turnover and greater use of APPs in place of physicians.18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23 Understanding how these patterns play out in EM remains an important area for future study.

Current Responses to PE in EM

Heavily enforced cost-cutting measures in PE-owned EDs risk deterring physicians who value their clinical autonomy, potentially attracting a workforce more aligned with corporate interests, violating the ethical principle of justice. The sustainability of PE-driven EM remains uncertain, with increasing resistance from physicians advocating for alternative practice models. Some groups are pushing back against PE takeovers, arguing for a physician-led approach that prioritizes patient care over profits.

On the positive side, federal and state regulatory scrutiny of PE firm investments is increasing, and advocacy groups are emerging, intending to limit PE’s role in health care through both legislative action21,35,36 and peer education.37,38 This movement reflects a desire among emergency physicians to reclaim control over their practice environments and defend the integrity of the patient-physician relationship.39 Greater oversight could reshape PE’s role in EM, potentially encouraging a shift toward more sustainable, physician-led emergency care models.

Current responses span several domains. Policymakers in some states have begun to explore or implement disclosure requirements and transaction review processes for PE acquisitions in health care, aiming to increase transparency around ownership and financial risk.35 Professional societies have issued position statements on physician autonomy and corporate influence.2,27,28,37 Physician-led advocacy organizations have organized campaigns to educate clinicians and the public about PE’s role, as well as broader corporatization trends.35,37 At the institutional level, some groups have sought to strengthen corporate practice of medicine protections, clarify reporting channels for safety concerns, and embed physician leadership in governance structures, regardless of ownership model.

These efforts do not frame PE as the sole source of tension between business imperatives and patient care. Rather, they situate PE within a continuum that includes academic health systems, nonprofit systems, governmental hospitals, and large FP chains, all of which may adopt business practices that pressure clinicians. Recognizing this broader context is essential for designing solutions that improve equity, safety, and sustainability across EM, not only within PE-owned entities.

Although PE investment is just beginning to influence the practice of EM, it has already stimulated a closer examination of critical questions about the long-term implications of the FP motive within our specialty. The potential for PE firms to abruptly exit in response to declining profits risks creating lasting changes to the ethical fabric and landscape of EM. The ED has been described as the “safety net” for communities seeking medical care, but what happens when that net is abruptly removed?40

Future Directions

Despite growing descriptive literature, many critical questions about PE in EM remain unanswered. We proposed several priority domains for future research:1.Clinical operations: comparative studies of physician and APP productivity, RVUs per hour, boarding times, left-without-being-seen rates, and time-to-provider metrics in PE-owned versus non-PE-owned EDs.2.Physician experience and workforce: assessment of job satisfaction, autonomy, burnout, turnover, and recruitment patterns, including how PE affects early-career physicians and trainees.3.Financial impacts on patients and payers: analysis of changes in patient billing, out-of-pocket costs, surprise billing rates, and payer mix, as well as downstream utilization.4.Market-level effects: examination of ED closures, regional consolidation, and access to emergency care in communities where PE enters or exits the market, including counterfactual analyses of what might have occurred without PE involvement.5.Comparative corporatization: studies that explicitly compare PE-owned entities with academic, nonprofit, governmental, and other corporate models to identify which outcomes are specific to PE and which reflect broader systemic underfunding and commercialization.

Rigorous, transparent work in these areas will be essential for informing policy, guiding professional society positions, and helping emergency physicians make informed decisions about employment and advocacy.

Conclusion

The rise of PE investment in EM highlights age-old tensions between the financial imperatives that drive the business of health care and the physician’s ethical obligation to prioritize patient outcomes over profits. PE ownership represents one prominent manifestation of broader corporatization across health care, and careful analysis is needed to distinguish its specific effects from those arising under academic, nonprofit, governmental, or other corporate models. Although PE firms can introduce needed capital investments and operational efficiencies, their profit-driven approach could lead to impaired clinical autonomy and diminished care quality. Addressing these conflicts will require physician input, thoughtful policy reforms, and professional advocacy to defend the rights of both patients and healthcare workers.

Lawmakers should enforce transparency in health care ownership, regulate corporate influence in medical decision making, and ensure that financial incentives do not undermine the quality of care. Professional organizations can help by supporting physician-led models, providing guidance on employment contracts for EM physicians, and advocating for increased governmental oversight of PE firm investments.

As PE continues to shape EM, physicians must engage in policy discussions and advocacy efforts to protect ethical standards and preserve the culture of EM. Balancing financial realities with patient-centered goals of care will demand a collective effort from policymakers, professional groups, and frontline physicians alike. Simultaneously, a focused research agenda, similar to one proposed above, is needed to generate the empirical evidence required to guide these policy and practice decisions.
