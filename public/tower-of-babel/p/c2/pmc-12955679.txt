Nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin for unresectable or metastatic urothelial carcinoma: health-related quality-of-life analyses from the phase III CheckMate 901 trial☆
Authors: J. Bedke, M.S. van der Heijden, G. Sonpavde, M.D. Galsky, W. Liao, L. Shi, S.I. Blum, S. Mitra, M.Y. Patel, T. Powles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn the phase III CheckMate 901 trial, nivolumab plus standard-of-care gemcitabine–cisplatin chemotherapy followed by nivolumab monotherapy significantly prolonged overall survival and progression-free survival versus gemcitabine–cisplatin alone in cisplatin-eligible patients with unresectable or metastatic urothelial carcinoma (UC). Here, using patient-reported outcome (PRO) data from CheckMate 901, we report effects of adding nivolumab to gemcitabine–cisplatin on health-related quality of life (HRQoL).Patients and methodsPRO assessments [European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire—Core 30 (EORTC QLQ-C30) and EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L)] were scheduled for both arms through the first 6 months of treatment (combination phase), and for the nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin arm thereafter (monotherapy phase). The prespecified primary PRO scale of interest was QLQ-C30 global health status/quality of life (QoL). Secondary scales of interest included QLQ-C30 physical functioning, role functioning, and fatigue, and the EQ-5D-5L visual analog scale. Noninferiority analysis involved a mixed-effect model for repeated measures to estimate differences in least-squares mean changes from baseline to week 16. The proportions of patients with meaningful changes were calculated using prespecified thresholds.ResultsIn total, 524 patients were analyzed (nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin, n = 276; gemcitabine–cisplatin alone, n = 248). Overall least-squares mean changes through week 16 indicated the noninferiority of nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin versus gemcitabine–cisplatin alone for global health status/QoL and all secondary scales. At week 16, the proportions of patients with meaningful changes in global health status/QoL (improvement, 29.2% versus 23.0%; deterioration, 28.3% versus 30.4%) and all secondary PRO scales were similar in both arms.ConclusionsAdding nivolumab to gemcitabine–cisplatin did not worsen HRQoL compared with gemcitabine–cisplatin alone in the first 16 weeks of therapy. These findings support nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin as standard first-line therapy for cisplatin-eligible patients with unresectable or metastatic UC.

Introduction

Historically, cisplatin-based chemotherapy has been the preferred standard of care for patients with previously untreated unresectable or metastatic urothelial carcinoma (UC).1, 2, 3, 4 Standard-of-care chemotherapy in this first-line setting has a response rate of >40% and a median survival of >12 months, but responses are rarely durable.1,2 To improve these outcomes, combining standard-of-care chemotherapy with immunotherapy has been investigated, especially with immune checkpoint inhibitors such as nivolumab—a monoclonal antibody directed against programmed cell death protein 1.5

The global, open-label, phase III CheckMate 901 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03036098) was designed to determine whether nivolumab in combination with ipilimumab or standard-of-care chemotherapy significantly prolonged overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) versus standard-of-care chemotherapy alone in patients with untreated unresectable or metastatic UC. CheckMate 901 was carried out in two parts: the first part assigned cisplatin-eligible patients to receive either nivolumab plus standard-of-care gemcitabine–cisplatin chemotherapy or gemcitabine–cisplatin alone6; the second part assigned patients (cisplatin-eligible and -ineligible) to receive either nivolumab plus ipilimumab or standard-of-care platinum-based chemotherapy. Results from the first part of CheckMate 901 showed that upfront concurrent treatment with nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin followed by nivolumab monotherapy provided statistically significant efficacy improvements over gemcitabine–cisplatin alone, with significantly longer PFS [hazard ratio (HR) 0.72, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.59-0.88, P = 0.001] and OS (HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.63-0.96, P = 0.02) after a median follow-up of 33.6 months.6 Median PFS was 7.9 versus 7.6 months, and median OS was 21.7 versus 18.9 months. Because the trial met its primary survival endpoints, both the United States Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency have approved nivolumab in combination with cisplatin and gemcitabine for first-line treatment of adult patients with unresectable or metastatic UC.7,8

In addition to the primary efficacy endpoints of PFS and OS prolongation, the effect of the treatment regimen on patient health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is an important consideration. Patients with UC report significantly worse symptoms and functioning than the general population—particularly fatigue, physical functioning, and role functioning—and this is often worsened through treatment courses with chemotherapy and radiotherapy.9 Data are scarce on how concurrent administration of immunotherapies with standard-of-care chemotherapy affects HRQoL of patients with advanced UC. Here, using patient-reported outcome (PRO) data collected in the first part of CheckMate 901, we evaluated whether HRQoL is adversely affected by the concurrent administration of nivolumab with gemcitabine–cisplatin in cisplatin-eligible patients with previously untreated unresectable or metastatic UC.

Patients and methods

Study design and participants

This was an analysis of PRO data from the first part of CheckMate 901, for which full details of the study design, participants, and outcomes/assessments have been published.6 In brief, adult patients with histologically confirmed unresectable or metastatic UC involving the renal pelvis, ureter, bladder, or urethra were randomly assigned 1 : 1 to either the nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin arm (intravenous nivolumab 360 mg plus gemcitabine–cisplatin once every 3 weeks for up to six doses followed by nivolumab 480 mg once every 4 weeks for a maximum of 2 years) or the gemcitabine–cisplatin alone arm (gemcitabine–cisplatin once every 3 weeks for up to six doses). Treatments were administered until confirmed progression of disease, unacceptable toxicity, withdrawal of consent, or up to 2 years of nivolumab treatment (for the nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin arm only), whichever occurred first. Permitted dose reductions and dose delays are described elsewhere.6

The trial was approved by each center’s respective institutional review board and was conducted following Good Clinical Practice guidelines outlined by the International Council for Harmonisation. All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Procedures

The European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire—Core 30 (EORTC QLQ-C30) and the EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L) health utility index and visual analog scale (VAS) were used to assess HRQoL. The EORTC QLQ-C30 consists of 30 questions that are scored on 15 different scales: a global health status/quality-of-life (QoL) scale, five multi-item functional scales (physical, role, emotional, cognitive, and social functioning), and nine symptom scales (fatigue, nausea/vomiting, pain, dyspnea, insomnia, appetite loss, constipation, diarrhea, and financial difficulties).10 All 15 scales are linearly transformed to a 0-100 score range: a higher score for the global health status/QoL scale and the functional scales represents a better overall HRQoL or healthier functioning; a higher score for a symptom scale/item represents a higher level of symptomatology or problems.11 The EQ-5D-5L descriptive system consists of five dimensions: mobility, self-care, pain/discomfort, usual activities, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension has five levels of severity corresponding to ‘no problems’, ‘slight problems’, ‘moderate problems’, ‘severe problems’, and ‘extreme problems’. A single utility index score can be calculated by applying a preference-based value set to the health state derived from responses to the descriptive system. In addition, the EQ-5D-5L has a VAS that allows the respondent to self-rate their health ranging from 0 (worst imaginable health state) to 100 (best imaginable health state).12 A ‘cross-walk’ mapping function, originally developed by van Hout et al.,13 was used to convert EQ-5D-5L responses in order to apply utility index scores based on the EQ-5D-3L value set. The EQ-5D-3L health utility index, based on UK population weights, ranges from −0.594 to 1.00 (0.00 indicates death, 1.00 indicates full health, and negative scores indicate states perceived as worse than death).14

The prespecified primary scale of interest for the current analysis was the QLQ-C30 global health status/QoL. Secondary scales of interest were the physical functioning, role functioning, and fatigue scales of QLQ-C30 and the EQ-VAS.15,16 The remaining functional and symptom scales of QLQ-C30 and the EQ-5D-5L utility index were evaluated as exploratory measures.

Per protocol, patients in the gemcitabine–cisplatin alone arm were scheduled to complete the QLQ-C30 and EQ-5D-5L before dosing of study treatment on day 1 of cycle 1 (baseline), and on day 1 of cycles 2, 4, and 6. Patients in the gemcitabine–cisplatin alone arm who continued with additional treatment according to local guidelines were scheduled to complete the PRO assessments on day 1 of every other subsequent cycle, starting with cycle 8. Patients in the nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin arm were scheduled to complete the QLQ-C30 and EQ-5D-5L before dosing of study treatment on day 1 of cycle 1 (baseline), and on day 1 of cycles 2 and 4, then every 4 weeks for three cycles, and every 12 weeks thereafter.

Statistical analysis

All patients who had an evaluable assessment of a given PRO/HRQoL measure at baseline and at least one evaluable assessment at post-baseline visits were included in the analyses. An evaluable assessment was defined as completion of at least one of the QLQ-C30 scales, all five items of the EQ-5D-5L descriptive system, or a non-missing EQ-VAS value at a scheduled assessment visit. For the QLQ-C30, scores for each scale were calculated if responses were given to at least 50% of the items in that particular scale; otherwise, it was considered to be missing.11 Completion rates for each PRO were analyzed over time. All analyses were prespecified unless otherwise noted.

The primary analysis assessed change from baseline to week 16 in QLQ-C30 global health status/QoL using a linear mixed-effect model for repeated measures (MMRM), which facilitated assessment of the noninferiority of nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin versus gemcitabine–cisplatin alone. Similar analyses were conducted for the secondary QLQ-C30 scales and EQ-VAS through week 16. For all MMRM models, change in PRO score from baseline was used as the dependent variable, with time (modeled as a continuous variable) and the intercept included as random effects, and covariates included as fixed effects were treatment arm (nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin versus gemcitabine–cisplatin alone), time (week as a categorical variable), stratification factors [programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression <1% (negative/indeterminate versus positive) for the analysis based on the PRO-assessable population, and presence of liver metastasis (yes versus no)], baseline score, interaction between baseline score and time, as well as interaction between treatment group and time. MMRM subgroup analyses were conducted post hoc by splitting the data per level within each subgroup of interest, with the baseline PRO score, treatment, time, and the interaction of treatment and time included in the model.

Longitudinal change from baseline was assessed descriptively through the trial duration, and mean scores and mean changes from baseline were plotted by visit and treatment for the PRO scale scores.

Differences in the proportions of patients experiencing a meaningful HRQoL improvement or deterioration between the treatment arms were assessed from baseline through week 16. For the QLQ-C30 scales, a ≥10-point change was used as the responder definition threshold to determine a meaningful within-patient change (improvement or deterioration) and within-group change minimum important difference (MID). This responder definition threshold, reported by Osoba et al.,17 has been the most commonly used threshold for QLQ-C30. MIDs for between-group differences were based on the lower bound of the MID thresholds for a ‘small’ improvement and deterioration reported by Cocks et al.18 These prespecified MIDs were used as the noninferiority margins19 to determine whether HRQoL outcomes among patients receiving nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin were noninferior to those among patients receiving gemcitabine–cisplatin alone. The responder definition thresholds and MIDs were 0.08 points for the EQ-5D utility index and 7 points for the EQ-VAS, respectively, based on estimates for EQ-5D-3L by Pickard et al.20 Responses to EQ-5D-5L were mapped to EQ-5D-3L utility index scores using the function developed by van Hout et al.13 All thresholds and MIDs were prespecified.

Results

Study population

Data on the QLQ-C30 were evaluable for 524 of the 608 randomized patients (86.2%), comprising 276 patients randomly assigned to the nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin arm and 248 to the gemcitabine–cisplatin alone arm (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106089). Overall, most patients presented with disease stage IV at baseline (89.3%), and over a third of the patients had tumor PD-L1 expression ≥1% (34.7%). More than half of the patients had been diagnosed with UC <1 year before randomization (62.6%). Baseline PRO scores

PRO scores at baseline were comparable between treatment arms for the PRO-assessable population (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106089).

Responder analysis of meaningful changes

The proportions of patients with meaningful improvement or deterioration at week 16 were similar between the nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin arm and gemcitabine–cisplatin alone arm for the primary PRO scale of global health status/QoL (29.2% versus 23.0% for improvement, 28.3% versus 30.4% for deterioration) and for all secondary and exploratory PRO scales (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106089).

Subgroup analysis

Post hoc subgroup analysis of the MMRM generally showed no notable between-group differences in overall least-squares mean change on the QLQ-C30 global health status/QoL when patients were grouped by demographic characteristics, visceral metastasis, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status at baseline, PD-L1 expression at baseline, or response status (complete response/partial response) (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106089). Although the subgroup of Asian patients showed significantly worse change (i.e. increased deterioration) in the nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin arm than in the gemcitabine–cisplatin alone arm, this pattern was not reflected in other PRO scales. For the secondary PRO scales, between-group differences were generally absent for these subgroupings, although a trend for better improvement was seen with nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin for patients with baseline PD-L1 expression ≥1% (especially for the QLQ-C30 physical functioning scale) and with an ECOG performance status of 1 but not 0 (especially for the QLQ-C30 physical functioning scale and EQ-VAS) (Supplementary Tables S5-S8, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106089).

Discussion

This study analyzed PRO data collected during the CheckMate 901 trial to assess whether the previously demonstrated efficacy of nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin6 was accompanied by an impact on patient HRQoL. Completion rates for the PRO scales were >50% for both treatment arms, except for the nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin arm at week 16 (40%). This may be attributed to factors such as changes in conditions upon initiation of nivolumab monotherapy or a narrow window for mapping dose-based to time-based visits.

Overall findings show that HRQoL was maintained at baseline levels throughout the study period for both treatment arms. Most PRO scores remained stable or slightly worsened on initial visits in both arms. The least-squares mean changes up to week 16 indicated that nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin was noninferior to gemcitabine–cisplatin alone, by predefined criteria,18,20 on all key PRO scales in the combination phase. Noninferiority was also demonstrated for nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin versus gemcitabine–cisplatin alone in most exploratory scales except for nausea/vomiting. At week 16, the proportion of patients experiencing meaningful changes (improvement or deterioration) was similar between the treatment arms during the combination phase. In the subgroup analyses, no notable between-group differences were observed for the primary or most secondary scales, indicating that the addition of nivolumab to gemcitabine–cisplatin did not worsen the patients’ HRQoL.

Comprehensive analysis of the PRO endpoints based on recommendations by the Setting International Standards in Analyzing Patient-Reported Outcomes and Quality of Life Endpoints Data Consortium is a strength of this study.21 Further, this study used the EORTC QLQ-C30,10 a well-validated questionnaire commonly used in cancer and other clinical research areas and one of the most used PRO/HRQoL instruments in clinical trials of bladder cancer.22 However, this study has some limitations. Firstly, data on PROs for the gemcitabine–cisplatin alone arm were only available until week 24; therefore, there was no comparator to evaluate HRQoL beyond this timepoint for patients on maintenance therapy (nivolumab monotherapy). Secondly, some timepoints had low completion rates. The low completion rate may introduce selection bias, as there may be systematic differences between participants who responded and those who did not. It also limits the generalizability of the study results, as those who responded may not represent the target patient population. Study results should be interpreted with these limitations in mind.

Conclusion

Treatment with nivolumab did not cause detrimental effects to patients’ HRQoL during the combined treatment phase (nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin) or the maintenance phase (nivolumab monotherapy). The addition of nivolumab to gemcitabine–cisplatin did not worsen HRQoL compared with gemcitabine–cisplatin alone. HRQoL is an important parameter for patients with UC and physicians to consider when selecting treatments, and there is a clear need for UC therapies that prolong survival while maintaining HRQoL. Our findings, taken alongside previous findings from the CheckMate 901 trial, show that concurrent administration of nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin (for up to six cycles) followed by nivolumab monotherapy (for up to 2 years) both maintained HRQoL and significantly improved OS and PFS compared with gemcitabine–cisplatin alone in cisplatin-eligible patients with untreated unresectable or metastatic UC. Findings support nivolumab plus gemcitabine–cisplatin as a standard first-line treatment option for cisplatin-eligible patients with previously untreated unresectable or metastatic UC.
