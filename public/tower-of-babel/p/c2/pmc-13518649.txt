Predictive Modeling of Coronary Artery Disease Using Color Fundus Photography-Based Features of Retinal Vasculature
Authors: Natasa Jeremic, Emese Sükei, Azin Zarghami, Michael Apata, Meltem Esengönül, Maximilian Pawloff, Andreas Pollreisz, Reinhard Windhager, Matthias Hasun, Alexander Niessner, Stefan Sacu, Hrvoje Bogunovic, Ursula Schmidt-Erfurth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCoronary artery disease (CAD) remains the leading cause of death and current screening methods are limited. Color fundus photography (CFP) has been explored in literature mostly on the basis of associations and exploratory deep learning approaches with only indirect end points. In this explorative study, we aimed to assess the predictive abilities and limitations of explainable CFP-based features using machine learning and same-visit coronary angiography (CA) outcomes as end points for the first time.MethodsPatients undergoing CA were imaged with CFP (Zeiss Clarus 500, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) during the same visit. Coronary plaque burden was assessed using the Gensini Score. Retinal features were extracted using Automorph. Machine learning models were trained and evaluated using fivefold cross-validation. Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) values quantified feature importance and interactions.ResultsOf 977 screened patients, 632 (1293 eyes) were assessed. CFP features alone reached moderate predictive performance (area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) 0.692). Adding dimensionally reduced CFP features to clinical baselines consistently improved performance, with the best configuration yielding an AUROC 0.775, average precision (AP) 0.752, and Brier score 0.203. Net reclassification improvement (NRI)/integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) analyses supported improved reclassification for age + sex and basic clinical baseline models, but not for extended clinical baseline models. SHAP analysis revealed vessel width, density, and tortuosity as important vascular retinal indicators of CAD burden. Interaction analysis revealed nonlinear, age-, sex-, and diabetes-dependent effects.ConclusionsCFP features modestly improved the CAD classification beyond clinical baselines. Our findings illustrate the potential and the limitations of CFP features and indicate the need for complex modeling, methodological improvement, and multimodal approaches to achieve valuable classification efficacy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40123-026-01444-8.

Introduction

Despite continuous scientific and therapeutic progress in cardiovascular diseases (CVD), coronary artery disease (CAD) is still the leading cause of mortality worldwide [1–3]. Owing to the aging population, the prevalence of CAD is still increasing [4, 5]. Therefore, a large unmet need for effective noninvasive screening persists, as currently available diagnostic methodologies are mostly limited by radiation exposure, restricted availability, and high costs [3, 6]. Despite existing risk score such as the Framingham risk score [7], SCORE2 [8] or atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) score [9] and few available radiological imaging techniques, current risk assessment strategies still show limitations. For example, Manesh et al. reported that those assessments tend to overestimate the probability of CAD, where 39.2% of the patients who underwent coronary angiography (CA) had no CAD [10, 11]. Advances in precision medicine may help address this problem by improving patient selection, with retinal imaging being one promising approach. Retinal biomarkers have been reported as surrogates for several cardiovascular diseases [12, 13]. Given the broad availability and technical simplicity of color fundus photography (CFP), it would be an ideal method for large-scale CAD screening, when assuming adequate predictive accuracy [14, 15].

Early studies have already described characteristic findings in fundus examinations and fundus camera images [16, 17]. Manual annotations enabled feature analysis but were time-consuming and inconsistent. Building on these, semi-automated tools such as Singapore “I” Vessel Assessment (SIVA) and the fully automated open-source Automorph pipeline were developed [18]. While deep learning (DL) based CVD predictions already showed promise in the recent literature, they display several limitations such as lack of interpretability and insight into the prediction driving features [19–23]. In contrast to that, machine learning models based on interpretable retinal features may offer more clinically meaningful insights [24, 25].

Despite growing interest, no DL or cross-validated machine learning (ML) study has yet evaluated CFP vascular morphology features for CAD assessment beyond acute coronary syndromes, and particularly, no study incorporated same-visit CA results. Therefore, we conducted the first comprehensive and robust ML analysis of CFP features for CA-based CAD classification, aiming to explore the predictive capability and limitations of CFP features over clinical baselines.

Methods

Study Design

For this cross-sectional study, ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Vienna (EK number: 1956/2020). The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to any study-related procedures.

Participants

The initial study cohort consisted of 861 patients who underwent CA at the cardiology department of Clinic Landstraße, Vienna, Austria. Additionally, 116 healthy age- and sex-matched control subjects were recruited from the department of Orthopedics and Trauma Surgery of the Medical University of Vienna (MUV). A total of 632 patients (1293 eyes) were included and additionally those with retinal diseases, poor-quality images, alcohol or drug abuse, significant confounding comorbidities (e.g., severe aortic valve stenosis, end-stage renal disease, active malignancy), as well as individuals with a prior history of coronary stenting were excluded. All patients received routine blood analyses and physical examination, additionally to the CA. All healthy controls underwent blood sampling and a comprehensive clinical examination by two internal medicine doctors. To ensure absence of subclinical, undetected CAD, only subjects with complete absence of systemic diseases and risk factors were included. A table with demographic and clinical information can be found in the Supplementary Table S1.

Cardiological End Point

The Gensini Score (GS) was calculated for all patients, and coronary plaque burden presence and absence was defined as cardiological end point [26]. The GS allows assessment of CAD severity by quantifying extent and location of all coronary stenoses in the coronary tree. This decision was motivated by the concept that cardiovascular event risk is believed to be driven by the overall coronary plaque burden, rather than by single focal obstructions [27–33]. Patients with no coronary stenoses in the CA (GS < 3) and the healthy controls were defined as CAD—while patients with a GS > 10 were considered as patients with relevant coronary plaque burden (CAD+). Patients in between were not considered to avoid misclassification, as they display only marginal coronary sclerosis and are therefore neither confidently CAD negative nor positive. The inclusion of healthy controls was intended to broaden the spectrum of cardiovascular status in the dataset, as a cohort restricted to patients with CA represents a population with naturally higher comorbidity burden. Healthy controls and CA-negative patients are not biologically interchangeable, but their combined inclusion allows analysis of a wider spectrum of vascular health. For transparency reasons, experimental calculations without healthy controls are included in the Supplementary Material.

CFP Imaging and Feature Extraction

All patients were imaged using a Zeiss Clarus 500, acquiring ultra-widefield (UWF) color fundus photographs. From each ultra-widefield image, two wide-field crops were generated: one macula and one optic disc centered. These were subsequently processed using AutoMorph [18], a deep learning pipeline for retinal vascular morphology quantification (Fig. 1A). Prior to this, the center points of the macula and the optic nerve head were manually determined in all images to ensure maximally standardized fields of view. Image cropping was performed to match the preferred AutoMorph input format and to exclude vessel distortions at the edges of UWF images [18]. All images were manually inspected for correct segmentation and adequate quality. In cases where multiple acquisitions were available, the extracted vessel morphometric features (VMF) were averaged across all acquisitions to increase feature robustness. Retinal features were aggregated across both eyes when available. In cases where only one eye was available the features were used directly. Any missing values were imputed during model training using the mean of the available observations for the corresponding feature. Dimensionality Reduction of Retinal Features

To reduce feature redundancy and increase model robustness, dimensionality reduction (dr) was applied (Fig. 1B). First, univariate feature selection was performed using an F-test to identify the most statistically informative predictors with respect to the target variable. The top 20 features were retained on the basis of their individual F-statistics. In parallel, a principal component analysis (PCA)-based reduction was conducted within each category of AutoMorph-derived features, such that 95% of the total variance was explained by the principal selected components. Aside from the full feature set, these two reduced representations were subsequently used for model development. Dimensionality reduction and feature selection were applied only to the VMF block to improve model stability and reduce multicollinearity and overfitting, as this feature group was high-dimensional and highly correlated. All imputations, dimensionality reduction, and feature selection processes were performed within cross-validation splits to avoid leakage. PCA specifically was recomputed independently within each fold using the optimal number of components determined from the full-dataset analysis. Keeping the number of components fixed across folds ensured consistent input dimensionality for comparability between folds and models. Sample distributions across folds are summarized in Supplementary Table S6.

Machine Learning Modeling

Several supervised machine learning algorithms were evaluated to capture different functional relationships between predictors and CAD (Fig. 1C). Hyperparameter optimization was performed for each model using an exhaustive grid search combined with stratified fivefold cross-validation, on the basis of predefined parameter ranges (Supplementary Table S2). Model training was conducted under multiple experimental scenarios to evaluate the predictive contribution of different feature sets: unimodal baselines, including (1) demographic variables only (age and sex), (2) a basic (noninvasive) set of comorbidities (BsCl), (3) an extended set of comorbidities (ExtCl), and (4) CFP features in raw (CFP), univariate-selected (CFP-FS), or PCA-reduced (CFP-PCA) form. Furthermore, multimodal configurations are composed, combining CFP-derived features with either demographic variables or comorbidity sets (basic or extended). Basic clinical features contain information that is available without invasive procedures (age, sex, arterial hypertension, hyperlipidemia, smoking, diabetes mellitus, kidney insufficiency, carotid artery stenosis, obesity) while extended clinical information contains additional information from blood analyses (BsCl + hemoglobin A1C (HbA1c), glomerular filtration rate (GFR)). All models, including clinical-only, VMF-only, and combined models, were evaluated using the same preprocessing, model-selection, and hyperparameter-tuning protocol. No specific feature-fusion strategy or model-specific preprocessing was performed for combined settings compared with unimodal settings. In addition to individual models, explorative ensemble strategies were implemented and reported separately to explore performance gains from model aggregation (Fig. 1C). The best-performing unimodal baselines were combined using (1) simple averaging of predicted probabilities and (2) a linear meta-classifier trained on the outputs of the selected base models.

Performance Metrics and Feature Importances

Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), the area under the precision–recall curve (AP), balanced accuracy (BAcc), and the F1 score. Calibration performance was evaluated using the Brier score, which quantifies probabilistic prediction accuracy by measuring the difference between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. To assess statistical significance for the differences in performance between top-performing models, bootstrapped resampling was performed. For adjustment of multiple comparisons, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was utilized. Moreover, net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were calculated using out‑of‑fold predictions from the same CV splits. For each fold, we compared the predicted probabilities on the identical test subjects, calculated fold‑level NRI and IDI, and then averaged them across folds. Confidence intervals were obtained by bootstrap resampling of the fold‑level estimates. To interpret the predictions of the best-performing multimodal model, Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) were applied, which quantifies the contribution of each feature to the model output on the basis of cooperative game theory [25]. SHAP values were averaged across cross-validation folds. Feature stability analysis was performed by calculating the selection frequency for each predictor, defined as the number of times it appeared among the top-ranked features divided by the total number of resamples. Moreover, SHAP dependence plots for the top features were computed to assess interaction effects.

Model Comparison (Averaged Models)

Mean performances across all nonensemble models can be found in Supplementary Fig. S2 and Supplementary Table S5. On average, VMF unimodels showed limited predictive ability (AUROC 0.590–0.628), whereas BsCI and ExtCI alone performed better (AUROC 0.686 and 0.703). Adding the large number of raw VMF features to clinical baselines did not improve and in some cases reduced performance (e.g. BsCI 0.686 versus BsCI + VMF 0.670). However, after dimensionality reduction the CFP features consistently enhanced the clinical baselines in all cases, with BsCI + VMF-PCA reaching an average AUCROC of 0.707 and ExtCI + VMF-PCA reaching the highest average AUROC of 0.714.

Model Calibration and Variance

Calibration analysis showed that models improved moderately over a noninformative baseline (Brier = 0.2498—given the CAD prevalence of 48.4%). The best multimodal models reached Brier scores around 0.20 after dimensionality reduction, corresponding to a relative gain of approximately 20%. Clinical baselines were well calibrated (0.21–0.22), while raw VMF models were not (Brier around 0.25). Multimodal settings, including PCA VMF, yielded the best results.

Performance varied a lot across model families (Supplementary Fig. S3). Ridge regression and ElasticNet were stable and strong, while support vector machines (SVM), Gaussian processes, and neural networks showed wide variability with strong dependence on tuning. Tree-based methods reached high AUROCs but were sensitive to parameterization, whereas K-Nearest-Neighbor and decision trees consistently performed poorly. Overall, we observed that careful parameter and model selection is critical to avoid unstable outcomes.

SHAP Analysis

As depicted in Fig. 3, clinical variables such as hyperlipidemia, sex, and HbA1c were most highly ranked and consistently among the most predictive features (5/5 folds). Additionally, VMF features as vessel caliber measures, vessel density, and various tortuosity metrics appeared repeatedly across folds. Positive SHAP values were observed with the presence of hyperlipidemia, higher HbA1c, and male sex, while wider arteries, lower vessel density, and greater venous tortuosity were associated with lower CAD risk. Hyperlipidemia, higher age (especially > 65 years), HbA1c above 6%, and DM presence were linked to increased CAD probability. Venous tortuosity metrics showed a rise in SHAP values with increasing metric values. Larger vessel calibers generally displayed protective effects, as higher metric values lead to lower SHAP values. Very low arterial fractal dimensions were associated with increased SHAP values and older age. Interestingly, in some cases, very low and very high venous widths tended to show protective associations, while intermediate values were linked to neutral or elevated risk. Several metrics displayed plateau effects with SHAP values around zero in some intervals and SHAP elevation/decrease in other ranges. Discussion

In this study, we performed the first explorative comprehensive machine learning analysis for CAD classification using CFP features with same-visit coronary angiography results as cardiological outcome. We observed a consistent incremental predictive value of vascular morphology features, when added to clinical baseline models, while VMF features alone had only limited predictive ability. Multimodal approaches with PCA-reduced VMFs yielded the highest performances, despite higher feature numbers. Ensemble strategies only marginally improved the results additionally. Raw VMF decreased predictive accuracies, but after dimensionality reduction, VMFs consistently improved AUCROCs of baseline models across all models, which underlines the need for appropriate feature dimensionality reduction, model calibration, and model selection. It is, however, important to consider that absolute gains were generally modest, and NRI/IDI analyses showed positive values for basic comorbidity comparisons but not for the extended comorbidity feature set. Analysis of feature importances revealed vascular width, vessel density, and tortuosity metrics as the most prediction driving features, alongside known clinical risk factors. Nonlinear patterns, threshold effects, and interaction effects with age, sex, and DM were observed and need to be considered and investigated further.

Numerous papers have associated retinal microvasculature and morphological changes with CVD. Classic CVD-associated findings such as arteriolar narrowing, venular widening, arteriovenous nicking, or retinal hemorrhages are visible even in fundus examinations [16, 17, 34, 35]. Building on these several studies applied ML or DL methods to retinal images and features for disease prediction [20, 36–41]. For example, Poplin et al. were among the first to develop DL-based prediction models for CVD risk factors and major cardiovascular events from retinal fundus photographs [19]. More recently, larger-scaled risk prediction models such as AutoPrognosis or RETFound were trained on extensive datasets and achieved AUCs of up to 0.774 for general CVD risk prediction (AutoPrognosis) and 0.737 for myocardial infarction prediction (RETFound) [21, 24]. Despite their large scale, those studies also had limitations such as noncontemporaneous end points, as cardiovascular status was evaluated retrospectively and not examined at the time the retinal images were acquired. Moreover, retrospective datasets from large biobanks have the disadvantage that patients with systemic diseases had eye examinations mostly performed owing to eye diseases and not in the course of a controlled study setting [42].

The specific association of the retina with CAD specifically is a more recent research focus. Several optical coherence tomography angiography (OCT-A)- and CFP-based studies have indicated a correlation between retinal microvasculature and CAD [43–45]. However, most artificial intelligence (AI)-based studies using CFP images either showed modest predictive accuracies, very small cohort sizes, limited model robustness and explainability, or they relied on indirect end points such as calculated risk scores or proxy metrics such as coronary artery calcium (CAC) [38, 44]. Huang et al. achieved a sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and AUC of 0.711, 0.697, 0.704, and 0.739, respectively, while relying on CAC scores as ground truth [20]. Arnould et al. [46] performed a pilot study assessing the predictive performance of three cardiovascular scores using metrics extracted from OCT-A and CFP images with SIVA. Their study, however, was limited by 144 patients, highly imbalanced data, and absent external- or cross-validation.

To quantitatively analyze CFP images, accurate extraction of retinal features is required. Earlier approaches are sometimes not open-source available and involved either manual annotation or semi-automated software (e.g. IVAN, QUARTZ, ARIAS, VAMPIRE or SIVA) [47–53] which is time-intensive and not completely standardized or scalable. With Automorph, Zhou et al. introduced a more automated open-source pipeline for quality grading, segmentation, and morphologic vascular feature extraction [18]. It was reported that the features showed good-to-excellent agreement with expert annotations and other feature extractors [18, 54]. A study by Giesser et al., however, which assessed the intra- and inter-visit retest reliability, reported a significant amount of volatility for some of the features [55]. Nevertheless, to our knowledge, Automorph represents currently the most comprehensive, standardized, and scalable open-source pipeline. In this study, all images were processed under identical conditions; therefore, the interpretation of the results relies on internally consistent relative comparisons within the cohort. Absolute comparability across different pipelines or devices cannot be ensured but is also not required for the current analysis. Its assessment may be explored in future works.

Despite their ability to capture hidden patterns and their occasionally superior performance, diagnostic DL models offer only limited interpretability and lower transparency compared with basic statistical and ML models [22, 23]. This makes their decision process less predictable and, therefore, problematic for clinical application. Thus, it is important to identify prediction driving features and test their predictive abilities and limitations, which we aimed to do in this work. When interpreting our results, it is evident that feature selection and dimensionality reduction lead to a performance boost in all configurations. By contrast, when raw VMF features were added to clinical baselines, the performance worsened in some cases owing to high dimensionality and intercorrelations. VMF alone demonstrated modest performance, which was comparable to models using age and sex alone. After dimensionality reduction (dr), however, their addition to clinical baselines lead to consistent performance gains and calibration improvements across all metrics, whereby ExtCI + VMF-PCA yielded the best performance with AUROC 0.775, AP 0.752, and Brier 0.203. Ensemble strategies further improved and overall stabilized the results, but only marginally, indicating that preprocessing and dr were more important than later model fusion. Other externally or cross-validated ML- or DL-based studies reported similar prediction performances [19–21, 24]. NRI/IDI analyses showed generally positive values for addition of VMFs to demographic or basic clinical information, while the reclassification and discrimination benefit was not observed for extended clinical baselines. In exploratory analyses excluding healthy control patients (Supplementary Material: Supplementary Table S8–S10), the aforementioned consistency could not be observed, and more mixed results were found overall, without clear superiority of models including VMFs, particularly in settings using the ExtCI baseline. Similarly, NRI/IDI analyses showed predominantly positive values for comparisons with age + sex and BsCI models, but not for ExtCI-based models. These findings question the incremental predictive benefit of CFP-derived VMFs in this specific high-comorbidity cohort. However, it should be considered that this represents a substantially more challenging classification task, as discrimination was performed within a high comorbidity population without clear negative baseline cases (healthy controls), while simultaneously reducing the sample size. Therefore, larger cohorts will be necessary to allow a reliable and adequately powered assessment in this setting. Consequently, these analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and were reported for transparency and completeness.

The observed top SHAP features are well in line with prior findings regarding systemic risk factors for CAD, as well as with studies reporting retinal microvascular changes as arteriolar narrowing, venous widening, or increased tortuosity [13, 19, 21, 24, 36, 56, 57]. Female sex was protective below 65 years, and above 65 years, female patients showed slightly higher SHAP values compared with male patients, which might possibly be explained by hormonal influences [58–60]. Vessel calibers showed threshold and zone-specific results, where wider arteries appeared to be protective, and narrowing increased CAD risk, aligning with findings of other studies [19, 21, 24]. Interestingly, the protective effect of wider arteries was stronger in women while arterial widths in zone C had age-related interactions. Fractal dimension seemed to primarily reflect microvascular aging, while being independent of sex or diabetes. Higher central retinal artery vein equivalent (CRVE)s seem to be protective in some cases, which might be explained by adaptive remodeling under hemodynamic stress [61–63]. Vein average width displayed more complex associations and interactions, which highlights the importance of distinguishing zone specific and global measures as well as consideration of nonlinear relationships. Venous tortuosity was associated with increased risk (especially in men), while arterial tortuosity had only modest effects. These associations were not clearly modified by DM, indicating more general microvascular alterations. Generally, the observed interaction effects with sex suggest that some retinal metrics could possibly capture microvascular alterations that are underrepresented in conventional risk scores, which could help improve CAD risk stratification in women [12, 64, 65]. Our results highlight the importance of accounting for nonlinear relationships and potential thresholding effects in retinal features to capture their predictive value appropriately. Furthermore, the observed interactions and sex-specific alterations may need further exploration and, if proved correct, multimodal approaches and advanced statistical modeling will be necessary to capture the complex interactions and patterns present. Future studies may also examine which of those interaction effects are reproducible and which ones are rather to be interpreted as noise. A total of 33.54% of the cohort had a migration background (non-Austrian), indicating that the study population was not ethnically homogeneous. This may increase the generalizability of our findings compared with more ethnically homogeneous datasets; however, more detailed information on ethnicities is necessary for analyzing their exact impact on predictions.

This study had several limitations that need to be acknowledged. A major limitation is the absence of an external validation set, which limits the generalization of our findings, even though rigorous internal validation was performed. While the cohort size was relatively small for ML applications, the data quality was very high thanks to the rare study cohort, the careful data preprocessing, cleaning and medical expert review of the images, and the systemic health information which was combined with CA as same-visit ground truth. The cross-sectional design of our study prevents conclusions regarding temporal progression and further development of retinal changes. In addition, a limitation we noticed regarding Automorph, was the occasional failure of optic disc detection and segmentation, even in apparently good quality images. Subsequently, this led to missing vessel segmentation and feature calculation which was not satisfyingly fixable with manual disc annotations or workarounds (Fig. S1). This primarily reflects practical limitations and generalizability of current automated retinal vessel extraction algorithms. As missing values were handled using mean-imputation during training within the respective splits, it is expected to reduce feature variability toward the cohort average and is therefore intentionally chosen as a rather conservative approach to not overestimate effects or artificially boost performance. Since all images were acquired using the same device, it cannot be ensured that the calculated features and absolute values are comparable across other devices. However, this was not the aim of the present study, and the results are intended to be interpreted in a relative and within-cohort context.

Conclusions

Our work outlines both the potential and the limitations of CFP-based retinal metrics for CAD prediction. Our findings showed consistent and reproducible incremental predictive value of VMF when added to clinical baselines. The best settings yielded AUCs around 0.777, but the general absolute performance gains over baselines were modest. NRI/IDI analyses indicated a benefit over demographic and BsCl baseline but not ExtCl. However, the steady increase in predictive performance is encouraging, as it underlines the potential that the retina and its microvasculature hold for CVD prediction. Additionally, the interaction analysis showed that nonlinear patterns, threshold effects, and sex- or DM-related effects seem to influence predictive values and need further investigation. This indicates that retinal biomarkers probably cannot be analyzed in isolation as they reflect complex vascular processes, and these complexities need to be addressed adequately in prediction modeling.
