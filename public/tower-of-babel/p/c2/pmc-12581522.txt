Evaluation of food intake, malnutrition, growth and development in children with esophageal atresia: a pilot study from Turkey
Authors: Dilara Berşan Konyalıgil, Nevra Koç, Doğuş Çalışkan, Aysun Kara Uzun, Emrah Şenel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the relationships among nutritional intake, malnutrition, and growth and development in children under 2 years of age with esophageal atresia.MethodsA survey was administered to the parents of healthy children and children with esophageal atresia who were followed up at Ankara City Hospital Pediatric Surgery Polyclinic. Nutrition education was provided, a follow-up visit was scheduled three months later, and the assessments were repeated.ResultsThe study included 20 children with esophageal atresia and 40 controls under 2 years of age. Height-for-age, weight-for-age and head circumference measurements were significantly lower in children with esophageal atresia than in the control group (p < 0.05). At the second interview, triceps skinfold thickness for age and mid-upper arm circumference for age were also significantly lower in children with esophageal atresia compared to control group (p < 0.05). Statistically significant differences were observed in dietary and food consistency preferences, eating status, and swallowing function (p < 0.05). In addition, according to the Pediatric Eating Assessment Tool-10 and Screening Tool for Risk of Impaired Nutritional Status and Growth, a high prevalence of swallowing disorders and a moderate risk of malnutrition were identified in children with esophageal atresia (p < 0.05). At the end of follow-up, significant increases in weight-for-age and height-for-age z scores were observed over time in children with esophageal atresia (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the percentages meeting daily requirements for protein, carbohydrates and dietary fiber increased over time (p < 0.05). Swallowing symptoms improved over time in children with esophageal atresia, and their current dietary patterns remained significantly different from those of the control group (p < 0.05).ConclusionsAppropriate nutritional support delivered with a multidisciplinary approach and long-term follow-up can help children to achieve a growth rate appropriate for their peers.Keywords: Nutrition, Growth, Development, Malnutrition, Esophageal atresia

Introduction

Esophageal atresia (EA) with or without tracheoesophageal fistula (TEF) is a congenital condition characterized by an interruption of the esophagus [1].

It occurs approximately 1 in 3000–4000 live births, and although it can sometimes be detected during pregnancy, in most cases (over 90%) it is diagnosed after birth [2, 3]. Modern treatments involving advanced surgery, antimicrobial care, respiratory support, and specialized nutrition have increased survival rates to over 90%[2].

Surgical repair is typically performed early in life to preserve the esophagus; however, children with EA may experience complications such as anastomotic stenosis, anastomotic leakage, dysphagia, peptic esophagitis, gastric metaplasia, Barrett’s esophagus, esophageal dysmotility, upper respiratory tract infections, growth retardation, and most importantly, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) [2, 4, 5]. Long-term consequences include growth impairment and nutritional challenges, which can affect quality of life [6]. Postoperative nutritional support via tube feeding helps maintain growth until oral feeding becomes possible, although prolonged tube feeding may influence eating behaviors and mealtime routines [7, 8].

There is a limited number of studies investigating to prevent or manage abnormal feeding behaviors in children with EA [5]. In this context, the present study was designed to evaluate the relationships among nutrient intake, malnutrition, and growth and development in children aged 1 month to 2 years with EA, compared with a healthy control group. We hypothesized that food intake, malnutrition, growth and development would be interrelated in this patient population.

Although previous studies have examined feeding difficulties, nutritional status, and growth in children with EA, the available literature remains limited in scope and depth. Most existing research does not simultaneously assess nutrient intake, malnutrition risk, and growth parameters in the early postoperative period, nor does it include direct comparisons with healthy controls. Furthermore, evidence evaluating the potential influence of different surgical techniques on these outcomes in the first two years of life is scarce. This study seeks to address these gaps by providing a comprehensive early-life assessment in a well-defined EA patient cohort alongside matched healthy controls.

Methods

Twenty children with EA aged between 1 month and 2 years and forty healthy children were included in the study between October 2022 and May 2023. The EA patients were followed at the Ankara City Hospital Pediatric Surgery Polyclinic by the same pediatric surgeon who also performed their surgical operations. The healthy children were recruited from the Ankara City Hospital Healthy Children Polyclinic. Although the children were evaluated in different outpatient clinics, the same physician(s) conducted the assessments in both clinics throughout the study period. The healthy children were confirmed to have no acute or chronic illness, growth retardation, or developmental delay based on physical examination and medical history taken during their routine pediatric check-ups. Out of a total of 123 patients who underwent surgery and follow-up for esophageal atresia at our center, 23 met the specified age criteria; however, owing to refusal from two families and the death of one child due to neurological deficit and epileptic seizure during the study period, twenty children were ultimately included in the study. Children outside the specified age range, those whose parents did not consent to participate, and those with chronic disease (for the control group) were excluded from the study. Patients who had completed dilatation therapy for esophageal stricture following EA repair were included, whereas those still undergoing dilatation treatment were excluded.

Nutritional education was provided to parents of patients undergoing follow-up in the pediatric surgery clinic after esophageal atresia repair. This program, which was based on recommendations from the World Health Organization (WHO), focused on counselling for breastfeeding and complementary feeding for infants with EA. It provided guidance on the gradual introduction of new tastes and textures, feeding positions that facilitate swallowing, managing of feeding difficulties and the avoiding foods that are likely to cause obstruction [9, 10]. Complementary feeding is the process of introducing safe, age-appropriate, and nutritionally adequate solid or semi-solid foods alongside continued breastfeeding from 6 months of age, when breast milk alone is no longer sufficient to meet the infant’s energy and nutrient requirements. It should be timely, hygienically prepared, and responsive to the child’s appetite and developmental abilities, with gradual progression in texture and variety [10]. Anthropometric measurements, food consumption records, nutritional screening, and swallowing assessments were conducted through face-to-face interviews with the parents. The expected outcomes were improvements in anthropometric z-scores/percentiles and in the proportion of children meeting their daily energy and nutrient requirements during the three-month follow up.

The WHO defines malnutrition as a deficiency or excess of nutrient intake, an imbalance in essential nutrients or impaired nutrient utilization. Malnutrition manifests in four forms: wasting, stunting, underweight and micronutrient deficiencies. Stunting is a growth and development disorder that children experience due to malnutrition, repeated infections and inadequate psychosocial stimulation. Children whose height-for-age is −2 SDs below the median value of the WHO Child Growth Standards are defined as stunted. Wasting (weight-for-height) measures body mass for height and describes current nutritional status. Underweight (weight-for-age) reflects a child’s body weight-for-age on a specific day. This indicator is used to assess whether a child is underweight or severely underweight [11]. All anthropometric measurements were evaluated and recorded according to age-specific WHO reference values ​​at each interview (z-scores, percentiles). Anthropometric measurements, including body weight (kg), height (cm), head circumference (HC) (cm), mid upper arm circumference (MUAC) (cm), and triceps skinfold thickness (TSF) (mm), were taken and recorded. Height-for-age, weight-for-age, and weight-for-height z scores and percentile values were calculated via the WHO 2006 Growth Standards [12]. HC, MUAC, and TSF z scores, as well as percentile values, were calculated via the 2007 WHO standards [13].

Data collection

To monitor nutrient intake, malnutrition status, and the growth and development of the children, the questionnaire evaluations were repeated after three months, and two face‒to-face interviews were conducted with the parents of both EA patients and healthy children (Fig. 1). Informed consent for participation was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants under the age of 16.

Flowchart showing study plan

The power analysis of the study was performed using the G*Power 3.1.9.7 statistical program. As a result of the power analysis, with α = 0.05, a 95% test power and an effect size of d = 1.109, the required sample size was calculated as 23 per group, and the total number of samples was determined to be 46 based on the two-tailed independent samples t test analysis [14].

The questionnaire consisted of six sections: general information about the child, feeding history, 24-hour dietary recall, swallowing assessment, Pediatric Eating Assessment Tool-10 (PEDI-EAT-10), and STRONGkids (Screening Tool for Risk of Impaired Nutritional Status and Growth) [15, 16]. The PEDI-EAT-10 is a scale that can be used to evaluate swallowing in pediatric patients. According to the ten-question scale, high PEDI-EAT-10 scores (4 and above) indicate a greater risk of swallowing disorders [17, 18]. STRONGkids is a pediatric nutrition screening method that consists of 4 questions, with scores interpreted as follows: 4–5 points, high risk of malnutrition; 1–3 points, moderate risk of malnutrition; and 0 points, low risk of malnutrition [16, 19]. Permissions for the use of these tools were obtained prior to the commencement of the study. In addition, anthropometric data were collected for all children included in the study.

Both groups received the same questionnaire, with the disease-related sections left blank in the healthy control group. In addition, the files of the children were retrospectively evaluated, and information regarding maternal age, type of EA, surgical method, date of operation, and postoperative nutritional support was obtained from hospital information systems’ electronic data. At the center, postoperative total parenteral nutrition (TPN) is routinely initiated in all patients following EA repair; these data were retrieved retrospectively for each patient. According to the hospital protocol, on postoperative day 5, an esophagogram is performed to evaluate anastomotic integrity. In the absence of leakage, enteral feeding is initiated with breast milk, or, if unavailable, age-appropriate infant formula, and TPN is continued until full oral feeding is achieved. In this study, “enteral products” referred to breast milk in neonates and age-appropriate formula in older infants when breast milk was unavailable.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained through questionnaires and measurements were statistically analyzed via IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 for Windows [20]. BEBİS version 8.2 software (Ebispro, Stuttgart, Germany) was used to analyze data from food consumption records [21]. The mean (x̄), standard deviation (s), and lower and upper values were calculated for the data obtained from the individuals participating in the study. The distribution of the qualitative data was presented as numbers and percentages. The independent samples t test was used for hypothesis testing of independent paired groups with a normal distribution, and the Mann‒Whitney U test was used for nonparametric hypotheses that were not normally distributed. The paired samples t test was used for hypothesis testing of normally distributed dependent dyadic groups, and the Wilcoxon test was used for nonparametric hypotheses that were not normally distributed. Fisher’s exact chi-square test was used to compare independent groups. The significance level was determined as p < 0.05.

Results

Among the children with EA who participated in the study, 90% had EA with distal tracheoesophageal fistula, 5% had EA with proximal tracheoesophageal fistula, and 5% had EA without fistula. Among children with EA, 60% underwent thoracoscopic (closed) surgery, and 40% underwent thoracotomy (open) surgery. The average time between birth and surgery was 3.6 ± 7.01 days. The early postoperative complication in these patients was anastomotic leakage in eight cases, which closed spontaneously without the need for additional surgical intervention. Anastomotic stricture was observed in 4 patients (25%), emerging no earlier than the third postoperative week and requiring at least one session of esophageal dilatation. Three of these patients underwent two sessions, and one underwent three sessions of dilatation, after which no additional surgical intervention was necessary. The total hospitalization period of the children with EA who participated in the study was 50.6 ± 33.44 days. In the EA group, 65% of children and 32.5% of children in the control group consumed infant formula. Complementary feeding was initiated at 6,8 ± 1,77 months in the EA group and at 5,8 ± 0,43 months in the control group, with the difference being statistically significant. PEDI-EAT-10 scores were significantly higher in children with EA than in healthy controls at both the first and second interviews (p < 0.05), with 15% of children with EA scoring ≥ 4 and all healthy controls scoring < 4 in both assessments. STRONGkids scores also differed significantly between groups (p < 0.05), with all children with EA classified as being at moderate risk of malnutrition and all healthy controls classified as being at low risk. No changes in either score were observed between the two interviews.

A comparison of some values of the children with EA over time is given in Table 5. When the z scores of the anthropometric measurements of the children with EA were compared, the weight-for-age z score increased significantly in the second interview (−0.07 ± 1.28) compared with the first interview (−0.3 ± 1.46) (p < 0.05). The weight-for-height score increased significantly in the second interview (0.1 ± 1.35) compared with the first interview (−0.09 ± 1.56) (p < 0.05). Height-for-age and HC z-scores were found to be similar in children with EA in both interviews (p > 0.05). The PEDI-EAT-10 score and STRONGkids score of the children with EA did not change over time (p > 0.05). When the percentages of children with EA meeting the daily requirements of the Turkish Nutrition Guide 2022 were compared, the percentages of those meeting the requirements for protein, carbohydrate, dietary fiber, thiamine, niacin, folate and iron intake were significantly greater at the second interview than at the first interview (p< 0.05)[22].

Discussion

EA, with or without TEF, is a congenital malformation characterized by an interruption of the esophagus. Growth monitoring and timely nutritional interventions are crucial for achieving age-appropriate growth in patients with EA. Children with EA often experience nutritional challenges due to esophageal dysmotility, GERD, and dysphagia, which can lead to malnutrition and delayed growth and development. This study aimed to investigate the relationships among food intake, malnutrition, and growth and development following thoracoscopic (closed) or thoracotomy (open) esophageal repair in children with EA under the age of two years. The study included 20 children with EA and 40 healthy controls, marking the first pilot study on this subject conducted at our center.

Esophageal atresia is known to be associated with low parity and low birth weight [23]. In our study, the mean gestational age at birth of children with EA was 35.2 ± 3.31 weeks, which was significantly lower than that of healthy children. In addition, the mean birth weight of children with EA was 2278.0 ± 711.92 g, which was significantly lower than that of children in the healthy control group. Our patient group had rates of gestational age and birth weight similar to those reported in the literature. Basuguy et al. retrospectively followed 98 children with EA between 2013 and 2018 and reported that the mean gestational age at birth was 35.4 weeks and that the mean birth weight was 2.41 kg [24]. It is known that 20–30% of children with EA are preterm, with a birth weight of less than 2000 g [25]. Birth weight is one of the most important prognostic factors for EA. In addition, many studies have reported that prematurity is among the factors that negatively affect the health-related quality of life (HRQOL) of patients who have undergone surgery for EA [26, 27].

In this study, compared with healthy controls, children in the patient group had significantly lower z scores and percentile values for height-for-age, weight-for-age, HC, and MUAC, as well as TSF percentile values. There was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of weight-for-height. Menzies et al. reported that 18% of the 74 children with EA had weight-for-age z scores less than − 2, whereas 9% had height-for-age z scores less than − 2[28]. Puntis et al. reported that 32% of children with EA had severe growth retardation relative to the normal population norms in both height-for-age and weight-for-height. Anthropometric assessments revealed that approximately one-third of patients exhibited growth retardation [29]. EA can cause malnutrition, swallowing difficulty, and other digestive issues in children. As a result, children with EA may have lower anthropometric measurements than healthy children do.

In this study, EA patients were found to have the most difficulty swallowing solid foods in both interviews, and the difference in difficulty was statistically significant compared with the healthy control group. In the first interview, 60% of the parents in the EA group and 50% in the second interview stated that they used strategies such as crushing and blending to help their children eat more easily. Significant differences in dietary patterns, foods that are difficult to consume, and methods used to facilitate eating in children with EA have important clinical implications, as feeding difficulties may reduce dietary diversity, limit nutrient intake, and delay progression to age-appropriate textures, ultimately affecting growth and development. In a survey of 128 parents of children with EA, 68% reported that their children experienced with feeding difficulties such as pain while eating, vomiting food and avoiding hard foods. In 38% of patients, recurrent episodes of food entrapment in the esophagus lasted only a few years, whereas 32% of patients never experienced swallowing problems. Apples, meat, and bread were the most common foods to become stuck [30]. In this study, the likelihood of coughing and choking during or after meals was significantly greater in children with EA than in healthy controls in both the first and second interviews. Vomiting and pain while swallowing were significantly greater only in the second interview. We believe that this occurred because more children had transitioned to solid food during the second interview, three months later. Compared with the healthy control group, it took significantly longer to feed children with EA. In a study of 124 children with repaired EA, Puntis et al. compared feeding difficulties with those of healthy controls and reported that children with EA were significantly more likely to eat slowly, cough or choke during meals, and vomit during meals [29]. In a study of 128 children with EA, 32% of patients never experienced feeding difficulties. Most of the remaining 68% experienced pain, regurgitation, and vomiting [30]. There may be more than one cause of feeding difficulties in patients who have undergone surgery for EA. Possible causes include esophageal stricture, esophageal dysmotility, GERD, esophagitis, vocal cord paralysis, laryngeal cleft, or feeding immaturity of preterm neonates [31]. Children with EA received parenteral nutrition for a mean of 20.1 ± 12.24 days following surgery. In our center, the relatively long duration of postoperative parenteral nutrition observed in this cohort was due to the protocol of continuing TPN until patients were able to tolerate full oral feeding.

In this study, when the values of the children with EA in the first and second interviews were compared, the weight-for-age and weight-for-height z scores in the second interview increased significantly compared with those in the first interview (p < 0.05). In the study by Lacher et al. body weight was below the 25th percentile in 70% of patients at the age of 1 year, whereas this percentage decreased to 41% at the age of 10 years [32]. Similarly, Little et al. reported improvements in body weight and height with age; the percentage of children whose body weight was below the 25th percentile was 49% at the age of 5 years, whereas this percentage decreased to 31% at the age of 10 years [33]. Furthermore, we believe that the significant increase in anthropometric measurements of children with EA over the three-month period between the first and second interviews can be attributed to families receiving nutrition education from a dietitian and becoming more aware of growth, development, and nutrition. No specific intervention was provided to the children during the study. However, changes in anthropometric measurements and percentages of patients meeting daily requirements may have been observed as a result of monitoring by a clinical nutritionist.

Our findings are consistent with recent studies reporting persistent feeding difficulties, altered food intake, and growth challenges in children with EA [34, 35]. Birketvedt et al. demonstrated that a considerable proportion of children with EA experience ongoing feeding problems, which may impact growth [34]. Similarly, Aydöner et al. evaluated mid- and long-term quality of life in patients operated on for EA and found that feeding-related issues can persist [35]. These results emphasize the importance of long-term follow-up and larger-scale studies to improve our understanding of nutritional and developmental outcomes in this population.

The limitations of this study include that the anthropometric measurements, nutritional intake, and malnutrition status of the children before surgery were not evaluated. This was a cross-sectional study. In addition, information obtained retrospectively from patient files relied on the accuracy of hospital records. Another limitation is that malnutrition was assessed only by anthropometric parameters and the STRONGkids tool, without biochemical or body composition data, so the results should be interpreted with caution. Another limitation is that weight and other anthropometric parameters were assessed according to chronological age without adjustment for prematurity. Given that the prevalence of prematurity was higher in the EA group than in the healthy controls, this factor may have influenced growth outcomes. Therefore, the observed differences in anthropometric z-scores (height-for-age, weight-for-age, HC, and MUAC) could be at least partially attributable to the higher prematurity rate in the EA group. In addition, longer hospital stays and prolonged postoperative parenteral nutrition may have limited weight gain independently of feeding difficulties or other nutritional issues. The most significant aspect of our study is the high percentage of the control group. In addition, it is performed in one of the rare centers where children with EA are treated via the thoracoscopic (closed) surgical esophageal repair method. Esophageal atresia is a congenital esophageal anomaly with an incidence of 1/3000–4000[3]. Therefore, studying 20 children with EA during our 2-year study period is important. There are not enough studies in the literature that evaluate the consumption status of formula food, information about the initiation of complementary feeding, and information about the method used to facilitate comfortable food consumption in children with EA. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate this information in children with EA.

Conclusion

Our findings revealed differences in food intake, malnutrition, and growth and development between children with EA and healthy controls. Evaluating anthropometric measurements, nutritional intake and malnutrition status in children with EA before surgery, after surgery and at discharge would provide a better understanding of the factors affecting growth and development. Prospective investigations of childhood outcomes should be conducted to better understand the impact of EA on nutritional intake, malnutrition, and growth and development. Future studies should include longer-term follow-up with a larger number of patients.
