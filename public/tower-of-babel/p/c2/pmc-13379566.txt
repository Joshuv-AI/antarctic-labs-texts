Cost Effectiveness of the Adjuvanted Recombinant Zoster Vaccine in Older Adults in Portugal
Authors: Eleftherios Zarkadoulas, Fábio Silva, Joana Alves, Francisco Madeira, Maarten J Postma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesHerpes zoster (HZ) and associated complications comprise a considerable public health burden in older adults. This study aimed to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV) versus no vaccine for the prevention of HZ in older adults in Portugal.MethodsA static multicohort Markov model (ZONA) was adapted to the Portuguese context. The base-case compared RZV with no vaccination in a hypothetical cohort of 1 million adults aged ≥65 years. Real-world data were used for first-dose coverage (56.1%) and second-dose completion (67.2%), with vaccine efficacy and waning based on 11-year follow-up data. Outcomes were calculated over a lifetime horizon from the societal perspective, with a 4% discount applied to costs and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). In the base-case, HZ incidence was based on previously reported European data. Sensitivity analyses were performed, and a scenario analysis explored the impact of alternative HZ incidence rates based on recent Portuguese data.ResultsOver a lifetime horizon, RZV was estimated to prevent 45,551 HZ cases, 9056 post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN) cases, 5580 cases with other non-PHN complications, and 6 HZ deaths. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) from the societal perspective was €48,707 per QALY gained, below a hypothetical willingness-to-pay threshold of two times the gross domestic product per capita per QALY gained (equivalent to €62,200). This base-case ICER was most sensitive to the annual incidence of initial HZ. In the scenario analysis, exploring higher HZ incidence based on Portuguese data, the estimated ICER was €41,877 per QALY gained. In both the base-case and the scenario analyses, ICERs were lowest in individuals aged 65–69 years, with ICERs ranging from €36,207 to €42,700 per QALY gained, in the scenario analysis and base case, respectively.ConclusionsRZV presents a cost-effective option for the prevention of HZ and its complications in Portuguese adults aged ≥ 65 years, with greater cost effectiveness in those aged 65–69 years.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s41669-026-00640-3.

Key Points for Decision Makers

This analysis suggests that herpes zoster (HZ) and its complications represent a significant health burden in older Portuguese adults. Costs for vaccination with recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV) were partially offset by substantial reductions in HZ and related complications and corresponding direct and indirect costs.From the societal perspective, RVZ vaccination was a cost-effective intervention compared with no vaccination in Portuguese adults aged ≥ 65 years. Earlier vaccination (i.e., in individuals aged 65–69 years) resulted in the most favorable ICERs.

Introduction

Herpes zoster (HZ) is a painful, debilitating condition that follows reactivation of latent varicella zoster virus (VZV) within dorsal root or cranial nerve ganglia [1, 2]. HZ typically presents as a unilateral blistering rash involving one or more dermatomes, lasting for 2–4 weeks [3]. In the acute phase, up to 10% of patients may develop serious complications, including severe neurological complications (e.g., meningitis, encephalitis) or more widespread disseminated disease, ocular complications (HZ ophthalmicus, HZO), or cutaneous complications (e.g., secondary bacterial infection) [4, 5]. Beyond the acute phase, 5–30% of individuals will experience post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN), often defined as chronic persistent pain lasting for more than 90 days after initial rash onset, and which may persist for many months or years [4–6]. Up to 10% of individuals experience HZ recurrence in the first decade after the initial HZ episode [7, 8].

HZ is common, with an estimated lifetime risk of developing HZ of 30% [4]. HZ risk increases with increasing age, associated with an age-related decline in immunity [9]. Risk is far higher in individuals aged ≥ 50 years [9, 10], and also in younger immunocompromised individuals (due to disease or if receiving immunosuppressant therapy), and those with chronic medical conditions [11–13]. Complications such as PHN are also more frequent in older individuals and in younger at-risk adults [4, 6]. In Europe, a global review has estimated the overall incidence of HZ to range from approximately 5.1 per 1000 patient years in individuals aged 50–54 years to 10.4 per 1000 patient years in individuals aged ≥ 80 years [14].

HZ and allied complications comprise a substantial economic burden [15, 16] and severely impact patients’ quality of life [17, 18]. Protective vaccination can help alleviate the clinical burden and reduce direct medical costs and broader societal costs. In Europe, two vaccines for the prevention of HZ have been or are currently used in various jurisdictions and settings. Zoster vaccine live (ZVL), a live attenuated vaccine for use in adults aged ≥ 50 years, was previously widely available, although contraindicated in individuals who are immunocompromised [19]. However, ZVL is no longer available in a number of countries, and since June 2025 ZVL is now unavailable in Europe [20, 21]. Furthermore, in Portugal ZVL was never adopted by the National Vaccination Program (NVP) [20]. An adjuvanted recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV; Shingrix, GSK), has been developed as a non-live vaccine, given in a two-dose schedule. RZV comprises recombinant VZV glycoprotein E in combination with the AS01B adjuvant system to provide stronger and more sustained immune responses [22–24]. AS01B is an adjuvant system containing 3-O-desacyl-4′-monophosphoryl lipid A (MPL), a saponin extracted from the bark of the Quillaja saponaria Molina tree (QS-21) and liposome (MPL 50 µg and QS-21 50 µg) [25].

Large-scale placebo-controlled trials (ZOE-50 and ZOE-70) have demonstrated high vaccine efficacy (VE against HZ; 97.2% in adults ≥ 50 years and 91.3% in those ≥ 70 years [26, 27], and with sustained efficacy for up to 11 years post-vaccination, where in year 11 efficacy against HZ in those aged ≥ 60 years was 79.4%, and 72.0% in those aged ≥ 70 years) [28]. RZV has been widely authorized across Europe, the Americas, and the Asia–Pacific region for the prevention of HZ and PHN in adults aged ≥ 50 years, and also for those adults aged ≥ 18 years at increased risk of HZ [29, 30]. While RZV is available for all adults aged ≥ 50 years in many countries, government funding for national immunization programs often adopts recommendations based on specific age groups [20, 29]. For example, in Germany, RZV is recommended for all individuals aged ≥ 60 years [31], while in Spain, RZV is offered to individuals aged 65 years, along with catch-up strategies targeting specific older age groups, for example, initially those aged 80 years [32].

In Portugal, RZV has been available for use since April 2022. RZV is recommended for all adults aged ≥ 50 years by the Portuguese Society of Internal Medicine and the Portuguese Association of General and Family Medicine [20, 33], and also by specialist societies such as the Portuguese Society of Endocrinology, Diabetes, and Metabolism for use in individuals with diabetes mellitus [34]. However, at the time of writing, RZV is not yet included within the Portuguese NVP, and so while use is approved and recommended for adults aged ≥ 50 years, there is no funding support or national policy [20, 33]. In addition, information on the potential public health benefits of RZV in Portugal is limited. The objective of the present study was to evaluate the potential clinical benefits and cost effectiveness of RZV from the societal perspective compared with a non-vaccination strategy in older Portuguese adults aged ≥ 65 years, to help understand such benefits and inform policy decision making.

Methods

Model Overview

The cost-effectiveness analyses were conducted using a static multicohort Markov model (ZOster ecoNomic Analysis, ZONA) developed in Microsoft Excel and adapted for the Portuguese setting (Fig. 1). This model has been described in great detail in a number of previous publications evaluating the public health impact and cost effectiveness of RZV for older adults in various countries [32, 35–48]. A static model was developed as it is generally recognized that there would be limited indirect effects of RZV on HZ epidemiology per se, with no herd immunity. Other modeling studies also adopt this approach [48–50]. The core ZONA model considers adults aged ≥ 50 years stratified into hypothetical age groups (e.g., 50–59, 60–64, 65–69, 70–79, and ≥ 80 years) and follows all adults within each age group over their lifetime horizon, utilizing annual cycles. The model captures key disease transition events and clinical outcomes associated with HZ, accounting for changes in risk for HZ and complications over time, and waning of vaccine efficacy. It adopts 1-year cycles as many of the input variables in the supporting literature are presented on an annual basis. Individuals can transition between various health states: no HZ, HZ, PHN, other non-PHN complications, recovery, and natural and HZ-related death. The model also accounts for the possibility of HZ recurrence.

In the present analysis, we only considered adults aged ≥ 65 years, stratified into three age groups: 65–69, 70–79, and ≥ 80 years. Each age cohort was considered under two strategies: no vaccination or vaccination with RZV. Model reporting was conducted in accordance with Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) [51]. This analysis did not involve human participants, and ethical approval for the development of the model was not required.

Population

Demographic inputs were based on population estimates for the mainland resident Portuguese population in 2023 [52]. The study population was a hypothetical target population of 1,000,000 adults aged ≥ 65 years stratified in proportion to the national demographic distribution into three age groups: 65–69 (n = 265,079), 70–79 (n = 443,470), and ≥ 80 years (n = 291,451). To accommodate the study horizon, age-specific all-cause mortality probabilities were progressively applied across the age cohorts over their remaining lifetime. All-cause mortality data were derived from national mortality statistics [53] (electronic supplementary material [ESM] 1: Online Resource Table 1).

Epidemiology

Epidemiological inputs are shown in Table 1. For the base-case, age-stratified annual probabilities for HZ were derived from European data reported in a comprehensive analysis of incidence rates by Curran et al. [14], as used in a recent global analysis [54]. HZ incidence increased with age, estimated to be 7.9 cases per 1000 patient years in those aged 65–69 years and 10.4 cases per 1000 patient years in those aged ≥ 80 years [14, 54]. For the scenario analysis, HZ incidence rates were derived from a recently reported burden-of-disease study conducted in the Portuguese population aged ≥ 50 years, with HZ incidence ranging from 8.9 cases per 1000 patient years in those aged 65–69 years to 11.3 per 1000 in those aged ≥ 80 years [55]. The proportion of individuals in specific age groups developing PHN was based on that reported by Hillebrand et al. [56], as used in previous health impact and cost-effectiveness evaluations for Germany [35, 39, 42]. The proportion of HZ cases developing other non-PHN complications (ophthalmic, neurological, cutaneous and other, non-pain-related complications) were based on estimates reported by Horn et al. for Germany [57], as also used in previous economic analyses [35, 39, 42]. The model assumed that these complication rates would apply uniformly across all age groups. For HZ mortality, as Portuguese national data are limited with respect to mortality in specific age strata, the model assumed similar HZ case fatality rates as reported for Spain and as used in a recent Spanish analysis [32, 58] (see ESM 1: Online Resource Table 1). The incidence of recurrent HZ was assumed to be the same as that for initial HZ, based on reporting by Yawn et al. [7], and as assumed in other evaluations [36, 38, 39, 46]. The model also assumed that recurrent HZ cases would be at the same risk of developing PHN and other complications as that associated with initial HZ episodes.

Vaccine Efficacy

Vaccine-specific inputs are shown in Table 1. The model assumed RZV vaccine coverage based upon real-world data. For the first RZV dose, in the absence of local data for RZV coverage, the model assumed 56.09% vaccine uptake, based on the reported average national uptake of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccine in mainland Portugal for all individuals aged ≥ 60 years between September 29, 2023 and March 17, 2024 [59]. For series completion, in the absence of Portuguese data, the model considered second-dose compliance of 67.2%, based on real-world data for RZV series completion rates in the United States [60]. The model assumed the second dose would be given after 2 months.

Vaccine efficacy inputs for individuals receiving two RZV doses were based upon the latest long-term follow-up data from individuals within the ZOE-50 and ZOE-70 studies, (each, randomized, placebo-controlled studies involving adults aged ≥ 50 and ≥ 70 years of age, respectively) reporting on data for up to 11 years post-initial vaccination [28]. As reported elsewhere, from these data, age-stratified initial efficacy rates and annual waning rates were derived using logistic regression [54]. Initial efficacy against HZ in individuals aged 65–69 years was estimated to be 97.8% (95% confidence interval [CI] 94.0–100.0), with annual waning of 1.2% (95% CI 0.3–2.3). For individuals aged ≥ 70 years, initial vaccine efficacy was 99.4% (95% CI 89.7–100.0), with annual waning of 3.1% (95% CI 1.5–5.0) [54]. Vaccine efficacy for those receiving only one dose of RZV was based on data derived from a post-hoc analysis of the ZOE-50/70 phase III studies, by Curran et al. [35]. One-dose RZV efficacy against HZ was 90.1% (95% CI 58.9–98.9) in individuals aged 65–69 years and 69.5% (95% CI 24.9–89.1) in individuals aged ≥ 70 years [35]. The model assumed annual efficacy waning for a single RZV dose of 5.4% (95% CI 1.0–7.4) over the first 4 years post-vaccination and 5.1% (95% CI 3.6–6.9) for all subsequent years (applicable to all age groups), as previously reported by Curran et al. [35] and used in previous public health impact and cost-effectiveness analyses [36, 38, 40, 42, 46]. As there were very few breakthrough PHN cases observed in the pivotal ZOE-50/70 studies [61, 62], the model assumed similar efficacies for RZV against PHN, and with similar rates of efficacy waning.

The model also accounted for adverse events (AEs) associated with RZV vaccination (most frequently local reactions and fever, unspecified allergy, and syncope) [63, 64]. As used in previous RZV cost-effectiveness studies [36, 39, 42], vaccine-related AEs were divided into four categories: local/general AEs not requiring medical attention, AEs leading to a general practitioner (GP) visit, those leading to an emergency room (ER) visit, and AEs requiring hospitalization, with relative frequencies based upon solicited and unsolicited events reported in the ZOE-50/70 studies [61, 62].

Cost Inputs

The model considered both direct and indirect costs from the societal perspective (Table 2). All costs are in 2024 euros (€). Direct costs were valued through approved prices for outpatient and hospital services set within the Portuguese National Health Service (NHS), as laid out in Ordinance 207/2017 [65] and Ordinance No. 254/2018 [66]. These costs continued to apply in 2024, as no further legislation was issued thereafter. Outpatient resource use for HZ, PHN, and other complications was informed by outputs from an advisory panel of national experts from across Portugal with clinical experience in managing HZ (five dermatologists, two internal medicine physicians and one infectious disease specialist). Each provided information on the typical healthcare resources used (outpatient consultations, diagnostic tests, antiviral medication prescriptions, medication use) and associated related costs based on approved prices [65, 66], from which an average cost was obtained.

Hospital resource use was derived from a recent burden-of-disease study conducted in Portuguese hospitals evaluating disease costs for HZ, PHN, and other complications in hospitalized cases [67]. Costings were weighted to accommodate the proportion of patients utilizing each resource, then aggregated to generate average costs per case: HZ without complications, HZ with PHN, and HZ with other non-PHN complications (Table 2). The mean total direct costs per HZ case without any complications ranged from €154.19 in individuals aged 65–69 years to €374.94 in individuals aged ≥ 80 years. For HZ with PHN, costs ranged from €681.27 to €2059.80 per case. The mean cost per case for other complications ranged from €1555.46 for cases with ophthalmic complications to €8399.47 for those with neurological complications other than PHN.

Direct vaccination costs included vaccine acquisition costs, administration costs, and costs per vaccine-related AE. The cost per RZV dose (€151.68) was based on its wholesale acquisition cost [68], with administration costs (€3.90 per dose) as per Ordinance No. 254/2018 [66]. For vaccine-related AEs, it was assumed that no resources were required for local/general AEs. For those requiring a GP visit, the assigned cost was €31.0, based on the cost of an (external) consultation in Portuguese NHS institutions [66]. For those AEs requiring emergency services, a cost of €94.41 was assigned, using a weighted average of costs for an emergency episode in medical-surgical (€85.91) or polyvalent emergency services (€112.07) [65], with weighting based on the availability of emergency service provision. For AEs requiring hospitalization, the model assumed costs based on the cost of a hospital stay due to a severe allergic reaction, with an average duration of up to 3 days (€611.92) [65].

The model also considered the indirect costs of HZ (although restricted to work absenteeism losses). As Portuguese data are lacking, the model assumed absenteeism losses for HZ and PHN cases (likelihood to incur time lost due to work absence, and average number of absent days) based on German data as reported by Ultsch et al. [69], multiplied by average wage rates for Portugal (as of December 2023) [70], then adjusted for Portuguese age-specific employment percentage estimates [71] (Table 2).

Quality of Life

Quality-adjusted life year (QALY) losses were included in the model to account for the impact of HZ outcomes and vaccine-related AEs on quality of life. Age-specific utility baseline values were derived from normal values for the general Portuguese population [72]. In the absence of utility loss data for HZ in Portuguese adults, QALY losses associated with HZ and with PHN were based on data reported by Curran et al., for a cohort in the United Kingdom [73], with these values applied for unvaccinated individuals (Table 2). Data indicate that QALY losses associated with acute HZ in vaccinated individuals experiencing breakthrough HZ are lower than those incurred in unvaccinated individuals, with QALY losses in vaccinated individuals estimated to be 48.6% of those experienced in unvaccinated individuals [74]. As such, QALY losses due to acute HZ in vaccinated individuals were adjusted to reflect this. The model assumed, conservatively, that QALY losses associated with PHN in vaccinated individuals were the same as those in the unvaccinated cohort. QALY losses due to vaccine-related AEs were based on those reported by Le and Rothberg (which sourced values based on data from the United Kingdom) [75]. As described in that study, the model assumed that QALY losses due to AEs requiring an outpatient or ER visit were the same as those associated with a local/general reaction, while hospitalization incurred greater QALY loss.

Modeled Outcomes and Analyses

To estimate the public health impact, the model was used to calculate the number of HZ cases, PHN cases, non-PHN complications, and HZ-related deaths that accumulate over the lifetime horizon of each age group under each intervention strategy. The estimated number needed to vaccinate (NNV) to prevent one case of HZ was calculated by dividing the number of vaccinated individuals by the number of HZ cases avoided as a result of vaccination. The NNV to prevent one case of PHN was calculated in a similar manner.

Economic outcomes included vaccination costs and direct and indirect disease costs. An annual discount rate of 4% for costs and outcomes was applied, in line with Portuguese guidance for economic evaluations of health technologies [76]. All costs are expressed in euros (€), as of 2024. Modeled outcomes with and without vaccination were compared to estimate the incremental impact of RZV vaccination on cost and QALY outcomes; the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was calculated as the incremental cost per QALY gained. The cost-effectiveness analysis was conducted from a societal perspective. In Portugal, there is no established willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold to define cost effectiveness for health interventions, however some consider the cost-effectiveness threshold to be twice the gross domestic product (GDP) per capita or twice the gross national income per capita [77]. In the present analysis, we selected a hypothetical WTP threshold of two times the GDP per capita per QALY gained (equivalent to €62,200) [78].

Deterministic Sensitivity Analysis

Deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses (DSA and PSA, respectively) were conducted to evaluate the sensitivity of ICER results to the model’s input values and to explore uncertainty; these were conducted for the overall cohort aged ≥ 65 years. For the univariate DSA, lower and upper bounds of parameter variations were applied as ±20% of the mean value or based on 95% CIs or upper and lower values of source inputs. The results are summarized in tornado diagrams to highlight parameters that had the largest impact on ICERs.

Probabilistic Sensitivity Analysis

For the PSA, key input parameters were simultaneously and randomly varied (within their pre-defined range, utilizing the standard error) across 1000 Monte Carlo simulations: this was enough to reach stabilization. Most parameters were varied according to a beta distribution, except for costs (gamma distribution) and vaccine coverage rates (uniform distribution). The percentage of simulations with resultant ICERs falling within the WTP threshold was computed, with results presented as a cost-effectiveness scatter plot and acceptability curve.

Scenario Analysis

A scenario analysis was performed to explore outcomes using Portuguese HZ incidence rates, based on a recently reported epidemiology study as outlined above [55]. All other model input parameters were unchanged.

Results

Base-Case Analysis

For the overall modeled population (a cohort of 1 million adults aged ≥ 65 years), the model estimated that, in the absence of vaccination, there would be a total of 141,564 cases of HZ, 28,350 cases of PHN, and 17,342 cases with other non-PHN complications over their remaining lifetime, with 21 HZ-related deaths (Table 3). Compared with no vaccination, RZV would prevent 45,551 cases of HZ and 9056 cases of PHN, with 5580 fewer cases of complications and 6 HZ-related deaths avoided, with 2412 discounted QALYs gained (Table 3). The HZ disease burden without vaccination and with RZV in specific age-strata is shown in Table 4. This is reflected in the NNV outcomes (ESM 1: Online Resource Figure 1). For the overall cohort (all aged ≥ 65 years), over the lifetime horizon the NNV to prevent one HZ case was 13, while the NNV to prevent one PHN case was 62. This varied in different age strata; for those aged 65–69 years, the NNVs to prevent one HZ case and one PHN case were 10 and 50, respectively; in those aged 70–79 years, 13 and 60, respectively; and in those aged ≥ 80 years, 18 and 86, respectively.

Scenario Analysis

For the scenario utilizing alternative (higher) HZ incidence rates based on Portuguese data, the model estimated that without RZV vaccination, there would be 158,071 cases of HZ, 31,644 cases of PHN, and 19,364 cases with other complications in adults aged ≥ 65 years, over their remaining lifetime, with 23 HZ-related deaths (Table 5). RZV would prevent 51,132 HZ cases, 10,162 PHN cases, 6264 cases of other non-PHN complications, and 6 HZ-related deaths, compared with no vaccination, resulting in 2772 discounted QALYs gained (Table 5). Similar to the base-case analysis, the greatest impact was seen in the younger age group aged 65–69 years. For the overall cohort (all aged ≥ 65 years), over the lifetime horizon, the NNV to prevent one HZ and one PHN case were 11 and 56, respectively. This varied in different age strata; for those aged 65–69 years, the NNVs to prevent one HZ and one PHN case were 9 and 45, respectively; in those aged 70–79 years, 11 and 53, respectively; and in those aged ≥ 80 years, 17 and 79, respectively (ESM 1: Online Resource Figure 1). The overall net societal costs were approximately €114 million, with a resultant ICER from a societal perspective of €41,877 per QALY gained (Table 5). Disease and associated cost outcomes in specific age strata are shown in ESM 1: Online Resource Table 2. The resultant ICERs varied in different age strata, ranging from €36,207 per QALY gained in those aged 65–69 years to €55,742 in those aged ≥ 80 years (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Vaccination against HZ offers an opportunity to reduce the public health burden of HZ and its complications in older adults [20, 24]. At present, data are lacking regarding the potential benefits of HZ vaccination in the Portuguese setting. In the present study, we evaluated the public health impact and cost effectiveness of RZV in adults ≥ 65 years of age in Portugal, with vaccine coverage for the two-dose schedule set at 56.1% for the first dose (in line with recent estimates for COVID-19 vaccine uptake in older Portuguese adults) [59], and with series completion rates of 67.2%, based upon real-world data from the United States [60]. Vaccine efficacy rates and duration of protection against HZ were based on the most recent long-term efficacy data from the initial RZV registration studies [28]. For the base-case, the model estimated that in the absence of vaccination, for a hypothetical cohort of 1 million older adults aged ≥ 65 years, there would be over 141,000 cases of HZ over their lifetime, of which more than 45,000 would experience HZ-related complications: 28,350 cases with PHN and 17,342 cases with other complications. Use of RZV substantially reduced the HZ disease burden; it was estimated to prevent more than 45,000 HZ cases, over 9000 PHN cases, and 5580 cases with other, non-PHN complications over a lifetime horizon. This corresponds to an approximate reduction of 32% in each of these outcomes.

These benefits in disease reduction with RZV are broadly comparable to those observed in other populations when adopting a similar modeling approach and cohort population size. In Germany, assuming 40% first-dose coverage and 70% second-dose compliance in a cohort of 1 million individuals aged ≥ 65 years, RZV was estimated to avoid 60,735 cases of HZ over a lifetime horizon (and 11,700 cases of PHN) [42]. The slightly greater reduction in absolute case numbers in that study compared with the present study can be explained by the higher incidence rates of HZ in Germany [56] applied in German cost-effectiveness analyses [39, 42].

These estimated reductions in HZ and related complications were accompanied by substantial reductions in treatment costs (and with some indirect cost savings associated with reduced productivity loss). In the cost-effectiveness analysis, for the overall population aged ≥ 65 years, disease cost savings led to an estimated ICER from a societal perspective of €48,707 per QALY. Univariate sensitivity analysis showed that these results were most sensitive to RZV price and the annual incidence of initial HZ. Varying the RZV cost per dose (€151.68) by ±20% resulted in societal ICERs ranging from €36,921 to €60,494. The impact of incidence on outcomes was also evident in our scenario analysis, in which we used alternative, slightly higher HZ incidence rates derived from recently reported Portuguese data, and where the estimated numbers of cases of HZ, PHN, and other HZ complications in the absence of vaccination were higher, and with a greater number of HZ cases and other complications averted with RZV. Consequently, the corresponding ICER was lower, at €41,877 per QALY gained.

Another pattern was that the greatest impact of RZV on HZ outcomes was found in those aged 65–69 years, as evidenced by the lower NNVs and lower (more cost-effective) ICERs in this age group. Lower NNV values have been reported in younger age strata in various countries where RZV impact has been evaluated using the ZONA model [32, 39, 42, 44, 47], and lower ICERs in the younger age strata have also been found in German cost-effectiveness analysis models [39, 42]. This highlights the impact of earlier vaccination on HZ disease outcomes; although the incidence of HZ and of developing PHN increases with age, the sustained duration of protection in younger age groups, along with the greater life expectancy, contributes to greater estimated HZ disease reduction with RZV in those aged 65–69 years than that predicted in the older age strata. For our cost-effectiveness outcome in the overall cohort aged ≥ 65 years, the resultant ICERs were below a hypothetical WTP threshold of €62,200 (equivalent to two times the GDP per capita per QALY gained), both for the base case (€48,707 per QALY gained) and for the scenario (€41,877 per QALY gained). These also fall within other commonly used hypothetical WTP thresholds, for example, €50,000 per QALY gained as used in previous economic evaluations of RZV in Germany [42]. In the most recent German analysis, reporting modeling data from a 2021 perspective, ICERs for RZV were lower in younger age groups; the ICER for RZV vaccination of a cohort aged ≥ 60 years was €40,661 per QALY gained, increasing to €48,632 per QALY gained when considering an older cohort aged ≥ 70 years. That pattern was also evident in the present analysis, where the greater disease reductions in younger age groups resulted in lower and more cost effective ICERs; €42,700 per QALY gained in the base-case and €36,207 per QALY gained in our alternative scenario. This said, the estimated ICERs we found for older strata (e.g., those aged 70–79 years) were also below our WTP thresholds, with an ICER of €46,107 per QALY gained in the base case and with an even lower ICER in the scenario (€38,852 per QALY gained). It is worth noting that while the ICERs we report in the present study are somewhat higher than those reported for Germany, this is influenced by the greater HZ incidence assumed and the lower vaccine costs associated with the 2021 model [42], as well as the higher discounting rate (4%) used in the present analysis for Portugal.

The present study has some limitations. Firstly, while the model input values for epidemiology and clinical outcomes were based upon Portuguese data when available, for some parameters such data were lacking; in such instances we assumed values based on appropriate regional sources wherever possible. For example, for HZ epidemiology we chose to use age-specific data for the wider European population in our base-case analysis [14, 54], although we specifically examined Portuguese HZ incidence in the scenario analysis [55]. For risk of PHN and for other non-PHN complications we used rates reported from Germany [56, 57], and German data were also used for productivity loss rates [69] (although Portuguese wage losses were applied). For HZ case fatality rates, we used data from Spanish sources [58], while for QALY loss, we used data reported from the United Kingdom [73]. As RZV is not currently included in the Portuguese NVP, data for vaccine coverage for the first dose (56.09%) was based on that reported for COVID-19 vaccine uptake in older adults (as a proxy) [59], while second-dose compliance (67.2%) was based on data from the United States [60]. While these may be greater than that realized in the Portuguese setting, and may overestimate the magnitude of clinical benefits in terms of reduction in HZ disease burden and treatment costs, they would not be expected to have any notable impact on the cost-effectiveness outcomes. In the univariate DSA, varying first-dose coverage by ±20% does not change the resultant ICER, and varying second-dose coverage by ±20% had minimal impact. However, we did not specifically examine this in any scenario analysis, which may be considered a further limitation. In addition, when measuring the economic impact of HZ and RZV vaccination, for indirect costs, we only considered HZ-related productivity losses due to absenteeism from paid employment (including time lost due to PHN). Indirect costs accrued due to travel for medical care and any additional caregiver costs were not considered. In this respect, our analysis from an overall societal perspective could be considered limited, and to some extent represents a conservative assumption (and may underestimate indirect cost savings with RZV). However, we should also note that for the RZV vaccination strategy, we did not account for productivity loss due to vaccine administration or for vaccine-related AEs requiring medical attention, although the economic impact of these may be relatively low. Finally, as is common for many economic analyses, this was an industry-sponsored study. Although the model was developed by the funding organization, it did not influence the interpretation of the results. These limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings.

Nevertheless, our results suggest that HZ represents a substantial health burden in Portuguese adults aged ≥ 65 years, and highlights the potential value of RZV in reducing HZ-related morbidity in older adults. RZV is included within adult immunization programs in a number of European countries, including Spain, Italy, Germany, and the United Kingdom, although age-based restrictions often apply (e.g., for those aged ≥ 60 years in Germany [31], and those aged 65 years in Spain [32]). At present, although there would seem to be support from medical specialists [20, 33, 34], discussion on inclusion of RZV within the Portuguese NVP is ongoing. Our results in different age strata show that HZ disease reduction and QALY gains are greater in individuals aged 65–69 years with lower resultant ICERs than found in older vaccinated individuals. Although this may suggest that some priority could be given to this age group, one cannot discount the substantial disease burden in older individuals, who experience higher rates of HZ and PHN. As such, we would consider our findings to be supportive of a broader policy for individuals aged ≥ 65 years. These findings can inform decision making regarding inclusion of RZV within the Portuguese NVP for the prevention of HZ and PHN in older adults.

A plain language summary of this article, outlining the relevance and results of this study, and its potential impact on policy, is available as a Supplementary Figure (ESM 2: Plain Language Summary).

Conclusions

Our modeling data indicate that HZ represents a substantial health burden in older Portuguese adults. From the societal perspective, RVZ vaccination was a cost-effective intervention compared with no vaccination in Portuguese adults aged ≥ 65 years. Earlier vaccination (i.e., in individuals aged 65–69 years) resulted in the most favorable ICERs.
