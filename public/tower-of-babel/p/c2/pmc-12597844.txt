Unraveling the Ecological Mechanisms Influencing the Structure and Composition of Lung Cancer Microbiomes
Authors: Jiandong Mei, Lianwei Li, Zhanshan Sam Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the ecological mechanisms governing the structure and composition of lung microbiome communities within tumor tissue from lung cancer patients. While this field has attracted increasing research attention, the ecological and etiological mechanisms driving microbial community assembly in this environment remain poorly characterized. To address this gap, we applied Sloan’s near neutral model, Ning et al.’s normalized stochasticity ratio framework and Harris et al.’s multi-site neutral model to evaluate the influences of stochastic and deterministic factors at species, community and metacommunity levels, respectively. Our findings include: (i) Stochastic drift exhibited predominant influence at both species and community levels in normal adjacent tissue (NT), exceeding its effects in LUAD (lung adenocarcinoma) and LUSC (lung squamous cell carcinoma). (ii) At the metacommunity level, neutrality was not rejected at the metacommunity or local community levels, which is consistent with the previous finding (i). (iii) Elevated metacommunity biodiversity (θ) and immigration rates (m) in LUAD/LUSC compared to NT (observed in ∼50% of cases) suggest that tumor occurrence/progression may actively promote microbial recruitment to tumor microenvironments. We propose three non-exclusive mechanistic interpretations: (i) Tumor-mediated immune modulation creates permissive ecological niches; (ii) structural remodeling of tissue enhances microbial colonization potential; (iii) selective enrichment of opportunistic taxa (e.g., Streptococcus) through tumor-specific microenvironmental changes. Our results demonstrate that LUAD and LUSC microbiomes are shaped by deterministic tumor-driven selection, in contrast to the predominantly stochastic assembly observed in NT microbiomes. Introduction

Historically, the lungs were considered sterile; however, recent research has revealed the presence of a diverse lung microbiome. The lung microbiome composition closely resembles that of the upper respiratory tract but exhibits lower biomass, influenced by a balance between microbial immigration from the upper airways and elimination by the lungs’ immune and mechanical defenses [27]. The composition of this microbiome is altered in various lung diseases, including COPD, asthma, bronchiectasis, and acute conditions such as pneumonia, sepsis, and COVID-19 [5, 27]. Emerging research has highlighted distinct microbial patterns in respiratory samples from lung cancer patients, with increased prevalence of bacterial genera such as Granulicatella, Abiotrophia, Streptococcus, Veillonella, Megasphaera, and Selenomonas in sputum and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) specimens (Cameron et al., 2017; Hosgood et al., 2014; [11]). In studies of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), sequencing-based analyses have revealed microbial imbalances during both stable disease phases and acute exacerbations, though findings vary due to differences in study cohorts and sample types. For instance, bronchial wash samples from individuals with stable COPD exhibited elevated levels of Pseudomonas and reduced abundance of Prevotella compared to healthy smokers and nonsmokers [6]. Additionally, a large-scale meta-analysis of sputum data identified heightened representation of Haemophilus, Streptococcus, Moraxella, and Lactobacillus in COPD patients, along with diminished levels of other bacterial groups relative to healthy individuals (Wang et al., 2020).

Pattaroni et al. [21] explored bacterial colonization and immune system maturation in the lower airways during the first year of life, revealing that the lower respiratory tract microbiota forms within the first two postnatal months. This microbial and immunological development may inform early-life interventions to prevent respiratory diseases. Additionally, research has identified a lung-brain axis, through which the pulmonary microbiome regulates central nervous system immune reactivity, influencing susceptibility to autoimmune diseases [8],Azzoni et al., 2022). Hosang et al. [8] demonstrated that alterations in lung microbiota composition reduced proinflammatory responses, impaired immune cell recruitment, and mitigated clinical symptoms. Their findings suggest that shifts in lung microbiota decrease susceptibility to disease via the lung-brain axis [8]. Infections with Mycobacterium tuberculosis or Streptococcus pneumoniae may promote lung cancer development by triggering inflammatory or immunological changes within the lungs. These microbial community alterations likely reflect biochemical changes in the lungs of cancer patients, associated with an increase in anaerobic environmental niches [19].

These findings primarily catalog what microbes are present, not why they dominate in cancerous niches. However, the mechanisms sustaining lung microbiome diversity—whether through stochastic or deterministic factors, random immigration from the upper respiratory tract, or immune selection pressures—remain poorly understood. The influence of stochastic and deterministic factors on microbiomes in various environments, including breast tissue, the gut, milk, animal guts, hot springs, and multiple human microbiome sites, has been extensively studied [4, 13–18].

While studies like those by Tsay et al. [25] have identified specific microbial taxa (e.g., Granulicatella, Streptococcus) and pathways (e.g., PI3K activation) associated with lung tumors, our study resolves the ecological mechanisms driving these associations. By quantifying stochastic vs. deterministic assembly, we reveal how tumor microenvironments filter microbial communities—a question unaddressed by taxonomic cataloging alone. This framework identifies processes (e.g., immigration, selection) rather than taxa, offering novel insights into how tumors alter microbial community dynamics. Our study aims to investigate the roles of stochastic (e.g., immigration from the upper respiratory tract) and deterministic factors (e.g., immune selection pressures and cancer pathology) in shaping the lung tissue microbiome. Here, we bridge this gap by applying a multi-scale ecological framework—neutral models, stochasticity ratios, and metacommunity analyses—to compare microbiome assembly in normal lung tissue (NT), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), and squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC). We hypothesize that the maintenance of microbiome diversity plays a critical role in the clinical manifestation of lung cancer.

Method and Datasets

Sloan’s Neutral Community Model for Microbial Community (Sloan 2006, 2007)

The Neutral Community Model for Microbial Community, as proposed by Sloan (2006, 2007), was employed in this research. This model, which was also utilized by Venkataraman et al. (2015), is designed to test the impact of stochastic births, deaths, and immigration on the structure of prokaryotes, based on Hubbell’s [10] assumptions. The model is particularly suited to large populations and communities, which are typical of microbiome communities. The goal of this model is to evaluate the fit of microbial communities with neutral community assembly.

The model operates under the assumption of a saturated community consisting of NT individuals. When an individual dies or leaves the community, they are replaced either by an immigrant from a source community with a probability of m, or by an offspring of a local community member with a probability of 1-m. The community evolves through cycles of immigration, birth, and death. This model necessitates a source community and a destination community. Previous studies have used different spatial communities as both source and destination communities. However, this study replaces spatial differentiation with temporal transformation, treating the solid tissue normal microbiome as the source community and the primary tumor (LUAD, LUSC) microbiome as the destination community. This approach allows us to investigate the role of deterministic and stochastic factors in the transformation of the microbiome community during tumor development.

Two schemes were implemented in this study. Scheme I involved designating normal tissue microbiome as both the source and destination communities to identify species assembled through neutral processes or selective forces. This was also done for LUAD and LUSC microbiome, respectively. Scheme II simulated the transformation of the microbiome community from normal tissue microbiome to LUAD and LUSC microbiome. This study aims to identify species inherited from the NT microbiome with neutral drift and those under positive or negative selection, thereby providing insights into the role of deterministic and stochastic factors in the transformation of the microbiome community during tumor development.

Normalized Stochasticity Ratio (NSR)

The framework proposed by Ning et al. [20] was used in this study to quantify ecological stochasticity. This mathematical framework is based on the theory that deterministic processes can make communities more similar or dissimilar than null expectations. The process of assessing stochasticity involves three main steps.

The first step involves calculating community similarity metrics, which are used to measure beta diversity. Various similarity metrics exist, but based on Ning et al. [20] recommendation and our data structure, the Ruzicka similarity metrics were used in this study. The observed similarity between two communities is represented by C.

The second step involves generating null expected (simulated) communities. For each metacommunity, a null model algorithm-based randomization was repeated 1000 times to generate 1000 simulated metacommunities and their corresponding simulated communities. The randomly expected similarity between two simulated communities is represented by E.

The final step involves estimating the stochasticity ratio (SR) and normalized stochasticity ratio (NSR). Assuming there are m communities in a metacommunity, Cij represents the observed similarity between the ith community and the jth community in the metacommunity. Eij represents the null similarity between the ith community and the jth community in one simulated metacommunity. Eij¯ represents the average of the null expected similarity between the ith and the jth communities in 1000 simulated metacommunities. The stochasticity ratio (SR) ranges from 0 to 1, with 0 indicating a completely deterministic community assembly and 1 indicating a completely stochastic assembly. However, when expected stochasticity is low, SR may overestimate stochasticity. To address this, SR is normalized to give the Normalized Stochasticity Ratio (NSR), which has been shown to have higher accuracy and precision than SR [20]. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were performed to compare NSR values between NT, LUAD, and LUSC within each dataset (p < 0.05). NSR values are sensitive to sequencing depth and technical biases; thus, comparisons should only be made within datasets processed under identical conditions.

Multi-Site Neutral (MSN) Model

In their 2017 study, Harris et al. [7] implemented Hubbell’s [10] unified neutral theory of biodiversity (UNTB) by approximating the multinomial (MN) species abundance distribution model with a hierarchical Dirichlet process (HDP) model. There are two important parameters in MSN model, biodiversity parameter (θ) and migration rate (m). In the metacommunity, equivalent neutral dynamics are observed, yet new species are generated through speciation with a certain probability. It is more expedient to utilise the speciation rate (or fundamental biodiversity number) to parameterise the metacommunity distribution.

where N is the fixed number of individuals in the metacommunity. The biodiversity parameter (θ) reflects species richness. while migration rate (m) quantifies microbial dispersal from source communities. Both parameters are estimated via hierarchical Dirichlet processes [7].

The derived algorithm is capable of simultaneously estimating the migration rates among a substantial number of sites, which is why we have designated the model as the multi-site neutral model (MSN). With MSN modelling, the neutrality test can be performed at both the local community and the metacommunity level simultaneously. In other words, there are p-values for local community neutrality and metacommunity neutrality, respectively. For a detailed description of the computational procedures and the software program associated with the MSN model may refer to Harris et al. [7].

Lung Tissues Microbiome Datasets

In this research, eight publicly available lung microbiome datasets from NCBI SRA were initially identified. In order to guarantee analytical rigor and biological relevance, the following inclusion criteria were applied: (1) Tissue-Specificity: Exclusion criteria included datasets derived from lung tissue biopsies (excluding sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage, and upper airway swabs). (2) Cohort Requirements: It is imperative that datasets encompass cohorts of normal tissue (NT), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC).

The four datasets that underwent filtration were subjected to 16S rRNA gene sequencing for the purpose of microbiome profiling, employing the V3-V4 region. Each of the datasets under consideration contains normal tissue microbiome, LUAD microbiome and LUSC microbiome communities. Table S1 provides a concise description of the four LCM datasets and the OTU tables information for each group of lung cancer microbiome datasets. The identification of species and their relative abundance is achieved through the utilisation of the Kraken2 + Bracken pipeline, a sophisticated computational framework capable of detecting a substantial number of species.

Results

Community Neutral Model for Species Level

The Sloan neutral model (2006, 2007) was utilized to estimate the relative importance of deterministic and stochastic factors in shaping the microbiome community. Two schemes were implemented to examine the assembly mechanisms of NT microbiome, LUAD microbiome, LUSC microbiome (Scheme I) and microbiome transformation from NT to LUAD, LUSC respectively (Scheme II). Table 1 presents the fitting results of those two schemes. In Scheme I, 50% of models demonstrated a successful fit with the community neutral model (R2 > 0), whereas in Scheme II, this number was 37.5%. The success rates in Scheme I (75% for NT, 50% for LUSC, 25% for LUAD) indicate a gradient of increasing deterministic selection from NT to tumor tissues. However, these values should be interpreted as relative differences rather than definitive thresholds due to dataset variability. Dataset #1 (LUAD) was excluded from neutral model analyses due to insufficient sample size (n = 6), as the Sloan model requires large populations for robust parameter estimation. The exclusion of Dataset #1 did not alter the overall interpretation, as the remaining datasets consistently showed higher stochasticity in NT. However, it is notable that more than 76% of species were neutral species, with below and above species accounting for less than 24% of the total.

Ecological Stochasticity Estimation for Community Level

In addition to the neutral model for species level, we aim to quantify the relative importance of deterministic and stochastic processes in microbiome community assembly. A mathematical framework, namely the Normalized Stochasticity Ratio (NSR), was performed for each dataset [20]. The authors developed a boundary point (0.5) between more deterministic (< 0.5) and more stochastic (> 0.5) assembly. As illustrated in Table 2, the NSR values and p-values of the Wilcoxon test for NSR between NT and LUAD, LUSC, are presented. The symbol “ ≠ ” denotes the presence of substantial disparities between NT and LUAD (LUSC) in NSR. The symbol “ < ” is used to denote the fact that the NSR of NT is significantly smaller than that of LUAD (LUSC), whereas “ > ” is used to indicate the opposite. The NSR of NT microbiome is significantly larger than that of LUAD and LUSC for the four datasets, with the exception of Dataset #1, which indicates that the mechanism of NT microbiome community is more stochastic than that of LUAD and LUSC microbiome community, and less deterministic process (Table 2, Fig. 2). It is notable that in Dataset #4, the NSR of the NT microbiome is larger than 0.5. In comparison, the LUAD and LUSC are less than 0.5. That is in according with the findings of the above neutral community model (Table 1). However, given the boundary point, 50% of the NSR of NT, LUAD, and LUSC are less than 0.5 (and another 50% is larger than 0.5), respectively. The absence of corresponding results in four datasets may be attributed to the influence of bias and the inherent limitations of each experimental procedure. But even there are bias in each experimental, NT exhibited significantly higher NSR than LUAD in Datasets #2 (p < 0.001), #3 (p = 0.004), and #4 (p < 0.001), but not in Dataset #1 (p = 0.503). The inconsistency in Dataset #1 aligns with its small LUAD sample size (n = 6), which reduces statistical power to detect differences (p-values in Table 2).

Multi-site Neutral Model for Metacommunity Level

In addition to the species-level neutral model, a multi-site neutral model was tested for each group: NT, LUAD, LUSC, NT & LUAD, NT & LUSC, LUAD and LUSC (Table 3). The random artificial metacommunity failed to reject neutrality at the metacommunity level and local community level (PM and PL), with p-values greater than 0.05. The biodiversity parameter θ of the NT microbiome metacommunity is significantly larger than that of LUAD and LUSC in Dataset #2 and Dataset #4 (Table 4). There is no significant difference in Dataset #3 and NT vs. LUAD in Dataset #1 (Table 4). The immigrate parameter m of the metacommunity displays almost simultaneous trends with parameter θ. May be due to the fewer samples for LUAD (6) and the greater number of samples for NT (214), Dataset #1 has contrary trends with Dataset #2 and Dataset #4. Therefore, the trends of parameter θ and m could be revealed by Dataset #2 and Dataset #4. Higher θ in LUAD/LUSC (Table 4) suggests greater biodiversity buffers against deterministic pressures (e.g., immune selection), while higher m in LUAD/LUSC implies increased microbial immigration due to tumor-driven selection (e.g., altered mucosal barriers). These parameters collectively indicate that tumors impose niche-based filtering, restricting stochastic microbial exchange observed in healthy tissues.

Discussion

The findings of this study provide critical insights into the ecological mechanisms governing the structure and composition of lung microbiomes in the context of lung cancer. By employing the Neutral Community Model, Normalized Stochasticity Ratio (NSR), and Multi-Site Neutral (MSN) Model, we have elucidated the relative contributions of stochastic and deterministic factors in shaping microbial communities across species, community, and metacommunity levels. Our results demonstrate that the assembly of the normal tissue (NT) microbiome is predominantly influenced by stochastic processes, with over 76% of species classified as neutral, compared to lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC). This suggests that the healthy lung microbiome is more susceptible to random ecological processes, such as microbial immigration from the upper respiratory tract, rather than deterministic factors like immune selection or cancer pathology. That would be the reason why most of the species are neutral species (more than 76%) but failed to fit neutral community model.

At the community level, the NT microbiome exhibited significantly higher stochasticity than LUAD and LUSC microbiomes, as evidenced by the NSR values. While NSR trends suggest higher stochasticity in NT than tumors, this pattern is dataset-specific. Technical variability and sample size constraints necessitate cautious interpretation, though the majority of datasets support the hypothesis. This aligns with the neutral community model results, which indicate that the NT microbiome is more likely to be shaped by random ecological drift. In contrast, the LUAD and LUSC microbiomes showed greater influence from deterministic factors, likely due to the selective pressures exerted by the tumor microenvironment. These findings are consistent with previous studies that have highlighted the role of deterministic factors, such as immune responses and metabolic changes, in shaping microbial communities in diseased tissues [5, 27].

The MSN model further supported these observations, as the artificially constructed random metacommunity did not reject neutrality at both metacommunity and local community levels. However, the biodiversity parameter θ and immigration rate m of the NT microbiome metacommunity were significantly lower than those of LUAD and LUSC in approximately half of the cases. This suggests that while stochastic processes dominate in healthy lung tissue, the tumor microenvironment may impose additional constraints on microbial diversity and immigration, leading to a more deterministic assembly process in cancerous tissues. While NT microbiomes provide a baseline, our findings highlight tumor-specific ecological pressures. LUAD/LUSC microbiomes exhibit higher biodiversity (θ) and immigration (m), suggesting tumors create niches favoring deterministic selection (e.g., Streptococcus enrichment). This aligns with Tsay et al.’s findings of PI3K pathway activation in tumor-associated microbiomes but extends them by quantifying ecological mechanisms.

One of the most intriguing findings of this study is the observation that the most abundant species in the lung microbiome are predominantly below-neutral or above-neutral, rather than neutral. This implies that deterministic factors, such as immune selection or metabolic competition, play a critical role in shaping the dominant microbial taxa, even in healthy tissues. Below-neutral species, which are less abundant than expected under neutral theory, may be actively suppressed by the host immune system, while above-neutral species may thrive due to specific adaptations to the lung environment. This dichotomy highlights the complex interplay between stochastic and deterministic processes in maintaining microbial diversity and suggests that the most ecologically significant species are those under strong selective pressures.

The implications of these findings extend beyond ecological theory and have potential clinical relevance. The observed differences in microbiome assembly between normal and cancerous tissues suggest that the tumor microenvironment may create unique ecological niches that favor certain microbial taxa. These taxa could potentially influence tumor progression, either by promoting inflammation, modulating immune responses, or altering metabolic pathways. For example, specific microbial taxa such as Streptococcus and Veillonella have been associated with lung cancer in previous studies (Hosgood et al., 2014; [25]), and their presence in tumor tissues may reflect their ability to thrive in the altered biochemical conditions of the tumor microenvironment. Understanding these interactions could open new avenues for therapeutic interventions, such as modulating the lung microbiome to enhance anti-tumor immunity or disrupt pro-tumor microbial communities.

Furthermore, our findings raise important questions about the bidirectional interactions between the tumor tissue microbiome and cancer cells. While this study focused on the impact of lung cancer on microbiome composition, it is plausible that the microbiome, in turn, influences tumor biology. For instance, certain microbial taxa may produce metabolites that promote tumor growth or suppress immune surveillance, while others may exert antagonistic effects by stimulating immune responses or producing anti-tumor compounds. Future research should explore these reciprocal interactions in greater detail, as they could reveal novel mechanisms of tumor-microbiome crosstalk and inform the development of microbiome-based therapies for lung cancer.

It is also worth noting that this study analyzed four datasets, but only Datasets #2 and #4 yielded consistent results. Dataset #1 was excluded due to disparities in sample size, while Dataset #3 lacked significant differences between non-tumor and tumor groups. These inconsistencies highlight the challenges of studying complex microbial communities in heterogeneous clinical samples. Variations in sample collection, processing, and sequencing protocols, as well as differences in patient demographics and disease stages, may contribute to these discrepancies. Future studies should aim to standardize these factors and include larger, more diverse cohorts to validate these findings.

Our integration of Sloan’s neutral model, NSR, and MSN models provides a multi-scale ecological lens absent in prior lung cancer microbiome studies. For example, while Tsay et al. linked Granulicatella to PI3K activation, we show that such taxa are ‘above-neutral’ species under deterministic host selection, bridging taxonomy and ecology. Our models demonstrate that tumors impose deterministic selection, favoring species like Streptococcus that thrive in immunosuppressed niches. This mechanistic insight complements Tsay et al.’s observations of dysbiosis-immune crosstalk by explaining why certain taxa dominate in cancer. Our study did not account for covariates such as smoking, COPD, or tumor stage due to incomplete metadata. While this limits causal interpretations, our primary aim was to resolve ecological mechanisms (stochastic vs. deterministic) rather than clinical subtyping. Future studies with richly annotated cohorts should integrate these variables to disentangle their effects on microbiome assembly.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that lung cancer significantly alters the structure and composition of lung tissue microbiomes, with stochastic processes dominating in healthy tissues and deterministic factors playing a greater role in cancerous tissues. The most abundant microbial species are shaped by selective pressures, while the majority of species are influenced by random ecological processes. These findings underscore the importance of considering both stochastic and deterministic factors in microbiome research and highlight the potential clinical implications of tumor-microbiome interactions. Future research should focus on elucidating the mechanisms underlying these interactions and exploring their therapeutic potential in lung cancer treatment.
