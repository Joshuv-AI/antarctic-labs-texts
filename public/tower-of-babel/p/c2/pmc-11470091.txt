PLA based biodegradable bionanocomposite filaments reinforced with nanocellulose: development and analysis of properties
Authors: Alok Kumar Trivedi, M. K. Gupta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The 3D printing technique has recently become more prevalent among researchers for the fabrication of nanocomposites. The low crystallinity of polylactic acid (PLA) leads to the poor mechanical and thermal properties of its 3D-printed products, which restrict their applications in many fields. To overcome the limitations of PLA, the present work aims to develop PLA-based bionanocomposite filaments with varying percentages (1, 3, and 5 wt%) of crystalline nanocellulose (CNC) through a single screw extruder. The filaments will be further utilized for the development of bionanocomposite samples to evaluate their properties. The effect of CNC reinforcement on the chemical structure of the filaments was analyzed by FTIR analysis. XRD analysis revealed that the crystallinity of the filaments was significantly improved due to the nucleating effect of CNC. The maximum crystallinity was observed in the filament containing 1 wt% CNC, which was 26% higher than the pure PLA filament. The thermal and mechanical performance of the filaments was also considerably improved after CNC reinforcement, which was confirmed by DSC-TGA and tensile test analysis. The maximum tensile strength and tensile modulus were observed to be 48.9 MPa and 1700 MPa, respectively, in the filament reinforced with 1 wt% CNC, which was 35.5% and 21.89%, respectively, higher than those of the pure PLA filament. Rheological analysis showed that the complex viscosity, storage modulus, and loss modulus of the filaments were significantly affected by the reinforcement of CNC.

Introduction

Frequent use of plastics, owing to their characteristics such as lightweight, high strength-to-weight ratio, non-corrosiveness, and cost-effectiveness, makes them suitable for replacing traditional and heavy-weight materials like wood, metals, leather, and glass for low to medium-strength applications1,2. In the present scenario, out of the total production of plastics, only a few, about 9–10%, can be recycled, 10–12% are reduced to ashes, and the remaining majority of more than 75% accumulates in different parts of the earth, including lakes, rivers, seas, and landfills, which leads to hazardous effects on society and the environment3. Plastic is the third largest type of waste worldwide3. Geyer et al.4, in their survey, reported that if the consumption of non-biodegradable plastics is not minimized, plastic waste will increase to 12,000 metric tons on earth by 2050. The utilization of non-biodegradable petroleum-derived plastics in huge quantities is a serious concern globally, as it could harm human health and the environment2–5.

The utilization of renewable resources for the development of biodegradable plastics might be an effective way to develop a sustainable society and environment. Among biodegradable polymers, PLA is one of the most promising biodegradable polymers because of its good biocompatibility, biodegradability, transparency, and abundance at an optimum cost6,7. It was the first biodegradable material that was proposed for biomedical applications8. In recent decades, it has been extensively investigated for packaging, textiles, orthopaedics, tissue regeneration, drug carriers, implants, and medical tools or equipment9,10.

The conventional methods primarily used for the fabrication of polymer composites are solution/solvent casting, injection molding, hand lay-up, and compression molding. These traditional techniques are time-consuming, difficult to operate, and expensive, especially for the fabrication of customized products. In addition, material waste during these processes and machining to obtain the desired shape and size ultimately increases the cost of products11. From the literature, it was observed that the incorporation of nanoparticles into polymer composites through conventional methods is somewhat challenging and costly as well12. Additive manufacturing (AM) is a new emerging technique that provides the opportunity to overcome the limitations involved in conventional methods of composite fabrication. The 3D printing of PLA polymer material through fused deposition modeling (FDM) is prevalent due to its low melting point, minimal shrinkage and warping, and low cost13. In the FDM process, material is supplied as a filament to the hot extruder assembly, where it melts and is deposited on the substrate in a controlled manner. In recent decades, 3D-printed composites have become very popular because they are lightweight, their mechanical properties can be tailored, and they are economical for developing customized products14–16. Apart from the several advantages of PLA, its low mechanical strength, brittle behavior, and low thermal decomposition temperature restrict its performance17,18. The reinforcement of nanoparticles seems to be an emerging and effective way to improve the performance of PLA plastics13,17,19. Crystalline nanocellulose (CNC)-reinforced PLA bionanocomposites possess good mechanical, thermal, and physical properties.

CNC is produced from bulk cellulose by breaking its internal bonds and removing the amorphous region through acid hydrolysis. CNC is a biodegradable material with at least one dimension in the nanoscale range (≤ 100 nm). The morphology and properties of CNC significantly depend on sources and extraction methods. Generally, the diameter and length of CNC vary in the ranges of 5–70 nm and 100–250 nm, respectively20. CNC attracts researchers due to its noteworthy properties, including high aspect ratio, high surface area, renewability, biodegradability, biocompatibility, and low density. From the literature, it was observed that the addition of CNC to the PLA matrix improved its crystallinity because it acts as a nucleating agent19. The enhanced crystallinity of the material offers improved mechanical properties and performance. Agbakoba et al.13 fabricated PLA reinforced with CNC and bio-additives (in minimal amounts of 0.1, 0.5, and 1 wt%) bionanocomposite filaments and investigated their performance. A significant improvement in the filaments’ mechanical and thermal properties was reported with the addition of CNC. Lu et al.21 extracted spherical nanocellulose from lyocell fibers and investigated its reinforcement effect on PLA nanocomposite films. They reported improved thermal and mechanical properties of PLA nanocomposite films due to the reinforcement of spherical nanocellulose. Recently, Zarna et al.22 published a review article which presented that the strength of lignocellulosic reinforced biocomposites prominently depends on the optimum shape, size, and source of the nanocellulose.

The present study has a sufficient research contribution in the field. The CNC was extracted from sisal fibers using a novel approach for the development of filaments. A detailed characterization of extracted nanocellulose has already been published in the literature23. Further, filaments from pure PLA and CNC were fabricated through a simple method using single screw extruder approach and its detailed characterization is presented. The present work aims to investigate the effect of higher concentration of CNCs up to 5 wt% and suggested optimal concentration of CNCs reinforcement as 1 wt% for better filament performance. Further, other studies presented a limited characterization and lower concentration of CNCs up to 2 wt%. Statistical analysis was also performed, and a significant difference between filaments was found.

Materials and methods

Materials

PLA (grade—LX175, density = 1.24 g/cm3, moisture content < 400 ppm) was procured from Solidspace Technology LLP, Maharashtra, India. Sisal fibers, used as a source of CNC, were purchased from the Women's Development Organization, Dehradun, India. All the chemicals utilized for the extraction of CNC were purchased from Uma Scientific Traders, India, and were used without further modification.

Fabrication of bionanocomposite filaments

CNC was extracted from sisal fibers using steam explosion-assisted chemo-mechanical treatment. The chemical method included alkali treatment, bleaching, and acid hydrolysis with hydrochloric acid (HCl), followed by mechanical grinding at high rpm. A detailed description of CNC extraction and its characterization has already been reported in our previously published article23.

Filaments from pure PLA and CNC were fabricated through a single screw extruder, as demonstrated by a flowchart in Fig. 1. Initially, the CNC powder and PLA pellets were dried in a hot air oven for two hours to ensure the complete removal of moisture before starting the filament fabrication process. The PLA pellets and CNC particles were mixed properly using ball milling. Both pure PLA and CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments of 1.75 mm diameter were fabricated using a melt compounding process in a single screw extruder setup (45 mm screw diameter, L/D ratio: 28:1, and screw rotation 40–45 rpm) at Solidspace Technology LLP, Nashik, Maharashtra, India. The processing temperatures during fabrication, from hopper to die, were set as 190–200–210 °C. After extruding from the hot die, the filaments were subjected to water cooling; the temperature of the first water bath was kept at 48 °C, and the subsequent bath was maintained at ambient temperature. The extruded filaments were spooled, dried in hot air, and packed in sealed bags for further use. The average diameter of the filament was observed to be 1.75 ± 0.05 mm. The filaments were fabricated from pure PLA and PLA-based nanocomposites with varying weight percentages of CNC. Characterization

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM)

The surface morphology of pure PLA and CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments was nalysed by FESEM. The FESEM images were acquired using the instrument (model: Nova Nano SEM 450, FEI) at Material Science Engineering, IIT Kanpur, India. All specimens were gold coated before the FESEM characterization and nalysed at a 10–12 mm working distance and EHT = 10 kV.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The effect of CNC reinforcement on the crystalline behaviour of PLA based bionanocomposite filaments was carried out by XRD analysis and compared with pure PLA filaments. XRD patterns of each type of filaments were recorded using an advanced X-ray diffractometer instrument (Smart lab 3 kW, Rigaku, Tokyo, Japan) in the range of 2θ = 4°–70° with a scanning speed of 3° per min at wavelength = 1.541 Å. This analysis was performed at CIR lab, MNNIT Allahabad, Prayagraj, U.P., India.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The chemical structure of both PLA and CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments has been studied by identifying different functional groups through FTIR analysis. This analysis was performed on the FTIR spectrometer (model: Jasco, FT/IR-4X, Tokyo, Japan) at the Department of Chemistry, MNNIT Allahabad, Prayagraj, U.P. India. The FTIR spectra of each sample were recorded in the wavelength range 500–3500 cm−1.

Thermal analysis

Thermal analysis of both PLA and CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments was carried out using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). Thermal analysis was carried out using an instrument (STA-TGA 6000, Perkin Elmer, USA) at the Material Research Center, MNIT Jaipur, Rajasthan, India. The samples (with a small quantity of about 4–5 mg) were heated from ambient temperature (29 °C) to 700 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C/min under a controlled atmosphere of nitrogen gas flowing at 20 ml/min.

Tensile analysis of bionanocomposite filaments

The tensile properties of pure PLA and CNC/ PLA bionanocomposite filaments were estimated according to the ASTM-638 standard. The filament specimen with dimensions for tensile testing and its experimental setup is shown in Fig. 2. This test was performed on Tinius Olsen Tensile Testing Machine with a load cell of 10 kN, model: ZD-40 at Applied Mechanics Department, MNNIT Allahabad, India. All samples were tested at a 2 mm/min crosshead speed at environmental temperature 28–30 °C. Five samples from each composition were tested, and their average values are presented.Fig. 2Tensile analysis of filaments: (a) specimen sample and (b) testing setup.

Tensile analysis of filaments: (a) specimen sample and (b) testing setup.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of the tensile properties was conducted to evaluate the significance of the tensile results for CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments. This analysis was carried out using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) approach using Microsoft Excel 2016 with a 5% significant level. The P value of less than 0.05 suggests substantial differences in the tensile results among the various filaments, and the null hypothesis is rejected.

Rheological analysis

Rheological analysis of pure PLA and CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments was carried out using an advanced compact rheometer (ARES-G2, TA instrument) at Advanced Rheology Facility, IIT Bombay, Maharashtra, India. For the rheological experiment, circular samples with 25 mm diameter and 1 mm thickness were 3D printed by FDM printer model: Wol3D, Creality Ender Pro. Each sample was analyzed for different temperatures (190 °C, 200 °C and 210 °C) and stain rates.

Results and discussion

Morphological analysis of bionanocomposite filaments

The surface morphology of the filaments fabricated from pure PLA and CNC-reinforced PLA bionanocomposite filaments was analyzed through FESEM, as shown in Fig. 3. It was observed that the surface of the pure PLA filament (PC0) was smooth due to the absence of CNC particles, as shown in Fig. 3a. As the reinforcement weight percentage of CNC increased, the filaments’ surface roughness changed significantly. Maximum surface roughness was noticed in the filament PC5 due to the agglomeration of CNC particles and the formation of an elevated surface due to the presence of particles on the surface of the filaments. These FESEM images reveal that increased surface roughness caused a decrease in the mechanical properties of the bionanocomposite filaments, as discussed in the tensile analysis section. Similar types of FESEM images were also observed for the CNC-reinforced PLA bionanocomposite films, which demonstrated that CNC particles tended to agglomerate with further increases in reinforcement21,25,26.Fig. 3FESEM images of CNC/PLA bionanocomposites filaments at surface with varying wt% of CNC content: (a) 0, (b) 1, (c) 3 and (d) 5.

XRD analysis

XRD analysis was conducted to examine the crystallinity of neat PLA and CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments. All the filaments exhibited quite similar trends for the XRD pattern, as shown in Fig. 4. The highest peaks (i.e. intensity peaks) were observed at 2θ = around 16° for all the composite filaments, which indicates the crystalline nature of filaments27–29. The peak positions observed in the XRD spectra align well with previously reported literature29. Notably, with the addition of CNC to the PLA matrix, the sharpness of the peaks increased significantly, indicating enhanced crystallinity in the CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments compared to the neat PLA (PC0) filament. This enhancement was attributed to the nucleating effects of CNC on the PLA matrix30.Fig. 4XRD patterns of the CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments.

XRD patterns of the CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments.

The highest intensity peak (I200) and lower intensity peak (Iam) for all CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments were observed at approximately 16.8° and 8.4° of 2θ, respectively31,32. The crystallinity index and degree of crystallinity of the filaments were measured by the peak height method and reported in Table 2. The crystallinity index term signifies the relative amount of crystalline material in the CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments. Meanwhile, the degree of crystallinity signifies the fraction of the crystalline region on the CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments. The crystallinity behaviour of composite filaments was seen to be higher than the pure PLA filament (PC0). The maximum crystallinity index and degree of crystallinity were observed for the PC1 filament as 60.90% and 0.72, respectively. This increased crystallinity value signifies the good interfacial bonding between the CNC and PLA matrix, leading to enhanced mechanical properties of the bionanocomposite filaments. The further increase of CNC reinforcement did not cause a significant improvement in the crystallinity of filaments. The crystallinity index of the PC3 (60.47%) filament was almost similar to that of PC1 (60.90%). Among composite filaments, lower values of crystalline properties were shown by PC5. It was also noticed that with the further increase in CNC reinforcement percentage, one more slight crystalline peak was also noticed at 2θ = 26.7° for PC3 and PC5 filaments. This peak indicates that further reinforcement of CNC offers new sites for the crystallization of PLA. FTIR analysis

The FTIR spectra of pure PLA and CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments have been recorded to investigate the impact of CNC on their chemical structure, as shown in Fig. 5. It was perceived that the bands of FTIR spectra of CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments were quite similar with enhanced emissivity compared to pure PLA filament. These similarities in the bands of the FTIR spectrum show that the prepared filaments have good homogeneity in the composition. Each filament exhibits peaks primarily in three adsorption bands of the spectrum: 3000–2850 cm−1, 1750–1745 cm−1 and 1500–1000 cm−1.Fig. 5FTIR spectra of CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments.

FTIR spectra of CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments.

The peaks in the adsorption band 3000–2850 cm−1, were observed for all types of filaments due to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibration of –CH33. The peaks at 1745 cm−1 and 1450 cm−1 were present for all the filaments due to carbonyl groups (C=O) stretching vibrations and asymmetric bending vibrations of –CH3, respectively. The reinforcement of CNC significantly affects carbonyl groups’ distribution in the amorphous and crystalline regions of the nanocomposite filaments34. Therefore, variations in the peak intensity were observed as the CNC reinforcement percentage was increased. The peaks at approximately 1385 cm−1 and 1360 cm−1 were due to symmetric and bending vibrations of –C–H. The peaks at 1182 cm−1, 1084 cm−1 and 1038 cm−1 were assigned for stretching vibrations of –C–O bonds. These peaks were increased in bionanocomposite filaments, which shows that adding CNC leads to forming more –C–O bonds in the filaments35. The CNC reinforced PLA bionanocomposite filaments exhibited higher peaks at 1750 cm−1 due to the carbonyl group (C=O), particularly for PC1 and PC3 bionanocomposite filaments. These higher peaks were attributed to the formation of more carbonyl bonds in the filaments due to the strong bonding of the CNC within the PLA matrix. Similar results have been reported in the literature13. The FTIR analysis clearly shows that incorporating CNC into the PLA matrix leads to noticeable changes in the chemical structure, as reflected by transmittance peaks. These changes indicate that CNC reinforcement affects both the crystallinity and bonding within the PLA matrix, resulting in improved properties of the bionanocomposite filaments. The transmittance peaks correspond to various functional groups, and their sharpness is influenced by the degree of crystallinity and the interactions between CNC and the PLA matrix.

The thermal characteristics of filaments were also confirmed through the DTG curve, as presented in Fig. 6c, which demonstrates the rate of weight loss with temperature. The DTG curves of all types of filaments follow a similar pattern and illustrate that peak decomposition temperature was not significantly affected by the change in reinforcement percentage. Tensile analysis

The stress–strain diagram of pure PLA and CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments was obtained through the tensile test as depicted in Fig. 7 and computed values of tensile properties (i.e. tensile strength, tensile modulus, maximum force, and elongation at break) are provided in Table 5. This analysis reveals that the addition of CNC causes a significant improvement in the tensile properties up to 1 wt% of CNC reinforcement, and then tends to decrease after further reinforcement of CNC. The maximum tensile strength was observed for filament PC1 as 48.9 MPa, which was 35.5% higher than that of PC0 filament. Further, the maximum tensile modulus was marked as 1700 MPa for the PC1 filament, which was approximately 21.89% more than that of the pure PLA filament (PC0). As anticipated, the filament with the highest tensile strength exhibited the maximum force at the break. The addition of CNC in CNC/PLA influenced the crystallinity ehavior and hydrogen bonding between the reinforcement and matrix of CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments, as shown in XRD and FTIR characterization (Figs. 4 and 5). Therefore, the main reason for improving tensile properties was increased crystallinity and strong interfacial bonding between the CNC and PLA matrix45,46. Further, the smooth surface of the filament due to the uniform distribution of CNC particles was also found to be responsible for the improvement in tensile properties. The enhancement in tensile strength and tensile modulus of the PC1 filament is likely due to the tendency of the low CNC content to align with the PLA molecular chains. This alignment facilitates effective stress transfer between fibrils, leading to a more uniform distribution of applied stress throughout the matrix, thereby enhancing the material’s load-bearing capacity47. In addition to this, the elongation at the break of PC1 filament was improved which exhibits that incorporation of CNC improved the elastoplasticity of the PLA. When more CNC was added (beyond 1 wt%), tensile properties were decreased constantly for filaments PC3 and PC5. It might have happened due to agglomeration and poor interfacial bonding with the PLA matrix, resulting in reduced stress transfer thereby lowering the mechanical performance of the filaments. Similar results were mentioned in the past literature21,48–51.

Rheological analysis

In 3D printing, filament passes through a heated nozzle and is subjected to dynamic shearing, so it is necessary to carry out the filaments’ rheological study. Rheological analysis has been conducted to investigate the flow ehavior and viscoelastic properties of PLA and CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments. An advanced Rheometer was utilized for the study and rheological properties such as complex viscosity, storage modulus and loss modulus were analyzed, as shown in Fig. 8. The variation in complex viscosity of the filaments with angular frequency at 220 °C is shown in Fig. 8a. It was observed that complex viscosity was found to be decreased with increase in angular frequency for all types of filaments. It demonstrates that pure PLA and CNC/PLA filaments exhibit non-Newtonian ehavior53. All filaments follow similar trends for variation of complex viscosity with angular frequency. It also was observed that the addition of CNC significantly increased the melt viscosity of the CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments compared to pure PLA filament. The addition of CNC restricted the mobility of PLA chains in the molten state due to the incorporation of CNC in the matrix. The rheological properties of the polymers depend entirely on the structure of the polymer chains. Hence, the viscosity of nanocomposite filaments was higher. Similar results have been discussed by Kamal et al.54 in their research work.Fig. 8Rheological analysis of CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments: (a) viscosity v/s shear rate curve at 220 °C, (b) viscosity v/s temperature curve at ω = 10 rad/s, (c) storage modulus at 220 °C and (d) loss modulus at 220 °C.

Rheological analysis of CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments: (a) viscosity v/s shear rate curve at 220 °C, (b) viscosity v/s temperature curve at ω = 10 rad/s, (c) storage modulus at 220 °C and (d) loss modulus at 220 °C.

The maximum complex viscosity was observed for the PC1 filament, followed by PC3, PC5, and PC0. The minimum complex viscosity was observed for PC0 filament due to minimum restriction in the mobility of PLA chains. However, further reinforcement of CNC beyond the 1 wt% does not restrict the mobility of PLA chains, probably due to CNC’s self-agglomeration. Subsequently, a decrease in complex viscosity was observed for PC3 and PC5 filaments, as shown in Fig. 8a. The reduction in viscosity observed in CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments with increasing shear rate demonstrates shear-thinning behavior, a typical characteristic of polymers. This indicates that the materials become less viscous at higher shear rates, potentially making them easier to process.

The variation of complex viscosity of filaments with temperature (180–220 °C at 10 °C/min) at a constant angular frequency (ω = 10 rad/s) has also been investigated, as shown in Fig. 8b. It was observed that as the temperature increased, the complex viscosity decreased sharply with increase in temperature. Initially, at the temperature of 180 °C, a significant difference in viscosity was observed among the filaments, but as the temperature increased further, this difference decreased gradually. Higher material viscosity can impair printability, resulting in reduced strength and delamination of printed products55. Therefore, this study suggests the 3D printing of CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filaments, particularly for PC1 at higher temperatures to achieve improved printability and higher quality products by forming good adhesion bonding between layers. At a temperature of 220 °C, the complex viscosity values for PC0, PC3 and PC5 filament were almost close to each other. However, the PC1 filament exhibited a slightly higher viscosity than other filaments. This behavior illustrates that the addition of CNC by 1 wt% probably formed the interconnected network structure with the matrix that inhibited the mobility of the PLA chains53. The reinforcement of CNC by 5 wt% causes a sudden decrease in viscosity even below the pure PLA filament at an angular frequency of 10 rad/s, as shown in Fig. 8b. But when the angular frequency was increased, the complex viscosity (PC5) at 220 °C was increased and found to be higher than the PC0 filament, as shown in Fig. 8a. This might be due to the strong interaction of CNC with the PLA matrix at higher shear rates.

Storage modulus signifies the energy stored inside the material, whereas loss modulus signifies the heat dissipation through the deformation of materials56. From the frequency sweep test, the storage modulus and loss modulus were also investigated and found to be considerably affected by the CNC reinforcement and depend on frequency as well (Fig. 8c,d). Both storage and loss modulus were initially increased and then decreased with increased reinforcement content. The maximum storage and loss modulus values were observed for PC1, followed by the PC3, PC5, and PC0 filaments. The increase in modulus was attributed to the uniform distribution of CNC in the matrix and inhibition in the mobility of PLA chains due to the reinforcement of CNC in the matrix. As the frequency increased, storage and loss modulus increased because the interaction between nano-fillers and matrix increased at higher shear rates57. The increase in moduli was attributed to the addition of CNC, which restricts the mobility of PLA chains. However, as the reinforcement percentage exceeded 1 wt%, CNC particles likely agglomerated, reducing their effectiveness in limiting polymer chain mobility. Consequently, the moduli of PC3 and PC5 filaments decreased, though they remained higher than that of the PC0 filament.

Conclusions

In the present work, CNC reinforced PLA bionanocomposite filaments were fabricated and characterized in detail for 3D printing purposes. The overall better performance of composite filaments was seen in filament containing 1 wt% CNCs as the maximum values of the glass transition temperature, tensile strength, crystallinity index and degree of crystallinity were offered by it. The addition of CNC led to considerable changes in the chemical structure and crystallinity nature of bionanocomposite filaments, confirmed through FTIR and XRD, respectively. A homogenous distribution of CNC particles into PLA and decreased surface roughness of filaments could be responsible for improved crystallinity followed by improved mechanical properties. Thermal analysis demonstrated that the addition of CNC by 1 wt% improved the initial thermal decomposition temperature of the bionanocomposite filaments, whereas decomposition temperature was not significantly affected by the change in reinforcement percentage. Rheological analysis shows that complex viscosity decreased with increased angular frequency and temperatures. The storage and loss modulus were greatly improved with CNC loading, and maximum values were observed for filament PC1. This study shows that the present fabricated CNC/PLA bionanocomposite filament could be utilized for 3D printed samples and parts with improved structural, thermal and mechanical properties.
