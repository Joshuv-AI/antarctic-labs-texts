A five-year retrospective evaluation of perinatal outcomes in Syrian refugee and Turkish citizen pregnant women and their newborns at a tertiary hospital
Authors: Deniz Taşkıran, Orhan Ay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate and compare perinatal outcomes, neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission rates, and neonatal immunization rates between Syrian immigrant and Turkish citizen pregnant women.MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis was conducted using hospital records of Syrian and Turkish citizen pregnant women who received care at a tertiary referral center between January 2019 and December 2024. Demographic characteristics, perinatal outcomes, and neonatal vaccination rates were collected and analyzed.ResultsA total of 10,565 pregnant women were included in the study. The participants were divided into two groups based on nationality: 10,407 were of Turkish origin and 158 were of Syrian origin. The mean age of all participants was 31.48 ± 5.54 years. The mean gestational age at delivery was 38 weeks, and the average birth weight was 3191.92 ± 591.11 g. The mean age of Turkish pregnant women was 31.48 ± 5.52 years, while the mean age of Syrian pregnant women was 31.67 ± 6.47 years. The mean gestational age at delivery was 38 weeks for Turkish women and 39 weeks for Syrian women. The mean birth weight of newborns born to Turkish women was 3190 ± 591.25 g, whereas the mean birth weight of newborns born to Syrian women was 3267 ± 581.64 g.The mean duration of hospital stay post-delivery was 40.16 ± 10.77 h. The incidence of multiple pregnancies was 1.4%. Cesarean section was the most common mode of delivery, with a rate of 67%. The mean length of hospital stay for Turkish pregnant women was 40.19 ± 10.76 h, whereas it was 37.92 ± 11.39 h for Syrian pregnant women. The rate of multiple pregnancies was 1.5% among Turkish women and 1.3% among Syrian women. The overall cesarean section rate was 67.2% in Turkish women and 56.3% in Syrian women.Among newborns born to Turkish mothers, the rate of hepatitis B vaccination was 86.3%, and the rate of vitamin K administration was 89.3%. In newborns born to Syrian mothers, the hepatitis B vaccination rate was 82.9%, and the rate of vitamin K administration was 88%. Hepatitis B vaccine was administered to 86% of all newborns, and vitamin K injection was given to 89% of them.Secondary-level NICU admission was observed more often among neonates of Syrian immigrant mothers (38.6%) compared to Turkish citizens (32.8%).ConclusionsPerinatal outcomes were comparable between Syrian immigrant and Turkish citizen pregnant women. Although neonates born to immigrant mothers did not require advanced intensive care more frequently, the need for secondary-level NICU admission was higher.Keywords: Newborn vaccination, Syrian national pregnant women, Turkish national pregnant women

Background

People have constantly migrated over the years due to war, natural disasters, and economic hardship. According to the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), there are currently 21.3 million refugees worldwide [1]. Due to its geographical location, Turkey has become a final destination for many migrants. Following the outbreak of the civil war in the Syrian Arab Republic (Syria) in March 2011, Turkey began hosting a large number of Syrian migrants under international protection. The ongoing lack of peace and security in Syria has led to an extended stay for these individuals in Turkey. As of 2020, there are 3,559,041 Syrians living in Turkey under temporary protection [2]. Basic needs such as shelter, education and health are among the most common challenges faced by refugees [3]. Cultural differences and language barriers among migrant and immigrant groups can hinder access to adequate healthcare services. In situations that disrupt social order and lead to mass migration—such as war—sudden population increases may challenge a country’s ability to provide sufficient health services. Vulnerable groups, particularly pregnant women and children, are the most affected in such circumstances. In some countries, births among migrants account for approximately 20% of the total number of births [4].

These factors significantly elevate health risks, particularly among vulnerable populations such as women, children, the elderly, and individuals with chronic medical conditions. Pregnancy represents a critical period not only from a physiological standpoint but also with respect to sociocultural challenges and accessibility of healthcare services. Adequate and timely antenatal care is essential for identifying and managing potential maternal and neonatal complications, thereby improving pregnancy outcomes and enabling early medical intervention. However, migrant women are disproportionately affected by barriers to healthcare access compared to the host population. These barriers may include language difficulties, cultural differences, legal status, and limited health literacy, all of which contribute to delayed or insufficient prenatal care. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends a minimum of four routine antenatal visits during an uncomplicated pregnancy. Nevertheless, numerous studies have highlighted significant gaps in adherence to this standard among migrant populations, which may contribute to poorer maternal and neonatal health outcomes [5].

Pregnancy complications have been among the leading causes of mortality among women in reproductive age throughout human history. Access to regular healthcare services during pregnancy significantly reduces pregnancy-related morbidity and mortality. Studies from various countries have consistently demonstrated that migrant women experience significantly poorer pregnancy and birth outcomes compared to native populations. Reported complications among this group include higher rates of preterm birth, low birth weight (LBW), increased incidence of cesarean section, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, preeclampsia, postpartum hemorrhage, and puerperal infections. These findings suggest that migrant status is associated with increased obstetric risk, potentially due to delayed antenatal care, socioeconomic disadvantage, and limited access to healthcare services [6, 7].

For instance, a study conducted by McCall et al. at a tertiary public hospital in Lebanon demonstrated that Syrian and Palestinian refugee women had significantly higher rates of neonatal morbidity and mortality compared to Lebanese nationals. The findings revealed that neonates born to refugee mothers were more likely to present with low APGAR scores at 1 and 5 min, require admission to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), and experience prolonged hospital stays, indicating poorer overall neonatal health outcomes [8].

Similarly, studies conducted in Turkey have shown that Syrian refugee women tend to delay the initiation of prenatal care, which in turn increases the risk of pregnancy-related complications. A substantial proportion of these women are unable to attend an adequate number of antenatal visits throughout pregnancy, often due to language barriers, limited health literacy, socioeconomic constraints, and unfamiliarity with the healthcare system. Furthermore, the process of integration into the host country’s healthcare infrastructure is frequently prolonged and challenging, further impeding timely and comprehensive maternal care [9].

The influx of migrants has placed considerable strain on Turkey’s healthcare system, particularly in border provinces and major urban centers. The sudden and substantial increase in population has overwhelmed existing healthcare infrastructure, leading to challenges in service delivery and a significant rise in the workload of healthcare professionals. This pressure has highlighted the need for resource expansion, workforce support, and targeted healthcare policies to effectively meet the demands of both the host and migrant populations [10].

Maternal and child health outcomes vary considerably across different countries due to diverse healthcare systems, sociocultural factors, and resource availability. Prenatal care and continuous monitoring during pregnancy are essential for the early identification and management of complications that may arise during gestation and delivery. Within this framework, assessing the pregnancy and birth outcomes of Syrian migrant women residing in our country compared to those of Turkish citizens is vital for evaluating the effectiveness and accessibility of healthcare services. The primary objective of this study is to compare maternal and neonatal health outcomes between pregnant Syrian women and pregnant Turkish women. Additionally, this study aims to raise awareness among obstetricians and family physicians regarding the unique needs of this patient population to improve clinical care and health outcomes.

Methods

Between 01.01.2019 and 15.12.2024, data from Syrian and Turkish pregnant women followed at a tertiary care center over the past five years were retrospectively examined as a cohort through the hospital’s data system, after obtaining ethics committee approval. Information not available in the digital system was retrieved from patient files archived at our hospital. The study included pregnant women with complete data, those who delivered and received follow-up care at our hospital, and whose nationality information was recorded. A total of 900 pregnant women with incomplete data and 210 refugee pregnant women who were not Syrian nationals were excluded (Fig. 1). Two groups were established: Syrian refugees and Turkish citizens.

Study Design (study design corrected figüre)

The primary outcomes of the study were obstetric outcomes and cesarean section rates. Maternal age, gestational week, mode of delivery, number of stillbirths, newborns’ sex and birth weight, as well as neonatal outcomes, were analyzed. Neonatal weight and height data were obtained from the birth records in the neonatal registration form; however, no neonatal follow-up was conducted. Indications for cesarean section were also recorded. Based on birth weight, infants were classified into four categories: (< 1500 gram) very low birth weight, (1500–2499 g) low birth weight, (2500–4000 g) normal birth weight, and (> 4000 gram) high birth weight.

Based on gestational age at delivery, pregnant women were categorized into three groups: early preterm (24–31 + 6 weeks), preterm (32–36 + 6 weeks), and term (37–41 + 6 weeks). Rates of vaginal delivery and cesarean section were compared between these groups. Additionally, the groups were evaluated according to indications for cesarean section. The administration frequencies of routine neonatal interventions — including vitamin K injection and hepatitis B vaccination — were evaluated and compared across the groups.

Ethical and scientific approval for the study was obtained from the tertiary local ethics committee, with decision number BAEK-165, dated 25.12.2024/05.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used for the statistical analysis of the study. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test assessed the normality of parameter distributions. The Student’s t-test and Mann-Whitney U test were applied to continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Subject characteristics and demographic features were analyzed descriptively. Means and standard deviations (SD) were calculated for continuous variables, while medians and minimum-maximum values were reported for non-normally distributed variables. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The mean age of the participants was 31.48 ± 5.54 years. The mean ages of Group 1 and Group 2 were similar (31.48 ± 5.52 vs. 31.67 ± 6.47; p = 0.798). In Group 1, 5,379 male and 5,028 female babies were born; in Group 2, 86 male and 72 female babies were delivered, with no significant difference in sex distribution (p = 0.493). All patients were discharged after an average hospital stay of 40.16 ± 10.77 h, with no significant difference between Group 1 and Group 2 (40.19 ± 10.76 vs. 37.92 ± 11.39; p = 0.095). Stillbirths were recorded in 87 pregnant women in Group 1, while no stillbirths occurred in Group 2 (p = 0.249). There were 155 multiple pregnancies in total: 153 in Group 1 and 2 in Group 2 (p = 0.832).

Although all pregnant women delivered at around 38 weeks on average, women in Group 1 delivered at 38 weeks and those in Group 2 at 39 weeks (p < 0.001). When categorized as early preterm, preterm, and term deliveries, no significant difference was observed between the groups. Among Syrian pregnant women, 7 had early preterm, 6 preterm, and 145 term deliveries.

The mean length of all newborns was 49.59 ± 3.30 cm, with no significant difference between Group 1 and Group 2 (49.59 ± 3.30 vs. 49.82 ± 3.37; p = 0.172). The mean birth weight was 3,191.92 ± 591.1 g, also similar between Group 1 and Group 2 (3,190 ± 591.2 vs. 3,267 ± 581.6; p = 0.061). When birth weights were classified into very low, low, normal, and high categories, no significant differences were found between the groups.

At birth, 89% of newborns received the vitamin K injection and 86% received the hepatitis B vaccine, with comparable vaccination rates between the two groups. The rate of second-step neonatal hospitalization was higher in Group 2 at 38.6% compared to 32.8% in Group 1 (p = 0.021). However, third-step neonatal hospitalization rates were similar across groups.

Among the deliveries, 3,488 (33%) were vaginal, and 7,077 (67%) were cesarean sections. The number of cesarean deliveries in both groups was significantly higher than vaginal deliveries (p = 0.004). Cesarean section rates were notably high in both Group 1 and Group 2 (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Discussion

In our study, we analyzed and compared the pregnancy follow-up, obstetric characteristics, and neonatal outcomes of Turkish and Syrian nationals. Firstly, we found that Syrian nationals delivered at a later gestational age, had a higher rate of vaginal delivery, and a lower rate of cesarean section compared to Turkish nationals. Secondly, the rate of second-level neonatal hospitalization was higher among Syrian women. The indications for cesarean section were similar in both groups.

Existing literature robustly supports the increased risk of adverse obstetric and neonatal outcomes among refugee and migrant populations. A recent meta-analysis reported that refugee women are at a significantly higher risk of stillbirth, low APGAR scores, and neonatal mortality compared to non-refugee populations [11]. The findings of this study suggest that disparities in antenatal care utilization, socioeconomic status, and access to healthcare services among Syrian refugee women may contribute to differences in neonatal outcomes. Similarly, international public health literature consistently highlights a significantly higher incidence of obstetric and neonatal complications among Syrian refugees compared to host populations. Providing interpreters to facilitate better communication with refugees could help reduce adverse obstetric outcomes [12]. The Turkish Ministry of Health has implemented several measures to improve healthcare services for refugees. Syrian refugee pregnant women are classified as high-risk due to their poor living conditions and low income. Language barriers can lead to miscommunication with refugees, which may be linked to increased maternal mortality.

Between April 29, 2011 and September 30, 2016, 177,568 Syrian refugees gave birth in Turkey, representing approximately 3% of the total number of births in Turkey [13]. In our study, it was observed that Syrian refugee births accounted for 1.5% of all deliveries in Giresun province over a five-year period. We believe that the lower rate of refugee births compared to the national average in Turkey may be attributed to limited job opportunities and the region’s geographical location. The limited number of Syrian pregnant women in our study makes it difficult to interpret and generalize the findings. Conducting further research in regions with a higher population of Syrian migrants may yield more definitive and reliable results.

Cultural differences can influence maternal age, leading to variations in the average age at childbirth between different societies. One study demonstrated that the average age of Syrian refugee mothers was lower than that of pregnant women from the Republic of Turkey [14]. In this study, it was observed that each group gave birth at a similar average age. Another notable finding was that Syrian refugee women tended to deliver at later gestational ages and were more likely to have vaginal deliveries, whereas Turkish citizens exhibited higher rates of early-term births and cesarean sections. This paradox may reflect variations in obstetric practice patterns, maternal preferences, and differing clinical thresholds for intervention within the host population. For instance, greater accessibility to elective cesarean sections among Turkish women may partly explain these observed differences.

A meta-analysis of 23 studies reported a higher risk of fetal death and preterm delivery in immigrant women [15]. Preterm delivery, anemia, low birth weight (< 2500 g) are risky conditions for both mother and newborn [16]. Stillbirth rates have been found to be higher among refugee women in some studies [17]. In this study, no fetal death was observed in refugees during a 5-year period. We believe that the absence of significant differences between the groups in terms of preterm and post-term deliveries, along with the timely delivery of refugee pregnant women, may have contributed to reduced fetal morbidity and mortality.

Refugee pregnant women delivered at mid-term, around the 39th week of gestation, whereas pregnant women from the Republic of Turkey delivered at early term, around the 38th week. Despite delivering later, Syrian refugee women had a higher rate of hospitalization in the second-level neonatal unit. The observed increase in secondary-level neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admissions among neonates born to Syrian refugee mothers likely reflects a multifactorial etiology [18]. Contributing factors may include nutritional deficiencies [19], language barriers, inadequate antenatal care [20], socioeconomic disadvantages, psychosocial stress related to forced migration [21], and limited access to comprehensive perinatal services. Notably, the absence of a concomitant rise in tertiary-level NICU admissions suggests that these factors predominantly contribute to intermediate neonatal morbidities rather than severe, life-threatening conditions. The health status of pregnant refugees and their newborns generally depends on similar factors as the local population. However, factors such as nutritional deficiencies, low education levels, and post-traumatic stress disorder related to the war environment can negatively affect fetal development [6]. Therefore, post-war nutritional support, early intervention, and promotion of healthy eating habits are crucial. Similar rates of neonatal hospitalization at the third-level unit suggest that follow-up care and health service provision are comparable between the groups and do not lead to serious complications. Furthermore, the fact that the mean age of the patients was far from adolescence likely contributed to reducing adverse obstetric outcomes.

Although disparities in maternal and neonatal health indicators have been reported among Syrian refugees in Turkey, a recent meta-analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity across studies regarding birth weight outcomes. While some studies have reported higher rates of LBW among refugee women, others have found no statistically significant differences compared to host populations [22]. In this study, the incidence of low birth weight (LBW) was found to be higher among migrant adolescent pregnancies compared to their Turkish counterparts. Specifically, the rate of LBW infants born to Syrian adolescent mothers was statistically significantly greater [23]. A study conducted in Kocaeli identified differences in the rates of preterm birth and the incidence of extremely low birth weight infants (< 1000 g) between Turkish women and Syrian refugees. However, the overall difference in low birth weight rates (< 2500 g) was smaller and appeared to be influenced by context-specific factors, such as healthcare access and sociodemographic conditions [14]. Variations in birth weight outcomes across studies can be attributed to several factors, including differences in population demographics, the quality of obstetric care, and the level of access to healthcare services. Moreover, migrant pregnant women often experience reduced prenatal follow-up and suboptimal nutritional status, which may contribute to subtle yet clinically meaningful differences in neonatal birth weights.

When the newborn weights were evaluated, it was observed that each group had similar weights. When neonatal weights were divided into subgroups, no difference was found between the two groups. In a study, birth weights were found to be similar [24]. A Canadian study found that pregnant women of Indian nationality were more likely to have low birth weight babies [25]. In teenage pregnancies, refugee women have been shown to be at risk for low birth weight [26]. While the available scientific evidence suggests that non-refugee status may pose a risk in terms of newborn weight, there are no clear conclusions.

When evaluating all deliveries, it was found that the number of cesarean sections was twice that of vaginal deliveries. The cesarean section rate was higher than the vaginal delivery rate among both Turkish and Syrian refugee pregnant women. The higher prevalence of cesarean deliveries and preterm births among Turkish citizens in our cohort is noteworthy. Existing literature suggests that systemic and cultural factors—including provider preferences, medicolegal concerns, and maternal request—may play a more significant role than biological differences in driving these disparities. Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings, highlighting that a variety of determinants such as maternal age, parity, clinical condition, and health system practices influence cesarean section rates [27].

However, in a study involving 2,866 Syrian refugee pregnant women, the rate of normal vaginal delivery was found to be twice as high as that of cesarean Sect [28]. In a study, Caesarean section rates were found to be higher in Turkish pregnant women [24]. Worldwide, the rate of medical necessity for cesarean section is above 10–15%, although this is controversial [29]. Comparing Syrian refugee women with their Jordanian counterparts found an increase in the overall cesarean section rate [30]. There was no difference in the indications for cesarean section in the two groups. One study showed that the cesarean section rate in migrant women was alarmingly high and most of these deliveries were emergency cesarean Sect [29]. Refugee pregnant women may be at high risk of emergency cesarean delivery due to lack of follow-up and inadequate access to health facilities. The most common reason for the high cesarean section rate may be the reported high pre-cesarean section rate. Despite having better access to healthcare services, Turkish citizens in our cohort demonstrated a higher prevalence of preterm delivery and cesarean section compared to Syrian refugees. Similar trends have been documented in previous studies, which often attribute elevated cesarean rates among non-refugee populations to provider preferences, medicolegal considerations, and maternal request. These systemic and cultural factors, rather than inherent biological vulnerability, may largely explain the observed disparities. Hepatitis B vaccination is the most cost-effective and efficient method for preventing and controlling hepatitis B infection. Both the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommend administering the hepatitis B vaccine to infants at birth. Currently, over 110 countries provide the hepatitis B vaccine free of charge to infants aged 0–1 year. In our study, the rates of vitamin K injection and hepatitis B vaccination were similar between both groups. Neonatal vaccination plays a critical role in primary prevention. However, inadequate prenatal follow-up may increase the risk of incomplete vaccination [31]. For neonatal immunization, it is important to follow vaccination schedules and vaccinate newborns.

Limitations

One important limitation of our study is the relatively small sample size of the Syrian refugee group. This inevitably decreases the statistical power and may mask subtle associations. Therefore, our results should be interpreted with caution, as they are more reliable for detecting moderate-to-large differences rather than small effect sizes. Larger prospective and multicenter studies are required to confirm the observed trends and to provide more robust evidence.

Conclusion

In conclusion, although our study reveals significant trends, the relatively small sample size of the Syrian refugee cohort limits the statistical power, potentially obscuring subtle differences between groups. It is imperative that healthcare facilities enhance the accessibility and quality of services to improve maternal and neonatal health outcomes among refugee pregnant women and their infants. Future large-scale, multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings and to inform targeted interventions aimed at optimizing care for refugee populations.
