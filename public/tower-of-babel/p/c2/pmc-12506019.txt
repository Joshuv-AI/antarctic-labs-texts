PD-L1 has a stronger effect on increasing headache risk than PD-1: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yuan Tian, Zixuan Feng, Feng Feng, Meng Fan, Yu Hu, Zhuoqi Li, Yuanyuan Wang, Kai Zhang, Qi Dang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis meta-analysis aimed to clarify the risk of headaches caused by programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) and programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors.MethodRelevant clinical trials were screened using PubMed. The risk of headache associated with PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors was calculated using the mirror principle and PRISMA guidelines.ResultsA total of 33 clinical trials were screened for this comprehensive meta-analysis, yielding nine mirror-pairing groups. The risk of headache with PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors was significantly higher than that with placebo (OR = 1.48, 95% CI: [1.06, 2.06], Z = 2.33, P = 0.02) and similar to that with chemotherapy drugs (OR = 1.06, 95% CI: [0.84, 1.34], Z = 0.49, P = 0.62). When PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors are combined with other immune-related drugs, the risk of headaches increases to varying degrees. However, when combined with chemotherapy, this risk did not increase significantly (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: [0.78, 1.32], Z = 0.11, P = 0.91). Compared with PD-1, PD-L1 inhibitors were associated with a higher headache risk (OR = 1.39, 95% CI: [0.64, 2.12], Z = 1.51, P = 0.13).ConclusionPD-L1 has a stronger effect on increasing headache risk than PD-1. Similar to the comparison of PD-1 or PD-L1 versus chemotherapy, PD-1 or PD-L1 plus chemotherapy did not significantly increase headache risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-03911-x.

Introduction

In numerous clinical trials, programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) or programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors have demonstrated promising clinical efficacy and are widely recognized by doctors [1–37]. However, reports of drug-related toxicities are increasing [1–37]. Although headache is a rare PD-1 or PD-L1-related neurotoxicity, it can significantly affect daily life and hinder clinical assessments, such as distinguishing headaches caused by brain metastases. Our previous meta-analysis included a limited number of clinical trials, necessitating further research to fully elucidate the risk of PD-1/PD-L1-related headaches [38].

Owing to the lack of direct comparative clinical trials between PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors, it is impossible to directly assess the differences in their headache risks [1–38]. However, meta-analysis using mirror-pairing principles may help address this issue [39, 40]. Therefore, by integrating the traditional PRISMA-guided meta-analysis with mirror-pairing principles, we designed this study to comprehensively evaluate the risk of headaches associated with PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors.

Method

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) reporting guidelines were adopted and prioritized for all analyses as previously outlined [41].

Screening process strategy

The search process for all clinical trials was completed according to the PICOS (participants, interventions, comparisons, outcomes, and study design) principle as specified [41]. Keywords were mainly limited to solid tumors and PD-1 or PD-L1 related drugs. Phase III clinical trials were the preferred choice for inclusion, whereas other types of clinical trials were considered as candidates. Irrespective of the phase, all clinical trials were required to include a control group. The publication period of the involved clinical trials was confined to the past decade, and the language used for publication was strictly English.

Four researchers (Yuan Tian, Feng Zixuan, Feng Feng, and Meng Fan) independently conducted the literature search and checked for clinical trial data inclusion eligibility. The corresponding author coordinated all the final disagreements and decided which data were ultimately adopted and used.

Assessment of study quality and publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, as described in previous studies [42–44]. The P-value of Egger’s test, which depicts the symmetry of funnel plots, was used to determine the existence of publication bias, and data with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant [42–44].

Quality assessment of all enrolled clinical trials, including selection, performance, detection, attrition, reporting, and other biases, was performed using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS) [45, 46].

Exposure of interest

The basic characteristics of all enrolled clinical trials, including publication year, NCT number, trial name, treatment regimens, treatment line, tumor types, number of patients involved, and occurrence of headaches, were collected and summarized in a single table. After screening, information on clinical trials that could be used for mirror-pairing was separately listed and summarized in another table. Based on different treatment regimens, all clinical trials were divided into different groups. Only the data for all grades of headaches were extracted and used for the final comprehensive analysis.

Assessment of heterogeneity and statistical analyses

Heterogeneity was examined and thoroughly assessed. Based on the Cochrane’s Q statistical test value [45, 46], heterogeneity was measured using the DerSimonian-Laird method and I² values. It was categorized into three levels: low (I² <25%), moderate (I² = 25–50%), and high (I² >50%) [41, 46].

The analyses were conducted using Review Manager version 5.3. For forest plot analysis, the random-effects (RE) model was used to calculate the OR and 95% CI. The fixed-effects (FE) model was applied solely to the funnel plots [47]. A P-value below 0.05 was set at P < 0.05. Further subgroup analyses were conducted when moderate to high heterogeneity was observed.

Mirror-principle pairing

The mirror-pairing principle aims to enhance similarity and minimize heterogeneity between selected clinical trials [39, 40]. Six key reference indicators were considered: tumor type, pathological type, treatment regimen, treatment line, phase stage, and patient number. Trials with the highest similarity and lowest heterogeneity were prioritized for pairing.

Furthermore, each dataset was used only once. When tumors were located in the same organ, further subdivision was required based on specific pathological types. If insufficient clinical trials were available for pairing, the reference indicators were excluded. However, such exclusions were permitted only if prior subgroup analyses confirmed that the omitted indicator had no statistically significant effect on the results. When pairing trials involving combination therapies with PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors, all non-PD-1 and PD-L1 therapeutic components should be identical.

Results of mirror-pairing

After reviewing the subgroup analysis results and mirror-pairing principles, nine mirror-pairings were identified (Table 2) [1– [5, 7, 9, 15, 18, 24, 26]28– [30, 32, 37]. Among these, eight mirror-pairings enabled direct comparisons between PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors [1– [5, 7, 9, 24, 26]28– [30, 32, 37], while one involved a combination regimen comparison [15, 18]. As observed previously, NSCLC accounted for the highest proportion of tumor types (n = 4; Table 2) [1, 2, 4, 5, 9, 24], followed by UC (n = 2; Table 2) [3, 26, 28, 37].

Risk of headache in Group A (PD-1/PD-L1 vs. Chemotherapy)

Nine clinical trials were included in this meta-analysis (Table 1) [1–10]. Preliminary analysis showed that headache risk associated with PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors was comparable to that associated with chemotherapy (OR = 1.06, 95% CI: [0.84, 1.34], I² = 19%, Z = 0.49, P = 0.62; Fig. 2). Similar results were observed in the subgroup analysis comparing PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors (OR = 1.09 vs. OR = 1.10; Fig. 2A). Further analysis indicated that PD-1 or PD-L1 treatment did not significantly affect the risk of headaches (Fig. 2B). Subgroup analysis suggested that the low heterogeneity originated from the two NSCLC clinical trials (CheckMate057 and KEYNOTE-01; Fig. 2C) [1, 2]. No significant publication bias was detected in the corresponding funnel plots (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Risk of headache in Group B (PD-1/PD-L1 + Chemotherapy vs. Chemotherapy)

Eleven clinical trials were included in this meta-analysis (Table 1) [10–21]. The addition of PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors to the treatment regimen did not significantly increase the risk of headache (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: [0.78, 1.32], I² = 56%, Z = 0.11, P = 0.91; Fig. 3) [10–21]. Headache risk was slightly higher in the PD-L1 subgroup (OR = 1.14) than in the PD-1 subgroup (OR = 0.89); however, this difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 3A). However, further subgroup analysis indicated that specific drugs and tumor types significantly affected the headache risk (Fig. 3B-D). When PD-1 was combined with chemotherapy in nasopharyngeal cancer, the headache risk was significantly reduced (OR = 0.48, 95% CI: [0.26, 0.88]; Fig. 3C-D).

Risk of headache in Group C (PD-1/PD-L1 vs. Placebo)

Eight clinical trials were included in this meta-analysis (Table 1) [22–32]. Compared to placebo, PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors increased the headache risk (OR = 1.48, 95% CI: [1.06, 2.06], I² = 63%, Z = 2.33, P = 0.02; Fig. 4), particularly in the PD-L1 subgroup (OR = 1.78, 95% CI: [1.00, 3.16], I² = 79%, Z = 1.97, P = 0.05; Fig. 4A). This finding confirmed that PD-L1 inhibitors posed a higher headache risk than PD-1 inhibitors did, as verified by further subgroup analyses (Fig. 4B).

Risk of headache for mirror-pairings

Eight mirror pairs (PD-1 vs. PD-L1) were used for preliminary analysis (Table 2) [1–5, 7, 9, 24, 26, 28–30, 32, 37]. Findings indicated that PD-L1 inhibitors had a higher risk of causing headaches than PD-1 inhibitors (OR = 1.39, 95% CI: [0.64, 2.12], I² = 70%, Z = 1.51, P = 0.13; Fig. 6A), which is consistent with previous analyses (Figs. 2A and 3A, and 4B). After excluding the influence of tumor type on mirror-pairing (mirror-pairing 07 and mirror-pairing 08) [7, 26, 30, 37], similar results were obtained (OR = 1.52, 95%CI: [0.89, 2.60], I2 = 78%, Z = 1.53 (P = 0.13); Fig. 6B). Furthermore, this risk trend was particularly pronounced when PD-L1 was used as a second-line regimen (OR = 1.63, 95% CI: [0.45, 5.93]; Fig. 6C) [1, 2, 4, 5].

Discussion

With increasing use of PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors, various toxicities have been reported [1–38]. Although headache is an occasional PD-1 or PD-L1-related neurotoxicity, it can significantly affect daily life and complicate clinical assessment, particularly when distinguishing it from headaches caused by brain metastases. This challenge is amplified in combined treatment regimens [10–21], making it harder to determine the source of the headache. Therefore, a comprehensive meta-analysis is essential to explain the roles of PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors in headache. Furthermore, identifying headache risk differences between PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors could aid the selection of immune drugs in clinical practice [39, 40].

Data from 33 clinical trials covering 37 studies were analyzed (Table 1) [1–37], allowing for detailed subgroup analysis to assess the factors influencing the results (Figs. 2, 3 and 5, and 6). PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors significantly increased headache risk compared to placebo (OR = 1.48, 95%CI: [1.06, 2.06], Z = 2.33, P = 0.02; Fig. 4) and showed a risk level comparable to that of chemotherapy drugs (OR = 1.06, 95%CI: [0.84, 1.34], Z = 0.49, P = 0.62; Fig. 2). When combined with other immune-related drugs, headache risk increased to varying degrees (Fig. 5) [25, 26]33– [36]. However, this risk did not significantly increase with chemotherapy (OR = 1.02, 95%CI: [0.78, 1.32], Z = 0.11, P = 0.91; Fig. 3) [10–21]. To minimize the risk of headaches, chemotherapy is preferred to other immune-related drugs in combination regimens (Figs. 3 and 5).

Various forms of subgroup analysis indicated that PD-L1 might increase the risk of headache (Figs. 3A and 4A) [10–32], creating the foundation for our subsequent mirror-pairing analysis. When subgroup analysis based on tumor type and treatment lines showed no statistical difference (Figs. 2B-C and 4B), the principle of mirror pairing was relaxed, as appropriate. After evaluating the impact of various factors on the analysis results, eight mirror pairs (PD-1 vs. PD-L1) were obtained and used for the preliminary analysis (Table 2) [1– [5, 7, 9, 24, 26]28– [30, 32, 37]. Regardless of the subgrouping method (Fig. 6), the analysis consistently suggested that PD-L1 inhibitors were more likely to cause headaches than PD-1 inhibitors, which is consistent with previous results (Figs. 3A and 4A) [10–32]. This study represents the first use of the mirror-pairing method to compare PD-1 and PD-L1 in headache risk, highlighting its innovation and clinical relevance.

Heterogeneity varied throughout the analysis, and an extensive subgroup analysis was conducted to identify potential sources (Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6) [1–37]. Based on subgroup analysis results and clinical trial data, heterogeneity was primarily attributed to intrinsic differences (Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 and Supplementary Fig. 1) [1–37]. A review of funnel plots (Supplementary Figs. 2–6) revealed no significant publication bias.

In the real world, the dosage of drugs and patient-reported outcomes may also be important factors affecting the risk of headaches. Owing to the limited data on different drug dosages included in all enrolled clinical trials, the impact of such factors on the risk of headache occurrence needs to be further verified.

The limitations of the mirror-pairing principle are also evident as this method relies heavily on the high similarity of related paired clinical trials, thus having significant limitations in its applicability. However, owing to the lack of head-to-head clinical trials on PD-1 and PD-L1, this method might be a better choice for comparing the toxic side effects of PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors.

Conclusions

PD-L1 has a stronger effect on increasing the headache risk than PD-1. Similar to the comparison of PD-1 or PD-L1 versus chemotherapy, PD-1 or PD-L1 plus chemotherapy did not significantly increase headache risk.
