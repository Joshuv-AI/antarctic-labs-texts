The class III peroxidase OsPrx20 is a key regulator of stress response and growth in rice
Authors: Tao Shen, Qingwen Wang, Huining Ju, Ruonan Tian, Donghuan Fu, Xiaona Bu, Runjiao Yan, Fengjuan Xu, Dan Chen, Huan Zhang, Jiexiong Hu, Zhengguang Zhang, Lan Ni, Mingyi Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In plants, reactive oxygen species (ROS) are maintained at strictly low intracellular levels, which is a prerequisite for their function as signaling molecules. Class III peroxidases serve as core regulators of intracellular ROS homeostasis when environmental stimuli trigger long-term responses, that cause ROS accumulation. Here, we identified a rice class III peroxidase, OsPrx20, which is a target of Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase (OsDMI3) and is phosphorylated at its threonine (Thr)-244 site by OsDMI3, thereby positively regulating osmotic stress tolerance while negatively affecting blast resistance in rice. Overexpression of OsPrx20 enhances osmotic stress tolerance through intracellular ROS scavenging and simultaneously promotes panicle enlargement with increased grain size. Conversely, the loss of OsPrx20 potentiates immunity against rice blast infection by elevating intracellular ROS levels, which results in reduced plant height and smaller panicles. Crucially, abscisic acid (ABA)-dependent phosphorylation of OsPrx20 at Thr-244 by OsDMI3 promotes intracellular ROS scavenging in rice, thereby enhancing osmotic stress tolerance without compromising growth, though at the expense of blast resistance. In summary, this study uncovers an essential regulatory mechanism that directly activates OsPrx20 within ABA signaling, highlighting its multifunctional role across contrasting physiological processes.

Introduction

Environmental stimuli (e.g., drought, salinity, and temperature extremes) and diseases (e.g., rice blast and bacterial blight) are the main external factors that threaten rice (Oryza sativa L.) growth (Yu et al., 2022). As a sessile organism, rice has evolved a wide range of signal response mechanisms to adapt to such challenges. These mechanisms include reactive oxygen species (ROS), which participate in numerous processes throughout the plant life cycle (Mhamdi and Van Breusegem, 2018; Mittler et al., 2022). Research advances have led to wide recognition of their roles as signaling molecules that regulate growth and mediate responses to environmental stimuli in plants (Apel and Hirt, 2004; Baxter et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2023a). In particular, the apoplast, which functions as the interface for signal exchange between plant cells and the environment, is involved in ROS accumulation during plant responses to environmental stimuli (Kimura et al., 2017). Apoplastic hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) production primarily originates from plasma membrane-localized nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidases (respiratory burst oxidase homologs, RBOHs) (Suzuki et al., 2011). In Arabidopsis, AtRbohD/F regulate salt-induced antioxidant defense (Ben Rejeb et al., 2015), AtRbohD modulates immunity (Lee et al., 2020; Pfeilmeier et al., 2021), and AtRbohC/H/J control root hair growth (Mangano et al., 2017). In rice, OsRbohB/E promote apoplastic H2O2 production in response to abiotic stresses (Shi et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2023b; Zhao et al., 2025); OsRbohI induces ROS to enhance blast resistance; mutations in various OsRboh genes reduce resistance to rice blast, bacterial blight, and sheath blight; and all osrboh mutants display one or more compromised yield traits (Zhu et al., 2024). Even under stress conditions, while RBOHs mediate elevated extracellular ROS levels, intracellular ROS levels remain tightly controlled. An increase in extracellular ROS production does not necessarily result in elevated intracellular ROS levels (Waszczak et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2024). Moreover, elevated ROS concentrations in individual subcellular compartments are short-lived (Mittler et al., 2022). However, abiotic and biotic stresses are long-term processes that generate diverse intracellular ROS with widely varying lifetimes, leading to ROS accumulation (Cejudo et al., 2021). This accumulation results in oxidative damage, including DNA and RNA damage, protein denaturation, enzyme inactivation, and membrane lipid peroxidation, ultimately causing cell death (Mittler et al., 2022). These observations highlight the importance and complexity of maintaining low intracellular ROS levels.

To address this challenge, plant cells possess a well-developed, interconnected antioxidant system to counteract the threat of ROS. This system includes superoxide dismutase, catalase (CAT), ascorbate peroxidase, and other related enzymes that regulate ROS (Mittler, 2002; Bhatt and Tripathi, 2011). In addition, a large family of peroxidases (EC 1.11.1.X; Prxs) in plants, which catalyze the detoxification of H2O2 and related peroxides, has been identified across multiple organisms. Based on protein structure, the peroxidase superfamily is divided into heme and non-heme peroxidases (Kidwai et al., 2020). According to their functions, the heme peroxidase family is further classified into three subclasses: class I (ascorbate peroxidases), class II (lignin peroxidases), and class III (secreted peroxidases). These subclasses share a similar three-dimensional structure and contain a heme group composed of protoporphyrin IX and Fe3+ (Liu et al., 2021). Class I Prxs are found in most organisms except animals, particularly in prokaryotes, suggesting an evolutionary origin for other Prxs. They include cytochrome c peroxidases (EC 1.11.1.5), CAT (EC 1.11.1.6), and ascorbate peroxidases (EC 1.11.1.11), whose primary cellular function is the scavenging of excess H2O2. Class II Prxs include manganese peroxidases (EC 1.11.1.13) and lignin peroxidases (EC 1.11.1.14), which play a key role in the degradation of lignin-containing detritus in soil because no other peroxidases are capable of degrading lignin (Cosio and Dunand, 2009; Jha et al., 2022). Class III Prxs (EC 1.11.1.7)—plant-specific heme oxidoreductases—are secreted into the extracellular space or vacuole. They are typically localized in the cytoplasm and cell wall, exhibit high thermal stability, and naturally occur as glycoproteins with a wide range of substrates and marked substrate specificity for phenols (Cosio and Dunand, 2009; Shigeto and Tsutsumi, 2016). However, the molecular mechanisms by which most class III Prxs regulate physiological processes remain unknown.

Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) and rice genomes encode 73 and 138 class III Prxs, respectively (Passardi et al., 2004; Liu et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2023). These enzymes participate in diverse physiological processes, including redox regulation, abiotic and biotic stress resistance, lignin biosynthesis, seed germination, and cell elongation; their roles in stress resilience and growth have received particular attention in recent years. In abiotic stress tolerance, most studies have emphasized that overexpression of class III Prxs enhances peroxidase activity to scavenge excess ROS. For example, TaPrx-2A overexpression enhances salt stress tolerance by scavenging ROS in wheat (Su et al., 2020), IbPrx17 overexpression improves salt and drought tolerance in sweet potato (Zhang et al., 2022a), and OsPrx114 overexpression enhances drought tolerance in rice (Zheng et al., 2023). Class III Prxs also exhibit functional diversity in biotic stress resistance. For example, downregulation of AtPRX33/34 blocks the oxidative burst and increases pathogen susceptibility in Arabidopsis (Daudi et al., 2012). Silencing of CaPO2 (CaPrx2) increases disease susceptibility in pepper (Choi et al., 2007). Conversely, suppression of Ep5C (LePrx06) enhances resistance to bacterial speck via H2O2 accumulation in tomato (Coego et al., 2005), and overexpression of OsPrx30 increases susceptibility to bacterial blight through excessive scavenging of H2O2 (Liu et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2025). Collectively, these results suggest that class III Prxs play distinct roles in response to abiotic and biotic stresses by regulating ROS levels. Furthermore, class III Prxs contribute to plant growth. For example, AtPrx62/69 promote root hair growth under low temperature (Pacheco et al., 2022), AtPrx17 participates in lignified tissue formation (Cosio et al., 2017), and AtPrx9/40 maintain tapetal cell wall integrity during development in Arabidopsis (Jacobowitz et al., 2019). Although these studies indicate divergent functions for class III Prxs, one point remains clear: they exhibit functional pleiotropy by regulating ROS homeostasis.

Importantly, enzyme activities must be precisely modulated by post-translational modifications during stress, such as phosphorylation (Zhang et al., 2022b). In rice, OsDMI3 is the only calcium (Ca2+)/calmodulin (CaM)-dependent protein kinase (CCaMK) activated by abscisic acid (ABA) under stress, enhancing antioxidant protection through phosphorylation. On one hand, OsDMI3 phosphorylates OsRbohB to generate H2O2, thereby amplifying ROS signaling (Wang et al., 2023b). On the other hand, it phosphorylates OsMKK1 to activate the OsMKK1–OsMPK1 cascade, transmitting signals downstream to scavenge excess ROS by activating the antioxidant defense system under stress (Chen et al., 2021). Moreover, OsDMI3 phosphorylates OsUXS3 to promote the OsUXS3–OsCATB interaction, enhancing CAT activity for H2O2 scavenging and improving oxidative stress tolerance (Ni et al., 2022). Collectively, because redox represents a dynamic equilibrium process, these studies demonstrate that OsDMI3 regulates both ROS production and scavenging via phosphorylation. While the direct mechanisms of OsDMI3-mediated ROS production are well established, its phosphorylation of OsMKK1 and OsUXS3 represents an indirect scavenging pathway. Direct catalytic mechanisms for ROS scavenging by OsDMI3 remain poorly characterized.

In this study, we identified OsPrx20, a rice class III Prx, as a phosphorylated substrate of OsDMI3, which positively regulates osmotic stress tolerance but negatively regulates blast resistance in rice. Importantly, ABA-dependent phosphorylation of OsPrx20 at Thr-244 by OsDMI3 promotes intracellular ROS scavenging in rice, thereby enhancing osmotic stress tolerance without compromising growth, albeit at the expense of blast resistance. Previous genetic approaches have improved stress resistance in plants by amplifying responses to stress signals; however, these have often come at the cost of impaired growth and reduced yield (Zhang et al., 2020). For example, ABA enhances stress responses but also inhibits growth (Julkowska and Testerink, 2015; Zhao et al., 2016). By contrast, OsPrx20 clearly overcomes this limitation, making it an ideal candidate gene. Although overexpression or knockout of OsPrx20 has not fully harmonized all desirable traits, its functional versatility provides a molecular link across contrasting physiological processes, offering new insights into breaking the growth–stress resistance trade-off.

Results

OsPrx20 is a target protein of OsDMI3

OsPrx20 was isolated by screening a rice complementary DNA (cDNA) library using OsDMI3 as bait. First, the OsPrx20–OsDMI3 interaction was confirmed through multiple assays. A yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assay showed that yeast cells co-transformed with OsPrx20-AD and OsDMI3-BD exhibited robust growth on SD/-Trp-Leu-His-Ade medium supplemented with X-α-Gal (Figure 1A). A glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assay demonstrated that OsDMI3 directly interacted with OsPrx20 in vitro (Figure 1B), while a co-immunoprecipitation (coIP) assay in tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) leaves verified the interaction in vivo (Figure 1C). A luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assay further showed that a reconstituted firefly luciferase signal was detected in tobacco leaves transiently co-infiltrated with OsPrx20-nLUC and OsDMI3-cLUC (Figure 1D). A bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay in rice protoplasts also revealed that OsPrx20 interacted with OsDMI3 to produce yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) in the cytoplasm; there was no overlap with the blue fluorescence of 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), used as a nuclear marker, or the N-(3-triethylammoniopropyl)-4-(6-(4-(diethylamino)phenyl)hexatrienyl)pyridinium dibromide (FM4-64), used as a plasma membrane marker (Figure 1E). Subcellular co-localization in tobacco leaves also showed that the expressed OsDMI3-mCherry fusion protein was localized to the cytoplasm and nucleus, whereas OsPrx20-green fluorescent protein (GFP) and OsDMI3-mCherry co-localized exclusively in the cytoplasm (Figure 1F). This result is consistent with the BiFC assay and further supports the interaction of OsPrx20 and OsDMI3 in the cytoplasm. Second, because OsDMI3 can phosphorylate multiple substrates, including OsMKK1, OsUXS3, and OsRbohB (Chen et al., 2021; Ni et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023b), recombinant OsPrx20-His was incubated with OsDMI3-GST, and polypeptide fragments of OsPrx20 were analyzed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) (Figure 1G). Thr-244 of OsPrx20 was identified as the phosphorylation site targeted by OsDMI3 in vitro (Figure 1H).

Additionally, to determine the role and localization of OsPrx20 in rice, we measured the transcript levels of OsPrx20 in various tissues. The gene was expressed in leaves, stems, and roots; transcript levels were highest in stems and almost undetectable in the elongation and meristematic zones of roots (Supplemental Figure 2A). β-Glucuronidase (GUS) staining in transgenic plants carrying proOsPrx20-GUS was consistent with the quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) results: staining was strongest in stems, followed by leaves and the maturation zone of roots. In contrast, no staining was observed in the elongation or meristematic zones of roots (Supplemental Figure 2B). Subcellular localization analysis showed that OsPrx20-YFP was confined to the cytoplasm and did not overlap with either the blue fluorescence of DAPI, used as a nuclear marker, or the red fluorescence of FM4-64, used as a plasma membrane marker, in rice protoplasts (Supplemental Figure 3A). In contrast, YFP was distributed throughout the cell when the empty vector (pXZP008-YFP) alone was transformed. This result was further supported by heterologous expression in tobacco cells, where OsPrx20 exclusively localized in the cytoplasm and showed no overlap with either DAPI or FM4-64 staining (Supplemental Figure 3B). In addition, OsPrx20-GFP was detected outside the tobacco plasma membrane after plasmolysis, indicating that OsPrx20 is a typical secretory protein (Supplemental Figure 3C).

OsPrx20 positively regulates osmotic stress tolerance but negatively regulates blast resistance in rice

CCaMKs and class III Prxs involved in responses to abiotic and biotic stresses have been reported in previous studies (Wang et al., 2015; Ni et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022a). To determine whether OsPrx20 also exerts regulatory effects on abiotic and biotic stresses, two knockout (KO) mutant lines and two overexpression (OE) transgenic lines of OsPrx20 (osprx20-KO1, osprx20-KO2, OsPrx20-OE1, and OsPrx20-OE2) were generated (Supplemental Figures 4A and 4B). These lines were treated with 20% (w/v) polyethylene glycol (PEG)-6000 solution to simulate abiotic stress or infected with the wild-type (WT) Magnaporthe oryzae (Guy11) strain to simulate biotic stress. After PEG treatment, osprx20-KO plants showed lower osmotic stress tolerance than WT, whereas OsPrx20-OE plants showed higher tolerance (Figure 2A). Following a week of recovery after re-watering, osprx20-KO plants had a lower survival rate (20%–30%) than WT (50%–55%), whereas OsPrx20-OE plants displayed a higher survival rate (75%–80%) (Figure 2B). Consistent with these phenotypic differences, osmotic stress induced excessive ROS accumulation in plant cells, leading to oxidative damage (Zhang et al., 2022b). After PEG treatment, osprx20-KO plants accumulated higher H2O2 levels and exhibited lower total peroxidase (POD) activity than WT, whereas OsPrx20-OE plants showed the opposite trend (Supplemental Figures 5A and 5B). 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining also revealed lighter staining in osprx20-KO and darker staining in OsPrx20-OE compared to WT (Supplemental Figure 5C). Moreover, malondialdehyde (MDA) content and electrolyte leakage rate—two critical indicators of oxidative damage—were significantly higher in osprx20-KO plants than in WT and OsPrx20-OE plants after PEG treatment, while OsPrx20-OE plants showed the lowest values (Supplemental Figures 5D and 5E). These results indicate that OsPrx20 acts as a positive regulator by scavenging intracellular ROS, thereby alleviating oxidative damage under osmotic stress. Conversely, osprx20-KO plants showed greater blast resistance than WT, whereas OsPrx20-OE plants were more sensitive (Figure 2C). After 7 days of spray inoculation with Guy11 spores, osprx20-KO leaves showed lower lesion severity (predominantly type 1) than WT leaves (predominantly type 2), whereas OsPrx20-OE leaves exhibited higher lesion severity (predominantly type 3 or 4) (Figure 2D). Quantification of M. oryzae propagation in infected leaves using the housekeeping gene MoPot2 showed that hyphal propagation was lower in osprx20-KO leaves compared to WT and OsPrx20-OE leaves (Figure 2E). Furthermore, both the transcript and protein levels of OsPrx20 were significantly downregulated under rice blast infection (Figures 2F and 2G). OsPrx20 modulates rice growth and panicle maturation

Plant height and tiller number are key agronomic traits in rice growth. At maturity, osprx20-KO plants exhibited reduced plant height compared with WT, whereas OsPrx20-OE plants showed no difference, suggesting that OsPrx20 is required for stem elongation (Figures 3A and 3B). This requirement is consistent with its tissue-specific expression in stems (Supplemental Figures 2A and 2B). However, neither loss nor overexpression of OsPrx20 affected tiller number (Figure 3C). Lignin contributes to the growth–defense trade-off in plants, and class III Prxs are known to promote lignin biosynthesis (Almagro et al., 2009; Shigeto and Tsutsumi, 2016; Xie et al., 2018). To investigate a potential connection between OsPrx20 and lignin biosynthesis, lignin content was measured in osprx20-KO, OsPrx20-OE, and WT plants. No significant differences were observed in 15-day-old seedlings (Supplemental Figure 6A). However, osprx20-KO plants displayed reduced lignin content, whereas OsPrx20-OE plants showed increased lignin content in adult stems and roots (Supplemental Figure 6B). Other important agronomic traits influenced by OsPrx20 were assessed by comparing panicles and grains across osprx20-KO, OsPrx20-OE, and WT plants. Panicles of osprx20-KO were smaller than those of WT, whereas panicles of OsPrx20-OE were larger (Figure 3D). Neither knockout nor overexpression of OsPrx20 affected the seed-setting rate; however, grain number was reduced in osprx20-KO and increased in OsPrx20-OE compared with WT (Figures 3E and 3F). Significant variation was also observed in thousand-kernel weight across the three genotypes: OsPrx20-OE seeds were ∼5% heavier than both osprx20-KO and WT seeds (Figures 3G and 3H). Further statistical analysis revealed no significant differences in grain length, width, or thickness between osprx20-KO and WT. Similarly, grain thickness did not differ between OsPrx20-OE and WT. However, OsPrx20-OE grains exhibited significant increases in length (∼9%) and width (∼7%) compared with WT (Figures 3I–3K). Taken together, these results demonstrate that OsPrx20 regulates rice growth and panicle maturation. Although no significant difference in thousand-grain weight was observed between osprx20-KO and WT, the loss of OsPrx20 still reduced single-plant yield due to a decrease in grain number per panicle.

Under PEG treatment, OsPrx20-OE, OsPrx20T244A-OE, and OsPrx20T244D-OE plants showed improved phenotype and survival rate compared with WT. However, significantly greater osmotic stress tolerance was observed in OsPrx20T244D-OE than in OsPrx20-OE, while OsPrx20T244A-OE showed weaker tolerance (Figure 4B). OsPrx20T244D-OE had the highest survival rate (∼85%), far exceeding those of OsPrx20-OE (∼75%) and OsPrx20T244A-OE (∼60%) (Figure 4C). Indicators of oxidative damage were also consistent with these phenotypes. OsPrx20T244D-OE displayed the lowest MDA content, lowest electrolyte leakage rate, and weakest DAB staining, whereas OsPrx20T244A-OE exhibited stronger oxidative damage, as indicated by more intense DAB staining, compared with OsPrx20-OE after treatment (Supplemental Figure 7). We further quantified OsPrx20 activity using a POD assay kit. Even without treatment, OsPrx20T244D-OE already showed higher activity compared with the other lines. After treatment, OsPrx20T244D-OE maintained the highest activity level, while OsPrx20T244A-OE showed lower activity than OsPrx20-OE (Figure 4D). These results indicate that phosphorylation of OsPrx20 at Thr-244 enhances its ability to scavenge accumulated ROS under osmotic stress. Interestingly, prokaryotic expression of OsPrx20—whether OsPrx20, OsPrx20T244A, or OsPrx20T244D—showed no POD activity (Supplemental Figure 8), suggesting that OsPrx20 requires post-translational modifications in vivo to acquire peroxidase activity. However, under M. oryzae infection, Thr-244 phosphorylation played the opposite role by further increasing susceptibility (Figures 4E and 4F). Although OsPrx20-OE, OsPrx20T244A-OE, and OsPrx20T244D-OE all exhibited more lesions than WT and osprx20-KO after spray inoculation with Guy11 spores, OsPrx20T244D-OE developed the most lesions, predominantly of types 4–5, with the greatest hyphal accumulation. In contrast, OsPrx20T244A-OE showed less severe symptoms than OsPrx20-OE, with lesions predominantly of type 3 (Figure 4G). H2O2 content remained lowest in OsPrx20T244D-OE leaves even after infection, whereas it was highest in osprx20-KO leaves (Supplemental Figure 9A). DAB staining also revealed the lightest staining in OsPrx20T244D-OE leaves, which were most severely infected; osprx20-KO leaves showed the deepest staining (Supplemental Figure 9B). The enhanced ROS-scavenging capacity conferred by Thr-244 phosphorylation in OsPrx20 significantly reduced blast resistance, thereby providing a clear explanation for the suppression of OsPrx20 under rice blast infection (Figure 2F and 2G). Furthermore, comparisons of panicle and grain characteristics among OsPrx20-OE, OsPrx20T244A-OE, and OsPrx20T244D-OE revealed no significant differences. Consistently, thousand-grain weight also showed no significant variation among these lines (Supplemental Figure 10). Similarly, lignin content did not vary among OsPrx20T244A-OE, OsPrx20T244D-OE, and OsPrx20-OE (Supplemental Figure 11). Taken together, overexpression of OsPrx20 promoted rice growth under natural conditions, but phosphorylation of OsPrx20 at Thr-244 did not further enhance this effect. Thr-244 is a specific phosphorylation site that functions under stress.

A previous study reported that knockdown of SlCCaMK significantly impaired disease resistance in tomato by suppressing H2O2 accumulation (Wang et al., 2015). However, in rice, no significant differences were observed among OsDMI3-OE, osdmi3-KO, and WT plants after 7 days of spray inoculation with Guy11 spores (Supplemental Figures 16A and 16B), although oxidative damage was significantly higher in osdmi3-KO compared with WT and OsDMI3-OE (Supplemental Figure 16C). These results indicate that OsDMI3 positively regulates antioxidant defense to alleviate oxidative damage during M. oryzae infection. In addition, a preliminary comparison of panicle and grain characteristics among OsDMI3-OE, osdmi3-KO, and WT plants revealed no significant differences (Supplemental Figure 17). This finding further suggests that the OsDMI3–OsPrx20 pathway primarily participates in stress responses without compromising growth.

Discussion

ROS levels fluctuate in response to physiological cues, but low intracellular levels are a prerequisite for proper signaling. Even as immune molecules, they are maintained at high levels only transiently (Mittler et al., 2022). A previous study demonstrated that transcript levels of two rice class III Prxs (Os05g04470 and Os10g39170) were repressed during blast infection (Li et al., 2017). Similarly, OsPrx20 is suppressed under blast infection (Figures 2F and 2G), likely allowing elevated ROS levels to be maintained for protection against pathogens. Notably, secondary ROS bursts trigger hypersensitive reactions to induce cell death during chronic infections but are absent in symbiotic interactions (Nanda et al., 2010), highlighting ROS-mediated trade-offs in immunity. The typical ROS producers, RBOHs, generate highly variable levels of apoplastic H2O2 with distinct roles in stress responses (Kaya et al., 2019; Shen et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024a). Similarly, the typical ROS scavengers, class III Prxs, play diverse regulatory roles in stress responses. OsPrx30, OsPrx114, and OsPrx130, all members of the rice class III Prx family, scavenge ROS generated under stress by increasing POD activity. Overexpression of OsPrx114 and OsPrx130 enhances drought tolerance, whereas overexpression of OsPrx30 reduces bacterial blight resistance (Liu et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2023; Tan et al., 2025). Critically, although these studies revealed functional divergence among different Prxs, whether a single Prx can mediate opposing responses under contrasting stresses has remained unexplored. Here, we demonstrate that OsPrx20 exhibits opposite effects under osmotic stress and M. oryzae infection (Figure 2A and 2C), establishing its central role in ROS-mediated stress responses. Furthermore, phosphorylation of OsPrx20 at Thr-244 enhances its ROS-scavenging capacity, driving these opposing effects (Figures 4B and 4E).

As a typical phytohormone, ABA critically regulates stress responses, particularly abiotic stress (Zhu, 2016), while also playing significant roles in biotic stress. Existing studies highlight the contradictory effects of ABA in biotic stress. For example, tomato chlorosis virus promotes infection by inhibiting ABA biosynthesis (Zang et al., 2025); OsSAPK10, an ABA signaling component, positively regulates blast resistance (Diao et al., 2025). In contrast, treatment with zinc oxide nanoparticles increases ROS accumulation and enhances blast resistance by reducing ABA levels in rice (Qiu et al., 2023). The rice synthesis-defective mutant osaba2 exhibits spontaneous cell death and enhanced disease resistance due to excessive H2O2 accumulation (Liao et al., 2018). These paradoxical outcomes originate from ABA-mediated ROS dynamics, as demonstrated by ABA-mediated phosphorylation of OsPrx20 at Thr-244, which enhances ROS-scavenging capacity but exerts opposing effects under osmotic stress versus blast infection. Loss of OsDMI3 (a Ca2+ signal decoder) or OsRbohB (a ROS producer) decreases ABA sensitivity (Ni et al., 2019). Conversely, loss of OsPrx20 enhances ABA sensitivity (Supplemental Figure 12). OsPrx20 scavenges ABA-induced intracellular ROS and alleviates the inhibitory effect of ABA on seed germination and root growth; phosphorylation at Thr-244 further strengthens these abilities (Figures 5A, 5C, and 5E). These observations underscore that the primary role of OsPrx20 in ABA-mediated stress responses is scavenging ROS to fine-tune cellular responses, rather than transmitting or amplifying ROS signaling. This critical role of OsPrx20 is regulated upstream by OsDMI3, a key factor that integrates and amplifies Ca2+ and H2O2 signaling via phosphorylation in ABA responses (Torres and Berlanga, 2023), such as ABA-mediated phosphorylation of Ser-191 in OsRbohB and Thr-25 in OsMKK1 (Chen et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023b), and Thr-244 in OsPrx20 (Figures 1G, 4A, and 5H). Importantly, unlike phosphorylation of OsRbohB by OsDMI3, which promotes H2O2 production to amplify ROS signaling, phosphorylation of OsPrx20 by OsDMI3 directly enhances its activity to scavenge intracellular ROS (Figure 5E). This mechanism is analogous to phosphorylation of OsUXS3 by OsDMI3, which enhances OsCATB activity and thereby scavenges ROS (Ni et al., 2022), but it is more direct than the OsDMI3–OsUXS3–OsCATB pathway. Another crucial point is that the ABA sensitivity of osdmi3/OsPrx20-OE was similar to that of osdmi3-KO, whereas osdmi3/OsPrx20T244D-OE exhibited significantly higher sensitivity than osdmi3-KO (Figures 6A and 6C). These results indicate that OsPrx20 acts downstream of OsDMI3 and participates in ABA signaling via OsDMI3-mediated phosphorylation at Thr-244. Consistently, ABA-induced phosphorylation of OsPrx20 at Thr-244 was not detected in osdmi3-KO (Figure 5G), providing direct evidence that Thr-244 phosphorylation depends on OsDMI3.

Paradoxically, ABA enhances stress responses but also inhibits plant growth. Although overexpression of OsPrx20 overcomes this limitation by enhancing osmotic stress tolerance without affecting growth and even promoting panicle development (Figures 3D and 3G), this approach is not an ideal breeding strategy because OsPrx20-OE increases susceptibility to M. oryzae. Moreover, under normal growth conditions, ABA levels and intracellular Ca2+ concentrations derived from environmental stimuli are low, which likely causes OsDMI3 inactivation. Remarkably, OsPrx20 activity was increased by phosphorylation at Thr-244 under normal conditions (Figures 4D and 6G), yet panicle and grain traits in OsPrx20-OE, OsPrx20T244A-OE, and OsPrx20T244D-OE showed no significant differences (Supplemental Figure 10), suggesting that phosphorylation of OsPrx20 at Thr-244 does not regulate rice growth. Thus, while single-gene manipulation faces trade-offs, targeted modification of a single phosphorylation site (e.g., Thr-244) may fine-tune stress responses without pleiotropic effects.

Lignin deposition requires H2O2 as a substrate, and lignin is essential for plant cell morphology, tissue integrity, and defense against environmental stresses and pests. A previous study reported that overexpression of CsPrx25 enhanced lignification as an apoplastic barrier against Xanthomonas citri subsp. citri infection in citrus (Li et al., 2020). However, while overexpression of OsPrx20 increased lignin content, it conferred inadequate disease resistance. During lignin synthesis, class III Prxs promote deposition in the cell wall (Cosio and Dunand, 2009; Shigeto and Tsutsumi, 2016). Nevertheless, these enzymes may also function beyond this compartment. Indeed, monolignols and enzymes are unlikely to directly interact because of their limited mobility in the cell wall during growth (Vanholme et al., 2010). ρ-Coumaric acid is an excellent peroxidase substrate in grasses and has been proposed as a radical shuttle for lignification, efficiently transferring its unpaired electron to sinapyl alcohol and lignin polymers (Hatfield et al., 2008). Plants deficient in lignin biosynthesis are severely impaired in development; most exhibit reduced plant height (Bonawitz et al., 2014). However, a previous study revealed that semi-dwarf crops typically display greater resistance to various diseases and insects (Liu et al., 2020), as observed in osprx20-KO (Figures 2C and 3A). Although lignin content increased in OsPrx20-OE plants (Supplemental Figure 6), plant height remained unaffected (Figure 3B). It is debatable whether the reduced plant height of osprx20-KO results directly from impaired lignin synthesis because lignification—particularly when ectopic—may also constrain growth (Ha et al., 2021). Lignin deposition is crucial for stem bending resistance, which directly determines rice’s ability to resist lodging. Unfortunately, although overexpression of OsPrx20 increased lignin content in stems and roots, this functional aspect was not further explored in the present study. Thus, it remains unclear whether the observed changes in growth were caused by disruption of ROS homeostasis under normal conditions due to loss of OsPrx20—leading to insufficient oxidation of substrates such as lignin precursors—or whether OsPrx20 directly regulates growth-related genes. In addition, osprx20-KO plants exhibited reduced plant height (Figure 3A) but no changes in tiller number or seed-setting rate (Figure 3C and 3F), consistent with the Green Revolution phenotype (Hedden, 2003). However, smaller panicles inevitably reduced yield per plant (Figure 3D), and the reduced degree of lignification is unlikely to improve resistance to lodging. Therefore, the growth phenotype associated with OsPrx20 warrants attention, suggesting that it is not always feasible to breed rice varieties that meet agronomic expectations by altering a single gene (e.g., knockout or overexpression of OsPrx20). Balancing growth and stress tolerance to achieve stable yield increases in rice while enhancing adaptation to the external environment is becoming increasingly important.

The primary targets of Green Super Rice breeding are greater stress resilience without compromising grain yield or quality (Yu et al., 2022). Notably, OsPrx20 regulates both stress responses and growth in rice. Enhancing OsPrx20 activity increases osmotic stress tolerance and promotes panicle development, whereas reducing its activity enhances blast resistance and decreases plant height. Specifically, OsPrx20 responds to ABA signaling through phosphorylation at Thr-244 by OsDMI3. This modification positively regulates osmotic stress tolerance and negatively regulates blast resistance but does not affect growth (Figure 7). Although not all desirable traits are harmonized in OsPrx20, this gene represents an exciting research direction. Materials and methods

Rice materials

Rice (Oryza sativa) materials used in this study included osprx20-KO, OsPrx20-OE, OsPrx20T244A-OE, OsPrx20T244D-OE, osdmi3-KO, OsDMI3-OE, osdmi3/OsPrx20-OE, osdmi3/OsPrx20T244D-OE, proOsPrx20-GUS, and wild type (Nipponbare). Among these, osdmi3-KO and OsDMI3-OE were previously reported (Ni et al., 2019). OsPrx20-OE, OsPrx20T244A-OE, OsPrx20T244D-OE, and proOsPrx20-GUS transgenic plants were generated via Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation using the pCambia1304-OsPrx20, pCambia1304-OsPrx20T244A, pCambia1304-OsPrx20T244D, and pCXGUS-proOsPrx20 (-2111 bp to -1 bp) constructs, respectively, into WT. osdmi3/OsPrx20-OE or osdmi3/OsPrx20T244D-OE transgenic plants were generated via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation of the pCambia1304-OsPrx20 or pCambia1304-OsPrx20T244D construct into osdmi3-KO. The osprx20-KO plants were CRISPR-Cas9-edited mutants; identification results are shown in Supplemental Figure 4A. All materials were selected on 50 μg/mL hygromycin B. Gene expression of each transgenic line was assayed using RT-qPCR, and results are shown in Supplemental Figure 4B. Homozygous T2 or T3 seeds of mutants and transgenic plants were used for further analyses. Growth and stress conditions of rice seedlings are provided in Supplemental Method 1. The primers used for mutant identification, RT-qPCR, and construction of recombinant vectors are listed in Supplemental Tables 1–3.

Pathogen inoculation

M. oryzae isolate Guy11, available in our laboratory, was used for rice blast inoculation. The Guy11 strain was first cultured on complete medium (CM) at 26°C under a 12-h/12-h light/dark cycle. Spores were collected 2 weeks after inoculation, resuspended to 1 × 105 spores/mL, and spray-inoculated onto three-leaf-stage rice seedlings. Inoculated plants were kept at 26°C with 90% humidity in a growth chamber in the dark for the first 24 h, then subjected to a 12-h/12-h light/dark cycle for 5 days. Disease symptoms were examined 5 days later. For fungal biomass assays, genomic DNA extracted from inoculated leaves was used for RT-qPCR as described in Supplemental Method S2. Relative fungal biomass was calculated as the ratio of Pot2 in M. oryzae to OsUbiquitin in rice. The primers used for RT-qPCR are listed in Supplemental Table 2.

Phenotypic analysis

ABA treatments for assessing seed germination and primary root growth were performed as previously described (Chen et al., 2021). Stress phenotyping with 20% (w/v) PEG-6000 solution was conducted as previously described (Wang et al., 2023b). Seedlings of uniform growth were pretreated in nutrient solution for 2 days to eliminate root damage. For PEG treatments, PEG was gradually added to the nutrient solution until a final concentration of 20% (w/v) was reached. For ABA treatments, seedlings were shielded to minimize light exposure. For M. oryzae infection, Guy11 cultures were grown on oatmeal agar medium and incubated for 2 weeks. Spores were collected in sterile water containing 0.05% (v/v) Tween-20. Three-week-old seedlings were used for spray inoculation with M. oryzae. Methods for spray inoculation were previously reported (Zhao et al., 2024b).

Immunoblotting (IB) analysis

Protein extraction and IB analysis were carried out as previously described (Ni et al., 2019). Target proteins were separated by 12% or 15% (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Antibodies used in this study included anti-GST antibody (AE001; ABclonal; Wuhan, China; 1:5,000, v/v), anti-His antibody (AE003; ABclonal; 1:5,000, v/v), anti-Actin (plant-specific) antibody (AC009; ABclonal; 1:5,000, v/v), anti-pThr antibody (AP1422; ABclonal; 1:1,000, v/v), anti-phospho-OsPrx20Thr−244 antibody (ABclonal; 1:1,000, v/v), anti-OsDMI3 antibody (Wang et al., 2023b), anti-OsPrx20 antibody (ABclonal; 1:1,000, v/v), and goat anti-mouse/rabbit IgG-HRP antibody (M21003; Abmart; Shanghai, China; 1:10 000, v/v). The specificity of the anti-OsPrx20 antibody is shown in Supplemental Figure 3C.

Agronomic traits analysis

To evaluate the developmental traits of different materials in the field, plant height and tiller number were measured at the tillering stage. At maturity, panicle phenotype, grain number, and seed-setting rate per panicle were recorded. Grain length, grain width, and grain thickness were measured using a vernier caliper, and thousand-grain weight was quantified. Data were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test using SPSS (version 21.0) software.

Protein–protein interaction assay

In the Y2H assay, the coding sequence (CDS) of OsPrx20 or OsDMI3 was inserted into the pGADT7 (AD) or pGBKT7 (BD) vector, respectively. The AD and BD recombinant plasmids were co-transformed into the yeast Y2HGold strain. In the GST pull-down assay, the CDS of OsPrx20 or OsDMI3 was inserted into the pET-30a or pGEX4T-1 vector, respectively. OsPrx20-His and OsDMI3-GST were expressed in Escherichia coli (BL21). OsDMI3-GST or GST alone was immobilized on GST magnetic beads and incubated with OsPrx20-His in binding buffer at 4°C for 2 h. Samples were separated via 12% SDS-PAGE and analyzed by IB using anti-GST or anti-His antibodies. In the coIP assay, the CDS of OsPrx20 or OsDMI3 was inserted into the pCAMBIA1305-GFP or pCAMBIA1300-6×Myc vector, respectively. The constructs were co-infiltrated into tobacco leaves via the Agrobacterium-mediated method. OsDMI3-Myc was immunoprecipitated from total protein extracts with anti-Myc antibody bound to protein A/G beads at 4°C for 8 h. The beads were then collected and washed. Samples were separated via 12% SDS-PAGE and analyzed by IB using anti-GFP or anti-Myc antibodies. In the LCI assay, the CDS of OsPrx20 or OsDMI3 was inserted into the pCAMBIA1300-nLUC or pCAMBIA1300-cLUC vector, respectively. The constructs were co-infiltrated into tobacco leaves. OsPP45-nLUC/OsDMI3-cLUC was used as a positive control. In the BiFC assay, the CDS of OsPrx20 or OsDMI3 was inserted into the pSPYNE or pSPYCE vector. Constructs expressing OsPrx20-YFPN and OsDMI3-YFPC were co-transfected into rice protoplasts using the PEG-mediated method. More experimental details are provided in a previous publication (Wang et al., 2023b) and in Supplemental Methods 3–6. The primers used to construct recombinant vectors are listed in Supplemental Table 3.

LC-MS/MS analysis

OsPrx20-His was incubated with OsDMI3-GST in kinase reaction solution (25 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.5], 5 mM MnCl2, 0.5 mM CaCl2, 2 μM CaM, 1 mM dithiothreitol, and 10 μM adenosine triphosphate [ATP]) at 30°C for 30 min. Trypsin (10 ng/μL, V5113, Promega) was then used to digest the phosphorylated OsPrx20-His, which was subsequently analyzed by LC-MS/MS (LTQ Orbitrap XL ETD; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) as previously described (Gampala et al., 2007). Proteome Discoverer software (version 1.2, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to analyze the phosphorylation site.

In vitro phosphorylation assay

Five micrograms of kinase OsDMI3-GST was incubated with 5 μg of substrate (OsPrx20-His, OsPrx20T244A-His, or myelin basic protein [Sigma-Aldrich]) in kinase reaction solution for 30 min, as previously described (Shen et al., 2024). The reaction was terminated by adding 5× SDS-PAGE loading buffer, and the mixtures were separated by 15% SDS-PAGE. Phosphorylated substrates were detected by autoradiography using a storage fluorescent screen (Typhoon TRIO; Amersham Biosciences, Piscataway, NJ, USA).

In vivo phosphorylation assay

OsPrx20 proteins were immunoprecipitated from total protein extracted from ABA-treated WT or osdmi3-KO plants with an anti-OsPrx20 antibody bound to protein A/G beads and separated by 12% SDS-PAGE. Phosphorylated OsPrx20 was detected with an anti-pThr antibody, and total OsPrx20 protein was detected with an anti-OsPrx20 antibody. Chemiluminescence was captured using the Tanon-5200 imaging system (Tanon; Shanghai, China).

Peroxidase activity assay

The Amplex Red Hydrogen Peroxide/Peroxidase Assay Kit (A22188; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to assay OsPrx20 activity according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A working solution of Amplex Red was prepared by mixing 50 μL of 10 mM Amplex Red solution, 500 μL of 20 mM H2O2, and 4.45 mL of 1× reaction buffer. Peroxidase standard samples were prepared by diluting an appropriate amount of 10 U/mL horseradish peroxidase (HRP) stock solution in 1× reaction buffer to yield HRP concentrations of approximately 0–2 mU/mL. For OsPrx20 protein preparation, OsPrx20 was immunoprecipitated from total protein extracted from different materials after PEG treatment using an anti-OsPrx20 antibody bound to protein A/G beads. For the assay, 50 μL of the Amplex Red working solution was added to each microplate well containing 50 μL of standards, controls, or samples. Plates were incubated at room temperature for 30 min, protected from light. Fluorescence was measured with a SpectraMax iD5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices; Sunnyvale, CA, USA) at 530 nm excitation and 590 nm emission. OsPrx20 activity in each sample was calculated using a standard curve.

Fluorescence probe for ROS detection

CM-H2DCFDA (C6827; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as a probe to detect ROS in rice protoplasts after rapid treatment with 1 μM ABA. Observations were made using a scanning confocal microscope (LSM880; Carl Zeiss; Oberkochen, Germany) with excitation at 450–490 nm and emission at 515–565 nm, as previously described (Kuběnová et al., 2023).
