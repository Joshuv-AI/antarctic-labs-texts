Association between monounsaturated fatty acids intake with Parkinson’s disease: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Lingyan Zhou, Lufeng Cheng, Xuechun Sun, Meijie Qu, Xingyu Zhang, Jing Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate the association between monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) intake and the prevalence of Parkinson’s disease (PD) among US adults using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database.MethodsCross-sectional data from the NHANES 2001–2018 were selected. PD cases were identified through self-reported PD medications. MUFAs intake was determined from dietary interview data for two 24-hour periods. To address severe outcome imbalance and improve covariate comparability, we conducted survey-weighted propensity score matching (PSM) incorporating NHANES sampling weights. In the matched sample, survey-weighted logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between MUFAs intake (quartiles) and PD prevalence, and a trend test was performed. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were applied to examine potential non-linear associations.ResultsA total of 12,122 participants were included, consisting of 84 PD patients and 12,038 participants without PD. MUFAs intake was significantly lower in the PD group than in matched controls after PSM (P = 0.010). Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested heterogeneity across some subpopulations. RCS analyses did not indicate a significant non-linear relationship between MUFAs intake and the prevalence of PD.ConclusionHigher MUFAs intake was inversely associated with PD prevalence in this cross-sectional analysis. Given the study design, causality cannot be inferred; prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings and clarify underlying mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-026-01102-7.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disease, especially among individuals over the age of 60, affecting nearly 1% of this population [1]. The incidence of PD tends to increase with age [2]. PD is pathologically manifested by a degenerative loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra [3, 4]. The mechanisms underlying the loss of these neurons have not been fully understood. However, researchers believe that inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and other molecular mechanisms may contribute to the development of PD [5–9]. Unfortunately, there is currently no cure for PD. Treatment options for PD mainly focus on managing clinical symptoms, slowing disease progression, and improving the quality of life for patients [10]. This is typically achieved through medication or surgical interventions [11]. There have been studies indicating that certain lifestyle and dietary habits may may be associated with PD risk and clinical features [12–14]. Accordingly, dietary modification has been proposed as a potential adjunctive strategy to support symptom management and possibly influence disease trajectories, although the current evidence remains mixed and further well-designed studies are needed [15–17].

Many studies have examined overall dietary patterns—such as Mediterranean or ketogenic diets—and suggested potential relevance to PD-related outcomes, possibly through antioxidative and anti-inflammatory properties of key dietary components. However, these patterns combine multiple nutrients and food groups, making it difficult to disentangle the effects of specific dietary components. Within macronutrients, dietary fats are a major focus due to their involvement in membrane structure, inflammation, oxidative stress, and energy metabolism, all of which are implicated in PD pathophysiology. Epidemiologic studies have therefore evaluated total fat intake and specific fatty-acid subtypes in relation to PD risk, but findings remain inconsistent. For example, Chen et al. reported that overall fat intake and major fat subtypes, including monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fats, were not significantly associated with PD in large prospective cohorts, although certain substitutions of fats showed potential associations in men [18].

Regarding specific fatty acids (FAs), epidemiologic evidence remains limited and inconsistent. In a Swedish prospective cohort, higher saturated fat intake was associated with an increased risk of PD, whereas monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) were not significantly associated with PD risk [19]. In contrast, a large cohort study in Singapore reported a dose–response inverse association between MUFAs intake and PD risk, particularly among women [15]. Some case-control studies in Japan and other settings also failed to find significant associations between MUFAs and PD, likely reflecting differences in dietary patterns, assessment methods, and population characteristics [20]. Meta-analyses have supported the notion that the effects of dietary fats on PD risk depend on the fatty acid subtype, with polyunsaturated fats often linked to lower PD risk and certain n-6 fatty acids or cholesterol associated with elevated risk, while MUFAs results remain mixed [21]. These conflicting findings may be attributable to differences in study design, variation in dietary assessment methods, insufficient control for confounding factors, and limited sample sizes in PD cases.

It is indeed true that there is still much to be learned about the relationship between dietary factors and the development of PD. FAs, particularly MUFAs, play a crucial role in maintaining the structure and function of neuronal cell membranes and synapses [22]. Recent evidence suggests that genes involved in FAs metabolism may also influence the toxicity of α-synuclein, a key protein associated with PD [23]. Previous research found that MUFAs had anti-oxidative stress properties [24, 25]. To address these gaps, we conducted a cross-sectional analysis of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to examine the association between total MUFAs intake and the prevalence of PD.

Materials and methods

Study design and subjects

NHANES, conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), is a survey aimed at examining the health and nutrition status of the United States (US). It collects data on demographic characteristics, dietary supplements, physical examination, laboratory tests, and questionnaire information [26–28]. To ensure a representative sample of US civilians, NHANES utilizes a stratified multistage sampling design. The NCHS Research Ethics Committee approved all procedures, and participants provided written informed consent. Detailed information about NHANES is available elsewhere [29, 30]. For this study, we utilized data from the NHANES 2001–2018 cycles.

We excluded participants younger than 18 years old and reported total calories intake less than 500 kcal/day or greater than 4000 kcal/day.

Dietary intake assessment

All NHANES participants provided detailed dietary interview data over two 24-hour periods. These data were used to assess the intake of energy, nutrients, and other food components. The first dietary recall was conducted at the Mobile Examination Center, while the second recall was conducted via telephone 3 to 10 days later. MUFAs intake was estimated by combining the reported intake from both dietary recalls, including both food and supplement sources. The assessment of MUFAs intake was based on the average of the two 24-hour dietary recalls. Additionally, we recorded the intake of vitamin A (VA) and vitamin D (VD), which have been previously reported to be associated with PD. The intake of these vitamins was also assessed from the same two 24-hour dietary recalls.

PD definition

PD cases were identified by checking self-reported PD-medication usage in the NHANES database. Based on a previous study [31], individuals were considered to have PD if they reported using any of the following medications: Benztropine, Carbidopa, Levodopa, Ropinirole, Methyldopa, Entacapone, and Amantadine.

Covariates

The following variables are believed to be potential confounders: age (years), sex (male and female), race (Mexican American, other races, non-Hispanic White, and non-Hispanic Black), educational level (below high school; high school grade (includes General Educational Development (GED) or equivalent); and over high school), family poverty-to-income ratio (PIR, < 1.3; > 3.5; 1.3–3.5), body mass index (BMI, underweight: < 25 kg/m2; 25–30 kg/m2; and>30 kg/m2), total calories (kcal), smoking, drinking (having at least 12 alcohol drinks per year or not). Smoking was defined as never (smoked < 100 cigarettes in life), former (smoking ≥ 100 cigarettes in life but did not smoke currently), and current (smoking ≥ 100 cigarettes in a lifetime and still smoking currently).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.3). NHANES uses a complex, multistage probability sampling design; therefore, survey design variables and dietary sample weights were incorporated in the analyses. Given the severe imbalance between PD cases and non-PD controls, we performed survey-weighted propensity score matching (PSM) to improve covariate comparability. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model including key covariates. Nearest-neighbor matching was conducted with a 1:2 case-to-control ratio. Sampling weights were incorporated in the matching procedure, which helps ensure the representativeness of the matched sample. After matching, we reconstructed the survey design object for the matched sample using the matched data and the updated weights. In the matched sample, we used survey-weighted logistic regression models to assess the association between total MUFAs intake and prevalence of PD, reporting odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Total MUFAs intake was categorized into quartiles, and P for trend was calculated. Exploratory subgroup analyses were performed across sex, race, BMI, PIR, education level, drinking status, and smoking status using survey-weighted logistic regression with adjustment for relevant confounders. To evaluate potential non-linear associations, restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was applied within the matched sample using survey-weighted models with covariate adjustment. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 91,351 participants were initially identified from the NHANES 2001–2018 datasets. Participants were sequentially excluded based on predefined criteria. First, individuals without valid dietary recall data required to derive total MUFAs intake were excluded (n = 21816). Second, participants with implausible total energy intake (< 500 or > 4000 kcal/day) were removed as a dietary data quality-control step (n = 3422). Third, participants younger than 18 years were excluded (n = 26065). Additional exclusions were due to missing information on covariates, including race, PIR, alcohol intake, BMI, education level, and smoking status. After applying all exclusion criteria, a total of 12,122 participants with complete dietary exposure and covariate data were included in the eligible sample (Fig. 1).

Supplementary Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the eligible sample. The average age of the 12,122 subjects was 47.99 ± 17.08 years. Among the 12,122 participants, 52.56% were women, and the majority were non-Hispanic white (67.82%). In total, 84 participants were classified as having PD. Compared with non-PD individuals, those with PD tended to be older (P < 0.001) and more likely to be non-Hispanic white (P = 0.015). Additionally, there were discrepancies in the consumption of calories (P = 0.005) and MUFAs (P = 0.003) between the PD and non-PD groups. The average intake of total MUFAs in the PD and non-PD groups was 22.11 ± 10.81 g/d and 27.49 ± 12.58 g/d, respectively. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of sex distribution, BMI, education level, alcohol consumption, smoking status, PIR, VA intake, or VD intake.

To address the severe outcome imbalance and improve comparability between PD cases and controls, we performed nearest-neighbor survey-weighted PSM with a 1:2 case-to-control ratio. After matching, baseline characteristics between the PD and matched non-PD groups were well balanced (Table 1). Notably, total MUFAs intake remained significantly lower in the PD group than in the matched controls (P = 0.010), supporting the consistency of the observed inverse association between total MUFAs intake and prevalence of PD.

The association between total MUFAs intake and PD

After survey-weighted PSM, baseline characteristics were generally well balanced across quartiles of total MUFAs intake (Table 2). No statistically significant differences were observed across MUFAs quartiles for age, education level, PIR, BMI, smoking status, or alcohol consumption. As expected, total energy intake and total MUFAs intake increased progressively across quartiles (both P < 0.001). Notably, the proportion of participants with PD showed a decreasing trend across increasing quartiles of total MUFAs intake, although this difference did not reach statistical significance in the matched sample. In comparison to the group with the lowest MUFAs intake, the prevalence of PD tended to decrease among those in the highest MUFAs intake group. Three logistic regression models were employed to examine the association between total MUFAs intake and PD (Table 3). The results showed a decreasing trend in the odds of PD with increasing MUFAs intake. However, although this trend was observed, the P for trend did not reach statistical significance. This suggests that while a trend indicating that higher MUFAs intake may be associated with a lower prevalence of PD exists, the current analysis lacked sufficient statistical power to confirm this association due to the limited sample size. Specifically, the reduced sample size after matching likely constrained the ability to detect significant associations. Future studies with larger sample sizes may provide greater statistical power and help confirm the significance of this trend.

Exploratory subgroup analysis

In the exploratory subgroup analysis (Fig. 2), we examined the relationship between MUFAs intake and PD across various subgroups, including sex, race, education level, smoking status, alcohol intake, BMI, and PIR. A marginally significant inverse association was observed in males (P = 0.065), and a significant inverse relationship was found in Non-Hispanic Black individuals (P = 0.004). No significant associations were found in females, Mexican Americans, and Non-Hispanic Whites. In terms of education level, a significant inverse association was found in individuals with less than high school education (P = 0.022), but no significant relationship was observed for those with high school education (P = 0.095) or higher education (P = 0.459). A significant inverse association was observed in current smokers (P = 0.021), while no significant associations were found in former or never smokers. No significant relationships were observed with alcohol intake, BMI, or PIR group. Overall, the analysis suggests MUFAs intake may have a protective effect in certain subgroups. However, the inconsistencies across these subgroups highlight the need for further investigation with larger sample sizes.

Analysis of restricted cubic spline regression

We utilized restricted cubic splines to depict the connection patterns between the consumption of MUFAs and PD (Fig. 3). The results indicated a significant overall association between MUFAs intake and PD, even after adjusting for multiple confounding variables (all P for overall association < 0.05; Fig. 3a, b). However, when we explored potential non-linear relationships using restricted cubic spline regression, we did not observe any significant non-linear associations between MUFAs intake and PD (all P for nonlinear > 0.05).

Discussion

To shed light on the connection between the consumption of MUFAs and the prevalence of PD, we selected data from 12,122 individuals in the NHANES cohort, consisting of US adults, using a cross-sectional approach. After survey-weighted PSM, significant differences in MUFAs intake between PD and non-PD groups were observed (P = 0.010). Exploratory subgroup analysis revealed statistical differences in certain populations. However, RCS analysis suggested that there was no significant non-linear relationship between MUFAs intake and the prevalence of PD. These findings highlight the importance of maintaining a healthy dietary intake, particularly with respect to MUFAs.

PD is a long-term progressive neurological disorder marked by the loss of dopaminergic neurons [32]. Increasing evidence suggests that nutrition plays an important role in influencing both the onset and progression of PD [33]. Various nutritional approaches have been reported to affect PD-related outcomes. For example, maintaining a ketogenic diet has been shown to improve both motor and non-motor symptoms in individuals with PD [16]. A single-center randomized clinical trial found that adherence to the Mediterranean diet significantly improved cognitive performance in individuals with PD [17]. Although the focus of our study is on MUFAs, it is worth noting that the Mediterranean diet, which incorporates foods rich in unsaturated fatty acids, has also been linked to a reduced risk of dementia [34]. Moreover, a case-control study demonstrated that higher dietary intake of vitamin E was inversely associated with PD incidence [35]. Low-protein diets have also been reported to reduce motor fluctuations in advanced PD patients [36]. Additionally, conflicting findings regarding the role of vitamin D levels in PD have been reported, and a case-control study in Japan found that low vitamin B6 intake was independently associated with an increased prevalence of PD [37, 38]. These studies collectively support the growing recognition of dietary factors as key contributors to the prevalence and progression of PD.

Multiple studies have explored the link between MUFAs consumption and PD, but findings have been inconsistent. The Rotterdam study revealed a significant association between total MUFAs intake and PD [39]. However, a study by Robert D Abbott.et al. found no clear relation between MUFAs intake and PD [40]. Similarly, Chen et al. observed no significant association between MUFAs intake and PD [18]. In Japan, Yoshihiro Miyake’s study concluded that the consumption of total MUFAs was not linked to PD [20]. Conversely, a recent study among Chinese women in Singapore revealed an inverse relationship between dietary MUFAs intake and PD (P for trend = 0.033) [15]. On the other hand, Essi Hantikainen’s research from the Swedish National March Cohort reported no significant association between total MUFAs intake and PD [19].

Our study found significant differences in MUFAs intake between the PD and PD-free groups. The exploratory subgroup analysis revealed statistical differences across some populations, suggesting that the relationship between MUFAs intake and PD may vary by factors such as race, educational level, and other demographic characteristics. These findings highlight the potential role of a higher MUFAs diet in preventing and managing PD. However, we acknowledge that some previous studies have found results consistent with ours [15, 39], while others have reported differing outcomes [19, 20]. We believe that these inconsistencies may largely stem from differences in study populations, methodologies, and the ways MUFAs intake was assessed. As demonstrated by our exploratory subgroup analysis, the association between MUFAs intake and PD differs across various groups, emphasizing the importance of considering population characteristics when evaluating such relationships. Future studies with larger, more diverse samples and longitudinal designs are needed to further clarify the potential protective effects of MUFAs on PD.

The specific mechanisms for the negative correlation between MUFAs consumption with PD remain unclear. The content of MUFAs in the brain is highly affected by the intake of essential nutrients in mammals [41]. Studies have demonstrated that MUFAs possess anti-inflammatory [42] and immune-modulating properties [43], which may provide protection against oxidative stress [44]. Postmortem examinations of PD patients who had higher cumulative doses of levodopa, an indicator of the disease’s severity, revealed lower levels of MUFAs in the brain cortex [45]. Collectively, these findings indicate the potential significance of MUFAs in reducing the prevalence of PD. The findings of this study could potentially provide valuable insights into the role of MUFAs in reducing the prevalence of PD. However, it is important to note that further research is needed to fully understand the complex relationship between dietary factors and the development of PD.

Strengths and limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of NHANES restricts our ability to draw causal inferences and introduces the possibility of reverse causation, where PD status might influence dietary behaviors. Second, dietary intake was assessed using two 24-hour dietary recalls, a method that may be subject to recall bias and may not fully capture long-term habitual dietary patterns. This could lead to misclassification of exposure, likely in a non-differential manner, which might bias the observed associations toward the null. Additionally, a large proportion of participants were excluded due to missing dietary recall data or missing covariate information, which may introduce selection bias. Since key exposure variables, such as total MUFAs intake, were unavailable for many of the excluded participants, a comprehensive comparison between the included and excluded individuals was not feasible. Although missing data in NHANES dietary recalls is primarily related to survey participation and interview completion rather than disease status, the possibility of selection bias cannot be entirely ruled out. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution. Third, although sampling weights were incorporated during PSM and subsequent analyses, matching may still alter the sample composition, and therefore, population representativeness should be interpreted cautiously. Although the PSM method helps balance covariates between PD cases and controls, it does not fully preserve the representativeness of the original NHANES sample. This is a limitation of using PSM, particularly in a large, diverse cohort like NHANES. In addition, seafood consumption, which is often influenced by geographical factors such as whether individuals live on the coast or inland, could introduce variability in dietary habits that were not fully accounted for in this analysis. This geographic variation in seafood consumption is a potential limitation of our study, as it may influence the generalizability of our findings regarding MUFAs intake and PD prevalence. Fourth, the subgroup analyses were exploratory and limited by the small number of PD cases within each subgroup, meaning the findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Lastly, PD status was defined based on self-reported use of antiparkinsonian medications, as clinically confirmed diagnoses are not available in NHANES. This approach may lead to misclassification of outcomes, including the potential inclusion of non-PD medication users and the omission of untreated PD cases. However, such misclassification is likely non-differential with respect to MUFAs intake and may bias the observed associations toward the null. Given these limitations, further longitudinal and mechanistic studies are needed to confirm these findings.

Conclusions

In summary, findings from a representative sample of US adults suggest an association between the consumption of total MUFAs and PD prevalence. Given the cross-sectional nature of this study, causal inferences cannot be drawn. Therefore, further longitudinal studies are needed to investigate the precise mechanisms and establish a causal relationship between MUFAs intake and PD.
