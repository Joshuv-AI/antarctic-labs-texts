HER3 is widely expressed across diverse subtypes of NSCLC in a retrospective analysis of archived tissue samples
Authors: Ross A Soo, Graham Clinthorne, Anu Santhanagopal, Chuntao Wu, Maha Karnoub, Parul Patel, Lukas Bubendorf
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aim: This noninterventional study (NCT05769764) aimed to characterize human epidermal growth factor receptor 3 (HER3) expression in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) by patient, clinical or tumor characteristics.Methods: HER3 immunohistochemistry was performed in archival tissue samples from patients with advanced or metastatic NSCLC. Samples were scored for membrane percent positivity and intensity. Membrane H-scores were calculated.Results: Of 203 evaluable samples, HER3 expression was observed in 98.5%, including all histologies, genomic subtypes and regardless of prior systemic anticancer treatments. The median H-score was 140, and 70.4% had a HER3 intensity of 3+.Conclusion: HER3 is widely expressed in NSCLC, indicating that HER3-directed therapy may be broadly applicable across diverse subtypes of NSCLC.

Introduction

HER3 is one of four members of the HER family of RTKs, which share a common structure and play a role in normal cell biology [1–4]. HER3 is widely expressed in numerous normal cell types, possesses little to no kinase activity and preferentially heterodimerizes with the HER2 receptor and EGFR [1–5]. Upon heterodimerization with other HER family members, HER3 can activate signaling pathways associated with oncogenesis, proliferation, migration and metastasis [3,4]. Additionally, HER3 can dimerize with non-HER family members, including the fibroblast growth factor receptor family, mesenchymal epithelial transition (MET) factor receptor and AXL, and in heterodimerization with other RTKs. HER3 promotes the activation of several signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT and the MAPK pathways, which have roles in cancer cell survival, metabolism and proliferation [1,4].

HER3 expression has been detected in various malignant solid tumors and has been associated with poor prognosis, including metastatic progression and reduced survival [6,7]. In one study, using immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining, HER3 expression was reported in 83% of primary non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) tumor samples (n = 44) [7]. A second study, using IHC staining, found that 29 of 45 samples (64.4%) from patients with adenocarcinoma NSCLC expressed any level of HER3 (1+/2+/3+). In 19 samples (42.2%), HER3 was categorized as highly expressed (defined as 2+ or 3+ staining intensity) [7,8].

Because of the high prevalence of HER3 expression in NSCLC, targeting HER3 may be an important treatment strategy to improve clinical outcomes and overcome resistance in advanced NSCLC. Currently, HER3-directed anticancer therapies are being investigated in multiple clinical trials. Some studies have shown that treatment with EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) increases HER3 expression both in vitro and in vivo [9,10]. Treatment with the EGFR TKIs gefitinib or osimertinib augments HER3 membrane expression in multiple EGFR-mutated NSCLC cell lines by increasing the proportion of HER3+ cells and the level of HER3 expression. In a retrospective study, augmented HER3 expression in EGFR-mutated tumor samples treated with EGFR TKIs was observed with acquired EGFR-TKI resistance compared with paired pretreatment samples [11].

The objective of this noninterventional study (NCT05769764) was to characterize HER3 expression in NSCLC by patient, clinical and tumor characteristics.

Methods

Study design

This noninterventional, retrospective multicenter study (NCT05769764) used archival specimens and de-identified associated data of patients with NSCLC documented in electronic medical records (EMR) from Guardian Research Network (GRN), a consortium of regional health systems in the United States, to analyze and assess archival tissue specimens and associated real-world data. Fully de-identified patient data associated with de-identified archival biospecimens were provided by GRN. All data were obtained and de-identified in a manner compliant with Federal HIPAA and Common Rule regulations.

Data sources

The EMR database provided by GRN, an oncology-focused, mission-driven hospital consortium comprising 37 cancer centers, 85 hospitals and more than two million oncology patients across 13 states (New York, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Maryland, Virginia, South Carolina, Florida, Indiana, Kentucky, Missouri, Arkansas, Kansas and Oklahoma), was used as the data source for this study.

GRN EMR databases collect both structured (e.g., International Classification of Diseases [ICD]-9 codes, ICD-10 codes) and unstructured data (e.g., physician notes, molecular sequencing, radiological images, pathological information and laboratory results). For unstructured data included in this study (e.g., patient and clinical characteristics, biomarker test results, treatment history and pathological information), a medical review was performed by clinical professionals to ensure relevant characteristics and data were captured. Manual curation, which is the process of manual review of unstructured data, was also performed.

De-identified, archived tissue samples (sourced from biopsies and resections performed as part of routine care) were procured from GRN sites for patients who fulfilled the study eligibility criteria. These tissue samples were shipped for analysis to Roche Tissue Diagnostics (Tucson, AZ), a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA)-certified laboratory. De-identified HER3-expression data resulting from testing were combined with existing clinical data from GRN's EMR database.

For secondary research, including collection of archival tissue specimens and associated data, GRN maintains an approved biobank protocol with the Western Copernicus Group Institutional Review Board titled, The Guardian BioBank Initiative: Accelerating Clinical Research and Access to Clinical Trials. No informed consent was required as all samples and patient data were de-identified.

All data collection for this study was completed at the site or GRN and was de-identified before extraction from GRN. The final curated dataset captured all the variables recorded in the data dictionary. Data were provided only for participants with tissue samples procured for analysis. Data regarding prior therapies refer to therapies received before sample collection.

Data on genomic alterations and PD-L1 expression were evaluated based on physician and/or laboratory reports. Presence or absence was assessed for common EGFR-activating mutations (exon 19 deletion and/or L858R), EGFR exon 20 insertions, ALK rearrangements, KRAS G12C mutations and MET exon 14 skipping mutations.

While HER3 expression was assessed from GRN-provided specimens, data on PD-L1 and genetic mutation status were gathered from physician notes and laboratory testing, and thus, it is unknown if these results for each patient are based off of the same specimen.

Testing methodology

HER3 expression was evaluated in archival tissue samples from patients with advanced or metastatic NSCLC. HER3 IHC staining was performed using a proprietary assay developed by Ventana Medical Systems (Roche Tissue Diagnostics, Tucson, AZ). HER3 IHC was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue using a HER3 (SP438) antibody assay (Investigational Use Only) (Roche Tissue Diagnostics). Slides with 4-μm-thick, freshly cut tissue were stained with hematoxylin II, and rabbit monoclonal negative control and the presence of HER3 were scored using the OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit (Roche Tissue Diagnostics). The samples were scored for membrane percent positivity and intensity: 0, 1+ (weak staining intensity), 2+ (moderate staining intensity) or 3+ (strong staining intensity). Membrane HER3 expression on tumor cells was quantified by H-scores. All specimens were scored by experienced surgical pathologists at Roche Tissue Diagnostics (Tucson, AZ). The IHC intensity score is a measure of the highest intensity staining exhibited by some proportion of the cells in the sample (Supplementary Table S1). IHC scoring cannot resolve a wide dynamic range of staining intensity; for example, two samples with 10% and 90% of cells with high-intensity staining will both be scored 3+. Conversely, H-score is a weighted average of all staining in the sample; for example, if a sample has 10% of cells at 0 intensity, 30% cells at 1+, 45% at 2+ and 15% of cells at 3+ and will thus have an IHC score of 3+, the sample H-score is (10 × 0) + (30 × 1) + (45 × 2) + (15 × 3) = 165. Another sample with 90% of cells with high-intensity staining will have an H-score greater than 270. The H-score gives a semiquantitative measure with a greater dynamic range compared with IHC score alone. It should be noted that no correlation between HER3 expression and clinical outcomes has been observed to date using either measure of expression. HER3 expression is being evaluated in ongoing clinical studies.

Eligibility

Before study start, the number of potentially eligible cases was first determined using de-identified and aggregated real-world EMR data based on the criteria listed below, which provided the basis for the data model used to conduct the study.

Samples were included in the study if patients met all the following inclusion criteria: age ≥18 years; histologically or cytologically documented advanced or metastatic NSCLC; at least one sample available and evaluable for HER3 expression, and collected >3 years from date received by testing laboratory, with evaluable defined as a formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor sample with ≥100 viable tumor cells; and tissue sample evaluable for HER3 expression collected between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2022.

Samples were excluded if patients met any of the following criteria: prior treatment with an anti-HER3 antibody or topoisomerase I inhibitor (single agent or combination) or with an antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) containing a topoisomerase I inhibitor or previous histological or cytological evidence of small cell or combined small cell/non-small cell disease.

This protocol was developed based on the eligibility criteria for HERTHENA-Lung01, a study that evaluated patritumab deruxtecan (HER3-DXd), an ADC with an exatecan-derivative payload [12]. Therefore, this study excluded patients with prior topoisomerase inhibitor exposure. For both HERTHENA-Lung01 and this analysis, it is unlikely that patients in clinical practice would have received topoisomerase I inhibitors; however, for consistency, this study retained that eligibility criterion.

Statistical methods

Using the final integrated, de-identified EMR database, all data analysis was conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Results were descriptive, based on observed cases with no imputation of missing data. Study variables are listed in Supplemental Table S2, and variables used to describe HER3 expression are included in Supplemental Table S3.

Results

Evaluable samples

Samples from 228 patients were obtained; 203 were considered evaluable, defined as ≥100 viable tumor cells (one sample from each patient). Of the 203 evaluable samples, the median age of the patients was 67 years, 119 (58.6%) were male, 148 (72.9%) were White and 172 (84.7%) had a history of smoking. Nearly 60% of specimens were from lung tumor samples, 17.2% were from brain metastases and the most common histological type was adenocarcinoma (64%; Table 1).

HER3 expression & tumor characteristics

Across all specimen types, brain tumor samples had the highest median membrane HER3 H-score of 165.0 (95% CI: 140.0–186.0), and lung specimens had a median of 130.0 (116.0–150.0) (Table 3). With regards to tumor histology, large cell carcinoma had the highest median membrane HER3 H-score of 170, with adenocarcinoma being the next highest, with a median H-score of 160.

HER3 expression & treatment history

Across all treatment-history categories, nearly all samples had some level of membrane HER3 expression (Table 4). The samples from 42 patients who were treatment-naive at the time of sample collection had a median HER3 membrane IHC H-score of 167.0, and 32 of those (76.2%) had a HER3 3+ membrane staining intensity. In samples from those patients who received immunotherapy plus chemotherapy (IO + chemo) only (n = 88), the median HER3 membrane IHC H-score was 140.0, and 66 of those (75.0%) had a membrane HER3 IHC score of 3+. Samples from patients receiving EGFR TKI + chemo + IO (n = 5) or EGFR TKI + chemo only (n = 6) had a median HER3 membrane IHC H-score of 155.

In samples for which mutation status was unknown (n = 108), the median membrane H-score was 135.0, and 77 (71.3%) had a HER3 3+ membrane staining intensity.

Discussion

Currently, effective treatment for advanced or metastatic NSCLC relies on detection of actionable genomic alterations and administration of targeted therapies, such as EGFR TKIs. Although standard-of-care treatments in earlier lines of therapy are well-established, there remains a gap in treatment options for patients who progress on these treatments. Novel molecular markers to direct therapies in NSCLC are needed. Our findings, together with prior studies, have reported that HER3 is overexpressed in the majority of NSCLC tumors [7], and more specifically, NSCLC tumors harboring EGFR mutations [6]. In addition, HER3 overexpression has been associated with resistance to EGFR TKIs in EGFR-mutated NSCLC [1] and with a poorer prognosis [6].

Though previous studies have evaluated HER3 expression in NSCLC, this study advances the understanding of the prevalence of HER3 in EGFR-mutated NSCLC in several ways. This study is the first to evaluate expression in retrospectively collected patient samples using the proprietary assay for HER3 IHC that is being used as an exploratory analysis in clinical trials of a HER3-directed ADC (patritumab deruxtecan). patritumab deruxtecan is a HER3-directed ADC composed of an IgG1 monoclonal antibody to HER3 that is linked to deruxtecan, a topoisomerase I inhibitor, via a cleavable linker [13]. Following binding to HER3, patritumab deruxtecan is internalized and is translocated to lysosomes, where the linker is cleaved, resulting in release of the cytotoxic payload into the nucleus [13]. Early-phase studies of patritumab deruxtecan in patients with NSCLC have reported promising activity [12,14]. Based on the activity reported in these early-phase studies, a randomized phase 3 study (NCT04676477) is ongoing in patients with advanced EGFR-mutated NSCLC with progression after treatment with a third-generation EGFR TKI [15]. Furthermore, the population of EGFR-mutated NSCLC with prior TKI and platinum-based chemotherapy overlaps that of the HERTHENA-Lung01 study and helps to describe and understand the real-world population analogous to that trial.

The results of this noninterventional study provide additional evidence that HER3 expression is present across varied subgroups of patients with NSCLC, suggesting that HER3 could be considered a candidate for directed therapeutic approaches. Nearly all of the tumor samples (98.5%) expressed a detectable level of membrane HER3 expression regardless of patient and tumor characteristics, such as age, sex, histological subgroups, and primary or metastatic tumors. More than 70% of samples expressed membrane HER3 with 3+ staining intensity. Most patients (≥60%) in all treatment history categories, including treatment-naive, expressed membrane HER3 with 3+ intensity.

In this study, we used HER3 3+ IHC membrane staining intensity to categorize samples with higher intensity levels. Other approaches to detect and quantify HER3 expression have been explored in other studies and highlight ongoing and emerging questions about which HER3 testing approaches are the most biologically meaningful and applicable to clinical practice. However, it should be noted that results of multiple studies utilizing separate methods for evaluating HER3 expression level in NSCLC have consistently observed broad HER3 expression. Several questions regarding HER3 measurement methodologies persist, including but not limited to resolving variations between IHC testing assays, the use of IHC intensity vs H-score for scoring HER3 expression level, measuring protein vs mRNA levels or circulating tumor cells vs nuclear, and evaluating HER3 expression in the membrane vs cytoplasm, or both [6,7]. Different studies have used various definitions and thresholds for what constitutes “high” vs “low” expression or “overexpression,” and a standard definition is yet to emerge [16]. Indeed, it may be difficult to define “overexpression” in the context of a protein such as HER3, where expression is widely observed, often at high intensity, and without a clear correlation with clinical outcomes. More importantly, future studies might attempt to understand how to translate HER3 expression data into clinically meaningful and actionable measures.

Limitations

Given that this study was retrospective and observational in nature, it was not designed to formally evaluate a hypothesis. Although the results were indicative of the HER3 membrane expression levels within each group, there were not sufficient samples to perform robust comparative statistical analyses. This study did not report matched pre and posttreatment samples and was not designed to evaluate any change from pre to posttreatment membrane HER3 expression intensity or H-scores. In this study, HER3 expression was evaluated in a large sample of patient-derived tissue samples and within subgroups based on PD-L1 status and mutation; however, it is notable that a limited number of samples had genomic testing data available. Thus, an analysis for HER3 expression by mutation status including EGFR, ALK and KRAS could not be performed. This is a limitation of this study that future studies could potentially address through prospective data collection.

Future considerations & emerging HER3-directed therapies

This study's findings support current knowledge on HER3 expression in NSCLC; questions remain about the role of clinicopathological characteristics/biomarkers as predictive factors for HER3-directed therapy. Although H-score variations across different subgroups in this study were observed, conclusions based on these differences cannot be made. Still, some general observations from the study could be used to spur future analyses. With respect to HER3 expression according to prior therapies, median H-scores were numerically higher in combination therapies with the exception of treatment with immunotherapy alone; patients with no prior therapy had the highest median H-score; and the median H-score numerically decreased as PD-L1 levels increased.

Of note, this is the first study in which HER3 expression was reported according to PD-L1 status. Further assessment of this finding must be done cautiously, as in this study IHC may have been done on different samples. There are two different pathways at play for ERBb family members and the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, and the exact mechanism is unknown due to limited sample availability for testing. Mechanistically, it is known that HER3 activation (via heterodimerization) leads to PI3K/AKT activation and is an important mechanism for the evasion of ERBb-family member inhibitors [17]. Additionally, resistance to EGFR inhibitors in NSCLC can be mediated by HER3; for example, MET amplification was shown to increase HER3-dependent activation of PI3K and lead to gefitinib resistance in NSCLC [18]. In turn, AKT-mTOR activation can increase the expression of PD-L1 in NSCLC [19]. Thus, there is a plausible mechanism for a relationship between HER3 expression and PD-L1 expression. Finally, the inverse relationship between PD-L1 and HER3 expression is intriguing but should be interpreted with caution given the heterogenous sample population. The relationship between PD-L1 expression and HER3 expression is contentious and may be tumor-dependent. In an IHC study of patients with oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma, there was no correlation between PD-L1 and HER3 expression [20]. In a study of patients with breast cancer, there was a correlation between positive HER3 and PD-L1 expression in triple-negative breast cancer [21]. Future research should be undertaken to confirm our findings as it might offer new insights into the tumor microenvironment and immune evasion mechanisms. We demonstrate here a numerical increase in PD-L1 expression with decreasing HER3 H-score; this may plausibly be due to a negative feedback mechanism in the HER3–AKT–PD-L1 axis. However, our study was not designed to test this hypothesis. These observations are not conclusive but indicate that additional studies of HER3 expression in patients pre- and post-IO therapy or according to PD-L1 expression should be undertaken. Along with addressing treatment gaps in later lines of therapy, there continues to be growing interest in the prevention and management of brain metastases in the NSCLC setting. Recent data indicated that HER3 was also overexpressed in brain metastases in lung cancer [7]; however, as observed earlier, HER3 levels did not correlate with survival in the setting of brain metastases [22]. Thus, the design of future trials should consider incorporating brain-metastases–specific analyses of HER3-directed agents.

Finally, although any direct role of HER3 in NSCLC oncogenesis has not been definitively established, several lines of evidence strongly suggest that HER3 remains an attractive molecular target. Based on the observed broad expression of HER3 across patient subgroups and consistent with previous reports, there is rationale to continue to evaluate HER3-directed therapies for previously treated patients with NSCLC. Although earlier development of a HER3-targeting monoclonal antibody did not demonstrate superior efficacy in trials in the past decade, a new generation of HER3-directed agents with varied mechanisms of action is now in development. Examples of therapeutic agents in development that target HER3 include monoclonal antibodies, bispecific antibodies, and ADCs [23]. Many are currently in phase 1 or 2 clinical trials; the investigational drug most advanced in clinical development is patritumab deruxtecan [15]. In the phase 2 HERTHENA-Lung01 trial in patients with advanced EGFR-mutated NSCLC whose disease had progressed on EGFR-TKI therapy and platinum-based chemotherapy, patritumab deruxtecan demonstrated a clinically meaningful confirmed objective response rate of 29.8%, with a median duration of response of 6.4 months [12].

Conclusion

In this study, membrane HER3 was expressed in 98.5% of archival tumor samples sourced from GRN, and expression was observed in all genomic subtypes evaluated. Additionally, membrane HER3 expression was present in tumor samples regardless of patients' prior treatment with systemic anticancer therapy. This study's findings confirm results of previous studies, and results may support the development of therapeutic strategies that use HER3 as a molecular marker to deliver an antitumor payload, a “HER3-directed” approach. Such an approach may depend on the prevalence of the marker more than any specific role played by HER3 in oncogenesis, tumor survival, or resistance mechanism.
