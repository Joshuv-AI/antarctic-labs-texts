Metformin alleviates aging-associated periodontitis via NRF2-mediated restoration of the IRE1α dependent unfolded protein response
Authors: Yu Sun, Qifeng Chen, Yan Zhao, Yuanyuan Yin, Qiao Chen, Lixin Yang, Zixuan Liu, Rui Chen, Xiaohui Xu, Jinlin Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProteostasis disruption due to impaired unfolded protein response (UPR) is a hallmark of aging and contributes largely to cellular dysfunction, but its role and associated mechanisms in aging-associated periodontitis remain unclear. This study reveals that senescent periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) exhibit impaired NRF2/IRE1α-dependent UPR signaling, leading to increased senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) and reduced osteogenesis. NRF2 overexpression restored IRE1α-dependent UPR signaling and suppressed SASP in senescent PDLSCs, whereas NRF2 knockout/inhibition showed opposite results. Mechanistically, the CUT-RUN assay indicates that NRF2 transcriptionally upregulates IRE1α, thereby restoring UPR activity. Notably, we found that metformin, a known anti-aging agent, attenuated SASP by enhancing NRF2/IRE1α-dependent UPR signaling in PDLSCs. To enhance the therapeutic efficacy of metformin, we developed a senescence-targeted, metformin-releasing nanocomposite hydrogel (TAM-GM@Met) by coating metformin-loaded mesoporous polydopamine nanoparticles with galactose (GM) and encapsulating them within a tannic acid and Ag-MOF-based hydrogel (TAM). This delivery system effectively upregulated NRF2/IRE1α-dependent UPR signaling, suppressed SASP in vitro, and alleviated bone loss in a mouse model of aging-related periodontitis. Our work identifies the NRF2- IRE1α-UPR axis as a critical regulator of PDLSC dysfunction in periodontal aging. We propose TAM-GM@Met-mediated metformin delivery as a promising therapeutic strategy for aging-associated periodontitis by restoring NRF2-regulated IRE1α-dependent UPR signaling.Keywords: Aging, Periodontitis, Unfolded protein response, Metformin, Nanoparticles

Introduction

With the global increase in the aging population, there is growing concern about age-related disorders, including periodontitis. Aging has long been recognized as a major risk factor for periodontitis [1,2]. The accumulation of senescent cells in the alveolar bone has been observed in both aged humans and animal models [3,4]. These cells are characterized by the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, etc.), known as senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), leading to a chronic low-grade inflammatory state [5]. Aging also impairs the regenerative and multipotent differentiation abilities of stem cells, including periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) and dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs), resulting in reduced proliferation and differentiation potential [[6], [7], [8]]. However, the specific mechanisms underlying the impact of aging on PDLSCs remain unclear.

Perturbed proteostasis caused by abnormal protein aggregates in senescent cells is identified as a salient feature of aging-related diseases. Dampened unfolded protein response (UPR) is the fundamental pillar leading to the reduction of proteostasis buffering capacity during aging. Reduced GRP78/IRE1α/XBP1 signaling may largely explain this content [9,10], with GRP78 acting as a sensor of accumulated unfolded proteins, leading to the activation of IRE1α. Activated IRE1α cleaves specific mRNAs and microRNAs to reduce protein loading in the ER and activate XBP1, which upregulates chaperones and degradation-related molecules. This mechanism prevents abnormal protein aggregation and proteotoxicity, improving cellular function [11]. Their expression was reported to be decreased during aging, including in the liver, lung, and brain in animal models [[12], [13], [14]], along with their response to stress being dampened [15,16]. In aging models, inhibition of this pathway is associated with cellular senescence and worsening of age-related disorders [17]. Notably, loss of IRE1α or XBP1 shortens lifespan in several organisms, while their upregulation can reverse aging-related changes [18]. Despite these findings, the regulating mechanism of UPR in aging-related periodontitis remains unexplored.

Extensive research has established a strong correlation between NRF2 and the progression of aging-related diseases [19,20]. Aging is associated with a decline in NRF2 expression across various tissues and cell types, and Nrf2 knockout mice exhibit an accelerated onset of multiple aging-associated pathologies [21]. Given that NRF2 functions as a transcription factor that upregulates the expression of antioxidant defense enzymes, its protective effects have predominantly been attributed to this mechanism. However, recent studies suggest that NRF2 may also contribute to cellular homeostasis by preserving proteostasis [18,22]. These results have never been tested, and the precise molecular mechanisms remain to be elucidated.

Due to the limited understanding of the mechanisms related to aging-associated periodontitis, effective therapeutic strategies to target senescent PDLSCs remain limited. Metformin, an established anti-aging agent, exerts broad protective effects by activating AMPK/SIRT1 while suppressing mTOR and insulin/IGF-1 signaling, thereby mimicking nutrient restriction and restoring metabolic homeostasis [23]. It also attenuates cellular senescence through inhibiting NF-κB signaling [24], enhancing of genomic stability, and promoting AMPK-dependent autophagy [25]. In the field of periodontology, accumulating evidence demonstrates that metformin contributes to periodontal tissue homeostasis by mitigating inflammation, enhancing the regenerative capacity of PDLSCs, and inhibiting osteoclast activity and alveolar bone loss under hyperglycemic or inflammatory conditions [26,27]. A recent study suggests that metformin activates NRF2 and restores impaired UPR in senescent cells, alleviating cellular aging [28]. However, contradictory findings have been reported [29], and the precise relationship between metformin, NRF2, and UPR remains unknown.

In this study, we examined the changes in NRF2 expression and IRE1α-dependent UPR in both naturally aged and D-galactose-induced subacute aging PDLSCs, as well as their responses to LPS stimulation. To explore the role of NRF2 in IRE1α-dependent UPR, we performed gain and loss of NRF2 experiments and a CUT-RUN assay to determine NRF2's transcriptional regulation on IRE1α. Furthermore, we silenced IRE1α in senescent PDLSCs with NRF2 overexpression and evaluated the subsequent effects. We then investigated the effects of metformin on aging PDLSCs, with a focus on NRF2/UPR and SASP levels. Our findings provide a deeper understanding of the mechanisms related to the pathophysiologic mechanisms in aging-associated periodontitis and highlight the potential of metformin as a therapeutic option.

To further explore the effect of metformin on aging-associated periodontitis in vivo, a composite hydrogel was fabricated for metformin delivery. First, we used mesoporous polydopamine nanoparticles (MPDA) to load metformin for their excellent biocompatibility and high drug-loading capacity [30]. For senescence-targeted, metformin-loaded MPDA was galactose-coated (GM@Met), which relies on the high activity of senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) [31,32] in senescent cells. Tannic acid (TA) and silver-based metal-organic frameworks (Ag-MOFs), known for their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antibacterial properties, respectively, were used to fabricate the composite hydrogel matrix. The resulting metformin delivery system (TAM-GM@Met) enables precise and sustained drug release in senescent PDLSCs. We evaluated the effect of TAM-GM@Met on NRF2/IRE1α-dependent UPR signaling and SASP in PDLSCs in vitro and a mouse model of aging-associated periodontitis. In addition, Nrf2−/− mice were used to confirm the mediating role of NRF2 in metformin's restoration of UPR. This innovative approach for metformin delivery not only elucidates the mechanism by which metformin modulates aging-associated periodontitis in vivo but also provides a potential versatile platform for treating this disease.

Methods

Animal experiments

The mouse study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Medical Ethics Committee of the College of Stomatology, Chongqing Medical University (2024-148). All mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal house at XXX, with a 12-h light/dark cycle and a controlled temperature (22-24 °C). Mice had ad libitum access to water and a standard normal chow diet (2212, Keao Xieli Feed Co., Ltd). Six-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co. (Nanjing, China). Nrf2+/− mice (Jackson Laboratory, Stock No. 017009), in which part of exon 4 and all of exon 5 are deleted, were kindly provided by Professor Gangyi Yang (Chongqing medical university). The donating investigator reported that these mice were backcrossed to C57BL/6J for at least 10 generations. To obtain Nrf2−/− mice, Nrf2+/− mice on a C57BL/6J genetic background were intercrossed, and their wild-type littermates (Nrf2+/+, WT) were used as controls. These homozygous Nrf2-deficient mice are complete null mutants with total loss of functional NRF2 protein expression [33].

To establish the aging mouse model, 8-week-old mice received intraperitoneal injection of d-galactose (D-gal) at a dose of 300 mg/kg once daily for 8 weeks [34]. After that, ligature-induced periodontitis was established as previously described. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and a sterile 5-0 silk ligature was tied between the first and second maxillary molar for 14 days [35]. The maxillae of mice were collected for micro-CT scanning (vivaCT40, SCANCO Medical AG, Switzerland) with a standard acquisition protocol (70 kV, 112 μA) at a thickness of 10 μm per slice. The acquired images were subsequently processed for 3D reconstruction using SCANCO Medical.

PDLSCs culture and treatments

PDLSCs were isolated and cultured as previously described [35]. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Medical Ethics Committee of the College of Stomatology, Chongqing Medical University (2024-148). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their guardians.

Briefly, a total of 51 outpatients (26 males and 25 females) undergoing tooth extraction at the College of Stomatology, Chongqing Medical University, were enrolled. Wisdom teeth from orthodontic patients with age <25-year-old, 35-45 years old, and 60 years adults with or without periodontitis were extracted and immediately soaked in cold PBS containing 5% penicillin-streptomycin. Regarding confounding factors, smokers and individuals with systemic complications (e.g., diabetes, hepatitis, or hypertension) were excluded. Additional clinical information is summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Periodontal ligament tissue from the middle third of the root was harvested and cut into small pieces, followed by digestion with 3 mg/mL type I collagenase (C0130, Sigma) for 30 min at 37 °C. The acquired cell suspension was cultured in complete α-MEM (8123288, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (S711-001S, Lonsera) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (15140122, Gibco). Cells at passages 3 - 5 were used for subsequent experiments unless otherwise indicated.

To induce senescence in PDLSCs, cells were treated with 30 g/L D-gal (G5388, Sigma) for 48 h. To simulate an inflammatory state of periodontitis in vitro, PDLSCs were treated with 10 μg/mL LPS (L2880, Sigma) for 24 h. To examine the effect of metformin on PDLSCs, 500 μM metformin (M2049, Abmole) was administered unless otherwise indicated. To assess the role of NRF2, PDLSCs were pretreated with ML385 (HY100523, MCE), an NRF2 inhibitor, for 24 h. For NRF2 overexpression, PDLSCs were transduced with an adenoviral vector encoding NRF2 (Adv-NRF2; titer: 7.11 × 1010) or a control vector (Adv-GFP; titer: 8.0 × 1010) (OBiO Inc., Shanghai, China) for 24 h. To knock down IRE1α, PDLSCs were transfected with siRNA targeting IRE1α (sense: 5′-GUUUGAUCCCGGACUCAAATT-3′; antisense: 5′-UUUGAGUCCGGGAUCAAAC TT-3′) or a scrambled control siRNA.

Primary culture of human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs)

HGFs were extracted from the gingival connective tissues of healthy subjects as previous study reported [36]. HGFs were cultured in complete DMEM (11965092, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (S711-001S, Lonsera) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (15140122, Gibco). Cells at passages 3 - 5 were used for subsequent experiments. HGFs were divided into two groups for senescence-targeting experiments (1): negative control group (NC), with cells cultured in medium with no treatment, (2): cells were treated with 30 g/L D-gal (G5388, Sigma) for 48 h to induce senescence.

Osteogenic differentiation

When PDLSCs reached 80% confluence, osteogenic differentiation was induced using α-MEM complete medium supplemented with 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid (A0278, Sigma), 5 mM L-glycerophosphate (G5422, Sigma), and 100 nM dexamethasone (D4902, Sigma). The medium was replaced every other day. After 7 days, cells were harvested for Western blot analysis of osteogenic markers and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining. After 21 days, calcium deposition was assessed by Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining.

ALP and ARS staining

ALP staining was performed using a BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Color Development Kit (P0321S, Beyotime). Cells were rinsed with PBS, fixed with paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China), and incubated in the staining buffer at room temperature for 30 min. Then, the staining was terminated by washing the cells with PBS.

For Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining, a commercial ARS Staining Kit (C0148S, Beyotime, China) was used following the manufacturer's instructions. After 21 days of osteogenic induction, cells were rinsed with deionized water, fixed with paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and incubated in ARS staining solution for 30 min. The staining was then terminated by washing the cells with deionized water.

Representative images of ALP and ARS staining were captured using a scanner (V330, Epson) and a light microscope (EVOS® FL, Thermo Scientific).

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining

Cell senescence was assessed using the Senescence β-galactosidase Staining Kit (G1580, Solarbio). Cells were rinsed with PBS, fixed with paraformaldehyde, and incubated in staining buffer overnight at room temperature. Images were captured using an optical microscope (EVOS® FL, Thermo Scientific).

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

For tissue IF staining, frozen sections were prepared from mouse alveolar bone that underwent decalcification in 20% EDTA at room temperature for 2 weeks. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating sections in a 98 °C citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 15 min. After cooling, tissues were permeabilized and blocked with 0.2% Triton X-100 and 10% goat serum for 30 min. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by 2 h incubation at room temperature with corresponding fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies. For PDLSCs IF staining, cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde for 10 min, then permeabilized and blocked as described for tissue IF staining. The subsequent staining steps followed the same protocol. The primary antibodies used were IRE1α (A17940, ABclonal) and NRF2 (ab62352, Abcam). The fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies were: Alexa Fluor™ 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (A-11034, ThermoFisher), Alexa Fluor™ 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (PA5-16891, ThermoFisher), Alexa Fluor™ 555-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (A-21429, ThermoFisher), and Alexa Fluor™ 555-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (A-21424, ThermoFisher). Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (DMi8, Leica).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson's, and TRAP staining

Frozen sections of mouse alveolar bone were stained with H&E, Masson's trichrome, and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP).

For H&E staining, sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, differentiated with 0.3% acid alcohol, rinsed in tap water, and stained with eosin for 2 min. The sections were then dehydrated and visualized using an optical microscope (VS200, Olympus).

For Masson's staining, a modified Masson's Trichrome Stain Kit (G1346, Solarbio) was used. Sections were stained with Weigert's iron hematoxylin for 10 min, differentiated with acid alcohol, and stained with Beibrich scarlet-acid fuchsin for 3 min. Excess stain was removed, followed by differentiation in phosphomolybdic-phosphotungstic acid for 15 min. Sections were then incubated in aniline blue for 5 min, rinsed with distilled water, and then differentiated with 1% acetic acid for 2 min. Representative images were captured using an optical microscope (VS200, Olympus).

For TRAP staining, sections were incubated in TRAP staining buffer (G1492, Solarbio) at 37 °C for 30 min. Hematoxylin was used as a counterstain to visualize nuclei for 3 min. Sections were examined under an optical microscope (VS200, Olympus).

Western blot

Proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime) supplemented with a protease inhibitor mixture (S506, Beyotime) and quantified using a BCA assay kit (P0010, Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein (30 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (ST023, Beyotime) and incubated with primary antibodies against NRF2 (1:1000, ab62352, Abcam), GRP78 (1:1000, 3177T, CST), p-IRE1α (1:1000, AP1442, Abclonal), IRE1α (1:1000, 3294T, CST), XBP-1 (1:1000, WL00708, Wanleibio), RUNX2 (1:1000, ab76956, Abcam), ALP (1:1000, ab307726, Abcam), OPN (1:1000, ab214050, Abcam), and GAPDH (1:5000, ET1601-4, Hua-bio). After incubation with corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1706516 and 1706515, Bio-Rad), protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (P0018AM, Beyotime) and imaged with a ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (v1.8.0).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells or gingival tissues using RNAiso Plus (9108/9109, Takara). Two micrograms of RNA per sample were reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (RR047A, Takara). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (RK21203, Abclonal) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (BIO-RAD) following the manufacturer's protocol. Pre-denaturation: 95 °C for 3 min; Amplification Cycles (40 × ): 95 °C for 3 s and then 60 °C for 30 s; Dissociation Curve Analysis: gradient 0.3 °C/s from 60 °C to 95 °C. Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primers are listed in Table S2.

CUT&RUN-qPCR

Cleavage under targets and release using nuclease (CUT&RUN) assays were performed in biological duplicates using the CUT&RUN Assay Kit (86652, CST). Briefly, cells were treated with Trypsin-EDTA (25300062, Gibco) for 60 s, and 250,000 cells were collected. Cells were incubated with Concanavalin A-coated beads for 10 min at room temperature, followed by membrane permeabilization using 0.01% freshly dissolved digitonin. Primary antibodies against NRF2 (#3724, CST) and IgG (#66362, CST) were added and incubated at 4 °C for 4 h pAG-Mnase was then added and incubated at 4 °C for 90 min to digest targeted genomic regions. DNA was extracted using phenol-chloroform purification and quantified by qPCR using SYBR Green Master Mix.

Fabrication of metformin loaded Galectin coated MPDA nanoparticles (GM@Met)

MPDA nanoparticles were prepared as described previously [37]. Briefly, 30 mg of Pluronic P-123 (435465, Sigma-Aldrich), 75 mg of Pluronic F127 (P2443, Pluronic F127), 75 mg of dopamine hydrochloride (D806618, Macklin), and 75 mg of tempo (T819129, Macklin) were dissolved in 10 mL of 40% ethanol, followed by sonication at 50 Hz for 5 min. Subsequently, 500 μL of 1,3,5-trimethylbenzene (T818958, Macklin) and 300 μL of ammonium hydroxide solution (A834475, Macklin) were added, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 4 h. The resulting MPDA nanoparticles were washed with deionized water and absolute ethanol, then dried using a vacuum oven.

For metformin loading, the MPDA nanoparticles and amine-polyethylene glycol-amine (N590849, Alladin) were dissolved in deionized water at a 1:2 ratio, with a nanoparticle concentration of 1 mg/mL. Next, 42 mg/mL of metformin (M2049, Abmole) was added and stirred at room temperature in the dark for 12 h. Following metformin loading, 2 mg/mL of galactan (YG71532, BIOSYNTH) was added, and the mixture was stirred for another 12 h. The solution was then centrifuged, and the sediment (resulting in GM@Met) was collected and washed with deionized water.

For fluorescence labeling, Nile red (HY-D0718, MCE) was added to 1 mg/mL MPDA nanoparticles and stirred at room temperature in the dark for 12 h. Then, galactan was added at a ratio of 1:2 with MPDA and stirred for another 12 h in the dark at room temperature. Finally, the solution was centrifuged to obtain the sediment (GM@NR).

Synthesis of GM@Met loaded tannic acid/Ag-MOF (TAM) based composite hydrogels

To prepare Ag-MOF, 1 mmol of AgNO3 was dissolved in 50 mL of anhydrous ethanol, while 1 mmol of 2-aminoterephthalic acid was dissolved in 10 mL of N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF). The DMF solution was added dropwise to the ethanol solution under continuous stirring to form a homogeneous mixture. The mixture was then transferred to a sealed high-temperature reaction vessel and heated at 130 °C for 12 h. The resulting precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed three times each with DMF and anhydrous ethanol, and followed by vacuum-dried at 60 °C for 8 h.

For developing the composite hydrogel, at 50 °C, 10 g of TA was dissolved in 10 mL of 1 M NaOH solution under continuous stirring. The resulting 10 mL solution was divided into two portions, followed by the addition of varying amounts of the crosslinker, trimethylolpropane triglycidyl ether (CAS: 30499-70-8, Adamas), to achieve TA-to-crosslinker molar ratios of 1.82:1 and 2.08:1, respectively. The mixtures were vortexed at 50 °C to ensure homogeneity. Subsequently, 20 mg of Ag-MOF and 10 mg of GM@Met were sequentially added to each mixture, followed by vortexing for an additional 10 min. The prepared mixtures were poured into 13 cm sterile petri dishes and placed in an autoclave at 90 °C for 2 h. The resulting composite hydrogels were vacuum-dried at 50 °C for 4 h to obtain the final TAM-GM@Met hydrogels.

Characterization of GM@Met nanoparticles and TAM-based hydrogels

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

The morphology of GM@Met and TAM hydrogels was analyzed using SEM (ZEISS GeminiSEM 300). Dry samples were glued on observation stubs and plated with gold using an ion sputtering coating machine (Quorum SC7620 Sputter) for 45 s at a sputtering current of 10 mA. Morphological imaging was performed at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV. Subsequently, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping was conducted at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV, using a secondary electron detector (SE2).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

GM@Met or MPDA was diluted with deionized water and dropped onto a carbon-coated copper grid. After drying, samples were scanned using a TEM (FEI Tecnai G2 F20) at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV, focusing on their size and shape.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) testing

XPS measurements were performed on GM@Met, Ag-MOF, TA hydrogels, and TAM-GM@Met hydrogels. Samples were dispersed and deposited onto Au/Cr/Si substrates for analysis using a Thermo Escalab 250Xi XPS instrument. Spectral data were processed, and peaks were deconvoluted using CasaXPS (Version 2319 dev1–1b, Casa Software, U.K.).

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy testing

The molecular structures of GM@Met nanoparticles, Ag-MOF, TA hydrogels, and TAM-GM@Met hydrogels were characterized using a Bruker VERTEX 70 FT-IR spectrometer. Spectra were recorded in the wavelength range of 400-4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 2 cm−1. A total of 32 scans were averaged to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio (50000:1).

Mechanical property test

The adhesivity of TAM-GM@Met, TAM-GM, and TA-based hydrogels was determined in pig gingival tissue using a universal test machine (MTS C43). Samples with 15 mm length, 15 mm width, and 2 mm thickness were tested at a speed of 1 mm/min and 100 N load.

In vitro degradation rate

The in vitro degradation rate of the TAM-GM@Met hydrogel was assessed by immersing 70 mg of freeze-dried hydrogel in 2 mL of artificial saliva. Samples were incubated in a shaker incubator (ZJZD-810A, ZHONGJIE) at 37 °C with an agitation speed of 100 rpm to simulate oral conditions. At indicated time intervals (0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, and 15 days), the hydrogels were retrieved, rinsed with deionized water, and freeze-dried. The remaining dry mass was recorded as WR, and the original mass was recorded as W0. The degradation rate was determined as the following formula: Degradation (%) = (1 - WR/W0) × 100%.

In vitro release study

The release of metformin was evaluated under simulated oral conditions. Briefly, 500 μL of TAM-GM@Met hydrogel was immersed in 10 mL of artificial saliva and agitated at 100 rpm using a shaker incubator (ZJZD-810A, ZHONGJIE) at 37 ± 0.5 °C. Every 24 h, the release medium was collected and centrifuged at 3000×g for 5 min. The supernatant was analyzed for drug concentration by measuring the absorbance at 233 nm using UV-Vis spectrophotometry (UV-2500, Shimadzu). Fresh artificial saliva was added after each sampling to maintain sink conditions. Cumulative release percentages were calculated using a standard calibration curve (R2 > 0.99) and plotted as a function of time.

Biological function of composite hydrogels

In vitro antibacterial assay

The antibacterial activity of composite TAM-GM@Met hydrogels was assessed using live/dead fluorescent imaging of Escherichia coli (E. coli), Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis). Bacterial cultures (1 × 108 CFU/mL) were incubated with 1 mg of composite hydrogels in 5 mL of culture medium at room temperature for 24 h. After incubation, the bacterial suspension was centrifuged at 10,000×g for 5 min, and the resulting pellet was stained with DMAO/PI kit (C2030S, Beyotime) in the dark for 30 min. Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (DMi8, Leica) to evaluate bacterial viability.

Cytotoxicity tests

CCK8 test and Calcein/PI staining

The cytotoxicity tests were performed in PDLSCs. Cells were seeded in a 24-well plate at a density of 2500 cells per well. TAM-GM@Met, TAM-GM, and TA-based hydrogels were placed in transwell chambers. Cytoxicity was measured using the CCK8 test (C0037, Beyotime) and Calcein/PI staining (C2015S, Beyotime) at days 1, 4, and 7 during the co-culture duration. For the CCK8 test, 10 μl/well CCK8 reagent was added and cultured for another 1 h. Then, absorbance at 450 nm was measured to calculate the cell viability. For live and dead cell determination, Calcein/PI staining buffer diluted in PBS was added to the well, and cultured for 25 min. Then, Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (DMi8, Leica) to evaluate the proportion of living (green fluorescence) and dead cells (red fluorescence).

Hemolysis test

Fresh blood was collected from 8-week-old mice using a sodium citrate anticoagulant tube, followed by centrifuging for 15 min (3000 rpm/min). Erythrocytes in the sediments were collected and washed with PBS. Then, 1 ml extractions from TAM-GM@Met, TAM-GM, and TA-based hydrogels were added to the erythrocytes and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h, followed by centrifuging for 10 min. The supernatants were collected, and absorbance at 577 nm was measured to calculate the hemolysis percentage. 1 ml of dd water and PBS were set as the respective positive and negative controls.

In vivo biological function tests

After establishing an aged and periodontitis mouse model in WT or Nrf2−/− mice. TAM-GM@Met, TAM-GM, and TA-based hydrogels were stereotactically injected into mouse periodontal tissues. After one week, the effects of the composite hydrogels on periodontal regeneration were measured using micro-CT, followed by H&E, Masson's, and IF staining.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance between two groups was determined using Student's t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were assessed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis and visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Results

Aging leads to intensified SASP and decreased NRF2 expression in PDLSCs and periodontal tissues

We collected PDLSCs and periodontal tissues from young (15-25 years), middle-aged (35-45 years), and aged (≥60 years) individuals, with or without periodontitis (Fig. 1A). As expected, aging increased SASP markers in periodontal tissues, an effect further exacerbated in aged individuals with periodontitis, as evidenced by heightened SA-β-gal activity, increased P21 staining, and elevated mRNA levels of IL1β, TNFα, IL6, IL8, MMP3, and MMP13 compared with the periodontitis group (Fig. 1B–D). Conversely, NRF2 expression was significantly reduced in periodontal tissues from aged and aged-periodontitis groups compared to young individuals (Fig. 1E). Similarly, PDLSCs from older individuals (middle-aged and aged groups) exhibited increased SA-β-gal staining and P16/P21 expression, while NRF2 expression was markedly diminished compared to PDLSCs from young individuals (Fig. 1F and Fig. S1A and B). Prolonged PDLSCs passaging also led to increased SA-β-gal staining, elevated P16/P21 protein and IL1β, TNFα, IL6, IL8, MMP3, and MMP13 mRNA expression, and NRF2 downregulation, alongside impaired osteogenic potential, both with and without LPS stimulation (Fig. 1G and Fig. S1C–G). Consistently, in vivo analysis of periodontal tissues from young (8-week-old) and aged (70-week-old) mice revealed greater enamel wear, alveolar bone defects, strengthened SA-β-gal staining, elevated P21, and decreased NRF2 expression in aged mice with periodontitis (Fig. S2A–D). Collectively, these findings indicate that aging is associated with decreased NRF2 expression in periodontal tissues and PDLSCs, accompanied by an intensified SASP phenotype.

To develop a stable senescent model for PDLSCs, we compared the effects of commonly used aging inducers, D-galactose (D-gal) and H2O2. D-gal treatment dose-dependently increased P16 and P21 expression, inhibited osteogenic potential, and reduced NRF2 levels in PDLSCs (Fig. S3A–C). Moreover, 30 mg/mL D-gal amplified the effects of LPS on SASP and osteogenesis impairment while dampening LPS-induced NRF2 expression (Fig. S3D–G). In contrast, although H2O2 increased P16 and P21 expression (Fig. S4A), it failed to suppress NRF2 when H2O2 concentration was less than 400μΜ; instead, it enhanced NRF2 expression, indicative of an acute stress response rather than chronic aging (Fig. S4B). Based on these findings, D-gal was selected to model senescence in vitro.

NRF2 regulates SASP in senescent PDLSCs in vivo and in vitro

To decipher the role of NRF2 in aging PDLSCs, NRF2 was overexpressed by transducing PDLSCs with an adenoviral vector carrying NRF2 (Adv-NRF2). The results showed NRF2 overexpression suppressed D-gal + LPS induced SASP, as indicated by suppressed SA-β-gal staining, P16/P21 expression, and mRNA expression of IL1β, TNFα, IL6, IL8, MMP3, and MMP13 (Fig. 2A–C). Moreover, NRF2 overexpression restored the osteogenic capacity of PDLSCs after LPS and D-gal + LPS treatment, as indicated by ALP and ARS staining and detecting protein levels of osteogenic markers (COL-1α1, RUNX2, ALP, and OPN). Specifically, under LPS treatment, NRF2 overexpression notably upregulated RUNX2 and OPN expression. Under D-gal + LPS treatment, NRF2 overexpression restored the protein levels of COL-1α1, RUNX2, ALP, and OPN (Fig. 2D–F).

To further sustain the protective role of NRF2 against SASP in vivo, we utilized Nrf2 knockout mice (Nrf2−/−) and constructed an aged mouse model by daily intraperitoneal injections of D-gal for two months (Fig. 3A). Consistent with our in vitro findings, Nrf2 knockout aggravated periodontitis and aged periodontitis induced SASP, exhibiting strengthened SA-β-gal and P21 staining and significantly increased mRNA expression of Il1β in periodontal tissues of Nrf2−/− groups (Fig. 3B–D). Furthermore, micro-CT analysis (ABC-CEJ), H&E, and Masson's trichrome confirmed that Nrf2 knockout led to worsened bone resorption in periodontitis and aged periodontitis groups. Specifically, micro-CT analysis revealed a significant increase in the cementoenamel junction to alveolar bone crest (ABC-CEJ) distance in Nrf2-knockout mice of both groups, indicating aggravated alveolar bone resorption; H&E staining further showed more severe periodontal tissue destruction, including increased disruption of the periodontal ligament structure. Consistently, Masson's trichrome staining demonstrated markedly reduced collagen fiber deposition in the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone tissue of these mice, reflecting impaired periodontal tissue regeneration and further exacerbated bone loss (Fig. 3E–G). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that NRF2 plays a protective role against SASP in the progression of aging-associated periodontitis.

NRF2 regulates IRE1α-dependent UPR signaling in senescent PDLSCs both in vivo and in vitro

Given the known role of UPR signaling in protecting against aging-related disorders, we determined UPR signaling in senescent PDLSCs. We found IRE1α was decreased in periodontal tissues from aged individuals with or without periodontitis (Fig. S5A). PDLSCs from aged people (≥60 years old) showed decreased protein levels of GRP78, IRE1α, p-IRE1α, and XBP1s (Fig. S5B). Moreover, PDLSCs with prolonged passages expressed fewer UPR markers under basic and LPS-treated conditions (Fig. S5C). In accordance, IRE1α expression in the periodontal ligament tissue decreased in aged and aged mice with periodontitis (Fig. S5D). Collectively, these results suggest that aging results in dampened IRE1α dependent UPR signaling, which might contribute to the proteostasis in senescent PDLSCs.

Considering the protective role of NRF2 in restoring proteostasis, we next tested whether NRF2 could restore the decreased UPR signaling in senescent PDLSCs. PDLSCs were overexpressed with NRF2. As shown, NRF2 overexpression restored the decreased expression of IRE1α, p-IRE1α, and XBP1s (Fig. 4A and Fig. S6A), and this was further sustained by the IF staining of IRE1α (Fig. 4B and Fig. S6B). To confirm NRF2's role in regulating UPR signaling, we used the NRF2 antagonist ML385. Treatment with ML385 produced phenotypes opposite to those observed with NRF2 overexpression (Fig. 4C, D and Fig. S6C and D). Moreover, we found Nrf2 knockout suppressed IRE1α expression in periodontal ligament tissues of the mice with an aged periodontitis model (Fig. 4E and Fig. S6E). These results support NRF2's protective role in IRE1α dependent UPR signaling in senescent PDLSCs.

To fully elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we used the JASPAR database to predict potential NRF2 binding sites in UPR-related gene promoters. Notably, the Ire1α promoter had the highest relative profile score for the NRF2 binding motif (M0150.1), suggesting that NRF2 binds to the Ire1α promoter (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, GEPIA database analysis revealed a positive correlation between NRF2 and Ire1α mRNA expression (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) (Fig. 4G). To verify NRF2 binding to the Ire1α promoter, we performed a CUT&RUN-qPCR assay in PDLSCs using an NRF2 antibody. As expected, NRF2 predominantly bound to the Ire1α promoter (Fig. 4H). These findings indicate that NRF2 may restore UPR by transcriptionally upregulating IRE1α expression.

To further determine whether IRE1α mediates the regulatory effects of NRF2 on UPR signaling, senescent PDLSCs overexpressing NRF2 were transfected with siRNA targeting IRE1α or control siRNA. UPR markers, SASP factors, and osteogenic capacity were subsequently evaluated. Silencing IRE1α largely abolished the restorative effects of NRF2 overexpression on UPR signaling, senescence-associated markers and osteogenic potency of senescent PDLSCs (Fig. S7A–F). These functional data demonstrate that IRE1α serves as a critical downstream effector of NRF2 in mediating UPR restoration, attenuation of cellular senescence, and functional rescue of PDLSCs.

Metformin suppresses SASP by upregulating NRF2-IRE1α-dependent UPR signaling in senescent PDLSCs

Metformin is well known for its role in protecting stem cells and mitigating aging-related disorders [38]. Here, we explored whether it could restore the decreased NRF2-UPR signaling in aging PDLSCs. As reported, metformin reduced P16/P21 expression in a dose-dependent manner in D-gal induced senescent PDLSCs (Fig. S8A). Moreover, it suppressed LPS and D-gal augmented SASP in D-gal pretreated PDLSCs (Fig. 5A–C). Notably, metformin restored expression of NRF2 and UPR markers in LPS and D-gal + LPS treated PDLSCs (Fig. 5D–F and Fig. S8B–D), along with enhanced osteogenic potential in D-gal and LPS treated PDLSCs, as evidenced by increased ALP and ARS staining and elevated COL-1α1, RUNX2, ALP, and OPN expression (Fig. 5G–I and Fig. S8E). These findings underscore metformin's protective role in maintaining NRF2-IRE1α-dependent UPR signaling and counteracting SASP in aging PDLSCs.

Fabrication and characterization of TA and Ag-MOF based hydrogel loading with GM@Met nanoparticles (TAM-GM@Met)

To enhance the targeting and efficacy of metformin in the treatment of aging-associated periodontitis, we designed an MPDA-based metformin delivery system. Specifically, we innovatively synthesized and coated galactose onto the metformin-loaded MPDA nanoparticles. The resulting nanoparticles, termed GM@Met, were expected to respond to the increased enzyme (β-Gal) in senescent cells, which enables nanoparticle internalization, galactose shell hydrolysis, and controlled metformin release for anti-inflammaging therapy.

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images showed that both MPDA and GM@Met exhibited a well-defined, mesoporous, spherical or near-spherical structure (Fig. S9A). SEM demonstrated uniform multi-mesopores on the surface of the MPDA nanoparticles, which facilitated metformin loading. However, after the galactose coating, the porous structure was no longer visible on the nanoparticle surface (Fig. S9B), indicating successful encapsulation of galactose on the outer layer. The synthesized drug-loaded nanoparticles were characterized. Compared to MPDA, the atomic percentage (At%) of nitrogen in GM@Met nanoparticles increased from 2.7% to 4.1% through EDS mapping (Fig. S9C and D). This substantial increase in nitrogen content is attributed to the high nitrogen content in metformin molecules, providing additional evidence that the drug was successfully loaded onto the nanoparticles. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements revealed that the size of GM@Met was 153.51 ± 19.78 nm, slightly larger than that of bare MPDA (135.54 ± 14.76 nm), probably due to the incorporation of the drug and the galactose coating (Fig. S9E).

For the loading of nanoparticles, TA-based composite hydrogels were fabricated. The preparation of the TA was conducted following previously reported methods with necessary optimizations to meet experimental requirements [39]. The morphology of the prepared TA hydrogels was analyzed using SEM, revealing a uniform interconnected porous structure that ensures their capacity to load nanoparticles (Fig. S9F). To enhance the antibacterial properties of the hydrogel, Ag-MOF nanoparticles were synthesized and characterized using HRTEM (Fig. S9G). The preparation procedure, detailed in the methods part, involves the coordination-driven self-assembly reaction between 2-amino-terephthalic acid (H2ATA) and silver nitrate (AgNO3) through a solvothermal method. EDS mapping demonstrated that the Ag-MOF primarily consists of C, O, N, and Ag (Fig. S9H and I).

The Ag-MOF was successfully loaded into the TA hydrogels, as confirmed by SEM and EDS mapping, which showed that the incorporation of Ag-MOF did not disrupt the porous structure of the hydrogel (Fig. S9J and K). Finally, GM@Met, Ag-MOF, TA-based hydrogels, and the composite hydrogel (TAM-GM@Met) were characterized using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), confirming the successful preparation of the composite hydrogel (TAM-GM@Met) (Fig. S10A and B).

Next, we proceeded to test the characterization of these three composite hydrogels, including the pure TA, TAM-GM, and TAM-GM@Met hydrogels. These composite hydrogels displayed excellent injectability, indicating their suitability for syringe injections into periodontitis bone defects with complex structure (Fig. S10C). To evaluate the degradation rate of these composite hydrogels in the oral environment, we immersed 80 mg of the composite hydrogel in 1 mL of artificial saliva at 37 °C. The results demonstrated that they were sustainably degraded under simulated oral physiological conditions with no statistically significant differences in degradation rates, and each hydrogel retained approximately 20% of its initial mass (Fig. S10D). To evaluate the metformin release of TAM-GM@Met, we used β-gal-supplemented artificial saliva to simulate the lysosomal microenvironment of senescent cells. In the absence of β-gal, only 54% of metformin was released over 7 days. However, the addition of β-gal not only accelerated the initial burst release (50% increase within 2 days) but also elevated the cumulative release to 60% (Fig. S10E and F). In addition to controlled degradation and drug release kinetics, an optimal hydrogel for periodontitis treatment must exhibit robust adhesion in wet oral environments to resist displacement caused by gingival crevicular fluid and salivary flow, thereby ensuring stable therapeutic delivery. To evaluate the wet-adhesion performance of the three hydrogels under clinically relevant conditions, we conducted standardized in vitro lap-shear testing using pig gingiva as a biomimetic substrate. The results demonstrated comparable adhesion strengths across all formulations, with an average interfacial adhesion strength of approximately 10 kPa (Fig. S10G). These properties potentiate the usage of the composite hydrogel in the periodontal environment.

The senescent cell-targeting ability of the nanoparticles was evaluated using Nile Red-labeled GM nanoparticles (GM@Nir). After co-culturing PDLSCs/HGFs with GM@Nir for 16 h, a time-dependent marked increase in red fluorescence was observed inthe D-gal + LPS group of both cell types, confirming the nanoparticles possess the ability of selective targeting of senescent cells in both PDLSCs and other oral fibroblasts. (Fig. 7A and Fig. S11A,). Confocal microscopy further confirmed colocalization of Nile Red with Lysotracker, with a Pearson's correlation coefficient of 0.82 ± 0.05 (Fig. 7B), indicating the nanoparticles were internalized by senescent cells and trafficked to lysosomes, where β-galactosidase-mediated cleavage of galactose triggered drug release.

The good biocompatibility of TA-based hydrogels and MPDA nanoparticles has been validated in previous studies [30,40]. In this study, we evaluated the viability of PDLSCs co-cultured with TA, TAM-GM, or TAM-GM@Met using CCK-8 assays and live/dead cell staining. The results showed that the composite hydrogels had no significant impact on cell viability (Fig. 7C and D). Hemolysis tests demonstrated that these composite materials did not induce hemolysis in vitro (Fig. 7E). Additionally, Mice treated with TA, TAM-GM, or TAM-GM@Met showed no apparent morphological abnormalities or inflammatory responses in major organs (Fig. S11B). These findings confirm the excellent biocompatibility of the developed material.

The DMAO/PI kit was used to conduct studies on bacteria co-cultured with composite hydrogels to reveal the antibacterial ability of E. coli, S. aureus and P. gingivalis. Most live E. coli, S. aureus and P. gingivalis were observed in green fluorescence with no hydrogel treatment. In contrast, the observed red fluorescence was significantly increased after being treated with hydrogels, indicating that most of the bacterial cells were disrupted without any integrated morphology, and thus inhibited bacterial growth. Among the tested groups, the tannic acid-based hydrogel exhibited limited antibacterial activity. In contrast, Ag-MOF itself demonstrated significantly superior antibacterial capability compared with tannic acid. Accordingly, the Ag-MOF incorporated TAM hydrogel demonstrated the highest bacterial mortality rate, which can be attributed to the superior antibacterial properties of Ag-MOF (Fig. 7F). The intracellular antioxidant activity of the hydrogels was further evaluated using the DCFH-DA probe. The results showed that the tannic acid hydrogel (TA) effectively reduced intracellular reactive oxygen species levels and alleviated oxidative stress. Notably, TAM-GM@Met exhibited the most potent antioxidant capacity among all groups. These findings indicate that both tannic acid and metformin contribute to the antioxidant activity of the composite hydrogel (Fig. S11C).

Effect of TAM-GM@Met on SASP, osteogenic potency, and NRF2-IRE1α-dependent UPR signaling in senescent PDLSCs

To investigate the hydrogel's impact on senescent PDLSCs, a transwell co-culture system was utilized. PDLSCs were seeded in the lower chamber, while the composite hydrogel was placed in the upper chamber, separated by a semipermeable membrane (Fig. 8A). As shown in Fig. 8B–D, and Fig. S12A, the composite hydrogels significantly reduced D-gal and LPS-induced SASP phenotype, including decreased β-gal activity, P16 and P21 expression, and mRNA levels of IL1β, TNFα, IL6, IL8, MMP3, and MMP13, which even showed greater efficacy than the metformin (Met) group. This additional protective effect may be attributed to the antioxidant properties of MPDA and TA. Consistently, TAM-GM@Met improved the osteogenic potential of PDLSCs under D-gal and LPS treatment (Fig. 8E–G and Fig. S12B). Furthermore, the composite hydrogel-mediated metformin delivery restored NRF2-IRE1α-dependent UPR signaling, which was suppressed in D-gal and LPS-treated PDLSCs (Fig. 8H, and Fig. S12C). These findings demonstrate that the composite hydrogel effectively rescues impaired NRF2/UPR signaling, reduces SASP, and promotes the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs.

TAM-GM@Met alleviates SASP and alveolar bone defects in an aging-associated periodontitis mouse model

To evaluate the in vivo protective effects of composite hydrogels on NRF2/UPR signaling and SASP, a D-gal and ligature-induced aging-associated periodontitis mouse model was treated with either TA, TAM-GM, or TAM-GM@Met (Fig. 9A). Stereotactic injection of TAM-GM@Met into the periodontal tissues significantly upregulated IRE1α expression in the periodontal ligament tissues (Fig. S13A). Consistent in vitro findings, the composite hydrogel delivering metformin reduced SASP markers, evidenced by decreased SA-β-gal staining and reduced mRNA levels of Il1β, Tnfα, Il16, Il18, Mmp3, and Mmp13 (Fig. S13B and C). Micro-CT, H&E staining, and Masson's staining further revealed that TAM-GM@Met alleviated alveolar bone defects in the aging-associated periodontitis model (Fig. 9B–D). These findings demonstrate that the composite hydrogels delivering metformin protect against aging-associated periodontitis, which might be through restoration of NRF2/UPR signaling.

To further explore the role of NRF2/UPR signaling in metformin's anti-SASP effect in vivo, an Nrf2−/− aging-associated periodontitis mouse model was constructed. Stereotactic injection of TAM-GM@Met hydrogels into periodontal tissues showed that metformin delivery upregulated IRE1α expression in the periodontal tissues of WT mice but not in Nrf2−/− mice (Fig. S14A). Furthermore, Nrf2 knockout exacerbated inflammaging, with increased SA-β-gal staining and higher SASP levels in Nrf2−/− mice compared to WT mice receiving metformin delivery (Fig. S14B and C). Additionally, the protective effect of metformin-delivering hydrogels on alveolar bone defects was diminished in Nrf2−/− mice (Fig. S14D–F). Overall, these results suggest that NRF2 is essential for the protective effects of TAM-GM@Met in alleviating aging-associated periodontitis through metformin delivery (Fig. 9E).

Discussion

ER homeostasis imbalance-induced proteostasis disruption is a key pathogenic mechanism of aging-related diseases. While the unfolded protein response (UPR) is central to maintaining this homeostasis, its specific regulatory mechanisms in aging-associated periodontitis have remained elusive. Our study addresses this gap by establishing the NRF2-IRE1α-UPR axis as a critical regulator of periodontal inflammaging and stem cell dysfunction. By uncovering that NRF2 transcriptionally regulates IRE1α in periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs), we expand the understanding of NRF2 beyond its traditional role in oxidative stress, positioning it as a fundamental pillar in proteostasis maintenance.

Aging significantly intensifies the SASP and diminishes the osteogenic potential of PDLSCs [7]. Previous studies have linked aging to decreased UPR signaling, in which the loss of key mediators, such as IRE1α, exacerbates cellular dysfunction [18,41,42]. Our functional assays reveal that the age-related decline in NRF2 directly drives this UPR impairment. IRE1α catalyzes and activates XBP1, thereby shifting its translational open reading frame, upregulating chaperone genes involved in protein folding, secretion, and degradation. Additionally, IRE1α facilitates the cleavage of a restricted set of mRNAs and microRNAs, leading to their degradation and preventing ER overload with misfolding proteins [18]. Previous studies have reported that aging is associated with decreased UPR signaling, and knockout of IRE1α, XBP1, or GRP78 exacerbates ER dysfunction and shortens lifespan [43]. Crucially, the observation that IRE1α knockdown abrogates the protective effects of NRF2 overexpression confirms a previously unrecognized hierarchical signaling relationship in periodontal tissues.

Recognizing this targetable axis, we investigated metformin, a pleiotropic drug that regulates multiple cellular processes via AMPK signaling, mTOR modulation, mitochondrial function, and redox homeostasis, all of which may contribute to its anti-senescent and pro-osteogenic effects [[44], [45], [46], [47]]. Recent studies have shown that metformin activates NRF2 and restores impaired UPR in senescent cells, thereby alleviating cellular aging [28]. Our data mechanistically reposition metformin not just as a metabolic regulator, but as a restorer of the NRF2-IRE1α-dependent UPR signaling cascade in senescent PDLSCs. To translate this systemic mechanism into a localized therapy, we engineered a senescence-responsive drug delivery platform. By utilizing galactose-modified MPDA nanoparticles, this system exploits the high β-galactosidase activity of senescent cells to achieve on-demand metformin release. The integration of these nanoparticles into a TA/Ag-MOF hydrogel provides a synergistic microenvironment: the TA mitigates oxidative stress [48], MOFs provide a versatile drug delivery platform and support tissue regeneration [49], while Ag-MOFs continuously release silver ions to inhibit periodontal pathogens and promote bone regeneration [50]. Our in vivo experiments confirmed that this nanomedicine not only attenuates inflammatory aging but also promotes alveolar bone regeneration, presenting a promising strategy for treating age-related periodontal diseases and other aging-associated bone disorders, such as osteoarthritis [51]. The scalability and biocompatibility of the components further support its potential for clinical translation, offering a novel approach for aging-related pathologies.

However, our study has several limitations. First, the present study was performed using global Nrf2 knockout mice, which may lead to systemic effects and thus cannot fully exclude the influence of other cell types or organs. Therefore, future studies employing PDLSC-specific conditional knockout mouse models will be necessary to precisely define the role of NRF2 in periodontal aging and regeneration. Second, although MPDA nanoparticles and TA-based hydrogels have been previously reported to exhibit biosafety [40,52], it should be noted that the biocompatibility and safety of the composite hydrogel and related nanoparticles were mainly verified via short-term in vitro and in vivo experiments. Potential long-term risks, including chronic immune responses, local material retention, gradual degradation profiles, and potential systemic effects, remain to be further elucidated. Thirdly, while the scalability and biocompatibility of the components provide the possibility for its clinical translation, it should be emphasized that the present findings are obtained from in vitro cell models and in vivo animal experiments. Therefore, further safety evaluations, efficacy validations, and large-animal studies are necessary before this therapeutic strategy can be translated into clinical practice. Last but not least, while the current study primarily focuses on the IRE1α-XBP1s axis as the key downstream mediator of NRF2, the UPR is a complex signaling network comprising parallel pathways, including the PKR-like ER kinase (PERK) and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) branches. Future investigations are warranted to delineate the specific contributions of these additional UPR modules. For instance, the PERK/eIF2α pathway is critically involved in regulating translational attenuation and cellular stress adaptation [53], while the ATF6 pathway modulates transcriptional programs related to protein folding [54]. Clarifying the potential crosstalk and hierarchical relationship between IRE1α and these parallel pathways will be essential to fully elucidate the spectrum of NRF2-mediated proteostasis regulation and its impact on PDLSC senescence, inflammatory resolution, and osteogenic commitment during periodontal aging.

In conclusion, our study mechanistically establishes the NRF2-IRE1α-UPR axis as a critical regulator of periodontal inflammatory aging and suggests that metformin could serve as a potential therapeutic agent for aging-associated periodontitis. The developed galactose-coated MPDA nanoparticle composite hydrogel enables senescence-targeted delivery of metformin, effectively exerting its anti-SASP effects.
