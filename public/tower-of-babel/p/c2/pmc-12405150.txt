DDX60 protects ischemic myocardial injury and heart dysfunction by improving mitochondrial function via promoting Arl2 mRNA translation
Authors: Tianyou Yuan, Changzuan Zhou, Yifan Long, Xiaoqiang Chen, Longzhe Gao, Ya Li, Songwen Chen, Xiaofeng Lu, Juan Xu, Xiaoyu Wu, Genqing Zhou, Shaowen Liu, Wenyi Yang, Yong Wei, Lidong Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mitochondrial dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a pivotal driver of cardiomyocyte apoptosis and cardiac deterioration following myocardial infarction (MI). This study identifies a significant upregulation of DDX60 in cardiomyocytes under hypoxic conditions. Elevated DDX60 levels enhance mitochondrial function and attenuate cardiomyocyte apoptosis in vitro, whereas its knockdown induces the opposite effects. In vivo, cardiomyocyte-specific DDX60 knockout markedly exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis, accelerating post-MI cardiac remodeling and functional decline. Furthermore, we found that Arl2 knockdown partially negates the protective effects of DDX60 overexpression on ATP production and apoptosis. Conversely, adeno-associated virus-9 (AAV9)-mediated Arl2 overexpression partially restores cardiac function, reduces infarct size, and rescues mitochondrial integrity in DDX60 CKO mice post-MI. Mechanistically, DDX60 forms a translational complex with eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4 gamma 1 (EIF4G1) that enhances Arl2 mRNA translation, a process essential for mitochondrial homeostasis. Collectively, these findings establish DDX60 as a key regulator of cardioprotection post-MI by enhancing Arl2 translation, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for ischemic heart disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05839-x.

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) remains a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality, characterized by acute coronary artery occlusion, ischemic cardiomyocyte death, and adverse ventricular remodeling. Whereas reperfusion therapies substantially improve clinical outcomes, residual mortality persists because of irreversible myocardial damage and progressive heart failure (HF) [1]. Mitochondrial dysfunction is central to this pathogenesis, which drives cardiomyocyte apoptosis, the predominant form of programmed cell death in ischemic myocardium [2]. Mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening, cytochrome c release, and downstream caspase-3 activation trigger a cascade of apoptotic signaling, exacerbating myocardial injury [3–5]. Despite advances in pharmacological and interventional strategies, targeted interventions aimed at mitochondrial preservation in the infarcted heart remain limited. This drawback necessitates identifying novel molecular regulators of cardiomyocyte survival.

Mitochondria serve as the cellular powerhouses responsible for generating 90% of cardiac adenosine triphosphate (ATP); they regulate calcium homeostasis, redox balance, and apoptosis [6–8]. Emerging evidence implicates mitochondrial dysfunction as a central mechanism underlying ischemia/reperfusion injury, characterized by impaired oxidative phosphorylation, excess reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and mPTP-driven apoptotic cascades [9]. Notably, the small GTPase ADP-ribosylation factor-like 2 (Arl2) has emerged as a key regulator of mitochondrial function. This evolutionarily conserved protein localizes to the mitochondrial matrix, where it facilitates oxidative phosphorylation complex assembly and ATP synthase activity [10]. Preclinical studies demonstrate that Arl2 deficiency exacerbates mitochondrial fragmentation in obesity-induced cardiomyopathy [11], whereas its downregulation aggravates pyroptosis in atrial fibrillation [12]. Furthermore, transcriptomic analyses have implicated Arl2 as a pivotal mediator in dilated cardiomyopathy pathogenesis [13]. Collectively, Arl2 holds promise in preserving mitochondrial integrity and cardiac function under pathological stress, highlighting its potential in cardiovascular disease management.

The D-E-X-H/D-box helicase family comprises ATP-dependent RNA helicases that regulate fundamental processes in RNA metabolism and protein translation [14]. Beyond their canonical roles in antiviral defense and tumorigenesis [15, 16], these enzymes have been increasingly implicated in cardiovascular pathophysiology. For instance, DHX9 exacerbates atherosclerosis progression by promoting pro-inflammatory macrophage activation [17], whereas DDX5 regulates phenotypic switching in vascular smooth muscles [18]. Notably, multiple D-E-X-H/D-box proteins directly regulate mitochondrial processes. For instance, the ABO6-encoded D-E-X-H/D-box RNA helicase regulates mitochondrial complex I gene splicing [19], whereas DDX28 orchestrates mitoribosome assembly [20]. Hence, D-E-X-H/D-box helicases play pivotal roles in cardiovascular pathogenesis and mitochondrial homeostasis. Nonetheless, their mechanistic involvement in myocardial infarction and the regulation of mitochondrial function remains unexplored. This study focuses on DDX60, a D-E-X-H/D-box RNA helicase previously associated with innate immunity through retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I) pathway activation [21]. Intriguingly, a marked upregulation of DDX60 was observed in hypoxia-exposed cardiomyocytes, a novel finding in cardiac pathophysiology. Though DDX60’s role in mitochondrial regulation remains unexplored, its structural homology to other mitochondrial helicases suggests potential involvement in organelle homeostasis.

In this study, a novel mitochondrial regulatory axis involving DDX60 and Arl2 was identified. Hypoxia-induced DDX60 expression preserves mitochondrial architecture and bioenergetics in cardiomyocytes. Mechanistically, DDX60 forms a translational complex with eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4 gamma 1 (EIF4G1) that enhances Arl2 mRNA translation. This axis maintains mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production. Cardiomyocyte-specific DDX60 deletion exacerbates post-MI ventricular remodeling, whereas Arl2 supplementation restores mitochondrial function and improves survival. These findings establish DDX60 as a master regulator of mitochondrial proteostasis, offering novel therapeutic insights into ischemic cardiomyopathy treatment.

Generation of cardiomyocyte-specific knockout of DDX60 mice

Mice were housed in a pathogen-free, temperature-controlled environment with a humidity of 55% ± 10% under a 12-hour light/dark cycle. DDX60flox/flox (DDX60fl/fl) and α-myosin heavy chain (αMHC)-Cre (Cre) mice were obtained from Gempharmatech Co., Ltd. To generate cardiomyocyte-specific DDX60 knockout mice, homozygous floxed DDX60 (DDX60fl/fl) mice were crossed with αMHC-Cre transgenic mice. The initial breeding produced offspring heterozygous for the floxed DDX60 allele and hemizygous for αMHC-Cre, which were subsequently bred with DDX60fl/fl mice to yield DDX60 knockout (KO) mice. Genotyping was performed via PCR, and gene deletion was validated by Western blot. DDX60fl/fl mice served as wild-type (WT) controls. The presence of the αMHC-Cre allele was confirmed using the forward primer 5′-CCTGCTGTCCATTCCTTATTCCATA-3′ and reverse primer 5′-ATATCCCCTTGTTCCCTTTCTGC-3′, yielding a 329 bp amplicon. The DDX60fl/fl allele was verified with specific primers: 3’arm Sense 5’- CATCGCATTGTCTGAGTAGGTG-3’ and 3’arm Antisense 5’-CCTGCTCTGAAATACGAGGCAA-3’, producing a 440 bp fragment. PCR conditions were as follows: 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 62 °C for 1 min, 72 °C for 1 min, and a final extension at 72 °C for 3 min.

Murine model of myocardial infarction (MI)

Eight-week-old male mice of various genotypes underwent either MI or sham surgery. Following intubation, anesthesia was induced with 2% isoflurane, and body temperature was maintained using heat lamps. MI was induced by ligating the left anterior descending (LAD) artery, whereas sham-operated mice underwent the identical procedure without LAD ligation. At predetermined time points, mice were euthanized, and organs were harvested for further analysis.

Primary mouse neonatal cardiomyocytes isolated, culture and treatment

Primary neonatal mouse cardiomyocytes (NMCMs) were isolated from male and female C57BL/6 mice at postnatal days 1 to 3 per established protocols [22]. To ensure the purity of NMCMs, two consecutive rounds of differential adhesion were utilized to avoid contaminating non-myocyte cardiac cells such as fibroblasts and endothelial cells. NMCMs were plated in 6-well plates and maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin under a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C with 95% humidity. On day 3, spontaneous rhythmic beating of the NMCMs was observed, Upon reaching ~ 80% confluence, cells were transduced with recombinant adenoviruses or their respective negative controls according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Infection efficiency was verified by Western blot. Hypoxic stress was induced by incubating NMCMs in a hypoxia chamber (1% O2, 5% CO2, 94% N2) for 24 h at 37 °C, while normoxic conditions served as controls.

Myocardial infarct size measurement

Myocardial infarct size was quantified using TTC staining. Harvested myocardial tissue was flash-frozen at − 30 °C for 25 min, sectioned transversely into 2-mm slices, and incubated in 2% TTC solution at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Infarcted areas appeared white to pale, contrasting with the dark red viable myocardium. Infarct size was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Echocardiography

Cardiac function was evaluated via transthoracic echocardiography 28 days post-MI, following standard procedures [23]. Key parameters, including left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVIDs), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVIDd), and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS), were measured.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence (IF) analysis, cardiomyocytes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 5 min, washed three times with PBS, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 2 h at room temperature. Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with DDX60 primary antibodies (1:200), followed by three 5-minute washes with cold PBS. Secondary antibodies were applied for 2 h in the dark, followed by three additional PBS washes. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min before imaging with a fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany).

Flow cytometry

For the detection of cell apoptosis, a fluorescein isothiocyanate/propidium iodide apoptosis detection kit was used. Briefly, NMCMs were trypsinized, washed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline, and resuspended in 500 µl of pre-cooled binding buffer. Cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI per the manufacturer’s protocols. Finally, flow cytometry quantified the proportion of apoptotic cells using a MoFlo™ XDP system (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and data are reported as means ± SD.

TUNEL assay

Cell and tissue apoptosis was assessed using TUNEL staining according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cardiomyocytes cultured in 12-well plates were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 10 min, washed three times with cold PBS, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min. Following a 60-minute incubation with TUNEL working solution at 37 °C, cells were washed again and counterstained with DAPI for 5 min at room temperature. For in vivo analysis, myocardial tissue was harvested 72 h post-AMI, rinsed with PBS, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C. Fixed tissue was paraffin-embedded, sectioned to 5 μm thickness, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Sections were then incubated with TUNEL staining solution at 37 °C in the dark for 1 h, followed by DAPI counterstaining. Apoptotic cells were quantified using fluorescence microscopy, and the apoptotic index was determined with ImageJ software.

Measurement of Cellular ATP

Cellular ATP levels were measured using a luciferin-luciferase assay kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein extraction was performed on ATP measurement samples, with protein content determined for normalization relative to cell count or heart tissue mass.

MTT assay

Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay kit. NMCMs seeded in 96-well plates (5000 cells/well) were cultured for 36 h before infection with Ad-shArl2 or control adenoviruses for an additional 36 h. MTT solution was then added, and after a 3-hour incubation, dimethyl sulfoxide was introduced to dissolve the formazan crystals. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Silicon Valley, CA, USA).

Western blot analysis

Protein expression levels were analyzed using Western blot. Myocardial and cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer supplemented with 1% PMSF. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA assay kit. Samples were resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% BSA for 2 h, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against DDX60, Cleaved Caspase-3, Cleaved PARP, Arl2, Col3a1, α-SMA, EIF4G1, Flag, GAPDH, and β-Tubulin. Secondary antibody incubation was performed at room temperature for 2 h. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection system, and densitometric analysis was conducted with ImageJ software.

RNA extraction and Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cardiac tissues and cells using TRIzol® reagent, and RNA quality and concentration were assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, ensuring an OD260/280 ratio of 1.8–2.0. PCR primers were custom-designed and synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on an ABI PRISM 7500 system using a SYBR Green I kit, with β-actin serving as the internal reference for normalization. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with all experiments conducted in triplicate. Histopathological examination

At 28 days post-MI, heart tissues were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C, embedded in paraffin, sectioned into 5-µm slices using a microtome, and mounted on glass slides. After baking, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E, Solarbio) staining for 10 min. Additional histological assessments included Masson’s trichrome and Picrosirius Red staining. Myocardial histopathology was examined using light microscopy.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Mitochondrial morphology was evaluated via TEM. Briefly, at 24 h post-MI, myocardial tissues were trimmed into 1-mm3 fragments and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 24 h. Standard dehydration, embedding, and polymerization procedures were performed, followed by ultrathin sectioning (50–100 nm). Sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and subsequently imaged using TEM.

DHE staining and MitoSOX staining

Myocardial reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were quantified using DHE. Freshly harvested myocardial tissue was flash-frozen at − 30 °C for 25 min, sectioned into 10-µm slices, and incubated with 3 µM DHE at 37 °C for 30 min before fluorescence microscopy analysis. Mitochondrial ROS was assessed using MitoSOX following the manufacturer’s protocol. Images were captured with a fluorescence microscope and processed using ImageJ.

Glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down

Recombinant plasmids encoding human DDX60 with a GST tag and human EIF4G with a His tag were designed and constructed by MIAOLING Biology. Plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21, and single colonies were selected from Luria-Bertani (LB) plates for large-scale culture in LB medium at 37 °C. Protein expression was induced with 0.4 mM IPTG overnight at 20 °C. Proteins were purified using a GST-Tag Protein Purification Kit with Magnetic Agarose Beads and a His-Tag Protein Purification Kit with IDA-Ni Magnetic Agarose Beads. Protein-protein interactions were analyzed using a GST Pull-Down Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. SDS-PAGE and Western blot were performed to confirm the interaction between EIF4G and DDX60.

Mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR)

Mitochondrial respiration was evaluated using the Agilent Seahorse XFp Analyzer (Santa Clara, CA, USA) to determine the OCR. NMCMs were seeded in XF96 cell culture microplates at a density of 105 cells/well and cultured in a complete growth medium at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 48 h. On the first day, the XFp Sensor Cartridge was hydrated with ultrapure water and incubated overnight in a non-CO2 incubator at 37 °C. On the second day, the culture medium was replaced with an assay medium supplemented with 10 mM glucose, 2 mM glutamine, and 1 mM pyruvate. Mitochondrial stress test compounds, including oligomycin (1 µM), FCCP (0.5 µM), and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 µM each), were loaded into the injection ports, and the assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Mass spectrometer

HEK293T cells were transfected with Flag-tagged DDX60 expression vectors or control plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 and incubated for 48 h. Post-transfection, cells were lysed in IP lysis buffer, and lysates were immunoprecipitated using an anti-Flag affinity gel. Immunoprecipitated proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE, followed by tryptic digestion and mass spectrometry-based peptide identification and interaction analysis, conducted by Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. Detailed mass spectrometry parameters, including the instrument model, ionization mode, and data acquisition methods, were described previously [24].

Co-Immunoprecipitations assay analyses

NMCMs were lysed in a buffer containing 20 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 0.3% Triton X-100, and 1× Roche phosphatase and protease inhibitors, followed by collection via scraping. Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min, transferred to fresh tubes, and centrifuged at 11,000 g for 15 min. Approximately 600 µg of total protein was incubated with antibodies against DDX60 or EIF4G1 conjugated to Protein A/G magnetic beads at 4 °C for 5 h under constant rotation. Beads were washed four times with 600 µL of lysis buffer and eluted in Laemmli buffer at 100 °C for 5 min.

Proteomics

Cardiomyocytes were cultured in 10-cm dishes for 24 h, followed by infection with AdshDDX60 or its negative control for another 24 h. Each group comprised three 10-cm dishes. Subsequently, cells were exposed to hypoxic conditions for 24 h, collected, lysed, and analyzed using a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker) coupled with an Evosep One liquid chromatography system (Denmark). Peptide identification, interaction protein analysis, and bioinformatics assessments were performed by Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in AdshDDX60-infected and control groups were identified based on |log2 (fold change)| >1 and p ≤ 0.05. Detailed mass spectrometry parameters, including the instrument model and ionization mode, were described previously [25].

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

RIP was performed using the Magna RIP Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, NMCMs were lysed in RIP buffer, and 5 µg of anti-DDX60 (or anti-Flag) antibody, or anti-rabbit IgG (negative control), was incubated with 50 µL of magnetic beads at 4 °C overnight. The RNA-antibody-magnetic bead complexes were washed five times and treated with proteinase K to release RNA-bound proteins. RNA was extracted using phenol-chloroform and analyzed by qPCR. Target regions were quantified relative to input controls and normalized against IgG controls.

DNA agarose gel electrophoresis

DNA agarose gel electrophoresis was performed as described previously [26]. Briefly, 1.5% agarose gels were prepared using agarose from Sangon Biotech and electrophoresed at 140 V for 30 min in 1 × TBE buffer. Gels were stained with Ultra GelRed and visualized under UV light. Images were captured and processed using Photoshop.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8. Two-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test, while one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for multiple-group comparisons. Survival analysis was assessed using the log-rank test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

DDX60 remarkably alleviated cardiomyocyte apoptosis upon hypoxia stimuli To investigate whether DDX60 regulates cardiomyocyte apoptosis under hypoxia, a time-course experiment was conducted using NMCMs exposed to hypoxia for 0, 12, and 24 h. Western blotting indicated a progressive increase in DDX60 expression at 12 h, which was significantly upregulated at 24 h (Fig. 1A–C), suggesting its potential involvement in cardiomyocyte apoptosis regulation. To further confirm its functional role, DDX60 expression was silenced or overexpressed using adenoviral vectors, with successful transduction confirmed by Western blotting (Fig. S1A–C). Interestingly, DDX60 overexpression significantly reduced apoptosis after 24 h of hypoxia, as indicated by decreased cleaved PARP and cleaved caspase-3 (Fig. 1D–F). In contrast, DDX60 knockdown reversed this effect, highlighting its protective action (Fig. 1G–I). Meanwhile, flow cytometry and TUNEL staining further evaluated the effects of DDX60 on cardiomyocyte apoptosis. Ad–shDDX60–infected NMCMs demonstrated an increase in the percentage of apoptotic cells after hypoxic exposure. However, NMCMs overexpressing DDX60 (Ad-D60 group) demonstrated significantly decreased apoptosis, compared with negative control (Ad-NC) cells (Fig. 1J–K and Fig. S1D-E). Discussion

Mitochondrial dysfunction has emerged as a central pathogenic mechanism underlying cardiomyocyte apoptosis and subsequent heart failure after myocardial infarction. Hence, mitochondrial preservation serves as a critical therapeutic strategy in ischemic heart disease [2–4]. In this study, a novel cardioprotective function of DDX60 was identified, demonstrating its ability to mitigate mitochondrial injury by modulating Arl2 protein synthesis. A combination of biochemical and functional analyses demonstrated that DDX60 enhances Arl2 protein synthesis by forming a complex with EIF4G1 (Fig. 9). This mechanism not only expands the understanding of RNA helicase functions beyond their canonical roles in antiviral defense but also positions DDX60 as a key regulator of mitochondrial proteostasis in cardiac function.Fig. 9Schematic representation of the cardioprotective mechanisms of DDX60 via mitochondrial enhancement and anti-apoptotic regulation Under hypoxic/ischemic conditions, myocardial DDX60 expression is markedly upregulated. The D-E-X-H/D-box helicase family has been traditionally studied in RNA metabolism and antiviral responses. A growing body of evidence demonstrates its regulatory function in cardiovascular diseases. For instance, DHX9 contributes to atherosclerosis progression [17], whereas DDX5 regulates vascular remodeling [18]. The present findings elucidate DDX60’s unique ability to orchestrate mitochondrial protection, a function distinct from its role in RIG-I-mediated antiviral immunity. This functional divergence highlights the contextual specificity of helicases and necessitates investigating understudied helicase members in disease pathogenesis. DDX60 upregulation under hypoxic conditions in cardiomyocytes, coupled with its mitochondrial regulatory function, positions it as a critical stress-responsive mediator in cardiac adaptation to ischemia.

Mitochondria are cellular powerhouses that control ATP generation through oxidative phosphorylation. They orchestrate energy metabolism, calcium homeostasis, and redox regulation, all of which are fundamental processes underpinning cellular viability and apoptotic control. Aberrations in mitochondrial function have been implicated in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular disorders ranging from ischemic cardiomyopathy to atherosclerosis [29, 30]. Beyond cardiovascular contexts, environmental toxicants target mitochondrial networks through distinct mechanisms. For example, arsenic exposure drives ferroptosis via ROS accumulation [31], microplastics induce pyroptosis through membrane destabilization [32], and phthalates activate apoptosis through inflammatory cascades [33]. These events converge on mitochondrial collapse as a universal pathomechanism. The systematic analysis of DDX60’s protective role—spanning transmission electron microscopy, oxygen consumption rate assays, and hierarchical validation through Arl2 overexpression—suggests a conserved regulatory axis that counters these destructive processes. Arl2 restoration in DDX60-deficient NMCMs reverses bioenergetic defects, confirming the DDX60–Arl2 axis as a hierarchical pathway. Moreover, it positions this axis as a universal regulator of mitochondrial resilience under stress.

The mechanistic interplay between DDX60 and its molecular partners forms a tightly coordinated axis critical for cardiac recovery after ischemic injury. Through a multi-layered experimental approach—combining proteomic screening that identified EIF4G1 as a DDX60-binding protein, glutathione S-transferase pull-down assay confirming direct interaction, and RNA immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction confirming DDX60’s association with Arl2 mRNA—a biologically relevant regulatory chain was delineated. This triad of molecular interactions positions DDX60 as a translational enhancer, leveraging EIF4G1’s canonical role in forming the eIF4F translation initiation complex. Stable Arl2 mRNA levels confirm this post-transcriptional regulation, despite protein depletion in DDX60-deficient cardiomyocytes. This finding elucidates translational control rather than transcriptional regulation as the primary mechanism. Notably, the DDX60-Arl2 axis converges on mitochondrial dynamics and cellular survival, with Arl2’s established roles in ATP synthase stabilization and cristae morphology maintaining bioenergetic integrity under hypoxia. Hence, DDX60-mediated Arl2 upregulation protects the ischemic myocardium.

Despite these substantial mechanistic insights, certain limitations merit discussion. First, translational regulation has been demonstrated using Arl2 as a target; nonetheless, further studies should use polysome profiling to delineate precise ribosome loading dynamics. Second, the exclusive use of male murine models limits the generalizability of the findings, specifically related to sex-based responses in MI pathogenesis and post-MI remodeling [34]. Third, the translational applicability of these findings necessitates future investigations using human cardiomyocyte models and pharmacological DDX60 activators. Nevertheless, this study has a few strengths. Advances in gene delivery technologies [35] and hypoxia-responsive vectors [36] may overcome therapeutic barriers, potentially enabling the targeted activation of DDX60 in ischemic tissues.

In conclusion, this study identifies DDX60 as a central mediator of mitochondrial adaptation to ischemic injury through its novel role in Arl2 translation. By bridging RNA helicase function with mitochondrial proteostasis, these findings advance the understanding of post-MI cardiac recovery and offer a novel therapeutic avenue for modulating mitochondrial resilience. The efficacy of AAV9-mediated Arl2 supplementation in rescuing DDX60-deficient phenotypes further supports the translational potential of this axis. Considering the role of mitochondrial dysfunction across several cardiovascular disorders, the DDX60-Arl2 axis holds promise as a strategy to improve clinical outcomes in ischemic heart disease and beyond.
