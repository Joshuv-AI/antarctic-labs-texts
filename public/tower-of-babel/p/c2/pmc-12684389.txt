Dynamic genome-wide mapping reveals how chromatin context shapes OGG1-mediated repair and related mutagenesis in human cells
Authors: Jie Li, Lin Li, Yuanqing Tan, Mengdie Yin, Yuxuan Li, Dongrui Yin, Jiao An, Guanglei Zhuang, Maoxiang Qian, Jinchuan Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPotassium bromate-induced DNA damage, including 8-oxo-7,8-dihydroguanine (OG) and 2,6-diamino-4-hydroxy-5-formamidopyrimidine (FapyG), are common oxidatively generated DNA lesions with mutagenic potential if not efficiently repaired. While sequencing-based studies have shown that damage formation is influenced by DNA sequence context, secondary structures, and chromatin features, how OGG1-mediated repair is regulated within chromatin remains unclear. Here, we apply CLAPS-seq to generate genome-wide, single-nucleotide resolution maps of OGG1-mediated repair over time in human cells, and systematically analyze how chromatin context affects repair efficiency across hierarchical scales. We find that chromatin accessibility governs rapid initial repair, whereas higher-order chromatin structures increasingly influence later-phase repair. In addition, nucleosome occupancy and transcription factor binding, exemplified by CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF), modulate OGG1-mediated repair at both local and base scales. Overall, mutational outcomes correlate more strongly with repair dynamics than with damage levels. Together, these findings establish a comprehensive framework linking chromatin organization, DNA repair kinetics, and oxidatively induced mutagenesis, and offer new insights into the origins of mutation patterns in cancer and diseases associated with oxidative stress.

Introduction

Physiological and pathological processes such as energy metabolism and inflammation, as well as many exogenous oxidants, can produce reactive oxidative species, damaging biomacromolecules including protein, lipid, and nucleic acids [1]. Among the four DNA bases, guanine (G) has the lowest oxidation potential and is readily oxidized to generate a series of G derivates. The one-electron oxidation product of guanine, 8-oxo-7,8-dihydroguanine (8-oxoG, abbreviated as OG), is one of the most abundant oxidatively generated DNA lesions and is regarded as the biomarker of cellular oxidative stress [2]. In addition, 2,6-diamino-4-hydroxy-5-formamidopyrimidine (FapyG) is also formed upon potassium bromate treatment [3].

Base excision repair (BER) is the primary pathway responsible for the removal of OG lesion from the genome. In eukaryotic cells, 8-oxo-7,8-dihydroguanine DNA glycosylase 1 (OGG1) recognizes and excises OGs paired with cytosine (C), generating apurinic/apyrimidinic (AP) sites that are subsequently cleaved by apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease 1 (APE1). The resulting gaps are filled by DNA polymerase β, and the nicks are sealed by DNA ligases to restore DNA integrity [4]. If not repaired in time, DNA polymerases have the chance to insert an adenine (A) instead of C opposite the OG site [5]. The misincorporated A is recognized by another glycosylase MUTYH and removed by the BER pathway [4]. These mechanisms can protect genome fidelity from OG-induced G-to-T substitution, which is one of the major sources of spontaneous mutations [6]. However, the DNA repair machinery cannot eliminate all lesions before the next round of DNA replication, resulting in inevitable mutations. It is worth noting that FapyG can also induce G-to-T transversions and be repaired by OGG1 [7, 8]. The accumulation of oxidation-induced G-to-T mutations is related to multiple human health problems, including cancer, neurodegeneration, and aging [9–11].

On the other hand, OG is not always a destroyer to organisms. It can serve as a sensor of oxidative stress and regulate transcription through various mechanisms [12, 13]. For instance, OG can attract OGG1, which in turn promote the recruitment of transcription factors such as NFκB and cMYC via direct protein interaction [14, 15]. Moreover, the repair intermediate of OG, namely AP sites, can facilitate the formation of DNA secondary structure G quadruplex, which can regulate transcription in the presence of APE1 [16, 17]. The repair machinery of OG is important for transcription regulation besides genome stability, as evidenced by the alleviation of inflammation upon treatment with a small molecular inhibitor of OGG1 [18, 19].

The biological consequences of oxidatively-generated DNA damage, both destructive and constructive, are largely determined by their genomic locations. In the past years, a couple of methods were developed to measure the genomic distribution of OGs [20–28]. However, the steady state frequency of cellular OG is as low as 1 per million nucleotides [29], making it difficult to be accurately mapped at genomic level. Previously we developed the CLAPS-seq (Chemical Labeling And Polymerase Stalling Sequencing) technology to detect the genomic distribution of both endogenous and exogenous OGs at single-nucleotide resolution in human cells [23]. Because the chemical labeling reaction also labels FapyG with lower efficiency than OG, CLAPS-seq measures FapyG in addition to OG, albeit to a lesser extent [30]. Our results suggested that damage formation is mainly determined by DNA sequence context and secondary structures, whereas other factors like chromatin states, transcription factor binding and transcription just have minor effects [31]. However, the repair process of oxidatively-generated DNA damage plays a vital role for its biological functions, and studies on other types of DNA damage showed that chromatin-related factors and events have a strong effect on DNA repair [32–34]. Indeed, biochemical and structural studies unveiled that the existence of nucleosome can impact the activity of OGG1 [35–38]. Nevertheless, how chromatin context affects OGG1-mediated repair on a genome-wide scale is still elusive.

In this study, we employed CLAPS-seq to map the genome-wide distribution of oxidatively induced lesions during the DNA repair process, generating the first dynamic landscape of OGG1-mediated repair in human cells. We systematically examined how different levels of chromatin organization influence distinct phases of OGG1-mediated repair and explored the relationship between repair efficiency and oxidation-induced mutagenesis. Together, these findings offer a comprehensive view of OGG1-mediated repair within the chromatin context and provide new insights into the interplay between genome structure and oxidatively generated DNA damage responses.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and potassium bromate treatment

The U2OS cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, HTB-96) and cultured in McCoy’s 5A medium (IJIN, SJ06625) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Royacel, RY-F22-05) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, 10378016) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 humidified chamber.

Potassium bromate treatment was performed as described previously [23]. Briefly, U2OS cells cultured to 70%–80% confluency in one 150-mm plate per sample and treated with 20 mM of KBrO3 (Sigma–Aldrich, 60085) and 2.5 mM of glutathione (Sigma–Aldrich, V900456). After incubation for 30 min, medium was removed and cells were washed three times with Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline (D-PBS, Sigma–Aldrich, D8662). Then cells cultured in fresh medium for 1, 3, and 12 h before harvested by centrifugation. To minimize the influence of artificial oxidation, at least 70 μg of genomic DNA was extracted by DNAzol as previously described for liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) or CLAPS-seq [23]. For the OGG1 inhibition sample (1h-i, also referred to as oxidized cellular DNA), 20 μM TH5487 (MedChemExpress, HY-125276) was added to the culture medium 10 min before KBrO₃ treatment and maintained throughout the entire treatment, including the subsequent 1-h incubation in fresh medium. For in vitro oxidation (oxidized naked DNA), DNA from untreated cells was incubated with 1 mM of KBrO3 and 2 mM of glutathione in sodium phosphate buffer (10 mM, pH 7.8) at 37°C for 80 min.

LC-MS/MS

Fresh DNA (≥ 30 μg) was digested into nucleosides by 8-OHdG Assay Preparation Reagent Set (Wako, 292-67801) and subsequently analyzed by an ultra-performance liquid chromatography system (Agilent, LC 1290 II), coupled with an electrospray ionization triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX, QTRAP 6500+). The analytes were separated on an ACQUITY UPLC® HSS T3 column (Waters, 1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 50 mm), kept at 35°C for the analysis of dG and 8-oxodG. The mobile phases consisted of two solutions: A (0.1% formic acid in water) and B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). For dG, the sample was first diluted 1:10 000 before analysis. Separation was carried out at a flow rate of 0.3 ml/min with the following gradient: 100% A (0–1 min), 100%–95% A (1–1.1 min), 95% A (1.1–2.5 min), 95%–20% A (2.5–2.6 min), 20% A (2.6–3 min), 20%–100% A (3–3.1 min), and 100% A (3.1–6 min) for re-equilibration. The analyte was monitored using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) with the transition from m/z 268.1 to m/z 152.1 [39]. For 8-oxodG, separation was carried out at the same flow rate (0.3 ml/min) with the gradient: 100%–98% A (0–0.5 min), 98%–95% A (0.5–1 min), 95%–90% A (1–2.5 min), 90%–20% A (2.5–2.6 min), 20% A (2.6–3 min), 20%–100% A (3–3.1 min), and 100% A (3.1–6 min) for re-equilibration. The analyte was monitored using MRM with the transition from m/z 284.2 to m/z 168. A 2 μl sample was injected, and analytes were quantified in electrospray ionization-positive mode using MRM [40]. Detailed instrumental and analytical parameters are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The raw mass spectrometry data were analyzed using SCIEX OS software. Since dG accounts for approximately one-fifth of total deoxynucleosides (dN), the frequency of 8-oxodG in dN was reported as 8-oxodG/(5 × dG).

CLAPS-seq

CLAPS-seq was performed as described previously [23]. Briefly, fresh DNA was immediately labeled with potassium hexabromoiridate (K2IrBr6, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 012651) and amine-tagged biotin probe [Amine-(PEG)11-biotin, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 26136] to reduce artificial oxidation. Purified biotinylated DNA was sonicated to generate fragments averagely 600 bp in length. DNA fragments (up to 5 μg) were ligated to adapter 1 (Ad1) by NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, E6040) and attached to Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin C1 beads. After thorough wash, extension primer O3P was attached to ligated fragments which were still bound on beads and extended by KAPA HiFi DNA polymerase, followed by ExoI treatment and ended by equal volume of 1% sodium dodecyl sulphate. Next, the beads were thoroughly washed, followed by elution with fresh NaOH to release the complementary strands (CSs) and neutralized by acetic acid. Purified extension products were ligated to adapter 2 (Ad2), followed by polymerase chain reaction amplification. Libraries were sequenced in PE150 format on Illumina NovaSeq platform by Mingma Technologies Company.

The repair samples (1, 3, and 12 h) had four biological replicates, while the 1h-i and naked DNA samples were performed once. However, since the correlations (r) of replicates calculated by deepTools (version 3.5.1) [41] suggested high reproducibility (Supplementary Fig. S1A), data from biological replicates are combined for further analysis.

Processing of CLAPS-seq data

Raw sequencing data were processed using the previously described pipeline [23] with modifications, to generate BED files aligned to the hg38 reference genome and containing G at the expected damage sites. BED files from individual samples or merged replicates were converted to BAM files using BEDTools (version 2.30.0) [42]. These BED or resulting BAM files were used to generate strand-specific and nonstrand-specific BigWig files at single-base resolution, normalized using reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM) and scaled according to the LC-MS/MS ratio.

Oxidation-induced C > A (G > T) mutations from cells and patients

C > A (G > T) mutations in KBrO3-treated TP53-KO RPE1 cells were obtained from reference [38]. Somatic mutation data from primary tumors were downloaded from the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC; https://docs.icgc-argo.org/docs/data-access/icgc-25k-data) using the file consensus_snv_indel/final_consensus_passonly.snv_mnv_indel.icgc.public.maf.gz, containing whole-genome sequencing results. Mutational signature analysis was performed with SigProfilerMatrixGenerator (version 1.2.31) [43] and SigProfilerExtractor (version 1.1.25) [44], identifying 76 patients with > 25% SBS18 contribution, a signature of oxidatively induced mutations [45]. All C > A (G > T) single base substitutions from these patients were extracted for downstream analysis. Mutation data in text or MAF format were converted to BED files, lifted over to hg38, and subsequently transformed into BAM files as well as RPKM-normalized BigWig files, both strand-specific and nonstrand-specific.

Compartments A/B analysis

Hi-C data for U2OS cells was downloaded from GEO (accession number GSE222637) in .cool format at 10 kb resolution [46]. Eigenvalue decomposition was performed on the 10 kb resolution Hi-C contact matrix using the call-compartments tool from the cooltools package (version 0.10) [47]. E1 values from eigenvalue decomposition were used to determine A/B compartment status, with positive values indicating A compartments and negative values indicating B compartments. Compartment A/B annotations for TP53-KO RPE1 cells were obtained in BED format from an online repository (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.7351767) [48]. The intersect function in BEDTools was applied to quantify the number of lesions and mutations in compartments A and B. The results were then normalized using RPKM and scaled according to the LC-MS/MS ratio. Based on the processed data, custom R scripts were employed to calculate and visualize the profiles of relative damage formation and the initial, early, and middle phases of repair in U2OS cells, as well as C > A (G > T) mutations in cells. The calculation formulas are as follows and were consistently applied in subsequent analyses:

relative damage formation = (damage1h-i)/(damagenDNA)

initial repair = 1 − (damage1h)/(damage1h-i)

early repair = 1 − (damage3h)/(damage1h)

middle repair = 1 − (damage12h)/(damage3h)

Here, “nDNA” refers to KBrO₃-treated naked DNA. “initial repair” represents the fraction of damage repaired during the initial phase, analogous to “early repair” and “middle repair”. These terms are used consistently throughout the article.

LaminB1 occupation

DamID-seq datasets for LaminB1 in U2OS and RPE1 cells were downloaded from the 4D Nucleome data repository under accession number 4DNESB2HMY7D and 4DNESHGTQ73M, respectively (https://data.4dnucleome.org/) [49, 50]. Average coverage of each sample was calculated in 250 kb resolution based on sorted DamID signal intensity. Downstream analysis and visualization were performed using custom R scripts, similar to the compartment analysis.

Chromatin states

To characterize the chromatin states of cells, ChIP-seq data for various histone modifications were collected for ChromHMM [51] analysis. For U2OS cells, ChIP-seq data for six histone marks were obtained from the GEO database with the following accession numbers: GSE141139 (H3K4me1, H3K4me3, and H3K27ac) [52], GSE31755 (H3K36me3 and H3K9me3) [53], and GSE130230 (H3K27me3) [54]. For RPE1 cells, ChIP-seq data were downloaded from GEO with accession numbers GSE141081 (H3K4me1) [52], GSE184030 (H3K36me3) [55], and GSE222387 (H3K4me3, H3K9me3, H3K27ac, and H3K27me3) [48]. ChIP-seq followed similar processing steps and tools as CLAPS-seq for BAM files generation. Chromatin states were defined using ChromHMM (version 1.25): BAM files were binarized at a 200 bp resolution with the BinarizeBam function, and chromatin states were learned using an 18-state model. For improved interpretation and visualization, the 18 chromatin states were consolidated into 10 categories. Damage levels across these 10 chromatin states were then quantified using RPKM values, and heatmaps were generated with the pheatmap package (version 1.0.12) in R. C > A (G > T) mutation density in RPE1 cells was computed for each of the 10 defined chromatin states.

Genomic accessibility, TSSs, and G-quadruplexes

Protein-coding genes without overlapping or adjacent genes within 2000 bp on either strand were extracted from the hg38 GTF annotation file, downloaded from the UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu), to obtain accurately annotated transcription start sites (TSSs) and transcription termination sites. Based on RNA-seq data from U2OS cells (GEO accession number GSE196722), these genes were further classified into highly expressed (TPM ≥ 5), lowly expressed (0 < TPM < 5), and nonexpressed (TPM = 0) groups. ATAC-seq data for U2OS cells, obtained from GEO (accession number GSE249700), followed similar processing steps and tools as CLAPS-seq for BAM and BigWig files generation. For peak calling, MACS2 (version 2.2.9.1) [56] was employed with no-model, 100 bp shift, and 200 bp extension parameters, with only peaks detected in both replicates for subsequent analysis. Genes were categorized into four groups based on whether their ATAC-seq RPKM values within ±1 kb of TSS and RNA-seq RPKM values were above or below their respective median values. The G-quadruplex (G4) CUT&Tag BED file for U2OS cells was downloaded from GEO (accession number GSE181373) [57]. The potential quadruplex sequences (PQSs) file (in bigBed format) was obtained from GEO (accession number GSE133379) [58], converted to BED format and lifted over to hg38. Metaprofiles of OGG1-mediated repair were generated using the computeMatrix function in deepTools by aligning signals to reference points and summarizing them within ±2000 bp using 10 bp bins.

CpG methylation and differentially methylated regions

The Bisulfite-Seq raw data for U2OS cells [59] was downloaded from Sequence Read Archive (SRA; accession number SAMN34207297). Methylation analysis was performed using msPIPE [60]. Only cytosine sites covered by at least two reads were retained. CpG dinucleotides were then stratified into 10 groups based on their methylation levels. Using the intersect function in BEDTools, the number of detected lesions overlapping CpG sites within each methylation group was calculated and normalized by RPKM. For chromosome-level differential methylation analysis, each chromosome was divided into 100 kb nonoverlapping windows. Average methylation level within each window was calculated using BEDTools based on the methylation levels of cytosines at CpG sites, and was used to rank the windows. Repair levels at non-CpG sites were quantified per window, with analysis and visualization using custom R scripts, similar to the compartment analysis.

Transcriptional strand asymmetry

Strand-specific BigWig files served as input for subsequent analyses and profile visualization conducted using custom Python and R scripts. Read counts for damage formation, repair, and mutations on the transcribed strand (TS) and nontranscribed strand (NTS) of genes with different expression levels were calculated using featureCounts (version 2.0.6) [61].

Nucleosome occupancy

Nucleosome occupancy regions conserved across seven human lymphoblastoid cell lines [62] were obtained in WIG format from GEO (accession number GSE36979). This file was then converted to BigWig format and lifted over to hg38. The nucleosome periodicity of relative damage formation, repair, and patient-derived mutations was analyzed using mutperiod [63]. The figures were generated using the R script provided by mutperiod, with minor modifications.

CTCF binding

Predicted CTCF motif locations were downloaded from the JASPAR database (http://expdata.cmmt.ubc.ca/JASPAR/downloads/UCSC_tracks/2022//hg38/). CTCF ChIP-seq peak data for U2OS cells were obtained in BED format from GEO (accession number GSE87831) [64], while CTCF peaks for TP53-KO RPE1 cells were obtained in BED format from an online repository (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.7351767) [48]. Overlapping regions of 19 bp between CTCF peaks and predicted CTCF motifs in the two cell types were identified using the intersect function in BEDTools. These regions were defined as CTCF-bound motif sites for downstream analyses, and the remaining predicted motif sites, excluding these binding sites, were used as unbound motif sites. Sequence logos for CTCF-bound and unbound motif sites were generated using WebLogo 3 [65] (https://weblogo.threeplusone.com/). Metaprofiles of damage formation, repair, cellular mutations, and nucleosome signals around CTCF-bound or unbound motif sites were generated using the computeMatrix function in deepTools, summarizing signals within ±500 bp using 10 bp bins.

At single-base resolution, metaprofiles of damage formation on the motif strand (MS) and CS at CTCF-bound and unbound motif sites were generated to capture signals from position 1 to 19 bp downstream. Repair levels at these sites on MS and CS were manually calculated using the aforementioned formula and visualized as bar plots.

Statistical analysis and visualization

Statistical analyses were conducted using R packages. The statistical tests used for each analysis are detailed in the figure legends. Plots were generated in RStudio using the ggplot2 package (version 3.5.1), and genomic signal tracks were visualized with the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV, version 2.14.1) [66].

Results

OGG1-mediated repair is suppressed in large-scale repressive chromatin domains

To investigate the dynamics of OGG1-mediated repair at genomic level, we need to separate damage formation and repair since both processes can occur simultaneously and are affected by chromatin factors. Thus, a strong exogenous oxidizing agent rather than pathophysiological oxidative stress is required to induce sufficient DNA damage within a short time. Potassium bromate, a former food additive, was chosen because it preferentially oxidizes guanine and generates few thymine oxidation products. In contrast, another common oxidant, hydrogen peroxide, produces hydroxyl radical which indiscriminately oxidizes all nucleobases [67, 68]. It is worth noting that potassium bromate is not a specific reagent for inducing OG, as it also produces FapyG and other G-derived lesions, such as 2,2,4-triamino-5(2H)-oxazolone [69]. While CLAPS-seq can detect FapyG, the efficiency is lower compared to that for OG. Consequently, under potassium bromate treatment, CLAPS-seq primarily measures OG, while also detecting FapyG at reduced levels. Nevertheless, since both lesions induce G-to-T mutations and are targets of the OGG1-mediated BER pathway, the repair maps generated by CLAPS-seq reflect OGG1 activity, thus serving as a valuable tool to analyze the relationship between repair and mutagenesis.

U2OS cells were treated with potassium bromate for 30 min, and the OG clearance (representing repair kinetics) was monitored by LC-MS/MS (Fig. 1A). Although LC-MS/MS is recommended for OG measurement in heavily oxidized samples [70], basal OG level in untreated cells may be overestimated due to artificial oxidation during DNA extraction and sample preparation. Nonetheless, OG level of untreated sample is used solely to correct for background and artificial OG in other samples (Fig. 1B). Because cellular OG levels continued to rise for a short period after oxidant exposure [23], the 1-h post-treatment time point was selected as the starting point for repair analysis. As shown in Fig. 1B, OG repair commenced rapidly, with 29% of lesions removed in 2 h (i.e. 3 h post-treatment). The repair rate then progressively declined, reaching 57% lesion removal at 12 h post-treatment. The biphasic kinetics we observed is consistent with previous studies [71, 72]. Based on this trend, most (∼85%) of the damage is expected to be repaired at a later time point (between 18 and 24 h). Consequently, the repair map of the last phase should resemble the damage map at 12 h. Therefore, we measured genome-wide distribution of oxidatively-generated DNA damage at 1, 3, and 12 h post-treatment by CLAPS-seq, and defined the 1–3 h window as early-phase repair and the 3–12 h window as middle-phase repair. Since damage formation and repair occur concurrently and immediately after oxidant exposure, we also treated cells with potassium bromate in the presence of an OGG1 inhibitor and profiled damage distribution at 1 h (1h-i, also referred to as oxidized cellular DNA). The difference of damage distributions between 1h-i and 1h samples was used to estimate the extent of initial-phase repair, although this does not reflect a direct time-resolved repair process. In addition, we mapped damage distribution in in vitro–oxidized naked DNA to evaluate the contribution of chromatin context to damage formation.

The spatial organization of the genome plays a critical role in regulating DNA metabolism, including damage formation and repair [73, 74]. In 3D nuclear space, the genome is partitioned into distinct domains known as A and B compartments, which broadly correspond to transcriptionally active and inactive regions, respectively [75]. Damage levels are consistently higher in compartment A than in compartment B, both in vivo (under oxidant and OGG1 inhibitor treatment, 1h-i) and in vitro (oxidized naked DNA) (Supplementary Fig. S1B). This difference is partially due to the higher GC content in compartment A than in compartment B (43.3% versus 38.6%). In addition, neighboring nucleotides and DNA secondary structures also affect damage formation [23]. To isolate the influence of chromatin organization from sequence context and secondary structures, we normalized in vivo damage levels to the corresponding in vitro profile, defining the result as relative damage formation. As shown in Fig. 1C and D, compartment A is slightly more susceptible to oxidation than compartment B. However, the biological significance of this susceptibility is unclear, as the difference is subtle, and the influences of sequence context and chromatin organization on damage formation cannot be separated in biological processes. Importantly, the repair rate is also faster in compartment A, with the difference between compartments becoming more pronounced from the initial to middle repair phases (Fig. 1C and E). To evaluate the mutagenic effects of these differences, we analyzed oxidation-induced C-to-A (G-to-T) mutations in potassium bromate-treated RPE1 cells [38]. As shown in Fig. 1F, compartment B exhibits nearly twice the mutation density compared to compartment A, even though damage levels were consistently higher in compartment A (Supplementary Fig. S1B), suggesting that mutagenesis is shaped more by repair efficiency than by damage abundance at this scale.

The spatial proximity of genomic regions to the nuclear periphery also influences both damage formation and repair. In diploid cells, spatial positioning of each chromosome can be directly measured by DNA-fluorescence in situ hybridization [76–78]. However, aneuploid cells usually have uncertain numbers of chromosomes with severe recombination and rearrangement, making it difficult to directly measure the 3D positioning of each chromosome. Given the aneuploid nature of U2OS cells, we used LaminB1 DamID signal intensity as a proxy for the likelihood of DNA localization at the nuclear lamina. As shown in Fig. 1G, Lamin binding negatively correlates with damage formation (r = –0.81). Strong Lamin association also inhibits OGG1-mediated repair, with this suppression becoming more pronounced at later time points (Fig. 1H). Lamin-associated domains substantially overlap with compartment B (Fig. 1C), representing compact genomic regions that are refractory to both damage formation and repair. Consistently, oxidation-induced mutations in RPE1 cells positively correlate with Lamin binding (r = 0.54) (Fig. 1I), further supporting the notion that large-scale mutagenesis is predominantly governed by repair efficiency rather than initial damage levels.

Chromatin states shape repair dynamics and mutagenesis

Our previous results showed that chromatin state has only a minor effect on damage formation when compared to naked DNA [23]. Similarly, ultraviolet (UV)-induced damage formation is largely independent of chromatin state; however, its repair is strongly promoted in open chromatin regions such as active promoters and enhancers, while being repressed in heterochromatin and repetitive regions [79, 80]. Therefore, we investigated OGG1-mediated repair across different chromatin states (Fig. 1J). Based on repair dynamics, the 10 chromatin states defined by ChromHMM (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section and Supplementary Fig. S1C) can be classified into three groups (Fig. 1J). The first group comprises the most open regions, including Active TSS, Flanking TSS, Active Enhancer, and Genic Enhancer. These regions exhibit rapid repair during the initial and early phases, followed by a decrease in middle-phase repair, likely due to depletion of remaining damage (Supplementary Fig. S1D). The second, intermediate group includes Weak Enhancer, Strong Transcription, and Weak Transcription states. These regions display relatively modest repair during the initial and early phases, but increased repair in the middle phase. The third, closed group consists of Repressed Polycomb, Heterochromatin, and Quiescent states. Among these, Repressed Polycomb, and Heterochromatin exhibit moderate initial-phase repair but sharply reduced repair in the early and middle phases, while the Quiescent state shows consistently low repair across all three phases. To investigate how repair dynamics relate to mutagenesis, we quantified oxidation-induced mutation frequencies in each chromatin state using potassium bromate-treated RPE1 cells (Fig. 1K and Supplementary Fig. S1E). Interestingly, both the open and intermediate groups show low mutation frequencies, whereas the closed group exhibits markedly higher mutation rates, suggesting that efficient repair during the early and middle phases plays a key role in suppressing mutagenesis.

Genomic accessibility drives strong initial repair

Among all chromatin states defined by ChromHMM, the Active TSS state exhibits the fastest initial repair. To further investigate this observation, we examined actual transcription start sites (TSSs) based on gene annotation and RNA-seq data. As expected, actively transcribed TSSs show strong OGG1-mediated repair during the initial phase; however, this signal diminishes in the early phase and becomes even weaker than the surrounding regions during the middle phase (Fig. 2A). This repair trend is less evident at weakly transcribed TSSs and is completely absent at nonexpressed TSSs (Supplementary Fig. S2A and B). The strong initial repair at active TSSs may result from transcription-associated processes or from the high chromatin accessibility at these loci. Supporting the latter, ATAC-seq peaks show a repair dynamic similar to that of active TSSs (Supplementary Fig. S2C), and transcription levels correlate positively with ATAC-seq signals at corresponding TSSs (Fig. 2B). To disentangle the relative contributions of chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activity, we stratified all TSSs into four groups based on their ATAC-seq and RNA-seq signal intensities (Fig. 2B). Group 1, with both high ATAC-seq and RNA-seq signals, displayed robust initial repair. Group 2, with high ATAC-seq but low RNA-seq signal, also showed strong repair, with no significant difference from Group 1 [Fig. 2C (left two panels) and D]. In contrast, Groups 3 and 4, both with low ATAC-seq signals but differing in expression levels, exhibited similarly flat repair profiles, again with no significant difference between them [Fig. 2C (right two panels) and D]. Notably, although all TSSs of Group 2 have lower transcriptional activity than those of Group 3, OGG1-mediated repair of Group 2 is significantly stronger than Group 3 due to its higher accessibility (Fig. 2C and D). These results indicate that genomic accessibility, rather than transcriptional output, is the primary determinant of the strong initial repair observed at active TSSs.

We previously reported that G4 structures which are DNA secondary structures often overlapping with active TSSs inhibit oxidatively generated damage formation, while PQSs without G4 peaks are hotspots for damage accumulation [23, 31]. As expected, repair dynamics around G4s resemble those of active TSSs, while PQSs exhibit an opposite pattern (Fig. 2E and Supplementary Fig. S2D). Notably, only G4s, but not PQSs, are associated with high ATAC-seq signals (Supplementary Fig. S2E). When G4s overlapping with ATAC-seq peaks are excluded, the remaining G4s exhibit markedly reduced initial repair (Fig. 2F). Conversely, ATAC-seq peaks that do not overlap with either G4s or TSSs still show robust initial-phase repair (Fig. 2G). These observations reinforce the conclusion that chromatin accessibility governs OGG1-mediated repair during the initial phase, likely by facilitating the access of OGG1 and other repair factors.

OGG1-mediated repair exhibits minimal transcriptional strand bias

Although transcription is not the primary driver of oxidation-induced damage formation or repair at TSSs, its potential influence on damage formation and repair within gene bodies remains unclear. In both in vitro- and in vivo-oxidized samples, oxidatively generated DNA damage is more abundant on the nontemplate strand (NTS) than on the template strand (TS) in highly expressed genes. This strand asymmetry diminishes in lowly expressed genes and is absent in nonexpressed genes (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S3A). Notably, the strand bias is more pronounced in the in vivo oxidized samples across all expressed genes (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S3B). These results suggest that intrinsic differences between the two strands, such as sequence context or DNA secondary structures, contribute to the observed transcriptional strand asymmetry in damage formation, and that transcription and/or chromatin environment may further amplify this bias in vivo.

Transcription-coupled repair (TCR) selectively removes various types of DNA lesions from the TS of actively transcribed genes and is essential for maintaining genome integrity [81]. However, whether OG and FapyG are also repaired by TCR has remained an open question [82–85]. Our analysis revealed that strand-specific differences in OGG1-mediated repair are minimal across all repair phases and gene expression levels (Fig. 3C and D, and Supplementary Fig. S3C and D). Consistently, only marginal transcriptional strand asymmetry of oxidation-induced mutations has been observed in either cancer genomes or potassium bromate-treated cells (Fig. 3E and F, and Supplementary Fig. S3E and F). These subtle strand asymmetries in both repair and mutagenesis are likely attributable to intrinsic differences in sequence composition between the template and NTSs, which show a modest impact on damage formation. Thus, unlike UV-induced lesions, OG and FapyG do not appear to undergo TCR at the genome-wide level.

DNA methylation enhances early-phase repair of neighboring lesions

Cytosine methylation, predominantly occurring at CpG dinucleotides, is a key epigenetic modification involved in transcription regulation, chromatin organization, and cell fate decision [86]. To investigate whether cytosine methylation influences the formation and repair of oxidatively generated DNA damage at adjacent Gs, we stratified CpG sites into 10 groups based on their methylation levels and examined damage formation and repair at neighboring Gs. Our previous results showed that the presence of an upstream C exerts a mild inhibitory effect on damage formation [23]. However, cytosine methylation has little additional impact on damage formation at downstream Gs, both in vivo and in vitro (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S4A). As expected, damage formation profiles are similar between in vivo and in vitro conditions (Supplementary Fig. S4B), given that extracted genomic DNA retains the methylation landscape of the cell.

Unlike its minimal influence on damage formation, cytosine methylation shows a more nuanced effect on OGG1-mediated repair dynamics. During the initial phase, methylation has little impact, except that CpG sites with the lowest methylation levels are repaired faster than others (Fig. 4B). Strikingly, in the early phase, repair rates display a linear positive correlation with cytosine methylation levels, although the overall effect is moderate (Fig. 4C). In the middle phase, this correlation persists at low methylation levels, but plateaus once methylation exceeds a certain threshold (Fig. 4D). Specifically, during early and middle phases, a positive correlation between repair and cytosine methylation is seen in gene body and intergenic regions, but not in promoter regions (Supplementary Fig. S4C–H). However, only ∼3% of lesions at CpG sites are located at promoters at any time points, as promoter regions have a low occurrence of oxidation-induced damage [23]. Furthermore, fast repair in promoter regions is mainly driven by chromatin accessibility (Fig. 2). Therefore, it is reasonable that promoter regions have a different relationship between repair and cytosine methylation from the rest of the genome and should not affect the overall pattern. Notably, OGG1-mediated repair at CpG sites is consistently faster than the genome-wide average during the initial and early phases, but becomes slower in the middle phase (Fig. 4B–D). The positive correlation between OGG1-mediated repair and cytosine methylation seems to be conflict with biochemical studies showing that cytosine methylation inhibits of OGG1 activity [19, 87, 88]. However, lack of correlation in the initial phase implies that cytosine methylation may promote OGG1-mediated repair through an indirect mechanism—possibly by recruiting repair factors via methylation-binding proteins—with a delayed response following damage induction. To test this, we examined repair in DMRs, excluding lesions located directly at CpG sites. As anticipated, DNA methylation had negligible impact on initial-phase repair (Fig. 4E). However, in the early and middle phases, weak but significant positive correlations between methylation and OGG1-mediated repair were observed (Fig. 4F and G), indicating that cytosine methylation enhances OGG1-mediated repair not only at adjacent guanines but also in nearby genomic regions. These results support the hypothesis that DNA methylation stimulates local repair via an indirect mechanism. Since the existence of OG (and OGG1) interferes with enzymes involved in cytosine methylation and demethylation, such as DNMT1, TET2 and TDG [19, 88], to disturb the homeostasis of DNA methylation, efficient repair of OG at methylated CpG sites may reduce the risk of DNA methylation dysregulation in response to oxidative stress.

Nucleosome organization imposes translational and rotational periodicity on OGG1-mediated repair

The nucleosome is the fundamental unit of chromatin organization. In vitro biochemical and structural studies have shown that nucleosome occupancy can influence OGG1-mediated repair by modulating lesion accessibility [35–38]. To assess this effect in vivo, we analyzed damage formation and repair relative to nucleosome positioning. Comparison of in vivo and in vitro damage distributions revealed that damage formation is suppressed around the dyad axis of central nucleosomes. However, the observed periodicity was weak and lacked biological relevance, as the calculated period (∼55.6 bp) does not align with known nucleosomal features (Fig. 5A). In contrast, repair exhibited a pronounced periodic pattern with a cycle of approximately 194 bp across all three phases (Fig. 5B–D), corresponding to the length of one nucleosome core particle (∼147 bp) plus linker DNA. During the initial and early phases, the linker regions are preferentially repaired, while repair within the nucleosome core is reduced—particularly at the dyad, which shows the slowest repair rate (Fig. 5B and C), consistent with previous in vitro findings [35]. Interestingly, this pattern is reversed in the middle phase, with enhanced repair at the dyad and reduced repair in the linker (Fig. 5D), likely reflecting a shift in the distribution of residual lesions. Consistent with these dynamics, oxidation-induced mutations in cancer genomes show a pattern complementary to repair profiles in the initial and early phases (Fig. 5E), mirroring the mutation landscape observed in potassium bromate-treated cells [38].

Beyond the ∼194 bp translational periodicity (reflecting the spacing between nucleosomes), we also observed a second, finer-scale ∼10 bp rotational periodicity within the nucleosome core. This rotation reflects the helical orientation of DNA, with the minor groove facing toward or away from the histone octamer [89]. Similar to the translational pattern, damage formation was not affected by this rotational positioning (Fig. 5F). However, repair showed a robust ∼10 bp periodicity in all three phases (Fig. 5G–I). During the initial and early phases, lesions facing away from the histone surface (i.e. more solvent-accessible) were repaired more efficiently than those oriented toward the histone core (Fig. 5G and H), again consistent with in vitro results [35]. This pattern, too, was reversed in the middle phase (Fig. 5I). Correspondingly, oxidation-induced mutations in cancer genomes displayed a ∼10 bp periodicity that is complementary to the repair patterns observed in the initial and early phases (Fig. 5J), further corroborating the mutation signatures seen in potassium bromate-treated cells [38].

CTCF binding modulates damage formation and repair at local and base levels

Binding of sequence-specific transcription factors can alter local DNA conformation and hinder the accessibility of repair proteins, thereby modulating both damage formation and repair [79, 90–92]. However, most transcription factors bind to promoters, where oxidatively generated damage formation and repair are predominantly governed by G4 structures and chromatin accessibility, respectively—factors that may mask the effects of transcription factor binding. CTCF (CCCTC-binding factor) is a GC-rich motif-binding protein that not only functions as a transcription factor but also plays a critical role in 3D genome organization by binding to insulators [93]. We therefore used CTCF as a model to assess how transcription factor binding influences oxidatively generated damage formation and repair. Due to the GC-rich nature of the CTCF motif, damage peaks were observed at both CTCF-bound and unbound motif sites in vivo and in vitro (Fig. 6A and B, and Supplementary Fig. S5A and B). However, damage levels at bound motifs are markedly reduced in vivo, whereas unbound motifs show only modest changes, indicating that CTCF binding protects its recognition sites from oxidation (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S5C).

We next analyzed damage formation at single-nucleotide resolution within the CTCF motif (Fig. 6D and Supplementary Fig. S5D), focusing on guanine-enriched positions. The 5′ G of GG or GGG has a lower oxidation potential than other G due to π-stacking, thus making it more susceptible to oxidation [94]. In agreement with the theoretical predication and sequencing data from HeLa cells [23], the 5′ G of potential GG or GGG in the CTCF-binding motif (positions 10 and 13 on the MS and position 5 on the CS) had a higher damage frequency than the immediately downstream G in the in vitro oxidized sample (Fig. 6E). This phenomenon is attenuated in the oxidized cellular DNA sample, probably due to the chemical microenvironment in the nucleus and/or CTCF binding. Moreover, the majority of damage hotspots, such as positions 10–13 on the MS and positions 5 and 8 on the CS, exhibited significantly lower damage levels in vivo compared to in vitro, suggesting reduced damage formation due to CTCF shielding. Conversely, a few positions, mostly with lower in vitro damage levels (e.g. positions 3, 14, and 16 on the MS; 15 and 17 on the CS), showed increased damage formation in vivo (Fig. 6E), though only the fifteenth position on the CS showed a notable enhancement. Overall, CTCF binding predominantly suppresses oxidatively generated damage formation, with minor site-specific exceptions. Unbound motifs displayed a similar but attenuated pattern (Supplementary Fig. S5E).

We then examined OGG1-mediated repair around CTCF peaks. In the initial phase, repair signals in the flanking regions displayed the translational periodicity consistent with nucleosome organization (Fig. 6F). Within the central region, repair levels were elevated compared to the flanks, but showed a dip at the center, forming a “volcano”-like pattern reminiscent of UV damage repair at transcription factor binding sites [90, 91]. This pattern may reflect nucleosome depletion in the central region and steric hindrance at the CTCF binding site. The overall repair signal in the central region declined during the early phase, while the volcano pattern was fully reversed during the middle phase, likely due to changes in the distribution of residual lesions. Notably, despite higher damage levels in the central region, mutation frequency was lower than in the flanking regions, with a slight peak at the center (Fig. 6G). In contrast, unbound motifs, which lack well-positioned nucleosomes in their flanking regions, exhibited relatively flat repair across all phases and displayed a central mutation peak (Supplementary Fig. S5F–H).

Finally, we assessed repair kinetics at single-nucleotide resolution across bound CTCF motifs. Interestingly, positions with enhanced damage formation in vivo (e.g. 3, 14, 16 on the MS; 15, 17 on the CS) displayed high initial-phase repair that declined in later phases (Fig. 6H). Conversely, positions with suppressed in vivo damage formation (e.g. 10–13 on the MS; 5 and 8 on the CS) showed low or moderate initial repair, which increased during the early phase and remained steady or slightly declined in the middle phase. These results suggest that site-specific solvent accessibility of bases dictates both damage susceptibility and repair accessibility. Partial preservation of these patterns at unbound motifs (Supplementary Fig. S5I) implies that, beyond CTCF binding, intrinsic sequence context and/or interactions with other chromatin-associated proteins, such as histones, contribute to these fine-scale effects.

Discussion

As a representative form of oxidatively generated DNA damage, the mechanism of OG repair via BER has been extensively characterized in vitro [4]. However, because both damage formation and OGG1-mediated repair occur in the context of chromatin and coincide with other nuclear processes, their interplay is likely to be complex. For example, biochemical and structural studies have shown that nucleosome occupancy can influence the activity of OGG1 [35–38]. Consistently, our in vivo data recapitulate the nucleosome-associated translational and rotational periodicity predicted from in vitro analyses (Fig. 5), supporting the reliability and accuracy of our approach. Beyond nucleosomes, chromatin is organized at multiple hierarchical levels. At the largest scale, the genome is partitioned into spatially distinct condensates known as compartments A and B, corresponding to transcriptionally active (loose) and inactive (compact) chromatin, respectively. These compartments also differ in their nuclear localization, with compartment A typically localized toward the nuclear interior and compartment B toward the periphery [75]. Notably, such large-scale chromatin architecture cannot be reconstituted in vitro. In this study, we found that these compartments significantly influence both oxidatively generated damage formation and repair (Fig. 1). Besides the higher GC content (as well as other DNA sequence and secondary structure features) in compartment A that leads to increased damage levels in in vitro–oxidized DNA, compartment A remains slightly more prone to oxidation in vivo, although the biological significance of this subtle difference is unclear. A recent study similarly reported that compartment A is more susceptible to cisplatin-induced DNA adducts, likely due to the preferential accumulation of the drug in these loose condensates [73]. It is plausible that compartment A is more accessible to oxidants, thereby facilitating oxidation-induced damage formation. Conversely, the accessibility of repair proteins to compartment A may also promote more efficient repair, as reflected by the faster repair kinetics we observed in this compartment. Intriguingly, the disparity in repair between compartments A and B becomes more pronounced at later time points. Given the heterogeneous nature of chromatin compartments, repair activity within each compartment is unlikely to be uniform. Even within compartment B, certain subregions may undergo relatively rapid repair, close to compartment A. As these lesions are cleared, the overall repair rate in compartment B slows down, thereby amplifying the apparent difference in repair efficiency between compartments A and B over time. Another nonexclusive explanation is that potassium bromate treatment might also oxidize the critical cysteines in the zinc finger domains of CTCF, impeding its DNA binding capacity and disrupting high-order chromatin architecture. It was recently reported that UV irradiation can quickly alter 3D chromatin structure [95]. Disrupted high-order chromatin architecture could be restored several hours after potassium bromate treatment, which would make the difference in the repair rate between compartments A and B more pronounced at later time points.

Regulation of OGG1-mediated repair at small genomic scales is more complex, as it involves multiple interrelated factors, including histone modifications, transcriptional activity, chromatin accessibility, and DNA secondary structures. For example, active TSSs, which exhibit strong initial repair, are consistently associated with active histone marks (as defined by ChromHMM chromatin states), high chromatin accessibility, and enrichment of G4 structures. This raises a key question: which of these features plays the dominant role in driving the observed initial repair? To address this, we performed stratified analyses to control for confounding variables, and found that chromatin accessibility is the primary determinant of initial repair efficiency. Unlike large-scale chromatin structures, whose influence becomes more apparent over time, the effect of accessibility is most prominent immediately after damage induction and diminishes in later repair phases. Mechanistically, repair proteins are able to efficiently recognize and engage lesions located in highly accessible regions right after damage occurs, resulting in robust initial repair. As these accessible lesions are resolved, the overall repair rate subsequently declines. At the nucleosome and sub-nucleosome levels, the initial repair rate is similarly governed by the local accessibility of lesions which are defined by their exposure to the solvent, and follows comparable repair kinetics with the dynamic repair pattern around active TSSs. These findings suggest a general principle for base lesion repair: damage accessibility is the predominant factor controlling initial repair, as efficient repair fundamentally depends on the ability of repair proteins to access damaged sites.

CLAPS-seq enables the detection of OG (and FapyG with lower efficiency) lesions at single-nucleotide resolution, allowing us to investigate damage formation and repair at base level. Previous studies on UV-induced DNA lesions have shown that transcription factor binding can locally inhibit repair in promoter regions, which are otherwise efficiently repaired, resulting in a characteristic “volcano” pattern [90, 91]. At the base scale, the binding of different transcription factors influences damage formation in a factor-, strand-, and position-specific manner, likely by altering DNA conformation [79]. However, the formation and repair mechanisms of UV-induced lesions and oxidatively generated damage differ substantially. Using CTCF as an example, we observed a global suppression of oxidatively generated damage formation at its binding motifs, along with a similar volcano-shaped repair pattern during the initial repair phase (Fig. 6). Moreover, CTCF binding modulates damage formation in a strand- and position-specific manner, primarily exerting an inhibitory effect at the base scale that is consistent with the global pattern. Intriguingly, nucleotides that are susceptible to oxidation in vivo also undergo efficient repair during the initial phase. This may reflect a shared mechanism in which CTCF binding exposes specific bases to the solvent, facilitating access by both oxidizing agents and repair proteins. Conversely, positions protected from oxidation in vivo exhibit slower initial repair that accelerates over time, possibly because CTCF shields these sites from solvent exposure until damage occurs, which may then disrupt the protein–DNA interaction and permit repair factor access in later phases. Due to the limited number of detected mutations, base-level analysis of mutagenesis was not feasible. Nevertheless, the globally reduced mutation frequency at CTCF-bound motifs, compared to the elevated mutation frequency at unbound motifs, suggests that CTCF protects its conserved, GC-rich binding sites from oxidation-induced mutagenesis through coordinated effects on damage formation and repair. Further investigation is needed to determine whether other sequence-specific DNA-binding proteins confer similar protective effects.

It is well established that OG and FapyG lesions can induce C-to-A (G-to-T) transversions if not repaired in a timely manner [1]. The occurrence of such mutations is governed by both the formation of damage and its subsequent repair. However, the same chromatin-related factor may exert opposing influences on mutagenesis depending on its impacts on damage formation or repair, and at which phase. For example, compartment A (or nuclear interior regions with low Lamin association) facilitates both the formation and the repair of oxidatively generated damage (Fig.1), promoting mutagenesis through the former while suppressing it through the latter. Although the promotion of damage formation by compartment A itself is subtle, the chromatin structure together with GC-rich sequence context leads to an obviously higher damage frequency in compartment A than in compartment B. Analysis of mutation distributions in potassium bromate-treated cells revealed significantly lower mutation frequencies in compartment A compared to compartment B. This was observed despite consistently higher levels of oxidatively generated damage in compartment A across all time points examined, suggesting that efficient repair, rather than enhanced damage formation, accounts for reduced mutation frequencies in compartment A. Similarly, mutation patterns across different chromatin states and around nucleosomes correlate more strongly with repair dynamics than with initial damage formation. Notably, genomic regions with low mutation frequencies exhibit distinct repair kinetics: active chromatin states, nucleosomal linker regions, and nucleosome outer positions undergo rapid repair during the initial and early phases, with repair efficiency declining in the middle phase. In contrast, compartment A and moderately active chromatin states display relatively slower or intermediate levels of initial repair, which then accelerates at later stages. These findings suggest that oxidation-induced mutagenesis is primarily shaped by the efficiency and timing of repair rather than by the extent of damage formation. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the temporal dynamics of OGG1-mediated repair is essential for accurately predicting mutation susceptibility across the genome.

The conclusions of this study may apply to both OG and FapyG. Unlike the selective type II photosensitizer Ro19-8022 which specifically induces OG under blue light, potassium bromate treatment generates both OG and FapyG, with the yield of FapyG being comparable to or even higher than that of OG [3, 71]. However, CLAPS-seq utilizes a selective oxidation reaction that is approximately three times more efficient on OG than on FapyG [30]. Therefore, the majority of damage sites detected by CLAPS-seq should be OG, although the contribution of FapyG cannot be neglected. It is worth noting that both OG and FapyG induce C-to-A (G-to-T) transversions [7]; hence, mutations observed in potassium bromate-treated cells and cancer patients likely arise from both types of damage. Nevertheless, since both OG and FapyG are repaired by OGG1 [8], the repair pattern detected by CLAPS-seq reflects the impacts of chromatin organization on OGG1 activity and should be applicable to both lesions. Indeed, FapyG is repaired faster than OG [8], suggesting that OG repair might be competitively inhibited under our experimental conditions, which also induced FapyG. This could explain the relatively slower repair of OG observed in this study compared to previous reports [72]. Alternatively, but not exclusively, the relatively strong oxidation condition used in this study may have also oxidized the repair proteins, including OGG1, leading to the inactivation of BER pathway [14]. Similarly, potassium bromate treatment may damage other DNA binding proteins, especially zinc finger-containing proteins, thus disrupting chromatin organization and 3D architecture and impairing the modulatory effects of these structures on OGG1-mediated repair.

In summary, this study provides integrated, dynamic maps of oxidatively generated damage formation and repair at single-nucleotide resolution across the human genome. By systematically analyzing the impact of chromatin organization at multiple hierarchical levels from nuclear compartments to nucleosome positioning and transcription factor binding, we demonstrate that OGG1-mediated repair is not uniformly distributed, but instead tightly regulated by the chromatin context in a scale- and time-dependent manner. These findings collectively highlight a unifying model in which the interplay between chromatin features and repair dynamics, rather than merely the extent of initial damage—determines the eventual mutational outcome. This paradigm helps explain the nonrandom nature of mutational signatures observed in cancer genomes and oxidant-exposed cells, and provides a valuable framework for interpreting how genome instability arises under oxidative stress. The approach and insights presented here pave the way for future studies aimed at identifying repair-sensitive regions, understanding chromatin-driven mutational biases, and ultimately developing strategies to predict and mitigate genome instability in disease.
