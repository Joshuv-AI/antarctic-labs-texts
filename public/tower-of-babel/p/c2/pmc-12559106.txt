Deep learning-based vessel and nerve recognition model for lateral lymph node dissection: a retrospective feasibility study
Authors: Shoma Sasaki, Daichi Kitaguchi, Tomohiro Noda, Hiroki Matsuzaki, Hiro Hasegawa, Nobuyoshi Takeshita, Masaaki Ito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeLateral lymph node dissection for rectal cancer is challenging because of the presence of blood vessels and nerves essential for postoperative genitourinary function and leg movements. Identifying these structures during surgery is crucial. We developed a deep learning-based semantic segmentation model to recognize and visualize critical anatomical structures during laparoscopic lateral lymph node dissection automatically.MethodsIntraoperative video data from laparoscopic lateral lymph node dissections performed on 22 patients between 2018 and 2021 were used. Specific scenes from the beginning to end of the procedures were extracted and divided into still images, which were annotated to delineate the external iliac artery, external iliac vein, and obturator nerve. The model was trained with pixel-level annotation labels, and its performance was evaluated using precision, recall, and the Dice coefficient through five-fold cross-validation.ResultsOverall, 992 images were extracted from 22 lateral lymph node dissection videos. The Dice coefficient values were 0.789 (± 0.009), 0.736 (± 0.033), and 0.574 (± 0.082) for the obturator nerve, external iliac artery, and external iliac vein, respectively. The model’s inference speed was 12.7 fps, corresponding to processing one still image in 0.08 s, enabling near real-time intraoperative analysis.ConclusionThe deep learning-based semantic segmentation model automatically recognized the obturator nerve, external iliac artery, and external iliac vein during laparoscopic lateral lymph node dissection, achieving reasonable segmentation accuracy as measured by the Dice coefficient. This technology will be used as a foundation for developing surgical navigation systems to improve the safety and efficiency of lateral lymph node dissection procedures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00423-025-03882-7.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the third most predominant cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with rectal cancer accounting for approximately one-third of all cases [1]. Total mesorectal excision (TME) combined with total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT) is the standard treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer in Western countries [2–4]. While lateral lymph node (LLN) metastasis was historically considered systemic disease and rarely treated surgically [2, 5], recent evidence highlights the potential benefit of lateral lymph node dissection (LLND) in reducing local recurrence [6, 7]. However, LLND presents surgical challenges.

The lateral pelvic region, which encompasses the vesicohypogastric fascia and obturator nerve (ON) compartment, is bound medially by the ureterohypogastric fascia and laterally by the parietal pelvic fascia. This region contains major blood vessels, posing a risk of intraoperative hemorrhage. Additionally, it houses numerous nerves critical for postoperative genitourinary function and leg movements. Accurately identifying these anatomical structures during LLND is essential, as they serve as crucial landmarks and vital structures that must be preserved. Proper recognition of these structures and their trajectories is not only crucial to prevent intraoperative iatrogenic injuries but may also contribute to reducing operative time, thereby enhancing surgical safety and efficiency.

Deep learning (DL)-based computer vision has gained recognition for enhancing surgical procedures [8, 9]. Semantic segmentation, an image-recognition algorithm that classifies each pixel, is extensively used in clinical applications, including computer-assisted diagnosis [10, 11], interventions [12, 13], and surgeries [8, 14]. Applying semantic segmentation to LLND enables a surgical support system to automatically identify critical vessels and nerves in real-time, addressing the surgical challenges associated with LLND and potentially improving both safety and efficiency. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to develop and evaluate a semantic segmentation model that automatically recognizes and visualizes critical anatomical structures during laparoscopic LLND.

Materials and methods

Study design

In this single-center retrospective feasibility study, we used intraoperative video data from laparoscopic LLND procedures for rectal cancer conducted at the National Cancer Center Hospital East, Chiba, Japan, between 2018 and 2021. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Cancer Center Hospital East (registration no. 2020 − 315). This study adhered to the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki, 1964 (as revised in Brazil in 2013). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients for video recording.

Dataset

All videos were preprocessed to standardize the format to MPEG-4 Part 14, high-definition (1280 × 720), and 30 fps. Specific scenes from the beginning and end of the LLND procedures were extracted and divided into still images. These images were meticulously reviewed and annotated by two experienced colorectal surgeons (TN and DK) using a computer vision annotation tool (https://opencv.github.io/cvat/about/). TN performed the initial annotations, and DK independently double-checked each annotated object. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was achieved. The annotations delineated the boundaries of three key structures: the external iliac artery (EIA), external iliac vein (EIV), and ON. Each pixel was assigned annotation labels to accurately identify the anatomical structures, deliberately omitting unclear or obscured areas. Different labels were applied when multiple structures appeared within the same frame to ensure precise identification. This rigorous annotation process aimed to maintain data quality and minimize potential errors or inconsistencies in subsequent analyses. We did not formally assess inter-rater reliability at the pixel level for this dataset.

Computing and model details

Ubuntu 18.04.5 LTS (Bionic Beaver; Ubuntu Technologies, Pretoria, South Africa) was utilized. The central processing unit was Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6230 (Intel, Santa Clara, CA, USA), with 20 cores and 40 threads. It had 48 GB of random-access memory (RAM) and was equipped with one Tesla V100S (Nvidia Corporation, Santa Clara, CA, USA) graphics processing unit with 32 GB of video RAM. The system ran Python, version 3.6.9, with TensorFlow, version 2.4.1 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA). It used CUDA, version 11.0, and cuDNN, version 8 (Nvidia Corporation). We trained a semantic segmentation model using a Feature Pyramid Network architecture with an EfficientNet-B7 backbone [15, 16]. The hyperparameters utilized for model training are presented in Online Resource 1. The model was trained to classify four classes: background, nerve, vein, and artery. The backbone was pre-trained on ImageNet [17].

Evaluation

Evaluation metrics included precision, recall, and the Dice coefficient (DC). Precision, or “positive predictive value,” measures the proportion of relevant instances among all retrieved cases. Recall, or “sensitivity,” indicates the proportion of relevant instances retrieved from all available instances. The DC assesses the overlap between the manually annotated ground truth area and the segmentation area predicted by the DL model (Fig. 1). To ensure robust evaluation, the model was subjected to five-fold cross-validation. In this method, the dataset is divided into five equal parts. The model is trained on four parts and tested on the remaining part. This process is repeated five times, each part used once as the test set. The results are averaged to provide a reliable performance estimate, reducing the risk of overfitting and ensuring unbiased evaluation. In our study, the data were split at a per-case level rather than a per-image level. Additionally, the inference speed of the developed DL-based model was assessed in frames per second. From these videos, 992 still images were annotated to mark the boundaries of three key anatomical structures. Discussion

In this study, we successfully developed a DL-based semantic segmentation model that automatically recognizes key structures in laparoscopic LLND, including the ON, EIV, and EIA. Quantitative evaluation using precision, recall, and the DC demonstrated considerable recognition performance, indicating the feasibility of identifying these structures. The model holds potential as a foundational technology for constructing surgical navigation systems to enhance the safety and efficiency of the LLND procedure. Interestingly, the DL model achieved higher DC and Recall values for the obturator nerve than for the larger external iliac artery and vein. The obturator nerve maintains a relatively consistent anatomical trajectory and has fewer adjacent structures with a similar appearance, which may make it easier for the model to identify. Conversely, the Dice coefficient for the artery class was lower than that for the nerve and vein classes, likely because of the lower contrast between arteries and surrounding tissues, as well as their reduced surface exposure compared to veins and nerves.

Despite its potential benefits [6, 7, 18], LLND remains challenging, owing to the limited number of eligible cases and high technical difficulty. To mitigate the risk of injuring critical anatomical structures, which could result in severe complications, these structures should be accurately identified and preserved during LLND [19]. Tsukada et al. reported that ON injury occurred at a frequency of approximately 1.3% following TME combined with LLND [20]. In contrast, ON injury was not observed in patients who underwent chemoradiotherapy plus TME without LLND. Moreover, bleeding from the iliac vessels during LLND is a critical event that must be avoided owing to its potentially fatal consequences [21]. While intraoperative bleeding can partially depend on the surgeon’s intuition and skill, anatomical misidentification of vessels poses a high risk of fatal accidental bleeding. Therefore, developing a rapid and accurate method to identify and delineate the position and contours of these vessels is crucial. In this context, the DL-based model developed in our study may assist surgeons in accurately identifying key structures, thereby enhancing the safety of the surgical procedure.

Considerable research has been conducted on vessel detection in endoscopic surgeries. Ryu et al. demonstrated the feasibility of identifying the superior mesenteric vein and ileocolic artery and vein during right-sided colon cancer surgery [14]. In addition, Kitaguchi et al. reported that their DL-based semantic segmentation model showed high accuracy in recognizing the inferior mesenteric artery in intraoperative images [22]. In this study, we aimed to develop an effective DL-based intraoperative support system using a large clinical dataset of videos from laparoscopic LLND procedures, which apply to rectal cancer and gynecological and urological malignancies. For instance, LLND may be indicated for cervical, endometrial, and ovarian cancers in the gynecological domain [23–25]. Moreover, in the urological field, it can be utilized for muscle-invasive bladder cancer and high-risk prostate cancer. Consequently, our model may also be relevant for gynecologic and urologic surgeries involving lateral pelvic lymphadenectomy.

This development not only helps avoid adverse events during surgery but also holds potential as a tool to evaluate the completeness of LLND. Mascagni et al. presented a DL-based model to evaluate the achievement of the critical view of safety in laparoscopic cholecystectomy by assessing whether the cystic duct is exposed using segmentation masks of anatomical structures [26]. In LLND, the ON, EIV, and EIA are landmarks for dissection. After lymphadenectomy, the perivascular adipose tissue must be removed entirely, and the ON, EIV, and EIA should be exposed to eliminate the possibility of lateral space recurrence [27]. Baghdadi et al. developed and validated an automated assessment of a surgical performance system for objectively evaluating LLND during robot-assisted radical cystectomy using console-feed videos, focusing on the number and cleared area of the vessels/nerve [28]. They demonstrated that the Pelvic Lymphadenectomy Assessment and Completion Evaluation (PLACE) score, which is a structured intraoperative scoring system designed to measure and quantify LLND for quality control purposes [27], was automatically calculated by the system and that it matched the PLACE score rated by expert surgeons with an accuracy of 83.3% [28]. Similarly, the DL-based model that we developed may enable not only securing the safety of LLND but also the automatic evaluation of the quality of the procedure by assessing the extent of vascular and nerve exposure. In addition to intraoperative support, this DL-based model has potential educational applications. Given the technical complexity of LLND and the limited exposure trainees have to this operation, the model could be utilized for simulation-based learning or structured video debriefing. Moreover, it may enable the quantitative tracking of trainee progress over time, using metrics such as nerve exposure rates or the extent of lymphadenectomy. These educational applications could complement established quality assessment tools, such as the PLACE score, and potentially help standardize LLND training and credentialing.

This study had some limitations. First, although the proposed DL model demonstrated feasibility in analyzing recorded videos as a proof of concept, its clinical usefulness still needs to be confirmed. We did not compare the model’s performance to that of experienced surgeons, nor did we conduct a systematic qualitative assessment by surgeons. Such benchmarking and expert evaluations are vital to validate predictive accuracy, verify anatomical boundary delineation, and build trust in surgical settings. Future research should include both quantitative and qualitative assessments and evaluate the model’s impact on surgical performance and patient outcomes. Second, the number of anatomical structures the model recognizes, limited to the ON, EIA, and EIV, is insufficient. Future studies should aim to expand the range of critical anatomical structures related to LLND, such as the internal iliac artery and vein, that the model can identify. Third, the dataset used in this study was limited in both size and diversity, as all video data were sourced from a single institution and, consequently, the median patient BMI was 22 kg/m². This narrow sampling may have introduced sampling bias toward lean patients, in whom identifying blood vessels and nerves is generally less challenging, thereby risking overfitting and limiting generalizability. To address this, future research should focus on expanding the dataset by enrolling patients from multiple institutions. This expansion will enhance the size and diversity of the DL model, thereby improving its performance. Finally, although annotations were carefully reviewed and reconciled by two experienced colorectal surgeons, inter-rater reliability (IRR) was not formally assessed at the pixel level. One surgeon performed the initial annotations, and the other double-checked each annotated object, resolving discrepancies through discussion. Pixel-level IRR metrics, such as kappa values or interobserver Dice scores, were not calculated. This limitation should be noted, as it may impact the ability to quantitatively assess annotation consistency.

Conclusion

The DL-based semantic segmentation model can automatically recognize the ON, EIV, and EIA in laparoscopic LLND videos. To achieve reliable surgical navigation, future improvements should focus on broadening the range of recognizable anatomical structures and enhancing prediction accuracy and efficiency through the use of larger datasets and model optimization. Furthermore, the practical application of the model during surgery is crucial for evaluating its effectiveness in enhancing surgical safety and efficiency.
