Assessing the lateral fabellotibial suture technique with an assistant device for cranial cruciate ligament rupture: a cadaveric investigation of pressure mapping in small dogs
Authors: Ingoo KWAK, Geonil MOON, Yong YU, Suyoung HEO
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to determine the optimal stifle joint angle for the lateral fabellotibial suture (LFTS) technique in small dogs with cranial cruciate ligament rupture (CCLR), by quantitatively assessing biomechanical parameters using pressure mapping and a 3D-printed tension assistant device. LFTS procedures were performed on 12 canine hindlimb cadaveric models divided into five groups: (1) Intact group, (2) cranial cruciate ligament-deficient (CCL) group, and (3–5) stifles fixed at 90°, 105°, and 135° (L90, L105, L135). Suture tension was quantitatively measured using a tensioner. Postoperative evaluations included pedobarography, tibial rotation angle, patellofemoral contact pressure, and femorotibial contact pressure, analyzed via force-sensitive resistors and pressure-sensitive films. Pressure mapping visualizations were used to evaluate joint mechanics and pressure distribution. Statistical analyzes were conducted using two-way ANOVA with post-hoc testing. The applied suture tension differed significantly among angle-fixed groups (P<0.05). Significant correlations were observed between experimental groups and pad pressure distribution (P<0.001). No significant difference in femorotibial contact pressure was noted between the L105 and intact groups. These results suggest that a stifle angle of 105° during LFTS most closely replicates normal joint contact mechanics, offering practical insights for optimizing tensioning protocols in small dogs.Keywords: lateral fabellotibial suture, pressure mapping, pressure-sensitive film, small dogs, tensioner

INTRODUCTION

Cranial cruciate ligament rupture (CCLR) is a common cause of hindlimb lameness in dogs, with differences in clinical presentation and pathophysiology between small and large breeds [3, 8, 17, 18]. In this study, small-breed dogs were defined as those weighing less than 10 kg, consistent with previous literature. However, the definition of “small breed” varies across studies, with some using a 15 kg threshold [3].

In large breeds, CCLR is typically associated with progressive ligament degeneration and immune-mediated synovitis. By contrast, in small-breed dogs, CCLR is more often linked to anatomical and biomechanical abnormalities, such as medial patellar luxation, shallow femoral trochlea, and a steep tibial plateau angle, which may contribute to altered joint mechanics and increased cranial tibial thrust [3]. These differences may influence both clinical presentation and surgical planning. Therefore, elucidating CCLR mechanics in small-breed dogs can provide valuable insights for breed-specific treatment strategies.

CCLR leads to stifle joint instability characterised by cranial tibial translation, internal tibial rotation, and stifle hyperextension. These biomechanical alterations often result in synovial inflammation, cartilage degeneration, meniscal injury, and eventual osteoarthritis [3, 7, 10, 19, 23, 25, 26].

Lateral fabellotibial suture (LFTS) is one of the most commonly employed surgical stabilisation techniques for CCLR treatment [3, 7, 8, 23, 25, 26]. However, the technical procedures are not standardised, and may vary depending on patient size, anatomical variations, or surgeon preference. Although alternative techniques, such as tibial plateau levelling osteotomy (TPLO) are widely used, particularly in medium to large breed dogs, LFTS remains a popular option in small dogs due to its relatively low technical demand, minimal equipment requirements, and affordability when osteotomy procedures are not feasible [8, 9, 14, 18, 23,24,25,26].

Although maintaining appropriate tension during the application of the LFTS is considered critical for surgical success. A clear definition of “appropriate tension” has not yet been established, and most procedures still rely on the surgeon’s subjective assessment at the time of surgery [8]. Studies and devices designed to quantitatively evaluate tension have been introduced to improve subjective assessment. However, their practical application and reproducibility when using suture tensioners are controversial due to inter-user discrepancies [8, 18].

In addition, the evaluation of many orthopaedic procedures or postoperative outcomes is subjective [5]. To improve subjectivity, many studies have reported objective measurement methods such as force-sensing resistors (FSR) or pressure-sensitive films (PF) [1, 6, 11, 15, 28]. Studies have also evaluated femorotibial contact pressure in large dogs using FSR following orthopaedic surgeries that affect weight bearing, such as tibial tuberosity advancement or TPLO [15, 16]. However, the size and cost of commercial sensors limit application in small dogs. Thus, no study has yet evaluated femorotibial contact pressure in small dogs weighing <10 kg. The PF can be applied by modifying the measurement area according to intention, making it a viable alternative to the FSR. By scanning the irreversible colour change of the PF according to the pressure range by pressing, this technique can be applied to joint structures that are difficult for sensors to access for which pressure pattern evaluation is required [1, 28].

This study aimed to determine the optimal stifle joint angle during LFTS that sufficiently suppresses cranial tibial translation and restores joint stability in small dogs with CCLR, by quantitatively evaluating biomechanical outcomes using pressure mapping and a 3D-printed tension assistant device (3DTAD). The secondary objective was to assess the postoperative outcomes using pressure mapping. We hypothesized that when LFTS is performed with appropriate tension and stifle angle without excessive extension or flexion, the recovery of paw load distribution, tibial rotation, and femorotibial pressure during standing would be similar to that in intact conditions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Six cadaveric dogs for which associated bone deformities and other musculoskeletal diseases were ruled out on manual examination and pre-experimental CT scans were included. The age, weight, and orientation of each experimental limb were recorded for each specimen. This study was approved by the ethics committee of Jeonbuk National University Animal Care and Use Committee.

The hindlimbs were harvested post-mortem by disarticulation at the coxofemoral joint from adult dogs euthanized for reasons unrelated to this study [21]. Soft tissues except the ligamentous structures and capsule of the stifle and hock joints, but including the pad and skin were dissected at a level distal to the hock joint. Approximately 5 cm of the quadriceps muscle was preserved from the proximal patella. All specimens were wrapped with a lap sponge soaked in sterile saline, and packaged under sterile conditions, stored at −20°C, and thawed at −4°C for 24 hr before the experiment.

Specimen preparation

Specimens were categorised into five groups based on ligament status and stifle angle during LFTS. A summary of each group is provided in Table 1. The stifle angles used during LFTS (90°, 105°, and 135°) were selected to represent a wide range of joint positions encountered during functional movement and clinical management. Specifically, 135° has been reported as the physiological stifle angle during quiet standing in dogs, whereas 90° and 105° represent flexed positions commonly used intraoperatively or during biomechanical testing [21, 22]. This range was chosen to evaluate the effect of joint angle on isometry and tension consistency of the extracapsular suture.

After completing data collection at one angle, the prosthesis was removed, and the procedure was repeated at a different angle. To minimize the potential influence of sequential testing on the same specimen (e.g., order bias, tissue fatigue), a randomized order of angle testing was employed. A turnbuckle links and braided steel cables were used to mimic the quadriceps mechanism and the gastrocnemius muscle. The experimental setup is provided in Fig. 1. To mitigate the influence of the hock joint, temporary tarsocrural immobilization was performed using a 2.7 mm self-tapping cortical screw (ABLE Inc., Jeonbuk, South Korea) at a joint angle of 145°, as previously described in ex vivo studies of canine stifle biomechanics [2, 12, 13]. All loading procedures were conducted using a universal testing machine (WL2100C; Withlab Inc., Gyeonggi, South Korea). The proximal femur was fixed to the testing machine using industrial resin, ensuring that the femoral long axis was oriented perpendicular to the horizontal plane of the machine. Although this positioning does not fully replicate the physiological femoral inclination during standing, it was chosen to provide consistent alignment for mechanical testing. The stifle joint angle was set to 135 ± 5°, replicating the typical joint angle observed during quiet standing in dogs, and measured as the angle formed between the longitudinal axes of the femur and tibia using anatomical landmarks: the greater trochanter, lateral femoral condyle, and lateral malleolus. Pad contact pressure was adjusted to 0 N to simulate neutral standing contact. A load corresponding to 30% of the donor’s body weight was applied, based on a prior study [21].

3D-printed tension assistant device

A 3DTAD was designed using 3D design software (Fusion 360; Autodesk, San Rafael, CA, USA) to apply tension to the prosthesis (Fig. 2). The 3DTAD features a spindle-shaped structure open at one end, with a wedge-shaped structure that can attach to the holes of the crimp clamp. The prosthesis traverses the crimp clamp and passes through this structure and into the 3DTAD, moving along the groove of the lateral edge to the tensioner assembly. The prosthesis converted at the tensioner assembly is connected to the tensioner.

Design of the 3D-printed tension assistant device. The crimp clamp is connected to a wedge-shaped structure with holes on both sides (black arrowheads). A suture passes through this arrangement via grooves located on either side of the wedge (red line), converging at a tensioner assembly that directly connects to the tensioner (white arrowhead), where it is installed.

Surgical procedure

The suture tensioner used was a commercially available product (AR-1529; Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA) equipped with a tensiometer. The surgical procedure was performed as previously reported (Fig. 3) [8, 26]. Size 0 ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene (UHMWPE; Textile Development Associates, Inc., Brookfield, CT, USA) was selected based on the patient size. The prosthesis formed a loop at the distal pole of the lateral fabella through the femorofabellar ligament. A bone tunnel was created using a 1.1 mm Kirshner wire from the caudoproximal to craniodistal direction through the lateral aspect of the tibia. The tunnel was placed at the intersection of two reference lines: a line drawn parallel to the cranial border of the tibial tuberosity, and a line drawn perpendicular to the course of the long digital extensor tendon, both identified on the lateral surface of the tibia. While this location may not provide perfect isometry throughout the entire range of motion, it was selected to reflect a commonly used clinical position and to ensure consistency across specimens. After passing through the tibial bone tunnel, the prosthesis was routed deep to the patellar ligament before being passed through a crimp clamp, connected to the 3DTAD, and attached to the suture tensioner. The stifle angle was adjusted to correspond with the specified group angle using an electronic goniometer. Cranial tibial translation was manually assessed using a standard cranial drawer test by the operating surgeon, applying moderate force. The amount of translation was evaluated by observing the relative displacement between the tibial tuberosity and the distal femur on the lateral aspect, using both visually and by palpation. Tensioning was gradually applied using the suture tensioner until cranial displacement was estimated to be less than 5 mm. Although <5 mm was used as an upper limit for acceptable stabilization, tensioning was typically continued until no appreciable drawer motion was observed [8, 26]. The applied tension was observed in real-time, and once an appropriate tension was reached, the crimp clamp was secured using a crimper.

Data collection

Pedobarography was performed using a piezoresistive FSR (SFC3400CXR2; Kitronyx Inc., Seoul, South Korea). The FSR was mounted with its sensing surface aligned horizontally and positioned to receive vertical loading from the universal testing machine, and the pressure applied to each pad was graphically displayed using a dedicated software (PetLAB2; Kitronyx Inc.). For clarity, the pads were numbered Pad1 to Pad4 starting from 1st medial digital pad, with the metatarsal pad defined as the central pad. A load was applied, and the total pressure exerted on the five pads was distributed and recorded as a percentage. The rotational angles of the hindlimbs were also recorded. Each specimen was aligned with the FSR, with the rotation angle defined as the angle between the line connecting the centre points of Pad2 and Pad3 parallel to the line from the central pad.

The PF sensor (Prescale; Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan) was used to measure femorotibial contact pressure. A medium-pressure film (sensitivity range: 0.5–2.5 MPa) was selected based on previous study [21]. The colour change intensity indicated the level of pressure, which could be quantitatively analyzed using the associated software. To measure the contact area, the PF was cut into a U-shape to precisely fit the condyles. The PF was configured to consist of two parallel straight sections directly contacting the stifle for pressure measurement, with an intermediate section allowing instrument manipulation without affecting the measured pressure owing to handling. A protective cover of the same U-shape made from polyethylene was fashioned to shield the PF from causing moisture-related damage to the sample. The PF sensor was carefully inserted through a medial parapatellar capsulotomy and positioned directly atop the femorotibial articular surface, ensuring contact with both the femoral condyle and the meniscus. No posterior capsulorrhaphy was performed following PF placement, to avoid altering joint pressure distribution. Due to the flexible nature of the PF, its position was manually stabilized during joint alignment to maintain proper contact throughout load application (Fig. 4). The load was applied as before, ensuring it did not exceed 30% of the cadaver’s weight or the PF pressure range.

Insertion of a pressure-sensitive paper into a stifle joint. Illustration demonstrating the insertion of a pressure-sensitive paper into a stifle joint.

After loading, the PF discoloured by pressing was scanned using a scanner (PERFECTION V600 PHOTO; EPSON, Suwa, Japan), with the resulting images processed using specialised pressure mapping software (FPD-8010E; Fujifilm) to analyze specific pressure areas. The contact area for analysis of the femoral condyle and meniscus was meticulously defined as a measured an Area of 200 mm2. The standardized contact area of 200 mm2 was used for all specimens to ensure consistency in pressure comparison across groups. Although individual variation in body size existed, this fixed area was selected based on preliminary measurements that represented the average contact area observed in the tested population. For clarity, the medial and lateral areas of each stifle joint were directly designated as the medial and lateral compartments and are labeled accordingly in Fig. 5.

Visualization of the pressure distribution after scanning. By scanning the image of the sample, the pressure distribution could be visualised using the linked software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyzes were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.2.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normality was not established for the number of measurements obtained using the suture tensioner. Therefore, the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by the Mann-Whitney test for pairwise comparisons was performed. One-way ANOVA was used to analyze rotation angle datasets, while Two-way ANOVA was applied to examine the correlation effects between groups for variables such as pressure on each pad measured in pedobarography and PF measurement. Post-hoc analyzes using Scheffe’s test were conducted. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

DISCUSSION

Herein, we quantified the tension applied to LFTS techniques in small dogs using surgical instruments designed to deliver the measured tension. The application of consistent tension was assumed based on previous reports [8]. A previous study measured the tension acting on a prosthesis using a load cell; however, the tension values reported were based on subjective criteria for joint stability, which limits their clinical relevance. A more robust approach would involve gradually applying tension while quantitatively assessing joint stability to determine an optimal tension threshold [9]. The tensiometer provides practical tension data that can assist surgeons in standardizing suture tension during procedures. While consistent tension may contribute to procedural reproducibility, other factors such as suture placement and tunnel location are also critical determinants of surgical outcomes.

In this study, tension values measured using a tensiometer showed more standardised results than intraobserver values reported in a previous study [8]. In that study, the investigators assessed joint stability by manipulating the stifle joint through a range of motion without knowledge of the applied suture tension, thereby evaluating drawer movement blindly. In contrast, the present study determined suture tension in real-time using a tensiometer, based on the surgeon’s subjective assessment of cranial drawer (<5 mm), followed by final fixation at the observed tension value. We suggest that the use of the 3DTAD may have contributed to reducing variability in suture tension application by enabling consistent tensioning and measurement; however, its direct impact on standardisation requires further validation. While the suture tension was applied using a mechanical tensioner to enhance consistency, the final fixation point was determined based on the surgeon’s subjective assessment of cranial tibial translation (<5 mm). This introduces a potential source of variability and limits reproducibility. Future studies should aim to establish objective tensioning thresholds by correlating applied suture force with quantifiable measures of joint stability, such as strain gauge readings, biomechanical joint stiffness, or three-dimensional kinematic analysis under simulated loading conditions. While the 3DTAD allows for consistent application and maintenance of suture tension, this study does not define an optimal tension threshold that ensures functional stabilisation. Therefore, rather than promoting the application of a fixed tension value, the 3DTAD is better positioned as a tool to improve further research into the relationship between tension magnitude and surgical outcome. However, it is important to acknowledge that the use of turnbuckles to simulate quadriceps and hamstring muscle tension does not fully replicate in vivo muscle contraction patterns, which are dynamic, coordinated, and modulated through neural input. In the current cadaveric model, the static and simplified force application may have influenced joint kinematics and contact pressure distributions. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the biomechanical fidelity of the results.

Studies addressing the effect of joint angle during tensioning suggest that further discussion is required [9, 26]. Expressions such as ‘slight flexion’ and ‘normal standing angle’ are commonly used to describe joint angles, yet many surgeons prefer a slight extension during surgery. Previous cadaver studies have indicated that uniform tension is achieved at 70–100° of flexion [9, 26]. In this study, tensioning at 105° was resulted in tension values that were lower than those at 135°, but higher than at 90°, without signs of excessive residual drawer or instability. While suture elongation or tibial rotation may contribute to variations in tension, these factors were not directly measured and require further investigation. Therefore, the 105° stifle angle may represent a practical compromise between minimizing drawer and avoiding over-tensioning, although definitive conclusions cannot be drawn without functional outcome data.

Numerous reports have applied pressure mapping systems to objectively assess surgical outcomes, [5, 15, 20, 27] with some reporting the mid-to long-term surgical prognosis of LFTS and TPLO using static pressure distribution and measuring changes in femorotibial contact pressure in surgeries aimed at stabilising CCL-deficient stifles [15, 26, 27]. This study employed pedobarography to assess the effect of tibial rotation on pad pressure under various conditions, with the degree of rotation verified by graphical analysis. In cases of CCLR, which is expected to induce rotation, pressure trends on the toe pads varied, both internally and externally, returning to near-normal post-surgery. Although no statistically significant difference was observed compared to the intact knee, a trend toward increased external rotation at the extension was noted relative to 90° of flexion.

While femorotibial contact pressure has previously been evaluated using pressure-sensitive film in studies by Kim et al., this is the first study to apply PF in the context of evaluating tension-controlled LFTS techniques in small breed dogs. In this study, the total contact force (in newtons, N) recorded in each stifle joint was lowest for CCL group. No significant difference was observed between the intact and L105 group, with the highest contact force was recorded in the L135 group. These findings partially support the hypothesis that appropriate joint angulation—such as 105°—may help restore more physiological weight bearing in stifles with CCL insufficiency. The pressure across the submeniscal area was higher in the medial than in the lateral compartment, regardless of group. Previous studies measuring femorotibial contact pressure post-LFTS reported that pressure in the lateral compartment increased without an axial load, normalising upon applying an axial load [27]. Another study on femorotibial contact pressure post-TTA surgery in a CCL-induced model showed higher peak pressures in the CCL than in the normal and postsurgical stabilisation groups [15]. These findings contrast with the data from this study, likely due to differences in measurement methodologies. Although rotational changes were noted in the CCL group, there were no significant alterations in pressure distribution across individual pads. This suggests that the overall load-bearing axis may have remained stable, potentially due to a restraining effect of the laterally placed suture against internal tibial rotation.

Previous studies have provided pressure values measured in real time using FSR, which cannot account for fluctuations due to joint fixation or tibial subluxation. This study adopted a pressure measurement technique that recorded the loads over time (1 min from load onset). Nevertheless, this experiment, being a multifactorial model setting in the LFTS, was not solely focused on the load between the femur and tibia, potentially distributing the load to the hock joint and pad, which is a limitation. Typically, each hind limb bears approximately 20% of the total body weight. However, precise data on weight distribution at the joint level-such as in the stifle, tarsus, or hip-is limited due to the technical and ethical challenges associated with intra-articular load measurements. Further investigation using advanced pressure mapping or biomechanical modeling is warranted.

In the pressure range analyzes, the CCL group exhibited a greater contact area in the low-pressure ranges (Pr <0.5 and 0.5 <P<1.5) compared to other groups, indicating a broader distribution of lower loads in the absence of surgical stabilization. This trend may reflect insufficient load transfer through the stifle joint due to instability and rotational laxity, as also supported by the increased rotation angle observed in the CCL group. Conversely, the relatively concentrated load distribution in L105 suggests that stabilization at this angle may restore more physiological weight-bearing. Notably, L105 showed no significant difference in pressure distribution area compared to the intact group, except in the excessive pressure range, implying a near-normal load transfer pattern. In contrast, tensioning at 135° resulted in the highest contact pressure, which may contribute to joint overloading. While the 135° stifle angle may approximate the physiologic standing angle in small dogs, it is important to note that, under the current experimental setup, this angle may have imposed excessive compression on intra-articular structures, including the meniscus. The cadaveric model used lacked active musculature and surrounding soft tissues, which normally buffer and distribute joint forces. Consequently, the absence of these structures may have led to unphysiological joint compression during extension, especially under tension, thereby exaggerating contact pressures at 135°. Although direct meniscal damage from the suture is unlikely unless excessive tension is applied, increased pressure at non-isometric angles may indirectly increase the risk of meniscal injury due to altered joint mechanics. The observation that L105 had larger areas under excessive pressure than intact suggests that further research is needed to determine whether 105° is the optimal angle.

This study had several limitations. First, surgery was only performed in cadavers; as with other biomechanical tests, the results may not directly translate to practical settings. Despite minimising tissue damage, cadaver studies cannot perfectly emulate real joint movements, and individual variances must be considered. However, we aimed to analyze multiple factors affecting weight-bearing in a dog’s hind limb. Further, the inability to determine the isometry point during LFTS is a limitation, particularly as Hulse et al. suggested a suture point for successful LFTS [9, 14]. However, the suture material used in their study differed from that of the present study, as they employed polybutylate-coated polyester sutures, whereas we used UHMWPE sutures. Another study demonstrated that UHMWPE sutures were stronger and stiffer than other materials, particularly when secured with a crimp clamp [4]. Excessive tension after securing the loop can lead to high postoperative forces on the joint or early stabilisation failures. Given the stiffness and limited elasticity of UHMWPE, the tibial suture point was selected with reference to previously reported placements [22, 26]. However, it should be noted that ref 26 does not recommend the tibial location used in this study, and that more isometric anchorage points—such as those described in ref 21—are typically positioned more proximally and cranially. Moreover, due to the complex nature of the native CCL’s fiber orientation and motion-dependent length changes, a single prosthetic suture cannot fully replicate true isometry. In addition, although the pressure mapping sensor (PF sensor) used in this study was appropriate for evaluating static load distribution, its low temporal resolution limited the ability to assess dynamic changes during joint motion in real-time. Taken together, these limitations underscore the need for further research to identify the optimal femoral and tibial anchorage sites for UHMWPE-based LFTS techniques, and to enhance dynamic load measurement approaches during joint function.

In conclusion, a stifle angle of 105 degrees for LFTS using UHMWPE and crimp-clamp can produce near-normal pressures for each pad, tibial rotation, patellofemoral contact pressure, and femorotibial contact pressure in small dogs. However, some areas may experience excessive pressure, and abnormally high tibial cranial translation may occur. This study provides quantitative tension values at stifle angles of 90°, 105°, and 135° measured using a tensioner and 3DTAD.
