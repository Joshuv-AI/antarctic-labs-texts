Synthesis and characterization of Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles via pulsed laser ablation in liquids: effects of solvent and laser fluence
Authors: Elder Alejandro Meza Ramírez, Armando Pérez Centeno, E. Campos-González, E. Camps, José Guadalupe Quiñones Galván
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, the synthesis and characterization of iron (III) oxide (Fe₂O₃) nanoparticles using the pulsed laser ablation in liquids (PLAL) technique are reported. A 1064 nm Nd: YAG laser was used to ablate a hematite target immersed in different liquid media (acetone, ethanol, isopropanol, and bi-distilled water) under two distinct laser fluences (0.75 and 1.00 J/cm²). Fluence was varied by fixing spot size and changing the laser energy using the laser Q-switch delays, generating colloid with different stabilities within 4 min. The resulting nanoparticles were characterized by UV-Vis spectroscopy, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), selected area electron diffraction (SAED), and Raman spectroscopy. The UV-Vis spectra confirmed typical Fe₂O₃ absorption bands, with ethanol samples showing the highest absorbance and colloidal stability; all 4 samples presented a bandgap of ~ 2.8 eV. The unique presence of the P0 component at approximately 710.6 eV in XPS high-resolution spectra confirmed the exclusive presence of Fe³⁺ species in all samples. TEM images revealed spherical nanoparticles with diameters around 35 nm across all conditions. At the same time, SAED analyses identified a mixture of α- and γ-Fe₂O₃ phases. Raman spectroscopy confirmed the hematite phase’s predominant presence, showing its characteristic signals near the 220, 298, and 400 cm−1. The results indicate that within the fluence range explored, the choice of liquid medium does not significantly alter the stoichiometry or morphology of the synthesized nanoparticles. These findings support the versatility and scalability of PLAL for producing Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles tailored for various applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20549-x.

Introduction

Interest in nanotechnology and nanostructures has steadily grown in various research areas, including information technology, optics, electronics, magnetics, biomedicine, and materials development. Nanostructures are structures with at least one dimension under 100 nm. Because of their reduced size, they exhibit new and interesting properties compared to their bulk counterparts, such as altered conductivity, melting point, fluorescence, and chemical reactivity1.

Magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles have attracted extensive research attention. Beyond their scientific interest, these nanoparticles offer technological applications in magnetic energy storage, targeted drug delivery, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contrast agents, and magnetic inks2–4. A variety of chemical methods exist for synthesizing iron oxide nanoparticles; however, these techniques often require additional chemical reagents for complex purification steps, making large-scale production inefficient and generating toxic residues.

By contrast, the PLAL technique provides a physical, eco-friendly approach to synthesizing nanostructures. It can produce high-purity nanoparticles and allows for precise control over various parameters, which can be optimized for different applications. For example, Sajti et al. (2010) synthesized ceramic nanostructures ~ 30 nm in size by varying laser fluence, pulse overlap, and the thickness of the liquid layer covering the ablated target5. Lam et al. (2013) fabricated Al₂O₃ nanoparticles with a 355 nm, 5 ns laser6, while Maneeratanasarn et al. (2013) produced iron oxide nanoparticles in different liquid media with sizes ranging from 6 to 12 nm7.

Several studies have investigated the synthesis of iron oxide nanoparticles through laser ablation. Although the literature contains numerous reports on the synthesis of magnetic nanoparticles, iron oxides in particular, the vast array of adjustable variables in pulsed-laser ablation in liquids (PLAL), pulse energy and fluence, pulse duration, repetition rate, focal position, and so forth, makes it difficult to find studies that systematically examine the combined influence of more than one parameter. For example, Amendola et al. (2011)8 compared six different liquids while keeping the fluence fixed at 5 J cm⁻², whereas Kanitz et al. (2017)9 investigated six solvents (water, methanol, ethanol, acetone, and toluene) with femtosecond pulses at a constant fluence 0.2 J cm⁻². In contrast, Svetlichnyi et al. (2017)10 varied the fluence from 0.7 to 7 J cm⁻², and Rivera-Chaverra et al. (2020)11 adjusted it to four values between 4 and 8 J cm⁻² with a 532 nm laser, yet both studies kept the liquid medium fixed (water). Even Zhang et al. (2018)12 performed their syntheses under a single set of parameters.

Although a few studies do operate in the low-fluence regime (< 1 J cm⁻²), the vast majority employ much higher energy densities, and many also rely on long ablation times (> 10 min). For example, Zhang et al. (2018)12 carried out ablation in acetone at 113 J cm⁻² for 60 min; Vahabzadeh and Torkamany (2014)13 used up to 91 J cm⁻² with a 1064 nm laser; and Attallah et al. (2024)14 applied 22–26 J cm⁻², although they shortened the irradiation time to 5 min. Other groups extend the process to several hours: Franzel et al. (2012)15 reported 13 J cm⁻² for 3.5 h, while Svetlichnyi et al. (2017)10 maintained 0.7–1 J cm⁻² for 3 h. These studies underscore the scarcity of systematic investigations that combine low fluence with sub-10-minute ablation, leaving this parameter space essentially unexplored.

Working at low fluence and short ablation times is crucial because extended irradiation and higher fluence raise the nanoparticle concentration in the colloid and trigger laser-induced fragmentation. This secondary process merely breaks the primary particles into smaller ones16,17.

Operating below this fragmentation threshold and keeping the exposure brief isolates the primary ablation mechanism, suppresses secondary fragmentation, and lowers energy consumption and processing time. These are key advantages for any scalable, energy-efficient synthesis.

On the other hand, it has been reported that even minor changes in laser or solvent conditions can steer the reaction away from a single, well-defined phase, turning stoichiometric control of the final Fe-oxide into one of the technique’s central challenges—for example, Svetlichnyi et al.18 and Pandey et al.19 used a Nd: YAG laser at 1064 nm to produce Fe₂O₃ and Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles, though they used different pulse energy, repetition rate, and ablation medium—meanwhile, Maneeratanasarn et al.7 employed a hematite target in organic solvents with a 355 nm laser wavelength, obtaining γ-Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles. Zhang et al. (2018)12 obtained Fe@FeOₓ/α-Fe₂O₃ mixtures, while Amendola et al. (2011)8 reported Fe₃C, Fe₃O₄, and metallic iron phases. Kanitz et al. (2017)9 showed that the dominant phase shifted among Fe⁰, FeO and γ-Fe₂O₃ depending on the liquid medium, and Vahabzadeh & Torkamany (2014)13 detected magnetite coexisting with wüstite. Rivera-Chaverra et al. (2020)11, in a study oriented toward biomedical applications, observed agglomerated magnetite–maghemite-hematite structures.

These studies demonstrate that factors such as fluence, solvent type, and precursor material significantly affect the synthesized samples, which often yield multiphase products (hematite or maghemite) or iron compounds containing multiple valence states (Fe⁰, Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺).

In summary, despite the numerous PLAL-based syntheses of iron-oxide nanoparticles reported in the literature, most studies vary only one synthesis parameter at a time and typically rely on high laser fluences and prolonged ablation periods. Consequently, the combined influence of multiple parameters in the low-fluence regime on phase purity and the preservation of Fe³⁺ stoichiometry remains unexplored.

In the present work, we address this gap by simultaneously varying the liquid medium and the fluence (< 1 J cm⁻²), aiming to obtain iron-oxide nanoparticles predominantly in the α-Fe₂O₃ phase through a short synthesis process. This multi-parameter, low-energy approach provides a clear framework for controlling the synthesis of iron-oxide nanoparticles while maintaining the desired Fe³⁺ oxidation state.

Methodology

Samples of iron (III) oxide were synthesized by ablating a 99.9% purity Fe2O3 target (Kurt J. Lesker), with a pulsed Nd: YAG laser, model Q-Smart 850, emitting at 1064 nm with a pulse duration of 6 ns and a repetition rate of 10 Hz, for this experiment no focusing lens has been used to focus the laser over the target to improve the synthesis yield. Using the experimental setup shown in Fig. 1, the target was immersed inside the liquid medium, and the latter’s height over the target was 33 mm. Four samples were synthesized by employing four different liquid media—acetone, isopropanol, ethanol, and bi-distilled water, all of reagent grade—and two distinct laser intensities, 475 and 640 mJ, to evaluate the effect of these parameters on nanoparticle formation.

Laser intensity was controlled using the Q-switch delay mechanism of the laser, fixing values of 640 mJ (1 J/cm2) and 475 mJ (0.75 J/cm2). Table 1 presents experimental parameters for the synthesized samples. It is essential to mention that the beam was not focused on the target. Previous studies from our group have found that using unfocused beams to obtain low fluences allows the synthesis of small nanoparticles with high concentration at shorter times.

We also synthesized the samples labeled Me2CO-640 mJ, IPA-640 mJ, H₂O-475 mJ, and EtOH-475 mJ for this study. However, because the results obtained for these four samples do not differ substantially and show no qualitatively distinct trends from those already presented in the main body of the manuscript, we chose to include only the four samples that best illustrate the key contrasts discussed in the article. This decision preserves the clarity and conciseness of the text without compromising experimental rigor. The complete characterization of the remaining samples is provided in the Supplementary Information, which is available for detailed consultation.

UV-Vis characterization

The samples’ absorption spectra were measured immediately after the synthesis. For this purpose, aliquots were analyzed using a Thermo Fisher Scientific equipment model Genesys 10. 4 mL of the sample was taken and characterized using a quartz cell, performing a scan from 250 to 800 nm with a 2 nm step size.

TEM characterization

The samples’ morphological characterization was performed using the TEM technique. The liquid samples were deposited onto carbon-coated copper grids. A Jeol JEM 2010 microscope was used for imaging at a voltage of 90 kV. The obtained micrographs revealed the synthesized nanoparticles’ spherical morphology and nanometric dimensions.

XPS characterization

XPS characterization was carried out to verify the synthesized samples’ stoichiometry. The samples were deposited onto silicon wafers and analyzed using a SPECS spectrometer equipped with a PHOIBOS 150 analyzer and an Al-kα X-ray source (1486.7 eV). A complete survey scan ranging from 1300 eV to 0 eV in binding energy was performed for each sample to identify the elements present. The energy step was set to 60 eV, and two scans were obtained per spectrum. Afterwards, high-resolution spectra were obtained for the main orbitals of the samples, specifically Fe 2p, O 1 s, and C 1s.

HR-TEM and SAED

The sample’s crystallographic characterization was performed using high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM), SAED, which identified the material’s crystalline structure. A Jeol JEM 2010 microscope with a lanthanum hexaboride filament at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV and image processing software was used for this characterization.

Raman spectroscopy

The samples were characterized by Raman spectroscopy to complement the crystallographic study carried out by SAED. For this purpose, they were analyzed using a micro-Raman instrument, a Horiba Jobin Yvon HR 800 Raman spectrometer with the 532 nm excitation line. The beam was focused using a 50X objective lens, performing a scan from 200 to 500 cm-¹.

Results and discussion

After synthesizing the samples, the ablated mass was estimated by pipetting a known volume onto a glass slide and weighing the deposited material. The concentrations obtained were 58 µg mL⁻¹ for EtOH-640 mJ, 50 µg mL⁻¹ for both H₂O-640 mJ and IPA-475 mJ, and 46 µg mL⁻¹ for Me₂CO-475 mJ.

UV-vis results

The typical absorption bands of iron oxide were observed, which show absorption in the ultraviolet region and a decay at longer wavelengths. No significant changes beyond intensity variations were observed among the different samples. Having similar absorption bands and edges means no additional energy levels are induced because of the chosen liquid variations. It has been reported that the liquid environment in PLAL experiments can generate energy states that modify absorption properties due to surface states, defects, and functional groups20–22. Additionally, the water, ethanol, and isopropanol samples exhibited a higher nanoparticle yield and colloidal stability, as seen by the increase in intensity and color perseverance in the colloids. These results agree with the concentration measurements: EtOH‑640 mJ shows the highest nanoparticle concentration, while Me2CO‑475 mJ has the lowest. The slight mismatch between the intensities of H₂O‑640 mJ and IPA‑475 mJ and their respective concentrations can be attributed to the rapid agglomeration and sedimentation observed in the water‑based colloid. Figure 2 presents the UV-Vis spectra of the synthesized samples, which exhibit similar absorption characteristics across all samples. The supplementary section provides the spectra of the other four synthesized samples (Fig. S1). The sample synthesized in ethanol shows the highest intensity, potentially reflecting more efficient material synthesis. However, another factor influencing this result is the stability of the nanoparticles in suspension. In the sample synthesized in water, the material began agglomerating and precipitating within minutes of synthesis. All samples exhibit strong absorption in the UV range, primarily driven by ligand-to-metal charge transfer transitions, with partial contributions from Fe³⁺ ligand-field transitions ⁶A₁ → ⁴T₁(⁴P) (290–310 nm) and ⁶A₁ → ⁴T₂(⁴D) (360–380 nm). As the wavelength increases, the absorption intensity diminishes, a characteristic feature of this material, resulting in lower intensities in regions 2, 3, and 4 compared to region 1. This behavior aligns with selection rules, where charge-transfer and electron-pair excitations generate stronger absorption than ligand-field transitions23,24.

Characterizing iron (III) oxide using UV-vis absorption spectroscopy often yields diverse results in different studies. According to Mizuno et al.25, which investigated the electronic transitions of hematite nanostructures with various sizes and morphologies, this material displays two well-defined bands: one between 380 and 410 nm, and another between 520 and 540 nm. In contrast, Ling et al.26 reported an absorption band for hematite nanostructures ranging from 380 to 450 nm. The shift of this band, as noted by Ling et al.26, can be attributed to finite-size effects. Furthermore, ultrafast studies of photoexcited electron dynamics in hematite and maghemite nanoparticles, conducted by Cherepy et al.27, indicate that these materials exhibit a broad absorption in the visible range extending into the ultraviolet, as well as distinct bands at approximately 200, 230, 285, and 340 nm. In another study, Y. P. He et al.24 explored the influence of size and structure on the optical transitions of hematite and maghemite and presented their absorption spectra, which showed behavior similar to those of earlier studies.

Overall, the absorption spectra of these materials encompass four regions. The first region (250–400 nm) primarily arises from charge transfer transitions between ligands and metal atoms, with additional contributions from Fe³⁺ ion transitions induced by the ligand field. The second region (400–600 nm) is associated with electron-pair excitation processes. The third (600–750 nm) and fourth (750–900 nm) regions can be attributed to all three previously mentioned types of transitions. Notably, the intensity in the first two regions is significantly higher compared to the latter regions, owing to the strong absorption caused by charge-transfer transitions and electron-pair processes—an effect governed by selection rules—relative to the weaker ligand-field transitions. The absorption profiles indicate that the synthesized nanoparticles are composed exclusively of iron (III) oxide, with no evidence of other iron oxides or species in the samples. This interpretation is corroborated by subsequent XPS, SAED, and Raman analyses.

Together with the UV–Vis absorption measurements, the optical band gap of each colloid was evaluated.

Reported values for hematite range from 2.3 eV to 2.8 eV28–32. The band gap was extracted from Tauc plots by plotting (αhν)² as a function of photon energy (hν) and extrapolating the linear region. All synthesized samples exhibited an optical band gap of ~ 2.8 eV, as shown in Fig. 3, consistent with the expected value for α-Fe2O3.

XPS results

The identified elements and their corresponding sub-orbitals for the synthesized samples were C1s, O1s, Fe2p, and Si2p. The presence of Si was anticipated because the sample was mounted on a silicon wafer. Carbon is generally present, sometimes as a contaminant, while Fe and O were the elements of primary interest for our samples. Fig. 4 shows a representative survey spectrum, showing the elements present in all samples. The carbon (~ 285 eV) and oxygen (~ 530 eV) signals are noticeably stronger than the iron signal (~ 710 eV). This could be attributed to the nanoparticle synthesis’s low yield, XPS’s surface sensitivity, and the deposited particles’ limited homogeneity.

The C1s (red) spectra shown in Fig. 5 displayed a similar profile across all samples, while the O1s (blue) spectra were likewise quite similar among the four. The additional high-resolution XPS spectra are provided in Fig. S2. Unlike in the other three samples, the most significant discrepancy occurred in the Me2CO-475 mJ sample, where the O–Fe signal could not be identified. This difference is also reflected in the Fe2p (green) orbital data, which exhibits similar behavior overall, except that the sample synthesized in acetone showed only a minimal signal, possibly due to the small quantity of material produced under those synthesis conditions. The last result agrees with the UV-vis analysis, where the absorption spectrum was the lowest intensity for this sample, confirming the low ablation yield obtained under these synthesis conditions.

Analysis of the carbon spectrum is standard in virtually every XPS study, mainly because its well-known peak at ~ 285 eV rarely overlaps with other signals. Moreover, the adventitious carbon (C–C) signal is commonly used to calibrate the binding energy of a sample and compensate for shifts arising from surface charging33,34. After fitting the carbon spectra, the primary C–C component (adventitious carbon) was adjusted to its commonly reported binding energy range of 284.8–285 eV, ensuring compensation for any charging effects in the samples. Once the binding energy shifts were determined for each sample, the O1s and Fe2p spectra were analyzed accordingly. In the O1s spectra, three components were fitted in each sample using Voigt functions, consistent with the approach used for carbon.

Fitting the Fe2p spectrum required additional considerations. Accurate analysis of Fe2p remains challenging because its first successful fitting procedure was published only in 2017. This difficulty arises from a dense set of overlapping components and significant background intensity, which together reflect the multiplet structure of the material subject that remains only partially explored in theoretical studies35.

Figure 6 depicts one of the fits performed for the Fe2p orbital. According to several references, the energy difference for iron (III) oxide is 13.6 eV, and the ratio of the Fe2p doublet components is 0.51735,36. These parameters were applied to achieve a reliable fit. To model the Fe2p spectra of all samples, double Lorentzian functions were used to accommodate the main peak’s asymmetry, and the area of each primary component was correlated with its doublet counterpart using the 0.517 ratio. A slope background and an SVSC (Shirley–Vegh–Salvi–Castle) background were also employed.

The resulting fits reveal that Fe2p exhibits a single primary component, P0, while the other peaks correspond to satellites. This main component lies between approximately 710 and 710.6 eV and is typically associated with Fe³⁺37,38, which is the oxidation state in iron (III) oxide. This finding is critical for identifying the compound likely formed during synthesis, implying that iron exists solely in the + 3 oxidation state in these samples. These findings agree with the UV-Vis results (Fig. 2), confirming that the synthesized nanoparticles consist solely of iron (III) oxides and ruling out the formation of other iron species during ablation. However, these techniques alone do not unambiguously establish the crystalline phase: although UV-Vis can, in principle, yield phase information, the absorption-band profiles of the two most common iron (III) oxide polymorphs, hematite and maghemite, are similar.

SAED Results

To confirm the crystalline structure of the material (α-Fe₂O₃), a Selected Area Electron Diffraction (SAED) analysis was performed. Figure 7 shows the diffraction patterns obtained for the four synthesized samples. Additional SAED patterns are presented in Fig. S3.

SAED diffraction patterns of the synthesized samples (a) EtOH-640 mJ, (b) H2O-640 mJ, (c) IPA-475 mJ and (d) Me2CO-475 mJ. Rings indexed to α‑Fe₂O₃ planes (104, 113, 024, 116, 300) dominate, while weaker γ‑Fe₂O₃ rings (111, 220, 311, 511) reveal a minor maghemite fraction

The particles exhibited ring-shaped diffraction patterns, confirming their polycrystalline nature. Rings corresponding to the (104), (113), (024), (116), and (300) planes of hematite were identified, in agreement with JCDPS Card No. 33–0664 for α-Fe₂O₃. This finding confirms the preservation of the hematite phase during the target ablation process, results like those reported in previous studies39.

However, rings corresponding to the (111), (220), (311), and (511) planes of the γ-Fe₂O₃ (maghemite) phase were also observed, matching JCDPS Card No. 39-1346. The presence of maghemite resulting from the ablation of an α-Fe₂O₃ target has been attributed to superheating, which drives homogeneous nucleation and explosive phase transformation. Under these conditions, iron reacts with other available oxygen species, leading to the phase transition7,40. Furthermore, the conversion of maghemite to hematite is commonly reported to occur through thermal processes or treatments41,42. Hence, the phase mixture detected in the samples may be attributed to the elevated temperatures reached during the ablation process.

However, the two phases detected are consistent with the earlier XPS and UV-vis findings. The diffraction patterns correspond exclusively to iron (III) oxide crystal structures, corroborating that the PLAL process preserves the Fe³⁺ stoichiometry of the target material.

Raman spectroscopy

Electron diffraction confirmed the crystalline structure of the synthesized samples, showing the presence of iron (III) oxide in its alpha and gamma phases. Raman spectroscopy was carried out to determine the predominant phase. Figure 8 presents a representative spectrum, in which three characteristic peaks of α-Fe₂O₃ can be identified near 220, 298, and 400 cm⁻¹, corresponding to the A1g, Eg, and Eg transverse modes. The first two peaks can be associated with the motion of iron and oxygen atoms (Fe–O)43,44. These results coincide with XPS, UV-vis, and SAED results and confirm that hematite is the primary phase present in the samples. Raman spectra of all samples are available in Fig. S4.

TEM results

TEM characterization allowed us to confirm the morphology of the synthesized samples. Figure 9 shows the micrographs obtained for each sample. Additional micrographs can be consulted in Fig. S5. It can be observed that morphology remains unchanged under the different synthesis parameters employed in this work, resulting in spherical nanoparticles smaller than 100 nm. Typically, the particle size obtained through laser ablation in liquids depends on several synthesis variables (laser fluence, repetition rate, liquid medium, wavelength, etc.)45–47.

In this study, the chosen parameter values led to highly similar particles across all samples, an unexpected result, as various studies have shown the sensitivity of this technique to changes in synthesis parameters7,18,19.

Bright field TEM micrographs of Fe2O3 nanoparticles synthesized in the four solvents. All samples presented quasi spherical morphologies; alcohol-based media presented a bimodal distribution suggestive of two different ablation mechanisms: thermal evaporation and phase-explosion

Figure 10 presents the statistical analysis of micrographs. The additional statistical analysis is compiled in Fig. S6. The results illustrate the impact of distinct synthesis conditions on the particle size distribution. All four samples exhibited a similar average particle size, ranging from approximately 30 to 40 nm. However, the samples prepared in alcohol appear to have a bimodal size distribution, most notably in sample IPA-475 mJ. This observation suggests the presence of more than one nanoparticle formation mechanism. In laser ablation in liquids using a 1064 nm wavelength, thermal vaporization and explosive boiling (or phase explosion) occur, often simultaneously under certain conditions. Thermal vaporization typically leads to the formation of smaller nanoparticles via nucleation and condensation, while explosive boiling can produce larger particles due to the violent ejection of molten material48. Previous studies have.

reported bimodal distributions in similar systems, supporting the hypothesis that the observed particle populations may originate from the coexistence of these two mechanisms49,50.

In general, the standard deviation of the particle sizes is relatively large compared to the mean particle size, which has previously been associated with the wavelength of the laser: larger wavelengths tend to produce broader size distributions51.

Conclusions

Overall, our low-fluence (≤ 1 J cm⁻²) and small ablation time (< 10 min) PLAL approach in four distinct liquid media yields Fe₂O₃ nanocolloids in the α- crystalline phase. The synthesized samples preserved the stoichiometry of the target material, yielding only iron oxides with a + 3 oxidation state, even when liquid media and fluence were varied simultaneously. Moreover, SAED and Raman analyses indicated a mixture of α- and γ- phases, with α-Fe2O3 being the predominant phase. TEM images confirmed the spherical morphology of the structures, with an average diameter of approximately 35 nm. TEM analysis revealed that the liquid medium strongly influences the particle-size distribution. A clear bimodal distribution emerged in the alcohol-based samples, suggesting the simultaneous action of two ablation mechanisms, explosive boiling and vaporization. Operating under this dual parameter window in PLAL produces small, spherical, phase-robust nanoparticles, and the liquid medium appears to have no significant effect on the stoichiometry or the crystalline phase of the synthesized material. Within this fluence range, the solvent does not hinder the formation of spherical particles below 100 nm, a result that contrasts with earlier high fluence or single parameter PLAL studies, which often report mixed Fe0/Fe2+/Fe3+ valence states under high fluence and ample synthesis time. Consequently, the present low-energy protocol offers a fast, green, and versatile route for tailoring Fe2O3 nanocolloid in various organic solvents to suit specific applications.
