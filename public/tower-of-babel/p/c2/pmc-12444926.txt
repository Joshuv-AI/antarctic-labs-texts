Stepping together for children after trauma: protocol for a randomized controlled trial of a parent-led treatment in first-line services (NorStep Study)
Authors: Silje Mørup Ormhaug, Tine K Jensen, Kate Louise Porcheret, Anne Lie Andreassen, Sarah Byford, Else Merete Kvalsvik Fagermoen, Pernille Gurandsrud, Kristin Johanne Økern Haabrekke, Marie Lindebø Knutsen, Heidi Maria Päivärinne, Ingeborg Skjærvø
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Childhood trauma is a public health challenge, and there is currently a service/needs gap. Low-intensive treatments where the task of leading the treatment is partially shifted to a caregiver can help bridge this gap by freeing therapist resources. Stepping Together for Children after Trauma (ST-CT), is a novel parent-led, therapist-assisted treatment with promising results in the U.S. Also, it has shown to be feasible as a first-line intervention in Norwegian municipal services and ST-CT is well accepted by children, caregivers, and therapists.Objective: This study is the first randomized controlled trial of ST-CT by independent researchers. The study consists of three work-packages: WP1: Investigates (a) the effectiveness of ST-CT compared to therapy as usual (TAU) in reducing post-traumatic stress symptoms (PTSS), related mental health symptoms, sleep problems and increasing daily functioning; (b) the cost-effectiveness of ST-CT compared to TAU in terms of quality adjusted life years; (c) future health care utilization. WP2: Looks at change processes in ST-CT, with changes in sleep, post-traumatic cognitions and child–parent relationship as potential mediators of reductions in child PTSS. WP3: Investigates keys to implementation into regular first-line mental health services.Methods: The study has a type 1 hybrid effectiveness-implementation design. Children between 7-12 years of age with symptoms of PTSS and their caregivers are randomized to either ST-CT or TAU and participants are assessed at five time points during the first year. Sleep quality is measured with a non-contact radar 1 week pre- and post-treatment and registry data of use of prescribed medications and mental health service utilization will be collected 3 years post-inclusion. The study aims to include 160 child–parent dyads from 30 municipalities.Conclusion: Results can help gain a better understanding of the effectiveness and change processes of ST-CT and may provide important knowledge for a future dissemination and implementation.Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05734547.KEYWORDS: Parent-led treatment, child trauma, post-traumatic stress symptoms, low-intensive treatment, first-line services

Introduction

Childhood trauma is a major public health challenge (Bellis et al., 2019; Sara & Lappin, 2017), and if left untreated, exposure to severe trauma increases the risk for all mental disorders, sleep disturbances, physical illness, self-harm and suicide, as well as externalizing behaviours, and school drop-out (Alisic et al., 2014; Bellis et al., 2019). Also, childhood trauma affects the parents and broader caregiving system and many caregivers struggle with feelings of guilt, shame, and distress (Holt et al., 2014), maladaptive post-traumatic cognitions related to their child’s trauma (Hiller et al., 2018), and symptoms of post-traumatic stress and other mental health problems (Hiller et al., 2016). Although effective psychosocial treatments have been developed and found to reduce stress and symptoms in both children and caregivers (Bisson & Olff, 2021), there is currently a gap between the need for, and access to evidence based treatments (Schweer-Collins & Lanier, 2021). Also, reports have shown there will be a greater need in the future to find effective treatments that can reduce the need for therapist resources (Bovim et al., 2023). To increase access to evidence-based care for traumatized children there is a need for interventions that are more accessible, efficient, and cost-effective.

Parent-led interventions represent a novel opportunity of delivering treatment where the task of leading the therapy has been partially shifted from the therapist to a caregiver. This mode of treatment has several advantages including freeing therapist resources so the therapist can spend more time with families who need more therapist support (Salloum et al., 2022), increasing caregiver’s skills and agency (Fagermoen et al., 2023), and improving the child–parent relationship (Haabrekke et al., Manuscript in review for publication; Næss et al., 2024). For traumatized children, the treatment model Stepped Care CBT for Children after Trauma (Salloum et al., 2014) is an innovative stepped care model where Step One, Stepping Together for Children after Trauma (ST-CT) is a parent-led treatment. For those who need additional support, Step Two consists of therapist-led trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy (TF-CBT) (Cohen et al., 2017). This stepped care model has shown promising results both in terms of effectiveness and cost-effectiveness (Salloum et al., 2016; Salloum et al., 2017; Salloum et al., 2022), but the model has yet to gain status as an evidence-based treatment (Bisson et al., 2019). In Norway, the parent-led Step One, ST-CT, was investigated in a pilot study as a stand-alone treatment, and results show that the model is feasible as a low threshold intervention in first-line municipal services (Ormhaug et al., 2024). However, no studies have investigated the effectiveness of the parent-led Step One compared to treatment as usual (TAU), and there are currently no effectiveness studies conducted by an independent research group. Further, there is a knowledge gap regarding the effect on secondary outcomes such as sleep disorders, caregiver’s symptoms, and the child–parent relationship (Salloum et al., 2022). Therefore, the first aim of the NorStep Study is to investigate the effectiveness of ST-CT as a stand-alone treatment (WP1). This includes assessing changes in child symptoms of posttraumatic stress (PTSS), depression, sleep disturbances, school-related functioning, social adjustment, and perceived relationship to their caregivers. Also, caregivers’ symptoms of PTSS, anxiety, depression, distress related to their child’s trauma, and caregiver’s posttraumatic cognitions will be assessed, and teacher reports will be included. Further, the cost-effectiveness of SC-CT compared to the usual care will be investigated, and the potential preventive effects of SC-CT will be assessed by comparing use of health and social services and medication up to 3 years after inclusion to the study.

The second aim of the NorStep Study is to learn more about the change processes in the treatment (WP2) as this knowledge is currently scarce (Salloum et al., 2023). A better understanding of when and how change occurs during the treatment can improve the overall quality and reach of the model by enhancing the effectiveness of training and implementation as well as identifying ways in which therapists may be able to tailor the treatment to individual families. First, the study will investigate the trajectory of PTS symptoms during the treatment. This will be a first step to understand the link between the specific treatment components and improvements in the child’s symptoms. Further, the study will focus on four modifiable evidence-based factors with hypothesized mechanistic underpinnings for the development of posttraumatic symptomatology. First, sleep disturbances, which are commonly found in trauma-exposed child and adolescent populations (Giannakopoulos & Kolaitis, 2021), are known to impair adequate emotional processing and learning. Such impairment is again hypothesized to be a causal mechanism for the development of many mental health problems including PTSD (Van Someren, 2021), as well as having the potential to influence the effectiveness of treatment (Brownlow et al., 2016). Secondly, studies on change processes in trauma treatment, have shown that reductions in child maladaptive post-traumatic cognitions influence reductions in child PTS (Ehlers & Clark, 2000; Jensen et al., 2018; Lindebø Knutsen et al., 2020). Although this was not found in a study of ST-CT, this non-finding warrants replication (Salloum et al., 2023). Third, changes in caregivers’ responses, in terms of their emotional reactions and caregivers’ post-traumatic cognitions have been found to affect child PTS and depressive symptoms (Cinamon et al., 2021). Fourth, a negative child–parent relationship has been found related to child PTSD (Fitzgerald et al., 2020) but little is known of how a potential change in the child’s perception of the relationship will affect child PTS during treatment.

The third aim of the NorStep Study is to investigate keys for implementation of ST-CT in first-line municipal services (WP3). Introducing new interventions is inefficient if they are not accompanied by a set of documented implementation strategies and the lack of implementation of evidence-based practices is the largest challenge for mental health services today (Williams & Beidas, 2019). Building on the results from the pilot study and other implementation studies (Skar, Braathu, Jensen, et al., 2022; Skar, Braathu, Peters, et al., 2022), successful implementation is defined as (1) the municipalities’ ability to identify children in need of trauma-focused treatment, (2) provide the ST-CT model with fidelity, and (3) gain sustainability by maintaining competency over time. Studies on implementation of regular trauma-focused treatments have identified lack of systematic screening for trauma, low organizational support, staff turnover and professional burnout, and lack of implementation leadership as key barriers to implementation in second-line mental health services (Aminihajibashi et al., 2022; Skar, Braathu, Jensen, et al., 2022). However less is known about the challenges in the first-line services.

In summary, the parent-led treatment ST-CT shows promise as an effective intervention for traumatized children and their non-perpetrator caregiver, but more knowledge of the model’s effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, change processes and effective strategies for implementation is needed before the model can reach the goal of increasing access to care.

Methods

Study design

The study has a hybrid effectiveness-implementation design type 1 (Curran et al., 2012) and participants will be randomized to receive either ST-CT or TAU. The implementation strategy will cover the main implementation drivers as identified by Fixsen and colleagues (Fixsen et al., 2018). The first is the organization drivers, where we will encourage organizational changes by providing training and introducing systematic screening for trauma to increase identification of children. Second, the competency drivers will be addressed with a model for training and supervision of therapists that has been found effective in previous studies (Skar, Braathu, Jensen, et al., 2022) to ensure fidelity. This is again expected to increase sustainability of competency since learning a new model that provides effective treatment has been found to reduce burnout and turnover intention in therapists (Aminihajibashi et al., 2022). Third, the leadership drivers will be included with regular dialogue meetings with leaders to enhance implementation leadership and the support of therapists in training.

Research setting

The study will be conducted in regular first-line mental health services in Norway. These services are located at the municipality level and aim to provide low-threshold and early help for children and adolescents. The treatment is free of costs and caregivers and children can contact the services directly without any referral from a second party.

Participants

Participants will be recruited from families that normally contact the municipal team for help. If a child reports exposure to at least one potentially traumatizing event (e.g. domestic violence, severe accident, sexual abuse, community violence), the caregivers will be informed about the ongoing study and, if they agree, the child will be screened for eligibility by an independent assessor from the NorStep Study. All screenings will be conducted over video calls with end-to-end encryption, and both the child and caregivers will be consulted before inclusion.

Child inclusion criteria: Aged 7–12 years; Exposed to a potentially traumatizing event according to the DSM-5 A-criterion (American Psychiatric Association & Association, 2013); At least 5 symptoms of post-traumatic stress (1 symptom must be re-experiencing or avoidance); ≥ 3 years of age at the time of the traumatic event to ensure an explicit memory of the event; ≥ 1 month since the traumatic event, according to the diagnostic criteria for PTSD (American Psychiatric Association & Association, 2013); The child must confirm in a conversation alone with the assessor that they feel safe at home and that they are not exposed to ongoing trauma.

Child exclusion criteria: Suspicions of psychosis, active suicidality, or serious intellectual disability; A psychotropic medication regime that has not been stable for at least 4 weeks (2 weeks for stimulants/benzodiazepines); Currently receives other trauma treatment.

Caregiver/family exclusion criteria: A caregiver that was the perpetrator of the trauma; If a perpetrator still lives in the same house as the child during treatment; Caregiver with a substance use disorder within the past 3 months; Suspected suicidality; Insufficient Norwegian language skills to complete the treatment without use of an interpreter.

Therapist inclusion criteria: Trained clinical psychologist or psychiatrist, or work in a team with a clinical psychologist/psychiatrist with diagnostic competence; Have a clinical specialization; Experience with clinical work with children and caregivers.

Interventions

Stepping Together CT: Consists of five main components: psychoeducation, stabilization skills, trauma narrative, in-vivo exposures, and consolidation. The caregiver and child work together to complete tasks in the workbook, Stepping Together (Salloum et al., 2010) which is based on the therapist-led Preschool PTSD Treatment (Scheeringa, 2016). The model consists of 11 parent–child at-home meetings, and three to five therapist-led sessions at the therapists’ office. In between these sessions, the therapist schedules short, weekly consultation calls (10–15 min) with the caregiver to provide guidance as they work through the at-home meetings with the child. After completion of the workbook (normally 6–9 weeks), the child’s symptoms are assessed (T2). For children who meet responder-criteria (see below) the family proceeds to a 6-week maintenance phase. After the maintenance phase they complete another assessment (T3) and if the child still meets the responder-criteria at T3, the treatment is considered complete. For those who do not meet responder-criteria, stepping up will imply that the responsibility for the treatment is transferred from the municipal service level to the child and adolescent mental health service (CAMHS) at the second line service (Step 2).

Responder-criteria: The child reports 4 traumatic stress symptoms or less, and the therapist rates the child’s clinical global impressions improvement (CGI-I) as a 3 (improved) or better. These responder criteria must be met both after the 6–9 weeks of at-home treatment (T2), and after the 6-week maintenance phase (T3).

Training: Therapists will receive 1 d training in trauma theory and assessments, and 3 half-day workshops with the method’s developer, Prof. Salloum, each day followed by a half-day workshop with the Norwegian supervisors. In addition, therapists will participate in at least 12 monthly consultation calls with Prof. Salloum. Also, weekly calls with the Norwegian supervisors will be provided, and supervision will be based on audio recordings.

Treatment fidelity: Trained supervisors from the NorStep Study project group will assess audio recordings of the ST-CT treatment sessions according to a 22-item fidelity check-list. All sessions in the first two cases will be assessed, and then one randomized session from each case.

Treatment as Usual (TAU): As there is currently no standard care or universal treatments in municipal health services, usual care is defined as any intervention provided in the first line services. This may include school consultations, parent supervision, individual treatment, and/or referral to CAMHS, and will be decided based on clinical judgement of the child’s need and the family’s preferences.

Training: Therapists in the usual care conditions will receive a half-day training in screening for trauma exposure and PTS symptoms.

Treatment content: Therapists will log the type of intervention provided, how many sessions the families have received, whether any specific methods or manuals were used, and if the intervention was trauma-focused.

Participant timeline

After screening and inclusion to the study, a baseline assessment (T1) will be conducted. This includes one week of measuring the child’s sleep with an objective sensor (Somnofy). The child and participating caregiver will be assessed again after the completion of the components in the parent-led ST-CT treatment/9 weeks for TAU (T2), and after the maintenance phase/15 weeks (T3). At T3, sleep will be assessed again for one week with the sensor. Follow-up assessments will be conducted after 6- and 12- months post baseline (T4 and T5). In addition, the children and caregivers in the parent-led treatment will be assessed by a brief measure before each of the 11 at-home meetings and during the 5 in-office meetings with the therapist (3 treatment sessions, and 2 short follow-up sessions, see below) (Figure 1).

Measurement

Primary outcome variable

The main study outcome is the child’s posttraumatic stress symptoms, assessed with the The Child and Adolescent Trauma Screen-2 (CATS-2) child and caregiver version (C. Sachser et al., 2022). CATS-2 assesses life-time exposure to trauma (15 items), symptoms of PTSD and complex PTSD (20 items, last 4 weeks), as defined by both DSM5 and ICD-11, and interference on psychosocial functioning (5 items).

Secondary outcome variables

Child sleep

An objective measure of the children’s sleep timings and potential disturbances will be obtained with the use of a non-contact sensor (Somnofy sensor, Vital Things). The sensor monitors movements and breathing allowing sleep timings and sleep stages to be determined for 7 consecutive days, at baseline (T1) and post-treatment (T3). The quality of measures from the Somnofy sensor has been validated against the use of polysomnography with good results (Toften et al., 2020). The caregiver will also complete a sleep diary related to their child’s sleep for the same 7 days and the validated, 26-item Sleep Disturbance Scale for Children (SDSC) at T1-T5 to assess development in their child’s sleep and sleep related impairments over time (Bonuck et al., 2017).

Economic measures

The Child Health Utility 9-dimension measure (CHU9D; Stevens, 2012) will be used to measure health-related quality of life of participating children. The CHU9D is a paediatric preference-based measure which can be used to generate Quality Adjusted Life Years (QALYs) suitable for economic evaluation. The CHU9D is designed for self-report by children aged 7–17 and consists of 9 items (worry, sadness, pain, tiredness, annoyance, school, sleep, daily routine and activities), each with a 5-level response category.

Use of services, to support costing for the economic evaluation, will be measured using the Child and Adolescent Service Use Schedule (CA-SUS), adapted for the Norwegian context, and taking a health and social care perspective (primary economic perspective). A broader perspective will additionally include parental productivity losses (days of absence from work due to child’s health; secondary economic perspective). The CA-SUS was designed for application to populations of young people with mental health problems, including childhood trauma (Allen et al., 2021; Shearer et al., 2018). The ST-CT CA-SUS assesses the type and quantity of health and social care services received by participants, including child protective services, specialist child and adolescent psychiatry services (BUP), prescribed medications for mental health conditions, educational support, and child’s accommodation, plus caregiver support.

Implementation measures

For WP3, Early identification of trauma-exposed children will be assessed by reports from employees in collaborative services (school nurses, child protective services, educational therapists, etc.) regarding their self-perceived competence (the Assessment Knowledge scale from the Broker Trauma-Related Knowledge scale) (Are et al., 2022) and actual screening for trauma exposure before training, and then every 4th month for a year (4 assessment points). Implementation leadership: Leader support will be evaluated with the Implementation Leadership Scale (ILS; Aarons et al., 2014) that measures proactive, knowledgeable, supportive and preservative leadership, as assessed by the therapists. Turnover intention will be assessed with the validated Turnover Intention Scale (TIS-6; Bothma & Roodt, 2013). The Professional Quality of Life Scale (ProQOL; Stamm, 2010) assesses therapists’ work-related quality of life and includes both negative (compassion fatigue) and positive (compassion satisfaction) aspects of therapeutic work. All therapist measures will be administered before training, and then every 4th month for a year (4 assessments). Qualitative interviews with therapists and leaders about perceived barriers and facilitators for the implementation of ST-CT will be conducted by the end of the project period.

Randomization and blinding

We use a block-randomization design (Efird, 2011) and randomization to ST-CT or TAU occurs within blocks of 4 or 6 children within each municipality (whether the block size is 4 or 6 varies randomly to avoid predictable patterns). The randomization list was generated through www.sealedenvelope.com (Ltd, 2022) and the randomization seed determined by the software ensures the randomization procedure can be recreated and verified if necessary. To avoid contamination within families, siblings will be randomized to the same condition.

Power-analysis

Sample size is calculated based on ISTSS’ definition of a clinically important difference. For attention control comparisons a standardized mean difference (SMD) for continuous outcomes should be >0.5 (Bisson et al., 2019). With 0.80 power and a significance level of 0.05, calculations show that we will need 64 children in each arm. Results from the pilot study indicate that we can expect about 20% of participants to drop out, thus approximately 80 children will be recruited per arm. To reach this goal we aim to include 30 municipalities and 50 therapists to be trained in ST-CT.

Statistical analysis

A full statistical analyses plan will be developed prior to data access. Our primary analysis will compare ST-CT to usual care on CATS-2 scores post-treatment with a two-sample t-test. We will follow the intention to treat principle, and all participants will be included and followed up for data collection irrespective of treatment adherence. Follow-up data will be assessed with linear models with recruiting site as a random factor, and treatment arm as a fixed effect (WPs 1a-c). Investigation of change processes (WP2) will be conducted by causal mediation analyses, and trajectories of symptom development during treatment will be explored with multi group latent growth curve analyses. To investigate the relationships between implementation drivers and implementation success (WP3), change in self-perceived competency, professional quality of life and turnover intention will be analysed with linear mixed models, and associations between leadership and sustainability will be explored with linear and logistic regressions. The qualitative interviews will be analysed with reflective thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006).

Economic analysis

The primary economic evaluation will be a cost-utility analysis taking the health and social care perspective and with effects measured in terms of quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) estimated from the CHU9D at post-treatment (T3), in line with the clinical analysis. Secondary analyses will include cost-utility analysis at the final follow-up (T5).

The ST-CT intervention will be directly costed using a micro-costing approach. The salary costs of the therapists, including employer costs (e.g. national insurance and superannuation) and relevant overheads (e.g. capital, management, administration, etc.) will be weighted to include non-face-to-face time spent on other activities (e.g. session preparation, writing up notes, meetings, training, etc.) and used to calculate cost per participant. All other services will be costed using nationally applicable unit costs, for example from the Norwegian Directorate of Health and Statistics Norway.

To estimate QALYs, health states based on CHU9D item scores will be converted into utility scores by applying general population quality weights. Utility scores vary between 0 (dead) and 1 (perfect health) and indicate population preferences for individual health states. QALYs will be calculated as the area under the curve defined by the utility scores at baseline and each follow-up, assuming linear change between timepoints (Richardson & Manca, 2004).

Economic analyses will be detailed in full in a health economic analysis plan to be finalized prior to data access. Costs and outcomes will be compared at the T3 and T5 follow-up points with mean differences and 95% confidence intervals obtained by non-parametric bootstrap regressions to account for non-normal distributions commonly found in economic data. Cost-utility will be assessed using standard net-benefit approaches (Briggs et al., 1997). A joint distribution of incremental mean costs and effects for the two groups will be generated using nonparametric bootstrapping to explore the probability that each of the treatments is the optimal choice, subject to a range of possible maximum values (ceiling ratio) that a decision-maker might be willing to pay for an additional QALY. Cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (showing the probability ST-CT is cost-effective compared to TAU) will be presented by plotting these probabilities for a range of possible values of the ceiling ratio (Fenwick & Byford, 2005). To provide more relevant treatment-effect estimates, all analyses will include adjustment for pre-specified baseline covariates in line with clinical analyses, plus baseline variables of interest (cost and CHU9D scores; Assmann et al., 2000), and will exclude influential cost outliers in the 99th cost percentile (Weichle et al., 2013).

Ethics

The study is approved by the Regional Ethics Committee Norway (REK), study registry number: 489801. Study inclusion will be contingent on written consent from the child’s legal caregivers and oral consent from the child. Participants may retract their consent at any time point and all the data they have provided will be deleted upon requests, unless they have already been published. To ensure the rights and freedoms of participants, a data protection and impact assessment (DPIA) has been conducted.

Negative effects

All participating children will be followed closely both by the assessor and a therapist, and if there is any sign of harm to patients, measures will be taken, or the study will be stopped. Potential negative effects from the interventions will be reported with effect sizes and confidence intervals to highlight their significance.

Discussion

In Norway, the first line municipal services have the responsibility to provide low-threshold preventive care. To date, there is a gap between the need for and access to trauma-focused care (Holt et al., 2016; Schweer-Collins & Lanier, 2021), as accessible, evidence-based treatment (EBT) models are lacking and there is no standardized treatment or care for children after trauma in the municipal services (Helland et al., 2021; Riksrevisjonen, 2021). The ST-CT model shows promise as a low-intensity and effective treatment that can increase access to care for traumatized children, but to date this model has not been investigated as a stand-alone treatment that is compared to the treatment as usual.

In addition to being the first RCT study of ST-CT conducted by an independent research group, another strength of this study is that it is conducted in regular first-line municipality services with regular therapists and participants recruited from the ordinary patient group. This increases the ecological validity of the study. Further, the study investigates the effect on a broad range of symptoms, including an objective measure of sleep, which will provide novel knowledge on the relationship between sleep quality and treatment outcome. Although the child’s reports of their own symptoms pertain the main outcome, both caregivers and teachers will report on child general functioning and will provide important information. Also, focus on potential change processes will help us gain a better understanding of which components are most central and can help therapist tailor the intervention to the individual child. Finally, with the hybrid design, results from this study can help improve implementation of the treatment in the future.

There are also some limitations that needs to be considered. First, since the workbook and other study materials have only been translated into Norwegian, participants who are in need of an interpreter to complete the treatment cannot be included in the trial. This means that we will not be able to investigate whether the ST-CT method can be helpful for refugee and asylum-seeking families, although many of these have experienced trauma and will potentially be in need of help (Lemonjava et al., 2020). Secondly, the study can only include children if written consent has been obtained from both the child’s caregivers. In Norway, even though caregivers may have exposed a child to violence and neglect and the child is no longer in their daily care, in most cases they retain the right to consent to any treatment or participation in research. This means that we will not be able to include a particularly vulnerable group of children that have been exposed to abuse from a caregiver if this caregiver is not willing to let the child participate in the study. Third, there are many other variables that could have been of great interest to investigate however these are omitted to reduce the burden of assessment for the participants.

Concluding, the NorStep Study is the first study to investigate ST-CT as a stand-alone treatment compared to TAU, and results will help increase our understanding of the effectiveness, cost-effectiveness and preventive effect of this parent-led treatment model. Given that this is an adaptation of the original model, with Step One and Step Two provided at different services levels, future studies should investigate how the process of stepping up best can be organized to ensure continuity in the treatment.
