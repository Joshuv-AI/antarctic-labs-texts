Molecular characteristics of chromosome-mediated colistin resistance in carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates from a tertiary hospital in China
Authors: Yanghua Xiao, Keyi Li, Rui Zhao, Zige Wang, Wei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the prevalence and underlying mechanisms of colistin resistance among 557 non-duplicate CRPA isolates collected from a tertiary hospital in Jiangxi, China, between 2021 and 2024.MethodsAntimicrobial susceptibility testing, whole-genome sequencing, bioinformatic analyses, site-directed mutagenesis, RT-qPCR and time-kill kinetics assay were performed to investigate the mechanisms of colistin resistance in CRPA.ResultsAmong the 557 CRPA isolates, colistin minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) ranged from 0.125 to 32 mg/L, with MIC50 and MIC90 values of 1/2 mg/L. Twenty‑three isolates (23/557, 4.1%) exhibited colistin resistance (ColR‑CRPA), all showing multidrug‑resistant phenotypes. The 23 ColR‑CRPA isolates displayed extensive genetic diversity, encompassing 20 sequence types, with only two potential nosocomial transmission clusters (ST235 and ST1236) identified. Mutations in two‑component systems (pmrAB, phoPQ, parRS, and cprRS), lipid A modification genes (arnBCADTEF, eptA/eptA2/eptC), efflux regulators, and oprD were ubiquitous. Thirteen distinct amino acid substitutions were identified in PmrAB and PhoPQ, among which PmrA L71R (19/23, 82.6%) and PmrB Y345H (22/23, 95.7%) were the most prevalent. Functional characterization of all thirteen substitutions revealed that only PmrA Q109K, PmrB L107P, and PhoQ V260G significantly contributed to colistin resistance. Introducing these substitutions into the colistin‑susceptible reference strain PAO1 increased MICs by 2–4 fold, whereas reversion of PmrA Q109K or PhoQ V260G in clinical isolates reduced MICs from 32 to 4 mg/L. RT‑qPCR analysis showed that these substitutions significantly upregulated arnB and pmrA expression (P < 0.0001). Time‑kill kinetics assays further confirmed the enhanced colistin tolerance conferred by these mutations.ConclusionsColistin resistance in CRPA remains uncommon but is mediated by complex networks of chromosomal mutations. These findings highlight the importance of continuous genomic surveillance and strict infection‑control measures to prevent the emergence and dissemination of colistin‑resistant CRPA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04872-5.

Background

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a ubiquitous Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen that poses significant challenges in clinical settings due to its remarkable adaptability and intrinsic resistance to multiple antimicrobial agents [1, 2]. The emergence of carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa (CRPA) has further complicated treatment strategies [3]. In high-burden settings, CRPA rates have increased markedly over the past two decades, with some regions reporting prevalence exceeding 30%, underscoring the need for continued surveillance and new therapeutic strategies [4].

Managing CRPA infections is particularly difficult among immunocompromised and critically ill patients, in whom therapeutic options are severely restricted [5, 6]. This crisis has revived the use of older polymyxins, especially colistin [7], originally introduced in the 1950s but later abandoned because of nephro‑ and neurotoxicity [8]. Colistin is a cationic lipopeptide (a cyclic heptapeptide with a tripeptide side chain acylated by a fatty acid) that exerts bactericidal activity primarily via electrostatic interactions with anionic lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in the outer membrane, leading to membrane disruption and cell death [9, 10]. Despite toxicity concerns, colistin remains a last‑resort agent against multidrug‑resistant Gram‑negative bacteria [11]. However, increased clinical selection pressure has been accompanied by the emergence of colistin-resistant CRPA (ColR-CRPA) [12, 13].

ColR‑CRPA is a major clinical concern because colistin is a last-resort agent for severe P. aeruginosa infections and resistance often arises in MDR/XDR backgrounds [11, 14], further narrowing therapeutic options. Its prevalence varies across regions and has increased over time; a recent meta-analysis of 619 studies reported ~ 1% global colistin resistance among clinical P. aeruginosa isolates, increasing from 2% (2006–2010) to 5% (2020–2023), with the highest prevalence in cystic fibrosis isolates (7%) [15]. Both clonal expansion of high-risk lineages, including ST235, and independent emergence have been reported [16, 17]. ColR‑CRPA is largely healthcare-associated and affects high-risk populations, particularly ICU patients and those with prolonged hospitalization, invasive devices, immunosuppression, mechanical ventilation, or prior broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure [14].

Colistin resistance in P. aeruginosa primarily results from adaptive modifications of the LPS target, reducing its electrostatic affinity for the drug [18, 19]. Central to this mechanism is activation of the arnBCADTEF operon, encoding enzymes that incorporate 4-amino-4-deoxy-L-arabinose (L‑Ara4N) into lipid A [18, 20], thereby masking surface negative charges and diminishing colistin binding [18]. Expression of the arn operon is controlled by multiple two‑component systems (TCS), chiefly PhoPQ and PmrAB [21, 22], which sense environmental stimuli and orchestrate adaptive responses [23, 24]. However, the functional consequences of specific mutations within the TCS are incompletely characterized, and their relative contributions to clinical resistance phenotypes require further elucidation.

This study aimed to characterize the genomic features and resistance determinants of ColR‑CRPA isolates and to evaluate the contributions of distinct PmrAB and PhoPQ mutations to colistin resistance using site‑directed mutagenesis.

Materials and methods

Isolate collection and identification

Between January 2021 and December 2024, a total of 557 non-duplicate clinical CRPA isolates were collected from the Department of Clinical Laboratory, The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University, Jiangxi Province, China, as previously described [25]. Only one isolate from each patient was included in the study. Species-level identification was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). Detailed metadata for the colistin-resistant CRPA isolates are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Carbapenem susceptibility (imipenem and meropenem) was determined using the broth microdilution (BMD) method in accordance with the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI, 2024; M100). Isolates exhibiting resistance to either imipenem or meropenem were classified as CRPA. Colistin resistance was assessed by BMD, and results were interpreted based on CLSI breakpoints (resistant, ≥ 4 mg/L). For colistin-resistant CRPA isolates, extended susceptibility testing was performed using BMD against cefepime, piperacillin-tazobactam, ceftazidime, amikacin, tobramycin, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, ceftazidime-avibactam, and aztreonam. P. aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 was used as the quality control strain.

Whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from 23 colistin-resistant CRPA isolates using a commercial bacterial DNA extraction kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China; Cat. No. B518225-0100). DNA concentration and purity were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Whole-genome sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequencing reads were assessed for quality using FastQC v0.11.9 (https://github.com/s-andrews/FastQC), and adapter sequences as well as low-quality bases were trimmed with Trimmomatic v0.39 [26]. High-quality reads were assembled with SPAdes v4.0.0 [27], and assembly quality was evaluated using QUAST v5.2.0 [28]. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) was performed in silico by querying the PubMLST database (https://pubmlst.org/) to assign sequence types (STs). Genome annotation was conducted with Bakta v1.6.0 [29]. In silico serotyping (O-antigen prediction) was performed using the PAst tool (https://github.com/Sandramses/PAst). Virulence-associated genes and antibiotic resistance determinants were identified by aligning annotated genomes against the Virulence Factors of Pathogenic Bacteria (VFDB) and the ResFinder 4.0 databases [30, 31].

Phylogenetic analyses and mutation identification

Pangenome reconstruction was performed with Panaroo v1.2.10 [32]. Three reference genomes, namely PAO1 (GCF_000006765.1), PA14 (GCF_000014625.1), and PA7 (GCF_000017205.1), were included to provide phylogenetic context. Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) sites were extracted from the alignment using SNP-sites v2.5.1 [33], and pairwise SNP distances were calculated with SNP-dists v0.8.2 (https://github.com/tseemann/snp-dists). A maximum-likelihood phylogeny was then reconstructed with IQ-TREE2 v2.2.6 [34]. The phylogenetic tree was visualized and annotated using Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL, https://itol.embl.de) [35]. High-quality sequencing reads were aligned to the P. aeruginosa reference genome PAO1 (GCF_000006765.1) using Snippy v4.6.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy) to detect SNPs and insertions/deletions (indels). Functional annotation and impact prediction of detected variants were performed using SnpEff v5.0 [36].

Construction of site-directed mutants

Site‑directed mutants of the pmrAB and phoPQ loci were constructed using a sacB‑based allelic exchange strategy, following procedures described previously [37]. Approximately 1,000 bp fragments upstream and downstream of the intended mutation site were PCR‑amplified. The two fragments were then fused to generate an allelic exchange cassette, with appropriate restriction sites incorporated at the ends. The resulting PCR products were digested with the respective restriction enzymes and cloned into the suicide vector pEX18Gm, yielding the recombinant plasmid pEX18Gm::UD. The resulting plasmid was introduced into P. aeruginosa PAO1 by electroporation, and transformants were selected for gentamicin resistance. Counter‑selection was performed on LB agar supplemented with 10% sucrose to select for double‑crossover recombinants. Putative double-crossover mutants were screened for gentamicin sensitivity and confirmed by PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing of the targeted regions. The primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR

P. aeruginosa isolates were cultured in CAMHB at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm until reaching the exponential growth phase. Total RNA was extracted using the Total RNA Purification Kit (Sangon Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was synthesized with the PrimeScript RT reagent kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara Bio, Japan). Gene expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene rpoD. Quantitative PCR was performed on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara). Relative transcript levels of arnB and pmrA were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Each assay was carried out in triplicate, and primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Time-kill kinetics assay

Bacterial colonies from overnight cultures were suspended in sterile saline, and the turbidity was adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard using a densitometer (bioMérieux, DensiCHEK Plus). The bacterial suspension was subsequently diluted in cation‑adjusted Mueller-Hinton broth (CAMHB) to achieve an initial inoculum of approximately 1.5 × 106 CFU/mL. Colistin sulfate was then added to a final concentration of 2 mg/L, and the cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. Samples were collected at predetermined time points (0, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h) for viable cell counts. Each sample was serially diluted, centrifuged for 5 min and washed to minimize colistin carryover, and the pellets were resuspended in sterile saline to the original volume. Appropriate dilutions were plated onto Mueller-Hinton agar and incubated at 35 °C for 18–24 h to determine colony‑forming units. All time‑kill assays were performed in biological triplicate, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Differences among three or more groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with statistical significance defined as a two-sided P < 0.05 (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001).

Results

Antimicrobial susceptibility and clinical characteristics of ColR-CRPA isolates

Among the 557 CRPA isolates, colistin MICs ranged from 0.125 to 32 mg/L (Fig. 1A). The MIC50 and MIC90 values were 1 mg/L and 2 mg/L, respectively. The majority of isolates exhibited MICs of 0.5 mg/L (29.6%, 165/557) or 1 mg/L (45.6%, 254/557). Based on the CLSI breakpoint (resistant ≥ 4 mg/L), 23 isolates (4.1%) were identified as colistin-resistant (ColR-CRPA). The antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of these 23 ColR-CRPA isolates are shown in Fig. 1B. Most isolates exhibited resistance to ceftazidime and ceftazidime-avibactam (each 91.3%), followed by aztreonam (69.6%) and cefepime (39.1%) (Fig. 1B). Fluoroquinolone activity was limited, with only 5 isolates susceptible to ciprofloxacin (5/23, 21.7%) and 8 to levofloxacin (8/23, 34.8%). In contrast, aminoglycosides showed the greatest activity, with 14 isolates susceptible to tobramycin (14/23, 60.9%) and 15 to amikacin (15/23, 65.2%). Piperacillin-tazobactam retained moderate activity, with 12 isolates susceptible (12/23, 52.2%). Detailed antimicrobial susceptibility results for each isolate are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Clinical information of patients infected with the 23 ColR-CRPA isolates is summarized in Table 1. Most isolates were obtained from patients admitted to the intensive care unit (13/23, 56.5%). Epidemiological characteristics of ColR-CRPA isolates

WGS short-read sequencing and phylogenetic analysis were performed for the 23 ColR-CRPA isolates. As shown in Fig. 2A, most ColR-CRPA isolates grouped within the major PAO1/PA14 clade, whereas the divergent strain PA7 formed a distinct outgroup. MLST revealed substantial genetic heterogeneity (Fig. 2B), with the isolates assigned to 20 distinct sequence types. The most frequent lineage was ST235 (3/23, 13.0%), followed by ST1236 (2/23, 8.7%), while the remaining isolates were distributed across 18 singleton STs. The predominant O-serotype was O1 (7/23, 30.4%), followed by O6 (5/23, 21.7%) and O11 (3/23, 13.0%). Other serotypes were less common, including O3 (n = 2), O4 (n = 2), O5 (n = 2), O10 (n = 1), and O2 (n = 1). We further characterized type III secretion system (T3SS) effector genes to assess the virulence potential of ColR-CRPA isolates. The T3SS effector gene exoT was universally present among all isolates, while exoY was detected in the majority (20/23, 87.0%). The cytotoxic effector gene exoU was found in 8 isolates (34.8%), with two strains (NC414 and NC9674) simultaneously carrying both exoU and exoS (Fig. 2B).

To further explore genetic relatedness and potential transmission events, pairwise SNP distances were calculated, and the resulting distance matrix was visualized as a heatmap (Figure S1). Pairwise SNP distances ranged from 0 to 41,805, with a median of 20,024 SNPs. Most isolate pairs exhibited large genomic distances, consistent with the absence of a dominant epidemic clone. Notably, only two isolate clusters displayed extremely low SNP distances: the ST235 isolates (NC219, NC200, and NC9525) were highly clonal, differing by 1–11 SNPs, and the ST1236 (NC4854 and NC4756) isolates differed by 3 SNPs, suggesting possible nosocomial transmission.

Antimicrobial resistance determinants of ColR-CRPA isolates

Antimicrobial susceptibility profiles and resistance determinants of the 23 ColR-CRPA isolates are summarized in Fig. 3. All ColR-CRPA isolates exhibited a multidrug-resistant (MDR) phenotype. Aminoglycoside resistance genes included aadA6, aph(3’)-XV_1, aph(3’)-IIb_1, and aph(3’)-IIb_2, detected in 13.0-73.9% of isolates. Carbapenemase genes blaKPC−2 (NC9674, ST463) and blaVIM−1 (NC202, ST9) were each identified in a single isolate (4.3%). GES-type β-lactamases were found in three isolates (3/23, 13.0%, all belonging to ST235), comprising blaGES−1 (1/23, 4.3%) and blaGES−5 (2/23, 8.7%). OXA-type β-lactamases exhibited considerable diversity. The most frequent allele was blaOXA−396 (8/23, 34.8%). Other variants were detected sporadically, including blaOXA−21 (1/23, 4.3%), blaOXA−395 (3/23, 13.0%), blaOXA−50 (3/23, 13.0%), blaOXA−485 (1/23, 4.3%), blaOXA−486 (4/23, 17.4%), and blaOXA−488 (4/23, 17.4%). In addition, blaCARB−4 was identified in one ST9 isolate (1/23, 4.3%). Chromosomally encoded blaPDC variants were present in all isolates, including blaPDC−22 (1/23, 4.3%), blaPDC−55 (2/23, 8.7%), blaPDC−134 (1/23, 4.3%), and blaPDC−374 (19/23, 82.6%).

Potential chromosomal mechanisms contributing to β‑lactam resistance were further investigated, focusing on genes involved in β‑lactamase regulation, efflux systems, and porin function (Fig. 4). Details of the specific mutations identified are provided in Table S3. Multiple disruptive mutations were identified, including frameshifts, insertions/deletions, and premature stop codons, affecting several key regulatory genes. Several isolates carried ampD disruptive mutations, including frameshifts (V101fs in NC414, R153fs in NC43) and deletions (H93_D94del in NC98, V13_C16del in NC1054). In NC34, a nonsense mutation (ampD Q26*) was detected. Frameshift mutations in mexR were identified, including R70fs in NC165, E27fs in NC1054, H105fs in NC9886, L123fs in NC4853, and G58fs in NC2354. Isolate NC374 harbored a duplication in mexR (Q106dup), while a premature stop codon in mexX (Q396*) was detected in NC9674. In addition, mexZ, the repressor of the MexXY efflux system, contained frameshift mutations, including E98fs in NC219, NC200, and NC9525, as well as C63fs in NC9674. Additional loss‑of‑function mutations were also observed in nalD, a repressor of the MexAB‑OprM system, and a premature stop codon in nalC (Q126*) was identified in NC414. Disruptive alterations in oprD, a substrate-specific porin implicated in carbapenem uptake, were widespread and included premature stop codons (W138* in NC219, NC200, NC9525; Q424* in NC2354).

Mutations in two-component regulatory systems and lipid A modification genes associated with colistin resistance

Since no mcr genes were detected, we next examined chromosomal alterations in two-component regulatory systems previously implicated in colistin resistance (pmrA, pmrB, phoP, phoQ, cprR, cprS, parR, parS, colR and colS) (Fig. 5). A total of 19 isolates (19/23, 82.6%) harbored the pmrA L71R substitution, with one strain additionally carrying Q109K, while the remaining four isolates retained wild-type pmrA. Mutations in the sensor kinase pmrB were more diverse: Y345H was nearly ubiquitous (22/23, 95.7%), followed by G68S (9/23, 39.1%), whereas A119T, P369S, L107P, and S94N were detected at lower frequencies. Within the PhoPQ system, phoP was wild-type in almost all isolates, except for a single isolate with a P131L substitution. phoQ carried mutations in four isolates, most frequently Y85F (3/23, 13.0%) and A391V (3/23, 13.0%), with V260G identified once. Mutations in cprS were present in ten isolates (10/23, 43.5%) and included substitutions such as T16S, E386D, V159I, L411M, E314Q, I164T, and S338N. Alterations in parR were identified in nine isolates (9/23, 39.1%), most commonly L153R and S170N, alongside L58R, V55I, M59I, and E87K. All isolates carried parS mutations, most consistently H398R, with other substitutions (A29V, D249N, D351N, A285V) and a deletion (E76_L87del) also observed. No mutations were detected in colR or colS.

We further examined mutations in lipid A modification genes, including the arnBCADTEF operon and the phosphoethanolamine transferases eptA, eptA2, and eptC (Fig. 4, Table S3). Nonsynonymous substitutions were widespread in these loci. Within the arn operon, arnA mutations were identified in seven isolates (7/23, 30.4%), most frequently F80Y (3/23, 13.0%). Alterations in arnB were more frequent (11/23, 47.8%); notably, one isolate (NC270) carried a frameshift mutation (H158fs). Substitutions were also observed in arnC (R27H in 2 isolates), arnD (S272N in 4 isolates), and arnE (R28H in 4 isolates). In contrast, arnT displayed marked allelic diversity, with multiple distinct substitutions distributed across isolates. In eptA, the most prevalent variants were N504D (11/23, 47.8%), G497S (5/23, 21.7%), and G247S (3/23, 13.0%). Mutations in eptA2 were infrequent, each detected in a single isolate, including V134L, F242L, and A550T. For eptC, eleven isolates (11/23, 47.8%) carried substitutions, most often N598D, with additional variants such as L269M, V162I, R547E, and D267G detected sporadically.

Functional validation of PmrAB and PhoPQ two-component regulatory system mutations contributing to colistin resistance

To evaluate the contribution of mutations in PmrAB and PhoPQ to colistin resistance, allelic exchange was used to introduce point mutations into the colistin-susceptible reference strain P. aeruginosa PAO1 (MIC = 1 mg/L). All mutants were confirmed by DNA sequencing. The MICs of these mutants, determined using the broth microdilution method, are presented in Fig. 6A. Introduction of pmrA Q109K increased the MIC to 2 mg/L, whereas pmrA L71R did not alter susceptibility. Among the pmrB variants, the L107P mutation elevated the MIC to 4 mg/L, while other substitutions (S94N, G68S, A119T, Y345H, G362S, P369S) did not affect colistin susceptibility. The phoP P131L substitution did not affect colistin susceptibility. For phoQ, the V260G mutation increased the MIC to 4 mg/L, whereas Y85F and A391V did not affect susceptibility. Consistent results were obtained from the disk diffusion test (Fig. 6B), further validating these findings. To further verify the roles of these mutations in clinical isolates, allelic replacement experiments were attempted in colistin-resistant strains. In strain NC202 (pmrA Q109K, MIC = 32 mg/L) and NC200 (phoQ V260G, MIC = 32 mg/L), reversion of the respective mutations substantially reduced MICs, both decreasing to 4 mg/L. However, attempts to revert pmrB L107P in strain NC270 were unsuccessful, likely due to the low efficiency of plasmid delivery in clinical isolates.

qPCR analysis demonstrated that specific mutations affected the transcription of genes involved in the lipid A modification pathway (arnB and pmrA) (Fig. 6C). Most substitutions, including pmrA L71R, pmrB G68S, pmrB S94N, pmrB A119T, pmrB Y345H, pmrB G362S, pmrB P369S, phoP P131L, and phoQ Y85F or phoQ A391V, did not significantly alter gene expression compared with the wild-type strain PAO1. In contrast, three mutations strongly activated transcription of lipid A modification genes. The pmrA Q109K variant induced a 12.92 ± 0.78 fold upregulation of arnB and a 3.83 ± 0.21 fold increase in pmrA expression. The pmrB L107P substitution, which also increased the colistin MIC to 4 mg/L, was accompanied by 27.77 ± 1.30 fold higher arnB expression and 9.30 ± 0.66 fold higher pmrA expression. The strongest effect was observed for phoQ V260G, which upregulated arnB (40.67 ± 2.81 fold) and pmrA (9.47 ± 0.96 fold). Time-kill assays performed with colistin (2 mg/L) further confirmed the distinct resistance phenotypes of the mutants (Fig. 6D). The viable cell count of the wild-type PAO1 declined rapidly within the first 3 h by approximately 3–4 log10 CFU/mL, then showed regrowth after 6 h. In contrast, the three mutant strains maintained high bacterial counts throughout the experiment, showing little initial killing and sustained growth.

Discussion

This study reports a colistin resistance prevalence of 4.1% (23/557) among CRPA isolates in Jiangxi Province, China. The observed resistance rate falls within the range reported in other regional and international studies, which have demonstrated considerable geographic variation in colistin resistance among P. aeruginosa isolates. These include studies by Xiao et al. in shanghai, China (2.2%) [13], Rossi et al. in Brazil (6.3%) [38], Wi et al. in South Korea (7.4%) [17], and Jafari-Ramedani et al. in Iran (9.0%) [12]. In our study, ColR‑CRPA isolates were predominantly recovered from ICU patients (56.5%), and most patients had substantial prior exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics, particularly carbapenems (78.3%) and β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (65.2%), with more than half also receiving colistin (56.5%). These findings are consistent with the ICU being a high-risk setting, in which prolonged hospitalization, invasive procedures, and frequent exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics may promote selection and transmission of resistant P. aeruginosa [39]. Importantly, clinical outcomes were poor (30-day mortality 47.8% and relapse 17.4%), indicating the substantial clinical burden associated with ColR‑CRPA infections. The frequent prior colistin exposure suggests that colistin could directly select for colistin resistance in some cases. These findings underscore the importance of antimicrobial stewardship in critical care and strengthened surveillance and infection-control measures to limit dissemination of ColR‑CRPA. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed that ColR‑CRPA isolates were extensively resistant, including reduced susceptibility to last‑line β‑lactam combinations. Aminoglycosides retained the greatest activity, suggesting potential use in combination regimens.

The potential for colistin-resistant P. aeruginosa to spread within hospitals has long been a major concern [40]. In this study, the 23 ColR‑CRPA isolates represented 20 sequence types, with pairwise SNP distances generally large (median, 20,024 SNPs), indicating that colistin resistance mainly arose independently across distinct genetic backgrounds rather than from a single epidemic clone. These results are consistent with global observations that P. aeruginosa displays high genetic diversity in hospital environments and that resistance usually emerges through chromosomal mutations rather than clonal dissemination [41, 42]. Nevertheless, two small clusters were identified, suggesting limited nosocomial transmission. Of note, ST235 is an internationally recognized high‑risk clone associated with multidrug resistance and hospital outbreaks [43]. Prior studies have linked exoU-positive lineages with increased cytotoxicity and poorer clinical outcomes compared with exoS-positive strains [44, 45]. In this study, all colistin-resistant ST235 isolates were exoU-positive, suggesting a potentially more virulent profile within this high-risk lineage.

A well‑characterized mechanism of colistin resistance is mediated by mcr genes [11], which confer resistance by adding phosphoethanolamine to lipid A. None of the ColR‑CRPA isolates in this study carried mcr, consistent with previous reports showing the absence of plasmid‑mediated resistance in P. aeruginosa [12, 13, 17, 38]. Unlike in Enterobacterales, where mcr is widespread, plasmid transfer appears limited in P. aeruginosa owing to ecological and genetic constraints. Instead, resistance predominantly arises from chromosomal mutations. Frequent substitutions were identified in the PmrAB TCS, including pmrA L71R and pmrB Y345H. However, prior studies showed that the pmrB Y345H substitution did not significantly increase pmrA expression [12]. Allelic replacement in the PAO1 reference strain showed that substitutions such as pmrB G68S, S94N, A119T, Y345H, G362S, and P369S did not alter colistin susceptibility. These variants may reflect naturally occurring polymorphisms. Nonetheless, functional analyses revealed that specific changes such as pmrA Q109K, pmrB L107P, and phoQ V260G can enhance colistin resistance. These findings suggest that although individual substitutions exert limited effects in isolation, they can become critical contributors to high‑level resistance within the complex mutational landscape of clinical strains.

The ParRS and CprRS systems enable P. aeruginosa to sense cationic antimicrobial peptides and regulate LPS modification [46, 47]. All isolates carried parS mutations, and several contained parR mutations (L153R and S170N). ParRS activation has been linked to downregulation of oprD [48], and multiple disruptive oprD mutations were identified in isolates with parR/parS variants. Colistin resistance in P. aeruginosa also involves the arnBCADTEF operon, which mediates the synthesis and transfer of L‑Ara4N to lipid A [49]. Mutations in arnB, arnC, and arnT have been linked to colistin resistance [16]. Nearly half of the isolates (11/23, 47.8%) carried nonsynonymous substitutions in arnB. Nonsynonymous substitutions in arnT may modify enzyme activity or substrate affinity, thereby enhancing the efficiency of LPS modification [50]. Chromosomally encoded phosphoethanolamine transferases (eptA, eptA2, eptC) catalyze the addition of phosphoethanolamine to lipid A in a mechanism analogous to plasmid‑borne mcr genes [51]. Prior functional studies have demonstrated that overexpression of eptA can markedly increase colistin resistance [52], although most evidence indicates that structural mutations alone are typically insufficient to confer resistance without concurrent activation by upstream TCS [22]. Taken together, these findings indicate that clinical colistin resistance arises not from a single regulatory mutation but from an integrated network of chromosomal regulators and structural gene alterations acting synergistically.

P. aeruginosa can acquire resistance through overexpression of efflux pumps, which expel antimicrobial agents from the cell [53]. Although classical RND‑type efflux systems contribute only marginally to colistin resistance, colistin‑resistant isolates in this study frequently harbored loss‑of‑function mutations in chromosomal resistance determinants, including the β‑lactamase regulator ampD, multiple efflux repressors, and the porin gene oprD. These alterations are likely to account for the broad multidrug‑resistant phenotype observed in these clinical strains. Although these mutations are unlikely to be primary drivers of colistin resistance, they may act synergistically with lipid A modification by altering outer membrane permeability or protein composition.

This study has several limitations. First, the number of colistin‑resistant CRPA isolates was small and derived from a single center, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, due to the large number of mutations identified and limited resources, not all candidate resistance mutations could be functionally validated. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes and comprehensive experimental validation are warranted to further elucidate the genetic mechanisms underlying colistin resistance in P. aeruginosa.

In summary, colistin resistance in CRPA remains infrequent but highly heterogeneous, with only sporadic clonal transmission, and arises mainly from diverse chromosomal mutations. These findings underscore the need for continuous genomic surveillance and targeted stewardship to mitigate the emerging threat of ColR‑CRPA.
