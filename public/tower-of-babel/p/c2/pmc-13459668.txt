Negotiating access to comprehensive HIV healthcare: Fear, stigma, and health system constraints in Tamale Metropolis, Ghana
Authors: Charles Owusu-Aduomi Botchwey, Ebenezer Agbenya Adzormahe, Lawrencia Aggrey-Bluwey, Samuel Kofi Miledzi Agblorti, Albert Ahenkan, Susuana Adutwumwaa, Doreen Oparebea Odei, Emmanuel Sei Nkpetri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Access to comprehensive HIV healthcare services remains uneven in many low-resource settings, despite the availability of antiretroviral therapy. In northern Ghana, limited qualitative evidence exists on how people living with HIV/AIDS experience, interpret, and navigate access to care within public health facilities. This qualitative study explored factors influencing access to comprehensive healthcare services among people living with HIV/AIDS in public hospitals in the Tamale Metropolis, Ghana. In-depth interviews guided by a semi-structured interview guide were conducted with 30 participants receiving HIV care at three public hospitals. Data were analyzed thematically using NVivo software. Access to comprehensive HIV care was shaped by intersecting psychological, social, and health system factors. Fear of confronting one’s HIV status influenced the depth of engagement with services, often limiting care to medication collection. Anticipated stigma and community reactions, including religious moral framing of HIV, discouraged disclosure and delayed or selective care-seeking. Participants also described health worker attitudes and service environments that undermined trust and constrained fuller engagement with care. While geographical distance was not perceived as a barrier, many participants deliberately sought care outside their communities to protect confidentiality. Inconsistent clinical assessment and non-functional diagnostic equipment further limited access to comprehensive care. For people living with HIV/AIDS in the Tamale Metropolis, access to comprehensive healthcare extends beyond physical availability of services or clinic attendance. Fear, stigma, and health system constraints shape how individuals engage selectively with care over time. Interventions aimed at improving HIV service delivery must address confidentiality, provider-patient interactions, and system reliability, alongside efforts to reduce stigma within communities.

Significance for public health

This study highlights the gaps between enrolment in HIV care and access to comprehensive healthcare services in a resource-constrained setting. It shows how fear, stigma, religious moral framing, confidentiality concerns, and health system limitations shape selective engagement with care. Further, the findings draw attention to barriers that are not captured by coverage or utilization indicators alone. The study emphasizes the need for HIV programs to move beyond medication provision toward approaches that prioritize respectful provider-patient interactions, psychosocial support, reliable diagnostic services, and protection of patient confidentiality. These insights are particularly relevant for public health planning in low- and middle-income settings, where achieving sustained engagement in HIV care is essential for treatment success, epidemic control, and health system resilience.

Introduction

The HIV/AIDS epidemic remains a global public health concern. As of the end of 2024, the Joint United Nations Program on HIV/AIDS estimated that 40.8 million people were living with HIV worldwide.1,2 In the same year, approximately 1.3 million individuals were newly infected, which translates into nearly 5,000 new infections each day. Despite improvements in testing coverage, only about 87% of people living with HIV are aware of their status, leaving more than 5.3 million individuals who have not yet accessed HIV testing services.2–4 These gaps continue to undermine efforts to control transmission and improve treatment outcomes.

Sub-Saharan Africa continues to bear a disproportionate share of the global HIV burden. Over the past two decades, HIV transmission in the region has remained persistent, with some of the highest prevalence rates reported worldwide.5,6 East and Southern Africa are particularly affected. Although the region accounts for only 6.2% of the global population, it is home to approximately 21.1 million people living with HIV, representing more than half of the global total. 1 In 2024 alone, an estimated 490,000 new HIV infections occurred in this region, accounting for 43% of all new infections globally. 4 These figures indicate the continuing vulnerability of African health systems to the epidemic.

In Ghana, HIV prevalence remains relatively low compared to some countries in the region, yet the public health implications are significant. The adult HIV prevalence was estimated at 1.4% in 2024.5,7 Although advances in HIV prevention, diagnosis, and treatment have improved survival and reduced transmission, access to comprehensive care remains uneven. Antiretroviral therapy (ART) has transformed HIV into a manageable chronic condition. However, effective treatment is dependent on timely diagnosis, sustained engagement with care, and supportive health systems. Many individuals living with HIV in Ghana continue to face barriers that limit their ability to fully benefit from available services.5,8

ART coverage in Ghana remains suboptimal. In 2023, only about 69.4% of people living with HIV were receiving ART.1,2 This shortfall presents major challenges for achieving national and global treatment targets. The situation is further complicated by declining external donor support, which has forced the country to make difficult decisions about how to sustain and expand HIV services in a cost-effective and equitable manner. 6 These constraints have direct implications for service availability, quality of care, and long-term health outcomes.

Regional disparities further shape access to HIV services within the country. The Northern Region has historically received less attention and fewer resources for HIV interventions, partly due to its lower population density and previously lower prevalence rates. 1 Recent data suggest a rising trend in HIV prevalence in the region, increasing from 1.9% to 2.1% in 2024 according to the Ghana AIDS Commission.5,7 This increase is concerning, especially in a context where traditional beliefs, limited health literacy, and persistent stigma continue to influence health-seeking behavior. 1 These factors may discourage individuals from accessing HIV testing, treatment, and follow-up care.

Within the Northern Region, Tamale Metropolis plays a central role in HIV service delivery. In 2024, the Metropolis accounted for 0.4% of the total 0.7% HIV prevalence reported for the entire region. 5 As a major urban center with a number of public health facilities, Tamale has been a focal point for national and international efforts aimed at expanding access to HIV services. Institutions such as the World Health Organization, the World Bank, the United Nations Population Fund, and the United Nations International Children’s Emergency Fund have consistently emphasized the need to strengthen HIV prevention and treatment services in the Metropolis to reduce disease burden and improve patient outcomes.1,2,9

Despite the availability of HIV services in Tamale Metropolis, evidence on how people living with HIV access and experience comprehensive healthcare remains limited. While several studies have documented HIV prevalence and treatment coverage in Ghana, there is a noticeable gap in context-specific qualitative research that explores the factors shaping access to comprehensive care for people living with HIV/AIDS in this setting. Understanding how social, cultural, psychological, and health system factors interact is essential for designing interventions that respond to the lived realities of affected individuals. This study therefore examines the determinants of access to comprehensive healthcare services for HIV/AIDS patients in selected public hospitals within the Tamale Metropolis, particularly how individuals may remain enrolled in care while experiencing limited or fragmented access to the full range of services required for effective HIV management.

Theoretical underpinning

The Behavioral Model of Healthcare Utilization, commonly referred to as the “access to care” model, serves as the theoretical framework for this study. 10 The model is widely used to explain factors that influence individuals’ use of health services within clinical and institutional settings. It focuses on behaviors exhibited by patients and healthcare providers in relation to care and treatment, including HIV services. 11 Within the context of this study, the model provides a useful lens for examining how multiple individual, social, and system-level factors shape access to comprehensive healthcare among people living with HIV/AIDS.

According to the model, healthcare utilization is influenced by three broad categories of population characteristics. These are predisposing factors, enabling factors, and need factors. 10 These components interact to influence patterns of healthcare use, even in contexts where services are physically available. Predisposing factors refer to characteristics that exist prior to illness and influence an individual’s inclination to seek care. These include demographic attributes such as age and gender, as well as social structure variables including education, occupation, culture, and social networks. 10 Predisposing factors also encompass individuals’ values, beliefs, attitudes, and knowledge regarding illness and the healthcare system. In the context of HIV care, such sociocultural characteristics shape perceptions of risk, illness, and treatment, which can either encourage or discourage engagement with health services. When prevailing social norms discourage disclosure or frame HIV as a moral failing, individuals may delay or limit engagement with care. 12

Enabling factors relate to the conditions that facilitate or constrain an individual’s ability to obtain healthcare services. These factors operate at individual, household, and community levels and include financial resources, social support, and access to information. 10 Social connections may facilitate access by providing emotional support or practical assistance. 13 At the health system level, enabling factors include the availability and organization of services, provider behavior, waiting times, and perceived confidentiality. The presence of a health facility alone does not guarantee effective access. The quality of interaction with health workers and the reliability of services strongly influence whether individuals engage fully with available care.

Need factors represent the third component of the model and refer to an individual’s perceived or evaluated need for healthcare. 10 Perceived need reflects how individuals assess their own health status and symptoms, while evaluated need reflects professional judgments made by healthcare providers. 11 Perceived need plays a critical role in shaping care-seeking behavior and continued engagement with services. Individuals who do not perceive themselves as ill, or who fear the social implications of diagnosis, may limit their engagement with healthcare even when services are available. Evaluated need influences the type and intensity of care provided once an individual enters the healthcare system.

In this study, the Behavioral Model of Healthcare Utilization is applied not only to explain whether individuals access HIV healthcare services, but also to understand variations in the depth and completeness of care accessed. This application of the model allows for a nuanced understanding of access as a process shaped by emotional responses, social environments, provider interactions, and health system capacity.

Methods

This study is reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR)14,15 guidelines.

Study design

A qualitative descriptive study design was adopted to explore determinants of access to comprehensive healthcare services among people living with HIV/AIDS in public hospitals within the Tamale Metropolis. Qualitative descriptive designs are particularly appropriate for studies seeking to understand experiences, perceptions, and meanings attached to health-related phenomena in their natural contexts. 16 A qualitative approach was appropriate given the study’s focus on understanding participants’ lived experiences, perceptions, and meanings attached to healthcare access. Data were collected at a single point in time, reflecting a cross-sectional qualitative design. 17

Study setting

The study was conducted in three public hospitals located within the Tamale Metropolitan Assembly in the Northern Region of Ghana. These facilities were Tamale Teaching Hospital, Tamale Central Hospital, and Tamale West Hospital. Together, they constitute the main public health institutions providing HIV/AIDS services in the Metropolis.

Tamale Teaching Hospital is a tertiary referral facility serving the five northern regions of Ghana, with an estimated bed capacity of approximately 800. The hospital provides specialized clinical services, supports undergraduate and postgraduate medical training, and undertakes health research. Tamale Central Hospital and Tamale West Hospital are secondary-level facilities with bed capacities of approximately 186 and 180 respectively, offering outpatient and inpatient services across multiple departments, including HIV/AIDS care.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study population comprised people living with HIV/AIDS who were registered and receiving care at the selected public hospitals. Eligible participants included adults who appeared mentally stable, receiving HIV-related services at the time of data collection, and willing to provide informed consent. Individuals who were not receiving care at the selected facilities or who declined participation were excluded from the study.

Sampling strategy

A purposive sampling technique was employed to recruit participants who had direct experience accessing HIV/AIDS services at the selected facilities. Purposive sampling is particularly useful in qualitative research where participants are selected based on their ability to provide rich and relevant information about the phenomenon under investigation.18,19 This approach enabled the inclusion of individuals with varied experiences of care across different hospitals. Recruitment continued until data saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new codes or substantive insights were identified from additional interviews. This approach is consistent with the distinction between code saturation and meaning saturation described in qualitative research literature. 20 Saturation was observed after approximately 24 interviews, after which six additional interviews were conducted to confirm stability of the emerging themes. A total of 30 participants were therefore included, which is consistent with qualitative research practices aimed at depth rather than statistical representation. 21

Participant characteristics

A total of 30 participants were included in the study. Participants varied in age, gender, and duration of engagement in HIV care. The sample included both male and female participants across a range of adult age groups. Participants were drawn from the three selected facilities, ensuring representation across different levels of care. Most participants reported having been on antiretroviral therapy for varying durations, ranging from recent initiation to several years of treatment. Participants also reflected diverse religious backgrounds, which was relevant to the study’s exploration of moral framing and healthcare decision-making.

Data collection

Data were collected through in-depth interviews conducted in February 2025. A semi-structured interview guide was developed based on the study objectives and informed by the theoretical underpinning.10–12 The guide included open-ended questions designed to elicit participants’ experiences of accessing comprehensive healthcare services. Prior to the main data collection, the interview guide was pretested at Savelugu Government Hospital in the Savelugu Municipality. The pilot exercise assessed clarity, relevance, and flow of the questions and informed minor revisions to the guide.

Interviews were conducted in private locations within the hospital premises, as preferred by participants, to ensure confidentiality and comfort. All participants provided informed consent before participation. Interviews were conducted in English or local languages, depending on participants’ preferences, and were audio-recorded with permission. Field notes were taken to capture contextual observations and non-verbal cues.

The development of the interview guide was informed by the Behavioral Model of Healthcare Utilization. Questions were designed to explore predisposing factors such as beliefs and perceptions about HIV, enabling factors including access to services and interactions with health workers, and need factors related to perceived illness and care requirements. This ensured that data collection captured both individual and system-level influences on access to comprehensive healthcare.

Data analysis

Interview recordings were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using NVivo qualitative data analysis software. Thematic analysis was employed to identify patterns and meanings across the data following the approach proposed by Braun and Clarke. 22 Analysis followed an iterative process involving familiarization with the transcripts, initial coding, development of categories, and abstraction of themes. 23 Coding was guided by both inductive insights from the data and sensitizing concepts drawn from the Behavioral Model of Healthcare Utilization. Themes were refined through repeated comparison across transcripts to ensure coherence and analytic depth. Reflexive memo-writing was used throughout the analysis process to support transparency and to document analytic decisions.

The Behavioral Model also informed the initial coding framework by sensitizing the analysis to patterns related to predisposing, enabling, and need factors. However, coding remained flexible and inductive, allowing new themes to emerge beyond the theoretical categories.

Trustworthiness and rigor

Several strategies were employed to enhance the trustworthiness of the study, consistent with SRQR recommendations and established principles of qualitative rigor. 24 Credibility was supported through the inclusion of participants from multiple facilities and through prolonged engagement with the data during analysis. Attention was also given to identifying divergent or disconfirming cases that did not fully align with dominant patterns, which helped refine and strengthen the interpretation of themes. Dependability was enhanced by the use of a semi-structured interview guide and a systematic analytic process. Confirmability was supported through careful documentation of coding and theme development using NVivo software and through reflexive memo-writing. While formal member checking was not conducted, findings were discussed within the research team to ensure consistency and interpretive rigor.

Researcher positionality and reflexivity

The interviews were conducted by members of the research team who had postgraduate training in public health and prior experience in qualitative interviewing. None of the interviewers had clinical responsibilities within the participating hospitals, and no prior professional or personal relationships existed between the interviewers and participants before recruitment. This helped reduce the likelihood that participants would perceive the researchers as part of the healthcare system responsible for their care.

The researchers were familiar with the broader health system context of northern Ghana and were aware of the social sensitivities surrounding HIV-related stigma, disclosure, and healthcare access within the region. While this contextual knowledge facilitated rapport and interpretation, the researchers remained attentive to the possibility that pre-existing assumptions could influence data collection and analysis.

Reflexivity was maintained throughout the study through field notes, reflexive memo-writing, and regular discussions among the research team. These processes encouraged critical reflection on how researchers’ professional backgrounds, expectations, and interpretations might shape coding decisions, theme development, and the presentation of findings. Particular attention was given to ensuring that participants’ accounts were represented as faithfully as possible, including perspectives that differed from dominant patterns within the dataset.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval and permission to conduct the study were obtained from the Department of Public Health of the University for Development Studies with reference number DPH/UDS/REC/2024/017. The authors also had permission from the authorities of the respective health institutions. Participants were informed about the purpose of the study, their voluntary participation, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Confidentiality was ensured by removing personal identifiers from transcripts and restricting data access to the research team.

Theme 1: Participants’ understanding of comprehensive HIV healthcare

Participants consistently described comprehensive HIV healthcare as care that extends beyond the routine collection of antiretroviral medication. From their perspectives, comprehensive care involved regular clinical review, access to viral load testing and laboratory investigations, counselling support, respectful communication with health workers, and strong assurances of confidentiality within health facilities. Participants emphasized that comprehensive care should address both physical health needs and emotional well-being.

Several participants drew a clear distinction between being enrolled in HIV care and receiving what they considered complete care. While most participants attended clinics and collected medication, they felt that important components of care were either missing or inconsistently provided.“Coming to the hospital to collect drugs alone is not enough. You need to know whether the drugs are working and how your body is responding” - Participant 14, TTH.“Sometimes they give you the medicine, but they don’t explain anything. You leave without really understanding your condition.” - Participant 6, TCH.“For me, comprehensive care means they should check everything about your health, not only give you tablets and say go home” - Participant 9, TWH.

These accounts show that participants defined access to comprehensive healthcare in relational and experiential terms, rather than solely in terms of service availability. Access was understood as the ability to receive continuous, respectful, and holistic care that addressed medical, informational, and emotional needs. This understanding provides a foundation for interpreting subsequent findings, as it clarifies that barriers identified later in the results limited not just clinic attendance, but access to the full spectrum of care envisaged in the study objectives.

Theme 2: Psychosocial pathways shaping access to comprehensive care

Subtheme 2.1: Fear of confronting HIV status and the limits of engagement

Fear emerged as a dominant factor shaping access to comprehensive healthcare services. Participants described fear as an ongoing emotional response rather than a one-time reaction to diagnosis. Health facility visits were often perceived as moments that forced participants to confront their HIV status, illness trajectory, and possible social consequences. This fear influenced access in nuanced ways. Rather than avoiding care entirely, many participants described limiting their engagement with services to the minimum required to remain stable. This included attending clinics primarily for medication refills while avoiding deeper interactions such as counselling sessions, additional testing, or prolonged discussions with healthcare providers.“Every time I come to the hospital, it reminds me that this sickness is with me for life. Sometimes I just come for the drugs and leave quickly because thinking too much brings fear” - Participant 7, TCH.“If you are not feeling sick, you try not to think about it. Coming to the hospital too often makes you worry about what will happen next” - Participant 12, TWH.“The effect of knowing your status creates the indelible death mark. The reaction of society alone can kill you. In this situation, not going around searching for your HIV status makes life go on without inviting trouble” - Participant 2, TTH.

This statement reflects a deeply internalized perception of HIV as a life-defining and socially visible condition. The phrase “indelible death mark” suggests that diagnosis is experienced not only as a medical condition, but as a permanent social identity associated with stigma and anticipated rejection. This perception reinforces participants’ tendency to avoid deeper engagement with care, as continued interaction with the health system serves as a reminder of this identity.

These accounts show that fear does not eliminate access to care but reshapes it. Participants remained partially engaged with healthcare services while avoiding components of care that required emotional vulnerability or deeper reflection. This selective engagement limits access to comprehensive healthcare, even when physical attendance at clinics continues. In this way, fear operates as a pathway that narrows care from comprehensive to basic maintenance, directly aligning with the study’s objective of examining determinants of access.

Subtheme 2.2: Religious moral framing and conditional care-seeking

Religious beliefs played a central role in shaping participants’ healthcare decisions. Participants described religious environments where HIV infection was interpreted as evidence of moral failure, punishment, or spiritual weakness. These interpretations heightened stigma and intensified fear of disclosure within families, congregations, and social networks. Religious influence shaped access through conditional care-seeking. Participants reported that seeking healthcare was considered acceptable only when illness became visible or severe. Preventive care, routine monitoring, and early engagement were often viewed as unnecessary or even as a sign of weak faith.“In church, people will say if your faith is strong, you will not worry yourself with such sickness. Going for tests makes people think you don’t trust God” - Participant 4, TWH.“You cannot talk about this sickness in church. If they hear, they will judge you and say you have lived a bad life” - Participant 16, TTH.“Some people believe that if you are living right before God, you cannot get this disease. Because of that, many people prefer to keep quiet and avoid discussing their condition.” - Participant 23, TCH.

Theme 3: Health system interactions and access to comprehensive care

Subtheme 3.1: Health workers’ attitudes and depth of service utilization

Participants consistently linked their level of engagement with healthcare services to their interactions with health workers. Respectful communication and supportive attitudes encouraged participation, while perceived judgment, impatience, or neglect discouraged deeper engagement. Negative experiences with health workers did not always result in complete withdrawal from care. Instead, participants described restricting their interactions to essential tasks such as collecting medication, while avoiding questions, counselling, or requests for additional services.“When you ask questions, they answer you in a way that makes you feel small. So you just take the drugs and go” - Participant 21, TWH.“They treat you like you caused the sickness yourself. It makes you feel ashamed to come often” - Participant 25, TWH.

“When you ask questions, they answer you in a way that makes you feel small. So you just take the drugs and go” - Participant 21, TWH.

“They treat you like you caused the sickness yourself. It makes you feel ashamed to come often” - Participant 25, TWH.

These experiences show that provider attitudes also influence the quality and completeness of care received. Participants’ reluctance to engage fully with health workers limits access to counselling, information, and monitoring services, thereby undermining comprehensive care despite continued clinic attendance.

Although negative experiences were dominant, a small number of participants described highly supportive relationships with health workers. These participants reported feeling respected, listened to, and encouraged to discuss concerns openly. Such accounts represented important disconfirming cases because they demonstrated that health worker interactions were not uniformly negative across facilities. Rather than contradicting the broader theme, these experiences helped refine the analysis by showing that supportive provider behavior can facilitate fuller engagement with comprehensive care.“The nurses here encourage me whenever I come. They explain things well and make me feel comfortable asking questions.” - Participant 10, TTH.

Subtheme 3.2: Distance as a deliberate strategy to safeguard confidentiality

Contrary to conventional assumptions about healthcare access, geographical distance was not perceived as a barrier by most participants. Instead, many intentionally travelled long distances to access HIV services in order to maintain anonymity and protect their social identities. Participants explained that attending nearby facilities increased the risk of recognition, gossip, and unintended disclosure. Distance was therefore used strategically to sustain engagement with care under safer social conditions.“If I go to the hospital near my house, someone will definitely see me. That is why I prefer coming to Tamale” - Participant 18, TCH.“Even though it costs money to travel, it gives me peace of mind.” - Participant 11, TWH.“Here nobody knows me. That is why I can ask questions freely.” - Participant 3, TTH.

These accounts demonstrate a novel pathway to access, where distance facilitates rather than restricts engagement with care.

Subtheme 4.1: Narrow clinical encounters and perceived incompleteness of care

Participants frequently expressed dissatisfaction with the scope of services received during clinic visits. They focus on the drugs and then the consultation is over. There is little opportunity to discuss other health concerns.” - Participant 17, TCH.

The unavailability of functional diagnostic equipment, particularly for viral load testing, was repeatedly cited as a major constraint. These system-level constraints directly limited access to comprehensive healthcare by disrupting monitoring and follow-up. Participants remained enrolled in care, but without key components needed to manage HIV effectively. This reinforces the finding that access is not binary, but exists along a continuum shaped by structural capacity.

Discussion

This study examined determinants of access to comprehensive healthcare services among people living with HIV/AIDS in public hospitals in the Tamale Metropolis. The findings show that access to care is not defined solely by physical attendance at health facilities. Instead, access operates along a continuum shaped by psychosocial responses, social norms, provider behavior, and health system capacity. While many participants were enrolled in care and receiving antiretroviral therapy, access to what they considered comprehensive healthcare remained constrained.

Access to comprehensive care as selective and negotiated engagement

A central contribution of this study is the distinction participants made between being registered for HIV care and receiving comprehensive care. From participants’ perspectives, comprehensive healthcare included regular clinical review, viral load monitoring, counselling, respectful interaction with health workers, and assurance of confidentiality. This understanding aligns with broader conceptualizations of comprehensive HIV care that emphasize continuity, monitoring, and psychosocial support alongside medication provision.9,25 However, the findings reveal that access to these components was uneven. Many participants described selective engagement with services, attending clinics primarily for medication refills while limiting interaction with other aspects of care. This challenges dominant assumptions within healthcare utilization literature that equate service use with effective access.10,11 In this context, utilization masked significant gaps in care quality and completeness, suggesting that access should be understood not only in terms of contact with services, but also in terms of depth and scope of engagement.

Fear as a pathway to partial access

Fear emerged as a critical determinant shaping access to comprehensive healthcare. Consistent with earlier studies, fear was linked to stigma, anticipated discrimination, and anxiety surrounding illness progression.26–28 However, this study extends existing evidence by showing that fear continues to influence care-seeking behavior even after treatment initiation.

Rather than preventing access entirely, fear narrowed engagement with care. Participants remained enrolled in treatment programs but avoided counselling, additional testing, and prolonged interaction with health workers. This finding contrasts with studies that frame fear primarily as a barrier to HIV testing or initial enrolment. 27 Instead, the results suggest that fear functions as a mechanism that limits access to comprehensive care over time, reinforcing patterns of minimal engagement that undermine long-term treatment outcomes.

Within the Behavioral Model of Healthcare Utilization, fear operates at the level of predisposing and perceived need factors. 10 Participants’ emotional responses shaped how they assessed the necessity and desirability of engaging fully with available services, even when those services were accessible.

Religious moral framing and conditional healthcare use

Religious beliefs played a significant role in shaping participants’ access to care. Similar to findings from other sub-Saharan African contexts, HIV infection was often interpreted within religious spaces as a moral failing, intensifying stigma and discouraging disclosure.12,29 This study confirms that religious moral framing continues to influence HIV-related health-seeking behavior in Ghana. Importantly, the findings show that religious influence did not necessarily prevent healthcare utilization altogether. Instead, it structured access in conditional ways. Participants often delayed seeking care until illness became visible or severe, viewing preventive or routine monitoring as unnecessary or inappropriate in the absence of symptoms. This pattern aligns with studies that document delayed care-seeking among people living with HIV in highly moralized social environments. 30

From a theoretical perspective, religious moral framing shapes both predisposing factors and perceived need within the Behavioral Model of Healthcare Utilization. 10 Beliefs about illness, morality, and faith influenced how participants judged the legitimacy of seeking care, thereby constraining access to comprehensive services.

Provider-patient interactions and depth of care

Health workers’ attitudes emerged as a decisive factor influencing the depth of care accessed by participants. Consistent with existing empirical evidence, negative provider behavior reduced trust and discouraged open communication.27,30 However, this study adds nuance by showing that provider attitudes shaped not only whether care was accessed, but also the extent to which care was comprehensive. Participants described limiting their interactions to transactional encounters focused on medication collection when they perceived health workers as judgmental or dismissive. This finding aligns with studies that associate poor provider-patient relationships with reduced adherence and disengagement from care. 31 In this study, however, disengagement was often partial rather than complete, resulting in fragmented care.

Within the Behavioral Model, provider behavior functions as an enabling or constraining factor at the health system level.10,11 Even when services were physically available, negative interactions reduced participants’ ability to benefit fully from those services.

Distance as a strategy for protecting confidentiality

One of the most novel findings of this study is the role of geographical distance as a facilitator of access rather than a barrier. Contrary to conventional health access models that associate proximity with increased utilization, 10 participants deliberately travelled to distant facilities to maintain anonymity and protect confidentiality. This finding resonates with emerging evidence from studies of stigmatized conditions, where patients prioritize privacy over convenience. 29 However, the present study extends this literature by demonstrating how distance becomes an active strategy for sustaining engagement with care in settings where local confidentiality cannot be guaranteed.

While distance enabled access to services, it also imposed additional financial and physical burdens on participants. This highlights a tension between confidentiality and equity, suggesting that decentralization of HIV services alone may not improve access unless confidentiality concerns are adequately addressed.

Health system constraints and the limits of comprehensive care

Structural constraints within health facilities further limited access to comprehensive care. Participants reported narrow clinical encounters and inconsistent access to diagnostic services, particularly viral load testing. Similar challenges have been documented in other resource-constrained settings, where health system capacity limits the delivery of comprehensive HIV services despite high enrolment rates.32,33 The absence of functional diagnostic equipment undermined participants’ confidence in treatment effectiveness and reduced motivation for sustained engagement. Within the healthcare utilization framework, these constraints reflect limitations in enabling factors at the system level. 10 The findings reinforce the argument that enrolment and medication coverage statistics alone are insufficient indicators of effective access to comprehensive care.

Summary, conclusion and recommendations

This study demonstrates that access to comprehensive HIV healthcare in the Tamale Metropolis cannot be understood solely in terms of service availability or clinic attendance. For people living with HIV/AIDS, access is shaped by how care is experienced, interpreted, and negotiated over time. Fear of stigma and social exposure, religious moral interpretations of HIV, and concerns about confidentiality strongly influenced how participants engaged with healthcare services. The findings show that many individuals remained enrolled in care while experiencing significant gaps in what they considered comprehensive healthcare. Provider attitudes and health system limitations further constrained the depth and quality of care received. These dynamics underscore the importance of viewing access not as a binary outcome, but as a process influenced by emotional, social, and structural factors. By foregrounding patients’ lived experiences, this study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of access to comprehensive HIV care in resource-constrained settings. It highlights the need for HIV programs to move beyond enrolment metrics and medication coverage toward approaches that prioritize trust, confidentiality, respectful care, and system reliability.

Based on the findings of this study, some recommendations are proposed to improve access to comprehensive healthcare services for people living with HIV/AIDS. First, psychosocial support should be strengthened within HIV care programs. Routine counselling services should be integrated more consistently into clinic visits to address fear, anxiety, and stigma that persist beyond diagnosis and treatment initiation. Continuous emotional support may help patients engage more fully with care over time. Next, targeted stigma-reduction interventions should involve religious leaders and faith-based organizations. Given the influence of religious moral framing on healthcare decisions, engaging faith communities in HIV education and advocacy may reduce judgment and promote supportive environments for care-seeking.

Also, health worker training should prioritize respectful communication, confidentiality, and patient-centered care. Improving provider-patient relationships is critical for encouraging open dialogue and fuller utilization of available services. Further, health system capacity should be strengthened to support comprehensive care delivery. This includes ensuring functional diagnostic equipment, particularly for viral load testing, and expanding the scope of clinical encounters beyond medication refills to include regular monitoring and holistic assessment.

Finally, HIV service decentralization strategies should explicitly address confidentiality concerns. While bringing services closer to communities is important, mechanisms must be in place to protect patient privacy so that proximity does not inadvertently discourage care-seeking.

Limitations of the study

This study was conducted in three public hospitals within the Tamale Metropolis, thus, the findings may not fully reflect the experiences of people living with HIV/AIDS who receive care from private facilities, community-based organizations, or informal sources of support. The perspectives of individuals who are not currently engaged in care were also not captured. Also, the qualitative design and purposive sampling approach were appropriate for exploring lived experiences in depth, but they limit the generalizability of the findings. The study does not aim to produce statistically representative results. Instead, it provides context-specific insights into how access to comprehensive healthcare is experienced and negotiated by participants in this setting.

Third, the study relied on self-reported accounts of experiences with healthcare services. Participants’ narratives may have been influenced by recall bias or social desirability, particularly given the sensitive nature of HIV-related stigma, religious beliefs, and interactions with health workers. However, the use of in-depth interviews and assurances of confidentiality helped to encourage openness and reduce these effects. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of the study captures experiences at a single point in time. Access to comprehensive healthcare is dynamic and may change as individuals’ health status, social circumstances, or health system conditions evolve. Longitudinal qualitative research could provide further insight into how access to comprehensive HIV care changes over time.
