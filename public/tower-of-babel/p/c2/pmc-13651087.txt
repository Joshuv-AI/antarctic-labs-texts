Three ct-miRNA signature predicts disease outcome in early breast cancer women
Authors: Giulia Cosentino, Mara Lecchi, Marta Giussani, Valentina Fogazzi, Angela Galardi, Claudia Tottone, Elisa Dell’Orto, Serenella M. Pupa, Paolo Verderio, Elda Tagliabue, Marilena V. Iorio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer is still a leading cause of tumor mortality in women. Indeed, despite advancements in early diagnosis, molecular profiling and novel therapeutic approaches, the disease outcome is still not always predictable. This evidence underlines the need for validated biomarkers to predict recurrences, to personalize both disease monitoring and tailored therapies. And to this aim, miRNAs have shown promising applications as circulating biomarkers. Starting from plasma samples collected from women with early-stage breast cancer at the time of diagnosis, we explored the expression of ct-miRNAs to define a molecular signature predictive of recurrence.MethodsTwo independent cohorts of plasma samples were retrospectively and prospectively collected at Fondazione IRCCS Istituto Nazionale dei Tumori di Milano (INT) for a total of 203 patients. Ct-miRNAs were previously profiled by using the OpenArray Human microRNA panel (OA) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Relapse-free survival (RFS) was analyzed using Cox regression models adjusted for cohort to assess associations with clinicopathological variables and circulating miRNA levels. Models performance was evaluated using c-statistics (95% Confidence interval) and an internal validation was performed with bootstrap resamples. Clinicopathological variables were added to the signature in multivariate models to evaluate their independent prognostic value.ResultsWe identified a three ct-miRNA (miR-125b, miR-26b-3p and miR-532-5p) signature associated with disease outcome, with a hazard ratio of 2.803 (95% CI, 1.721–4.565). The c-statistic was 0.72 (95%, CI 0.62–0.83) and was confirmed by the resampling procedure, with a c-median bootstrap statistic of 0.73 (IQR, 0.65–0.81). The 3-circulating miRNA signature retained its significant prognostic performance with respect to RFS even after the inclusion of clinicopathological variables in multivariate models. Considering both ct-miRNA signature and Nottingham Prognostic Index (NPI), the c-statistic of the bivariate model was equal to 0.80 (95% CI, 0.70; 0.90).ConclusionWe identified a three ct-miRNA prognostic signature in early breast cancer women. Considering the accessibility and stability of ct-miRNAs, this signature might improve the recurrence risk prediction identifying who, despite the early diagnosis, might need a more intensive screening or secondary prevention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13058-026-02344-2.

Background

Breast cancer (BC) is still a leading cause of cancer mortality in women, despite the increased awareness of the need of early diagnosis and the constant search for new therapeutic approaches and predictive biomarkers.

Even when the neoplasm is diagnosed at an early stage, and chances to cure it are higher, unfortunately the disease outcome can vary and is not always predictable. Indeed approximately 20–30% of early stage breast cancer cases will eventually experience recurrence and develop distant metastases [1]. Several prognostic factors have been described, either tumor-intrinsic or host-related, however the prediction accuracy is often limited. Several models to predict BC risk are available, as Gail [2], which considers clinical factors, biopsy history and family history; Claus [3], based only on family history; and Breast Cancer Intervention Study (IBIS) [4], which integrates more detailed family history and higher number of clinical factors.

Each model has however several limitations. One example is represented by hormone-receptor luminal-like tumors. Luminal B tumors, highly proliferative and/or characterized by overexpression of HER2 in absence of amplification, have a worse prognosis. In addition to clinicopathological features, gene expression signatures as MammaPrint, Oncotype DX, EndoPredict, Prosigna, and Breast Cancer Index are used to estimate the risk of recurrence [5]. However, more than 5% of patients classified as ‘low-risk’ according to these gene expression signatures progress within five years, whereas most (70%) of ‘high-risk’ patients never develop metastatic disease [6].

Moreover, limited studies have described circulating (ct) biomarkers able to predict disease outcome (as CTC -circulating tumor cells, ctDNA etc.). Despite the increasing interest, this analysis in early BC has been limited due to low levels of ctDNA shedding in this setting and the relatively low sensitivity of available techniques [7]. Concerning CTC, to date no data have demonstrated the clinical utility in early BC, likely due to the undetectable levels of CTCs in early stage of the disease [8].

MicroRNAs are small non-coding RNA molecules regulating gene expression at post-transcription level, mainly through unperfect base pairing to 3’UTR of target mRNAs. A single miRNA can simultaneously regulate hundreds of targets, orchestrating crucial biological signaling pathways. MiRNAs have been described as promising biomarkers for diagnosis as well as prediction of response to treatments and survival. Produced and released by both neoplastic cells and components of tumor microenvironment, unbound or bound to proteins, or packaged into extracellular vesicles as exosomes, they can be detected in biological fluids as plasma, and are relatively stable in the circulation. Ct-miRNAs have been shown to mirror tumor and host features and holds a great promise as a non-invasive liquid biopsy. In breast cancer patients, a correlation between tumor tissue miRNAs and serum miRNAs has been described [9, 10], however the cells of origin are unknown and might be both tumor tissue and host cells.

Our previous analysis identified 5 ct-miRNAs that, when properly combined, distinguished malignant from benign breast disease in women with a high likelihood of malignancy [11]. In addition to the potential role as early diagnostic biomarkers, miRNAs are also associated to disease outcome, and the possibility to use ct-miRNAs as non-invasive tool to predict disease recurrence is of high interest.

Farina et al. have described a predictive miRNA signature for breast cancer risk, however the cohort are women at high risk due to family reasons [12]. Lambrechts and colleagues [13] identified a multi-modal biomarker model (including circulating miRNAs but also chemokines etc.) at diagnosis which correlates with distant metastases, focused on early luminal-like breast cancer. Also, in TNBC, a 4-miRNA signature was associated with tumor relapse and overall survival [14].

Given the availability of a substantial number of plasma samples derived from women with early-stage BC, we decided to explore to expression of ct-miRNAs to define a molecular signature predictive of recurrence.

Materials and methods

Breast cancer cohorts: patients and plasma samples

Two independent cohorts of plasma samples were retrospectively (1, Retrospective Cohort) and prospectively (2, BABE—BreAst Blood Early diagnosis) collected at Fondazione IRCCS Istituto Nazionale dei Tumori di Milano (INT) between 2013 and 2017. For the Retrospective Cohort, 100 plasma samples were collected from women with breast carcinoma before surgery between 2013 and 2015, stored in the Biobank of INT and Institutional approval from our independent ethics committee was obtained for this study (approval numbers INT111-13, INT144-14).

BABE samples were collected between 2015 and 2017 from women with suspicious imaging findings (BI-RADS 4–5) who underwent biopsy to obtain a correct histopathological diagnosis of malignant or benign disease. Institutional approval from our independent ethics committee was obtained for this study (approval numbers INT66-15). Patients gave informed consent to the use of their samples before a core biopsy. All procedures were conducted per the Declaration of Helsinki. Ct-miRNA profile was previously performed, by using the Open Array Human microRNA panel (OA), (Thermo Fisher Scientific), a fixed-content panel containing 754 validated human TaqMan miR assays, with the aim to identify an early diagnostic signature able to discriminate carcinoma from breast lesions [11].

For the purpose of this study, only patients with available follow-up data were included. Accordingly, 93 patients from the Retrospective Cohort and 110 patients from the BABE Cohort were pooled and analyzed together to ensure a sufficient number of relapse events for the prognostic analysis, while accounting for their cohort of origin.

Patients characteristics are described in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Relapse-free survival (RFS) was defined as time from sample collection to first cancer relapse. The associations between clinico-pathological variables or ct-miRNAs levels (RQ considered on log2 scale) and RFS were assessed by using Cox regression models using penalized maximum likelihood estimations according to Firth method [15] to account for the limited number of events per variable (EPV) [16, 17] and by adjusting for cohort. The relationship between each identified miRNA and clinical outcome was investigated by restricted cubic splines [18]. Cox regression models were fitted for each variable of interest (miRNAs or clinico-pathological variables), adjusting for cohort.

Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to evaluate correlation between the identified miRNAs.

The associations between individually miRNA expressions (or miRNA signature) and clinical variables were assessed through the ANOVA model adjusted by cohort variable.

Starting from circulating miRNAs associated with RFS and identified as hemolysis-free based on a conclusive forced hemolysis experiment [19], only those miRNAs detected in more than 10 events, not mutually correlated (limit of the 95% Confidence Interval [CI] > 0.70 in absolute value) and not associated with any clinical variables, were included in multivariate Cox regression models following the all-subset approach [20] using penalized maximum likelihood estimations according to Firth method.

For each model, the c-statistic (and its 95% CI) was used as pivotal measure for performance evaluation and a bootstrap procedure with 100 resamplings having a median follow up equal to that observed in the original dataset and comparable number of events and non-events, will be computed. The median of the bootstrap c-statistics distribution with the corresponding interquartile range (IQR) were compared to the original one.

Extended (multivariate) Cox models were generated by individually adding clinico-pathological variable to the selected signature, the performances of which were then valuated in terms of c-statistics. All statistical analyses were carried out with SAS Studio (version 5.2; SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA) by adopting a significance alpha level of 5%.

Results

Among the 203 BC patients, tumors were predominantly of grade II (49.8%) and classified as pN0 (or i+) (65.5%) and pT1 (74.9%). Luminal A/B were the predominant subtypes (69,0%), followed by HER2-related subtypes (13.3%), triple negative (11.3%), and in situ lesions (5.4%). The majority of tumors were ER-positive (79.8%), PR-positive (70.4%) and HER2 status ‘low’ (1 + or 2+/FISH neg) in 63% of cases, Ki-67 was positive in 70.0% of patients. RT ± HT (RT alone or RT + HT) was the most frequent treatment (32.0%), followed by CT ± HT ± RT (28.1%, CT alone, CT + HT, CT + RT, CT + HT + RT, and BEVA + CT + HT), ‘No therapy’ included 3 patients who declined treatment (Table 1). The cohort has a median follow-up time of 80 months (interquartile range: 31–98 months) and were observed 32 relapse events (12 local, 37.5%). Ct-miRNA profile was previously performed [11]. Table 2 reported the 15 circulating miRNAs that resulted significantly associated with RFS by cohort-adjusted analysis. For all these circulating miRNAs, a linear relationship between the RFS probability and their expression was found by resorting to a restricted cubic splines approach. According to the forced hemolysis experiment, 8 miRNAs were confidently classified as hemolysis-free. No significant correlations were identified between the miRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 1), whereas 4 miRNAs showed to be associated with some clinical variables (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 2).

By combining the 4 miRNAs, not associated with any clinical variables (Table 2), a total of 11 promising prognostic signatures including 2, 3 or 4 miRNAs were assessed. Models with highest c-statistics tended to be applied on fewer patients, so these models are constrained by the inclusion of miRNAs with inconsistent detection, which limits their future applicability and generalizability. To note, 4 models could be fitted by exploiting data from more than 80% of samples (> 163 subjects) (Fig. 1a).

The model ‘F’ including miR-125b, miR-26b-3p (hsa-miR-26b#-002444), miR-532-5p was selected as the “best” one, since characterized by the highest c-index between the most applicable models. The c-statistic of the 3-circulating miRNA signature computed over 165 patients (of which 24 recurrences) was equal to 0.72 (95% CI 0.62–0.83), and a c-median bootstrap statistic of 0.73 (IQR 0.65–0.81) was obtained by resampling (Fig. 1b). The hazard ratio (HR) of the 3-miRNA based selected signature was equal to 2.803 (1.721–4.565) and the corresponding RFS probability curve is shown in Fig. 2. By evaluating the prognostic value of clinical variables, ER status, grade, Ki-67, subtype, adjuvant therapy and the Nottingham Prognostic Index (NPI) [21] were found to be associated with RFS as reported in Table 3. The highest discriminative performance was observed for NPI (c-index = 0.745; 95%CI 0.666–0.824), followed by tumor subtype (c-index = 0.723; 95%CI 0.638–0.809) and grade (c-index = 0.700; 95%CI 0.623–0.775).

The miRNA signature showed no significant association with any baseline clinical variables (prior to treatment initiation) (Supplementary Table 2). Bivariate models, including the miRNA signature and each of the available clinico-pathological variable, were built to evaluate the role of each component. Across all bivariate models, the 3-ct-miRNA signature always retained its prognostic performance with respect to RFS. Among the clinical variables included in these adjusted models, only grade and NPI remained significantly associated with RFS, whereas ER status, Ki-67, molecular subtype and therapy lost statistical significance after accounting for the signature (Fig. 3; Table 4). Specifically, NPI demonstrated good prognostic discrimination (c-index = 0.745, 95% CI 0.666–0.824), similarly, the miRNA signature alone showed a c-index of 0.721 (95% CI 0.615–0.827), but the combined model incorporating both, achieved the highest performance (c-index = 0.797, 95% CI 0.696–0.899), indicating that the miRNA signature adds complementary prognostic information to the clinical risk stratification provided by the NPI.

Discussion

This work underlines how profile of ct-miRNAs in early BC patients before any intervention already holds the information of disease outcome.

There is apparently a molecular predisposition, already determined and present at the baseline which -if correctly identified- might provide indication for more intense monitoring, follow up or aggressive treatment.

Indeed, despite the overall better prognosis of early diagnosed BC, nearly 30% will eventually recur. Disease outcome is mostly predicted by clinical variables, and even when molecular biomarkers are available (as for instance Oncotype Dx in luminal BC), the prediction accuracy is far from perfect, raising the issue and the urgent need for new tools.

We identified a 3 ct-miRNA prognostic signature. The expression levels of these 3 miRNAs at the baseline in early BC patients is able to predict disease outcome, underlining that even at the very beginning of the disease development, when the tumor is localized, either neoplastic cells, components of the tumor microenvironment and/or host features hold the potential to cause recurrence. Importantly however, they also hold the information able to predict which patients will eventually relapse, information crucial to suggest a stricter intervention or follow up. Huo and colleagues have previously identified a ct-miRNA signature predictive of recurrence which retained the significance adjusting for clinical variables, however the analysis was performed on a series of BC patients heterogeneous for disease stage, with smaller sample size [22]. In addition, serum samples for some recurrent patients were collected at the time of recurrence, whereas for others they were obtained around the time of cancer diagnosis. The reported median time from diagnosis to recurrence of cases was 2.3 years but time to event was not considered in the analyses. Differently we focused on a cohort of early BC patients with all serum samples collected at the time of diagnosis and we defined a prognostic signature associating basal ct-miRNA expression to recurrence over time.

Interestingly, the predictive capacity of our ct-miRNA signature (c-index = 0.72) exceeded that of tumor grade (c-index: 0.70) and was only slightly lower than that of the Nottingham Prognostic Index (NPI; c-index = 0.74).

Even more noteworthy is the fact that the ct-miRNA signature, combined with NPI, achieved a predictive performance for recurrence approaching 0.80. Since NPI reflects the characteristics of neoplastic cells, it can be speculated that the three circulating miRNAs may capture additional tumor features that are independent of differentiation grade, lymph node involvement, and tumor size, and/or a specific interaction with the patient that may promote tumor recurrence. In addition, the fact that the ct-miRNA signature retained its significance even when analyzed in combination with the type of adjuvant therapy received by the patients suggests that these three circulating miRNAs reflect an underlying pathobiological context that extends beyond the effects of treatments selected on the basis of tumor clinicopathological parameters, as occurs in clinical practice.

A limitation of our study is the lack of an independent validation cohort. To partially address this, we applied bootstrap resampling with confidence intervals, which provides a robust internal assessment of model stability and helps mitigate the risk of overfitting. Nonetheless, external validation in an independent cohort would further strengthen the generalizability of our findings. In addition, given the retrospective use of data from a study originally designed for other purposes, these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating; accordingly, formal type I and II error considerations are not fully applicable as in a prospectively designed confirmatory study.

Among the 3 miRNAs of the signature, miR-125b maps on chromosome 11q24.1, and in this locus, it is part of a cluster including the LET7A2 and MIR100 genes. Interestingly, circulating miR-125b-5p has been found deregulated during the process of NeuT carcinogenesis in mice [23]. In patients, circulating miR-125b-5p and miR-143-3p were upregulated in BC plasma and able to discriminate BC patients from healthy subjects [24]. This is a bit surprising, since this miRNA was initially reported as downregulated in BC in comparison with normal tissue [25]. However, there is contradictory evidence in literature concerning its functional role: a miR-125b cancer suppressive function has been described, consistent with the observed downregulated expression, as for instance the capacity to inhibit the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) through MAP2K7 (Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase Kinase 7) [26] in TNBC or to impair Treg function by targeting TNFR2 and FoxP3 [27, 28]. Nevertheless, different reports indicate a role in tumor progression [29, 30] and resistance to therapy [31]. Preliminary data indicate indeed that this miRNA is induced upon proliferative signaling (EGF treatment) in a model of breast cancer, consistently with the association with poor prognosis observed in our dataset. Future biological studies would certainly clarify whether these circulating microRNAs can be only exploited as biomarkers or they actually play a functional role, and which is the cell of origin responsible for production and release in the circulation.

Tissue miR-532-5p has been associated to poor prognosis in TNBC [32]. Interestingly, circulating miR-532-5p and − 3p have been described as diagnostic biomarkers for BC [33]. At a functional point of view, miR-532-5p promotes BC proliferation and migration by targeting RERG (ras-related and estrogen-regulated growth inhibitor), a tumor suppressor in BC [34]. However, this miRNA has also been reported as tumor suppressor in other tumor types, as osteosarcoma, where it impairs the MAPK/ERK, NF-κB, PI3K/AKT pathways through direct targeting of CXCL2 (chemokine C-X-C motif ligand) [35].

MiR-26b is highly expressed on adaptive (B and T cells) and innate (macrophages and NK cells) immune components. Even though in our dataset is associated with an increased risk of event (HR = 1.37), miR-26b has been mainly described as an oncosuppressive miRNA [36, 37], in particular in luminal BC through the regulation of ER pathway by direct targeting of CDH1 (Cadherin1), GREB1 (Growth Regulating Estrogen Receptor Binding 1) e KPNA2 (Karyopherin Alpha 2) [38]. Only more recently the attention has been focused on both miRNA strands, including the − 3p, which also plays a tumor suppressive role in BC regulating the circWWC3/Ras pathway [39]. In detail, circWWC3 acts as a sponge for miR-26b-3p, which is able to target and inhibit Ras pathway.

Conclusions

We have identified a 3-ct-miRNA signature able to discriminate between early BC women who relapse and cancer-free over a median follow-up of 80 months (IQR 31–98), to be further validated in an independent cohort.

Considering the accessibility and stability of ct-miRNAs, this signature might improve the recurrence risk prediction in early BC women, identifying those who, despite the early diagnosis, might need a more intensive screening or secondary prevention strategies.
