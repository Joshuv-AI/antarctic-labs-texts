Maternal Dyslipidaemia Aggravates Offspring Metabolic Dysfunction–Associated Steatotic Liver Disease
Authors: Kamilla Pedersen, Sanne Fredslund Buhl, Cecilie Højgaard‐Jensen, Jens Lykkesfeldt, Pernille Tveden‐Nyborg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPaediatric metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and steatohepatitis (MASH) display a distinct and severe histopathology. Maternal metabolic status may program offspring susceptibility to MASLD. This study investigated if maternal dyslipidaemia and low vitamin C (VitC) status influence offspring MASLD using a validated guinea pig model. Thirty female guinea pigs received a low‐fat (LF) diet until pregnancy was confirmed, then remained on LF or switched to a high‐fat (HF) diet with or without reduced VitC. Offspring were evaluated at birth and after 10 weeks on postweaning HF or LF diets. In dams, HF diet induced dyslipidaemia and elevated hepatic triglycerides and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) compared to LF dams, without causing obesity. Newborns exposed to maternal HF diet showed increased hepatic triglycerides, liver‐to‐body weight ratio and ALT. By 10 weeks, liver fibrosis and MASLD activity scores were higher in offspring maintained on a postnatal HF diet compared to LF. Notably, offspring viability was markedly reduced on maternal HF diet with low VitC. These findings demonstrate that even short exposure to maternal HF diet, independent of obesity, can adversely affect offspring MASLD risk and highlight the need for early prevention. Additionally, findings support the need to investigate interactions between maternal diet, VitC status and offspring survival.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and its progressive form, steatohepatitis (MASH), are closely linked to obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia and cardiovascular disease [1]. MASLD is the most prevalent chronic liver disease in children and adolescents, affecting an estimated 5%–10% of the global paediatric population and over one third of obese children [2]. Notably, paediatric MASH differs from its adult counterpart in both histopathology and severity, often presenting with a more aggressive phenotype [3, 4]. Alarmingly, the incidence of paediatric MASLD has risen rapidly in the past decade [5], with early‐life factors suggested to play a key role in driving this increase.

The Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) theory proposes that intrauterine and early postnatal exposures shape long‐term health outcomes [6]. In line with this concept, epidemiological and animal studies indicate that an adverse maternal metabolic state increases the offspring's risk of MASLD [7, 8, 9, 10]. This risk may be driven by excess maternal nutrient exposure in utero, combined with placental and foetal oxidative stress and inflammation [11]. An unfavourable intrauterine environment can lead to lasting epigenetic and metabolic modifications that predispose the offspring to metabolic disease later in life [12].

The link between maternal obesity, a high‐fat (HF) diet during pregnancy and offspring MASLD risk has been studied across species [8, 9, 10]. However, maternal dyslipidaemia—rather than obesity per se—may be a key driver of foetal programming of MASLD, yet the independent contribution of dyslipidaemia to disease development remains unclear [7, 11]. Because foetal hepatic de novo lipogenesis and fatty acid oxidation are minimal [13], maternal lipids transferred via the placenta are the primary source of foetal liver fat [11]. Moreover, foetal adipose tissue develops late in gestation, making excess foetal energy more likely to be stored ectopically in the liver rather than in adipose depots [11]. As circulating lipids are closely linked to diet [14], maternal nutrition during pregnancy may play a crucial role in foetal programming of MASLD.

Most human and animal studies investigating foetal programming in MASLD focus on maternal prepregnancy obesity [8, 9, 10]. To isolate the effect of a HF diet during pregnancy, this study introduced the HF diet only after pregnancy was confirmed. Guinea pigs were selected as a high validity animal model due to their human‐like lipoprotein profile and lipid metabolism, which closely mirror the changes observed in humans when exposed to a HF diet [15]. Additionally, guinea pigs share key reproductive traits with humans, including a similar placental structure, metabolic function and nutrient transfer [16]. Their relevance as a MASLD/MASH model is further supported by their liver histopathology, fibrosis progression and hepatic transcriptomic profiles, which closely resemble those of humans [17]. Furthermore, recent findings from our group demonstrate that juvenile guinea pigs develop MASH with histopathological features and disease severity comparable to paediatric MASH in humans, yet distinct from the adult guinea pig phenotype [18].

Because MASLD is strongly associated with diets high in fat and carbohydrates—often low in fresh fruits and vegetables—micronutrient deficiencies may contribute to disease progression. Both obesity and a HF diet have been associated with low VitC levels, and plasma VitC inversely correlates with MASLD severity in adults [19, 20]. Unlike most mammals, guinea pigs and humans lack endogenous VitC synthesis and depend entirely on dietary intake [21]. Guinea pig studies suggest that low‐VitC status exacerbates liver inflammation [17, 18]. Additionally, VitC‐dependent ten–eleven translocation (TET) enzymes facilitate DNA demethylation, an essential process during foetal development, and may be influenced by VitC availability [18, 22].

This study tested the hypothesis that maternal dyslipidaemia during pregnancy exacerbates hepatic endpoints of early‐life MASLD in offspring, with further aggravation by low VitC levels, using guinea pigs as a highly translational model.

Methods

In Vivo Experiments

All in vivo experiments were approved by the Danish Animal Experiments Inspectorate (licence numbers 2021‐15‐0201‐00892 and 2021‐15‐0204‐00007) and the Institutional Ethics Committee of University of Copenhagen (Protocol P23‐286, 09.05.2023) and conducted under European animal experimentation regulations (Directive 2010/63/EU). The study was conducted in accordance with the Basic & Clinical Pharmacology & Toxicology policy for experimental and clinical studies [23].

Upon arrival, 30 nulliparous female guinea pigs (450–500 g) and three males (600–650 g) (Charles River Laboratories, Lyon, France) were subcutaneously microchipped (E‐vet, Haderslev, Denmark) and randomly assigned to three weight‐stratified groups (10 females, one male per group). They were housed in floor pens with wood shavings, shelters, enrichment and a 12‐h light–dark cycle at 20°C–24°C. All animals had ad libitum access to water, hay and a low‐fat (LF) pelleted diet (3.8% fat, 0% sucrose, 0% cholesterol and 1500 mg VitC/kg feed) (see Section 2.2) [24]. Welfare was monitored twice daily.

After 10 days of acclimatization, females were weighed, shaved and ultrasound scanned (Logiq vet P10, GE Healthcare, Brøndby, Denmark) to detect pregnancy. Weekly scans were performed to detect pregnancy and monitor foetal development. Pregnant females were transferred to separate pens and either remained on the LF diet or switched to high fat (HF; 20% fat, 15% sucrose, 0.35% cholesterol and 1500 mg VitC/kg feed) or HF with low‐VitC diet (HFLC; 100 mg VitC/kg feed). Diet allocation was randomized by body weight at arrival and boar, ensuring that the three males were evenly distributed across maternal diet groups to avoid sire bias.

To prevent fertility bias in HF and HFLC groups, intervention was initiated after confirmed pregnancy. Obesity is a risk factor for infertility in humans and rodents [25, 26], while low VitC may impair conception rates in guinea pigs [27]. Group housing prevented timed and controlled matings [27], and copulatory plugs are an unreliable indicator of mating in guinea pigs. These constraints made pregnancy detection reliant on ultrasound, which is feasible no earlier than ~3 weeks after conception [27], but in practice, pregnancy was confirmed at approximately 4–5 weeks of gestation for most guinea pigs.

Within 24 h of birth, all pups were recorded; dams and viable pups were weighed. Pups were microchipped and sexed. Dams and pups remained on the pregnancy diet until weaning at 1 week of age, after which pups were randomized to LF or HF diet based on dam and sex to minimize bias. Diets continued for ~10 weeks (70–75 days), yielding five offspring groups: LFdam/LFpup (n = 8), LFdam/HFpup (n = 12), HFdam/LFpup (n = 8), HFdam/HFpup (n = 10) and HFLCdam/HFpup (n = 6) (Figure 1).

Study design. Thirty nulliparous female guinea pigs were mated with three males (1 male per 10 females) while on a low fat (LF) diet. Upon confirmation of pregnancy via ultrasound scanning, females were transferred to new pens and either remained on the LF diet or were switched to a high fat diet (HF) or high fat, low‐VitC diet (HFLC). In the HFLC group, one dam died spontaneously, and three miscarried, leaving six dams, from which only six viable pups were born (see Section 3.1). Within 24 h of birth, three dams and five and seven neonatal pups from the LF and HF groups, respectively, were euthanized (♰). In the HFLC group, two dams were euthanized 1 week postpartum when their offspring were weaned. The remaining pups were weaned after 1 week and randomized to either an LF or HF diet for 10 weeks before euthanasia. This resulted in the following five offspring groups: maternal LF diet and offspring LF diet (LFdam/LFpup, n = 8); maternal LF diet and offspring HF diet (LFdam/HFpup, n = 12); maternal HF diet and offspring LF diet (HFdam/LFpup, n = 8); maternal HF diet and offspring HF diet (HFdam/HFpup, n = 10); and maternal HFLC diet and offspring HF diet (HFLCdam/HFpup, n = 6).

Three dams in the LF and HF groups and their corresponding neonatal pups (LF: n = 5; HF: n = 7) were euthanized within 24 h of delivery. The neonatal pups originated exclusively from these dams. The number of dams included at this timepoint was intentionally low to reduce overall animal use in accordance with the 3R principles, while still ensuring sufficient power for the primary study endpoints. These data are intended as reference values for the immediate postparturition period and were not powered for statistical comparison. Dams were stratified by sire before random selection for euthanasia. Two HFLC dams, but no neonatal pups, were euthanized after weaning due to unexpectedly low delivery of live pups (see Section 2.3).

Group sizes were based on variances in fibrosis quantification from previous studies with a significance level < 0.05 and a power of 0.80, rendering eight guinea pigs per group sufficient to detect histopathological differences in the liver [18, 28]. However, distinguishing differences between HF‐fed groups requires a larger sample size due to smaller expected effect sizes [18]. Twelve HF offspring per group were targeted. Assuming 3.4 pups per nulliparous dam [27], 10 females per group allowed for litter variation and ensured enough dams to reduce maternal effect confounding. This design accounted for the expected need to euthanize neonatal pups (3 dams and ~10 pups per group), with approximately 12 offspring continuing on the HF diet and a minimum of eight on the LF diet for 10 weeks.

Feed

Feed was stored at −18°C and thawed weekly to prevent auto‐oxidation and preserve palatability. Diets were chow‐based, supplemented with phosphorylated ascorbate (Stay‐C), and VitC content was verified postproduction (Ssniff Spezialdiaten GmbH, Soest, Germany). The LF diet (1500 mg VitC/kg) was formulated by mixing feed containing 1700 mg VitC/kg (S9406‐S042) with VitC‐free feed (S9406‐S043). The HF diet (1500 mg VitC/kg) was obtained by titrating 2100 mg VitC/kg feed (S9406‐S0253) with 220 mg VitC/kg feed (S9406‐S0256). The HFLC diet (100 mg VitC/kg) was prepared by mixing 220 mg VitC/kg feed with VitC‐free feed (S9406‐S0255). A 1500 mg VitC/kg feed dose ensured VitC saturation [29], 100 mg VitC/kg feed previously has induced a nonscorbutic VitC deficiency in pregnant guinea pigs [27, 30]. HF groups were fed ad libitum, while LF groups were restricted to two‐thirds of the caloric intake of HF groups throughout the dietary intervention (pregnancy, lactation and the postweaning period) to ensure a metabolically healthy control group. Pair‐feeding was used to prevent excess caloric intake and accompanying weight gain in LF dams, which we and others have shown can occur with ad libitum low‐fat feeding and in turn may compromise metabolic homeostasis [24, 31]. As the LF diet contained two‐thirds of the caloric density but the same VitC concentration per gram as the HF diet (1500 mg VitC/kg), pair‐feeding based on gram intake resulted in identical VitC intake between LF and HF groups [28].

Offspring Viability and Dietary Adjustments

One HFLC dam was found dead 1 week after pregnancy confirmation resulting in n = 9 dams. Autopsy revealed intrauterine haemorrhage without any other pathology. This event was considered accidental and unrelated to diet, as pathophysiologically relevant low‐VitC status requires ≥ 3 weeks to develop [32].

Three HFLC dams miscarried after ≥ 2 weeks on the diet, identified as foetal death on ultrasound and subsequent delivery with no pups found—likely due to maternal cannibalism of underdeveloped foetuses, a common occurrence in guinea pigs. In response, HFLC VitC was first increased from 100 to 125 mg/kg and then to 200 mg/kg after the second miscarriage. Despite this, a third miscarriage occurred shortly after. Among the six remaining HFLC dams, two had nonviable foetuses detected days to weeks predelivery, and three others delivered at least one stillborn pup. Stillborn pups appeared fully developed with varying sizes, including two > 100 g. All six live‐born HFLC pups were clinically healthy with appropriate postnatal weight gain. No HFLC dam showed signs of scurvy or illness.

In the HF group, four stillborn pups were delivered by one dam, with intact foetal membranes covering the foetal nose/mouth, suggesting asphyxia.

Euthanasia

Before euthanasia, guinea pigs were semifasted overnight, that is, with access to hay and water but not feed. Preanaesthesia was induced with a Zoletil mixture (1.25 mL/kg), containing 125 mg tiletamine, 125 mg zolazepam (Zoletil 50 vet; Virbac, Nice, France), 200 mg xylazine (Narcoxyl vet 20 mg/mL; Intervet International, Boxmeer, the Netherlands) and 7.5 mg butorphanol (Torbugesic vet 10 mg/mL; Scanvet, Fredensborg, Denmark) diluted 10–fold in isotonic saline before subcutaneous administration. After 15–20 min, anaesthesia was maintained with 3%–5% inhaled isoflurane (Isoba vet 100%, Intervet International, København, Denmark). Once interdigital reflexes were absent, intracardiac blood collection was performed, followed by euthanasia via decapitation. Offspring euthanized after 10 weeks were euthanized over the course of 3 days in a blinded, randomized manner. For dam‐neonate euthanasia, families were kept together, and dams were euthanized last to minimize offspring distress.

Plasma Samples

Intracardiac blood was collected using 5‐ or 10‐mL syringes preflushed with K3‐EDTA and transferred into K3‐EDTA‐coated vacutainers. Samples were centrifuged at 2000 × g for 4 min at 4°C, and the plasma was stored at −80°C until analysis. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aminotransferase (AST), gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), triglycerides (TG) and total cholesterol (TC) were measured using an Atellica CH 930 Analyzer (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Liver Samples

The liver was excised, rinsed with cold phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS; 140 mM NaCl, 10 mM phosphate, 3 mM KCl, pH 7.4, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), weighed and photographed. Tissue from the left lateral lobe was sliced (3–5 mm) for histology and preserved in 10% formalin. Additional sections were placed on dry ice and stored at −80°C for biochemical assessments. Hepatic TG content was assessed using a colorimetric assay kit (Item No. 10010303; Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Liver was homogenized in NP40 Substitute Assay Reagent with HALT protease inhibitor (EDTA‐free; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Roskilde, Denmark), then centrifuged (10 000 × g, 10 min, 4°C). The supernatant was diluted fivefold in assay reagent and analyzed in triplicate under blinded, randomized conditions per manufacturer's guidelines. For VitC quantification, liver was homogenized in cold Dulbecco's PBS (D8537; Sigma‐Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany), stabilized in 10% MPA‐EDTA and centrifuged (16 000 × g, 1 min, 4°C). Supernatant was stored at −80°C and analyzed by HPLC. Only livers from dams, perinatal offspring and stillborns were assessed for VitC.

Liver Histology

Liver sections (2–4 μm) were prepared from formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded samples and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or Picro Sirius Red (PSR). Sections were assessed in blinded, randomized order for MASLD severity using Kleiner et al. guidelines [33], adapted for guinea pigs [18]. To ensure scoring reliability, 10 randomly selected sections were reblinded and rescored. Scoring proceeded only if Cohen's weighted kappa index met thresholds (≥ 0.8 for steatosis, ballooning and fibrosis; ≥ 0.7 for inflammation) [17].

H&E‐stained sections were used to assess steatosis, inflammation and ballooning, and PSR‐stained slides for fibrosis. Steatosis was graded as 0 (< 5%), 1 (5%–33%), 2 (33%–66%) or 3 (> 66%) based on the proportion of lipid‐laden hepatocytes (×5/×10 magnification). Lobular inflammation was scored at ×20 magnification by counting inflammatory foci (≥ 3 adjacent cells) in six lobules: 0 (none), 1 (1 focus/×200 field), 2 (2–4) or 3 (> 4). Portal inflammation was scored at high magnification by counting foci (≥ 5 adjacent cells) in six portal areas: 0 (< 2 foci), 1 (2–3) and 2 (> 3). Ballooning was assessed at ×40: 0 (absent), 1 (few) and 2 (many). The MASLD activity score (MAS) was the sum of steatosis, lobular inflammation and ballooning (range 0–8): 0–2 (no MASLD), 3–4 (borderline) and ≥ 5 (MASH).

Fibrosis was staged as F0 (no fibrosis), F1 (A: mild perisinusoidal, B: moderate perisinusoidal and C: portal/periportal), F2 (perisinusoidal and portal/periportal), F3 (bridging fibrosis between central veins, central and portal veins or between portal veins) and F4 (A: mild cirrhosis, B: moderate cirrhosis and C: severe cirrhosis). Fibrosis quantification was performed using Visiopharm software (Version 2020.08.4.9377, VisioPharm, Hørsholm, Denmark), measuring collagen‐stained area relative to total liver tissue [17].

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.4.1, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Fisher's exact test was applied to dichotomous variables, with results expressed as percentages. For continuous, normally distributed data with homogeneous variances, Student's t‐test, two‐way ANOVA or one‐way ANOVA with Dunnett's multiple comparisons test was applied. Offspring body weights were analyzed using a mixed‐effects model with repeated measures. These data are expressed as means ± standard deviations (SDs). If assumptions were not met, data were transformed before reanalysis. If nonnormality persisted or for categorical data, Kruskal–Wallis' test with Dunn's post hoc test or the Mann–Whitney test was used. Transformed or nonparametric data are presented as medians with quartiles (25th–75th percentile) or ranges for small groups. Data from both sexes were pooled unless sex‐specific effects were observed.

Results

Pregnancy Outcomes

Pregnancy outcomes are presented in Table 1. In the HFLC group, only six live pups were born, compared to 25 in both the LF and HF groups. This finding highlights the significant effect of low VitC intake combined with a HF diet on the ability to deliver viable offspring, as reflected by the higher rates of stillbirths and miscarriages (p = 0.0005 and p = 0.023, respectively, Table 1). It occurred despite doubling the VitC dose compared to previous guinea pig pregnancy studies [27, 30], as described in Section 2.3.

Dams were kept on the pregnancy diet for approximately 5 weeks (Table 1), accounting for about half of the total gestation period, which lasts ~69 days (10 weeks) in guinea pigs [27], as dietary intervention was initiated after ultrasound confirmation of pregnancy. The postpartum dam weights did not differ significantly between the three groups, and post‐mortem inspection of dams euthanized following parturition did not indicate any signs of compromised nutrition. Together this supports that the HF diets did not induce obesity during pregnancy and that the LF pair‐feeding regime did not negatively affect dams or offspring. The live‐born pups of HF dams were marginally smaller than those born to LF dams (p = 0.044, Table 1), whereas the HFLC pups were substantially smaller (p < 0.0001, Table 1).

Liver TG levels also appear elevated in HF and HFLC dams compared to LF, further supported by higher plasma ALT, indicating hepatocellular injury (Table 2).

Neonatal HF pups also exhibited dyslipidaemia, with significantly higher plasma TC (p = 0.003, Table 3). Both HF and HFLC pups had significantly higher liver TG content compared to LF pups (p = 0.045 and p = 0.023, respectively), which was further reflected in a higher liver‐to‐body weight ratio and increased plasma ALT in HF pups compared to LF pups (p = 0.048 and p = 0.011, respectively, Table 3). Although reference values for neonatal guinea pig BUN are lacking, the difference between HF and LF pups does not appear to be biologically relevant [34, 36].

Histopathology for Neonatal Pups

Histopathological scores of neonatal pups were similar in LF and HF pups, except for lobular inflammation, which was higher in LF pups (p = 0.033, Figure S1b). Steatosis scores were high in both groups (Figure S1a), consistent with the liver TG measurements (Table 3), but the categorical scoring system did not capture the between‐group difference observed in the quantitative assessment. Representative macroscopic images of neonatal livers are shown in Figure S2, where steatosis is clearly evident in the two HF livers. Overall, inflammation was minimal, and no fibrosis was detected (Figure S1).

Body Weights for Week 10 Offspring

After 10 weeks of dietary intervention, body weights did not differ significantly between guinea pigs receiving HF and LF diets after weaning (p = 0.075), although the growth curves showed a clear separation, with HF groups clustering together and LF groups clustering (Figure 2a). Despite being significantly lighter at birth, particularly in the HFLC group (Table 1), pups born to HF dams exhibited greater postnatal weight gain, and the initial differences were rapidly eliminated (Figure 2b).

Histopathology for Week 10 Offspring

Histopathological scores are shown in Figure 3, with representative images in Figure 4. Fibrosis scores were significantly higher in the HFdam/HFpup and HFLCdam/HFpup groups compared to controls (LFdam/LFpup) (p = 0.044 and p = 0.001, respectively), whereas the LFdam/HFpup group did not differ from controls (Figure 3f). Fibrosis fraction, quantified as mean ± SD percent, showed no significant group differences: 0.90 ± 0.17 (LFdam/LFpup), 0.87 ± 0.31 (HFdam/LFpup), 0.77 ± 0.17 (LFdam/HFpup), 0.98 ± 0.18 (HFdam/HFpup) and 1.01 ± 0.14 (HFLCdam/HFpup). Only the HFdam/HFpup group had a significantly higher MAS score than controls (p = 0.033) (Figure 3e), primarily driven by higher lobular inflammation. Though not statistically significant, this group also showed numerically higher median lobular and portal inflammation scores compared to LFdam/HFpup, while steatosis and ballooning scores were similar. These elevated inflammatory scores likely contributed to the increased fibrosis observed in this group, reflecting ongoing liver injury with subsequent stimulation of hepatic fibrogenesis.

All histopathological scores are pooled male and female data. No sex‐related differences were observed in individual steatosis, ballooning, lobular or portal inflammation scores. When cumulating the scores, females in the LFdam/HFpup group had a significantly higher MAS score than males (p = 0.030). This sex difference was not present in other groups or when comparing MAS scores across groups.

Collectively, these findings indicate that a maternal HF diet exacerbates MASLD severity and fibrosis in offspring subjected to the same diet, independent of maternal obesity.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that a HF diet during the second half of pregnancy induces maternal dyslipidaemia and hypercholesterolaemia, and increases liver TG content and plasma markers of liver injury in neonatal offspring. When these offspring continued on a HF diet for 10 weeks postweaning, the adverse in utero environment further exacerbated MASLD and fibrosis development. Additionally, this study highlights that a HF diet with low VitC during the second half of pregnancy severely impaired reproductive outcomes, reducing the ability to produce viable offspring.

The accelerated MASLD development in HF‐fed offspring of HF‐fed dams is consistent with previous animal studies showing that maternal and postweaning HF diets can have additive effects on liver injury [9, 10, 37, 38]. Likewise, it aligns with human research linking maternal obesity to increased MASLD risk in children [8, 39, 40]. The novelty of the present study lies in introducing the HF diet during pregnancy, allowing the evaluation of dietary effects independent of obesity and prepregnancy metabolic status. Baseline data from postpartum dams and neonatal pups clearly show that in utero dyslipidaemia was transferred to offspring, leading to intrahepatic lipid accumulation and liver injury, as reflected by elevated ALT levels. Similar clinical findings show intrahepatic TG content in infants correlate with maternal prepregnancy BMI and gestational diabetes [41]. High maternal plasma triglycerides, fatty acids and glucose have also been linked to increased infant adiposity [42, 43], suggesting excess maternal nutrition is passed to the foetus. In human studies, body weight and BMI are often available, but long‐term dietary intake is rarely reported and difficult to control. Consequently, most research examines prepregnancy obesity's association with MASLD risk rather than isolating effects of diet composition and dyslipidaemia [8, 39, 40]. Similarly, many animal studies introduce a HF diet well before mating, making it difficult to separate nutritional effects from obesity [9, 10, 37, 38, 44]. While obesity and high‐fat, high‐sugar diets are closely linked, MASLD can develop in individuals with normal BMI, with 15%–20% of cases occurring in lean individuals [45]. Given the high prevalence of poor dietary habits during pregnancy [46], it is essential to determine whether clinical focus should prioritize obesity and weight gain or if diet composition during pregnancy is equally critical.

The dietary intervention was implemented during approximately the second half of pregnancy, which may have reduced its impact on offspring MASLD. Earlier exposure could have intensified disease severity, as maternal hypertriglyceridaemia in early pregnancy has been linked to childhood hepatic steatosis in humans [7]. Despite this, MASLD was exacerbated in offspring of HF‐fed dams, underscoring maternal dyslipidaemia as a potent in utero stressor. This finding is likely relevant to humans due to the metabolic and reproductive similarities between guinea pigs and humans [16]. Their lipoprotein profile is similar, with low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) as the predominant fraction, unlike mice and rats, where high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) dominates [15]. In response to a HF diet, LDL increases in guinea pigs, mirroring human lipoprotein changes [15]. Moreover, their haemomonochorial placenta closely resembles the human placenta in structure, endocrine and metabolic function [16]. Maternal circulating insulin‐like growth factors are high in both species and play a key role in foetal nutrient delivery [47, 48]. Like human foetuses, guinea pig foetuses rely on maternal glucose transfer due to limited foetal gluconeogenesis until late gestation, and amino acids and fatty acids are actively transported against the foeto‐maternal gradient [49, 50]. Although guinea pigs carry multiple offspring per pregnancy, which may reduce the absolute nutrient exposure per foetus and increase variability compared to singleton human pregnancies, the direction of the observed effects is likely preserved. Overall, the physiological parallels and present findings suggest maternal HF diet and dyslipidaemia may pose a major risk to long‐term offspring health in humans.

The offspring dietary intervention lasted 10 weeks, shorter than the 16 weeks typically required to induce MASH in this model [17, 18, 28]. This timeframe was chosen to allow for histological differences between HF groups to emerge, as fully developed MASH limits differentiation between severe and very severe inflammation and steatosis [17, 18]. Following the 10‐week intervention, most guinea pigs did not develop MASH, with only the HFdam/HFpup group falling into the borderline MAS category. The median steatosis score remained low in HF‐fed offspring, likely due to their rapid growth phase, which increased caloric utilization and reduced intrahepatic fat accumulation. This aligns with our previous study, where juvenile guinea pigs with MASH exhibited less steatosis than adults but had greater inflammation and comparable fibrosis [18]. Similarly, despite relatively low steatosis in the present study, moderate to severe inflammation and mild to moderate fibrosis were observed. These findings further support the aggressive progression of MASLD in juvenile guinea pigs, mirroring the more severe disease phenotype seen in children and adolescents [3].

In this study, there was no indication of increased adiposity in neonatal pups from HF and HFLC dams. On the contrary, these pups, particularly those from HFLC dams, had lower birthweights than LF pups. However, epidemiological studies have shown that rapid catch‐up growth following low birthweight increases the risk of MASLD in children [51], and both HF and particularly HFLC pups quickly caught up in body weight with LF pups. Regarding low VitC, the findings align with human data showing a positive correlation between maternal VitC levels and birthweight [52]. However, previous guinea pig studies found no effect of low VitC, in combination with an LF diet, on birthweight [27, 30]. Given the small subset in the current study of six viable pups, this observation remains inconclusive. The low VitC dose (100 mg/kg feed) for the HFLC dams was based on previous studies in pregnant guinea pigs fed LF diets [27, 30]. However, the higher caloric density of the HF diet may have reduced the expected gram intake per animal, thereby decreasing the absolute VitC intake early in the intervention period. In addition, VitC requirements may be higher under HF feeding [17, 18, 53]. Consequently, the VitC dose was increased during the study to mitigate this. Though important for the continuation of the study, the dose adjustments and unexpected foetal losses limit the strength of conclusions that can be drawn regarding the specific effects of combined HF feeding and low VitC exposure.

Human studies on low VitC and pregnancy are limited, but low VitC levels have been associated with preterm birth due to premature rupture of the foetal membranes, low birthweights and an increased risk of pregnancy complications [52, 54]. In guinea pigs, low but nonscorbutic VitC levels have been linked to early foetal resorption when present before mating but not to a significantly higher incidence of miscarriage, preterm birth or stillbirth [27]. Hence, the high rates of miscarriage and stillbirth observed in the present study were unexpected, especially since the diet switch occurred later in pregnancy. This trend persisted even after doubling the VitC dose used in previous studies [27, 30]. Even more surprising was that pups continued developing in utero and died just before or during birth. Although the exact timing of conception was not recorded, all stillborn pups in the HFLC group appeared fully developed, making preterm birth and premature rupture of foetal membranes unlikely. As part of normal gestation, foetal VitC levels increase late in pregnancy, but when maternal VitC is low, this process is impaired [30]. This may disrupt critical late‐stage foetal development and, potentially in combination with pre‐eclampsia and oxidative stress in the foetus and placenta, could have contributed to the adverse outcomes observed [55]. However, further research is necessary to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Sex‐related differences were absent for all histopathological endpoints except for the cumulative MAS score in the LFdam/HFpup group, where females scored higher than males. While males are generally more susceptible to MASH in humans and mice, findings are inconsistent [56]. The observed difference was limited to the cumulative MAS score and not reflected in its individual components (steatosis, inflammation, ballooning). The difference appeared driven by steatosis score, yet liver TG levels did not differ by sex. Although no conclusion can be drawn due to the small subgroup size (n = 6/sex), this likely represents a false positive without biological relevance.

In the current study, maternal glucose tolerance was not assessed due to animal welfare considerations to minimize stress during pregnancy. This is a putative limitation, preventing us from determining whether hyperglycaemia, in conjunction with dyslipidaemia, contributed to the observed effects in the offspring. However, studies suggest that circulating maternal lipids, rather than hyperglycaemia, are the primary drivers of neonatal adiposity [11]. Hypertriglyceridaemia has been identified as a predictor of infant adiposity independent of glycaemic control and maternal BMI [57]. A meta‐analysis found increased infant adiposity in mothers with gestational diabetes who maintained optimal glycaemic control [58], and infant adiposity does not correlate with glucose levels in women with gestational diabetes [59]. Maternal hypertriglyceridaemia is also positively correlated with offspring hepatic fat at 5 years of age, even after adjusting for maternal BMI and gestational diabetes [7]. Future studies could include glucose tolerance tests during late pregnancy to assess their impact on offspring phenotype.

In conclusion, the present study highlights the detrimental impact of foetal exposure to maternal dyslipidaemia on early‐life MASLD development in offspring. Notably, these effects occurred despite a relatively short exposure to the maternal HF diet and in the absence of maternal obesity, underscoring the deleterious potency of maternal dyslipidaemia. Surprisingly, the combination of HF diet and low VitC severely compromised offspring viability. Because guinea pigs serve as a highly translational model for MASLD, foetal development and dietary VitC dependency, these findings may have considerable relevance for human health and warrant further investigation.
