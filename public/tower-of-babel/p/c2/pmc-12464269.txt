Evaluation of planning policy scenarios for the water-food and energy nexus through the development of a multi-objective optimization model
Authors: Sajad Ashkevari, Somaye Janatrostami, Afshin Ashrafzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study addresses the increasing interdependence of water, food, and energy systems by developing an integrated simulation–optimization framework for the Sefidroud irrigation and drainage network. Leveraging system dynamics modeling in Simulink and multi-objective genetic algorithm (GA) optimization within MATLAB, the model simultaneously pursues four critical objectives: minimizing water scarcity, maximizing agricultural profit, reducing greenhouse gas emissions through pump electrification, and optimizing hydropower production. Trade-offs among these objectives are resolved using a weighted sum approach, with seven distinct weight combinations tested to capture a range of policy priorities. Simulation outputs, validated against empirical data, demonstrate the model’s high accuracy in achieving balanced resource management. Among the scenarios, the fourth weighting combination, emphasizing equal priorities for water scarcity reduction and economic profit, proved the most balanced. Under this configuration, 78.9% of the water demand is met through integrated sources (reservoir dams: 80.4%, groundwater: 7.6%, drainage: 3.4%, rivers: 5.4%, reservoirs: 3.2%). This scenario also improves pump electrification ratios for rice paddies (0.44, a 16.9% increase) and tea gardens (0.74, a 12.5% increase) over current conditions, and boosts hydropower output by 8.4%. The findings highlight that farmers can adjust cultivated areas in line with water availability to optimize profits, managers can customize objective weights to prioritize emission reduction or energy security, and the framework enables adaptive strategies to balance sectoral demands under dynamic hydrological and socio-economic conditions. Overall, this study underscores the value of simulation–optimization tools for nexus management and provides actionable insights for harmonizing water, food, and energy objectives.

Introduction

Global forecasts suggest that in the future years, factors such as population growth, economic progress, cultural shifts, and climate change will amplify the difficulties related to essential resources such as fresh water, energy, and food1,2. Consequently, this trend exerts detrimental pressure on natural resources, jeopardizing the ability of ecosystems to flourish. The agricultural sector’s food production heavily relies on access to critical resources like water, land, and energy. The absence of these resources poses substantial risks to human well-being3. In recent years, significant research efforts have been focused on investigating the complex relationship between water and energy resources4–6. Considering the common challenge of the lack of water and energy resources, effective resource management is necessary to improve the current situation.

The Nexus approach, initially introduced by the World Economic Forum in 2011 to address challenges such as natural resource scarcity, identifies the interconnection of water, food, and energy across both time and space7. The WEF nexus involves complex interactions in which water, energy, and food systems are not only interdependent but also compete with each other8,9. The nexus approach represents a new paradigm for environmental governance, systematically analyzing interdependencies within a holistic framework to identify management policies that can integrate diverse inter-sectoral goals10. The FEW Nexus is highly sensitive and vulnerable. Insecurity in one element can affect other elements and even disrupt FEW Nexus interactions11. Numerous studies have been conducted to examine the efficient management of the water-energy-food nexus, utilizing methods such as life cycle assessment modeling12–14, input-output modeling15–18, and dynamic system modeling19–21.

The System Dynamics method, as a powerful assessment approach, emphasizes the dynamic complexity arising from feedbacks, nonlinearity, and time delays in the system22. Software packages such as Stella and VENSIM are widely used for SD models to understand and visualize complex causal feedbacks23,24. These tools can analyze inter-sectoral resource interactions without complex programming code25. Consequently, researchers have recently focused on modeling the FEW nexus. Wa’el A et al.21, Wicaksono et al.26 and Ravar et al.27 have developed SD models for the water, food, and energy nexus using VENSIM. Purwanto et al.28 and Sušnik et al.29 used STELLA for modeling the water, food, and energy nexus with a system dynamics modeling approach. Mediavilla et al.30 examined the use of the simulation tool Simulink in the MATLAB environment for developing low-cost custom analysis tools without extensive programming. Wang et al.31, employed a system dynamics model to gradually optimize the food-energy-water-waste nexus in agriculture, taking economic risks for potential carbon reduction into account. Their simulations of carbon emissions and reduction strategies were carried out using the MATLAB/Simulink platform. Naghdi et al.32 utilized Simulink for dynamic system modeling in a multi-objective optimization model for surface and groundwater allocation, employing the NSGAII algorithm for optimization. Cherif et al.33 implemented dynamic system modeling for the multi-objective optimization of water and energy models using genetic algorithms in the MATLAB/Simulink software. A review of past studies on the use of dynamic system simulation tools has shown that in cases where optimization is performed alongside modeling, having both simulation and optimization models in the same programming environment makes the simulation-optimization process easier. Therefore, it is preferable to use the MATLAB/Simulink tool.

Multi-objective optimization is a valuable tool for decision-making in the food-water-energy nexus34–36. Researchers such as Medina-Santana et al.37, Okola et al.38, and Cansino-Loeza and Ponce-Ortega39 have employed multi-objective optimization techniques to optimize a WEFN system. Initially, weighting methods and constraint handling were developed for formulating multi-objective problems by linearly combining parameter variables from weights or constraints imposed on multiple objectives. Despite the advantages of using mathematical programming in linear optimization models, evolutionary algorithms have become the preferred solvers for non-linear multi-objective problems10,40.

Despite notable progress in Water-Energy-Food (WEF) nexus research, several critical knowledge gaps persist in the current literature. Although system dynamics and optimization methods have been widely applied, the integration of MATLAB/Simulink for dynamic modeling with genetic algorithm (GA) optimization in WEF nexus models has yet to be explored, especially in region-specific contexts. Furthermore, most existing models remain theoretical and lack empirical validation in real-world agricultural systems characterized by unique hydrological and socioeconomic constraints. While some quantitative studies incorporate economic risk assessment and carbon reduction strategies, there remains a need for practical, dynamically optimized frameworks to support effective decision-making.

To address these challenges, this study develops the first GA-optimized system dynamics model in MATLAB/Simulink for the WEF nexus, specifically tailored to the Sefidrud irrigation network and accounting for local cropping patterns and water allocation complexities. Our approach integrates hydrological data, agricultural statistics, and stakeholder input to ensure both relevance and practical applicability. Additionally, the model quantitatively evaluates trade-offs among resource allocation, energy efficiency, and environmental sustainability, providing actionable guidance for regional planners. By bridging these gaps, our work advances WEF nexus management in the study area and offers a replicable methodology for similar agricultural systems worldwide, providing valuable insights for policymakers and stakeholders facing resource constraints and climate change challenges.

Materials and methods

Introduction of the study area

The Sefidroud irrigation and drainage network is located on the western coast of the Caspian Sea in Guilan Province, Iran, and is characterized by a moderate, humid climate with high levels of rainfall and humidity. Operational since 1961, the network was established after the construction of the Sefidroud Reservoir Dam, which has a storage capacity of 1,800 million m³ and created a 56 km² reservoir in the Manjil area. The dam currently operates with an adjustable water capacity of 2,090 million m³ and produces 12.5–15 GWh of electricity annually (Guilan Regional Water Authority).

The Sefidroud River is the primary water source for agricultural lands in the Guilan Plain, especially those within the irrigation network. Satellite imagery and field surveys indicate that the network covers approximately 284,180 hectares, divided into three distinct irrigation areas: Fumanat (F), Central (G), and East Guilan (D). Rice cultivation occupies about 167,000 hectares of this area (Guilan Regional Water Authority). Water released from the dam is distributed through diversion dams and branching canals to various segments of the network (Fig. 1).

Water resources for the network include groundwater (extracted from wells) and surface water (from rivers, drains, and local ponds). The Sefidroud Reservoir Dam further supplements these surface water supplies through its diversion infrastructure.

Meteorological and hydrological data

According to the Guilan Regional Water Organization, precipitation, temperature, and evaporation data from rainfall measurement stations within the study area were collected for 2009–2018. These data were used to obtain the necessary meteorological and hydrological information for calculating the water balance.

Selection of representative crops for the study

To identify the dominant crops in each irrigation zone, we selected those with the largest cultivated areas based on statistical data from the Jihad Agriculture Organization. Our analysis of regions F, G, and D showed that rice accounts for over 66% of the arable land, with the remainder devoted to other crops, including tea. The predominance of rice cultivation and its high-water requirements, particularly under the flooded cultivation practices common in northern Iran41, warranted its selection as a primary crop for this study. Tea, a historically significant and native crop, was also chosen due to its longstanding cultivation and regional importance. Therefore, tea and rice were selected as the representative crops for this study.

Methodology

This study employs an integrated modeling framework to address the water-food-energy nexus in the Sefidroud Irrigation and Drainage Network. The methodology combines system dynamics simulation with multi-objective optimization to analyze system interactions and propose sustainable management strategies. The research steps are illustrated in the flowchart shown in Fig. 4.

Dynamic system modeling

System dynamics modeling is used to simulate the water-food-energy nexus by capturing hydrological processes, agricultural water demands, and energy requirements over time. This approach effectively represents nonlinear feedback loops between social and physical systems using interconnected modules for water, agriculture, and energy. Governing equations, derived from the law of mass conservation, integrate these subsystems to model complex interactions. System dynamics enables simultaneous simulation of interdependent processes, providing valuable insights into resource management challenges.

A dynamic systems model was developed in Simulink (MATLAB) to simulate the feedback loops among water, agriculture, and energy subsystems. The governing equations for each subsystem follow the fundamental principles of system dynamics and employ mass conservation. Simulink software is used to simulate the water-food-energy nexus system. Simulink inputs include: (1) Annual abstraction from rivers, irrigation canals, ponds, drainage and groundwater, (2) Water consumption from each irrigation area, (3) Rainfall, (4) Volume of river and canal inflow and outflow, (5) Evaporation from surface water, (6) Hydropower generation and energy consumption in power production sector, (7) Agricultural crop production. This model was designed so that by adjusting the inputs for each scenario, the model’s outputs can be determined.

The simulation employs a dual time-step framework to balance computational efficiency with operational precision. At the macro-scale, a 114-day agricultural season governs reservoir-wide water release planning, aligning with downstream irrigation demands. At the micro-scale, daily intervals drive energy generation calculations to capture turbine performance dynamics under variable flow conditions. This hierarchical approach leverages Simulink’s native support for multi-rate simulation, where reservoir management modules operate at the seasonal scale to reduce computational overhead, while turbine performance modules run daily to capture transient efficiency fluctuations and spillway energy recovery opportunities.

Optimization model for integrated resource management

This study presents a multi-objective optimization model designed to achieve integrated management of the Sefidroud Irrigation and Drainage Network within the water-food-energy (WFE) nexus framework. The model simultaneously addresses four core objectives:

⦁ Minimizing water scarcity through optimized resource allocation,

⦁ Maximizing economic profit via strategic expansion of cultivated areas,

⦁ Minimizing greenhouse gas emissions from energy and agricultural activities, and.

⦁ Maximizing electricity production from hydropower and other sources.

A genetic algorithm (GA) was employed to solve the multi-objective optimization problem, utilizing a weighting approach to balance the priorities of each objective. The assignment of different weights reflects the diverse perspectives of regional managers and planners, allowing the model to accommodate various management scenarios. This approach provides a flexible tool for stakeholders to evaluate strategic outcomes, understand trade-offs, and make informed decisions to support sustainable development. Integration of simulation and optimization model

After simulating the water-food-energy nexus in SIMULINK, we used a genetic algorithm in MATLAB to optimize the four-objective management model. The integrated setup allowed us to test different objective weights and identify solutions best matched to regional priorities.

Results and discussion

The integrated water-food-energy nexus was simulated using Simulink to model various subsystems, including crop-specific irrigation demands, water availability from diverse sources, and energy consumption for groundwater extraction and surface water pumping in irrigation zones F, G, and D. These subsystems were simulated with an annual time step over a 10-year period (2009–2018), while a daily time step was adopted specifically for hydropower energy production to better capture operational variability. Model validation against observed data demonstrated strong agreement, with a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.91 for hydropower production and an average R2 of 0.89 for the other subsystems.

The multi-objective optimization model, solved using a genetic algorithm (GA) in MATLAB, converged to optimal solutions within 100 generations (Fig. 5).

Weighting and trade-off analysis in multi-objective optimization

Since the optimization model employed in this study is multi-objective with four distinct goals, a weighted sum approach was used to transform it into a single-objective model (see Eq. 7). Optimal weights for each objective were identified by evaluating different combinations (Table 6) to align with the priorities of regional planners and managers.

The assignment of these weights directly reflects strategic objectives: for example, prioritizing economic profit results in a higher weight for that objective, whereas focusing on water scarcity assigns a greater weight to the corresponding function. The third and fourth objectives, both linked to the energy sector, target greenhouse gas mitigation—either by increasing electrification in groundwater extraction or by maximizing electricity production. Due to their similar environmental focus, adjusting their respective weights produces limited variation in overall environmental outcomes.

This flexible weighting methodology enables planners to tailor decision-making to local needs and visualize the trade-offs among water, food, and energy sectors. Presenting these weight scenarios provides valuable insights for future planning, guiding stakeholders toward balanced strategies for resource sustainability and improved regional outcomes.

Balancing water scarcity and economic profit: optimal weighting in agriculture

Analysis reveals that changes in the weights of the first (minimizing water scarcity) and second (maximizing economic profit) objectives do not yield a consistent trend in the optimal cultivation areas. This is because cultivated area acts as a decision variable in both objectives, with the first objective aiming to reduce it to conserve water, and the second seeking to expand it for higher profit. Optimization of hydroelectric energy production

The fourth objective of the optimization model is to maximize hydroelectric power production at the Sefidroud Dam by assessing the operational capacity of individual turbines and optimizing water release strategies. This approach balances energy generation with reservoir management by accounting for three key factors: water overflow volume during high-inflow periods, turbine efficiency under variable flow rates, and seasonal inflow dynamics across the 114-day release period driven by downstream agricultural demands.

Figure 7 highlights key operational improvements, comparing electricity production between optimal and current scenarios. A significant disparity is evident from the 5th to the 38th day, where the optimized scenario achieves a peak output of 2,243 MWh—a 24% increase over the current scenario’s maximum of 1,807 MWh. Cumulatively, the model demonstrates an 8.4% increase in total hydroelectric generation across the 114-day period, largely attributed to fully utilizing installed turbine capacity during high-flow events such as dam overflows. This optimized schedule also enhances the dam’s critical role in supporting the national electricity grid during peak demand without compromising reservoir stability.

To further contextualize these results, a sensitivity analysis of power plant efficiency (e) was conducted. The analysis revealed a linear correlation whereby each 5% increase in e (ranging from 0.80 to 0.95) corresponded to a proportional 6–7% rise in energy output; for example, at t = 1, production increased from 241.81 MWh (e = 0.80) to 287.15 MWh (e = 0.95). The study identified an economic threshold at e > 0.85, below which cumulative output decreased by 6%, while gains beyond 0.90 yielded diminishing returns. Temporal trends showed peak production between t = 8 and 10 (2,241–2,243 MWh at e = 0.85), declining to approximately 500 MWh by t = 114, with efficiency improvements having the most significant impact during peak periods, underscoring their importance in maximizing generation.

These findings reveal important synergies and implications. The 8.4% increase in generation at the Sefidroud Dam parallels the sensitivity analysis results, highlighting untapped potential within existing infrastructure. Both analyses emphasize that marginal improvements in power plant efficiency can lead to substantial gains in energy output. Moreover, hydropower’s low-carbon footprint supports global clean energy objectives; the spill-based optimization minimizes wasted runoff, while efficiency sensitivity underscores the value of targeted upgrades to aging equipment. Finally, the proposed framework invites future research to expand in incorporating climate-driven flow variability for resilience, refining turbine performance data under extreme conditions, and exploring adaptive strategies to address hydrological uncertainties.

Discussion

Key findings and interpretation: The weighting scheme adopted in this study was carefully crafted to reflect the region’s socio-cultural, economic, and institutional context, ensuring both stakeholder alignment and practical feasibility. Stakeholder-driven weighting was executed via a multi-criteria framework that balanced the priorities of farmers, regional officials, and environmental concerns. Notably, the fourth weight combination emerged as the most balanced, effectively managing trade-offs among minimizing irrigation water shortages (highest priority), maximizing cultivated area (secondary priority), reducing environmental impacts (e.g., dependence on diesel pumps), and optimizing hydropower generation. This outcome underscores the inherent complexities of multi-objective water resource management, where economic and environmental goals often compete. The framework’s adaptability to stakeholder inputs highlights its potential for broader applications in regions facing similar trade-offs.

Sensitivity Analysis and Regional Implications: Irrigation efficiency is a critical parameter in water resources management, though its measurement is inherently uncertain due to variability in field conditions and methodological constraints. To quantify the impact of this uncertainty, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by increasing irrigation efficiency by 10% and 30% across three irrigation regions (F, G, and D). The results revealed significant nonlinear reductions in irrigation water demand deficits, with a 10% improvement decreasing deficits by 18.9%, 25.1%, and 20.0% in regions F, G, and D, respectively. A 30% increase produced even more pronounced reductions of 51.5%, 61.9%, and 114.4% in the same regions. These varying impacts underscore the necessity for context-specific irrigation management strategies; notably, Region G exhibited the highest sensitivity to efficiency gains, whereas the extreme response observed in Region D (+ 114.4%) suggests the need for further examination of underlying data or model assumptions, such as baseline water stress levels.

Practical implementation and policy relevance: The model’s constraints were calibrated to ensure operational viability for regional managers, with the explicit inclusion of hydropower generation addressing dual energy-water security challenges, particularly during peak demand periods. This integration enhances the model’s utility for policymakers by enabling mitigation of irrigation deficits, balancing agricultural productivity, supporting sustainable transition from diesel pumps, and leveraging hydropower without compromising environmental goals.

Limitations and Future Directions: A key limitation is the exclusion of climate change impacts, which may alter hydrological regimes (e.g., precipitation patterns, evapotranspiration). Future research should incorporate climate scenarios to improve model resilience, particularly for regions vulnerable to climate variability.

Conclusion and Broader Implications: This study provides a robust framework for addressing irrigation challenges while balancing socio-economic and environmental objectives. The results highlight the necessity of adaptive governance structures to navigate evolving climatic and socio-economic pressures. By integrating stakeholder priorities with empirical sensitivity analyses, the approach offers a replicable model for integrated water resource management in comparable contexts.

Conclusion

This study presents a multi-objective optimization framework to address the interconnected challenges of water scarcity, agricultural productivity, energy efficiency, and greenhouse gas mitigation in the Sefidroud Dam and its associated irrigation networks. Water-food-energy nexus dynamics were simulated in Simulink using regional data, and optimization was achieved through a genetic algorithm (GA) and weighted sum approach within MATLAB. This methodology effectively reconciled competing objectives and generated actionable guidance for integrated, sustainable resource management in the region. The main findings can be summarized as follows:

⦁ The weighting framework demonstrated significant flexibility, allowing alignment with local management priorities. Notably, the fourth weighting combination—with equal emphasis on reducing water scarcity and maximizing economic profit—produced the most balanced set of outcomes. This strategy managed trade-offs among water conservation, agricultural expansion, and environmental sustainability, offering a replicable approach for comparable contexts.

⦁ The model showed that increasing the weight assigned to water scarcity reduction substantially decreased irrigation deficits, particularly in regions F, G, and D. Surface water from the Sefidroud Dam, delivered through irrigation canals, remained the primary supply source, emphasizing the need for efficient canal management to mitigate shortages.

⦁ Greater electrification of groundwater pumps, especially in tea cultivation, was closely associated with reduced diesel dependence and lower greenhouse gas emissions. In contrast, the inflexible energy consumption patterns observed in power plant cooling highlight the need for alternative mitigation strategies in those sectors.

⦁ Optimizing turbine operations and water release schedules increased hydroelectric production by 8.4%, demonstrating significant untapped potential in the existing infrastructure. This not only advances renewable energy targets but also strengthens grid resilience during peak demand.

These outcomes underscore the importance of adaptive governance capable of integrating priorities across water, energy, and food sectors, especially under increasing climatic uncertainties. The model creates clear opportunities for policymakers to develop incentives for electrification, optimize hydropower management, and allocate water resources more equitably. However, it should be noted that the present analysis does not incorporate the impacts of climate change, such as shifts in precipitation or evapotranspiration. Future research should integrate climate-driven hydrological variability to assess long-term sustainability, further refine turbine performance under extreme flows to enhance operational resilience, and address socio-economic barriers to electrification and clean energy adoption in inflexible sectors.

Overall, this study advances the water-energy-food nexus discourse by providing a scalable optimization-based approach to balancing resource demands. The results highlight the central role of electrification, optimized hydropower, and adaptive governance as foundations for sustainable development in water-scarce regions.
