Investigation of Metformin Contamination in Horses: Implications for Environmental Exposure and Doping Control
Authors: Megan E. Jacobs, Jeff Blea, Michael Hardy, Daniel S. McKemie, Megan Traynham, Heather K. Knych
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMetformin is a diabetes medication with minimal therapeutic efficacy in veterinary medicine. However, recently there have been several adverse analytical findings in regulatory samples collected from racehorses. Metformin is commonly prescribed to humans, is excreted unchanged in urine, and is resistant to environmental degradation, raising concerns with respect to environmental contamination. In this study, six horses were exposed to shavings contaminated with metformin‐positive urine at concentrations of 10, 135, and 270 μg/mL. Blood (plasma, serum, whole blood, and red blood cells) and urine samples were collected for 168 h post‐exposure for determination of metformin concentrations. All horses had detectable metformin concentrations in blood samples at least once during the sample collection period. Metformin concentrations in urine samples greatly exceeded those in blood and were detectable as early as 4 h post‐contamination. The presence of metformin in these samples raises the possibility of metformin exposure through contamination, especially given the housing conditions at racetracks. The potential for exposure through contamination, resulting in adverse analytical findings, should be considered in the regulation of metformin in racehorses.

Introduction

Metformin (1,1‐dimethylbiguanide) is a medication used for the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus. It is very widely prescribed in human medicine and prescriptions of metformin dispensed in the United States have increased significantly in recent years for both Food and Drug Administration (FDA)‐approved and off‐label uses [1]. Metformin is a water‐soluble small molecule drug that is excreted in urine as unchanged metformin [2]. The degradation‐resistant nature of the drug, along with its widespread use, has led to detectable metformin levels in wastewater and surface waters near residential areas due to excretion in human urine [3].

Metformin has recently been detected in post‐race blood and urine samples from horses at racetracks throughout the United States. Unlike in human medicine, metformin is rarely prescribed to horses, largely due to its ineffectiveness in treating insulin resistance in this species [4]. Its lack of recognized therapeutic utility in young, fit horses has led to its classification as a prohibited substance by the Horse Racing Integrity and Safety Authority (HISA), making the adverse analytical findings (AAF) from horses at racetracks a cause for concern among horseracing regulators.

The prevalence of use in human medicine has led to speculation that some metformin AAFs are a result of exposure to human urine containing metformin. It has been suggested that stalls become contaminated when handlers urinate in the stalls. The potential for exposure to metformin‐contaminated urine resulting in AAF in horses warrants further study. To that end, the primary objective of this study was to explore environmental contamination as a source of metformin exposure by describing the blood and urine levels of metformin in horses that were in contact with metformin‐positive urine.

Methods

Six university‐owned exercised Thoroughbred horses (2 mares and 4 geldings, aged 3–5 years, weighing 452–570 kg) were used for this study. Prior to beginning the study, horses were subject to a physical examination, complete blood count, and serum biochemistry panel to determine health status. Horses were housed in 12 × 12‐ft stalls bedded with approximately 18 cubic feet of premium Douglas wood shavings for the duration of the study. Stalls were stripped of all shavings and waste, power‐washed with soap and water, and re‐bedded with clean shavings the day before contamination. Automatic waterers were also emptied, scrubbed, and allowed to refill prior to contamination of the stall.

To create the metformin‐positive urine, equine urine that tested negative for metformin was spiked with commercially available metformin powder (> 95% purity) to concentrations of 10, 135, and 270 μg/mL. As published data describing urine concentrations in humans are limited, concentrations were chosen based on unpublished data of concentrations in the urine of humans taking a 1000 mg oral dose of metformin. The prepared urine (800 mL/per stall) was spread over shavings in one corner of the stall (n = 2 horses per concentration of prepared urine). Stalls were spot‐cleaned every morning for the duration of the study, leaving most of the urine‐soaked shavings in the stall but removing the solid waste.

Blood samples were collected via direct venipuncture into two EDTA blood tubes, one tube devoid of anticoagulant, and one serum separator tube (SST) for determination of drug concentration at times 0 (prior to contamination), 30 min, and 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 30, 36, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, and 168‐h post contamination. The EDTA tubes were kept on ice until processing, wherein one tube was centrifuged at 3000 g at 4°C for 10 min; plasma and red blood cells (RBCs) were separated, and an aliquot of each was stored at −20°C until drug concentration analysis. An aliquot of whole blood was taken from the second EDTA tube and stored at −20°C until drug concentration analysis. The serum tubes were kept at room temperature for approximately 30 min prior to centrifugation at 3000 g for 10 min. Serum was then separated and stored at −20°C until drug concentration analysis.

Horses utilized in this study are trained to urinate at the sound of a whistle, so that urine can be collected via free catch. Urine was collected for determination of drug concentration at times 0 (prior to contamination), 4, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, and 168‐h post contamination. Samples were kept on ice, then stored at −20°C until drug concentration analysis.

Metformin concentrations were determined in urine and blood by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry using a previously validated method [5]. Calibration curves and negative control samples were prepared fresh for each quantitative assay. In addition, quality control samples (drug‐free equine blood matrices and urine) were fortified with analyte at three concentrations within the standard curve as an additional check of accuracy.

Results

Quality control samples for all matrices met accuracy and precision acceptance criteria as described in the FDA's Guidance for Bioanalytical Method Development and Validation [6] (Table S1). The technique was optimized to provide a limit of quantitation (LOQ) of 0.25 ng/mL for serum and plasma and 0.3 ng/mL for whole blood and RBCs [5]. The limit of detection (LOD) was approximately 0.10 ng/mL for serum and plasma and 0.2 ng/mL for whole blood and RBCs [5]. For urine, the LOQ and LOD were 0.5 and 0.25 ng/mL, respectively [5].

Metformin concentrations following exposure to 10, 135, and 270 μg/mL of urine are listed in Tables 1, 2, 3, respectively. Metformin was detected at low levels in serum samples collected from all horses at some time during the sample collection period. Detection times differed between serum samples collected in tubes containing the silicon separator and those without. Although in some instances, concentrations were below the LOQ, metformin was detected in plasma samples from all horses (Tables 1, 2, 3). In whole blood samples, metformin was above the LOQ in one horse in the 10 μg/mL group and detectable but below the LOQ in four horses (Table 1). Red blood cell metformin was detectable in all horses and above the LOQ at some time points in four horses (135 and 270 μg/mL groups; Tables 2 and 3). For all matrices, metformin concentrations did not follow a predictable pattern with concentrations fluctuating unpredictably for some horses.

Discussion

Metformin detection in the blood and urine of horses housed at racetracks and claims of environmental exposure are a concern for horseracing regulators. The current study describes blood and urine metformin concentrations in horses following exposure to bedding with metformin‐contaminated urine.

Reports in humans demonstrate that metformin partitions into RBCs, accounting at least in part for the prolonged residence time of this drug in the body [7]. A recent study conducted by our laboratory demonstrates that metformin behaves similarly in horses [8]. In the current study, concentrations of metformin in several different blood matrices (serum, plasma, RBCs, whole blood) were assessed. All horses exposed to metformin‐positive urine in the presently reported study had detectable concentrations of metformin in their blood at some time during the study. Although metformin concentrations in different types of blood sample (serum, RBCs, etc.) varied within and between horses, detection in all horses supports the potential for low‐level environmental exposure. Interestingly, with respect to serum concentrations, metformin concentrations differed between the two serum tubes used in this study (serum separator versus no serum separator). Some drugs will bind to the silicon plug present in serum separator tubes, leading to differences in concentrations between the two types in the harvested serum. However, based on a previous study, this appears unlikely for metformin as it does not appear to bind to the silicon plug to an appreciable extent [8]. That same study demonstrated that metformin, as with other drugs that partition into RBCs, may “leak” from RBCs into serum as part of the liberation of the RBC‐bound fraction into the serum during the clotting process. As the serum separator reduces exposure of the serum to the RBCs, this may explain, at least in part, situations where concentrations are higher in tubes devoid of the separator. From a regulatory perspective, this could be an important consideration in the type of tube used for drug testing purposes.

One unexplained finding is the high concentration of metformin (34.6 ng/mL) detected in whole blood collected from Horse 2 following exposure to 10 ug/mL of metformin. Additionally, while metformin was detected at very low levels in plasma and RBCs, it was not detected in serum. The reason for this finding is not apparent. Notably, the horses had detectable concentrations of metformin in RBCs, suggesting that similar to humans, this drug partitions into RBCs in horses. This supports the previous study whereby investigators suggest that RBCs act as a compartment for metformin sequestration in horses; the drug would be allowed to slowly leach back into the blood plasma, leading to longer detection times in plasma or serum and thus a longer half‐life [8]. This may also explain the pulsatile‐like behavior of this drug, whereby metformin concentrations decline only to increase later. Recontamination offers another plausible explanation for this behavior. As horses continued to reside in the originally contaminated stalls throughout the sample collection period, they could be continuously exposed. It is also possible that horses re‐contaminate themselves through their own metformin‐containing urine, a theory supported by the relatively high metformin concentrations detected in the urine of the horses in the current study. Urine metformin levels were above the LOQ in all horses for the entire duration of the study (168 h), beginning as early as 4 h post‐contamination. The findings reported here, both for blood and urine, should be considered when implementing screening methods for metformin, especially those using urine.

When horses are housed at racetracks, they are inevitably going to be exposed to the urine of other horses or humans. It should be noted in the current study that horse urine was used as the source of exposure and even though it is not anticipated that horses would react differently to human urine (i.e., avoid it), it is possible that concentrations detected in horses may be different depending on the source of the urine. While notably the sample size was small and the exact route of exposure (oral and/or transdermal) is unknown, low levels of metformin were detected in blood and urine samples collected from all horses (not previously administered metformin) housed in stalls that were intentionally contaminated with metformin‐containing urine. These findings suggest that exposure of horses to metformin‐contaminated stalls may result in detectable concentrations of drug in biological samples for at least 168 h (last time point sampled). However, given the low blood and urine concentrations and the question surrounding how this drug is absorbed following low‐level exposure, it may be prudent to study absorption of low levels of metformin via the oral and transdermal routes, as an additional means of validating this conclusion.

Results from the current study demonstrate that exposure to metformin via contaminated bedding can result in detection of the drug in biological samples collected from horses. Data from this contamination study, combined with concentrations from horses knowingly administered metformin, can be used to establish a screening limit that accounts for inadvertent exposure through contamination, thus reducing the likelihood of AAF due to inadvertent exposure.
