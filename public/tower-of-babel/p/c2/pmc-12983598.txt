The multifaceted functions of selective autophagy in cancer: molecular basis, consequences, and clinical prospects
Authors: Mingyang Jiang, Ke Zhang, Meng Li, Yu Tian, Tao Wang, Raquel Alarcón Rodríguez, Zhanghui Lin, Jinfeng Meng, Chenghao Li, Zheng Wang, Sichang Wu, Yunfei Cai, Wenyang Zhang, Rui Guo, Yao Liu, Yuqi Bai, Guichuan Lai, Chengcheng Zhang, Zhandong Bo, Ruqiong Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Selective autophagy is a critical cellular process in eukaryotic cells, characterized by the targeted degradation of specific organelles and proteins. This intricate mechanism plays a vital role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and has significant implications in the pathogenesis and progression of various diseases, notably cancer. Recent studies have highlighted the dual role of selective autophagy in cancer, acting both as a suppressor in the early stages and potentially facilitating progression in later stages. The complexity of selective autophagy, particularly in the context of oncogenesis, presents unique challenges. This review focuses on the molecular mechanisms underlying selective autophagy, its impact on cancer development and progression, and the challenges in leveraging this process for therapeutic interventions. We discuss the interplay between autophagy and cancer, examining how alterations in autophagic pathways contribute to tumorigenesis and the resistance of cancer cells to therapy. Additionally, we explore the potential of targeting selective autophagy pathways as a novel approach in cancer treatment, considering the challenges and prospects of such strategies.

Introduction

Selective autophagy represents a pivotal cellular mechanism in the life of eukaryotic cells, finely tuned to manage and maintain the intricate balance of cellular homeostasis [1]. This process, selective in nature, specifically targets and degrades damaged or superfluous organelles, misfolded proteins, and pathogens, ensuring the preservation of cellular integrity and function [2]. The importance of selective autophagy extends beyond mere cellular cleanup; it is fundamentally intertwined with the pathogenesis and progression of various diseases, with cancer being among the most researched and clinically significant [3]. The nuanced role of selective autophagy in cancer – serving as both a potential suppressor in its early stages and as a facilitator for progression in advanced stages – underscores the complexity and duality of this cellular mechanism in oncogenesis [4]. This introductory overview aims to set the stage for a comprehensive review of the challenges inherent in understanding selective autophagy, its impact on cancer development and progression, and the prospects of harnessing this process for therapeutic interventions [5]. The relationship between selective autophagy and cancer is paradoxical and multifaceted, reflecting the process's dual capacity to both suppress and promote tumorigenesis depending on the context and stage of the disease [2]. In the early stages of cancer, selective autophagy acts as a guardian of cellular integrity, eliminating potential sources of DNA damage and genomic instability, thus preventing the initiation of tumorigenic processes [6]. However, as cancer progresses, tumor cells may hijack the autophagic machinery to meet their increased metabolic demands and to counteract the hostile tumor microenvironment, thereby enhancing their survival, growth, and resistance to therapy [7]. This adaptive mechanism employed by cancer cells illustrates the complexity of selective autophagy and its ambivalent role in oncogenesis [8].

Understanding the molecular mechanisms that govern selective autophagy is crucial for unraveling its role in cancer and cellular homeostasis [9]. This entails a detailed examination of the signaling pathways, receptors, and adaptors that mediate the recognition and sequestration of cargo, as well as the subsequent fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes for degradation [1]. Such mechanisms are not only central to the process of selective autophagy but also represent potential therapeutic targets [10]. Alterations in these pathways can lead to dysregulated autophagy, contributing to tumorigenesis and the development of resistance to cancer therapies [11, 12]. Therefore, elucidating the precise molecular events and regulatory networks involved in selective autophagy is essential for advancing our understanding of cancer biology and for the development of novel therapeutic strategies [2].

The potential of targeting selective autophagy pathways in cancer treatment is a promising yet challenging frontier [3]. The idea of manipulating autophagy to either promote cancer cell death or to inhibit the survival advantage it provides to established tumors offers an innovative approach to cancer therapy [7]. However, several obstacles must be overcome to translate this knowledge into effective treatments [13]. These challenges include the development of reliable markers for autophagic activity in cancer, understanding the context-dependent roles of autophagy in different types of cancer, and designing drugs that can specifically modulate autophagic pathways without disrupting the delicate balance of cellular homeostasis in healthy cells [10]. Moreover, the therapeutic targeting of selective autophagy in cancer involves navigating the intricate interplay between autophagy and other cellular processes, such as apoptosis, metabolism, and immune responses [11]. This requires a nuanced understanding of how alterations in autophagic pathways contribute to cancer progression and therapy resistance, as well as how these pathways interact with the broader cellular and tumor microenvironment [7].

The primary aim of this study is to elucidate the complex role of selective autophagy in the development and progression of cancer, with a focus on understanding its dual function as both a tumor suppressor and a facilitator of tumor growth in various stages of cancer. This investigation seeks to dissect the molecular mechanisms that govern selective autophagy, identify how alterations in these pathways contribute to oncogenesis, and explore the resistance of cancer cells to therapies. Furthermore, this study aims to evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting selective autophagy pathways, with the goal of developing novel strategies for cancer treatment. By achieving a deeper understanding of the intricate relationship between selective autophagy and cancer, this research aspires to uncover novel insights into tumor biology, identify new therapeutic targets, and pave the way for innovative approaches to cancer therapy that could ultimately enhance clinical outcomes for patients.

Mitophagy

Mitochondria are pivotal for cellular energy production through the synthesis of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the energy currency of the cell [14]. These organelles host the electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation processes, which are critical for ATP production from glucose and oxygen [15]. However, mitochondrial dysfunction can severely impact cellular processes [16]. Dysfunction leads to decreased ATP production, affecting energy-dependent cellular functions [17]. Additionally, it increases the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can damage cellular components such as DNA, proteins, and lipids [18]. Excessive ROS generation contributes to oxidative stress, linked to various pathological conditions including neurodegenerative diseases, cancer, and aging [19]. Furthermore, dysfunctional mitochondria play a significant role in initiating programmed cell death or apoptosis by releasing pro-apoptotic factors like cytochrome c into the cytosol, triggering the apoptotic cascade [20]. This mechanism is crucial for removing damaged cells, but its dysregulation can lead to pathological cell death or survival of damaged cells, promoting disease progression [19]. Mitophagy, a specific form of autophagy targeting mitochondria, plays a critical role in regulating mitochondrial quality and preventing cellular damage [21]. It selectively removes damaged or dysfunctional mitochondria, thereby maintaining cellular homeostasis and preventing the accumulation of defective mitochondria that can lead to excessive ROS production and cell death [22]. Key players in mitophagy include PINK1 (PTEN-induced putative kinase 1) and Parkin in mammals, which detect and tag damaged mitochondria for degradation [23]. PINK1 accumulates on the outer membrane of damaged mitochondria, recruiting Parkin, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, to ubiquitinate mitochondrial proteins, marking the mitochondrion for autophagic degradation [24]. This process ensures the elimination of mitochondria that are beyond repair, thus safeguarding the cell against mitochondrial-induced oxidative stress and apoptosis [25, 26]. Additionally, mitophagy is crucial for the adaptation to metabolic needs by removing excess mitochondria during nutrient scarcity, further illustrating its role in cellular protection and metabolic regulation [22]. The initiation and regulation of mitophagy in mammalian cells involve complex molecular mechanisms, primarily orchestrated by the PINK1 and Parkin proteins [27]. Upon mitochondrial damage and loss of membrane potential, PINK1 stabilizes on the outer mitochondrial membrane, serving as a signal for mitochondrial distress [28]. PINK1 then recruits Parkin from the cytosol to the damaged mitochondria, where Parkin ubiquitinates various outer mitochondrial membrane proteins [29]. This ubiquitination serves as a signal for the autophagic machinery to recognize and engulf the damaged mitochondria using a double-membrane structure called the autophagosome [30]. The autophagosome then fuses with a lysosome, where the engulfed mitochondria are degraded [31]. Other proteins like BNIP3, NIX, and FUNDC1 also mediate mitophagy by interacting with LC3, a protein associated with the autophagosome membrane, promoting the recruitment of autophagic machinery to damaged mitochondria [27]. These mechanisms ensure precise control over mitochondrial quality and quantity, crucial for cellular health [28]. Mitophagy plays a significant role in preventing neurodegenerative diseases by eliminating dysfunctional mitochondria, thus reducing oxidative stress and preventing neuronal cell death [32]. In neurodegenerative diseases like Parkinson's and Alzheimer's, the accumulation of damaged mitochondria leads to increased ROS production and reduced ATP levels, contributing to neuronal damage and death [29]. Enhanced mitophagy removes these defective mitochondria, thereby reducing oxidative stress and protecting neurons from apoptosis [27]. For example, mutations in PINK1 and Parkin, which are critical for mitophagy, have been linked to familial forms of Parkinson's disease, highlighting the importance of mitophagy in neuronal health [28]. By maintaining mitochondrial quality and function, mitophagy helps prevent the accumulation of cellular damage, which is a contributing factor to the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases [32]. Targeting mitophagy presents a novel therapeutic approach in cancer treatment by manipulating the removal of dysfunctional mitochondria to either promote cancer cell death or to inhibit cancer progression [33]. Cancer cells often exhibit altered metabolism and increased ROS production, relying on mitophagy for survival under metabolic stress and therapeutic intervention [34]. By inhibiting mitophagy, cancer cells can be sensitized to chemotherapy and radiation therapy, as the accumulation of damaged mitochondria increases ROS levels and promotes apoptosis [35]. Conversely, in certain contexts, stimulating mitophagy can remove excessively damaged mitochondria, preventing malignant transformation and tumor progression [36]. Key targets for therapeutic intervention include modulating the activity of PINK1 and Parkin, or targeting other regulators of mitophagy like BNIP3 and FUNDC1, to influence the balance between cell survival and death in cancer cells [34]. Such strategies require a deep understanding of the specific roles of mitophagy in different cancer types and stages, offering a promising but complex avenue for cancer therapy [37, 38]. BNIP3 and NIX are mitophagy receptors located on the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM), playing a significant role in the degradation of mitochondria within autophagosomes [57]. Both proteins share a 56% homology and possess a Bcl-2 homology 3 (BH3) domain for interacting with Bcl-2, influencing apoptosis and programmed cell death [58]. Their ability to bind LC3 directly through an LIR motif is crucial for attracting mitochondria to autophagosomes [59]. BNIP3 and NIX also help scavenge excess reactive oxygen species (ROS), maintaining cellular homeostasis [57]. BNIP3, in particular, is critical in hypoxic conditions for mitochondrial turnover, indicating its role in adapting to environmental stress by promoting mitophagy [59].

p62/SQSTM1 is a versatile autophagy cargo receptor that plays a central role in selective autophagy by mediating the degradation of protein aggregates and damaged organelles [60]. It contains multiple domains, including PB1, LIR, and UBA, which are crucial for its function in linking ubiquitination and autophagy [61]. p62 facilitates the delivery of ubiquitinated substrates to autophagosomes and proteasomes for degradation, maintaining cellular homeostasis [62]. The interaction of p62 with LC3 and ubiquitinated substrates, coupled with its ability to bind the 19S proteasome subunit, underscores its essential role in coordinating the selective degradation of cellular components, thereby preventing the accumulation of damaged proteins and organelles [63]. The phosphorylation of BNIP3 at Ser 17/24 is a critical post-translational modification that enhances its binding affinity for LC3, thereby initiating the process of mitophagy [34]. This phosphorylation event is essential for BNIP3's role in mitochondrial quality control, particularly under stress conditions such as hypoxia [64]. Phosphorylated BNIP3 promotes the recruitment of mitochondria to autophagosomes for degradation, facilitating the removal of damaged or unnecessary mitochondria [29]. This process is crucial for maintaining mitochondrial integrity and function, highlighting the significance of BNIP3 phosphorylation in regulating mitophagy and cellular homeostasis [30].

The interaction between autophagy-related genes (Atgs) and mitophagy receptors such as Atg32, BNIP3, and NIX is crucial for the selective degradation of mitochondria [65]. These receptors identify and target damaged or superfluous mitochondria for degradation, ensuring the maintenance of mitochondrial quality and cellular health [30]. Atg proteins, facilitated by adaptor proteins like Atg32, assemble the autophagic machinery required for the engulfment and transport of targeted mitochondria to lysosomes or vacuoles for degradation [29]. This selective process is essential for cellular homeostasis, energy regulation, and the prevention of cellular stress and apoptosis, underscoring the critical role of Atgs and mitophagy receptors in mitochondrial quality control [58].

Cells regulate the activation and function of p62/SQSTM1 in selective autophagy through a variety of mechanisms, including post-translational modifications, interaction with ubiquitinated substrates, and changes in p62 expression levels [66]. Phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and oligomerization of p62 are critical for its ability to bind to LC3 on autophagosomes and ubiquitinated cargo, facilitating their selective degradation [67]. p62's PB1 domain allows for self-oligomerization, enhancing its capacity to aggregate cargo [68]. The UBA domain's interaction with ubiquitinated proteins and LC3 is pivotal for recruiting cargo to the autophagy machinery [69]. Additionally, the cellular levels of p62 are tightly regulated through transcriptional mechanisms in response to autophagic activity; an accumulation of p62 can signal a reduction in autophagic flux, whereas its degradation indicates active autophagy [70]. This regulatory balance ensures that p62 efficiently manages cellular debris and protein aggregates, maintaining cellular homeostasis [71]. Environmental stress conditions, such as hypoxia and nutrient deprivation, significantly influence the role of BNIP3 and NIX in mitophagy by modulating their expression levels and activity [72]. Under hypoxic conditions, the transcription of BNIP3 and NIX is upregulated as part of the cellular adaptive response, enhancing mitochondrial turnover to remove damaged mitochondria and to adapt to reduced oxygen availability [73]. This upregulation is critical for maintaining energy homeostasis and preventing the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can lead to oxidative stress and cell death [74]. Moreover, environmental stresses can also trigger post-translational modifications of BNIP3 and NIX, such as phosphorylation, which enhance their ability to initiate mitophagy by improving their interaction with LC3 [75]. Thus, BNIP3 and NIX play a vital role in the cellular response to stress by modulating mitophagy, highlighting their importance in maintaining cellular and mitochondrial homeostasis under adverse conditions [76]. The interplay between autophagy receptors, such as p62, and the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) is central to the cellular protein degradation pathways, ensuring the efficient turnover of proteins and maintenance of proteostasis [60]. p62 serves as a critical link between these two systems by recognizing and binding to ubiquitinated proteins through its UBA domain and facilitating their degradation either through autophagy or the proteasome [77]. This dual role allows cells to selectively degrade misfolded, aggregated, or damaged proteins that cannot be processed by the proteasome, especially under conditions where the UPS is overwhelmed or impaired [78]. The PB1 domain of p62 also interacts with components of the proteasome, directing cargoes for proteasomal degradation [62]. This dynamic coordination between autophagy receptors and the UPS plays a crucial role in cellular responses to stress, protein quality control, and the prevention of diseases related to protein aggregation [79]. The BH3 domain in BNIP3 and NIX is crucial for their function in mitophagy and apoptosis regulation, acting as a key mediator in their interactions with the Bcl-2 family of proteins [57]. This domain enables BNIP3 and NIX to bind to anti-apoptotic proteins like Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL, inhibiting their function and promoting apoptosis under certain conditions [64]. In the context of mitophagy, the BH3 domain is essential for the integration of mitochondrial quality control with cellular apoptosis pathways [80]. By influencing mitochondrial respiration and ROS levels through their localization to the mitochondria and ER, BNIP3 and NIX can trigger cell death in response to excessive damage or stress, ensuring the removal of dysfunctional cells [81]. The dual role of the BH3 domain in promoting mitophagy and apoptosis highlights the sophisticated mechanisms cells employ to maintain homeostasis and respond to cellular stress [34].

Signals that initiate autophagy, like lack of nutrients, activate the 5′-AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK). PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1)/Parkin pathway:

The PINK1/Parkin pathway plays a critical role in selective autophagy, specifically in the process known as mitophagy, which is the targeted degradation of damaged mitochondria [27, 37]. This pathway begins with the accumulation of PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1) on the outer membrane of impaired mitochondria [92]. PINK1's accumulation signals the recruitment of Parkin, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, which then ubiquitinates various mitochondrial proteins, marking the mitochondria for autophagic degradation [33]. The efficient removal of dysfunctional mitochondria through this pathway is vital for preventing the buildup of mitochondrial DNA mutations and oxidative stress, two factors that are intimately linked with the development and progression of cancer [93]. By ensuring the clearance of damaged mitochondria, the PINK1/Parkin pathway helps maintain cellular homeostasis and prevents the cellular stress and genomic instability that can lead to cancer [94]. Dysfunction within the PINK1/Parkin pathway can lead to the inadequate removal of damaged mitochondria, resulting in the accumulation of mitochondrial DNA mutations, increased oxidative stress, and the release of pro-inflammatory factors [95]. These conditions foster a cellular environment conducive to tumorigenesis by promoting genomic instability, altering cellular metabolism, and inducing chronic inflammation, all of which are recognized hallmarks of cancer [96]. Specifically, the failure to remove damaged mitochondria efficiently can lead to enhanced production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and a shift towards aerobic glycolysis, known as the Warburg effect, which supports rapid cancer cell growth and survival [97]. Alterations in the PINK1/Parkin pathway significantly impact cellular metabolism, contributing to the metabolic reprogramming observed in cancer cells [98]. The accumulation of damaged mitochondria due to an ineffective PINK1/Parkin pathway can lead to increased ROS production and a shift towards glycolysis, even under aerobic conditions (Warburg effect), which is a common metabolic adaptation in cancer cells [99]. This shift not only provides a growth advantage to cancer cells by supplying energy and biosynthetic precursors at a higher rate but also contributes to the acidic tumor microenvironment that favors cancer progression and resistance to therapy [100]. Addressing these metabolic alterations through the restoration of the PINK1/Parkin pathway could reverse the metabolic advantages that cancer cells gain, making them more susceptible to conventional therapies [101]. While targeting the PINK1/Parkin pathway presents a promising therapeutic strategy, several challenges must be addressed to translate this into effective cancer treatments [102]. First, the complexity of mitochondrial biology and the diverse roles of autophagy in cancer make it difficult to predict the outcomes of modulating this pathway [103]. Second, the development of specific and effective modulators of PINK1 or Parkin activity that can selectively target cancer cells without affecting normal cells poses a significant challenge [104]. Additionally, understanding the interplay between the PINK1/Parkin pathway and other cellular processes involved in cancer progression is crucial for designing therapies that can effectively combat the disease without unintended side effects [105]. BNIP3/NIX pathway

In the tumor microenvironment, where low oxygen levels are common, the BNIP3/NIX pathway is crucial for targeted autophagy, especially for the removal of mitochondria [57]. This pathway, distinct from the PINK1/Parkin route, utilizes BNIP3 and its counterpart NIX, both BH3-only proteins, to trigger the breakdown of mitochondria [106]. They do this by binding with LC3, a key component in forming autophagosomes, which leads to the consumption and recycling of mitochondria [73]. This process is of particular importance in cancer as it helps cancer cells to survive by avoiding cell death caused by mitochondrial malfunction in hypoxic conditions [30]. Key players in this mechanism include BNIP3, which codes for a protein that aids in getting rid of mitochondria in low oxygen conditions, thus dodging cell death; NIX, similar to BNIP3, contributes to mitophagy during hypoxia, promoting cell adaptation and endurance; LC3, a significant protein in autophagy that assists BNIP3 and NIX in the autophagic process; Bcl-2, known for its role in preventing apoptosis, also influences autophagy through its interaction with BNIP3; and HIF-1α, which under hypoxic conditions, stabilizes and boosts the levels of BNIP3 and NIX, facilitating mitophagy and enhancing cell survival [73]. The BNIP3/NIX pathway plays a pivotal role in contributing to therapy resistance in cancer cells by enhancing their ability to survive under stress conditions, such as hypoxia, which is often a consequence of therapeutic interventions [34]. This pathway is instrumental in the selective degradation of damaged mitochondria through mitophagy, thereby maintaining mitochondrial function and cellular energy production [59]. These are key factors that enable cancer cell survival and proliferation [108]. The process involves the intricate involvement of specific genes and factors: Firstly, BNIP3 is crucial in promoting mitophagy, allowing cancer cells to eliminate damaged mitochondria [109]. This reduction in dysfunctional mitochondria decreases the likelihood of apoptosis, contributing significantly to the resistance against therapies that target mitochondrial dysfunction [29]. Secondly, NIX operates similarly to BNIP3, aiding in the survival of cancer cells under therapeutic stress by ensuring the efficient removal and recycling of dysfunctional mitochondria [110]. Another vital component is ATG5, an essential gene related to autophagy [111]. ATG5 facilitates the expansion of the autophagosome membrane, crucial for the sequestration and degradation of mitochondria mediated by BNIP3 and NIX [112]. In addition, p62/SQSTM1 acts as a bridge between LC3 and ubiquitinated substrates, including damaged mitochondria [113]. The regulation of p62/SQSTM1 is critical for the selective autophagic process, thereby influencing the resistance to therapy [114]. Lastly, Beclin-1 is a key player in autophagosome nucleation [115]. Its interaction with Bcl-2 and the BNIP3/NIX pathway is vital for the autophagic response under therapeutic stress [116]. This interaction promotes cancer cell survival, further complicating the challenge in cancer therapy [117]. The regulation of BNIP3 and NIX by hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) plays a pivotal role in cancer progression, primarily due to the influence of HIFs in adapting to hypoxic conditions within the tumor microenvironment [57]. This regulation manifests in various ways that significantly impact cancer progression [106]. HIF-1α, a critical transcription factor, directly upregulates the expression of BNIP3 and NIX in response to hypoxia [118]. This upregulation facilitates mitophagy, aiding in the survival of cancer cells in low oxygen conditions [73]. BNIP3, as a target of HIF-1α, becomes upregulated under hypoxic conditions [119]. Its increased expression is vital for maintaining mitochondrial quality and energy production, which are crucial for the proliferation and metastasis of cancer cells [120]. Similarly, the upregulation of NIX by HIF-1α under hypoxic conditions enables the selective removal of damaged mitochondria [121]. This process is essential for the metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells, contributing to their aggressive behavior [122]. Furthermore, the influence of HIF-1α extends to the regulation of other genes like REDD1, a target gene that modulates mTOR signaling [123]. This modulation indirectly influences the expression of BNIP3 and NIX and their autophagic activities, impacting cancer cell survival and growth [124]. In addition to these factors, VEGF, another target of HIF-1α, plays a significant role in promoting angiogenesis [125]. However, it also affects the oxygenation status of the tumor microenvironment [126]. This change in oxygenation indirectly impacts BNIP3/NIX-mediated mitophagy, further influencing the progression of cancer [127]. These examples underscore the complex interplay between HIFs, BNIP3, NIX, and other related factors in the tumor microenvironment, highlighting their critical roles in the progression of cancer [128].

BNIP3/NIX-mediated mitophagy plays a crucial role in cancer cell metabolism and influences therapeutic outcomes significantly [34]. This selective autophagy process is pivotal for cancer cells to adapt to metabolic stress, as it ensures the removal of damaged mitochondria [59]. This maintenance of mitochondrial function is vital for energy production, which in turn is essential for cancer cell growth and survival [129]. The influence of this process on cancer cell metabolism and therapeutic outcomes is multifaceted: Firstly, BNIP3 facilitates the removal of dysfunctional mitochondria, thereby maintaining efficient mitochondrial respiration and ATP production [35]. These functions are critical for the sustenance and proliferation of cancer cells [94]. Secondly, NIX plays a key role in mitophagy, enhancing the metabolic flexibility of cancer cells [109]. This ability allows cancer cells to adjust to varying energy demands and stress conditions, giving them a survival advantage [37]. Additionally, AMPK, activated in response to metabolic stress, can induce mitophagy via BNIP3/NIX [130]. This activation promotes cellular energy homeostasis and may influence responses to metabolic therapies [131]. mTOR, a central regulator of cell growth and metabolism, is also affected by the status of mitophagy [132]. The modulation of mTOR signaling by mitophagy can affect cancer cell sensitivity to mTOR inhibitors [133]. PGC-1α, which is involved in mitochondrial biogenesis, can be regulated by levels of mitophagy [134]. This regulation impacts the metabolic phenotype of cancer cells and their adaptation to therapy-induced stress [135]. The FUNDC1 pathway also plays a significant role [34]. FUNDC1, a protein in the mitochondrial outer membrane, mediates hypoxia-induced mitophagy [136]. This process is essential for maintaining mitochondrial quality and efficient ATP production under hypoxic conditions, supporting tumor cell adaptation and survival in the tumor microenvironment [59]. AMBRA1, acting upstream of PINK1 and Parkin, facilitates the recruitment of Parkin to mitochondria, enhancing mitophagy [129]. Its role in cancer is not only limited to mitochondrial quality control but also extends to the regulation of cellular growth signals [84]. Furthermore, the Bcl-2/Beclin 1 complex is crucial in this context [133]. Beclin 1, a key autophagy protein, can be inhibited by Bcl-2 [131]. The disassociation of Beclin 1 from Bcl-2 is essential for initiating autophagy, including mitophagy [135]. Alterations in the expression of Bcl-2 and Beclin 1, often observed in cancer, affect mitochondrial integrity and autophagy [86]. Lastly, hypoxia plays a significant role in this scenario [136]. Hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), key regulators of cellular responses to low oxygen levels in the Tumor Microenvironment (TME), are pivotal [137]. HIF1α can induce the expression of BNIP3 and NIX, promoting mitophagy to adapt to hypoxic conditions [58]. This adaptation is crucial for cancer cell survival and can contribute to increased aggressiveness [138].

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)

Elevated ROS levels in the TME can trigger mitophagy as a protective mechanism to eliminate damaged mitochondria and reduce oxidative stress [139]. However, persistent ROS can also lead to DNA damage and cancer progression [140]. Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) play a critical role in modulating selective autophagy within the tumor microenvironment (TME), acting through a dual mechanism that impacts cancer cell survival and progression [141]. Elevated ROS levels can initiate protective autophagy, specifically mitophagy, to remove damaged mitochondria, thereby reducing oxidative stress and potentially limiting cancer progression [142]. For example, genes such as PINK1 and PARKIN are instrumental in identifying and targeting damaged mitochondria for degradation, while BNIP3, NIX, and FUNDC1 mediate mitophagy under hypoxic conditions often found in the TME, contributing to cell survival under stress [143]. Each of these factors plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis by ensuring the removal of dysfunctional mitochondria, thus preventing the accumulation of ROS and minimizing DNA damage [144]. However, persistent high levels of ROS can exceed the cell's autophagic capacity, leading to DNA damage, mutation accumulation, and ultimately, cancer progression [145]. This dichotomy underscores the complex interplay between ROS, selective autophagy, and cancer dynamics within the TME [146]. Several key genes and factors regulate ROS-induced selective autophagy in cancer, with each playing a unique and vital role in this process [23]. PINK1 (PTEN-induced putative kinase 1) acts as a mitochondrial damage sensor [24]. It accumulates on the outer mitochondrial membrane, initiating mitophagy by recruiting PARKIN [25]. PARKIN (PRKN), an E3 ubiquitin ligase, is recruited by PINK1 to damaged mitochondria [147]. There, it ubiquitinates outer mitochondrial membrane proteins, marking them for degradation [148]. BNIP3 (BCL2/adenovirus E1B 19kDa interacting protein 3) induces mitophagy under hypoxia by disrupting the BCL2-BECN1 complex [149]. This promotes autophagy and contributes to cell survival in low oxygen conditions [150]. Similarly, NIX (also known as BNIP3L) promotes mitophagy during erythrocyte maturation and under hypoxic conditions, aiding in the adaptation of cancer cells to the Tumor Microenvironment (TME) [151]. Lastly, FUNDC1 (FUN14 domain containing 1) mediates hypoxia-induced mitophagy through its interaction with LC3 [152]. This plays a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial quality and cellular homeostasis under stress [153]. Persistent ROS (Reactive Oxygen Species) levels play a significant role in DNA damage and cancer progression through various mechanisms [154]. When ROS levels are elevated, they can cause oxidative damage to DNA, which is a key factor in mutations and genomic instability, both of which are hallmarks of cancer development [155]. Several key mechanisms and proteins are involved in countering this damage: Firstly, OGG1 (8-Oxoguanine DNA glycosylase) is crucial as it initiates the base excision repair of 8-oxoguanine, a common DNA lesion caused by ROS, thus preventing mutagenesis [156]. This process is essential for maintaining the integrity of the DNA [157]. Secondly, APE1 (Apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease 1) plays a vital role in repairing DNA damage induced by ROS [158]. APE1 cleaves the DNA backbone at apurinic/apyrimidinic sites, which is a critical step in facilitating nucleotide insertion and repair [159]. In addition, PRDX1 (Peroxiredoxin 1) acts as an antioxidant [160]. It functions by reducing hydrogen peroxide levels, thereby limiting oxidative damage to cellular components, including DNA [160]. This reduction in hydrogen peroxide is crucial for preventing further DNA damage [153]. Furthermore, NRF2 (Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2) is a transcription factor that regulates the expression of antioxidant proteins [161]. These proteins provide protection against oxidative damage induced by ROS, thereby playing a protective role against DNA damage [149]. Lastly, TP53 (Tumor Protein p53) is a critical tumor suppressor [162]. It activates DNA repair proteins in response to DNA damage [163]. If the damage is irreparable, TP53 can initiate apoptosis, thus preventing the proliferation of damaged cells that could lead to cancer [164].

Therapeutic strategies targeting ROS-mediated autophagy in cancer focus on modulating ROS levels or enhancing the autophagic response to prevent cancer progression [154]. Potential approaches include using antioxidants, such as N-acetylcysteine (NAC), to reduce ROS levels, thereby minimizing oxidative stress and its associated DNA damage, which could potentially prevent cancer initiation and progression [155]. Another strategy involves the use of mitophagy inducers like urolithin A, which can specifically induce mitophagy, aiding in the elimination of damaged mitochondria, reducing ROS production, and limiting cancer cell survival [156]. Additionally, autophagy modulators, such as hydroxychloroquine, can inhibit late stages of autophagy and might be used to prevent cancer cells from exploiting autophagy for survival under high ROS conditions [157]. In certain contexts, promoting ROS generation to cytotoxic levels with drugs like arsenic trioxide can overwhelm cancer cells' antioxidant defenses, leading to cell death [158]. Finally, targeting ROS-producing enzymes with inhibitors of enzymes like NOX (NADPH oxidases) could help in reducing oxidative stress in cancer cells, offering another avenue for therapeutic intervention [159]. Alterations in autophagic pathways are significantly linked to the resistance of cancer cells to therapy, with reactive oxygen species (ROS) playing a pivotal role in this adaptive resistance mechanism [110]. Key autophagy-related genes such as ATG5 (Autophagy-related 5) and ATG7 (Autophagy-related 7) are essential for normal autophagic processes [165]. When these genes are altered, they can lead to abnormal autophagic flux, which in turn affects cancer cell sensitivity to chemotherapy and targeted therapies [166]. Similarly, BECLIN-1 (BECN1), a crucial regulator of autophagy, can contribute to autophagy-mediated therapy resistance in cancer cells [136]. Its dysregulation enhances cancer cell survival under therapeutic stress [167]. Furthermore, modifications in LC3 (Microtubule-associated proteins 1A/1B light chain 3B) associated phagocytosis can influence the degradation of extracellular material, thereby impacting the response to therapy [168]. Another significant player is SQSTM1/p62 (Sequestosome 1), which acts as a bridge between LC3 and ubiquitinated substrates [169]. The accumulation of SQSTM1/p62 is linked with defective autophagy and can contribute to therapeutic resistance [170]. Lastly, AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase), which regulates cellular energy homeostasis and autophagy, plays a role in this complex mechanism [171]. Alterations in AMPK signaling can lead to enhanced survival mechanisms in cancer cells, including resistance to apoptosis, further complicating the challenge of effectively treating cancer [172].

Nutrient deprivation

Starvation conditions in the TME can activate AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), which in turn initiates mitophagy by phosphorylating components of the mitophagy machinery, including ULK1 and Beclin 1, to maintain energy homeostasis [173]. In the context of nutrient deprivation within the tumor microenvironment (TME), the activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) plays a pivotal role in promoting selective autophagy to maintain energy homeostasis in cancer cells [174]. AMPK, a cellular energy sensor, responds to low energy states by initiating autophagy through the phosphorylation of key components involved in the autophagy machinery [175]. For example, in response to nutrient deprivation, AMPK phosphorylates ULK1 (Unc-51 Like Autophagy Activating Kinase 1) to initiate autophagy. This signaling axis is not only essential for general autophagic response but is also specifically critical for executing mitophagy, ensuring the clearance of defective mitochondria and maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis during energy stress. ULK1 acts at the initiation step of autophagosome formation, integrating nutrient signaling to autophagy machinery [176]. Beclin 1 is part of the Vps34 complex that nucleates the phagophore, the initial step in autophagosome formation [177]. Through these actions, AMPK ensures that energy production is optimized under nutrient-scarce conditions, facilitating cancer cell survival and adaptation in the challenging TME [178]. Under starvation conditions, AMPK plays a pivotal role in triggering selective autophagy, primarily through the phosphorylation and activation of several vital proteins and factors [179, 180]. One of these is ULK1, a serine/threonine-protein kinase critical for autophagy initiation [181]. AMPK phosphorylates ULK1, leading to the activation of autophagy-related proteins and the formation of the autophagosome [182]. Another key player is Beclin 1, which forms part of the class III PI3K complex [183]. This complex is essential for the nucleation of autophagosomal membranes, and its function is enhanced by AMPK-mediated phosphorylation of Beclin 1, thereby boosting autophagy induction [184]. Additionally, ATG7, an E1-like enzyme, has a significant role in two ubiquitin-like conjugation systems, which are crucial for the expansion and completion of autophagosomes [185]. Inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 regulate mitophagy, a selective form of autophagy targeting mitochondria, in several ways [114]. TNF-α can induce mitochondrial dysfunction and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, triggering the removal of damaged mitochondria to maintain cellular homeostasis [204]. This process is crucial for cancer cells to avoid apoptosis and sustain energy production [205]. IL-6, through its action on the JAK/STAT pathway, may upregulate expression of BCL2 family members, indirectly influencing mitophagy by modulating mitochondrial integrity and autophagosome formation [206]. This cytokine-driven regulation of mitophagy demonstrates a survival mechanism employed by cancer cells, highlighting potential therapeutic targets within the autophagic and inflammatory signaling pathways [207]. TNF-α and IL-6 contribute to immune evasion in cancer by modulating selective autophagy pathways that affect antigen presentation and cytokine production [208]. TNF-α can alter the degradation of immune regulatory molecules, such as MHC class I molecules, through autophagy, affecting the visibility of cancer cells to the immune system [209]. IL-6, by promoting autophagy, can suppress the intracellular processing and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby reducing their accumulation within the tumor microenvironment and subsequently limiting the recruitment and activation of immune cells [210]. Additionally, IL-6 can influence the differentiation of T cells into regulatory T cells (Tregs), further suppressing the immune response against cancer cells [211]. These mechanisms underscore the complex role of inflammatory cytokines in shaping the immune landscape of tumors, facilitating cancer cell escape from immune surveillance [212]. TNF-α and IL-6 impact cancer cell proliferation through selective autophagy by regulating cellular metabolism and the removal of damaged organelles [213]. TNF-α can induce autophagy to recycle cellular components, providing substrates for energy production and biosynthesis, thus supporting cancer cell growth [214]. IL-6, through the activation of the STAT3 pathway, can enhance the expression of autophagy-related genes, promoting survival and proliferation under stress conditions [215]. These cytokines ensure the maintenance of cellular homeostasis and the availability of resources necessary for continuous cancer cell proliferation, highlighting their dual role in cancer progression and autophagy regulation [216]. Targeting TNF-α and IL-6 pathways presents a promising therapeutic strategy in cancer by modulating selective autophagy [217]. Inhibiting TNF-α can disrupt its pro-survival signaling, potentially sensitizing cancer cells to apoptosis and reducing their ability to sustain proliferation under stress [218]. Blocking IL-6 signaling may impair cancer cell survival mechanisms that depend on autophagy, such as nutrient recycling and organelle turnover [219]. Therapeutic agents targeting these cytokines could, therefore, limit cancer progression by altering the autophagic balance towards cell death rather than survival [220]. This approach underscores the therapeutic value of understanding and manipulating the intricate connections between inflammation, autophagy, and cancer [221].

ER-phagy

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) consists of two primary structural components: rough ER and smooth ER [222]. The rough ER is characterized by the presence of ribosomes on its surface, which are essential for protein synthesis [223]. It serves as a pathway for the transport of newly synthesized proteins and provides a platform for ribosome attachment [224]. In contrast, the smooth ER lacks ribosomes on its surface, which distinguishes it from the rough ER [225]. The smooth ER plays a critical role in various cellular processes, including the synthesis of lipids and steroids, the detoxification of harmful substances, and the regulation of calcium ion concentration [226]. These structural differences between the rough and smooth ER underscore their distinct functional roles within the cell, highlighting the ER's versatility and importance in cellular homeostasis and metabolism [227]. ER stress is triggered by the accumulation of misfolded proteins and aggregates within the ER lumen, often resulting from stressful or adverse conditions [228]. To counteract this stress, cells activate two key quality-control mechanisms: ER-associated degradation (ERAD) and the unfolded protein response (UPR) [229]. ERAD facilitates the identification and transport of terminally misfolded proteins from the ER to the cytoplasm for degradation by the ubiquitin–proteasome system [230]. Concurrently, UPR enhances the ER's protein-folding capacity and reduces overall protein translation through the PERK-eIF2α signaling pathway [231]. This pathway also activates the transcription of genes involved in the ER stress response, helping to restore ER function and maintain cellular homeostasis [232]. Excessive ER stress, caused by the buildup of aggregated or misfolded proteins in the ER lumen, triggers ER-phagy [233]. This process involves the selective degradation of ER fragments through autophagy, effectively removing the excess or damaged ER material [234]. ER-phagy plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis by ensuring the ER's functionality and health are preserved [235]. By eliminating problematic proteins and organelle fragments, ER-phagy helps to prevent further stress and potential cellular damage, highlighting its importance in the cellular stress response and survival mechanisms [236].

FAM134B

FAM134B plays a pivotal role in maintaining endoplasmic reticulum (ER) homeostasis by mediating the selective degradation of ER sheets, a process essential for the proper functioning of the ER [237]. Positioned on the ER sheets, FAM134B identifies and targets damaged or redundant portions of the ER for degradation through autophagy, a process known as ER-phagy [238]. This selective degradation is crucial for maintaining the proper size and function of the ER, particularly under stress conditions when the ER undergoes expansion to meet increased protein-folding demands [239]. FAM134B's role is vital for protein quality control, ensuring that misfolded or excessive proteins are efficiently removed to prevent cellular stress and maintain cellular homeostasis [240]. This process is especially important in cells with high protein synthesis rates, such as secretory cells [241]. By regulating ER-phagy, FAM134B indirectly influences various cellular processes, including the unfolded protein response (UPR), lipid metabolism, and cellular signaling pathways, reflecting the multifaceted role of the ER in cellular physiology [242]. The dysfunction of FAM134B is linked to several disease pathologies, notably neurodegenerative diseases [243]. FAM134B mutations or deficiencies can lead to the accumulation of damaged ER, resulting in ER stress and triggering a cascade of pathological events [244]. In the context of neurodegenerative diseases, the inefficient removal of damaged ER can lead to the accumulation of misfolded proteins, a hallmark of various neurodegenerative conditions like Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [245]. Furthermore, FAM134B dysfunction impairs neuronal homeostasis, affecting the survival and function of neurons [246]. This can lead to neuronal cell death, contributing to the progressive loss of neural functions seen in these diseases [247]. Additionally, FAM134B dysfunction may disrupt intracellular signaling pathways and lipid metabolism, exacerbating the disease pathology [248]. Research into FAM134B's role in neurodegeneration not only aids in understanding the molecular basis of these diseases but also opens up potential therapeutic avenues targeting ER-phagy and protein quality control mechanisms [249]. In cancer biology, FAM134B's role is emerging as a significant factor influencing cancer progression and treatment [250]. FAM134B-mediated ER-phagy can impact cell survival, particularly under stress conditions common in tumor microenvironments, such as hypoxia and nutrient deprivation [251]. In some cancers, the upregulation of FAM134B may facilitate tumor survival by enabling cancer cells to manage ER stress effectively and maintain protein homeostasis [252]. Conversely, in other contexts, FAM134B downregulation or mutations could contribute to cancer progression by disrupting cellular homeostasis and promoting oncogenic signaling pathways [253]. The dual role of FAM134B in cancer reflects the complexity of autophagy-related processes in tumorigenesis [254]. Understanding FAM134B's role in specific cancer types can provide insights into tumor biology and highlight potential therapeutic targets [255]. For instance, modulating FAM134B activity could enhance the effectiveness of cancer therapies that induce ER stress or could be used to target vulnerabilities in cancers that rely on ER-phagy for survival [256]. FAM134B interacts with a network of proteins and signaling pathways to regulate ER-phagy and cellular homeostasis [257]. These interactions are crucial for the selective targeting and degradation of ER sheets [255]. For example, FAM134B interacts with ATG proteins, essential components of the autophagic machinery, to facilitate the encapsulation of ER fragments into autophagosomes [258]. It also interacts with LC3/GABARAP proteins, linking the ER to the autophagic process [259]. Additionally, FAM134B's function is influenced by signaling pathways that regulate ER stress and the unfolded protein response (UPR) [260]. Under conditions of ER stress, the UPR is activated, leading to the upregulation of FAM134B expression as part of the adaptive response to manage ER load [261]. Moreover, FAM134B may interact with signaling pathways involved in apoptosis, cell survival, and metabolism, reflecting its broader impact on cellular physiology [262]. By understanding these interactions, researchers can gain insights into the regulatory mechanisms governing ER-phagy and identify potential intervention points for diseases associated with ER dysfunction [263].

RTN3L

RTN3L plays a pivotal role in the process of autophagy, specifically within the context of the Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER), a phenomenon termed ER-phagy [264]. Autophagy is a crucial cellular process where cells degrade and recycle their own components [265]. ER-phagy, a selective form of autophagy, targets the ER specifically [262]. In this process, RTN3L facilitates the identification and isolation of parts of the tubular ER that require degradation [266]. It serves as a marker or signal for autophagosomes, the cellular structures that encase and transport cellular waste to lysosomes for degradation [267]. RTN3L's role is vital in ensuring that only the portions of the ER that are damaged or redundant are earmarked for autophagy [268]. This selective action is essential for maintaining ER homeostasis, particularly under stress conditions or damage scenarios, such as exposure to toxins, metabolic shifts, or disease states [269]. The degradation of the tubular ER through RTN3L-mediated ER-phagy assists in the reshaping of the ER network, adjusting to cellular needs, and averting ER stress, which, if left unchecked, can lead to cell death [270]. Several genes and factors are instrumental in ER-phagy [271]. These include FAM134B, a reticulophagy receptor responsible for the fragmentation and lysosomal degradation of the ER; SEC62, which plays a role in ER-phagy under conditions of ER stress to restore ER functions; the ATG8 family, a group of ubiquitin-like proteins essential for the formation of autophagosomes; LAMP2, or Lysosome-associated membrane protein 2, crucial for the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes; and VPS34, a class III PI3 kinase, fundamental for initiating the formation of autophagosomes [272]. These components collectively contribute to the efficient functioning and regulation of ER-phagy, highlighting the complexity and sophistication of cellular maintenance mechanisms [263]. RTN3L plays a crucial role in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress response, a vital cellular defense mechanism triggered under conditions of ER dysfunction [265]. ER stress arises when the ER's capacity for protein folding is exceeded, leading to an accumulation of misfolded or unfolded proteins [273]. RTN3L steps in to alleviate this stress by mediating the degradation of specific parts of the tubular ER, particularly areas burdened with misfolded proteins [252]. This degradation, facilitated by RTN3L, is key in restoring normal ER function and averting the activation of apoptotic pathways that prolonged ER stress can initiate [274]. The significance of RTN3L in mitigating ER stress extends to disease prevention, especially in neurodegenerative diseases and cancers, where ER stress is a known contributing factor [275]. By maintaining ER homeostasis, RTN3L plays a pivotal role in preventing conditions linked to protein misfolding and aggregation [276]. Furthermore, RTN3L may influence the immune response and inflammation, both of which are associated with various diseases [277]. Several key genes and factors are involved in the ER stress response [278]. These include BiP/GRP78, a chaperone that binds misfolded proteins in the ER, thus reducing ER stress [253]. CALR, or calreticulin, is another chaperone crucial for proper protein folding and calcium homeostasis [279]. ERN1, Endoplasmic Reticulum to Nucleus Signaling 1, acts as a primary sensor of misfolded proteins within the ER [280]. EDEM1, or ER Degradation-Enhancing Alpha-Mannosidase-Like Protein 1, is involved in disposing of misfolded glycoproteins [281]. Lastly, SEL1L, a component of the ERAD complex, aids in the degradation of misfolded proteins, further contributing to the management and resolution of ER stress [282]. RTN3L is a key component in the protein quality control system of the cell, particularly within the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [283]. Protein quality control refers to the cellular mechanisms that ensure proteins are properly folded and functional [284]. RTN3L plays a significant role in this system by participating in the degradation of the tubular ER [285]. This involves the removal of regions where misfolded or aggregated proteins may accumulate, thus aiding in reducing the load of defective proteins within the ER [286]. Such action is vital in preventing the harmful consequences of protein aggregation, which include cellular toxicity and the activation of stress pathways [287]. The role of RTN3L in maintaining protein quality is crucial for cellular health, as the accumulation of misfolded proteins can lead to various diseases [288]. These include neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease, and conditions like diabetes and cancer [289]. By ensuring the degradation of dysfunctional ER components, RTN3L supports the cell's ability to adapt to changes in protein folding demands and environmental stressors, thereby upholding cellular function and preventing disease [290]. In addition to RTN3L, there are other significant genes and factors involved in protein quality control [291]. These include HSP70 (Heat Shock Protein 70), a chaperone that assists in protein folding and prevents aggregation; UBC (Ubiquitin-Conjugating Enzyme), involved in tagging misfolded proteins for degradation; PDI (Protein Disulfide Isomerase), which assists in the formation of disulfide bonds in proteins; NEF (Nucleotide Exchange Factors), which regulate the activity of HSP70 chaperones; and the Proteasome, a complex that degrades ubiquitinated proteins, thus maintaining the overall quality control of proteins [292]. Each of these components plays a unique and vital role in ensuring that the cellular machinery operates efficiently and effectively, safeguarding the cell against the detrimental effects of protein misfolding and aggregation [293]. Targeting RTN3L for therapeutic purposes in diseases related to ER stress and autophagy is an area of growing interest [294]. Given its role in ER-phagy and the ER stress response, RTN3L represents a potential target for modulating these processes in diseases where they are dysregulated [295]. For example, enhancing RTN3L function could be beneficial in conditions characterized by excessive ER stress or protein aggregation, such as neurodegenerative diseases [296]. In contrast, inhibiting RTN3L activity might be advantageous in cancer, where tumor cells exploit autophagy for survival [297]. Therapeutic interventions targeting RTN3L could potentially restore balance in autophagy and ER stress pathways, thereby contributing to the treatment of these diseases [298]. However, such approaches must be carefully developed to avoid unintended consequences, as both ER stress and autophagy are critical for normal cellular function [299]. The implications of targeting RTN3L in therapy include the possibility of alleviating ER stress-associated pathologies, regulating autophagy in cancer therapy, and potentially providing a new avenue for treating diseases with underlying issues in protein quality control [300]. In the context of therapeutic targeting, several genes and factors could be involved [301]. These include the Mammalian Target of Rapamycin (mTOR), a key regulator of autophagy that could be modulated alongside RTN3L [302]. BCL-2, an anti-apoptotic protein, can be targeted to regulate cell death in ER stress-related diseases [303]. AMP-Activated Protein Kinase (AMPK) is involved in cellular energy homeostasis and autophagy regulation, making it a potential co-target [304]. Transcription Factor EB (TFEB), a master regulator of lysosome biogenesis and autophagy, could also be co-targeted [305]. Lastly, Beclin-1, a central player in autophagosome formation, represents another potential co-target in modulating autophagy [306]. These factors offer multiple avenues for therapeutic intervention in conjunction with RTN3L targeting, providing a multifaceted approach to treating diseases associated with ER stress and autophagy dysregulation [269].

TEX264

TEX264 plays a crucial role in the selective autophagy of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), specifically in the degradation of tubular ER segments [270]. This process, termed ER-phagy, is vital for the maintenance of ER health and functionality [307]. TEX264 identifies and targets damaged or superfluous tubular ER segments for degradation, thus ensuring the ER's structural integrity and proper functioning [307]. This selective degradation is essential for several reasons: it prevents the accumulation of damaged ER components that could lead to cellular stress, maintains the correct balance of ER size and shape, and ensures efficient protein folding and trafficking [308]. By participating in ER-phagy, TEX264 directly contributes to the cellular response to ER stress and maintains the homeostasis of the ER, which is crucial for numerous cellular processes including protein synthesis, lipid metabolism, and calcium storage [309]. TEX264 interacts with several proteins and factors to facilitate its role in ER-phagy [307]. Firstly, distinct from autophagy receptors like FAM134B, which primarily mediate the degradation of ER sheets [271]. TEX264 specifically recognizes and targets the tubular ER segments for degradation [270]. Secondly, TEX264 works in conjunction with ATG8 family proteins, which are key components of the autophagosomal membrane [310]. These proteins help in the expansion and closure of the autophagosomal membrane around the targeted ER segment [311]. Thirdly, TEX264 also interacts with VPS34, a class III PI3-kinase, which is crucial for the nucleation of the autophagosomal membrane [312]. Additionally, the interaction with SEC62, a protein involved in ER stress response, suggests a role for TEX264 in responding to ER stress [313]. Lastly, TEX264's interaction with LC3, a key autophagy-related protein, indicates its involvement in the broader autophagy pathway [314]. Given its role in maintaining ER integrity, TEX264 dysfunction can result in unresolved ER stress, which is a contributing factor in several pathological conditions including neurodegenerative diseases, diabetes, and cancer [315]. In neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer's, persistent ER stress due to impaired ER-phagy can lead to the accumulation of misfolded proteins, exacerbating neuronal damage [315]. In diabetes, dysfunctional ER-phagy can disrupt insulin production and secretion, as the ER plays a critical role in the synthesis of insulin in pancreatic beta cells [316, 317]. In the context of cancer, impaired ER function due to TEX264 dysfunction can affect cellular homeostasis and the immune response, potentially contributing to tumorigenesis and cancer progression [318]. Furthermore, TEX264 dysfunction might impact lipid metabolism and calcium homeostasis, given the ER's role in these processes [319]. This could have implications for metabolic disorders and diseases linked to calcium signaling [252]. The significance of TEX264 in the context of therapeutic targets is particularly relevant in diseases associated with ER stress [320]. By targeting TEX264, it may be possible to modulate ER-phagy, thereby alleviating ER stress and its associated pathologies [321]. For instance, in neurodegenerative diseases, enhancing TEX264 function could aid in the clearance of misfolded proteins, potentially slowing disease progression [315]. In diabetes, stabilizing ER function via TEX264 could improve insulin synthesis and secretion [316, 317]. In cancer therapy, manipulating TEX264 activity might help in controlling tumor growth and resistance to therapy, as ER stress is a known factor in cancer cell survival [271]. Additionally, targeting TEX264 could have implications in treating metabolic disorders, where ER function is crucial [322]. The development of small molecules or biologics that can specifically modulate TEX264 activity offers a promising avenue for therapeutic intervention in these diseases [309]. TEX264's role in ER-phagy is distinct yet complementary to other genes and factors involved in this process [267]. For example, FAM134B, another ER-phagy receptor, targets a different subset of the ER (ER sheets) compared to TEX264's focus on tubular ER segments [231]. This suggests a division of labor among ER-phagy receptors for comprehensive ER maintenance [269]. ATG9A, involved in supplying membrane for autophagosome formation, is essential for the autophagy process in which TEX264 participates [268]. SEC62, like TEX264, responds to ER stress, but it specifically mediates macroER-phagy during the recovery phase from acute ER stress to clear selected UPR-upregulated proteins via the lysosomal pathway [323]. RTN3, another ER-phagy receptor, plays a role in the degradation of ER tubules, similar to TEX264, but it might target different ER populations or respond to different signals [324]. Finally, CCPG1, yet another ER-phagy receptor, is involved in the degradation of ER sheets, highlighting the specificity of different receptors for distinct ER structures [38]. Each of these factors contributes uniquely to ER-phagy, demonstrating the complexity and specificity of this process in cellular homeostasis [325].

ATL3

Like RTN3L and TEX264, ATL3 is situated in the tubular ER and participates in its degradation, highlighting the importance of maintaining tubular ER integrity [326]. ATL3, located in the tubular endoplasmic reticulum (ER), plays a crucial role in maintaining the integrity of the ER structure [327]. The ER is a vital organelle responsible for protein folding, lipid synthesis, and calcium storage [328]. ATL3 contributes to the degradation process of the ER, a process known as ER-phagy, which is critical for the removal of damaged or unnecessary ER components [329]. By participating in this selective autophagy, ATL3 helps to regulate the size and function of the ER, ensuring efficient cellular operations [329]. The degradation process mediated by ATL3 is essential in preventing ER stress, which can lead to various cellular dysfunctions and diseases [330]. For instance, improper ER function is linked to neurodegenerative diseases, diabetes, and cancer [331]. By maintaining ER integrity, ATL3 indirectly influences numerous cellular processes, including protein synthesis, lipid metabolism, and calcium signaling, all of which are vital for the maintenance of cellular homeostasis [332]. ATL3 interacts with several proteins within the tubular ER, playing a significant role in ER morphology and dynamics [333]. Its interactions are crucial for the formation and maintenance of the ER network [334]. For example, ATL3 binds with reticulons and DP1/REEP5, proteins involved in shaping the curvature of the ER membrane [335]. These interactions facilitate the formation of three-way junctions in the ER, essential for the tubular network structure [336]. ATL3 also interacts with proteins involved in lipid metabolism and protein folding, such as calnexin and calreticulin, indicating its role in these processes [337]. Disruptions in these interactions can lead to ER stress and contribute to the pathogenesis of diseases like hereditary spastic paraplegia [338]. Understanding ATL3's interactions provides insight into the mechanisms of ER maintenance and the potential impact of its dysregulation on cellular health [231]. The expression and activity of ATL3 are regulated by multiple mechanisms, ensuring proper ER function and cellular homeostasis [339]. Gene expression regulation of ATL3 can occur at the transcriptional level, influenced by factors like ER stress and cellular signaling pathways [340]. Post-translational modifications (PTMs) such as phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and sumoylation also modulate ATL3's activity [341]. These PTMs can affect ATL3's localization, stability, and interaction with other proteins [342]. Moreover, ATL3 activity is influenced by calcium levels and lipid composition in the ER membrane, which can alter its conformation and function [343]. The regulatory mechanisms of ATL3 are crucial in maintaining ER morphology and function, with implications for protein folding, lipid metabolism, and calcium signaling [344]. Dysregulation of ATL3 can lead to ER stress and associated pathologies, highlighting the importance of its proper regulation in cellular health [345]. Dysfunction in ATL3 has been linked to several pathologies, primarily due to its role in maintaining ER integrity [346]. Mutations or altered expression of ATL3 can lead to ER stress and cellular dysfunction, contributing to various diseases [1]. For example, mutations in ATL3 are associated with hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathy type 1F (HSAN1F), a group of neurodegenerative disorders [275]. ATL3 dysfunction can also contribute to neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer's and Parkinson's by disrupting protein folding and processing in the ER [267]. In metabolic diseases like diabetes, impaired ATL3 function can affect insulin production and secretion due to altered ER calcium signaling [264]. ATL3 dysfunction has also been implicated in cancer, where ER stress can influence tumor progression and response to therapy [270]. Understanding the pathological implications of ATL3 dysfunction is crucial for developing therapeutic strategies targeting ER-related diseases [271].

Targeting ATL3 and its associated pathways offers potential therapeutic strategies for diseases related to ER dysfunction [268]. For example, modulating ATL3 activity could be a strategy to alleviate ER stress in neurodegenerative and metabolic diseases [347]. Small molecule modulators or gene therapy approaches could be used to correct ATL3 dysfunction in hereditary spastic paraplegia [348]. In cancer therapy, targeting the pathways influenced by ATL3, such as ER-phagy and ER stress response, could enhance the effectiveness of existing treatments or counteract resistance mechanisms [349]. Additionally, drugs that stabilize the ER network or enhance ATL3's protective functions could be beneficial in diseases where ER integrity is compromised [350]. The development of therapies targeting ATL3 and related pathways requires a deep understanding of its molecular functions and interactions within the ER [351].

CALCOCO1

CALCOCO1, analogous to Epr1 in fission yeast, plays a crucial role in the selective autophagy process, specifically targeting the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) in human cells [352]. This gene is a part of a conserved mechanism across species known as ER-phagy, a selective form of autophagy that focuses on the degradation and recycling of the ER [353]. ER-phagy is essential for maintaining ER homeostasis, particularly under conditions of stress or damage [354]. CALCOCO1 functions by recognizing and binding to components of the ER that are marked for degradation, thereby facilitating their encapsulation within autophagosomes [316]. This process ensures the efficient removal of damaged or superfluous ER, thus maintaining cellular health and preventing various diseases associated with ER stress and dysfunction [355]. The importance of CALCOCO1 in ER-phagy highlights the evolutionary conservation of autophagy mechanisms across different species, from yeast to humans [356]. In fission yeast, Epr1 is a key player in the selective autophagy of the ER, a process analogous to what CALCOCO1 performs in human cells [357]. Both CALCOCO1 and Epr1 share a similar function in recognizing and targeting parts of the ER for autophagic degradation [354]. This similarity underscores the evolutionary conservation of ER-phagy mechanisms [283]. Epr1 in yeast and CALCOCO1 in humans both act as adaptors that identify and bind to ER components marked for degradation [354]. Their role is pivotal in maintaining ER health, especially under stress conditions [358]. This evolutionary conservation indicates the fundamental importance of ER-phagy in cellular health and survival across different species, highlighting the critical role of these genes in the autophagic process [359]. The dysfunction of CALCOCO1 in human cells can lead to significant implications in various diseases, particularly those associated with ER stress and dysfunction [360]. Since CALCOCO1 is integral to ER-phagy, its malfunction can result in the accumulation of damaged or redundant ER, contributing to cellular stress and pathology [361]. Diseases such as neurodegeneration, cancer, and metabolic disorders have been linked to disruptions in ER homeostasis [362]. In these conditions, the failure of CALCOCO1-mediated ER-phagy could exacerbate disease progression by allowing the buildup of dysfunctional ER [363]. Understanding the role and regulation of CALCOCO1 in ER-phagy thus provides insight into the pathogenesis of these diseases and opens avenues for potential therapeutic interventions targeting ER-phagy pathways [364]. ER-phagy is a critical process in maintaining cellular homeostasis, particularly under conditions of ER stress or damage [355]. CALCOCO1 contributes significantly to this process by selectively targeting portions of the ER for autophagic degradation [182]. This selective degradation is crucial for the removal of dysfunctional ER components, preventing the accumulation of misfolded proteins and maintaining the proper functioning of the ER [276]. CALCOCO1’s role in ER-phagy is therefore vital for preserving the overall health and balance of the cell [365]. By facilitating the turnover and renewal of the ER, CALCOCO1 helps to ensure that the cell can respond adequately to various stresses and maintain its metabolic and protein-folding functions, which are essential for cell survival and function [38]. The study of CALCOCO1 in human cells offers valuable insights into the evolutionary conservation of autophagy mechanisms, especially in relation to ER-phagy [284]. The functional similarities between CALCOCO1 in humans and Epr1 in fission yeast highlight a conserved evolutionary pathway across different species [366]. This conservation suggests that the fundamental mechanisms of selective autophagy, particularly those targeting the ER, are crucial for cellular health and have been preserved throughout evolution [367]. Understanding CALCOCO1’s role and regulation in human cells can provide deeper insights into the evolutionary significance of autophagy processes, helping to unravel the complexities of cellular homeostasis and disease across different species [365]. The AIM/LIR/GIM motif found in ER-phagy receptors is crucial for the selective autophagy of the ER [284]. This motif enables ER-phagy receptors to bind to Atg8/LC3/GABARAP proteins, facilitating the connection between autophagosomes and the ER [284]. By linking autophagosomes to the ER, these receptors ensure the targeted degradation of specific ER compartments or fragments, playing a vital role in cellular quality control and the maintenance of ER health [368]. The presence of the AIM/LIR/GIM motif in ER-phagy receptors underscores the mechanistic specificity of ER-phagy and highlights the complex interplay between autophagy and the ER in cellular homeostasis and stress response [276].

Mitophagy provides nutrients and energy to support cancer cell survival under hypoxia

Mitophagy plays a crucial role in fulfilling the nutritional and energy demands of cancer cells [376]. In the hypoxic and nutrient-deficient tumor microenvironment, cancer cells undergo rapid proliferation, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and subsequent induction of mitophagy [43]. This process is essential for recycling nutrients and providing amino acids for cell growth through lysosomal degradation [377]. For instance, the genes PINK1 and PARKIN are involved in identifying and marking damaged mitochondria for degradation, thus contributing to the recycling process [378]. BCL2L13, another key gene, acts as a facilitator of mitophagy, assisting in the breakdown and recycling of mitochondrial components [379]. FUNDC1, a mitochondrial outer membrane protein, regulates hypoxia-induced mitophagy, ensuring efficient nutrient recycling under low oxygen conditions [380]. NIX/BNIP3L, a protein involved in developmental mitophagy, plays a crucial role in removing superfluous mitochondria during erythrocyte maturation, reflecting its importance in cellular remodeling [34]. Lastly, BNIP3, another protein involved in mitophagy, is crucial in adapting to hypoxic conditions in the tumor microenvironment [94]. These genes and factors ensure that cancer cells efficiently recycle nutrients and maintain their energy production, primarily through glycolysis, as mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) is often downregulated in cancer cells due to mitochondrial dysfunction, a phenomenon known as the Warburg effect [381]. The Warburg effect refers to the preference of cancer cells for glycolysis over oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) for energy production, even in the presence of oxygen [382]. Mitophagy directly influences this metabolic reprogramming [383]. Mitophagy clears dysfunctional mitochondria, a process that accompanies the reduced reliance on OXPHOS and the metabolic shift toward glycolysis [384]. Key genes and factors involved include: AMPK, a cellular energy sensor that can induce mitophagy under energy stress, supporting the shift towards glycolysis; mTOR, a central regulator of cell growth and metabolism, its inhibition can trigger mitophagy, thus facilitating the Warburg effect; BECN1/Beclin 1, essential for the initiation of autophagy and mitophagy, plays a role in the metabolic switch observed in cancer cells; LC3, a protein involved in autophagosome formation, its involvement in mitophagy also impacts the metabolic pathways utilized by cancer cells; and DRP1, a protein that promotes mitochondrial fission, which is often a prerequisite for mitophagy, thereby influencing the metabolic profile of cancer cells [385]. The interaction between mitophagy and the Warburg effect is a complex interplay that allows cancer cells to adapt their metabolism to the challenging tumor microenvironment [386]. Mitophagy is a specific form of autophagy that selectively degrades mitochondria [376]. This process is crucial for maintaining the health and function of cells by removing damaged or superfluous mitochondria, thus preventing potential cell damage [395]. In the context of cancer stem cells (CSCs), mitophagy plays a vital role in preserving their stemness – the ability of CSCs to self-renew and differentiate into multiple cell types, which is central to tumor growth and progression [396]. Mitophagy regulates metabolic flexibility in CSCs, allowing them to survive in various microenvironments and contributing to their resistance to therapy [397]. For example, genes like PINK1 and PARK2 are involved in the identification and tagging of damaged mitochondria for degradation [398]. BNIP3 and NIX function as mitophagy receptors on the mitochondrial membrane [395]. FUNDC1 is involved in hypoxia-induced mitophagy, particularly relevant in the hypoxic core of tumors [399]. Each of these genes plays a crucial role in modulating the mitophagy process, thus influencing the maintenance of CSC properties. By selectively degrading mitochondria, mitophagy helps CSCs switch between oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis, depending on the environmental conditions [420]. Mitophagy promotes cancer cell invasion and metastasis

Mitophagy significantly impacts metastasis by enabling cancer cells to survive under stressful conditions encountered during the metastatic process [43]. The removal of dysfunctional mitochondria through mitophagy ensures metabolic flexibility and energy efficiency, vital for cancer cells to thrive in distant organs [433]. Genes like BCL2L13, which encodes a protein facilitating the removal of damaged mitochondria, are crucial for maintaining mitochondrial health during metastasis [434]. DRP1, a key regulator of mitochondrial fission, is involved in mitophagy and facilitates cancer metastasis by promoting cell migration and invasion [433]. MFN1 and MFN2, which control mitochondrial fusion, are also involved in the selective removal of mitochondria, playing roles in cellular adaptation during metastasis [435]. The gene BECN1, part of the autophagic core machinery, is implicated in mitophagy and influences metastatic potential by affecting cellular energy homeostasis [436]. Each of these genes contributes to the ability of cancer cells to adapt to new microenvironments during metastasis, enhancing their survival and colonization capabilities [437]. Modulating mitophagy can have profound effects on cancer cell invasion and metastasis [433]. Enhancing mitophagy can prevent the accumulation of damaged mitochondria, reducing oxidative stress and potentially limiting the aggressive behavior of cancer cells [434]. Conversely, inhibiting mitophagy can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and increased intracellular stress, which might suppress cancer progression [393]. For example, the upregulation of PARK2, a gene encoding Parkin, can enhance mitophagy, potentially reducing the invasive capacity of cancer cells [438]. Similarly, regulating ULK1, a key initiator of autophagy, can influence mitophagy levels and thus impact cancer cell behavior [181]. The modulation of LAMP2, involved in lysosomal degradation of mitochondria, can directly affect mitophagy efficiency and subsequent cancer cell invasion [392]. Additionally, ATG5 and ATG7, essential for autophagosome formation, are key players in mitophagy regulation and can influence the metastatic potential of cancer cells [392]. Modulating these genes and factors can provide insights into the therapeutic potential of targeting mitophagy in cancer treatment [393].

Mitophagy promotes carcinogenesis by activating inflammasomes

Mitophagy is a specific form of autophagy that targets damaged or dysfunctional mitochondria for degradation, playing a significant role in carcinogenesis, especially in activating inflammasomes [433]. Inflammasomes are multi-protein complexes crucial for the innate immune system as they activate inflammatory responses [376]. The process of mitophagy during carcinogenesis can inadvertently activate inflammasomes through various mechanisms [440]. One such mechanism involves the degradation of damaged mitochondria, which limits the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and reactive oxygen species (ROS), both potential triggers for inflammasome activation [441]. However, this protective measure can paradoxically lead to the accumulation of oncometabolites, which are known to promote inflammation and cancer progression [442]. Several key genes and factors are involved in this complex interplay [443]. The PINK1/Parkin pathway is one such example [443]. PINK1, a mitochondrial kinase, senses mitochondrial damage and recruits the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin to initiate mitophagy [444]. Parkin then ubiquitinates mitochondrial proteins, marking them for degradation [445]. This pathway's impairment can result in increased mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammasome activation [445]. Another important component is the NLRP3 inflammasome, which can be activated by mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS [442]. Activation of NLRP3 leads to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β, thereby contributing to a tumorigenic environment [446]. ROS, as byproducts of mitochondrial respiration, can damage cellular components, and excessive ROS production due to impaired mitophagy can activate NLRP3 inflammasomes [444]. Additionally, mtDNA, released during mitochondrial stress or damage, can act as a danger signal to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome [441]. Lastly, oncometabolites like 2-hydroxyglutarate, produced under mitochondrial stress conditions, can promote tumorigenesis by inducing genomic instability and inflammation, further illustrating the complex relationship between mitophagy, inflammasome activation, and cancer progression [447]. Clinical trials targeting autophagy in cancer therapy

The data from clinical trials targeting autophagy in cancer therapy presents a wide range of findings across different tumor types, autophagy inhibitors, and treatment combinations. These studies, encompassing phases I to III of clinical trials, offer insights into the potential benefits and side effects associated with autophagy inhibition as a cancer treatment strategy [387]. In brain metastases, a phase II trial with Chloroquine (CQ) combined with radiation therapy showed an overall response rate (ORR) of 54%, nearly matching the control group's 55%. Notably, the progression-free survival (PFS) of brain metastasis at one year was significantly higher at 83.9% compared to 55.1% in controls, albeit with side effects such as headache, dizziness, nausea, vomiting, anorexia, and myelosuppression, and more severe cases of nausea, constipation, headache, and drowsiness [467]. For glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), trials with CQ and Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) in combination with radiation therapy and temozolomide revealed promising outcomes. A phase III trial with CQ extended mean survival to 33 months from the 11 months in controls, though increased seizure frequency was a notable side effect. A phase I/II trial with HCQ reported a median survival of 15.6 months, with neutropenia and thrombocytopenia as significant side effects [468, 469]. In metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), a phase II study using Pantoprazole with Docetaxel showed a median overall survival (OS) of 15.7 months and median PFS of 5.3 months. The treatment was associated with severe fatigue, anaemia, febrile neutropenia, grade 4 neutropenia, and anorexia [470]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) trials with HCQ, in combination with Carboplatin and Paclitaxel or Erlotinib, highlighted modest PFS and ORR rates, accompanied by side effects like neutropenia, neuropathy, anaemia, rash, nausea, diarrhoea, and fatigue [471, 472]. Pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and pancreatic cancer studies involving HCQ with Gemcitabine or nab-paclitaxel illustrated varying degrees of success in terms of ORR, median OS, and histopathologic responses. While some trials reported significant improvements, side effects ranged from myelosuppression to neuropsychiatric symptoms, albeit one study noted no side effects [473]. In renal cell carcinoma (RCC) and refractory myeloma, HCQ and Ricolinostat showed disease control and response rates with manageable side effects such as myelosuppression, fatigue, peripheral neuropathy, and gastrointestinal issues. Additionally, thrombocytopenia, anaemia, fatigue, hypokalaemia, and renal insufficiency were observed in a trial with Ricolinostat [474]. Trials on solid tumors with various autophagy inhibitors like Pantoprazole, HCQ, and combinations with chemotherapy agents such as Doxorubicin, MK-2206, Temsirolimus, Temozolomide, and Vorinostat showed overall response rates ranging from modest to promising, with side effects primarily including fatigue, rash, gastrointestinal symptoms, and myelosuppression [475–481].

Conclusion

In this comprehensive review, we have explored the intricate dynamics of selective autophagy and its dual role in the realm of cancer biology. Selective autophagy, a fundamental process in eukaryotic cells, is instrumental in maintaining cellular homeostasis through the targeted degradation of specific organelles and proteins. Its significance is particularly pronounced in the context of cancer, where it plays a paradoxical role—acting as a tumor suppressor in early stages while potentially aiding cancer progression in advanced stages. The dichotomous nature of selective autophagy in cancer presents a complex landscape. Initially, it acts as a guardian of cellular integrity, eliminating damaged organelles and proteins that could otherwise contribute to oncogenic transformation. This protective role underscores the importance of autophagy in preventing the initial steps of tumorigenesis. However, as cancer progresses, the autophagic machinery can be co-opted by cancer cells. In this altered state, selective autophagy facilitates tumor survival and growth by supporting the metabolic demands of rapidly proliferating cancer cells and by conferring resistance to therapeutic agents. Understanding the molecular mechanisms that govern selective autophagy is pivotal in delineating its role in cancer development and progression. Our review delved into these mechanisms, shedding light on how alterations in autophagic pathways contribute to tumorigenesis and therapy resistance. It is evident that the regulation of selective autophagy is a finely balanced act, influenced by a multitude of factors including genetic, epigenetic, and environmental cues. The complexity of these regulatory networks poses significant challenges in fully comprehending the role of autophagy in cancer. One of the most promising yet challenging aspects of selective autophagy is its potential as a therapeutic target. The ability to modulate autophagic pathways offers a novel approach to cancer treatment, possibly allowing for the circumvention of drug resistance and the targeting of cancer-specific vulnerabilities. However, the dual role of autophagy in cancer necessitates a nuanced approach to such therapies. Strategies that aim to inhibit autophagy may be beneficial in cancers where autophagy promotes survival and proliferation. Conversely, in contexts where autophagy prevents cancer progression, its induction could be therapeutically advantageous. The path to leveraging selective autophagy in cancer therapy is fraught with obstacles. Among these, the most prominent is the need for a deeper understanding of the context-dependent roles of autophagy in different cancer types and stages. This necessitates the development of sophisticated models and analytical tools to dissect the complex interplay between autophagy and cancer. Additionally, identifying reliable biomarkers for autophagy is crucial for the effective implementation of autophagy-targeted therapies. These biomarkers could provide insight into the autophagic status of tumors, aiding in the stratification of patients and the optimization of treatment regimens. Another significant challenge lies in the development of specific and safe autophagy modulators. Current agents that target autophagic pathways often lack specificity and may have off-target effects. The development of more refined agents, capable of selectively modulating autophagic activity, is essential for the safe and effective application of these therapies in clinical application.
