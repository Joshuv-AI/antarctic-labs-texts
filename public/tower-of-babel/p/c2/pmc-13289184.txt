Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO): patient education series – understanding trauma and emergency surgery conditions
Authors: Allan E Stolarski, Dane R Scantling
Published: 2026

Background

The heart and lungs work together in your body to give your organs clean air or “oxygen.” Your organs need oxygen to survive and work properly. When you breathe in, your lungs bring oxygen from the air into your blood. Your heart is the pump that sends blood through little tunnels called blood vessels to the rest of your body.

Once your organs use up oxygen, they make carbon dioxide which needs to leave your body. Too much carbon dioxide in your blood can make you sick. Your blood travels back to the lungs so that you can “clean” your blood by breathing out carbon dioxide.

ECMO stands for “extracorporeal membrane oxygenation,” and is a system that can help patients whose lungs or heart are very sick and no longer working. When a patient is not getting better with other medicine for the heart or lungs, ECMO is a way to “rescue” these patients. ECMO works like a back-up heart and/or lungs for the patient when their own heart or lungs do not work. The goal is to use ECMO for as little time as possible until a patient’s own heart and lungs are working again.

To put a patient “on ECMO,” a surgeon or other doctor will place big tubes into blood vessels in the groin and/or neck. Blood then leaves the body through these tubes and enters the ECMO machine where it is cleaned and filled with oxygen. It is then pumped into the body through the tubes to fill the blood vessels, as if it was being pumped by the heart.

What happens in the hospital/intensive care unit

Patients on ECMO are located in an intensive care unit (ICU) to be checked on often by many kinds of healthcare workers consisting of nurses, doctors, therapists, and people who run ECMO machines. (The people that run ECMO machines are called perfusionists). In the ICU, the care team will draw a patient’s blood to run tests and look at important numbers that show how a patient’s heart, lungs, and blood vessels are doing, called “vital signs”. This information will help the team make sure the ECMO is working, or change it as needed. A patient may be on ECMO anywhere from a few days to several weeks.

Diagnosis/reason for procedure

Patients that need ECMO are often the sickest patients in the hospital. Some problems that may need ECMO support are: severe lung diseases, heart attacks, after heart operation, or large wound to the chest. Patients that need ECMO cannot get enough oxygen for themselves with breathing, release enough carbon dioxide and/or their heart is not strong enough to pump enough blood through the lungs and body. The ECMO machine does the work for the heart and/or lungs to give these body parts time to rest and heal.

Medical options

Patients go on ECMO when no other medicines have worked to help their heart or lungs. Doctors will only use it if they think the problem can get better with rest and more treatment. Patients cannot stay on ECMO forever. If the medical team does not think the patient can get better over time, they will not offer ECMO.

Care after treatment

When the ECMO team decides the patient’s heart/lungs are working again, the ECMO machine is slowly “turned down”, reducing the help given to the lungs and/or heart, before it is turned off. After a patient demonstrates that their heart/lungs are healthy enough without the ECMO machine, the large tubes in the patient’s blood vessels are removed. Sometimes they are taken out in the operating room. Even after a patient is taken off the ECMO machine, they may still need to be watched and supported in the ICU.

Discharge location

After a patient is taken off ECMO support, they will remain in the ICU to keep getting better. Patients on ECMO are quite ill and a long recovery is normal.

Wound care

The tubes that drain blood to and from the ECMO machine usually enter large blood vessels in the groin and/or neck. Sometimes after the tubes are taken out, the blood vessels need to be fixed in the operating room. If this happens, the patient may need a longer cut over the neck or groin. These cuts are closed with stitches and will heal over time. Please ask your doctor for specific wound care instructions.

Diet

If a patient is too sick, they may get food through a tube that goes directly to their stomach through their nose. They may also get “fed” through a small plastic tube in their blood vessel called an IV. The IV will give them a fluid that has all the nutrition found in normal food. Once their heart and lungs are working and are off the ECMO machine, your doctor will let you eat as soon as they think it is safe.

Activity

After a patient is taken off ECMO, they are allowed to move around as much as they can. Patients are often still sick and weak after ECMO and may need physical therapy to get strong enough to move normally again.
