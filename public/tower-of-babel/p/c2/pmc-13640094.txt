Macrophagic myofasciitis associated with transferrin receptor 2 and solute carrier family 40 member 1 variants and iron dysregulation: a case report
Authors: Gwénola Le Dref
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMacrophagic myofasciitis (MMF) is a rare inflammatory muscle disease linked to the long-term persistence of aluminum-based adjuvants in macrophages. Its clinical spectrum includes fatigue, myalgia, and cognitive impairment, and genetic susceptibility has been suggested. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case suggesting an association between MMF, mild yet clinically relevant iron dysregulation, and variants in key iron regulatory genes, including transferrin receptor 2 (TFR2) and solute carrier family 40 member 1 (SLC40A1), also known as ferroportin.Case presentationWe report the case of a 55-year-old white French woman of Breton maternal origin and predominantly Northwestern European/Celtic ancestry, presenting with chronic fatigue, musculoskeletal pain, and cognitive impairment. Muscle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of MMF with aluminum deposition in macrophages. Iron indices had remained within the normal range for many years. After menopause, she gradually developed increasing ferritin levels, intermittent elevations in liver enzymes, and transferrin saturation values occasionally approaching or exceeding the upper normal limit, despite regular voluntary blood donations. These episodes showed a recurrent temporal pattern, with increases in ferritin and transferrin saturation coinciding with elevations in hepatic enzymes and clinical worsening. Both biological parameters and symptoms improved following blood donations and later after dietary iron restriction. Direct-to-consumer ancestry genetic testing with secondary raw data analysis identified homozygous variants in TFR2 and heterozygous variants in SLC40A1. Repeated blood donations led to a marked decrease in ferritin and transferrin saturation, normalization of liver enzymes, and transient improvement in several symptoms, but were poorly tolerated, causing prolonged fatigue and episodic dyspnea. After discontinuation of blood donations, progressive dietary iron restriction reduced transferrin saturation and normalized liver enzymes without further phlebotomy, while ferritin gradually increased toward the upper reference range.ConclusionsThis case highlights a possible contribution of genetic susceptibility to MMF, involving variants affecting iron regulation and macrophage function. It raises the hypothesis of a gene-environment interaction in which altered iron handling could contribute to both an enhanced hepatic response to modest iron loading and prolonged persistence of aluminum adjuvants within macrophages. Further studies are needed to better define the potential role of iron-related genes, particularly TFR2 and SLC40A1, in MMF susceptibility and to explore whether individualized preventive approaches might be relevant in genetically predisposed individuals.Keywords: Case report, Macrophagic myofasciitis, Aluminum adjuvants, Iron metabolism

Introduction

Macrophagic myofasciitis (MMF) is a rare inflammatory myopathy characterized by aluminum-containing macrophages at prior vaccine injection sites and clinically associated with fatigue, myalgia, and cognitive impairment. Genetic susceptibility has been suggested but remains incompletely characterized [1].

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case suggesting an association between MMF, mild yet clinically relevant iron dysregulation, and variants in TFR2 and SLC40A1. The patient exhibited longitudinal fluctuations in iron indices and liver enzymes responsive to iron depletion strategies. Given the central role of ferroportin and TFR2 in macrophage iron handling and emerging data linking altered TFR2 signaling to inflammatory polarization, this case raises the hypothesis of a gene-environment interaction in which variants affecting iron regulation may modulate macrophage behavior and organ-specific responses to aluminum-based adjuvants [2, 3, 16].

Case presentation

Patient information and early history

The patient is a white French woman born in 1970, adopted in early childhood, with a known Breton biological mother and unknown paternal identity. She was conceived while her mother was working on Jersey island, suggesting that her biological father may have been from the Channel Islands or the British Isles. According to a direct-to-consumer ancestry genetic analysis, her genetic background is predominantly Northwestern European/Celtic (98.7%), consistent with Irish and Anglo-Saxon origins.

At the age of 1 year, after a Vaxicoq vaccine injection near the scapula, a hollow at the injection site developed, suggestive of localized tissue necrosis. The sterile wound remained open for several months, and the scar is still visible more than 50 years later.

Throughout her childhood, she experienced recurrent infections (otitis), muscle pain, fatigue, sleep disturbances, emotional dysregulation, learning difficulties, digestive disorders, and restless legs syndrome. Her mother consistently reported poor tolerance to vaccinations, compared with her brother and sister.

Major clinical events

In 1996–1997 (age 26–27), following three doses of the Genhevac B hepatitis B vaccine, the patient developed new symptoms: chronic fatigue, muscle tetany, cognitive impairment, arthralgias, sleep disturbances, and mood instability. This vaccination marked the onset of progressive physical and cognitive decline.

In 2010 (age 40), after a DTPolio (Repevax) vaccine, her health deteriorated further, with the development of a goiter, erythema nodosum, digestive disturbances, and recurrent otitis. Another hollow formed at the injection site in the left deltoid.

Later, a subsequent muscle biopsy performed at the site of deltoid symptoms confirmed the diagnosis of macrophagic myofasciitis, with typical aluminum containing macrophages on histology. The patient’s clinical history, diagnostic findings, and management are summarized in Table 1.

In 2017, the patient underwent neuropsychiatric evaluation and was diagnosed with Asperger's syndrome with mood disorder, revising the previous diagnosis of bipolar disorder obtained in 2005.

Iron parameters and liver enzymes over time

In 2012, iron supplementation with lithothamnion, was briefly attempted to address iron deficiency but was discontinued due to a metallic body odor and concerns regarding aluminum intoxication.

Starting in 2013, the patient began donating blood regularly, while maintaining normal iron levels without supplementation. However, after menopause in 2019, laboratory iron markers began to indicate mild overload despite continued blood donations. Transferrin saturation approached or exceeded the upper limit of normal, and ferritin slowly rose from clearly normal to upper normal or mildly elevated values. Liver enzymes levels were also intermittently elevated, correlating with periods of higher ferritin and transferrin saturation. Both iron indices and liver enzymes rose concomitantly during symptomatic phases and normalized following blood donations. In December 2023, when ferritin and transferrin saturation reached their highest recorded values (up to 298 ng/mL and 59%, respectively), liver enzymes were markedly elevated [alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 126 IU/L, gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) 146 IU/L], and the patient reported increased fatigue, nocturnal polyuria, anxiety, and sleep disruption.

Two blood donations (approximately 500 mL each) performed in January and May 2024 were followed by a substantial decrease in ferritin and transferrin saturation, normalization of liver enzymes, and transient improvement in several symptoms, including nocturia and sleep quality. However, these procedures were poorly tolerated, leading to prolonged post phlebotomy exhaustion and transient dyspnea, which prompted the patient to discontinue blood donation after May 2024.

Following discontinuation of phlebotomy, the patient initially adopted a moderate reduction in dietary iron intake, consisting of reduced consumption of iron-rich foods and avoidance of combined intake with vitamin C. Despite these measures, follow-up testing over the subsequent year showed a gradual upward drift of ferritin and persistently elevated transferrin saturation.

Recent course and response to dietary iron restriction

A comprehensive evaluation in mid-2025 did not identify any metabolic syndrome, notable alcohol consumption, or other common causes of hyperferritinemia or liver enzymes abnormalities.

In June 2025, follow-up tests showed ferritin at 162 ng/mL and transferrin saturation at 44%, indicating persistent elevation despite prior dietary moderation. Given the continued poor tolerance of blood donation, dietary measures were further intensified, and a low-iron diet was implemented, characterized by very low meat intake, limited fish consumption, and avoidance of iron-rich foods combined with vitamin C, including iron-rich plant sources.

Under this regimen, successive blood tests in 2025–2026 showed a slow but steady increase in ferritin (from 162 to 180 ng/mL in June and August 2025 to 225 ng/mL in February 2026), while transferrin saturation decreased from 44 to 39% and then to 23%. Concurrently, liver enzymes returned to normal values (aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 27 U/L, ALT 32 U/L, GGT 36 U/L in February 2026).

Thus, dietary iron restriction alone appeared sufficient to reduce iron availability and hepatic biochemical reactivity in this patient, although ferritin levels continued to gradually increase within the upper reference range.

Genetic testing results

In 2016, at the patient’s own initiative, an ancestry genetic test (non-clinical grade) was performed. Analysis of the raw data identified several variants in genes implicated in iron metabolism. These included heterozygosity for HFE C282Y and multiple homozygous variants in TFR2, as well as heterozygous variants in SLC40A1.

Several studies have investigated the functional impact of genetic variants in HFE, TFR2 and SLC40A1 on iron homeostasis. While some variants (for example HFE C282Y, certain TFR2 missense mutations and clearly pathogenic SLC40A1 mutations) are well established in hereditary iron disorders, the contribution of many other polymorphisms remains under active investigation, and possible synergistic effects of combined variants in multiple iron‑related genes are even less well understood. In this patient, some of the identified TFR2 variants have been associated in literature with subtle alterations in iron parameters, whereas the functional significance of the SLC40A1 variants remains uncertain and may depend on gain or loss-of-function effects [4–10].

Although not clearly pathogenic SLC40A1 mutation was identified, the combined presence of multiple common TFR2 variants, heterozygous HFE C282Y, and SLC40A1 polymorphisms may theoretically influence the hepcidin-ferroportin regulatory axis. Such a polygenic background could contribute to subtle modulation of iron handling and might favor relative macrophage iron retention consistent with a partial loss-of-function ferroportin profile. However, these findings derive from a direct-to-consumer genetic platform and have not been confirmed by clinical-grade sequencing; therefore, the precise functional impact of the identified variants in this individual cannot be determined [11].

TFR2‑related hemochromatosis is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner, whereas ferroportin disease due to pathogenic SLC40A1 mutations follows an autosomal dominant pattern.

Therapeutic interventions and follow up

Repeated blood donations were consistently followed by prolonged post-phlebotomy fatigue, leading to a gradual reduction in their frequency. After the two most recent donations in January and May 2024 were further complicated by transient dyspnea, blood donation was permanently discontinued. No chelation therapy was administered. Current management consists of clinical follow-up, periodic monitoring of iron parameters and liver enzymes, and long-term dietary adaptation aimed at moderating iron intake. The long-term outcome remains uncertain.

Clinically, the patient continues to experience chronic fatigue, musculoskeletal pain and sleep disturbance, with fluctuating intensity. She reports that blood donations and, more recently, dietary iron restriction were associated with some improvement in nocturnal urinary frequency, anxiety and sleep quality, although these benefits have been partial. These observations suggest that while modulation of iron availability may influence some symptoms, other aspects of her condition likely reflect persistent MMF-related and neuropsychiatric factors.

Discussion

This case illustrates a complex interaction between environmental exposure to aluminum-containing vaccine adjuvants, genetic variants affecting iron metabolism, and macrophage-driven chronic inflammation. Early recurrent local reactions and classical histological findings of aluminum-laden macrophages on muscle biopsy support the diagnosis of MMF, and the patient’s long-standing fatigue, musculoskeletal pain, and cognitive disturbances are consistent with its recognized clinical spectrum [1].

The patient did not exhibit overt iron overload in the classical hemochromatosis sense but developed, after menopause, mild yet persistent ferritin elevation, intermittent increases in transferrin saturation, and parallel fluctuations in liver enzymes. In the absence of metabolic syndrome, significant alcohol intake, or other obvious causes of hyperferritinemia, this pattern may reflect a primary disturbance of iron handling rather than secondary hyperferritinemia.

Importantly, transferrin saturation and liver enzymes were highly responsive to interventions reducing iron availability. Whole blood donations led to decreased ferritin and transferrin saturation, normalization of liver enzymes, and transient clinical improvement, but were poorly tolerated. After discontinuation of phlebotomy, dietary iron restriction did not prevent a gradual rise in ferritin toward the upper reference range, yet it markedly reduced transferrin saturation and normalized liver enzymes. These observations suggest that modulation of circulating iron availability, rather than total iron stores, may be a key determinant of hepatic reactivity in this genetically susceptible context [12, 13].

This pattern resembles that observed in certain non-HFE iron disorders, including TFR2-related hemochromatosis and ferroportin disease, where phenotypes are heterogeneous and hepatic or macrophage iron handling may be altered despite only moderate abnormalities in ferritin and transferrin saturation. In these conditions, the threshold for hepatic reactivity may be lower than in classical HFE-related hemochromatosis. Although some variants in HFE, TFR2, and SLC40A1 are clearly pathogenic, the functional significance of many polymorphisms, including those identified in this patient, remains under investigation [7, 14, 15].

Experimental data support a direct role of TFR2 in macrophage biology. In murine models, TFR2 deficiency promotes M1-like polarization, enhances phagocytic activity, and exacerbates inflammatory arthritis independently of massive systemic iron overload, indicating that altered TFR2 signaling can directly modulate inflammatory pathways and particle engulfment. By analogy, subtle TFR2 alterations in humans could influence macrophage phenotype and contribute to chronic pain and persistence of particle-containing inflammatory foci [16].

Ferroportin, encoded by SLC40A1, is the principal iron exporter in macrophages. Pathogenic variants reduce iron export and promote macrophage iron retention, as described in ferroportin disease, where elevated ferritin may coexist with only moderate systemic abnormalities. Together with evidence that macrophage polarization regulates iron storage and inflammatory responses, this supports a plausible macrophage-centered iron dysregulation in this case, although the functional impact of the identified SLC40A1 variants remains unknown [17–19].

In MMF, aluminum-laden macrophages persist at injection sites for years and contribute to local and systemic inflammation. If TFR2 and ferroportin variants alter iron recycling within these cells, they could influence macrophage polarization, oxidative stress responses, and aluminum clearance kinetics, potentially modulating the intensity and duration of inflammation. Although speculative, the convergence of MMF, iron dysregulation, and TFR2/SLC40A1 variants in this patient suggests a biologically plausible, yet unproven, gene-environment interaction [1].

From a practical standpoint, this case raises questions about individualized iron-modulating strategies. Standard 450–500 mL phlebotomies were biochemically effective but poorly tolerated, whereas dietary iron restriction reduced transferrin saturation and normalized liver enzymes despite a gradual rise in ferritin. Low-volume phlebotomy could represent a potential alternative, as described in selected non-HFE conditions, but remains hypothetical pending genetic confirmation and clinical evaluation.

However, several limitations must be emphasized. This is a single case and cannot establish causality. The degree of iron overload is mild, and similar ferritin values are commonly observed in postmenopausal women without MMF. The genetic findings are derived from raw data of a non-clinical direct to consumer test and were not confirmed by clinical grade sequencing; the exact functional impact of the variants in this patient is unknown. No mechanistic in vitro or in vivo studies were performed to demonstrate altered macrophage polarization or aluminum handling. Additionally, MMF itself remains a subject of debate in parts of medical literature, which may influence the interpretation of such complex cases. It also remains possible that the coexistence of these findings in this patient is coincidental.

Within these acknowledged limitations, this case may be viewed as a hypothesis-generating observation. It raises the possibility that, in a subset of patients with MMF, individual variability in genes involved in iron homeostasis could interact with environmental exposures to influence macrophage behavior and symptom persistence. Confirmation in independent cohorts, together with functional studies exploring iron recycling, macrophage polarization, and particle persistence, will be necessary to determine whether this hypothesis has broader relevance.

Conclusion

This case raises the possibility of a synergistic interaction between genetic variants involved in iron metabolism and environmental exposure in the clinical expression of macrophagic myofasciitis. In this patient, mild but reproducible disturbances in iron indices, a reactive hepatic biochemical profile, and differential responses to blood donation and dietary iron restriction coexisted with MMF and TFR2/SLC40A1 variants.

While causality cannot be established, these observations suggest that subtle modulation of iron homeostasis may contribute to disease expression in selected individuals. Confirmation in larger cohorts and functional studies will be necessary before any clinical implications can be drawn. At present, this report should be interpreted as a hypothesis-generating observation highlighting the potential relevance of macrophage-centered iron regulation in complex inflammatory conditions.
