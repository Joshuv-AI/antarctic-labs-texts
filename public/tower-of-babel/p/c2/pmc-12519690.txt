ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGiant cell arteritis (GCA) is a large-vessel vasculitis occurring in people aged over 50 years. Recent studies have shown that tocilizumab (TCZ), an anti-IL-6 receptor monoclonal antibody, is remarkably effective in treating GCA and allows significant dose sparing of glucocorticoids. However, it makes it difficult to monitor disease activity. Furthermore, treatment is often prolonged over 1 year due to the fear of relapse after stopping TCZ and/or the absence of an optimal discontinuation scheme.Methods and analysisThis study aims at comparing two discontinuation regimens in a population of GCA patients who have been treated with TCZ for 12–36 months and have discontinued glucocorticoids for at least 12 weeks. Patients will be randomised with a 1:1 ratio between two arms: immediate discontinuation (cessation) versus gradual discontinuation of TCZ (162 mg subcutaneously every 2 weeks for 12 weeks and then every 4 weeks for 12 additional weeks). Patients will be followed up for 78 weeks. The primary endpoint is relapse-free survival after 26 weeks of follow-up. A total of 120 patients will be randomised (60 in each group) for a period of 3 years.Ethics and disseminationThe trial was approved by an independent ethics committee (CPP Sud Ouest et Outre Mer IV) and the French health authority (French National Agency for Medicines and Health Products Safety—ANSM) through the Clinical Trials Information System (CTIS) provided by the European Medicines Agency (EMA). The informed consent complies with the ICH GCP guideline and regulatory requirements. Eligible patients may only be included in the study after providing informed consent. Findings will be published in peer-reviewed journals and conference presentations.Trial registration numberNCT06037460.Keywords: INTERNAL MEDICINE, Clinical Trial, Randomized Controlled Trial

Introduction

Giant cell arteritis (GCA) refers to large-vessel vasculitis occurring in people older than 50 years.1 2 Its incidence follows a north to south gradient, with the highest rates in Northern Europeans and North Americans. The incidence of GCA in France has been evaluated at 9.4–13.4 cases per year per 100 000 people aged over 55.3 4

GCA is characterised by granulomatous inflammation of the walls of large vessels, especially the aorta and extracranial branches of the external carotid artery, with intimal hyperplasia and luminal obstruction leading to ischaemic manifestations such as temporal headaches, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness and temporal artery involvement. Vision loss, which may occur as a consequence of acute anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy, central retinal artery occlusion or retrobulbar optic neuritis, is the most serious complication of GCA. About 50% of GCA patients have polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR), which is responsible for hip and shoulder tenderness. Most also present with signs of systemic inflammation, including weight loss, fatigue and fever, together with an increased erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) level. The diagnosis of GCA is confirmed by temporal artery biopsy (TAB), showing segmental and focal panarteritis with non-necrotising granulomatous inflammation, or by vascular imaging (18FDG-PET, CT angiography, MR angiography or Doppler ultrasound) of the temporal arteries, other cervical arteries or the aorta and its main branches.1 5

Glucocorticoids (GC) are the cornerstone of GCA treatment. At diagnosis, guidelines recommend prescribing high-dose GC.6,9 French recommendations then advise gradually decreasing the dose to stop GC after 12 months of follow-up.6 7 GC-sparing treatments are used in patients for whom the duration of GC treatment exceeds 12 months due to GCA activity (relapse) or in the event of GC toxicity. When there is a risk of adverse effects, French recommendations suggest combining a GC-sparing drug such as tocilizumab (TCZ) or methotrexate (MTX) with the aim of stopping GC after 6 months.6 7 A meta-analysis of pivotal trials9,14 indicated that TCZ is superior to MTX.15 Therefore, the main recommendations place TCZ as the reference GC-sparing drug in GCA and MTX as an alternative.6,9 ACR guidelines even suggest 12 months of TCZ in combination and 6 months of GC as first-line treatment, as evaluated in the GiACTA trial.9 13

TCZ is a humanised anti-IL-6 receptor antibody which is approved in combination with GC for GCA treatment and is remarkably effective for GC sparing (about 2000 mg of prednisone in cumulative doses).13 16 As a result, more and more GCA patients are being treated with TCZ, with numbers continuing to rise.

While there is no doubt that TCZ treatment is beneficial and fairly well tolerated in the elderly population,13 it is costly and induces unsolved problems. First, the disease is difficult to assess in these patients because the biological markers usually used to monitor GCA activity (CRP, fibrinogen and ESR) can no longer be measured since TCZ blocks their production by hepatocytes. Monitoring disease activity therefore requires a very careful clinical examination and sometimes the use of expensive imaging tests such as17 FDG-PET, which is still difficult to analyse in treated patients.18 Indeed, GCA can be active with normal levels of ESR, CRP and fibrinogen.17 19

Some studies suggest that monitoring serum IL-6 may help identify patients with active disease,20 but this assay is not always readily available and the threshold for suspecting relapse is not easy to define because TCZ, by blocking IL-6 receptors, induces increased serum IL-6 levels, even in patients with inactive disease.21 22 Second, and for the same reasons, the detection of infectious events is challenging in TCZ-treated patients.23

This raises the question of how to stop the treatment, especially since other therapies that do not interfere with the measurement of CRP, ESR or fibrinogen are being evaluated.24 Currently, it is recommended that TCZ be prescribed for at least 1 year with the goal of discontinuing GC. Experience shows that patients are actually treated for much longer with TCZ. In the ARTEMIS study, which described 306 GCA patients managed by 69 French physicians (internists: 85%, rheumatologists: 15%), 47 patients (15.4%) were receiving TCZ treatment, of whom only nine stopped it after a mean duration of 21.9±16.6 months.25

This shows that the treatment tends to be prolonged beyond 1 year when the disease is often in remission without GC, probably due to two factors: (1) the fear of relapse on discontinuation and (2) the absence of a scheme for stopping TCZ.

The risk of relapse after stopping TCZ has been reported in several studies, particularly the long-term follow-up of pivotal phase 2 and 3 trials.13 14 Overall, the risk of relapse is about 40% 6 months after the last TCZ injection and appears to be higher in cases involving the large arteries (aorta and its branches).2126,28

Few data are available on the procedures for stopping TCZ. A Spanish multicentre study described 471 GCA patients treated with TCZ. Of the 231 who achieved prolonged remission, the dose of TCZ was optimised in 125 patients after a median follow-up of 12 months. Intravenous TCZ was optimised from 8 to 4 mg/kg/4 weeks in 44% of patients, while subcutaneous TCZ was optimised from 162 mg/week to 162 mg every other week in 65%. The authors compared patients who had undergone dose optimisation with those who had not. There was no difference between the two groups at the start of TCZ treatment. At the end of follow-up, prolonged remission (78.2% vs 84.2%; p=0.29) and relapse (5.6% vs 10.4%, p=0.177) were similar in both groups (optimised vs unoptimised).29

In another study involving 23 patients with difficult-to-treat GCA (14 relapsers, 5 GC-dependent and 4 adverse reactions to GC), patients were treated with TCZ 162 mg/week for 12 months, then the dose was reduced to 162 mg every other week for the following 12 months, after which TCZ was stopped. By the time TCZ was stopped, all patients had discontinued GC. Two (9%) relapsed during the period when patients received 162 mg every other week and only six (26%) relapsed within 6 months after stopping TCZ.30

More recently, a Danish team reported on their experience with 155 GCA patients treated with TCZ, in whom the dose was reduced or treatment stopped in 104. In patients treated with TCZ 162 mg/week, the risk of relapse appeared to be lower when the dose was reduced to 162 mg every other week rather than stopped immediately.31

As well as prolonging the duration of exposure to TCZ, which in itself may reduce the risk of relapse, tapering TCZ makes sense from an immunological point of view. In fact, TCZ blocks the IL-6 receptor and therefore induces increased serum IL-6 and soluble IL-6 receptor levels, which, when TCZ is discontinued, allows IL-6 to exert a proinflammatory effect via the trans-signalling pathway, which could be implicated in the occurrence of relapse.16

Therefore, although available data are limited, it seems that gradual rather than immediate discontinuation of TCZ could decrease the risk of relapse after treatment withdrawal. The aim of this study is to compare two TCZ discontinuation regimens (gradual vs immediate) in a population of GCA patients who have been treated with TCZ for at least 1 year and have discontinued GCs for at least 12 weeks.

Methods/design

Study design

MAGICA (MAGICA, tocilizuMab discontinuAtion in GIant Cell Arteritis) is a multicentre, open-label, randomised and controlled superiority trial designed to compare two strategies for stopping TCZ in GCA patients in GC-free remission: immediate versus gradual discontinuation over 24 weeks. Objectives and endpoints

The primary objective is to compare, after 26 weeks of follow-up, the efficacy on relapse-free survival of immediate versus gradual TCZ discontinuation over 24 weeks. The primary endpoint is relapse-free survival after 26 weeks of follow-up, defined as the time from W0 to relapse or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Due to the open-label nature of the trial, relapse will be confirmed by an adjudication committee.

This committee, composed of three GCA experts who do not participate directly in the study, will review all patients’ files to identify and confirm relapse. They will be blinded to both the assigned study arm and the CRP, ESR, haptoglobin, fibrinogen, neutrophil, platelet, serum glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase and serum glutamate pyruvate transaminase levels that could reveal the randomisation group. The information reviewed will include laboratory data (except where blinded), clinical data and all other relevant data.

It is possible that a treatment (TCZ, GC or other) will be reintroduced by the clinician during follow-up for a potential relapse that is not subsequently confirmed by the adjudication committee, preventing the occurrence of a true relapse thereafter. In this particular case where the committee does not confirm the suspected relapse and the clinician prescribes a different treatment than the one intended in the study, the patient would be considered in remission and censored at the time the clinician wrongly concluded that a relapse had occurred.

Sample size

Given an expected relapse-free survival rate at 26 weeks of 60% if TCZ is stopped immediately,26 27 we assume a relapse-free survival rate increased to 85% with gradual discontinuation (corresponding to a HR of 3.14). Given a lost to follow-up rate of 5% and a two-sided log-rank test, an overall sample size of 114 analyzable patients (57 per group) will achieve 81% power at a 5% significance level. A total of 120 patients will be randomised to account for possible non-analysable patients.

Study proceedings

Screening and inclusion

Eligible participants will be identified by a clinician during a follow-up visit scheduled as part of the routine care of GCA patients. If the eligibility criteria are met, the investigator will present the study to the patient, who will be given time to consider and informed of their right to withdraw consent at any time without prejudice and without having to justify their decision. When written informed consent (online supplemental material) has been obtained and the inclusion criteria verified, the patient is enrolled and randomised (W0).

Randomisation will be performed online by the investigator using the secure CleanWeb platform after identification through a personal password and a final check of the eligibility criteria. The method of randomisation will be minimisation on the history of relapse at inclusion (no relapse, one relapse, two or more relapses) and the TCZ treatment duration (12–18, 18–24, 24–30 and 30–36 months). According to the randomisation arm, patients will either stop TCZ immediately (immediate discontinuation) or receive a subcutaneous injection of TCZ (gradual discontinuation). Allowed and forbidden treatments are detailed in online supplemental table S1.

Patients will be asked to complete self-administered questionnaires (Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ), 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36, Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy (FACIT)-Fatigue).

Patients will be given a diary to record any adverse events and treatments.

Bone densitometry (W6–W8) will be measured according to each centre’s usual practice. Blood samples (routine laboratory tests) will be taken.

18FDG-PET/CT scans will be performed between W0 and W8 in participating centres and anonymised data will be sent for centralised reading. FDG-PET scans will be performed when patients’ blood glucose levels are below 11 mmol/L. Patients will fast for at least 4 hours before receiving an intravenous injection of FDG at a dose of 3 MBq/kg (0.08 mCi/kg). Sixty minutes later, scans will be taken from the top of the head (vertex) to the knees. The head will be supported on a headrest, and the arms will be positioned alongside the body. Following a low-dose, non-contrast-enhanced CT scan, PET data will be acquired in a craniocaudal direction using one of two protocols: 1/step-by-step acquisition (5 min per bed position for the head and neck, followed by 2 min per bed for the rest of the body); and 2/FlowMotion acquisition (the acquisition time in the head and neck region will be three times longer than in the rest of the body).

Follow-up

Follow-up visits are scheduled at W2, W4, W8, W12, W16, W20, W26, W36, W52 and W78 (end of the study), and in cases of suspected relapse (supplementary visit). Patients and nurses will complete the patient diary to report TCZ subcutaneous injections. Data from the diary will be collected at each visit to ensure compliance and traceability. W2, W8, W16 and W20 follow-up visits may be performed as consultations or teleconsultations with a biological sample the day of the visit ±72 hours. Self-administered questionnaires (HAQ, SF-36, FACIT-Fatigue) will be completed at W12, W26, W52 and W78. Online supplemental table S2 summarises the data collected during each visit. GCA status (active, inactive and occurrence of relapse) will be recorded at each one. Adverse events will be recorded from the time of inclusion until the time of discharge from the study. These events will be identified during clinical, biological or other examinations as set out in the protocol. They will also be identified through interviews with investigators and through spontaneous reporting by research subjects, who will be informed of their responsibility to contact the investigator in the event of an adverse event. Vital status will be recorded at the end of the study. Routine laboratory tests will be conducted at least every month. Bone densitometry will be measured at W52. Research samples (serum, DNA and peripheral blood mononuclear cell) will be collected at W12, W26 and W52 and in the event of relapse to create a collection for further analyses.

In the event of relapse, the investigator will freely determine the best treatment following current recommendations.7,9 In the event of temporary or permanent discontinuation of the TCZ discontinuation strategy, for whatever reason, safety and efficacy data will be collected until the end of the planned study follow-up.

Data management

The data will be entered into an e-CRF (electronic Case Report Form) specifically developed for this study using a Clinical Data Management System (CDMS—CleanWeb). All data will be captured anonymously in the CleanWeb secured and dedicated database, consistent with the recommendations of the MR01 reference method of the CNIL. The names of participants and their addresses will never be recorded in an unencrypted form. Only the first letter of the surname and the first letter of the given name will be recorded, along with the centre number followed by a serial inclusion number. All information required for the study will be entered as and when it is obtained (at screening, inclusion, each follow-up visit and W78). Thanks to automatic checks, in cases with missing or inconsistent data, requests for correction will be sent to participating centres via the CDMS. If corrections are necessary, they will be made directly using the CDMS. At the end of the study, a blind review of the data will be performed and may result in additional queries. All queries should be resolved before the final database lock.

Quality control and monitoring

A clinical research assistant appointed by the sponsor will regularly visit each study centre (at the beginning of the study and then at least once a year) to review informed consent, compliance with the study protocol and the procedures defined within it, and the safety and quality of the data collected in the e-CRF. An audit may be performed at any time by individuals appointed by the sponsor, independently of those responsible for the study.

Statistical analyses

Population definition

The intention-to-treat (ITT) population is defined as all randomised patients who have not withdrawn consent. This analysis will be performed according to the randomisation arm, regardless of the treatment received. The per-protocol population will exclude all subjects in the ITT population with a major protocol deviation. Safety analyses will be conducted according to the randomisation arm in all randomised patients who consented to participate in the study.

Statistical analyses

Patients’ characteristics will be described as frequencies for categorical variables, and as means (±SD) or medians (IQR) for continuous variables depending on their distribution.

The main analysis will be conducted on the ITT population. Survival curves according to the randomisation arm will be plotted with the Kaplan–Meier method. A summary of the number of relapses, deaths, lost to follow-up or censored patients will be presented. Relapse-free survival curves until W26 will be compared between arms with log-rank tests.

Additionally, a Cox proportional hazards regression model, stratified on the centre and adjusted on the minimisation factors, will be used to provide the HR and its two-sided 95% CI. The proportional hazards assumption will be evaluated using a Kolmogorov-type test of supremacy, and alternative analyses, including time-dependent HR models (a piecewise Cox model or flexible spline modelling), will be performed if the assumption is strongly violated. The same strategy will be applied for the comparison of relapse-free survival until W52 and W78.

For quantitative outcomes (quality of life, FACIT-Fatigue, GTI score, ….), a multivariate analysis will be conducted using linear regression for one-measure outcomes or mixed models for repeated-measure outcomes.

For binary or qualitative outcomes, such as remission without prednisone at W26, W52 and W78, proportions will be computed with their 95% CI and a multivariable logistic regression will be performed.

The effect of some predefined covariates (previous duration of treatment, previous duration of corticosteroid therapy, gender, number of previous relapses, etc) on treatment efficacy will be studied using stratified analysis and the interaction tested between the variable of interest and the treatment effect.

Factors predictive of relapse will be assessed using a Cox proportional hazards regression model. Candidate variables will include the main demographic, clinical, phenotypic and biological characteristics of GCA, as well as the discontinuation strategy arm. Variables associated with relapse-free survival with a p<0.20 value in the univariate analysis will be included as covariates in the multivariable Cox model.

The performance of PETVAS and TVS scores18 to predict the risk of relapse after 78 weeks of follow-up will be assessed using area under the receiver operating characteristic curves.

The significance level is set at 5% for all analyses.

Discussion

GCA is the most common form of vasculitis in adults. For decades, its treatment has been based on the use of high doses of corticosteroids. They are still effective but difficult to stop because of the occurrence of relapses in about 50% of patients when the doses are decreased.32 These relapses are not severe but contribute to prolonging the duration of treatment with GC and to causing the onset of adverse effects, which are very frequent and responsible for significant morbidity and mortality.32,35

Progress in understanding the pathophysiology of GCA has led to the identification of new therapeutic targets, including IL-6, which has emerged as a prime target because its serum level is closely correlated with disease activity.1636,39 Therefore, TCZ, whose effectiveness was demonstrated in the GiACTA study,13 has revolutionised the management of GCA, changing the recommendations of learnt societies by making GC withdrawal accessible to almost all patients.7,9

TCZ is naturally being prescribed more and more for these patients. This is a considerable step forward, but the treatment makes monitoring the disease activity very difficult because blocking the IL-6 receptor suppresses production of the usual markers of inflammatory syndrome by hepatocytes (CRP, fibrinogen and ESR).16 Furthermore, treatment is often prolonged well beyond 1 year for fear of relapse after stopping TCZ. Indeed, previous work has put the risk of relapse when stopping TCZ at around 40% 6 months after its last administration.21 26 27 The relapses occurring relatively early after stopping TCZ can be explained in various ways. Either the disease was in fact active, but the absence of biological inflammatory syndrome prevented it from being known, and this is unmasked when treatment is stopped, or there is a rebound phenomenon when TCZ is discontinued, which seems more likely given the frequency of relapses. This rebound phenomenon could be explained by the fact that TCZ causes increased serum levels of IL-6 and its soluble receptor (sIL-6R), meaning that when it is stopped, the trans-signalling pathway can suddenly activate and lead to a relapse of vasculitis.16 21 Along this line, a study has shown that patients whose serum IL-6 concentration decreases despite TCZ treatment have a lower risk of relapse after stopping TCZ.20

Various strategies can be considered to reduce the rebound phenomenon when TCZ is discontinued. First, introduce MTX at TCZ discontinuation. Quick et al suggest in a recent study that this did not lower the risk of relapse after 1 year of TCZ treatment in a UK cohort of 336 GCA patients.28 However, the decision to prescribe MTX when stopping TCZ was not randomised, suggesting selection bias. A prospective randomised trial entitled Meteoritics is underway to explore that strategy’s value.40 Another strategy being explored by the MAGICA study is gradually spacing out TCZ injections, based on the hypothesis that this could cut the risk of inflammatory rebound via the trans-signalling pathway. Indeed, several cohort studies have reported that gradually spacing out TCZ injections and then stopping them could lower the risk of relapse.29,31 In addition, strategies for reducing the dose of biologics have been explored in rheumatoid arthritis and shown to keep patients in remission.41

To conclude, MAGICA is a prospective, randomised and controlled trial that will provide a clearer picture of how to discontinue TCZ in GCA in remission without GC. We also hope to identify factors that predict relapse to help clinicians better manage these patients.
