Study on the repair effect of photothermal hydrogel with controllable adhesiveness regulated by melanin and magnesium ions on diabetic wounds
Authors: Lan Guo, Keying Wu, Ruxin Wang, Yadong Xue, Yanru Diao, Junfeng Ai, Qingqing Bi, Baoqin Han, Xubo Wang, Jing Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic non-healing wounds, characterized by prolonged disease duration and low compromised reparative potential, not only cause patients to suffer from persistent physical pain and impaired daily living ability, but also are prone to inducing severe complications such as infection and amputation, posing a serious threat to patients' health and quality of life. In this study, a melanin derivative (M-HPCS) with excellent photothermal properties was successfully prepared through the condensation reaction between natural squid melanin and a chitosan derivative (HPCS). Subsequently, by virtue of charge interaction, M-HPCS was assembled with a hyaluronic acid derivative (HA-PEG) to construct a photothermal hydrogel (M-HPCS/HA-PEG) with controllable adhesion performance. This hydrogel could further enhance its own adhesion capacity by chelating magnesium ions, making it suitable for the repair and treatment of non-healing wounds. The results demonstrated that, relying on the dual regulatory effect of sepia melanin and Mg2+, the hydrogel could achieve controllable adhesion to the wound and non-invasive detachment after use. The combination therapy of hydrogel and photothermal treatment exhibited the optimal repair efficacy by effectively regulating the inflammatory level in the microenvironment of chronic non-healing wounds, promoting angiogenesis, accelerating the epithelialization process, and thereby significantly facilitating wound healing. The photothermal hydrogel possessed ideal adhesion adaptability to different types of wounds and could effectively promote wound repair through hyperthermia. This study provides novel insights and technical support for the clinical treatment of chronic non-healing wounds.Keywords: Chronic non-healing wounds, Squid melanin, Magnesium ion complexation, Controllable adhesion, Photothermal hydrogel

Introduction

Chronic refractory wounds (CRWs) represent a class of highly intractable pathological conditions, categorized by their etiologies into pressure-induced, metabolic, infectious, traumatic, and neoplastic subtypes, with further stratification by anatomical locations (e.g., cutaneous, oral, and orthopedic trauma-related), underscoring their substantial clinical burden and unmet therapeutic needs [[1], [2], [3]]. Repairing CRWs remains a daunting challenge rooted in multifactorial dysregulation of the wound microenvironment, including persistent inflammatory cascades, recalcitrant colonization by drug-resistant bacterial biofilms, tissue regeneration arrest due to ischemia-hypoxia, and oxidative stress-induced cellular dysfunction [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. These interconnected factors form a deleterious feedback loop, rendering conventional interventions ineffective in over 60% of cases [8]. Beyond inflicting severe physical distress, CRWs exacerbate socioeconomic healthcare burdens, with global incidence escalating annually and necessitating urgent therapeutic innovations [9].

Photothermal therapy (PTT) has garnered significant attention owing to its non-invasiveness, spatiotemporal precision, and broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, where the development of high-performance photothermal agents remains pivotal [10,11]. Cuttlefish melanin, a natural pigment abundant in cephalopods, exhibits unparalleled advantages. Its unique indole ring-conjugated structure confers a photothermal conversion efficiency exceeding 45% within the 900–1064 nm near-infrared window, surpassing synthetic counterparts such as gold nanoparticles while obviating risks of heavy metal accumulation [12,13]. Upon NIR irradiation, cuttlefish melanin converts light energy to localized heat, directly eradicating pathogens and disrupting biofilms via thermal ablation when tissue temperatures reach 42–50°C [[14], [15], [16]]. Furthermore, melanin-induced hyperthermia activates vascular endothelial growth factor signaling, fostering angiogenesis [17], while its molecular architecture, rich in phenolic hydroxyl and quinone moieties, endows robust antioxidant and free radical scavenging capacities, directly ameliorating wound oxidative stress [18].

Hydrogels, as three-dimensional network scaffolds mimicking the extracellular matrix niche, maintain moist wound microenvironments via high water contents, mitigating desiccation-induced scab formation and accelerating wound healing by 30–50% [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. In tissue regeneration, their 3D networks recapitulate ECM-like scaffolds, guiding migration of fibroblasts and vascular endothelial cells toward the wound bed while offering mechanical support for proliferation. Integrating melanin into hydrogels to construct photothermal hydrogel systems synergizes PTT-mediated precise antibiosis with ECM-mimetic scaffolding, and photothermal stimulation further potentiates angiogenesis via pathway activation. This “photothermal therapy-microenvironment modulation-tissue regeneration” synergy circumvents the limitations of conventional single-functional materials, marking a paradigm shift in wound care [[25], [26], [27], [28]].

Magnesium ions represent a pivotal class of metal ions intricately involved in wound repair, exerting multi-dimensional regulatory effects across hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling phases [[29], [30], [31], [32]]. CRWs are frequently associated with perturbed magnesium homeostasis: the pro-inflammatory microenvironment accelerates magnesium consumption, while ischemia-hypoxia impairs transmembrane transport, culminating in localized depletion that perpetuates cellular dysfunction [33,34]. Magnesium deficiency precipitates aberrant immune hyperactivation, excessive pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, and rampant ROS generation [[35], [36], [37], [38]]. Localized magnesium delivery attenuates calcium influx, stabilizes cellular membranes, inhibits NADPH oxidase activity to reduce ROS production, and promotes angiogenesis and proliferation [[39], [40], [41]]. Consistent with these findings, magnesium supplementation enhances total antioxidant capacity by boosting glutathione peroxidase activity, accelerating wound healing through synergistic anti-inflammatory, ROS-scavenging, and pro-angiogenic effects [42].

Effective integration of wound dressings with tissue is pivotal for healing, wherein adhesiveness emerges as a critical physicochemical attribute. Divergent adhesiveness requirements exist across CRW subtypes and locations [43,44]. For instance, oral ulcers in moist niches and high-mobility anatomical sites (e.g., joints, plantar surfaces) demand superior adhesiveness [[45], [46], [47], [48]]. Intimate material-tissue interactions orchestrate pro-regenerative microenvironments by activating intracellular signaling, driving cytoskeletal reorganization, and guiding directed migration of epithelial cells and fibroblasts from wound margins to the center [[49], [50], [51], [52]]. Endowing hydrogels with tunable adhesiveness enables conformal contact with irregular wound topographies, mitigating mechanical abrasion-induced damage to neo-tissues. Importantly, post-healing removal must be non-invasive to avoid secondary trauma, necessitating adhesiveness that is both effective and reversible [[53], [54], [55], [56]]. Deciphering adhesiveness-mediated mechanisms in CRW repair is thus paramount for advancing next-generation regenerative materials.

Herein, we report the synthesis of a nanostructured cuttlefish melanin derivative (M-HPCS) via amidation-driven conjugation with a chitosan derivative (HPCS), and its subsequent hybridization with a hyaluronic acid derivative (HA-PEG) through a second-phase microgel assembly strategy to fabricate M-HPCS/HA-PEG, an adhesiveness-tunable photothermal hydrogel tailored for diabetic CRW repair. Cuttlefish melanin serves an irreplaceable dual function: as a photothermal transducer enabling near-infrared-triggered PTT, and as a structural motif that stabilizes gelation and dictates baseline adhesiveness through non-covalent interactions with HA-PEG amine groups; concurrently, Mg2+ chelation fine-tunes adhesiveness and provides sustained NF-κB suppression in the early inflammatory phase. While both melanin and Mg2+ possess ancillary ROS-scavenging and pro-angiogenic properties, these are beneficial synergistic side effects rather than primary design drivers, the core innovation lies in the mechanical coordination (melanin- and Mg2+-dual-regulated adhesion switching) and temporal sequencing (Mg2+-mediated early anti-inflammation followed by photothermal-accelerated late-stage tissue regeneration) that together enable on-demand adhesiveness and non-invasive post-healing removal. In vivo evaluations demonstrate that this hydrogel, in combination with Mg2+ and PTT, effectively modulates wound microenvironment inflammation, accelerates angiogenesis and re-epithelialization, and enhances overall healing efficacy, thus providing a translational framework for diabetic CRW management that bridges materials innovation and clinical need (Scheme 1).

Extraction and synthesis of melanin, HPCS, M-HPCS, and HA-PEG

Fresh Sepiella maindroni were rinsed, and their ink sacs were dissected to collect ink. The ink was washed with absolute ethanol and centrifuged three times to obtain crude melanin. The crude melanin was soaked in deionized water for 24 h, and the precipitate was collected by centrifugation. The precipitate was dispersed in 50-fold its mass of deionized water; the pH was adjusted to 10.5 with NaOH, followed by adding 1.5 wt% alkaline protease. Enzymatic hydrolysis was performed at 50°C for 4 h, and the protease was inactivated. The resulting precipitate was washed to neutrality with deionized water, lyophilized, and stored at 4°C in the dark.

3 g of chitosan was dissolved in 20 mL of NaOH solution, frozen at −40°C overnight, thawed, and suction-filtered to remove excess alkali. The chitosan was transferred to a three-necked flask; 40 mL of isopropyl alcohol was added and stirred at room temperature for 30 min. Propylene oxide was added dropwise, and the mixture was stirred at 50°C for 24 h. The product was suction-filtered, washed with absolute ethanol, dissolved in deionized water, and neutralized with 1 mol/L HCl. After dialysis and lyophilization, Hydroxypropyl Chitosan (HPCS) was obtained.

HPCS was dissolved in Tris buffer (0.01 g/mL) at room temperature. 0.66 g of melanin was dispersed in 33 mL of Tris buffer (0.02 g/mL) by sonication for 20 min, then transferred to a three-necked flask. 0.31 g EDC and 0.23 g NHS were added and stirred at room temperature for 2 h. The HPCS solution was added dropwise, and stirring continued for 12 h. The mixture was dialyzed against deionized water for 48 h (water changed every 3 h) to remove EDC/NHS and lyophilized. Melanin-Hydroxypropyl Chitosan (M-HPCS) was obtained.

HA was dissolved in ultrapure water (0.01 g/mL) at room temperature. 0.31 g EDC and 0.23 g NHS were added to activate carboxyl groups (stirred 1 h at room temperature). NH2-PEG-NH2 was added, and the mixture was stirred for 12 h. The product was dialyzed against deionized water for 48 h (water changed every 3 h) and lyophilized. HA-PEG was obtained.

Hydrogel preparation

M-HPCS/HA-PEG Hydrogels: M-HPCS and HA-PEG were dissolved in deionized water to 7% and 4% (w/v), respectively. The solutions were mixed at volume ratios of 1:9, 3:7, 1:1, 7:3, 9:1 (total volume 2 mL) and incubated in water baths at different temperatures. Gelation time was determined by vial inversion (absence of flow for at least 1 min).

M-HPCS/HA-PEG + Mg2+ Hydrogels: A 4% (w/v) MgCl2 solution was prepared (0.4066 g MgCl2 in 10 mL deionized water). 0.1 mL of MgCl2 solution was added to 2 mL of M-HPCS/HA-PEG mixture (ratios 3:7, 1:1, 7:3) to reach 10 mM Mg2+ (non-toxic and pro-angiogenic). Gelation time was measured as above.

Infected burn wound model

Building upon our original non-infected deep partial-thickness burn model in STZ-induced diabetic rat, we established an infected variant by inoculating S. aureus (1 × 107 CFU/mL, 100 μL) onto the burn wounds immediately after injury. Rats were divided into 6 groups (n = 5/group): blank, positive control (hemostatic gauze), M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3, M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 + NIR, M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+, M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ + NIR. Hydrogels were applied and changed every 2 days. 0.2 mL hydrogel was applied to burns, irradiated with 808 nm laser (1 W/cm2, 10 min).

Wound bacterial load

Male SD rats were euthanized at the experimental endpoint. Dorsal skin was excised, weighed, and homogenized in sterile normal saline (1 mL/100 mg tissue) at 4°C. The homogenate was centrifuged at 200×g for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was serially diluted (10−1 to 10−4), and 100 μL of each dilution was spread onto tryptic soy agar (TSA) plates in triplicate. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 48 h. Colonies were counted on plates with 30-300 colonies.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed via GSEA software, to better understand the gene function of the inhibition of tumor.

The characteristic peaks of chitosan in the FTIR spectrum at 1024 cm−1, 1080 cm−1, 1155 cm−1, and 891 cm−1 confirmed its polysaccharide structure. The peak at 1155 cm−1 arises from the C-O stretching vibration at the C6 position of chitosan. In the FTIR spectrum of hydroxypropyl chitosan, the disappearance of the characteristic peak at 1155 cm−1 (present in chitosan) suggested that the -OH group at the C6 position had reacted with propylene oxide. New peaks at 2973 cm−1 (CH3 stretching vibration) and 1463 cm−1 (CH3 bending vibration) indicated the introduction of hydroxypropyl groups, which was further supported by the peak at 1068 cm−1. Additionally, the disappearance of the absorption peak at 1595 cm−1 implied minor hydroxypropyl modification of -NH2 groups, confirming the successful synthesis of hydroxypropyl chitosan.

In the FTIR spectrum of melanin-hydroxypropyl chitosan, the absorption peaks at 3417 cm−1, 1523 cm−1, and 1646 cm−1 were considered representative of melanin. The peak at 1378 cm−1 was attributed to O-H deformation and C-O stretching of phenolic hydroxyl groups; the peak at 1523 cm−1 arose from C=C stretching vibration in the aromatic ring; the peak at 1646 cm−1 was due to C=O stretching vibration in amide bonds; and the strong, broad band at 3417 cm−1 resulted from the stretching vibrations of OH and NH. These results confirmed the successful grafting of melanin onto hydroxypropyl chitosan.

In the FTIR spectrum of hyaluronic acid, the characteristic peak at 1323 cm−1 was caused by -OH bending vibration; peaks at 1375 cm−1 and 1406 cm−1 were due to C-H bending vibrations; the peak at 1563 cm−1 arose from N-H stretching vibration; the peak at 1618 cm−1 was attributed to C=O stretching vibration; and the peak at 2923 cm−1 was assigned to -CH2 and -CH3 stretching vibrations. In the FTIR spectrum of HA-PEG, the peak at 1738 cm−1 corresponded to C=O stretching vibration in amide bonds, and the peak at 1480 cm−1 resulted from -C-O-C- vibrations in NH2-PEG-NH2, confirming the successful synthesis of HA-PEG.

As shown in Fig. 1E, the 1H NMR spectrum of hydroxypropyl chitosan exhibited signals at 0.97 ppm and 1.08 ppm, corresponding to C9 protons, confirming the hydroxypropylation of chitosan. Signals between 3.20 ppm and 3.84 ppm were attributed to C3-C8 protons, the signal at 3.01 ppm corresponded to C2 protons, and the signal at 4.76 ppm reflected C1 protons. Compared to the 1H NMR spectrum of hydroxypropyl chitosan, new chemical shifts in the spectrum of melanin-hydroxypropyl chitosan at δH 3.49 ppm and δH 2.48-2.69 ppm further confirmed the successful synthesis of M-HPCS.

In the 1H NMR spectra of HA and HA-PEG (Fig. 1F), the signal at approximately 8 ppm corresponded to protons on the amide N-H; the proton peak b at ∼1.6 ppm were attributed to NH2 protons of PEG; the e peak at ∼3.5 ppm corresponded to protons of methylene groups adjacent to oxygen in the PEG structure; and proton peaks c and d between 2.5 and 3.0 ppm belong to methylene protons linked to amino groups in PEG. These results confirmed the successful grafting of PEG onto HA.

The absorbance in the visible region decreased with increasing wavelength, characteristic of the conjugated structure of melanin. Fig. 1I presented the SEM and TEM images of melanin. These findings indicated that melanin grafted onto hydroxypropyl chitosan via amidation could self-assemble into nanospheres. TEM image clearly showed a core-shell structure in M-HPCS, confirming the successful grafting of melanin onto hydroxypropyl chitosan.

Gelation of M-HPCS/HA-PEG and M-HPCS/HA-PEG + Mg2+ hydrogels

Melanin contained abundant phenolic hydroxyl groups, which were oxidized to quinones by oxygen in air under alkaline conditions, leading to melanin aggregation. After reacting with hydroxypropyl chitosan, melanin formed a core-shell structure, which protected most phenolic hydroxyl groups from oxidation and reduced aggregation. Additionally, the numerous -OH groups in melanin interacted with positively charged functional groups (e.g., protonated -NH3+), facilitating temperature-responsive gelation of the hydrogel. Thus, when M-HPCS and HA-PEG solutions were mixed, non-covalent interactions occur between phenolic hydroxyl groups in melanin and amino groups in HA-PEG. At a certain temperature, chain entanglement and hydrogen bonding between natural polymers (hyaluronic acid and chitosan) form a 3D network hydrogel. Upon adding Mg2+, chelation occurred with phenolic groups and hydroxyl groups in melanin, and combined with polymer chain entanglement and hydrogen bonding at a specific temperature, the M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ solution formed a 3D network hydrogel.

At a certain temperature, the hydrophilic-hydrophobic balance shifted, altering the solubility of the polymer network and inducing sol-gel transition. As shown in Fig. 2A, all M-HPCS/HA-PEG mixtures with different concentrations exhibited fluidity (solution state) before water bath heating. After heating at 60°C for 30 min, all mixtures except the 1:9 vol ratio formed hydrogels, while the 1:9 ratio remained fluid. These results indicated that M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7, M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1, M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3, and M-HPCS9/HA-PEG1 solutions could form hydrogels under heating, which was attributed to interactions between -OH groups in melanin and protonated -NH3+ groups in HA-PEG, enabling temperature-responsive gelation.

At room temperature, all M-HPCS/HA-PEG + Mg2+ mixtures remained in solution, but formed hydrogels after heating. Table S2 shows the gelation temperatures of M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+, M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 + Mg2+, and M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 + Mg2+ hydrogels as 45°C, 60°C, and 60°C, respectively. The significantly lower gelation temperature of M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ was due to Mg2+ interacting with hydroxyl groups of melanin, stabilizing the network at lower temperatures. For M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 and M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3, without Mg2+, phenolic hydroxyl groups strongly interacted with amino groups; with Mg2+, some phenolic hydroxyl groups chelated with Mg2+, disrupting the balance. However, M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ provided a higher proportion of phenolic hydroxyl groups, reducing its gelation temperature, while Mg2+ had little effect on the gelation temperature of M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 and M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3.

Rheological properties of M-HPCS/HA-PEG hydrogels

Rheological measurements of M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7, M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1, and M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 hydrogels (Fig. 2B) showed that storage modulus (G′) was higher than loss modulus (G″) from 25°C, with similar trends between 25°C and 60°C, due to the viscous nature of the M-HPCS/HA-PEG mixture, approximating a hydrogel. The gap between G′ and G″ widened at 62°C (M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7), 61.91°C (M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1), and 60°C (M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3), indicating this as were the gelation temperatures.

For the M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+, M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 + Mg2+, and M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 + Mg2+ hydrogels (Fig. 2D), the storage modulus (G′) was greater than the loss modulus (G″) from 25°C to 60°C with similar trends, indicating their elastic, hydrogel-like nature. The G′-G″ gap widened at 46.55°C (M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+), 59.85°C (M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 + Mg2+), and 61.9°C (M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 + Mg2+), confirming these as gelation temperatures. Consistent with the vial inversion method, Mg2+ reduced the gelation temperature of M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+.

Frequency-dependent modulus measurements (Fig. 2C) evaluated network stability. Within a certain frequency range, G′ was greater than G″ for all M-HPCS/HA-PEG hydrogels, indicating good gel status. M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 and M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 showed a larger G′-G″ gap than M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7, suggesting lower crosslinking and greater vulnerability to disruption in M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7, while the former two had higher crosslinking and stable structures. G′ values were 339.48 Pa (M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7), 1120.6 Pa (M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1), and 7492.75 Pa (M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3). The significantly higher G′ of M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 indicated increased crosslinking density and viscoelasticity with melanin content, enhancing network compactness and making it suitable for wound dressings.

Strain-dependent modulus measurements further assessed network stability. The linear viscoelastic region (LVR) reflected structural stability: within the LVR, hydrogels underwent reversible deformation without structural damage; beyond it, internal disruption or collapse occurred. For 1%-100% strain, G′ was greater than G″ for all hydrogels, with M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 showing the widest LVR, indicating higher crosslinking, better stability, and sustained gel integrity.

Stress and frequency dependence tests (Fig. 2E) evaluated viscoelasticity, determined by G′ and G'': higher G′ indicated better viscoelasticity when G′ was greater than G''. M-HPCS/HA-PEG + Mg2+ hydrogels showed G′ was greater than G″ within the tested range, confirming gel status and viscoelasticity. G′ values at 1-100 Hz were 938.7 Pa (M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+), 933.23 Pa (M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 + Mg2+), and 297.92 Pa (M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 + Mg2+), with M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ exhibiting the highest G′, indicating superior mechanical properties.

Dynamic strain scanning showed that G′ and G″ crossed when strain exceeded 761% (M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+), 429% (M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 + Mg2+), and 180% (M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 + Mg2+), indicating structural disruption. M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ resisted higher strain before failure, demonstrating stronger network crosslinking, better toughness, and greater deformation tolerance.

Microstructure of M-HPCS/HA-PEG hydrogels

Hydrogels used as wound dressings require appropriate pore sizes to concentrate blood for rapid hemostasis in early wound stages, provide a moist, breathable environment suitable for healing, and must avoid excessive small pores that could become blocked with blood clots, hindering exudate absorption and cell adhesion/proliferation. Thus, pore size was critical for wound healing. Fig. 3A shows SEM images of M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7, M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1, M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3, and M-HPCS9/HA-PEG1 hydrogels. All the hydrogels exhibited porous structures, but M-HPCS9/HA-PEG1 had a loose, irregular network and was excluded from further performance tests. M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7, M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1, and M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 showed uniform porous networks, with decreased pore size and increased compactness as M-HPCS proportion increases (Fig. S3): average pore sizes were 435.26 μm, 191.22 μm, and 128.04 μm, respectively. This trend was attributed to the fact that higher crosslinking density reduced porosity and pore size. Compared to M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7, M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 and M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 had significantly smaller pores and lower porosity, indicating denser networks with better structural stability and mechanical strength, making them more suitable for wound repair.

(C) Digital photographs demonstrating hydrogel adhesion to glass, porcine skin, rubber and aluminum substrates. Previous research results suggested that a hydrogel pore size of 125 μm facilitated the migration of contractile fibroblasts, promoting wound repair [57]. SEM images of M-HPCS/HA-PEG + Mg2+ hydrogels (Fig. 3B) showed porous structures with average pore sizes of 103.44 μm (M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+), 128.04 μm (M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 + Mg2+), and 97.59 μm (M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 + Mg2+) (Fig. S4). Although M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 + Mg2+ had smaller pores, its microstructure showed loose crosslinking and poor stability. In contrast, M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ and M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 + Mg2+ exhibited dense crosslinking and uniform pores. Mg2+ addition significantly reduced porosity and pore size in these hydrogels; M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ showed a distinct reduction in average pore size compared to M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 + Mg2+, indicating enhanced crosslinking, improved stability, and suitable pore size for wound repair. These results confirmed that Mg2+ promotes tighter molecular crosslinking in the hydrogels.

Adhesiveness of M-HPCS/HA-PEG hydrogels

Effective adhesiveness is essential for wound dressings to inhibit bacteria, promote healing, prevent bacterial infection, and seal blood/fluid leakage, maintaining a favorable wound microenvironment. Melanin contained abundant phenolic groups, phenolic hydroxyls, and quinone groups, which interacted with amino and thiol groups in tissues, enabling hydrogel adhesion. Fig. 3C shows that the hydrogel could adhere to various materials (e.g., polytetrafluoroethylene, glass) and conformed tightly to skin; even after finger bending at different angles, the hydrogel remained attached, demonstrating good adhesiveness and flexibility. Upon peeling, the hydrogel detached completely from the skin without causing tissue damage, indicating appropriate adhesive strength that avoids secondary injury. Quantitative assessment of skin adhesiveness using a universal testing machine (Fig. 3D) showed adhesive strengths of 3.512 kPa (M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7), 3.943 kPa (M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1), 4.613 kPa (M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3) and 1.891 kPa (positive control). Melanin imparted good adhesiveness, which increased with melanin content. M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 exhibited the best adhesiveness, suitable for wound repair and superior adhesion performance relative to the commercial product.

After skin injury, blood and fluid leakage required hydrogel dressings to seal the wound for hemostasis, rendering strong adhesiveness indispensable. Inspired by mussels, polyphenols were widely used to enhance hydrogel adhesiveness. Melanin, a polyphenol with catechol structures, conferred good adhesiveness to M-HPCS/HA-PEG hydrogels. The effect of Mg2+ on adhesiveness was evaluated using a universal testing machine (Fig. 3E). Mg2+ increased the adhesive strengths of M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ (6.701 kPa) and M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 + Mg2+ (4.457 kPa), with the most significant improvement in M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+, likely due to enhanced internal crosslinking. In contrast, M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 + Mg2+ showed reduced adhesiveness (2.562 kPa) because Mg2+ chelated with phenolic hydroxyls, occupying binding sites and reducing network crosslinking, consistent with microstructure observations. These results confirmed that M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ and M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 + Mg2+ stably adhered to skin without causing secondary injury during removal, while M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 + Mg2+ had poor network stability.

In the absence of Mg2+, high melanin content disrupts the M-HPCS/HA-PEG hydrogen bonding network and reduces adhesion; however, upon Mg2+ coordination, the same high melanin content becomes the optimal adhesive through melanin-Mg2+-polymer ternary complex formation. This Mg2+-dependent inversion of the melanin-adhesion relationship, converting an adhesion-disruptive component into an adhesion-promoting one, cannot be achieved by simple physical mixing and represents the core rationally designed feature of our stage-responsive hydrogel.

Swelling properties of M-HPCS/HA-PEG hydrogels

Swelling behavior was evaluated by immersing lyophilized M-HPCS/HA-PEG hydrogels in PBS and measuring mass changes over time. Fig. 4A shows 24-h swelling ratios of 54.15 (M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7), 35.47 (M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1), and 26.94 (M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3). M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 and M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 exhibited significantly lower swelling ratios within the optimal range, enabling effective absorption of wound exudate without excessive expansion or detachment. Consistent with microstructure observations, M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 had larger pores and loose crosslinking, facilitating water penetration and higher swelling, while M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 and M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 showed lower swelling due to melanin's hydrophobic benzene rings and higher crosslinking density. These results confirmed that increasing melanin content enhanced crosslinking and improved hydrogel performance.

Photothermal properties of M-HPCS/HA-PEG hydrogels

Hot water baths or mild thermal stimulation promote angiogenesis, increased vascular density in wound granulation tissue, and accelerated healing. M-HPCS/HA-PEG hydrogels were irradiated with 880 nm near-infrared (NIR) light at different powers to evaluate photothermal conversion. Fig. 4C shows temperature increases of 41.3°C, 43.4°C, 47.9°C, 51.5°C, and 56°C after 10-min irradiation at 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.25, and 1.5 W/cm2, respectively, exhibiting power-dependent behavior: higher power densities resulted in faster heating rates and elevated maximum temperatures. At 1 W/cm2 (Fig. 4E), M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7, M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1, and M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 reached maximum temperatures of 47.9°C, 51.3°C, and 55.45°C within 10 min, respectively, confirming melanin imparted photothermal properties, with efficiency increasing with melanin content (M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 > others). Thermal images (Fig. 4I) visually confirmed temperature elevation, supporting the potential of M-HPCS/HA-PEG hydrogels for photothermal therapy in wound repair.

Fig. 4G shows minimal temperature variation after five NIR irradiation cycles (maximum temperatures: 51°C, 50.2°C, 51°C, 51.5°C, 51.5°C), indicating good photothermal stability.

For M-HPCS/HA-PEG + Mg2+ hydrogels irradiated with 808 nm NIR (Fig. 4D and F), temperatures rose rapidly and plateaued, reaching 46.17°C (M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+), 48.6°C (M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 + Mg2+), and 50.1°C (M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 + Mg2+) after 10 min. Similar to M-HPCS/HA-PEG, photothermal performance depended on melanin content, with Mg2+ showing no significant effect, confirming good photothermal conversion, and thermal imaging results were consistent with this observation (Fig. 4J).

Photothermal stability tests (Fig. 4H) showed consistent maximum temperatures (∼47.4°C) over five cycles, confirming good stability of M-HPCS/HA-PEG + Mg2+ hydrogels.

Antioxidant properties of M-HPCS/HA-PEG hydrogels

In normal physiology, free radicals are balanced between production and scavenging. However, tissue damage induces excessive free radicals, which causes inflammation, oxidative stress, infection, and necrosis, thereby delaying healing. Thus, wound-healing hydrogels must scavenge excess free radicals to regulate the wound microenvironment. Melanin, a complex macromolecule formed by oxidative polymerization of hydroxyphenols or indoles, exhibits good antioxidant activity: its phenolic hydroxyls donated protons to bind reactive radicals, and p-π conjugation of oxygen stabilized the bound radicals. Melanin's antioxidant mechanism involves redox reactions via dihydroxyindole (DHI) and 5,6-dihydroxyindole-2-carboxylic acid (DHICA) monomers, which undergo reversible diphenol/benzoquinone redox cycling, thereby scavenging free radicals.

DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging assays evaluated M-HPCS/HA-PEG antioxidant activity. DPPH, a stable nitrogen-centered radical with an unpaired electron, was scavenged by antioxidants donating electrons or hydrogen ions, measurable via absorbance. Fig. S5 shows DPPH scavenging rates of 71.99% (M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7), 74.98% (M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1), and 77.16% (M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3), indicating good antioxidant activity increasing with melanin content. ABTS radical scavenging rates showed similar trends: scavenging rates of 72.18%, 73.23%, and 74.59%, confirming melanin enhances antioxidant activity in a dose-dependent manner.

Highly reactive free radicals, byproducts of cell metabolism, accumulated in chronic wounds due to excessive inflammation, attacking cell membranes and reacting with unsaturated fatty acids to promote lipid peroxidation. Thus, free radical scavenging accelerated healing. Polyphenol-rich melanin exhibited significant antioxidant activity, and metal chelation might further enhance this. Mg2+-containing hydrogels were tested using the same methods. Fig. S6 shows DPPH scavenging rates of 84.10% (M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+), 84.13% (M-HPCS1/HA-PEG1 + Mg2+), and 85.26% (M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 + Mg2+), indicating enhanced antioxidant activity with Mg2+. ABTS assays showed scavenging rates of 73.91%, 75.19%, and 76.82%, all higher than M-HPCS/HA-PEG, confirming Mg2+ enhanced radical scavenging in M-HPCS/HA-PEG + Mg2+ hydrogels.

Cytocompatibility evaluation of hydrogels

Skin tissue repair involves proliferation and migration of fibroblasts and vascular endothelial cells. Thus, L929 and HUVEC cell lines were used to evaluate M-HPCS/HA-PEG cytocompatibility. MTT assays assessed M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3's effect on cell viability and proliferation. Fig. S7 shows relative proliferation rates of L929 and HUVECs co-cultured with different concentrations of M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 extracts for 24 and 48 h. All rates exceeded 85% at 24 h and 90% at 48 h, indicating no cytotoxicity per toxicity grading standards.

Optical microscopy of cell morphology after 24 and 48 h (Fig. S8) showed healthy growth: L929 cells were mostly spindle-shaped, HUVECs were cobblestone-like, with clear contours and no atrophy or death. The increased cell numbers at 48 h further confirmed the non-toxicity and good biocompatibility of the hydrogel extracts.

M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ biocompatibility was evaluated similarly. Fig. 5A shows relative proliferation rates of L929 and HUVECs exceeding 80% at 24 and 48 h, indicating Mg2+ was non-toxic and M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ had good biocompatibility.

Angiogenesis assay

Neovascularization provided oxygen and nutrients for collagen synthesis and re-epithelialization. In vitro angiogenesis assays (HUVECs on Matrigel) showed that hydrogel groups exhibited angiogenic trends at 2 h, minimal vessels at 4 h, and distinct tubular structures at 8 h, while controls showed minimal trends (Fig. 6B). Quantification (Fig. 6D–E) showed 8-h node/vessel counts of 48/78 (M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3) and 46/77 (M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+), twice that of controls, confirming pro-angiogenic effects.

Antioxidant capacity analysis

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) regulate intracellular signaling but excessive ROS (oxidative stress) causes tissue damage, delaying healing via prolonged inflammation. MTT assays were employed to assess H2O2-induced cytotoxicity in L929 damage, and Fig. 6F shows more than 80% viability at 0-400 μM H2O2 but 13.42% and 9.60% at 600 and 800 μM, respectively. Thus, 500 μM H2O2 was used to test hydrogel protective effects.

Fig. 6G shows 500 μM H2O2 reduced viability to 67.36%, while M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 extracts (20%-100%) restored viability to 94.71%-99.87%, indicating protective effects. M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ extracts (20%-100%) restored viability to 98.15%-107.67%, with stronger antioxidant activity, likely due to Mg2+ reducing transmembrane calcium influx, protecting membranes, and decreasing ROS production.

DCFH (hydrolyzed from DCFH-DA) was oxidized by intracellular ROS to fluorescent DCF, reflecting ROS levels. Fig. 6C shows minimal green fluorescence in untreated controls (no ROS overproduction) and healthy cell morphology. H2O2-treated controls showed strong green fluorescence (high ROS) and atrophic, rounded cells (apoptosis). Hydrogel-treated groups M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 and M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ showed weak green fluorescence (low ROS) and healthy spindle-shaped cells, confirming hydrogels regulate ROS balance, prevent oxidative stress, and protect cells.

Wound healing observation

Deep second-degree burns were induced on rat backs, with immediate blanching, mild swelling, and no exudate. On day 3, control wounds remained blanched, swollen, and slightly purulent (severe inflammation) while positive controls were partially scabbed, non-swollen, and reduced in size, and experimental groups were fully scabbed, flat, non-swollen, and reduced in size. On day 7, blank controls showed swollen, uneven, purulent exudate with necrotic debris (persistent inflammation) while controls and experimental groups formed flat, regular scabs with no inflammation, and experimental groups had smaller wounds, especially NIR-treated groups (accelerated healing). On day 14, blank controls were slightly swollen with minor reduction while positive controls and experimental groups showed reduced wound size. On day 21, controls had red, large wounds while positive controls had scab loss with minor bruising, and experimental groups were mostly re-epithelialized with pink, smooth skin and new hair. On day 28, controls were partially unhealed while positive controls were incompletely healed, and experimental groups were fully healed with normal-appearing skin and no scarring (Fig. 7B–C).

Wound healing rate analysis

Fig. 7D shows healing rates over 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days. On day 3, the control rate was 2%, while others were around 15%. On day 7, the control rate was 14.87%, while hydrogel groups were around 30% and hydrogel + NIR groups around 38%. On day 14, the control rate was 40.32%, while others were 52.73%-63.75%, with M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 + NIR and M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ + NIR showing the highest rates (62.25%, 63.75%). Hydrogel groups showed rapid improvement from day 7 to day 14, likely due to hydrogels' anti-inflammatory and anti-fibroblast hyperproliferation effects, transitioning wounds from inflammation to proliferation. Hydrogel groups had higher day 7 rates but slower fibroblast proliferation thereafter to avoid scarring. On day 21, all groups except controls were more than 90% healed. On day 28, M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 + NIR and M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ + NIR achieved 100% healing, while controls and positive groups remained partially unhealed. These results confirm hydrogels promote healing, with photothermal therapy enhancing efficacy.

In vivo photothermal conversion analysis

Hydrogel combined with photothermal therapy groups (M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 + NIR, M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ + NIR) were tested for optimal power. Hydrogels were applied to wounds and irradiated with 808 nm NIR at 1.0, 1.25, 1.5 W/cm2 for 5 min; temperatures were recorded (Fig. 7E). At 1 W/cm2, maximum temperatures reached around 42°C, while at 1.25 W/cm2 they reached around 43°C, and at 1.5 W/cm2 they reached around 45°C at 3 min and around 50°C at 5 min, all of which are suitable for healing (Fig. 7F–G). Thus, subsequent treatments used 1.5 W/cm2 for 5 min per dressing change.

Adhesion strength variation

As shown in Fig. 7H–I, lap shear adhesion on both healthy skin and diabetic wound surfaces was measured at 0.5, 2, 4, and 6 h post-application. The adhesion strength progressively decreased from 7.034 kPa (0.5 h) to 2.589 kPa (6 h), reflecting the hydrogel's tissue-adaptive interface formation. The initial high adhesion ensures rapid wound sealing and hemostasis upon application, while the subsequent decay to moderate levels prevents excessive bonding that would complicate later dressing removal. This temporal profile is governed by the melanin-Mg2+-polymer coordination network, which responds dynamically to tissue fluid composition and hydration state. The commercial chitosan hydrogel control exhibited lower adhesion over the same time course.

In diabetic burn wounds, this short-term adhesion decay is particularly advantageous because the prolonged inflammatory phase and excessive exudate in diabetic tissue can cause conventional hydrogels to either lose adhesion prematurely or bond excessively causing secondary injury upon removal. Our hydrogel maintains effective sealing during the critical early hours while transitioning to a removable state within the same wound care cycle.

Consistent with H&E results as shown in Fig. 8B, Masson staining results on day 3 showed epidermal damage, obscure structure, collagen denaturation, and destroyed appendages. The results on day 7 showed necrosis, inflammation, vacuoles, and disorganized collagen. The results on day 14 showed that controls retained necrotic tissue with no proliferation; hydrogel groups showed inflammation and disorganized collagen but with capillaries, fibroblasts, and new collagen. The results on day 21 showed that controls had incomplete re-epithelialization, fibrosis, and no appendages and hydrogel groups showed scab loss, intact epidermis, papillary layer, clear demarcation, organized collagen, complete dermal repair, and partial appendages. The results on day 28 showed that controls had congestion and incomplete repair and hydrogel groups fully healed with differentiated epidermis, normal structure, uniform appendages, and organized collagen. Quantitative analysis in Fig. S9 showed that the M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ group exhibited the greatest epithelial thickness and highest collagen area percentage, significantly outperforming the control group, indicating that this hydrogel effectively promotes re-epithelialization and extracellular matrix remodeling in diabetic wounds.

Macrophages played a key role in wound healing as M1 macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory factors in early stages but prolonged inflammation hinders healing. iNOS immunofluorescence (M1 marker) showed high red fluorescence in saline-treated controls with severe inflammation while reduced fluorescence in other groups indicated normal inflammation confirming M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 and M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ hydrogels have in vivo anti-inflammatory activity and NIR further promoted M1-to-M2 polarization regulating the immune microenvironment as shown in Fig. 9A.

In diabetic wounds, high glucose disrupted macrophage polarization (excessive M1, insufficient M2), causing prolonged inflammation. M2 macrophages secreted anti-inflammatory factors and growth factors, promoting granulation tissue formation. CD206 immunofluorescence (M2 marker) on day 7 showed stronger green fluorescence in M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 and M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ groups than controls, with further enhancement in NIR-treated groups, indicating NIR promotes M2 polarization and transition to proliferation (Fig. 9B). Controls showed weak CD206 fluorescence (persistent inflammation). These results confirmed hydrogels could promote M2 polarization, regulate inflammation, and accelerate healing, with NIR enhancing efficacy.

CD31, an early angiogenesis transmembrane protein, and α-SMA, a late angiogenesis cytoplasmic protein and marker of vascular smooth muscle maturity, were evaluated via immunofluorescence for in vivo angiogenesis. Fig. 10A–B shows stronger fluorescence in M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 and M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ groups than controls on day 7, with further enhancement in NIR-treated groups, confirming hydrogels could promote angiogenesis, with NIR augmenting this effect. In summary, M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 and M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ hydrogels could promote diabetic wound healing by regulating macrophage polarization and angiogenesis, with photothermal therapy enhancing efficacy, and quantitative histological analysis results were consistent with these findings (Fig. 10C–F).

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of M-HPCS/HA-PEG in a clinically relevant infection setting, we established a S. aureus-infected diabetic deep partial-thickness burn wound model in rats (Fig. 12A). Diabetic rats were subjected to deep partial-thickness burn injury, followed by topical inoculation of S. aureus to induce wound infection. Animals were randomly divided into groups and treated with M-HPCS/HA-PEG with or without NIR irradiation, alongside appropriate controls. Wound photographs were captured on days 0, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 to monitor the healing process macroscopically (Fig. 11B).

As demonstrated in Fig. 11B–D, the Hydrogel + NIR groups achieved significantly faster wound closure and superior healing quality compared to the Hydrogel alone and Control groups under infected conditions. Quantitative analysis of wound area over time revealed that photothermal treatment markedly accelerated wound closure, with substantial differences observed from day 7 onward (Fig. 11C). By day 14, the control group exhibited a wound closure rate of 69%, while the hydrogel groups reached approximately 76% and the hydrogel + NIR groups achieved around 87%. Notably, M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 + NIR and M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ + NIR demonstrated the highest closure rates among all treatment groups (Fig. 11D).

To assess the in vivo antibacterial activity, wound tissue homogenates were collected on day 7 and subjected to agar plate culture. Although colonies of other bacterial species were observed on the agar plates due to the polymicrobial nature of the wound environment, our analysis focused specifically on S. aureus enumeration, consistent with the established infection model. Representative images demonstrated markedly reduced S. aureus growth in the Hydrogel + NIR groups compared to controls (Fig. 11E). Quantitative bacterial counting revealed a significant reduction in S. aureus load in the Hydrogel + NIR groups by day 7, comparable to the positive control (Fig. 11F). These data validate the photothermal-antibacterial efficacy of our hydrogel system against S. aureus in an infected diabetic burn wound setting.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that M-HPCS/HA-PEG-mediated photothermal therapy effectively eradicates S. aureus infection and substantially promotes wound healing in diabetic burn wounds, highlighting its promising translational potential for the management of infected chronic wounds.

Transcriptome analysis

To further investigate the mechanism by which M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7+Mg2+ hydrogel promoted diabetic wound healing, transcriptome analysis was performed on samples collected from the blank group and the M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7+Mg2++NIR group for differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Using DESeq2 (|log2 fold change| > 1.0, FDR <0.05), we identified 1914 DEGs among which 570 were upregulated and 1344 were downregulated between the optimal treatment group (M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7+Mg2++NIR) and the diabetic control (Fig. 12A). Keratin gene expression was regarded as a hallmark of epidermal biology and could accelerate wound healing. Some researchers had incorporated keratin into hydrogels, which exhibited efficient performance in cell adhesion, proliferation, and migration, thereby accelerating wound healing. Notably, we found that compared with the control group, the expression of KRT family genes (Krt1, Krt5, Krt10, Krt14, Krt16) was upregulated in diabetic rats with dorsal burns treated with M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7+Mg2+ hydrogel combined therapy, suggesting that M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7+Mg2+ hydrogel might promote wound healing through epidermal development, consistent with enhanced wound closure. The downregulation of Nfkbia, Il1b, and Traf1 aligns with our immunofluorescence data showing reduced iNOS+ M1 macrophages and increased CD206+ M2 macrophages, suggesting that the suppression of these genes could inhibit the NF-κB and TNF signaling pathways, thereby attenuating the inflammatory response (Fig. 12A–B). These findings indicated that M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7+Mg2+ hydrogel combined with photothermal therapy accelerated diabetic wound healing by promoting epidermal development and inhibiting inflammatory responses.

Subsequently, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was performed on DEGs (Fig. 12D–E). The results showed that upregulated DEGs were enriched in pathways related to cell adhesion, proliferation, and tissue regeneration, such as the Hippo signaling pathway, Rap 1 signaling pathway, cadherin signaling, and Wnt signaling pathway (Fig. 12D). Downregulated DEGs were mainly enriched in pathways associated with chronic inflammation and immune response, including the chemokine signaling pathway, NF-κB signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, and TGF-β signaling pathway (Fig. 12E). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted on DEGs (Fig. 12F), including Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), and Molecular Function (MF). The results showed that DEGs were mainly enriched in terms such as skin development, epidermis development, intermediate filament, intermediate filament cytoskeleton, nucleoside-triphosphatase regulator activity, and extracellular matrix structural constituent, indicating that M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7+Mg2+ hydrogel combined with photothermal therapy promoted wound healing. Wang et al. demonstrated that diabetic wound healing could be accelerated by regulating pro-inflammatory a pathway. The Circos chord diagram (Fig. 12C) further revealed the gene-level architecture of these enriched pathways. Key inflammatory genes including Nfkbia, Tnfaip3, Il1b, and Map2k6. All significantly downregulated, were centrally involved in the NF-κB, TNF, and MAPK signaling pathways, confirming the suppression of chronic inflammation. Conversely, the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway comprised the largest gene set, encompassing multiple collagen genes (Col1a2, Col6a5, Col6a6), extracellular matrix proteins (Comp), and growth factors (Pdgfd, Fgf 5), indicating active tissue remodeling. Thbs1 and Pik3cd were identified as hub genes bridging the Hippo and PI3K-Akt pathways, potentially coordinating the transition from inflammation to repair. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7+Mg2+ hydrogel combined with photothermal therapy orchestrates a coordinated response: suppressing pro-inflammatory signaling while maintaining reparative pathway activity, thereby promoting physiological healing in infected diabetic burn wounds.

Finally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted on DEGs (Fig. 12G), and the results showed that DEGs were downregulated in the NF-κB signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway and TGF-β signaling pathway which further confirmed that M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7+Mg2+ hydrogel combined with photothermal therapy could coordinately suppresses multiple signaling pathways, attenuating excessive inflammation, reducing pathological fibrosis, and restoring homeostasis in the diabetic wound microenvironment. Collectively, M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7+Mg2+ hydrogel combined with photothermal therapy accelerated diabetic wound healing by promoting epidermal development, skin development, and inhibiting inflammatory responses.

We can therefore conclude that the priming phase initiates inflammation resolution through sustained Mg2+ release from the coordination network, which suppresses the multiple signaling pathway as indicated by transcriptomic GSEA analysis. Immunofluorescence quantification corroborates this, showing reduced iNOS+ M1 macrophages and increased CD206+ M2 macrophages, thereby driving macrophage polarization toward the pro-repair phenotype and establishing an anti-inflammatory microenvironment independent of photothermal input. The activation phase amplifies angiogenesis via NIR irradiation of the melanin component, which activates the PI3K-Akt/HIF-1 signaling cascade and initiates a pro-angiogenic transcriptional program. This is corroborated by immunofluorescence showing increased CD31+ endothelial cell density and α-SMA+ pericyte coverage, indicating enhanced neovascularization and vascular maturation.

Biocompatibility

As shown in Fig. 13A, the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys maintained normal histological structures without obvious pathological changes. Fig. 13B shows that the subcutaneous and intramuscular implantation sites healed well on days 3, 7, and 14, with no obvious redness, swelling, exudation, abscess, necrosis, or abnormal granulation tissue. Fig. 13C shows that the levels of ALP, AST, ALT, and BUN did not differ significantly among groups and remained within the normal range. As shown in Fig. 13D, the hemolysis ratios of the M-HPCS7/HA-PEG3 and M-HPCS3/HA-PEG7 + Mg2+ hydrogels at different concentrations were 2.78 ± 0.41%, 3.35 ± 0.48%, 2.43 ± 0.97%, and 3.10 ± 0.34%, respectively, all of which were markedly lower than that of the positive control, indicating good hemocompatibility.

Conclusion

Based on the intermolecular hydrogen bonding interactions between M-HPCS and HA-PEG, this study constructed a multifunctional hydrogel dressing with controllable adhesiveness for application in diabetic skin wound repair. Results of in vitro characterization demonstrated that the hydrogel exhibited excellent swell ability, which enabled it to absorb excess exudate from diabetic wounds while maintaining a moist wound microenvironment. The melanin component in the hydrogel, enriched with phenolic hydroxyl and quinone groups, could interact with moieties such as amino groups in tissues, thereby endowing the hydrogel with prominent tissue adhesiveness. Furthermore, the tissue adhesion strength increased with the elevation of melanin content. The melanin component also conferred favorable antioxidant activity and photothermal performance to the hydrogel. Experimental data showed that its DPPH free radical scavenging rate reached 77.16% and ABTS free radical scavenging rate reached 74.59%, while the maximum temperature could reach 55.45°C following 10 min of near-infrared light irradiation at a power density of 1 W/cm2. When the hydrogel chelated Mg2+, its adhesiveness and antioxidant performance were further enhanced, which could meet the clinical demands of special wounds that pose challenges for adhesion under humid conditions. Cellular-level experiments verified that the hydrogel could effectively promote the proliferation and migration of L929 cells and HUVECs, and exhibited potent pro-angiogenic activity. Experiments on skin wound repair in diabetic rats revealed that the hydrogel could significantly accelerate wound healing, and the group receiving hydrogel combined with photothermal therapy showed higher healing efficacy. Notably, the hydrogel also demonstrated effective antibacterial capability against S. aureus infection in diabetic burn wounds, significantly reducing bacterial load and thereby facilitating the repair of infected wounds. Histological analysis results indicated that the hydrogel could efficiently regulate the inflammatory response, and promote neovascularization, granulation tissue proliferation, and epithelialization. Transcriptome analysis showed that in the hydrogel group, the expression of epidermal development-related KRT family genes (Krt1, Krt5, Krt10, Krt14, Krt16) was upregulated, while the expression of inflammation-associated genes (TRAF1, IL-1β, Nfkbia) was downregulated. Additionally, the hydrogel could inhibit the NF-κB signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, and MAPK signaling pathway. In vitro cytotoxicity assays and in vivo biocompatibility evaluations confirmed that the hydrogel exhibited favorable biosafety, with no significant adverse effects observed on cell viability or tissue morphology. In conclusion, this study successfully developed a multifunctional bioactive hydrogel dressing. It features controllable adhesiveness, can effectively alleviate the inflammatory microenvironment and oxidative stress in chronic diabetic wounds, promote angiogenesis and skin tissue regeneration, and is expected to provide a novel combinatorial therapeutic strategy for the clinical management of chronic diabetic wounds.
