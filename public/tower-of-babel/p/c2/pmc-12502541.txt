Clinical presentation and management outcome of abdominal stab wounds in a low-resource setting: experience of a central hospital in Sudan
Authors: Mohamed Soud Mohamed Saadeldien, Mohamed Daffalla Awadalla Gismalla, Adam Mohamed Ibrahim Adam, Aimen Elsheikh Khalil Abuelnour, Mustafa Abdullah Alhamed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAbdominal stab injuries are a major public health concern worldwide, especially in African countries. This study aimed to examine the presentation patterns and management outcomes of abdominal stab wounds.MethodsThis descriptive, prospective, cross-sectional, hospital-based study was conducted at Wad Medani Teaching Hospital, including all patients with abdominal stab wounds from January 2022 to December 2022. Patient characteristics and clinical presentations were documented. Factors influencing management outcomes were analyzed and correlated.ResultThe total number of patients admitted to the hospital with abdominal stab wounds is 47. The most common age group is between 20 and 29 years (38.3%), with the majority being male (97.9%). They are mainly from Gezira State (61.7%) and live in rural areas (38.3%). The most common occupation among them is labor, making up 51.1%. Patients were admitted within the first two hours in 57.5% of cases. The organs most often involved in this study include the small bowel, large bowel, and mesentery, affecting 27.7%, 23.4%, and 17.0% of patients, respectively. Additionally, 25.4% of patients sustained associated injuries. Of the 40 patients treated, 30 (60.8%) underwent a laparotomy. From a total of 30 laparotomies, 14 patients (46.6%) underwent resection and anastomosis, while 7 patients (23.3%) had their small bowel injuries repaired. Most patients recovered, while four (8.5%) developed complications.ConclusionIn our setting, abdominal stab wounds predominantly affect young males in their productive years. The small intestine is the most frequently injured organ, and most patients require an exploratory laparotomy as part of their treatment.

Background

Abdominal trauma is a critical surgical emergency, classified into blunt or penetrating injuries. Penetrating injuries include gunshot (bullet) wounds and stab (knife) wounds [1]. The World Health Organization has emphasized knife-related interpersonal violence as an urgent public health concern, as it is a leading cause of death among young people worldwide [2]. In Africa, penetrating abdominal injuries make up 30% to 66% of all abdominal trauma cases in emergency departments [3, 4]. Young men aged 18–35 are disproportionately affected by stab wounds across various African countries, as this age group is more likely to engage in risky behaviors such as substance abuse and criminal activity, which increases their risk of violent injuries [3, 5–7].

Abdominal stab wounds present with diverse clinical features and modes. This variability can influence treatment approaches and management outcomes. The management of penetrating abdominal trauma has progressed considerably over the years. After years of mandatory exploration, expectant management of selected patients has become more common. In specialized trauma centers located in developed countries, the non-operative selective management protocol has been used for selected cases of penetrating abdominal trauma. It could decrease unnecessary laparotomies in hemodynamically stable patients without signs of evisceration or sepsis [5, 8]. However, these protocols could not be generalized to developing countries such as Sudan, but were used with some limitations.

Resource constraints and infrastructural limitations complicate the management of stab wounds in low-resource countries like Sudan [9]. Poor road networks and unreliable transportation systems delay patient transfer to tertiary care facilities, and delayed recognition of life-threatening complications due to overcrowded emergency departments and shortages of trained personnel hinders effective triage and initial stabilization. A shortage of essential diagnostic tools, such as ultrasound (US) machines and computed tomography (CT) scans, which are crucial for assessing the extent of internal injuries [10].

Abdominal injuries had a great effect and impact on the health system, causing prolonged hospital stay [5, 11]. Additionally, it had prolonged morbidity and a social impact on the family [12]. Recent research conducted over the past decade in various Sudanese cities has revealed that penetrating abdominal injuries are common among patients in their twenties. These injuries are primarily treated with exploratory laparotomy [7, 13]. This research was carried out to highlight the demographic character and clinical pattern of patients presenting with abdominal stab wounds to the Wad Medani Teaching Hospital. The management outcomes for those patients were also evaluated.

Methods

Study types

This prospective descriptive cohort study was conducted at a Wad Medani Teaching Hospital from January 2022 to December 2022.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Any patient who presented to the hospital with a single stab wound or multiple stab wounds to the abdomen and received treatment in the general surgical department was included in the study. Patients with abdominal stab wounds with associated organ injuries were also included. In contrast, patients with blunt abdominal trauma or gunshot wound/s to the abdomen were excluded. Other patients with isolated chest stab wound/s were excluded.

Study setting

The study was conducted at Wad Medani Teaching Hospital, the central and secondary referral hospital for Gezira State, Sudan, and surrounding states. The data was collected via a data collection sheet. The sheet contains demographic data (age, sex, occupation, and residence of the patients), abdominal trauma data (time of presentation, type of injury, mode of injury, organ involved), and abdominal trauma intervention data (including observation of time of intervention, initial management, and surgical management, and the short term outcome of abdominal stab wound).

Participant clinical evaluations

The patient was transported to the emergency department by a relative or attendant and was subsequently evaluated by the surgical team on call. A B C D E assessment according to the Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) protocol was initiated. The surgical emergency team consists of medical officers, the registrar of general surgery, and house officers. The general surgery consultant on duty leads a team of doctors who are always available. His vital signs and examination were performed. Circulation status and shock were assessed. Patients with grade 3 and 4 shock will receive blood transfusions. Investigations: Focused Assessment with Sonography in Trauma (FAST) scan, US, chest X-ray, and CT scan were requested for selected patients. Image indications are assessed on a case-by-case basis; usually, for patients not scheduled for exploratory laparotomy, vital signs are stable, and there is no evidence of evisceration.

At the time of discharge, any patient with no clinical, laboratory, or imaging evidence of complications is classified as cured. The second group, patients with surgical site infection or abdominal infection, will be classified as having complications.

Data collection tools

Data was collected using a flowchart sheet. Characteristics of the patients, such as age, sex, residence, marital status, occupation, and comorbidity, were recorded. Information regarding time of arrival, associated injuries, severity of injuries, management, and outcome was also collected.

Data analysis

The data was processed using the IBM SPSS program Version 26, and the results were presented in the form of tables and figures. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine of Gezira University.

Results

This study included 47 patients. Their mean age was 27 years (SD ± 10), ranging from 14 to 60 years. Stab injuries to the abdomen were common in the 20 to 29 age group (36.2%). Males were predominant, with 46 (97.9%) of the patients being male, and only one female was reported in this study. Most patients were residents of Gezira state (61.7%) and from urban regions. The most common occupations were industrial and service laborers, who made up 24 (51.1%) of the total, followed by students at 14 (29.8%). Detailed sociodemographic characteristics are shown in Table 1. A majority of patients, nearly half, 27 (47.4%), are brought to the hospital within less than two hours after their injury. About a quarter 12 (25.5%) of these patients also have other associated injuries.

Discussion

The majority of the victims were younger than 30 years. These findings were reported by many African and low-income countries [5–7]. Men represented the vast majority of patients with abdominal stab wounds in our series, with only one victim being a woman. The higher number of men can be attributed to their greater involvement in acts of violence and criminal activities, which were the primary causes of stab wounds [14]; these findings confirm other local and international figures [5–7]. The incidence of abdominal stab wounds was shown to be more common among people of low socioeconomic class [15]. In our study, half of the victims were industrial and service workers. We observed that a substantial proportion of victims in our study were students. We did not assess the use of alcohol and drugs among our patients, which is a recognized risk factor for knife violence in this age group [16].

Regarding the anatomical profiles of abdominal stab wounds in our study, we recognized that some patients had other site stab wounds besides abdominal stab wounds, among 25.5% of victims, of which the chest was involved (19.1%), followed by the head (4.3%) and upper limb (2.1%). The gastrointestinal tract (GIT) was the site most involved in abdominal stab injuries in our study, with the small intestine the most common site in the GIT, followed by the large intestine, mesentery, and stomach. These findings were consistent with other similar studies [17–19]. The GIT is more prone to abdominal stabbing injuries because most of our patients were victims of violence associated with events in which victims faced their attackers or engaged in a fight. Stabs were directed to the anterior abdominal wall (57.5%), and the upper abdominal regions were the main affected sites (40.4%). The stabbing to the flanks constitutes (25.5%). Solid organ injuries are less frequent in abdominal stab injuries [17]. In our study, we recognized three patients (8.5%) with splenic injury and one patient with liver injury. Diaphragmatic injury occurred in two patients (4.2%). Most of the patients (57.5%) were brought to the hospital within two hours after injury, and 14.9% presented after more than four hours. Fourteen patients, 29.7%, presented to the accident and emergency department with hypovolaemic shock, while 18 (38.2%) presented with features of peritonitis. Evisceration through the stab wound was noted in five (10.6%) of the patients. The majority of them were omentum alone in 3 (6.3%) patients. The evisceration case was resuscitated, and the omentum was covered with a wet gauze dressing. Subsequently, the patient underwent an emergency laparotomy via a midline incision. This approach aligns with findings from a similar study conducted in Sudan [17].

The treatment of patients with penetrating abdominal trauma continues to evolve. After many years of compulsory exploration, expectant management of selected patients has become commonplace. About five patients (10%) in our study responded to conservative treatment. The non-operative selective management protocol for penetrating abdominal trauma has reduced unnecessary laparotomies [20]. Selective patients, without evidence of peritonitis, haemodynamically stable without shock, no evisceration of omentum, can be treated nonoperatively, with restricted follow-up [21]. In our study, 63.8% of the cases underwent laparotomy, which is comparable to similar studies from Ethiopia (79.4%) and Kenya (70%) [22, 23]. This study is concentrated mainly on stab abdomen, and with a small number, while the other studies present gunshot injuries, which can lead to a high laparotomy rate. Local wound exploration may be a valuable diagnostic aid depending on the wound’s mechanism and location. The patient was admitted and followed for another 24 h before being discharged. Stab wounds to the anterior abdominal wall are selected for local wound exploration because many do not penetrate the fascia [24].

The mortality rate in our series was 2.1%, and the short-term morbidities were 8%. The lower mortality rate is most likely related to the type of injuries, as most of the stab wounds were due to civilian trauma, and most of them are defensive stabs. Surgical site infection was the common complication and occurred in 8.5%, and abdominal collection in 2.1%. The rest of the patients (87.2%) recovered completely. We did not follow the patient for long-term morbidities, that is, incisional hernia and postoperative bowel adhesion. In a series of abdominal gunshot wounds alone, the mortality rate was 34.3% [25]. In South Africa and the British health systems, the mortality rate for abdominal stab wounds was less than 3% [26]– [27]. Mortality rate of Kenyatta National Hospital, Kenya (12.5%) and (10%) in a similar study in Ethiopia [22, 23]. In Germany, where a more effective healthcare system is present, Malkommes reported a mortality of 1.7% and a morbidity of 8.7% [8].

This low mortality rate, when compared with other studies, could be due to the bias of selection in abdominal stab wounds. While other studies added gunshot injuries with road traffic accidents, abdominal trauma, and both can be considered high-risk injuries. The second explanation, the unavailability of emergency ambulance transport services in our setting, adds another bias, as it did not include other serious injuries. Which died before reaching the emergency department. Most of the patients died before getting the appropriate treatment due to a lack of emergency ambulance service, which can not reach the emergency department promptly. In our situation, the exact data of those patients and those who have already died are not determined. This outcome, despite the similarity of some high-income countries, does not mean the out is the same; this contradicts the fact that the presented patient selected naturally with a good status, and a seriously ill patient died before reaching the hospital.

Limitations of the study

One limitation of this study is the small sample size and selection bias, which prevent thorough analysis and accurate results. The lack of extended post-operative and treatment follow-up is also a concern. Additionally, the descriptive nature of the study without a control group results in limited data. Lastly, the difficulty of the imaging study and the management protocol can be considered limitations as well. Future research should include an analytic, comparative, and intervention-based study.

Conclusion

Managing abdominal stab wounds in a low-income country is challenging. Limited investigations and infrastructure hinder treating surgeons from choosing and advocating for non-operative management when appropriate. In our setting, exploratory laparotomy is the most common surgical procedure, which does not rely on radiological evidence for decision-making; it depends on clinical judgment, haemodynamic instability, evisceration of organs through the wounds, and peritonitis. Conservative non-operative treatment should be considered for selected patients, hemodynamically stable, with no peritonitis or other indications for exploration besides closed and meticulous follow-up. More research is needed to compare outcomes between other abdominal traumas, such as gunshot injuries and blunt trauma, versus stab wounds. Additionally, the number of patients who die before reaching the hospital or at the primary care level should be documented and reviewed.
