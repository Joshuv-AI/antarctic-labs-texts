Unmasking the silent culprit: recurrent exercise-induced acute kidney injury in a Chinese adolescent with renal hypouricemia
Authors: Yuan Gao, Lu Xu, Mingming Wei, Xiaohan Qu, Tongtong Pan, Xinjian Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary renal hypouricemia (RHUC) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder with a mean duration of end-stage acute kidney injury (EIAKI) of 14 days. The pathogenesis of EIAKI in patients with RHUC remains unclear. Several hypotheses have been proposed, including those related to the renal vasoconvulsive effect and the elevating effect of xanthine oxidase (XO). The effect of xanthine oxidase (XO) is most often observed following strenuous anaerobic exercise, which is frequently accompanied by low back pain, nausea, and acute kidney injury (AKI). Consequently, we postulate that EIAKI could be prevented by avoiding strenuous exercise, thus preventing the onset and recurrence of EIAKI. In this paper, we present a case of recurrent EIAKI in a patient with RHUC and a mutation in the SLC2A9 gene.Keywords: RHUC, EIAKI, SLC2A9 gene, autosomal recessive disorder, Kidney, biopsy

Primary renal hypouricemia (RHUC) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by impaired reabsorption of uric acid into the bloodstream with the proximal renal tubules, resulting from mutations in either the URAT1/SLC22A12 gene, which encodes uric acid transporter protein 1, or the GLUT9/SLC2A9 gene, responsible for encoding glucose transporter protein 9. Clinically, RHUC is often indicated by diminished blood levels of uric acid (UA < 2 mg/d or 120 μmol/L), concurrently with elevated fractional excretion of uric acid (FEUA) and/or enhanced uric acid clearance (CUA) [1]. These alterations significantly increase the likelihood of urinary tract stones and exercise-induced acute kidney failure (EIAKI).

The pathogenesis of EIAKI in RHUC patients remains elusive. It is hypothesized to be associated with the renal vasopressor effect of Xanthine Oxidase (XO), particularly following strenuous anaerobic exercise, which is often accompanied by symptoms such as low back pain, nausea, and acute kidney injury (AKI) [1]. Consequently, we propose that the onset and recurrence of EIAKI might be prevented by avoiding strenuous exercise. This paper presents a case study of recurrent EIAKI in a patient with RHUC, specifically with a mutation in the SLC2A9 gene.

We report a case of a Chinese adolescent male with RHUC who initially presented with AKI as the first manifestation of the disease, and conducted a review of available data to investigate predisposing factors and clinical presentations of this condition.

A 17-year-old Chinese adolescent male, previously healthy, was admitted to the pediatric intensive care unit in September 2022. He developed abdominal pain after strenuous exercise, predominantly in the right lower abdomen, accompanied by nausea. Renal function tests at a local hospital revealed a creatinine level of 1083 μmol/L, urea nitrogen of 26.64 mmol/L, and urinary findings included latent blood at 2+ and protein at 1+. Upon hospitalization, further diagnostic tests were conducted (Table 1). An ultrasound of the urinary system showed diffuse bilateral renal damage and increased medullary echogenicity. No abnormalities were detected in tumor marker assessments, chest CT, or electrocardiogram. After ruling out contraindications, creatinine levels were reassessed post three cycles of CVVHDF (continuous veno-venous haemodiafiltration), showing a reduction to 166 µmol/L. Treatment, including Prednisone Acetate Tablets (initiated at 10 tablets/day), continued. Post exclusion of contraindications, a renal biopsy was performed to determine the pathology (Figure 1). Following 10 days of treatment, creatinine levels decreased to 100 μmol/L, and the patient was discharged in good condition. Genetic testing of the patient and his parents, including whole-exon sequencing, identified a pathogenic variant hotspot mutation in chrM:13042 among mitochondrial whole-gene mutations. Family lineage analysis showed a mutation frequency of 17/(17 + 8618) (0%) in the patient’s mother. Additionally, nuclear genetic analysis of the SLC2A9 gene revealed variants highly suggestive of association with the disease phenotype.

After discharge, the patient’s hormone therapy was gradually tapered off based on his condition, and during intermittent follow-ups, renal function and urinary analyses remained normal. However, in November 2023, after running a men’s 1000 m race, he experienced nausea and muscle pain in the lower limbs, along with reduced urine output, dark urine, and urine sediment. Upon readmission, laboratory tests were conducted (Table 1), revealing renal function parameters: creatinine at 527 μmol/L, urea nitrogen at 14.7 mmol/L, and uric acid at 108 μmol/L. Symptomatic treatments, including hydration, urine alkalinization, and rehydration, were administered. Subsequent renal function tests indicated a progressive increase in creatinine and a gradual decrease in uric acid levels, with creatinine rising to 631.3 μmol/L by the third day of admission. After ruling out contraindications, three sessions of hemodialysis were performed, following which renal function normalized in subsequent tests.

The case we report involved two episodes triggered by strenuous exercise, while routine physical activity appeared safe for the patient. At the onset of each episode, gastrointestinal symptoms were the initial presentation. Laboratory findings showed a progressive increase in creatinine, a decrease in uric acid, and an elevated uric acid excretion fraction. Ultrasound findings were consistent with renal injury. Based on these data and current studies, we diagnosed this rare case as renal hypo-uricemia (RHUC), and the two episodes were characterized as exercise-induced acute kidney injury (EIAKI).

Hypouricaemia is classified into two forms: transient and persistent. It arises either due to decreased uric acid (UA) production or increased urinary excretion. Transient hypouricaemia, primarily resulting from mutations in xanthine oxidase, is clinically marked by xanthuria. In contrast, persistent hypouricaemia stems from genetic defects in UA production or tubular UA reabsorption [2]. RHUC, a rare autosomal recessive disorder, is attributed to a malfunctioning uric acid reabsorption transporter protein in renal proximal tubular cells. Diagnosis typically involves detecting hypouricaemia and an increased uric acid excretion fraction (UA > 10%) [1]. We calculated that the reported cases met this diagnostic criterion. RHUC is categorized into two types based on the mutation: Type 1 RHUC (OMIM: 220150) due to SLC22A12 mutations, and Type 2 RHUC (OMIM: 612076) resulting from SLC2A9 mutations. These mutations affect the uric acid transporter proteins URAT1 and GLUT9, respectively, leading to impaired UA reabsorption in the kidney’s proximal tubules. Previous studies have identified prevalent RHUC1 variants in Japanese and Korean populations as c.774G > A (p.W258X, frequency 2.30-2.37%) and c.269G > A (p.R90H, frequency 0.40%), and in European Roma populations as c.1245_1253del (p. L415_G417del, frequency 1.92%) and c.1400C > T (p.T467M, frequency 5.56%) [3]. A study of Czech patients found that URAT1 variant p.R92C was located within the putative first intracellular loop, variant p.R203C is present in equivalent positions within the membrane. GLUT9 variant p.G72D has been located within the putative first extracellular loop, variants p.I118HfsX27, p.G216R and p.N333S are present within the membrane. RHUC2 has more wide clinical variability than RHUC1: values found in heterozygous patients with GLUT9 mutations are similar to those with compound heterozygous and/or homozygous URAT1 mutations [4]. In compound heterozygous and/or homozygous RHUC2 patients the serum uric acid levels are much lower (near 0) and renal excretion is markedly higher (>100%) while heterozygous RHUC2 family relatives are normouricemics [5]. Heterozygous GLUT9 mutations cause hypouricemia haplo-insufficiency rather than dominant-negative effects. Homozygous GLUT9 mutations cause severe hypouricemia through loss-of-function and precludeuric acid absorption by all apical transporters (including URAT1) by completely blocking uric acid efflux, however, they do not necessarily lead to nephrolithiasis and acute kidney injury. Our reported case was genetically linked to an SLC2A9 mutation inherited from the patient’s mother. Initially identified as a glucose transporter, SLC2A9 also facilitates uric acid transport. Mutations in the SLC2A9 gene are associated with hyperuricemia and gout [6]. A total loss of URAT1 activity in RHUC Type 1 results in a partial UA reabsorption defect, while a complete loss of GLUT9 function in Type 2 leads to a comprehensive reabsorption defect, affecting all apical transporter proteins [7]. Subjects with renal hypouricemia had significantly lower blood hypoxanthine levels and increased urinary hypoxanthine excretion after exercise than healthy subjects. Oxidative stress markers did not differ between healthy and hypouricemic subjects before and after exercise, and no effect of uric acid as a radical scavenger was observed. As hypoxanthine is a precursor for adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production via the salvage pathway, loss of hypoxanthine after exercise in patients with renal hypouricemia may cause ATP loss in the renal tubules and consequent tissue damage [8]. Previous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of allopurinol in the treatment of hypouricaemia. The rationale for use of allopurinol in this hypouricemic patient was to decrease the generation of UA, thus, decreasing the filtered UA load and lowering the risk of precipitation of UA in the tubules [9].

In conclusion, our investigation into RHUC-AKI indicates that a diagnosis of RHUC in AKI patients can be inferred from the patient’s medical history and test results, with strenuous exercise identified as a key predisposing factor. However, defining the precise amount of exercise for these patients remains a challenge. Determining what constitutes strenuous exercise and how to prevent it is crucial for improving patient quality of life. Additionally, clinical evaluation is often prioritized in diagnosing this disease, playing a critical role alongside renal biopsy and genetic testing for rare diseases. In our study, genetic testing was conducted only on the patient and his parents. The need for extended genetic testing among the patient’s younger brother and other close relatives warrants further investigation. To substantiate our conclusions about this rare genetic condition, more clinical cases and research are essential.
