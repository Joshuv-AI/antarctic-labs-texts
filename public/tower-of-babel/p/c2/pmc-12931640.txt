Salutogenic Impact of Yoga on Emotional Intelligence and Perceived Stress of Medical College Students: An Interventional Study
Authors: Monica Edwin, Arun Sugumaran, Dayanidy Ganesan, Vishrutha Muralidharan, G. Kiruba Sankar, Ananda Balayogi Bhavanani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Emotional intelligence (EI) is defined as the ability of a person to understand and respond to one’s own and others’ emotions and use this ability to guide one’s thoughts and actions. Stress is defined as a condition or feeling experienced when a person perceives that demands exceed the personal and social resources the individual is able to mobilize.Aim:The aim of the study was to assess the level of EI and perceived stress before and after yoga training among medical students in Puducherry.Materials and Methods:A nonrandomized interventional trial was conducted at the School of Yoga Therapy, Sri Balaji Vidyapeeth, Puducherry, involving 30 healthcare students (22 female and 8 male), with a mean age of 21.63 ± 1.95. Approved by the institutional ethics committee, the 6-week yoga program was held at Patanjali Yogashala on weekdays. EI and perceived stress were measured pre- and post-intervention using validated questionnaires.Results:The mean age of participants was 21.63 ± 1.95 years, with 73.3% females and 26.7% males. Initially, perceived stress and EI had a weak negative correlation (r = −0.24), which shifted to a weak positive correlation (r = 0.22) postyoga intervention. A significant reduction in perceived stress was noticed before and after yoga intervention. In addition, improvements in “trustworthiness” and “achievement” were significant with P = 0.049 and 0.032, respectively.Conclusion:This interventional trial with yoga therapy has shown a reduction in perceived stress levels and an increase in certain components of EI among medical college students in Puducherry. This study highlights the supremacy of Yoga therapy in achieving mental and psychosocial well-being.

Introduction

In this fast-paced era of medical education, where students regularly have to deal with personal challenges, clinical demands, and academic expectations, the advancement of emotional intelligence (EI) and practical stress management approaches is vital. The capacity to recognize, respond to, and use one’s own and others’ emotions as a way to inform one’s decisions and actions is known as EI.[1] Doctors with high EQs exhibit effective interpersonal and communication skills and are less likely to get complaints from patients and more likely to significantly contribute to lowering medical errors.[2] Patients recover faster when doctors show empathy and compassion.[3] It is crucial to maintain open lines of communication with the patients’ loved ones while they are receiving treatment.[4] Stress is defined as a state of mental or emotional strain or tension resulting from adverse or very demanding circumstances.[5] In recent years, there have been a number of incidents of violence against medical personnel in India and other low- and middle-income nations. Many of these violent incidents are caused by the fragility of the patients and their families, the unpredictability of the therapies, overcrowded hospitals, and overworked medical staff.[6] Worldwide, reports have indicated that medical education is one of the most demanding academic programs, which has a detrimental impact on the students’ physical and mental well-being.[7] As medical students, they must be aware of the adjunct therapy of yoga for the betterment of their health and that of their patients. In this context, the study aimed to assess the level of EI and perceived stress before and after yoga training among medical students in Puducherry.

Materials and Methods

This nonrandomized interventional trial was conducted at the School of Yoga Therapy (SYT), Institute of Salutogenesis and Complementary Medicine of Sri Balaji Vidyapeeth Deemed to be University, located in Puducherry, India. Thirty students (22 female and 8 male) with a mean age of 21.63 ± 1.95, enrolled in healthcare programs, participated in the study. The study was initiated with the approval of the institutional ethics committee (MGMCRI/IRC/03/2020/88/IHEC/221). Yoga training sessions were conducted exclusively for medical students at the Patanjali Yogashala, SYT, on weekdays between 3 and 4 PM. The sessions were in a tranquil environment with comfortable temperatures and subdued lighting. Participants were advised to consume lunch at least 2 h before the session and ensure empty bowels and bladder before attendance.

EI and perceived stress were assessed using validated questionnaires before and after a 6-week yoga training program. The EI questionnaire consisted of two components: personal and social competence. The yoga sessions followed a structured format including warm-ups (jathis and surya namaskar), breath-body movement coordination practices (kriyas), static stretching postures (asana), breathing techniques (pranayama), relaxation, and chanting. The yoga training methods and schedules were based on the principles outlined in the book “Yoga Chikitsa: Application of Yoga as Therapy.”[8] The schedule of the yoga training and duration are expounded in Table 1.

The sample size in our study was estimated to be 36 subjects, based on the previous study conducted by El-Masry et al. where the prevalence of perceived stress among medical students was found to be 71.7%.[9] The study subjects were included in this intervention program through a convenience sampling method during the screening period of 15 days. We were not able to achieve the required sample size because of the COVID-19 pandemic situation, and hence we proceeded with a sample of 30 subjects. The study spanned 2 months from September 2020 to October 2020 with strict COVID-19 precautionary protocol guidelines.

Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test with JASP software, version [0.17.1] (JASP Team, University of Amsterdam, Amsterdam, North Holland, The Netherlands). Statistical analysis was performed using Student’s paired t-test, with significance set at P < 0.05 for pre-post comparisons. Results

This study was an interventional study conducted for 2 months in a private medical college of Puducherry to assess the level of EI and perceived stress before and after yoga intervention in private medical college students. A total of 30 medical college students were included in the study, and the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants are depicted [Table 3]. The mean age group of the participants was 21.63 ± 1.95. The majority of the participants, i.e. 73.3% were females and 26.7% were males. Before intervention, the Pearson correlation coefficient (r = −0.24) suggests a weak negative correlation between perceived stress and EI [Figure 1]. After intervention, the Pearson correlation coefficient (r = 0.22) which indicates that because of the yoga sessions intervention, the EI increased even when the stress level was higher [Figure 2]. The comparison of various components of EI and perceived stress before and after intervention using a paired t-test. The total perceived stress scale score was found to be statistically significant with P = 0.004. Similarly, the mean difference for trustworthy and achievement was found to be significant with P = 0.049 and 0.032, respectively [Table 2].

Discussion

The current study was done to assess the level of EI and perceived stress levels before and after yoga training, which included a series of yoga asanas, pranayama (breathing practices), and yogic relaxation for 2 months among medical college students in Puducherry district. There are various studies documented that yoga has decreased many parameters related to stress, such as cortisol levels, heart rate, and systolic blood pressure.[10] Healthcare students experience high levels of stress in day-to-day activities; thus, EI can moderate stress levels and help to improve the well-being of an individual. A multidisciplinary cross-sectional study done in healthcare students in Australia showed that EI had a protective effect on stress levels, and its need to be increased through educational interventions was highlighted.[11] In our study, the mean age group of 30 students for whom the current study was conducted was determined to be 21.63 ± 1.95, with 73.3% of the students being female [Table 3]. EI has the potential to be a predictor of both high performance and higher competence in patient care. The four main domains of EI – self-awareness, self-regulation or Self-management, self-motivation, empathy, and social awareness, which are essential elements of good leadership.[12] The “emic” perspective of EI enables an individual to comprehend and manage their own emotions, thereby facilitating more effective interpersonal interactions.[13] The “etic” perspective of EI facilitates an understanding of emotions, enabling individuals to empathize with and respond to the emotional states of others. Both of these viewpoints are crucial for fostering meaningful human interactions. The significance of EI for physicians lies in its capacity to facilitate effective collaboration with nurses, hospital managers, and other allied health professionals within a team-oriented environment.[14] The “emic” perspective of EI enables an individual to comprehend and manage their own emotions, thereby facilitating more effective interpersonal interactions.

In our study, EI was recorded and found that certain components such as trustworthiness, achievement the pretest values of 13.70 ± 2.56, 13.43 ± 2.67 increased to 14.47 ± 3.45, 14.70 ± 3.77 after intervention and was found to be statistically significant with P = 0.049 and 0.032 respectively [Table 2].

The Pearson correlation coefficient (r = −0.24) suggests a weak negative correlation between perceived stress and EI [Figure 1]. Decrease in Perceived Stress Score appears to have had a notable effect on EI levels among the participants. This reduction in perceived stress suggests that the intervention likely targeted stress management techniques or coping mechanisms that were effective in helping individuals better handle stressful situations. This decrease is significant as high levels of perceived stress can have detrimental effects on both mental and physical well-being.

Increase in Total EI score also seems to have positively influenced the participant’s EI. An increase in total EI scores indicates that individuals may have developed better self-awareness, self-regulation, social skills, empathy, and overall emotional well-being. Hence, it was evident that the levels of EI among medical students had increased with the intervention of yoga training.

This is similar to a previous study in Bangalore among mid-life managers which showed 72% increase in EI following yoga training.[15] A study done on Pakistani medical students showed a higher level of stress among female students and the major sources for stress were academic and psychosocial causes, i.e.: “High parental expectations,” “frequency of examinations,” “vastness of academic curriculum,” “sleeping difficulties,” “worrying about the future,” “loneliness,” “becoming a doctor,” “performance in periodic examinations” were some of the most common ones.[16] Worldwide, 10%–20% of children and adolescents experience mental disorders. During medical school, students utilize various coping strategies to manage stress. However, chronic, intense stress can arouse feelings of fear, uselessness, anger, incompetence, and guilt. If it is not managed correctly, stress can lead to high levels of depression, substance abuse, relationship problems, anxiety, and suicide.[17] In India, the Medical Council of India’s Vision 2015 document, the apex body in charge of curriculum development for medical education in the country, focused on the ATCOM module, to impart education on EI, empathy, communication skills, and ethics.[18] A study done to assess the perceived stress among medical students in Nagpur revealed that female students were more stressed (17.19%) than male students (14.93%). It was also seen that academic groups, especially those belonging to medical specialties, were going through higher levels of stress especially posed by academic-related problems, and coping strategies are essential for the mental well-being of these students.[19] A study done on Iranian students showed that pranayama-a breathing exercise helps to lessen the symptoms of anxiety, depression, attention deficit disorder, and also helps in improved test performance; thus, this technique could be followed by students before examinations to help them combat stress.[20] Another study done in JIPMER, Puducherry to assess the perceived stress levels among healthcare students with pranayama showed a reduction in stress levels postintervention. Pranayama was beneficial for cognitive functions, and fast pranayama seemed to help in auditory working memory, central neural processing, and sensory-motor performance, and slow pranayama benefited cardiovascular function.[21]

Practice of Yoga stimulates a dormant parasympathetic nervous system and enhances the inhibitory function of a less active gamma aminobutyric acid (GABA) system within brain pathways and structures crucial for managing threat perception, regulating emotions, and responding to stress. Additionally, yoga promotes improved sleep, heightened energy levels, enhanced muscle tone, alleviation of muscle pain and stiffness, boosted circulation, and overall better holistic health.[22] These studies made it evident that yoga plays an important role in psychosocial well-being. Scientific data support the notion that yoga improves physical and mental health by down-regulating the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and the sympathetic nervous system.[23] Deep relaxation and mental calming reduce blood pressure, pulse rate, and respiration rate through lowering cortisol levels, which normally rise in response to stress.[24] Yoga, also, reduces the level of acetylcholine, which reduces sympathetic nervous system activity and increases vagal activity.[25] As a result, it reduces psychological conflicts, suppressions, and hypersensitivities, all of which are known to cause psychosomatic illnesses. Yoga, through incremental muscular relaxation techniques, disturbs the “fight and flight” stress response, which is characterized by a stressed-out mind and tight muscles.[26]

Study limitations

Our study has certain limitations, including the need for longer follow-up and a larger sample size to ensure yoga has a lasting positive effect. We have not included the control group in our intervention trial due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Randomized controlled trials and cohorts are also necessary to strengthen the case for yoga as an alternative treatment for stress and anxiety.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that yoga training significantly improved EI and reduced perceived stress levels among the medical students. The 2-month yoga program, incorporating asanas, pranayama, and relaxation techniques, effectively enhanced key components of EI such as trustworthiness and achievement.

Ethics approval

All subjects gave their informed consent for inclusion before they participated in the study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Institutional Human Ethics Committee (MGMCRI/IRC/03/2020/88/IHEC/221).
