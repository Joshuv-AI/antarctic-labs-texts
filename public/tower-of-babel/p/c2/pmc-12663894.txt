Preclinical Assessment of Mitochondrial-Targeted ND4 Gene Therapy for Leber Hereditary Optic Neuropathy
Authors: Yingjia Dong, Zixuan Hao, Yuan Liu, Aiqin Chang, Vittorio Porciatti, Byron L. Lam, Xuemin Jin, Hong Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to establish preclinical safety data to advance mitochondrial targeted AAV-delivered human ND4 (MTSAAV/hND4) for treatment of Leber Hereditary Optic Neuropathy (LHON).MethodsMTSAAV/hND4 was bilaterally injected into mouse eyes to evaluate potential adverse effects on visual function using serial pattern electroretinograms (PERGs) and spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT). In rats, MTSAAV/hND4 was administered unilaterally to assess toxicity, biodistribution, and neutralizing antibody levels. Control groups include PBS-injected and untreated naïve mice and rats.ResultsNo significant adverse effects on visual function were observed in mice treated with MTSAAV/hND4. Retinal thickness remained largely stable, with only a transient, benign increase in RNFL-IPL thickness observed in the low-dose group at 1 month. Postmortem analysis showed no significant differences in RGC density among MTSAAV/hND4-injected mice, PBS-injected controls, and naïve controls (P = 0.214), and axons morphology and distribution were preserved. In rats, viral DNA was primarily detected in the injected eyes with copy number of 4.63 × 10⁴ at day 7 and 4.94 × 103 at day 90 per 100 ng of genomic DNA. Viral DNA levels in non-ocular tissues were low (<500 copies). Histopathological analysis showed no vector-related toxicity. At 6 months post-injection, overall hematological parameters remained within normal limits. All MTSAAV/hND4-injected rats seroconverted by day 90, with neutralizing antibody titers reaching 1:1280.ConclusionsIntravitreal delivery of MTSAAV/hND4 in rodents resulted in favorable biodistribution, with vector DNA largely confined to the injected eye and minimal systemic exposure. No vector-related adverse effects were observed based on functional, anatomic, and histopathological assessments, supporting the ocular safety of this gene therapy strategy. These findings provide preclinical evidence supporting the advancement of MTSAAV-mediated ND4 gene therapy toward clinical trials for LHON.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a key contributor to numerous diseases and aging-related processes.1 A significant proportion of mitochondrial disorders arise from point mutations or deletions in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA).2,3 Leber Hereditary Optic Neuropathy (LHON), the most prevalent inherited primary mitochondrial disease, is characterized by abrupt, profound, and permanent vision loss, primarily occurring in adolescents and young adults. Over 90% of LHON cases are linked to 3 specific mtDNA point mutations: m.11778G>A, m.3460G>A, and m.14484T>C, which affect the ND4, ND1, and ND6 subunits, respectively. These subunits are essential components of mitochondrial complex I in the respiratory chain.4–6

As with other mitochondrial disorders, pharmacological interventions for LHON have been limited in their success, with no US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved treatments to date. Alternatively, gene therapy is recognized currently as a candidate approach for treating LHON. One approach that has advanced to human clinical trials, allotopic expression, is directed against the m.11778G>A mutation.7 The allotopic expression strategy entails re-coding a mitochondrial gene for expression in the nucleus, followed by the transport of the resulting protein into the mitochondria, facilitated by an N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS) fused to the protein. Ongoing clinical trials with this approach are in China (NCT01267422),8–11 France (NCT02064569, NCT02652767, NCT02652780, and NCT034061104),12–15 and the United States (NCT02161380).16 Although these clinical trials have shown some improvement in vision for patients with LHON, the overall benefit of these therapeutic interventions is limited compared to the natural history of the disease.13 In addition, the hydrophobic character of the ND4 protein may present an obstacle for the efficient transportation of ND4 across the mitochondrial membrane, which may decrease sustained and long-term expression of the ND4 gene as well. Consequently, there is a pressing requirement for novel and efficient treatment strategies in order to obtain substantial visual recovery in LHON-affected individuals.

Certain viruses possess the capability to cross the mitochondrial double membrane and deliver DNA directly into the mitochondrial matrix.17 Studies have shown that fusing an MTS to the capsid of adeno-associated virus (AAV) produces an MTSAAV that preferentially localizes to the mitochondria rather than the nucleus.18 Using this approach, we introduced the mutant human ND4 gene (m.11778G>A) into mouse zygotes, generating a bona fide LHON mitomouse model harboring the pathogenic mutation.19 The expressed mutant hND4 protein integrated into the host's mitochondrial respiratory complex I, resulting in impaired respiratory chain activity and elevated oxidative stress levels. This model faithfully recapitulates key features of human LHON, including progressive retinal ganglion cell (RGC) dysfunction, measured via pattern electroretinogram (PERG), as well as degeneration of RGCs and the optic nerve. In our recent work, we compared the therapeutic effects of MTSAAV/hND4 and allotopically expressed hND4 in an LHON mouse model. Although both strategies significantly restored functional and structural integrity of RGCs, MTSAAV/hND4 achieved a more pronounced rescued effect than allotopic therapy.20

To advance MTSAAV-mediated ND4 gene delivery toward clinical application, we conducted a series of preclinical studies to characterize AAV genome distribution and transgene expression, key parameters for evaluating gene therapy safety. This paper presents a detailed biodistribution profile of MTSAAV/hND4 following intravitreal injection in normal rats, along with complete ophthalmic assessments and histopathological examinations to evaluate biocompatibility across different vector doses. Neutralizing antibody (NAb) titers were also measured to determine potential host immune response and their influence on vector biodistribution. Taken together, these studies address key aspects of the preclinical safety evaluation necessary for regulatory approval and eventual clinical translation of this therapeutic platform.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Viruses

All animal experiments in this study were conducted in compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

The sc-HSP-hND4 was constructed as previously described.21,22 In brief, the human ND4 gene was cloned into self-complementary AAV (scAAV) backbones under the control of the mitochondrial heavy strand promoter (HSP). The resultant plasmid was purified using Qiagen endotoxin free maxiprep and then packaged with the VP2COX8 plus VP1, VP3(Y444, 500, and 730F) and helper plasmid PXX6 into recombinant virus MTSAAV/hND4.

The doses used here (mice = 1 × 10⁸ – 1 × 10⁹ vg/eye; rats = 2 × 10⁹ – 2 × 10¹⁰ vg/eye) are consistent with those applied in current intravitreal AAV gene therapy trials for retinal diseases (approximately 108–1011 vg/eye)23 when scaled by vitreous volume (5 µL in mice,24,25 50 µL in rats,24,26 and 4 mL in humans).27 Based on vitreous volume, these doses correspond to approximately 1011 to 1012 vg/eye in humans. Considering the thicker human inner limiting membrane (ILM), which can reduce AAV-mediated RGC transduction by approximately 10-fold,28 the adjusted human-equivalent exposure would be about 1010 to 1011 vg/eye, aligning with the clinically relevant range.

Tissue Collection

Tissues, including the eyes, optic nerves, cerebrum, submandibular lymph node, lungs, heart, liver, spleen, kidneys, pancreas, jejunum, skeletal muscle, and gonads were collected at study endpoints for toxicity and biodistribution analyses. Blood was collected at the baseline via tail vein puncture and at endpoints through cardiac puncture for hematology and neutralizing antibody assessment.

Quantitative PCR Biodistribution (Rats)

Genomic DNA was extracted from tissue samples using the Qiagen DNA Blood & Tissue Kit (Cat. no. 69554; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Quantification of AAV vector genomes was performed using SYBR Green-based quantitative PCR (qPCR) with bGH polyA-specific primers: forward 5′-AGCTCGCTGATCAGCCTCGACTGTG-3′ and reverse 5′-AAGGACAGTGGGAGTGGCACCTTCC-3′. qPCR was conducted using the iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. As a positive control, 10 pg of standard DNA was spiked into 1 of 4 replicate reactions to validate assay sensitivity, as previously described.29

Pattern Electroretinogram (Mice)

RGC function was assessed by PERG, recorded simultaneously from both eyes with a commercially available instrument (Jorvec Corp., Miami, FL, USA), as previously described in detail.30 PERG recordings were obtained from mice (n = 46) at baseline (prior to injection; time point 0), and at 1, 3, and 6 months post-injection (time points 1, 3, and 6, respectively). Briefly, mice were weighed and anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). Animals were positioned to allow unobstructed vision and maintained at a constant body temperature of 37.0°C using a feedback-controlled heating pad regulated by a rectal probe. Pupils remained undilated and small (<1 mm), ensuring a large depth of focus. PERG signals were recorded from a subcutaneous stainless-steel needle (Grass, West Warwick, RI, USA) inserted into the snout. Reference and ground electrodes were placed medially on the back of the head and at the root of the tail, respectively. Visual stimuli were presented independently at each eye from a distance of 10 cm and consisted of contrast-reversing gratings (0.05 cycles/degree, 98% contrast) displayed on two light-emitting diode (LED) tablets (15 × 15 cm field, mean luminance = 800 cd/m2), flickering at slightly different temporal frequencies (OD = 1.016 hertz [Hz]; and OS = 1.008 Hz). Retinal signals were amplified (10,000-fold) and band-pass filtered (1–30 Hz). Independent PERG signals from each eye were retrieved using one-channel continuous acquisition and phase-locking average over 372 epochs per eye. Three consecutive PERG recordings were obtained, superimposed to verify consistency, and then averaged. PERG amplitude was quantified as the peak-to-trough distance, typically from the P1 peak to the N2 trough, using automated waveform detection software (SigmaPlot; Systat Software, Inc., San Jose, CA, USA).

Retinal images were acquired using the Heidelberg spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) as described before.31 Mice were anesthetized and pupils were dilated using 1% tropicamide applied topically to both eyes. To keep the cornea clear during imaging, a drop of Systane Ultra (Alcon Laboratories, Inc., Fort Worth, TX, USA) was placed on the eye. Rectangular volume scans centered on the optic nerve head (ONH) were collected from both eyes. All OCT image datasets were reviewed to ensure consistent scan quality. Eyes with media opacities (corneal or lenticular) that attenuated signals, or obscured retinal layers were excluded from analysis. Among 92 eyes (46 mice, bilateral) analyzed, 3 (3.3%) were excluded: one low-dose eye (lens opacity, month 1), one high-dose eye (corneal opacity, month 3), and one naive eye (congenital lens opacity, baseline); no exclusions occurred in the PBS group. Exclusion rates did not differ significantly among the four groups (P = 0.80, Fisher's exact test). Excluded eyes were omitted from both OCT and PERG analyses to maintain data consistency. Retinal layer thicknesses were quantified using the IOWA Reference Algorithms OCT Explore software (version 3.8.0).32

Histology and Ultrastructure (Mice)

At 6 months post-injection, retinas were collected from mice that received low-dose or high-dose viral injection, PBS injection, or no injection (age-matched naïve control). The RGC quantification was performed on samples (n = 5–6 per group). Immunostaining was conducted using a guinea pig polyclonal anti-RBPMS antibody (1:200; Lot NB9160; PhosphoSolutions, Aurora, CO, USA) and a donkey anti-guinea pig IgG secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor 547 (1:300; 706-545-148; Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA). Confocal imaging was performed using a Leica TCS SP5 microscope (Leica Microsystems Inc., Buffalo Grove, IL, USA). Fifteen fields per retina, sampled from different retinal regions, were imaged with a 63 × objective and analyzed by observers blinded to the experimental conditions.

Optic nerves were transected immediately posterior to the globe, and ultrathin sections were prepared from a region 1 mm distal to the transection site (n = 4 per group). For axonal quantification, 8 transmission electron micrographs were acquired at 1500 × magnification per specimen using a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEOL JEM-1400, Tokyo, Japan) by an observer blinded to group allocation. Axon number and diameter were manually quantified by a second masked observer as described previously.33,34 Axons were identified based on the presence of a distinct axoplasmic cytoskeleton and/or a surrounding electron-dense myelin sheath.

Hematology (Rats)

Whole-blood specimens were transferred to the Comparative Pathology Laboratory at the University of Miami. A fully automated hematology analyzer was used to measure key hematological parameters, including white blood cells (WBCs), red blood cells (RBCs), hemoglobin (HGB), packed cell volume (PCV), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), segmented neutrophils (NEUs), lymphocytes (LYMs), monocytes (MONOs), eosinophils (EOSs), basophils (BASs), and platelets (PLTs).

Ophthalmic Examinations (Rats)

The rats were positioned onto a purpose-built platform that was affixed to a slit lamp. To ensure sustained sedation throughout the procedure, a nose cone was used to administer isoflurane gas to the rats. Pupils were dilated with a topical application of 1% tropicamide to allow better visualization of the fundus. To maintain corneal hydration and clarity, during imaging, a drop of Systane Ultra (Alcon Laboratories, Inc., Fort Worth, TX, USA) was placed on the eye. The slit lamp was set up with the observation and illumination arms properly aligned, and initial magnification was kept low to aid in orientation. The examination began with an evaluation of the anterior segment. The cornea was inspected for tear film quality and any abnormalities, such as opacity, mineralization, inflammation, or ulceration. The anterior chamber was checked for signs of hyphema or hypopyon. The iris was examined for structural defects, including colobomas, synechiae, or persistent pupillary membranes. The lens was assessed for the presence of cataracts or other opacities. Ophthalmic examinations were performed at baseline (BL), day 30 (D30), and day 180 (D180).

Histopathology (Rats)

Histopathological analysis was conducted by Comparative Pathology Laboratory at the University of Miami. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed by the University of Miami Research Histology Laboratory. All histological evaluations were conducted in a blinded manner with respect to treatment groups.

Neutralizing Antibody Assay (Rats)

The experiment was performed as previously described.35,36 Briefly, serum samples collected from the injected rats were heat-inactivated at 56°C for 30 minutes. The heat-inactivated serum was then serially diluted (1:80 to 1:327,680) and mixed with AAV2-mCherry, which was used to infect RGC5 at a MOI = 10,000 vg/cell in a 24-well plate. RGC5 cells infected with AAV2-mCherry without rat serum served as the control. After 4 hours of incubation at 37°C, with 5% CO2, an equal volume of complete media was added to each well. Following a 72-hour incubation, cells were collected for flow cytometry (BD FACS Aria III) to quantify AAV infection efficiency based on mCherry expression. The neutralizing titter was defined as the highest serum dilution that resulted in a >50% reduction in mCherry expression, as determined by the percentage of mCherry-positive cells within the parent gate.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10 software. All statistical parameters, including sample size (n), measures (means ± SEMs), and significance levels, are presented in the figures and corresponding legends. Statistically significance was determined using an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test for pairwise comparisons, 1-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for comparisons involving 2 variables, and 2-way ANOVA for analyses involving 3 variables. The P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data collection and analysis were performed in a blinded manner with respect to experimental conditions, and no data points were excluded from the analyses.

Results

Localized Ocular Retention and Limited Systemic Dissemination of AAV Vector After Intravitreal Injection

The qPCR at day 7 post-injection demonstrated a clear compartmentalization of vector DNA. The injected eye showed the highest concentration of viral genomes per 100 ng DNA (on average, 4.6 × 10⁴ ± 4.6 × 10⁴ copies), although with marked interindividual variability. Contrastingly, the contralateral non-injected eye showed only scarce vector presence (302 ± 262 copies), ruling out widespread transocular spread. Intermediate levels were found in the optic nerve (2.6 × 10³ ± 2.7 × 10³ copies), indicating that the vector partially travels along the visual pathway. Viral DNA loads in systemic tissues, including the central nervous system (CNS), cervical lymph nodes, lungs, heart, liver, spleen, kidneys, pancreas, intestines, muscle, and gonads in the rats, were consistently low in the treated rats (61–413 copies), indicating minimal systemic spread (Fig. 1A).

Viral DNA copies in ocular/systemic tissues (heatmap). (A) Day 7: injected eyes (n = 16) contained 4.6 × 104 ± 4.4 × 10⁴ vector copies per 100 ng genome DNA. In contrast, 12 of 16 contralateral eyes and 12 of 16 optic nerve samples showed ≤500 copies. (B) Day 90: injected eyes (n = 16) showed 4.9 × 103 ± 1.5 × 104 copies per 100 ng genome DNA, whereas all contralateral eyes (n = 16) and other systemic tissues (n = 16) had ≤500 copies. (C) PBS-injected control group.

At day 90, the concentration of viral DNA in the injected eye had substantially decreased to 4.9 × 10³ ± 1.5 × 10⁴ copies. However, for all the persisting heterogeneity, the general decrease indicates a gradual vector elimination or genomic attenuation over time. Viral DNA levels were also low in the non-injected eye and systemic organs at or below 100 copies, demonstrating minimal vector dissemination (Fig. 1B). This consistent low signal in numerous tissues implies an attractive clearance and biodistribution profile.

For PBS controls, all of the tested tissues showed very low baseline signals (viral DNA copy numbers of approximately 25 to 137 copies, likely background or assay noise; Fig. 1C). These findings emphasized that biodistribution in treated groups is vector mediated and is not because of environmental or procedural contamination. Together, these results show that upon ocular delivery the vector is predominantly retained in the injected eye and only marginally disseminated via the optic nerve to the rest of the body. With increasing time, there is a dramatic decline in vector presence at the treatment site whereas there are no accumulating signals of systemic exposure, with all levels measured well below known safety thresholds (<500 copies per 100 ng genome DNA). The steady-state low levels of viral DNA in PBS controls provide additional evidence for the specificity of the assay and for the safety of the delivery system.

Functional Assessment of Retinal Safety via PERG

The PERG amplitude was assessed at baseline and at 1, 3, and 6 months post-injection across 4 experimental groups: naïve, PBS, low-dose AAV (10⁸ vg/eye; E8), and high-dose AAV (10⁹ vg/eye; E9). At baseline, PERG amplitudes did not differ significantly between groups (naïve = 13.72 ± 4.96 µV; PBS = 13.58 ± 3.37 µV; E8 = 13.58 ± 2.49 µV; and E9 = 13.82 ± 4.27 µV; n = 10–13, P > 0.05), indicating comparable retinal function prior to treatment (Fig. 2C).

Longitudinal pattern electroretinogram (PERG) amplitude changes in naïve, PBS-controlled, and AAV-treated mice over 6 months. The effect of age and different groups on PERG amplitude (A) and latency (B). PERG amplitudes were measured at baseline (0 months) (C, D, E), 1 month (F, G, H), 3 months (I, J, K), and 6 months (L, M, N) post-intravitreal injection to evaluate RGC function in DBA1/J. The naïve (UINJ), PBS control, low-dose AAV (1 × 10⁸ vg/eye; E8), and high-dose AAV (1 × 10⁹ vg/eye; E9) groups showed a steady decline in amplitude over time with no significant differences between AAV-treated and control groups at any time point (1-way ANOVA, P > 0.05). Dots in panels C, D, F, G, I, and J represent the mean amplitude or latency values of OD and OS for each mouse.

PERG amplitudes progressively declined across all groups, with reductions of approximately 20% to 40% from baseline to 1, 3, and 6 months (Fig. 2A). Two-way ANOVA revealed a highly significant effect of age on PERG amplitude (P < 0.001), but no significant effect of treatment group (P = 0.561) or interaction between age and treatment group (P = 0.990). Similarly, PERG latency increased over time in all groups, however, only the effect of age was significant (P < 0.001; Fig. 2B). No significant effect of treatment group (P = 0.421) or interaction between age and treatment group (P = 0.800) was observed. Specifically, no significant differences in PERG amplitude or latency were observed among the MTSAAV/hND4-treated groups (E8 and E9), PBS controls, or naïve controls at any time point (Figs. 2C–N, P > 0.05).

The observed age-related decline in PERG amplitude was consistent with previous reports. Chou et al. reported a 35% PERG amplitude reduction between 6 and 12 months of age in DBA/1J mice, attributed to age-related RGC mitochondrial complex I dysfunction.37 Similarly, Liu et al. reported approximately 15% RNFL thinning in 12-month-old DBA/1J mice, consistent with the functional decline observed in our study.31 The absence of significant differences in the rate of decline between MTSAAV-treated and control groups (P > 0.05) indicates that the effect is age-related rather than treatment-induced.

Together, these findings suggest that intravitreal delivery of the MTSAAV/hND4 does not exacerbate the natural, age-related decline in retinal function. Overall, the PERG data support the functional safety of the tested article, MTSAAV/hND4.

Structural Evaluation of Retinal Integrity via OCT

To assess potential RGC loss over time, SD-OCT was used to measure the thickness from the retinal nerve fiber layer to the inner plexiform layer (RNFL-IPL), as well as the total retinal thickness, at the same time points as the PERG assessments. One-way ANOVA revealed no significant differences in RNFL-IPL or total retinal thickness among all groups at baseline and at 1, 3, and 6 months post-injection (P > 0.05; Figs. 3A–I), except for a transient increase in RNFL-IPL thickness in the E8 group compared to the PBS-injected group at 1 month (adjusted P < 0.001; see Fig. 3C). By month 3, this transient increase had resolved, and RNFL-IPL thickness in the E8 group (42.3 ± 3.1 µm) was comparable to that in the PBS group (41.8 ± 2.9 µm, P = 0.68). This temporary thickening likely reflects mild, non-inflammatory tissue stress, such as transient RNFL hypertrophy, without accompanying PERG dysfunction (P > 0.05) or RGC loss (P = 0.214), supporting its benign and self-limited nature. Overall, these findings indicate that intravitreal administration of MTSAAV/hND4 does not induce structural retinal degradation.

No Detectable Retinal Ganglion Cell Loss After MTSAAV/hND4 Administration

To further assess potential RGC loss induced by MTSAAV/hND4, we performed RGC counting at 6 months post-injection using retinal whole mount staining with RBPMS, a pan-RGC marker. No significant differences in RGC density were observed between the MTSAAV/hND4-treated groups (E8 and E9) and control groups (naïve and PBS control = naïve = 3355 ± 149 cells/mm2; PBS = 3378 ± 102 cells/mm2; E8 = 3137 ± 162 cells/mm2; and E9: 3060 ± 82 cells/mm2; n = 5–6, P = 0.214; Figs. 4A–D, 4I). Ultrastructural analysis revealed no obvious differences in axon morphology within the optic nerve across the groups (Figs. 4E–H). Axon quantification further confirmed no difference in axon number (P = 0.766; Fig. 4J) or diameter distribution (P = 0.395; Fig. 4K) among all four groups. Together, these results suggest that neither low-dose nor high-dose intraocular MTSAAV/hND4 administration causes detectable optic atrophy in mice.

No Anterior Segment Toxicity Following MTSAAV/hND4 Injection

There were no differences in the anterior segment among study groups (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. S1). As summarized in Table 1, the anterior chamber profiles of eyes receiving MTSAAV/hND4 injections were similar to those of PBS-injected control eyes for all parameters considered. Aqueous flare and iris hyperemia were not observed in both groups (0/18 vs. 0/18, P > 0.999), presenting no evidence of intraocular inflammation. Moreover, cataract incidence did not follow a dose-dependent trend. No cataracts were observed in eyes treated with the highest AAV dose (2.00 × 10¹⁰ vg/eye), similar to PBS controls (0/18 vs. 0/18, P > 0.999). One cataract was detected in one un-injected eye of the low-dose (2.00 × 10⁹ vg/eye) group (1/18, P = 0.492), presumably a spontaneous, age-related finding. These results suggest that intravitreal MTSAAV/hND4 administration is not associated with anterior segment toxicity, and thereby, the ocular safety of the procedure is established.

Summary of Ophthalmic Observations

Hematological Parameters Remain Within Normal Ranges Following MTSAAV/hND4 Administration

Blood tests were performed at the baseline, day 30, and day 180 in both PBS-treated and AAV-treated animals (dosed at 2 × 10⁹ and 2 × 10¹⁰ vg/eye). No signs of systemic toxicity were detected (Tables 23–4). Over time, WBC counts dropped slightly in the PBS group—a trend often seen with aging—but stayed steady in the AAV groups. The RBC and hemoglobin levels remained stable across all groups, suggesting normal RBC production. The high-dose AAV group showed a small, nonsignificant decrease in platelet counts, although they stayed within normal limits. All groups had minor drops in lymphocyte and eosinophil counts over time, likely due to aging rather than treatment effects. Importantly, all blood values stayed within normal ranges, supporting the safety of AAV at both doses over the 6-month study.

Antibody Neutralization Following MTSAAV/hND4 Administration

The NAbs were assessed in rats that received unilateral intravitreal injections of MTSAAV/hND4 at a dose of 2 × 10¹⁰ vg/eye. Rats injected with PBS served as controls. No neutralizing activity was detected in any animal prior to injection, confirming the absence of pre-existing antibodies (Fig. 5A). By day 7 post-injection, a subset of MTSAAV/hND4-treated rats developed detectable NAb titers (Fig. 5B). One animal exhibited high neutralizing activity with a serum titer of 1:1280, whereas another showed a moderate response at 1:80. The remaining animals displayed titers below the threshold associated with effective neutralization, indicating a low-level and variable early humoral response.

By day 90, however, all animals in the MTSAAV/hND4-treated group exhibited robust neutralizing antibody activity (Fig. 5C). Serum from each rat was capable of inhibiting AAV function at a dilution of 1:1280, as evidenced by a >50% reduction in AAV2-mCherry infection. This confirmed the development of a consistent and measurable immune response to the vector over time.

In contrast, no neutralizing activity was detected in the PBS-injected control group at any time point, including days 7 and 90 (Fig. 5D), supporting the specificity of the assay and excluding background or nonspecific immune activation.

Comprehensive Histopathologic Assessment Reveals no MTSAAV/hND4-Related Toxicity Across Organ Systems

We examined 236 tissue sections from 59 Sprague-Dawley rats and found no signs of AAV-related toxicity in any organ system (Table 5). Mild cardiomyocyte degeneration (44%–64%) and mild myocarditis (≤11%) were observed at similar frequencies in MTSAAV- and PBS-treated groups (Supplementary Fig. S2). Lung inflammation affected 38% to 75% of animals regardless of treatment (Supplementary Fig. S3). Liver findings, including mild fat changes (≤33%) and rare hepatitis (one case per group), were likewise unrelated to treatment (Supplementary Fig. S4). Kidney and spleen changes showed no treatment- or dose-dependent pattern (Supplementary Figs. S5, S6). The pancreas was largely unremarkable across groups (Supplementary Fig. S7); one case each of ductal hyperplasia and pancreatitis occurred in the MTSAAV-treated group, whereas three cases of pancreatitis and one pancreatic adenocarcinoma were identified in the PBS-treated control group (Fig. 6A). Brain abnormalities, such as focal meningitis with vascular congestion (Fig. 6B), were seen only in older rats (>15 months), occurring at similar frequencies in MTSAAV (78%–90%) and PBS (89%) groups (P = 0.71; Supplementary Fig. S8). Inflammation of the digestive tract (43%–71%) and lymph node changes (≤17%) were common findings but not treatment related. No abnormalities were observed in the small intestine or gonads (Supplementary Figs. S9, S10). Overall, all histopathological observations were consistent with normal aging in rats, with no MTSAAV/hND4-induced damage detected at either dose.

Discussion

Current LHON treatments are limited, driving development of novel approaches like gene therapy. Although allotopic ND4 gene therapy has shown partial visual recovery in clinical trials (improving by approximately 15 Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) letters after 12–24 months), most patients remain severely impaired, especially with longer disease duration.16,21,38-40 Early intervention is therefore critical to preserve RGCs before irreversible damage occurs. Mitochondrial-targeted gene therapy using MTSAAV to deliver ND4 directly into mitochondria may provide faster and more efficient restoration of mitochondrial function than allotopic methods.20 Unlike conventional AAV-based allotopic expression systems that depend on nuclear transcription and subsequent cytosolic import of the ND4 protein, the MTSAAV strategy targets the transgene directly to mitochondria, minimizing cytosolic exposure and off-target expression. This design may reduce the likelihood of immune activation or proteotoxic stress. The confined biodistribution and absence of retinal toxicity observed in this study are consistent with such localized mitochondrial delivery, underscoring the potential safety advantages of the MTSAAV-directed approach.

In this report, the intravitreal injection of MTSAAV/hND4 has shown a strong localization of vector DNA in the injected eye and very limited spread to peripheral tissues. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed only low systemic exposure with vector DNA levels in peripheral tissues below 5 × 10² copies/100 ng genomic DNA at 90 days after injection. A 10-fold reduction in viral DNA in the injected eye by day 90 further indicates transient transgene expression, alleviating potential long-term safety concerns. There was no evidence of vector-related local or systemic toxic effects as determined by stable hematological, histopathological, and clinical chemistry findings. These results reinforce the selective targeting and biosafety of the MTSAAV/hND4 approach, both of which are essential for clinical translation.

RGC density and axonal integrity did not differ significantly between MTSAAV/hND4-treated and PBS control eyes (3060 ± 82 vs. 3378 ± 102 cells/mm², P > 0.05), suggesting no cytotoxicity. Similarly, PERG amplitudes declined uniformly across both MTSAAV/hND4- and PBS-treated groups during the 6-month observation period (8.3 ± 0.7 µV vs. 9.1 ± 1.2 µV, P = 0.28), consistent with age-related functional decline rather than vector-mediated deficit. No anterior chamber abnormalities, cataracts, or ocular inflammation were observed following MTSAAV/hND4 administration. Collectively, these data demonstrate the biocompatibility and ocular safety of MTSAAV/hND4, supporting its advancement toward clinical application.

Surprisingly, a paucity of viral DNA (302 ± 262 copies per 100 ng genome DNA) was also observed within the contralateral uninjected eyes, indicative of a restricted bilateral biodistribution. Because there is anatomic continuity between RGCs and the rest of the central visual pathways, optic nerve axonal transport may suggest a potential mechanism for vector transfer. Yet, because the contralateral eyes’ viral loads were <1% of those seen in the injected eyes, the likelihood of unilateral treatment achieving therapeutic levels bilaterally appears limited. To further explore this phenomenon, future studies using anterograde tracers or real-time imaging modalities are needed.

Histopathological analysis revealed no ocular or systemic inflammation, however, all AAV-injected animals developed NAbs by day 90, with titers of 1:1280, indicating a disconnection between humoral immune activation and tissue inflammation. NAbs can bind to AAV capsids, blocking viral entry into target cells and reducing therapeutic efficacy. Consequently, a second intravitreal administration may have limited or no efficacy unless NAb barriers are mitigated. Potential solutions for clinical translation include: (i) short-course immunosuppressant pretreatment in patients with high NAb titers23, (ii) using capsid-engineered AAV variants with reduced NAb sensitivity,41–43 and (iii) switching to sub-ILM injection to minimize systemic exposure.44–47

An approximately one log reduction in vector DNA copies was observed in the injected eyes between days 7 and 90, coinciding with the emergence of high-titer serum NAbs (1:1280) in the AAV-injected group. This temporal association suggests that systemic humoral immune responses may contribute to partial clearance or reduced persistence of vector genomes within ocular tissues. However, this gradual decline could also reflect the natural turnover of episomal AAV genomes and transduced retinal cells over time. Importantly, retinal structure (OCT), function (PERG), and histologic features remained stable throughout the study, indicating an absence of overt immune-mediated cytotoxicity. Although our data do not establish a direct causal link between serum NAbs and reduced ocular vector DNA, future studies should include the quantification of intraocular NAb titers (aqueous or vitreous), detailed characterization of T-cell responses, and evaluation of vector re-administration strategies to better elucidate whether the observed decline in ocular vector DNA primarily reflects immune-mediated clearance or natural genome dilution.

This study demonstrates the favorable ocular safety profile and restricted biodistribution of MTSAAV/hND4 in rodents; however, several translational challenges remain before these findings can be extended to humans. A major limitation lies in species-specific differences in AAV tropism and transduction efficiency. In rodents, intravitreal delivery of AAV2 efficiently transduces RGCs, whereas in primates and humans, the thicker ILM poses a significant barrier, reducing vector penetration and RGC transduction. Although the MTSAAV vector used here incorporates Y444F, Y500F, and Y730F capsid mutations known to enhance RGC infection,48 efficient and cell type-specific delivery to human RGCs remains a challenge.49 Recent advances, including engineered AAV variants with improved RGC tropism (e.g., AAV7m8 and AAV-quadY-F),28,50,51 ILM modulation,44 and alternative delivery routes such as sub-ILM injection,44,45,47 may help overcome this limitation.

The quantification of vector genomes in the present study was based on whole-retinal homogenates, which may underestimate transduction efficiency within specific cell populations. Future work incorporating cell type-specific assays, in situ hybridization, or single-cell transcriptomic analyses will provide a more detailed understanding of vector distribution and expression patterns across retinal layers. Collectively, these efforts will be essential to refine and optimize the translation of MTSAAV-mediated mitochondrial gene therapy from rodent models to human clinical application.

In summary, MTSAAV/hND4 is associated with a strong safety profile for intravitreal gene therapy with minimal vector systemic biodistribution and retention of the retinal structure and function. Two significant observations—the low-level contralateral viral distribution and the delayed NAb response—highlight the requirement for additional studies to investigate the underlying mechanisms of axonal transport and immune regulation. Together with previous efficacy studies in the LHON model, these data provide a solid basis for initiating phase I clinical trial in patients with mitochondrial optic neuropathies.
