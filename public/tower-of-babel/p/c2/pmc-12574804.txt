Two Cases of Low Intersphincteric Anal Fistulas with Intrafistulous Calculi
Authors: Yoshiyuki Suzuki, Kosuke Okamoto, Joji Kuromizu, Nobuyoshi Miyajima, Yasuhiro Shimojima, Yukihito Kokuba, Naomi Matsumura, Yoichi Kono, Ayumi Beniya, Sayuri Matsushima, Yu Sakai, Makoto Matsushima
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnal fistulas with intrafistulous calculi are extremely rare. Enterolithiasis generally occurs due to the precipitation, aggregation, and calcification of non-digestible materials; however, its occurrence within anal fistulas remains poorly understood. Here, we report two cases of low intersphincteric anal fistulas with intrafistulous calculi, diagnosed via endoanal ultrasonography and treated surgically. We discuss the possible pathophysiological mechanisms, imaging characteristics, and clinical significance of these calculi. Keywords: anal fistula, fistulous calculi, enterolithiasis, endoanal ultrasonography, surgery

Introduction

Enterolithiasis is an uncommon condition resulting from the deposition, aggregation, and calcification of non-digestible substances[1]. The formation of calculi within an anal fistula is exceedingly rare, with only a few reported cases. We present two patients with low intersphincteric anal fistulas containing calculi, with a literature review.

Case Reports

Case 1

A 61-year-old male presented with persistent anal pain for one year. His medical history included hypertension, and he was on amlodipine (2.5 mg daily). He had no prior anorectal surgery. Visual and digital rectal examinations revealed multiple fistulous tracts. Endoanal ultrasonography revealed intersphincteric fistulas at the 1, 2, and 6 o'clock positions, along with a larger fistula at 9 o'clock. The latter contained multiple hyperechoic nodules with acoustic shadowing, suggestive of calculi (Figure 1B). They were located between the internal and external sphincteric muscles nearby primary opening.

Intraoperatively, 4 fistulas with induration and hemorrhoids were found. Under spinal anesthesia, fistulotomy and fistulectomy were performed in the jackknife position. Four fistulas were treated, and four internal hemorrhoids were simultaneously excised. The 9 o'clock fistula underwent sphincter-preserving surgery, and three calculi (5, 3, and 2 mm in diameter) were identified within the tract (Figure 1A).

Histopathology of the excised fistula tract with calculi confirmed granulation tissue and fistula formation with acute inflammatory infiltration. No evidence of dysplasia or granulomatous disease was observed. Chemical analysis of the calculi revealed 65% calcium phosphate, 20% protein, and 15% calcium carbonate.

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on the sixth postoperative day. Complete epithelialization of all wounds was achieved by 8 weeks.

Case 2

A 44-year-old male presented with perianal swelling and pain for several days. He had no significant medical history or prior anorectal surgery. Examination revealed a perianal abscess at the 1 o'clock position, which was incised and drained under local anesthesia. Subsequent endoanal ultrasonography identified an intersphincteric fistula containing a hyperechoic nodule with posterior acoustic shadowing, consistent with an intrafistulous calculus (Figure 1D).

As the intraoperative findings, there was a fistula with induration and no hemorrhoids. Surgical intervention under spinal anesthesia was performed, with a fistulotomy revealing a single black calculus measuring 3 mm (Figure 1C). Histopathological examination of the excised fistula tract around the secondary opening showed chronic inflammatory changes and fibrosis, with no dysplasia or granulomas. Chemical analysis of the calculus revealed 83% calcium phosphate and 17% protein.

The patient had an uneventful recovery, with discharge on postoperative day eight and complete wound healing by 7 weeks.

Discussion

Enterolithiasis is classified into true enteroliths (e.g., bile acid and calcium salt stones) and false enteroliths, which form due to the precipitation of ingested substances, such as food debris or medication. Fecaliths belong to the latter category and typically form as a result of mechanical obstruction, reduced peristalsis, or chronic stasis, as seen in diverticular disease, Crohn's disease, or postoperative anastomotic changes[2,3]. Pathologically, ectopic calcifications are classified into metastatic and dystrophic types. While metastatic calcifications occur due to systemic calcium-phosphorus imbalance, dystrophic calcifications arise in locally inflamed or necrotic tissues, as seen in chronic abscesses[4]. The fistulous environment, with recurrent infections and local tissue necrosis, likely promoted dystrophic calcification in these two patients. In our cases, the presence of intersphincteric fistulas likely created an environment conducive to fecalith formation. Chronic stasis of an anal discharge may have served as a nidus for calcium phosphate deposition, leading to stone development.

Endoanal ultrasonography was pivotal in identifying these calculi preoperatively. The characteristic appearance of hyperechoic nodules with posterior acoustic shadowing aligns with sonographic findings of appendicoliths in acute appendicitis[5]. Given that appendicoliths are associated with poorer prognoses in conservatively managed appendicitis, the presence of fistulous calculi may similarly contribute to recurrent abscess formation and chronic fistula persistence. Further studies are needed to elucidate their clinical implications.

A PubMed literature search identified only four previous cases of intrafistulous calculi, reported from Sudan, Italy, and the UK. (Table 1)[6-9]. Notably, Sudan accounted for two cases, implying potential environmental or dietary influences. Unlike prior cases with prolonged disease courses (10-40 years), our patients had calculi despite relatively short symptom durations. This raises the possibility of asymptomatic fistulas that persist undiagnosed until calculi formation.

Conclusion

We report two rare cases of low intersphincteric anal fistulas containing intrafistulous calculi. These calculi likely formed due to prolonged anal discharge stasis, leading to dystrophic calcification. Our findings emphasize the role of endoanal ultrasonography in early detection and the potential implications of fistulous calculi in recurrent abscess formation. Further research is needed to understand the natural history and optimal management strategies for these rare entities.
