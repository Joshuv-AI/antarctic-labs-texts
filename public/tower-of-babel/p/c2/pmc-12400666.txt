Epidemiological analysis of human brucellosis in North Khorasan province, Iran (2018–2023): a six-year multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Niloofar Sadooghi, Yaser Panahi, Afshin Delshad, Max Maurin, Maryam Dadar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrucellosis remains a significant zoonotic issue in Iran, especially in areas with substantial livestock agriculture. This study explores the epidemiological profile of human brucellosis in North Khorasan Province, northern Iran, over six years from 2018 to 2023. A retrospective analysis was performed on 24,104 suspected cases of human brucellosis reported to healthcare facilities in both urban and rural regions of North Khorasan. Cases were detected through clinical symptoms and validated via serological assays, including the standard tube agglutination test (SAT), the 2-mercaptoethanol (2ME) test, and the Combs Wright. Demographic characteristics, regional distribution, occupational exposure, animal contact, and the intake of unpasteurized dairy products were evaluated utilizing SPSS version 22. Chi-square testing identified relationships between variables, with significance established at p < 0.05. Of the total suspected cases, 1877 (7.7%) were confirmed positive for brucellosis. Incidence rates escalated over time, reaching a zenith in 2023. The most significant incidence of positive cases was observed in Shirvan (21.7%), followed by Faruj, Maneh, Samalqan, and Bojnurd. A majority of positive cases (81.1%) originated from rural areas, demonstrating a substantial correlation between infection and rural residency (p < 0.001). Livestock farming constituted the predominant occupation among infected persons (56.6%), with a higher prevalence in males than females (55.9% vs. 44.1%). The age cohort of 31 to 50 years exhibited the most significant infection rate at 39.1%. A substantial percentage of patients reported consuming unpasteurized dairy products (93.1%) and having direct interaction with animals (87.5%). Infection among family members was also significant (19.6%). Our results confirmed a considerable public health concern in North Khorasan, particularly affecting rural communities, livestock producers, and those who use unpasteurized dairy products. These findings highlight the necessity for focused control tactics, public awareness initiatives, and improved surveillance, especially in areas with high incidence rates.Keywords: Brucella, Milk, North khorasan, Public health, Livestock farming

Introduction

Brucellosis is a common disease prevalent in many regions, except where livestock brucellosis has been reported eradicated [1]. Effective control of animal brucellosis can lead to a marked decrease in human cases [2]. The disease remains a significant concern for both livestock and public health in various parts of the world, including Iran, where it impacts livelihoods [3, 4]. Preventing, controlling, or eradicating brucellosis in any country or region requires informed policymaking supported by accurate epidemiological data [5]. Individuals at higher risk of contracting the disease include farmers, veterinarians, and slaughterhouse workers [6]. Non-pasteurized dairy products, especially fresh cheese made from unpasteurized milk, are important sources of infection. Therefore, it is essential to avoid these products to prevent disease [7]. In Iran, brucellosis has a high infection rate among both animals and humans, classifying it as a native disease [8, 9]. Brucellosis remains a significant public health issue in some areas of Iran, with a recent increase in incidence [10]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, brucellosis cases appeared to decrease, likely due to self-protection measures such as wearing masks and gloves, particularly among individuals in rural and urban areas who are in close contact with contaminated livestock and their products. These precautions help minimize exposure to the sources of Brucella infection [11].

In endemic areas, several factors contribute to the ongoing prevalence of animal brucellosis, including low vaccination rates, disparities between the number of infected livestock and those sampled due to insufficient monitoring, and a lack of continuous oversight of brucella infections. Additionally, there is often reluctance to cull infected animals due to the absence of compensation from supervisory bodies or organizations. Repeated abortions and infertility are essential, although nonspecific, markers of animal brucellosis. Acute brucellosis in humans usually manifests with nonspecific flu-like symptoms, including recurrent fever, sweating, chills, muscle pain, joint pain, and fatigue. If not accurately diagnosed and treated during the acute phase, the disease may progress to a chronic state, which could last for years. Both acute and chronic infections can cause complications related to the dissemination of Brucella to almost any organ, including the musculoskeletal, digestive, genitourinary, central nervous, and cardiovascular systems [12].

However, the clinical diagnosis of brucellosis remains challenging because its symptoms are not specific and may be confused with those of many other diseases [12]. In particular, brucellosis can evoke tuberculosis due to the clinical, radiological, and histological similarities between these two diseases. Early and accurate diagnosis, along with prompt treatment of brucellosis, are crucial for a favorable recovery [13]. In recent years, various studies have been conducted across different regions of Iran to explore the epidemiology, risk factors, and management strategies for brucellosis. However, there remains a lack of research focusing on brucellosis in northeastern Iran. This study aims to determine the seroprevalence and investigate the clinical characteristics of human brucellosis across different age groups in North Khorasan, Iran, from 2018 to 2023. This multicenter study seeks to identify patterns of infection, clinical symptoms, and potential risk factors, providing valuable insights for public health interventions and prevention strategies in the region.

Methods

Study area

North Khorasan is located in northeastern Iran, bordering Turkmenistan to the north. It is bordered to the west by Golestan Province, to the southwest by Semnan Province, and to the south by Razavi Khorasan Province. The province covers an area of approximately 28,434 km2 (Fig. 1). North Khorasan Province has ten cities: Bam and Safiabad, Bojnord (center of North Khorasan Province), Esfarayen, Faruj, Garmeh, Jajarm, Maneh, Raz and Jargalan, Samalqan, and Shirvan.

Study design and data sources

This retrospective study was conducted in urban and rural regions of North Khorasan province, involving patients suspected of having brucellosis (n = 24,104) who presented between 2018 and 2023 with at least one of the following clinical symptoms suggestive of brucellosis: abdominal pain, headache, night sweats, fatigue, muscle and bone pain, joint pain, back pain, arthralgia, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, loss of appetite, weight loss, fever, or chills. Patients were identified and referred for brucellosis testing by physicians at primary care centers, hospitals, or infectious disease clinics, based on clinical suspicion and local health protocols. Symptom duration of more than five days and absence of an alternative diagnosis were generally required for referral.

It aimed to clarify the epidemiological features of human brucellosis in northeast Iran. This study utilized data on managing infectious diseases from the Zoonotic Diseases Control Department (Portal of the Deputy of the Ministry of Health and Medical Education) to assess the epidemiological characteristics of brucellosis in North Khorasan Province, located in the northeast of Iran, over a six-year period. The necessary data were acquired by identifying brucellosis patients in public or private healthcare facilities using examination forms and their registration in the Ministry of Health portal in North Khorasan province. We collected demographic and clinical information and administered a risk factor questionnaire according to previous study [14]. The questionnaire used in this study was a structured, interviewer-administered tool designed to collect comprehensive information on demographic, occupational, and clinical risk factors associated with brucellosis. It included variables such as residence (categorized as rural, urban, or nomadic), occupation (butcher, agricultural farmer, livestock farmer, housewife, child, student, or other jobs), age group (< 10 years, 11–30 years, 31–50 years, and > 51 years), and gender (male or female). The questionnaire also assessed whether other family members were infected with brucellosis (yes, no, or not determined) and recorded the presence or absence of specific clinical symptoms, including abdominal pain, headache, night sweats, fatigue, muscle and bone pain, back pain, arthralgia, loss of appetite, weight loss, fever, chills, or no symptoms at all. Additionally, participants were asked about their history of direct animal contact and their consumption of unpasteurized dairy products (both recorded as yes or no responses). The questionnaire was adapted from previously validated epidemiological studies and was translated into the local language to ensure clarity and consistency during data collection.

Brucellosis diagnosis

Following national guidelines established by the Iranian Center for Disease Control (ICDC), a confirmed case of human brucellosis is defined as a patient presenting with clinical signs and symptoms compatible with brucellosis, along with serological confirmation. The diagnostic criteria include a Standard Tube Agglutination Test (SAT) titer of ≥ 1:80 and a 2-Mercaptoethanol (2ME) agglutination titer of ≥ 1:40. For diagnostic testing, 3 mL of venous blood was collected aseptically from each symptomatic subject using sterile vacutainer tubes. The samples were immediately labeled and transported to the laboratory under cold chain conditions to maintain sample integrity. Upon arrival, blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to separate the serum, which was then aliquoted and stored at 2–8 °C until analysis. All serum samples were subjected to the SAT and 2ME tests, which were performed using standardized protocols recommended by the ICDC. Additionally, the Coombs-Wright test was conducted to detect incomplete or blocking antibodies in chronic or complicated cases. For quality control, each batch of reagents was validated using known positive and negative control sera, and tests were performed in duplicate to ensure reliability and reproducibility of the results. Laboratory personnel followed strict biosafety and quality assurance procedures throughout the diagnostic process [15].

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using SPSS software version 22. For each serological test, the number of seropositive human cases was divided by the total number of individuals tested within the human population during the study period. The prevalence was calculated using the formula:

The total number of individuals tested served as the population denominator, and this number was based on the actual human sample size collected during the study period. If the study spanned multiple years, population adjustments were made annually based on available local health records or census data to reflect accurate denominators for each year. The completed questionnaires’ output was analyzed as an Excel file. Information was extracted through a standardized template derived from the question guide, and summaries of the interviews were generated. To compare the results across variables in positive persons and evaluate whether the differences are statistically significant, we used the Chi-square (χ²) test. The value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. These cases with undetermined infection status were excluded from analyses to avoid introducing bias due to uncertain classification. To assess the potential impact of this missing data, a sensitivity analysis was performed. In one scenario, all missing cases were assumed to have a positive family history; in another, they were assumed to have none. The results of the main analyses remained consistent across both scenarios, indicating that the missing data had a negligible effect on the overall conclusions.

Results

Between 2018 and 2023, 24,104 individuals with suspected human symptoms of brucellosis were evaluated. Among them, 1,877 cases (7.7%) tested positive based on serological criteria (Table 1). Of these confirmed cases, 1,554 individuals (approximately 82.8%) were included in the study and completed the questionnaire. The positivity rate increased in 2023 (Fig. 2). The highest number of positive cases over the six years was reported in Shirvan, followed closely by Faruj, Maneh, and Samalqan. Seven cities in the North Khorasan province revealed positive samples with different frequencies. With 407 positive samples (26.2%) of the whole, Shirvan had the most positive samples among them. Faruj with 353 instances (22.7%), Maneh and Samalqan with 329 cases (21.2%), and Bojnurd with 322 cases (20.7%), came next. Raz and Jargalan had 69 instances (4.5%), Jajarm had 48 cases (3.1%), and Garmeh had the lowest number of positive samples with 25 cases (1.6%). These findings highlight a notable geographical variance in the positive case distribution among the investigated counties (Fig. 3).

Based on residence, 1260 (81.1%) positive cases were noted among people residing in rural areas (Table 2). Residents of urban areas accounted for 270 cases (17.4%), while the nomadic population represented the smallest proportion, with 23 cases (1.5%). These findings indicate that rural populations were the most affected group in terms of positive cases. Rural habitation was predominant in all regions except Garmeh, where urban inhabitants constitute 80% of the population. The nomadic population is negligible overall, comprising 2.5% in Shirvan, 3.7% in Maneh and Samalqan, and 0.3% in Bojnurd. There was a statistically significant association across cities and residence type in the distribution of brucellosis cases (p < 0.001). The distribution of positive cases by occupation revealed that the highest proportion was among livestock farmers, with 879 cases (56.6%), followed by housewives with 346 cases (22.3%). Students represented 103 cases (6.6%). Individuals who were agricultural farmers included 60 cases (3.9%). Smaller proportions were seen among children (36 cases, 2.3%), and other cases such as employees (15 cases, 0.9%), taxi drivers (6 cases, 0.4%), butchers (2 cases, 0.1%), and livestock vaccinators (1 case, 0.1%). Results also showed a statistically significant relationship between occupation and county (p < 0.0001). Individuals involved in direct contact with livestock, particularly livestock farmers in every city, are the most affected occupational group. Shirvan and Faruj have a very high number of livestock farmers. Garmeh has fewer cases of brucellosis, but still shows livestock as the dominant occupational risk.

Regarding the age categories, the 31–50 age group had the highest number of brucellosis-positive cases, making up about 39.1%. The > 51 age group follows with about 30.5%, and the < 10 age group had the lowest number of cases (3.4%). Of the overall positive cases, males were the majority with 867 cases (55.9%), and females constituted 686 instances (44.1%). There is a significant variation in the gender distribution of participants across the different regions. For example, Garmeh, Raz, and Jargalan have noticeably higher proportions of male participants, while Bojnurd and Faruj show a more balanced distribution. This variation is unlikely due to random chance (p < 0.01). Concerning the infection status of further family members, 305 cases (19.6%) reported a history of brucellosis infection within the family. Conversely, 1209 individuals (77.8%) showed an absence of familial infection history, while cases with undetermined familial infection status (2.5%) were treated as missing data.

in 39 cases (2.5%), the infection status of other family members remained undetermined. Shirvan has the highest proportion of family members infected with brucellosis (33.9%), suggesting a statistically significant familial spread in that region. Garmeh and Bojnurd show the lowest rates (8% and 9%), indicating less family-based transmission (p < 0.0001). Examining clinical symptoms in positive cases revealed that the predominant symptom was musculoskeletal pain, observed in 1297 people (83.5%). This was succeeded by fever in 1054 instances (67.9%), loss of appetite in 781 cases (50.3%), and back pain in 662 instances (42.6%). Other commonly reported complaints included fatigue, weight loss, and arthralgia. Night sweats, chills, abdominal pain, and headache were infrequently reported symptoms. Most patients (1322 cases; 85.1%) indicated a history of animal contact, while 231 individuals (14.8%) reported no such contact. The Chi-Square test shows a statistically significant association between region and history of animal contact that varies significantly between areas. Discussion

Brucellosis, a global concern, is frequently ignored and poses considerable health, economic, and livelihood difficulties in Iran [12]. It is especially common among livestock workers due to their frequent interaction with infected animals. Moreover, families within these groups encounter a significant risk of potential domestic exposure owing to the proximity of animals in home environments. Inadequate healthcare infrastructure in low- and middle-income countries like Iran is further strained by socioeconomic disparities, where diseases such as brucellosis disproportionately impact marginalized and impoverished communities, compounding public health challenges and hindering effective disease control and prevention [16, 17]. The epidemiology of human brucellosis has changed over the past 25 years due to factors such as cattle and sheep production, control programs for brucellosis, hygienic conditions, and socioeconomic considerations [18, 19]. Climate factors like rainfall, humidity, temperature, and sunshine length impact human brucellosis transmission during the seasons in Iran [20]. To develop effective control strategies for human brucellosis, it is crucial to assess the epidemic situation, demographics, incidence rate, geographical distribution, and genetic profiles of brucellosis in the province. This retrospective study analyzed the epidemiology and clinical characteristics of 1553 human brucellosis cases in Iran. Our results show a consistent increase in brucellosis suspected cases, from 2553 in 2018 to 5876 in 2023. They also highlight that the incidence rate varies over time, reaching a peak in 2023 at 51.7 per 100,000 people, the highest figure in the data provided. Another study in Iran also reported incidence rates of 25.89 and 12.07 in 2014 and 2009, respectively [21]. A study from North Khorasan, based on a sample size of 1310, reported an incidence of 26.6, which is lower than our study conducted during 2018–2023 [22]. According to the Ministry of Health, brucellosis rates in Iran range from 114/100,000 to 225/100,000 in highly endemic areas [1]. According to a 2003 study, the average incidence of brucellosis in Iran was 21 cases per 100,000 individuals, ranging from 1.5 to 107.5 in different locations [23]. Another study also reported the rate of 44.9% by RBPT, 20.7% by SAT, and 8.7% by 2-ME in suspected persons for brucellosis in Iran [12]. The majority of brucellosis cases between 2014 and 2021 in southern Iran were reported among women, individuals from rural areas, and homemakers, with the highest incidence in the 21–30 age group and a peak in 2015 [24]. The seroprevalence of human brucellosis in Kurdistan, Western Iran, was reported to be 3.9% among participants [24]. From 2009 to 2017, Iran recorded 138,448 brucellosis cases, with the highest incidence in 2014, primarily affecting males, rural residents, and individuals aged 16–44, with the highest rates reported in Hamadan, Lorestan, Kurdistan, and Kermanshah provinces [21].

The observed rise in incidence rates from 28.04 in 2018 to 51.7 in 2023 may be influenced by several potential contributing factors. These include a possible increase in brucellosis prevalence among the animal population, improved detection and reporting of human cases, and changes in population dynamics, health-seeking behaviors, or disease transmission patterns in animals. However, due to the lack of supporting data, particularly regarding animal prevalence and diagnostic practices, these explanations remain hypothetical. Further research is needed to confirm and quantify the impact of these factors.

Animal brucellosis in North Khorasan province of Iran remains an endemic and significant issue [25]. Although the most detailed recent data on animal brucellosis specifically in North Khorasan are limited, the region, like much of northeastern Iran, faces persistent challenges with brucellosis in livestock, primarily involving sheep, goats, and cattle [26, 27]. The prevalence of brucellosis has been reported as 0.56% in cattle, 3.4% in small ruminants, and 2.5% in horses in this province [28]. This information has highlighted the correlation between the observed increase in human brucellosis cases and the existing infection rates among livestock. Our study found that a greater proportion of anti-Brucella antibodies in counties like Shirvan and Faruj may warrant enhanced surveillance, targeted public health interventions, and community education. However, the low frequency in Garmeh and Jajarm may be due to underreporting or insufficient screening. The search results provide little information on brucellosis prevalence in Shirvan, Faruj, Garmeh, and Jajarm districts; however, some essential facts regarding North Khorasan province showed that in this Province, 15.4% of slaughterhouse workers, farmers, butchers, and veterinarians had positive Brucella serological tests, indicating high-risk occupational persons in this category [29].

Our study also showed that rural residents had a higher brucellosis risk (81.1%) than urban residents (17.4%). Brucellosis seroprevalence was about 4.6 times greater in rural areas than in cities. Rural regions showed the highest number of cases in almost all cities. Nomadic populations were more affected in some counties (e.g., Maneh & Samalqan and Shirvan). This significant result supports the need for targeted public health strategies that consider geographic and residential contexts when addressing the prevention and control of brucellosis. However, where people live (rural, urban, or nomadic settings) significantly affects the likelihood of brucellosis infection, and this pattern varies by city. A similar study showed that Asian and African countries with human brucellosis have identical findings [30–32]. This can be explained by poverty among rural populations, lack of awareness, close contact with domestic animals, and consumption of raw dairy products. A similar study in Iran and India identified that farming, contact with livestock, and animal husbandry were significant risk factors for human brucellosis [32, 33]. We observed a higher rate of positive Brucella serology in male compared to female participants, supporting previous studies that showed males are more likely to develop human brucellosis due to their engagement in animal husbandry [32, 34].

The study results indicate that middle-aged adults, specifically those aged 31 to 50, were the most impacted by the disease, with a slightly greater incidence in males. Similar to our study, it has been revealed that this disease affects younger people in the provinces of Hamadan and Mazandaran in Iran. The disease affects everyone who is economically and socially exposed [35]. Our results also showed that many positive cases without a family history of infection may be spread through animal or environmental exposure rather than households. This indicates that public health initiatives should go beyond household contact tracing to address environmental, occupational, and societal variables that spread disease. Previous studies found that brucellosis occurred outside of family transmission chains. In studies of zoonotic diseases like brucellosis, occupational exposure (farming, animal husbandry, or butchery) and environmental contact (contaminated water or soil) have been linked to disease acquisition [1, 36]. These transmission routes of brucellosis are crucial in urban, rural, or semi-urban areas with frequent animal contact or raw milk supplies [37].

Our study’s occupational distribution of brucellosis cases showed that livestock farmers (56.6%) were the most crucial target population. Consistent with our results, most brucellosis studies place livestock farmers at the highest risk group due to their frequent interaction with infected animals and their products [11, 38]. Similarly, other studies found that livestock handlers and farmers accounted for over 50% of brucellosis infections in Iran [33, 39]. Research in Turkey and Saudi Arabia showed that farmers and shepherds were the predominant occupational group affected, often exceeding 50% of cases [40, 41]. Our results also showed that musculoskeletal pain (83.5%) was the most common symptom, followed by fever (67.9%), loss of appetite (50.3%), and back pain (42.6%). This is consistent with other studies and systematic reviews on human brucellosis [12, 16, 42]. Arthralgia, myalgia, and back pain are symptoms of brucellosis [43]. A systematic review by Dean et al. (2012) found fever, arthralgia, myalgia, and back pain as prominent symptoms in brucellosis patients, matching our findings [17]. In China, a meta-analysis of clinical manifestations found muscle pain, fever, arthralgia, and fatigue to be the most prevalent symptoms, ranging from 56 to 87% [44].

The large number of patients with animal contacts in our study confirms global research showing that direct contact with infected animals is the main transmission route. Al Dahouk et al. (2013) found occupational or domestic animal interaction in over 80% of brucellosis patients [45]. According to Pappas et al. (2006), direct exposure exposes farmers, abattoir workers, and veterinarians to risk [4]. Our data also shows regional diversity in animal contact history, as seen in endemic places like Iran and Turkey, where intensive livestock production leads to greater animal contact rates in rural areas [33, 39, 46, 47].

Our findings showed a significant regional difference in the types of dairy products consumed by brucellosis patients. Raz and Jargalan had the highest milk consumption rate (100%). Raw milk was highly consumed across all cities, although cream and butter consumption is much lower in Bojnurd and Garmeh. However, cheese consumption is high in Shirvan and Raz & Jargalan but very low in Jajarm (14.6%). Colostrum is relatively high in Garmeh (42.1%), much higher than elsewhere. Ice cream has overall low consumption and does not show considerable variation. These differences are statistically significant and likely reflect regional dietary practices, traditions, or access to specific dairy products. Consistent with our data, it has been noted that unpasteurized dairy is a primary transmission route in endemic rural and urban areas [37]. In Saudi Arabia and Jordan, 70–90% of brucellosis patients eat raw milk or dairy products [48, 49]. Our results showed that milk and cheese are the most common unpasteurized dairy products connected to brucellosis. It has been shown that raw milk and soft cheeses were India’s primary sources of human brucellosis [50]. In Iran and Turkey, handcrafted cheeses and fresh milk are eaten unpasteurized [2, 51]. However, one of the significant limitations in the diagnosis of brucellosis in Iran is the continued reliance on conventional serological tests such as the SAT, 2-ME, and the Coombs-Wright test. Although these methods are widely used due to their low cost and accessibility, their diagnostic accuracy is limited, particularly in hyperendemic regions like Iran. In such settings, background antibody levels in the general population may be elevated, making it difficult to distinguish between past exposure and active infection. Additionally, the Coombs-Wright test is considered outdated and is no longer routinely used in most laboratories. However, due to insufficient laboratory infrastructure and limited access to more advanced diagnostic tools such as culture, ELISA, or molecular diagnostic techniques in many regions of the country, the Iranian Ministry of Health continues to employ these older methods in routine clinical practice. As a result, the reported prevalence may be somewhat overestimated due to possible false-positive results. This highlights a critical gap in the diagnostic capacity, which may result in delayed or inaccurate diagnosis and underscores the need for investment in more modern diagnostic infrastructure.

Conclusion

This retrospective analysis covers 1553 human brucellosis cases in North Khorasan Province, Iran, from 2018 to 2023. This research on incidence rates and geographic and demographic heterogeneity in disease distribution and presentation in this region demonstrates the continuous public health burden of brucellosis. In 2023, the incidence reached 51.7 per 100,000, which may indicate increased disease transmission and better case discovery and reporting. As with earlier regional and worldwide research, rural residents, livestock farmers, and middle-aged males were most affected. Nomadic and animal husbandry groups exhibit higher rates of brucellosis, suggesting a possible occupational exposure risk linked to direct contact with diseased animals and consumption of unpasteurized dairy products. Based on the results of this study, the primary source of human infection is contact with infected livestock and their secretions. For these reasons, effective control strategies must focus on improving livestock health, enhancing public awareness about the risks of consuming unpasteurized dairy, and strengthening veterinary and public health collaboration. Addressing these key sources of transmission is essential for reducing the incidence of human brucellosis and improving disease management in endemic areas. Moreover, the brucellosis patients were characterized by musculoskeletal pain, fever, loss of appetite, and back pain, typical of brucellosis. Most patients had no family history of infection, and geographical disparities in animal interaction and dairy intake suggest that community and environmental exposures drive disease transmission. These findings necessitate focused initiatives like public education, occupational health, veterinary control, and food safety. Reducing brucellosis requires improving rural healthcare facilities, encouraging safe dairy use, and monitoring high-risk people and locations. Sociocultural behaviors, economic restraints, and environmental influences on human and animal health in endemic areas must also be considered. Furthermore, considering the high prevalence of brucellosis among farmers, the most practical and effective solution in these regions is the implementation of targeted educational programs. These initiatives should aim to raise awareness about the disease, its transmission routes, and preventive measures. Raising awareness and educating at-risk groups is a cornerstone for brucellosis prevention and control. It empowers communities with the knowledge and tools needed to protect themselves, supports broader public health objectives, and is essential for long-term eradication efforts. This study emphasizes the necessity of a One-Health approach that integrates human, animal, and environmental health to prevent brucellosis in North Khorasan and other endemic locations.
