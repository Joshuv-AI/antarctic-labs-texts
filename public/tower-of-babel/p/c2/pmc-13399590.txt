Effectiveness of tirzepatide in Japanese patients with type 2 diabetes but no obesity: A sub‐analysis of Hokkaido‐TZP study data
Authors: Tomoe Abe, Fumika Maruyama, Eisuke Nishikawa, Miki Ito, Yukie Miyano, Hiroki Yamagata, Taiga Sasaki, Hidemitsu Sakagami, Akinobu Nakamura, Hiroshi Nomoto, the Hokkaido‐TZP Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlthough tirzepatide efficaciously reduces glucose concentrations and weight, phase 3 trials evaluated patients with a body mass index (BMI) ≥23 kg/m2. Consequently, clinical evidence in patients with type 2 diabetes but no obesity, common in Asia, is insufficient. In this secondary analysis of the multicentre, real‐world Hokkaido‐TZP study, we evaluated 107 Japanese patients with T2D and BMI <25.0 kg/m2. Over 6 months, tirzepatide significantly reduced HbA1c in all the BMI tertiles (Low, Middle, High). Seven patients discontinued treatment due to adverse events; notably, all belonged to the Low or Middle BMI groups. The overall BMI also significantly decreased, but the reduction was more pronounced in participants with higher baseline BMIs. Multiple regression analysis identified baseline BMI and dietary consultations as significant independent factors contributing to the BMI reduction. Thus, tirzepatide provides effective glycaemic control in patients without obesity, and its weight‐lowering effect might be modulated by baseline BMI and lifestyle interventions.Keywords: Non‐obese, Tirzepatide, Type 2 diabetes

INTRODUCTION

Multiple studies have revealed various benefits of tirzepatide, including potent glucose‐lowering and weight‐reduction effects, in patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D) 1 , 2 . However, most phase 3 trials, such as the SURPASS programmes, focused primarily on individuals with a body mass index (BMI) ≥23 or 25 kg/m2 2 .

In comparison with other populations, East Asian patients with T2D have a lower mean BMI and distinct pathophysiological features, such as impaired insulin secretion 3 . The average BMI of Japanese patients with T2D is approximately 25 kg/m2 4 , but there have been few studies in which the effectiveness of tirzepatide has been characterized in Asian patients without obesity. Therefore, it is important to determine whether potent incretin‐based therapies lead to excessive weight loss in this group, to ensure their safety.

Here, we studied a cohort of patients with BMI <25 kg/m2 from the Hokkaido‐TZP study, aiming to evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of tirzepatide in a real‐world clinical setting.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We performed a secondary analysis of data from the Hokkaido‐TZP study, a multicentre, retrospective, observational study that was conducted at 10 specialized diabetes centres in Hokkaido, Japan (Appendix S1) 5 . Briefly, the original cohort comprised all the patients with T2D who initiated tirzepatide between April 2023 and December 2024 and were followed for ≥6 months. Patients with obesity, defined using a BMI ≥25.0 kg/m2, according to the Japanese criteria 6 , and those with missing baseline BMI data, were excluded from the present analysis. Additionally, patients who discontinued tirzepatide within 6 months due to adverse events were excluded from the efficacy analysis. The remaining patients were then placed into three groups based on the baseline BMI tertile: Low BMI (<22.7 kg/m2), Middle BMI (22.7–24.1 kg/m2), and High BMI (≥24.1 kg/m2) groups. The primary outcomes were the changes in HbA1c and BMI between baseline and 6 months after the initiation of tirzepatide, and the secondary outcomes were changes in other metabolic parameters. Clinical data, including the prior use of glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RAs), insulin therapy, and the occurrence of dietary consultations or medication changes during follow‐up, were collected from electronic medical records.

Continuous datasets for the BMI tertile groups were compared using appropriate parametric or non‐parametric tests. The changes in HbA1c and BMI were compared among the patient subgroups and BMI tertiles. To identify factors influencing the clinical outcomes, Spearman's rank correlation analysis and multiple regression analysis were performed for variables that showed potential effects in the subgroup assessments.

Statistical analyses were conducted using JMP version 18 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asahikawa Medical University Hospital (approval no. 24142) and all the participating institutions, and conformed to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained via an opt‐out method.

RESULTS

From the original cohort of 828 patients, 107 with BMI <25.0 kg/m2 were identified as having initiated tirzepatide treatment. Of these, seven patients discontinued the treatment within 6 months, owing to adverse events, and the remaining 100 patients met the final inclusion criteria (Figure 1). The details of the seven patients who discontinued treatment are summarized in Table S1. Importantly, all seven discontinued patients belonged to either the Low BMI group (n = 3) or the Middle BMI group (n = 4), whereas no treatment discontinuation occurred in the High BMI group. As shown in Table 1, the majority of the participants were being treated with incretin‐based therapies, including GLP‐1RAs (63%), at baseline. There were no significant differences in the clinical characteristics of the BMI tertile groups, except with respect to BMI‐related parameters (Table S2).

After 6 months of tirzepatide treatment, both HbA1c and BMI significantly decreased in the cohort as a whole (7.83 ± 1.48% to 6.83 ± 1.21%, P < 0.001; and 23.5 to 22.5 kg/m2, P < 0.001; respectively; Table 1). Although there was a decrease in HbA1c in all the BMI tertile groups (Figure 2a, Table S2), the reduction in BMI was significantly larger in the High BMI group than in the Low BMI group, with respect to both the absolute and percentage change (Figure 2b,c, Table S2). Correlation analysis showed a significant negative correlation between the baseline BMI and the change in BMI (ρ = −0.245, P = 0.014; Figure S1, Table S3). The mean maintenance dose of tirzepatide after 6 months was 5.1 ± 2.2 mg. When the patients were stratified by the baseline BMI tertile, the final dose was slightly higher in the High BMI group than in the Low BMI group (Table S4, P = 0.032).

Subgroup analyses revealed that the reduction in HbA1c was not significantly affected by prior GLP‐1RA use, insulin therapy, medication changes, or dietary consultations (Figure S2). In contrast, the reduction in BMI was significantly larger in GLP‐1RA‐naive patients than in prior users (P = 0.030) and tended to be larger in those who had participated in dietary consultations (P = 0.083) (Figure S3). Notably, the baseline clinical characteristics, including BMI and HbA1c, of the patients were comparable between those who participated in dietary consultations (n = 17) and those who did not (n = 83) (Table S5). Multiple regression analysis including the maintenance dose of tirzepatide as a covariate identified high baseline BMI (β = −0.214, P = 0.037) and participation in dietary consultations (β = −0.205, P = 0.043) as being independently and significantly associated with the reduction in BMI (Table 2). No participants newly reached a BMI <18.5 kg/m2 during the 6‐month study period. Regarding other safety concerns, mild hypoglycaemia occurred in two patients; however, both were managed by adjusting the doses of concomitant medications, and no severe hypoglycaemia developed.

DISCUSSION

This is the first multicentre study to evaluate the effectiveness of tirzepatide in individuals without obesity, thereby filling a clinical evidence gap for such individuals, who were underrepresented in the phase 3 trials. Although it was associated with hypoglycaemic efficacy regardless of the baseline body weight of the patient, the reduction in BMI achieved depended on the initial BMI. Notably, dietary consultations played a pivotal role in weight management in the present cohort, thereby emphasizing that pharmacological treatment does not affect the value of lifestyle interventions.

Tirzepatide caused consistent reductions in HbA1c in all the subgroups, consistent with the results of previous trials in East Asia 7 , 8 . Unlike in Western populations, T2D in East Asian patients is characterized by impaired insulin secretion. Previous studies have suggested that Asian patients may have higher sensitivity to incretin‐based therapies, possibly because of specific pathophysiological and/or genetic characteristics, including variations in incretin‐related genes 9 , 10 . We identified significant hypoglycaemic efficacy, even in the Low BMI group, despite minimal weight loss, suggesting that tirzepatide addresses insulin secretory defects independently of its weight‐reducing effect.

In the present study, 43% of the patients had a BMI <23.0 kg/m2. The finding that the reduction in BMI depended on the baseline BMI is clinically reassuring. Mechanistically, tirzepatide‐induced weight loss is driven by reductions in energy intake and fat mass, with GIP agonism potentially promoting adipocyte lipolysis 11 . Because a high baseline BMI can reflect greater fat mass, more pronounced weight reduction in this group is physiologically plausible. Conversely, the attenuated weight loss in the Low BMI group, with no patients becoming underweight (BMI <18.5 kg/m2), might suggest a potential physiological defence mechanism that mitigates the risk of becoming underweight in patients with lower baseline physiques. However, given that all seven patients who discontinued treatment due to adverse events belonged to the Low or Middle BMI groups, clinicians should remain cautious regarding the tolerability of tirzepatide in non‐obese patients, particularly those with a lower BMI. Although the maintenance dose was slightly higher for patients with high baseline BMIs, the multivariate analysis also showed that the baseline BMI was independently associated with the decrease in BMI, irrespective of the dose. This finding suggests that the baseline physique of patients without obesity plays a crucial role in their weight‐loss response to tirzepatide. Multivariate analysis also showed that dietary consultations were independently associated with the reduction in BMI. This represents an important real‐world insight: even when potent agents are administered, lifestyle interventions, the cornerstone of T2D management, remain essential to optimize therapeutic outcomes. Thus, clinicians should remain mindful that pharmacological treatment does not replace the fundamental role of dietary support.

The present study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective observational study without a placebo or untreated control group, making it difficult to determine the net effect of tirzepatide in comparison with usual care. Second, the identified associations—specifically the baseline BMI‐associated weight loss and the link between dietary counselling and the reduction in BMI—should be interpreted with caution. The former may partly reflect statistical phenomena such as “regression to the mean” or baseline‐dependent scaling, and the latter may be influenced by residual confounding, such as by patient motivation or the clinical indication because counselling was provided at the discretion of the physicians rather than through randomization. Additionally, the relatively small sample size is a limitation. Nevertheless, the study provides rare evidence obtained from patients without obesity, including some with a BMI <23 kg/m2, who have been underrepresented in clinical trials. The identification of dietary consultations as a key factor in BMI reductions represents valuable real‐world evidence that reinforces the importance of lifestyle interventions, even when used alongside potent pharmacological agents.

In conclusion, tirzepatide was associated with consistent anti‐hyperglycaemic efficacy in Japanese patients with T2D but no obesity, regardless of their baseline BMI or prior incretin‐based therapy. Although overall weight reduction occurred, the extent of this was significantly influenced by baseline BMI and lifestyle support. These findings suggest that tirzepatide is a useful therapeutic option for patients who do not have obesity, in whom a personalized approach remains essential to optimize metabolic outcomes.
