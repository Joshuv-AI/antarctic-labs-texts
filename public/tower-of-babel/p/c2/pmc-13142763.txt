Bispecific antibodies cross-link and redirect CD8+ T cells to kill cytomegalovirus-infected cells
Authors: Ayub Ali, Arumugam Balamurugan, Francisco J. Ibarrondo, Minh Nguyen, Sara Habibipour, Jaimie M. Lim, Christian Hofmann, Hwee L. Ng, Otto O. Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Adoptive transfer studies of ex vivo–expanded autologous cytomegalovirus (CMV)–specific CD8+ T lymphocytes (CTLs) in immunocompromised hosts demonstrate a central role for viral control, but this therapeutic approach is not generally feasible. We present T cell–redirecting bispecific antibodies (TRBAs) that cross-link CD3ε on CTLs to gH protein on CMV-infected cells, including versions where a single-chain antibody is appended to the carboxyl terminus of the heavy chain of a regular antibody, or where both light and heavy chains have two tandem variable regions in a crossover ordered design. Both versions bind both antigens and mediate the specific clearance of infected cells with the release of interferon-γ when added to CTLs with CMV-infected cells. TRBA-mediated clearance is visualized with physical clustering of CTLs with CMV-infected cells. These results illustrate the mechanism and utility of these TRBAs as potential therapeutic candidates for disseminated CMV infection, with potential advantages compared to two prior CMV-specific TRBAs.

INTRODUCTION

Cellular immunity is the crucial response for clearing virus-infected and malignant cells. Killer CD8+ T lymphocytes (CTLs) in particular have a pivotal role, recognizing and clearing such cells through direct cytolysis and inducing apoptosis. They normally do so by recognizing target cells via T cell receptors (TCRs) that bind specific peptides presented by cell surface human leukocyte antigen class I molecules when such peptides are derived from foreign or abnormal proteins. In general, each effector cell has a single TCR sequence determining its specificity, and TCR engagement sends an activating signal through the associated CD3 complex to trigger its functions including target cell killing and cytokine release.

This mechanism is crucial in the control of human cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection. This highly prevalent virus (1) causes lifetime infection that is contained asymptomatically in the vast majority of persons infected during adulthood, but congenital infection is the most common nongenetic cause of deafness, and disseminated infection can cause severe pathology in persons who are severely immunocompromised. Although there are several drugs available for prophylaxis and the treatment of CMV infection, these agents can have limiting toxicities such as bone marrow suppression and nephrotoxicity, and drug resistance is an increasing problem (2, 3). CMV infection in immunocompromised patients has orphan disease status with the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) (4).

Trials of adoptive transfer of ex vivo–expanded autologous CMV-specific CTLs have demonstrated the importance of CMV-specific CTLs in clearing active infection (5–7) and thus the potential for an immunotherapeutic approach. Broad application, however, is limited by the prolonged time required for isolation and expansion of the cells in the setting of a life-threatening infection. An alternative is redirecting CTLs against CMV-infected cells using chimeric antigen receptors (CARs), which are artificial TCRs that target cell surface proteins. This technology has revolutionized the treatment of B cell–derived malignancies since the first FDA-approved CAR in 2017. To date, a handful of CARs directed against CMV-infected cells has been reported (8–10), but none has progressed to clinical application. Current CAR-T therapy also requires a time-consuming protocol of ex vivo expansion, typically about 28 to 44 days from initial leukapheresis to harvest lymphocytes to reinfusion of the final modified cells (11). Moreover, infusion of these processed cells requires chemotherapeutic lymphodepletion during hospitalization (12). The urgency of disseminated CMV infection in immunocompromised hosts thus limits the feasibility of CAR-T therapy as well.

Given these shortcomings, there has been focus on an alternative method to redirect CTLs against target cells bearing a given surface protein: T cell–redirecting bispecific antibodies (TRBAs). These molecules recruit CTLs to target cells by cross-linking their surface CD3 to a protein on the surface of the target cell, resulting in directed cell killing. The first TRBA to reach the clinic was blinatumomab (13), which was FDA approved in 2014. It consists of a single-chain antibody against CD3 linked to a single-chain antibody against CD19, a subclass of TRBAs termed bispecific T cell engagers (BiTEs), and activates bound CTLs to kill cross-linked B cells. TRBAs act immediately without the need to genetically transduce CTLs. The few TRBAs that have been FDA approved all target malignancies (14), but three experimental TRBA designs against CMV have been reported (15–17). Here, we describe novel TRBAs against CMV with distinct advantages compared to prior strategies.

RESULTS

Genetic construction and production of modified immunoglobulin molecules that bind both the human CD3ε subunit and the human CMV gH protein

We pursued two antibody engineering strategies for creating TRBAs against CMV-infected cells by combining variable regions from the anti-human CD3 antibody 12F6 (18) and the anti-CMV gH antibody 21E9 (19). The first strategy used the “crossover dual variable” (CODV) design where the tandem variable regions on the light and heavy chains are in reverse order from each other (Fig. 1, B and C), as described by Steinmetz et al. (20), to result in the two binding domains being less sterically hindered than a simple tandem approach. This construction was created in two versions corresponding to the two possible orientations of 12F6 and 21E9 variable regions. The second strategy involved appending a single-chain version of 21E9 onto the C terminus of the heavy chain of 12F6 in the context of human immunoglobulin G, subclass 3 (IgG3) (Fig. 1A), as previously described by Coloma and Morrison (21). All three versions were expressed as proteins of about the expected sizes (Fig. 2).

The CMV TRBA constructs bind to both CD3+ and CMV-infected cells

To confirm that these modified antibodies retain the desired specificities of their parental antibodies against human CD3 and CMV gH, they were tested for binding to primary CD8+ T cells (expressing CD3 without CMV infection) or CMV-infected ARPE-19 cells (not expressing CD3) (Fig. 3). All three antibodies demonstrated binding to both CD3-expressing and CMV-infected cells. These results demonstrated that the transferred variable regions retained specificity in the context of both strategies of Steinmetz et al. (20) and Coloma and Morrison (21) for creating bispecific antibodies.

Open histograms indicate negative control staining, while closed histograms indicate staining by the TRBAs. These findings are representative of three independent experiments.

The CMV TRBA constructs mediate clearance by CMV-infected cells in the presence of CTLs

The CMV TRBAs were tested for the ability to mediate the clearance of CMV-infected cells by addition to cocultures of primary CD8+ T cells with ARPE-19 cells that were acutely infected with CMV carrying a green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporter gene (Figs. 4 and 5). When added to these cocultures, each TRBA mediated the dose-dependent clearance of CMV-infected cells, exhibited by the reduced size and number of GFP-expressing cells. By comparison, addition of the control parental monospecific anti-CD3 antibody (12F6) demonstrated some background clearance of infected cells that was much lower than mediated by the TRBAs, presumably through nonspecific activation of the T cells. The dose responsiveness of the three versions of TRBAs was similar, with the Coloma and Morrison (21) version showing possibly greater potency. Overall, these findings showed that all three TRBAs specifically directed CD8+ T cells to clear CMV-infected cells.

The CMV TRBA constructs mediate the specific release of cytokines by T cells in response to CMV-infected cells

To further confirm the activity of the TRBAs, cytokine release by the CD8+ T cells exposed to CMV-infected cells was evaluated (Fig. 6). In the absence of an antibody or in the presence of the control parental anti-CD3 antibody 12F6, low levels of cytokines were detected in the supernatant of CD8+ T cells cocultured with CMV-infected cells. However, in the presence of the TRBAs, there were increased levels of cytokines, indicating specific triggering of the CD8+ T cells when directed by each of the TRBAs against the CMV-infected target cells.

CMV TRBAs elicit CD8+ T cells to release IFN-γ in response to CMV-infected cells.

Each indicated antibody was added to cocultures of primary CD8+ T cells with CMV-infected ARPE-19 cells. Cytokine concentrations are shown (means and standard deviations of triplicates). These findings are representative of three independent experiments.

The CMV TRBA constructs physically recruit T cells to CMV-infected target cells

The interaction of CD8+ T cells cocultured with CMV-infected cells mediated by the TRBAs was assessed by fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 7 and movies S1 and S2). In the absence of antibodies or the presence of the control parental anti-CD3 antibody 12F6, red fluorescence–tagged CD8+ T cells were widely distributed in relationship to the green fluorescent CMV-infected cells. However, in the presence of TRBAs, there was clustering of the CD8+ T cells around the infected cells, which were also smaller and rounded. Furthermore, this clustering was associated with disintegration of CMV-infected cells over time (movies S1 and S2). These findings confirmed the mechanism of action of TRBAs through direct cross-linking of T cells to CMV-infected cells.

DISCUSSION

To date, TRBAs available in the clinic have been focused on the therapy of malignancies, the prototype being blinatumomab (22), an anti-CD3 and anti-CD19 “BiTE” composed of two linked single-chain antibodies. Some investigators have also considered TRBAs for the therapy of viral infections as well, including against HIV-1 (23), hepatitis B virus (24–26), severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (27), and CMV (15–17), although none has advanced to clinical application. TRBAs for CMV are particularly attractive, given the demonstration in adoptive transfer studies that cellular immunity is necessary and sufficient to control disseminated CMV infection. The current study presents new candidate TRBAs for application to clearing CMV infection.

Our constructs differ substantially in design from the three previously reported TRBAs. Lum et al. (17) provided the first proof of concept for a CMV-specific TRBA by chemically linking “Cytogam” (pooled human immunoglobulin selected for CMV seropositivity) with OKT3. Meng et al. (15) used a conventional heterodimeric IgG approach, with one arm targeting CMV gB and the other targeting CD3, with proper pairing enhanced by the “knob-in-hole” approach. Most recently, Brey et al. (16) created a “BiTE” against gB and CD3. By contrast, our designs are bispecific by combining two tandem variable regions in crossed-over orientations on the light versus heavy chains, or appending a single-chain antibody on the C terminus of the heavy-chain constant region. Given the variability in the Lum et al. approach, it would not be amenable to consistent manufacturing for clinical application, but the other two bispecific antibody design strategies have been used in humans for other applications. A major difference of our approach from those two designs is that our constructions have two binding sites each against CMV and CD3, whereas the BiTE and heterodimeric antibody structures have one of each.

In theory, having two binding sites for each target per antibody molecule (rather than one site per target) should translate to greater potency. Our TRBAs each reached peak activity at about 10 to 20 μg/ml in our antiviral assay, which is less potent than the prior reported monoclonal CMV-specific TRBAs that peaked around 120 ng/ml for the design of Meng et al. (15) and 300 ng/ml for the design of Brey et al. (16). Even accounting for the greater molecular mass of our TRBAs of about 210 and 222 kDa for the appended single-chain and CODV designs, respectively, compared to about 150 kDa for the Meng et al. approach and 55 kDa for the Brey et al. approach, our antibodies seem be less potent than those reported values. An important caveat, however, is that these measures of potency are assessed with very different assays that likely do not yield comparable results; Meng et al. (15) assessed the proliferation of infected cells, and Brey et al. (16) used an isotope-labeled cell killing assay. Moreover, the FDA-approved BiTE blinatumomab was shown in vitro to reach maximal killing at about 1 μg/ml (28), which is close to the potency of our TRBAs on a per-molecule basis. Last, relevant to potency in vivo, BiTEs have very short half-lives because they lack Fc domains, only about 2 hours for blinatumomab (29). Our bispecific antibody constructions both have IgG Fc domains and thus substantially longer persistence in vivo and half-lives comparable to wild-type antibodies (21, 30), typically 10 to 14 days. This could be advantageous for more prolonged biologic activity with intermittent dosing compared to BiTEs that require continuous intravenous infusion because of their limited persistence in vivo. Moreover, beyond improved half-life, the presence of the Fc domain could offer additional harnessing of antibody effector function such as antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity to further facilitate the clearance of CMV-infected cells.

Our approach also differs in the targeting of CMV. Our constructions target gH, while the other two monoclonal antibody approaches target gB (15, 16). Both proteins are found on the surface of CMV-infected cells and important for viral infectivity and are targets for neutralizing antibodies. Both are also highly conserved between strains of human CMV (31); thus, it is unclear whether there is an advantage to targeting gH versus gB.

In summary, we present three CMV-specific TRBAs using two bispecific antibody designs. All show the ability to trigger the clearance of CMV-infected cells and cytokine release by CTLs. This activity is marked by physical clustering of the CTLs on the CMV-infected cells, confirming the mechanism of TRBA activity through direct cross-linking of CTLs via binding of CD3 to the infected cells by binding of gH. The relative potency of our TRBAs compared to the two previously reported monoclonal TRBAs (a BiTE and a heterodimeric antibody) is unclear given the different methodologies used to measure their potencies but may be similar to that of the BiTE blinatumomab. In addition, our designs include two binding sites per target antigen, whereas the others have a single site per target antigen, and our designs have Fc domains that confer considerably longer in vivo persistence than BiTEs. Last, the prior CMV-specific monoclonal TRBAs target the viral gB protein, while ours target gH; both proteins are highly conserved and important for CMV infectivity.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Parental antibody sequences

The parental monoclonal antibody against CD3ε was 12F6 (18). The parental monoclonal antibody against CMV gH was 21E9 (19). The light-chain and heavy-chain variable region sequences of these antibodies were used for constructing the CD3-binding and gH-binding portions, respectively, of the TRBAs. The genetic sequences used in these constructions were obtained as commercially synthesized codon-optimized DNA.

Construction of the TRBAs based on the bispecific CODV antibody design of Steinmetz et al.

In separate vectors, genes for tandem light-chain variable regions and heavy-chain variable regions from 21E9 and 12F6 were added to the N termini of human kappa light-chain and IgG3 heavy-chain constant regions, respectively. Two light chains and two heavy chains were created, corresponding to the two possible orders of the variable regions. Linker sequences were inserted between the variable regions and separated variable regions from the constant regions, as reported by Steinmetz et al. (20), using overlapping polymerase chain reaction. The final amino acid sequences of these constructions are shown in figs. S1 to S4. These genes were inserted into the context of the eukaryotic expression vector pCI under the CMV enhancer/promoter (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI).

Construction of the TRBAs based on the bispecific antibody design of Coloma and Morrison

According to the scheme described by Coloma and Morrison (21), this design was engineered by appending a single-chain version of 21E9 to the C terminus of the human IgG3 heavy-chain constant region, where the N terminus was that of 12F6. In a separate vector, the light-chain sequence of 12F6 in the context of the human kappa light-chain constant region was unmodified. The final amino acid sequences of these constructions are shown in figs. S5 and S6. These genes were inserted into the context of the eukaryotic expression vector pCI under the CMV enhancer/promoter (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI).

TRBA production and purification

Expi293F cells were grown in serum-free FreeStyle Expi293 Expression Medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and transfected with corresponding heavy-chain and light-chain plasmids (heavy chain:light chain ratio of 2:1) using the ExpiFectamine 293 Transfection Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) per the manufacturer’s protocol. The transfected cells were cultured in a 125-ml flask on an orbital shaker rotating at 125 rpm in a 37°C incubator with 8% CO2. Six days after transfection, the cell culture supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.2 μM bottle top filter unit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). These supernatant bispecific antibodies were purified by protein A agarose (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After elution from the column with 0.1 M citrate at pH 3.0, the antibodies were desalted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) using an Ultracel-10K column (Millipore-Sigma, St. Louis, MO). Purity was confirmed by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis under reducing conditions. Antibody concentrations were determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) by measuring absorbance at a 562-nm wavelength. The resulting purified antibodies were stored at −80°C in aliquots until use.

Western blot analysis of TRBAs

Western blot analysis was performed using a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated anti-human IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA) and the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc gel imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) as previously described (32). Briefly, the antibodies were run through 10% SDS–polyacrylamide electrophoresis gel, followed by transfer to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Millipore-Sigma, Temeculah, CA). The membrane was then blocked with 5% dried milk for 1 hour and incubated overnight at 4°C with HRP-conjugated human IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA). The following day, the membrane was washed five times with PBS-Tween 20 buffer, and bands were visualized using an HRP chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) on a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc Gel Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA).

Primary CD8+ T cells

Primary CD8+ T cell lines were generated and maintained as previously described (10, 32, 33). Briefly, peripheral blood mononuclear cells with no identifiers from healthy donors were obtained from the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) AIDS Institute Virology Core Facility (Institutional Review Board exempt, not considered to involve human participants). Immunomagnetic purification of CD8+ T cells was performed using the EasySep Human CD8+ T Cell Enrichment Kit (StemCell Technologies, Cambridge, MA), per the manufacturer’s instructions. These cells were cultured with recombinant human interleukin-2, with periodic restimulations using the 12F6 antibody and irradiated feeder peripheral blood mononuclear cells, and verified to be >95% CD3- and CD8-expressing by flow cytometry. They were used at least 14 days after the last restimulation to avoid the background as a result of nonspecific activation.

Flow cytometric assessment of target cell binding by TRBAs

CD8+ T cells were prepared as above, and CMV-infected cells were produced as previously described (10). Briefly, 2 × 106 ARPE-19 cells were seeded in a T25 flask. After 24 hours, 5 × 106 infectious units of the CMV strain TB40/E were added, followed by a 2-hour incubation and then replacement of the medium. Four days later, the cells were mobilized using enzyme-free cell dissociation buffer (Gibco, Waltham, MA) and washed twice with cold PBS. Purified TRBA was added to either CD8+ T cells or CMV-infected ARPE-19 cells at 1 μg/ml in 50 μl for 30 min at 4°C, washed, and counterstained with phycoerythrin-conjugated goat anti-human FAb antibody (10 μg/ml; no. 109-006-003, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA) at 4°C for 30 min. The cells were then washed twice with staining buffer (PBS with 1% fetal bovine serum) and fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde in PBS. Flow cytometry was then performed using a MACSQuant flow cytometer (Miltenyi Biotec, Auburn, CA), and the data were analyzed with FlowJo software (Becton Dickinson, Ashland, OR).

Assays for the clearance of CMV-infected cells

These assays were performed as described in detail in our prior study of CMV-specific CARs (10), with modifications. Briefly, ARPE-19 cells were seeded in 96-well flat-bottom plates at 2 × 104 cells per well and infected the following day with 6 × 104 infectious units of the GFP-expressing strain TB40/E. After 3 days, primary CD8+ T cells were added at 105 cells per well with antibodies at the indicated concentrations. Fluorescence microscopy was performed after one further day with a Keyence BZ-X710 inverted fluorescence microscope equipped with a linear motorized stage (Keyence, Itasca, IL). Fluorescence reflecting CMV-infected cells was quantified using Adobe Photoshop by counting green pixels in the resulting images.

Assay for IFN-γ release by CD8+ T cells in response to TRBAs and CMV-infected cells

ARPE-19 cells were seeded at 104 cells per well in a 96-well flat-bottom plate and infected with 2 × 104 infectious units as described above. Primary CD8+ T cells produced as above were added at 105 cells per well with each TRBA (or a control human isotype control antibody, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA) at 1 μg/ml and incubated for 48 hours, followed by the collection of supernatants for interferon-γ (IFN-γ) quantitation by ELISA [enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay; Human IFN-γ ELISA Kit, per the manufacturer’s instructions (RayBio, Norcross, GA)].

Evaluation for localization of CD8+ T cells with CMV-infected cells in the presence of TRBAs and time-lapse microscopy

Coculture experiments were performed as above in 96-well flat-bottom plates starting with 104 ARPE-19 cells infected with TB40/E, except the CD8+ T cells that were prelabeled with the ethanolic dye PKH26 per the manufacturer’s protocol (Millipore-Sigma, St. Louis, MO). The wells were then assessed by fluorescence microscopy using a BZ-X710 fluorescence microscope (Keyence, Itasca, IL). Time-lapse microscopy was performed using a Keyence BZ-X710 microscope with a 4× objective lens using the Time Lapse Module, Tokai Hit Incubator, Multi-Stack Module, and Image Cytometry Module. Fifteen images per well were captured over ~24 hours and stitched into a movie using the Multi-Stack Module.
