Impact of Expanded Hemodialysis on Subjective Experience Using Dynamic Patient-Reported Outcome Measurement Tool
Authors: Jarrin D Penny, Dariuz Gozdzik, Tanya Tamasi, Justin Dorie, Kathy Koyle, Zabrina Bailey Lozon, Rosemary Cesario, Gihad Nesrallah, Christopher W McIntyre
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationale & ObjectivesCurrent hemodialysis (HD), using conventional high-flux dialyzers, is handicapped by clearance limitations, contributing to poor health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and symptom burden. Recent international consensus guideline-setting efforts have prioritized the identification and management of symptoms and subjective experience, while acknowledging the lack of appropriate tools available to fully appreciate and continuously monitor symptoms.Study DesignMulticenter interventional trial.Setting & ParticipantsForty-seven patients undergoing established, conventional, thrice weekly HD in 2 centers in Ontario, Canada.InterventionsFifteen-month study with the following 5 phases: (1) 1 month observation (high-flux HD), (2) 3 months expanded hemodialysis (HDx); (3) 2 months wash-out (high-flux HD), (4) 6 months HDx, (5) 3 months wash-out (high-flux HD). HRQoL and symptom burden were evaluated using a dynamic patient-reported outcome measurement (PROM) tool throughout the study.OutcomesThis study aimed to use a dynamic PROM to interrogate patient experience and confirm HRQoL and symptom benefit of expanded hemodialysis (HDx) using a medium cutoff dialyzer. Patients undergoing hemodialysis (HD) frequently experience unpleasant symptoms and poor health-related quality of life (HRQoL),1 partially because of accumulated and retained larger-sized uremic toxins poorly cleared by conventional high-flux HD.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Advanced therapies including high-volume exchange hemodiafiltration (to improve solute clearance) are inaccessible to most patients (because of selection criteria, vascular access function, and perceived cost).11, 12, 13 However, medium cutoff (MCO) dialyzers are designed to improve the clearance of larger middle-molecular-weight uremic toxins without the need for added resources, infrastructure or patient-selection criteria.10,14 HD using MCO dialysis membrane has been termed “expanded HD” (HDx), allowing more effective targeting of molecules of up to 45 kDa (principal causes of systemic inflammation, cardiovascular disease, comorbid condition, symptoms, poor HRQoL, and mortality), without the risk of removing essential proteins, such as albumin.7,10,14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20 Unmanaged symptoms and poor HRQoL contribute to the economic burden of health care and social systems as a result of high admission rates and length of stay, polypharmacy, disease burden, and social dependencies.21, 22, 23, 24, 25 Although recent expert-led guideline and consensus initiatives have recognized the importance of managing symptoms, the “what, when, and how” of effective symptoms management remains largely unclear.26,27

Routine symptoms assessment in the HD population is neither standardized nor universally practiced, with unclear guidance on who is ultimately responsible for this aspect of care.1,26,27 Additionally, priorities differ between patient and provider; providers tend to prioritize key performance indicators, numerical values, biomarkers, outcomes, and mortality whereas those living with the disease prioritize how they feel and the impact of the disease on lived experience.1,26 Furthermore, the disease–treatment dyad creates cause and effect complexities with many unanswered questions. Symptom physiology is often not well understood, and treatments are limited in terms of efficacy, durability, and tolerability,1,26,27 resulting in apprehension about treatment options because of uncertainty of their benefit and/or fear of adversity. Extreme variations or fluctuations in symptom severity further distort the ability to identify priorities, patterns, and effective treatment options.

Shifting toward a patient-centered approach to care that includes effective symptoms recognition and management has gained recognition as a framework for the management of disease states. This perspective stresses the importance of patient preference and life goals in decision-making processes aiming to “live well with chronic disease.”1,26,27 Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) provide important information relating to the patient’s subjective experience with the disease, thereby evaluating and supporting health care quality and decision-making.19,20,26,28,29 Traditionally used PROM’s (specific to chronic kidney disease have been used inconsistently in some settings to evaluate HR-QoL and the burden of disease-related symptoms) are cross-sectional, usually capturing the patient’s perspective at one moment in time. PROMs are paper-based, time-consuming, and have lengthy recall periods resulting in a generalized snapshot and ignoring the nuances and fluctuations inherent to intermittent treatment with cyclical variation that fluctuate to extremes. Such tools have limited sensitivity to detect signal of need and/or monitor response to treatment.

The London Evaluation of Illness (LEVIL) is a dynamic instrument, developed specifically (in conjunction with user input) to target symptoms most relevant to HD patients. LEVIL evaluates overall HRQoL, general wellbeing, energy, sleep quality, pruritus, restless leg syndrome (RLS), bodily pain, appetite, mood, shortness of breath, and HD recovery time. LEVIL uses visual analog scales, has a very short recall period (24 hours), and is intended to be used repeatedly, taking only seconds to complete with responses automatically uploaded for real-time viewing. Initial studies using LEVIL proved its acceptability and ease of use, with evidence demonstrating sensitivity to detect clinically relevant changes over both short and long periods of time that correlate with biomarkers of significance.28, 29, 30

This study aimed to use this dynamic PROM tool to iteratively interrogate the patient experience and to confirm previously reported HRQoL benefits of HDx therapy. Furthermore, this study aimed to extend appreciation of effects on patient subjective experience to include durability of effect, variability of negative HRQoL measures, and establish a higher degree of causality by monitoring the effects of withdrawal and reintroduction of therapy.

Methods

The study was conducted according to International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki with the appropriate ethical approvals. All patients gave written informed consent before participation. The trial was registered at clinicaltrials.gov as NCT03640858.

Study Design

This was a multicenter, unblinded interventional study in the prevalent adult HD population in Ontario, Canada conducted over a 2-year period. There were 5 phases in this 15-month study (Table 1).

Study Population

Patients were approached if they were aged >18 years and receiving chronic thrice weekly conventional high-flux HD for >3 months. Forty-seven patients consented to participate; 11 participants were lost throughout the course of the study for unrelated reasons (Fig 1). A total of 36 patients completed the study with full analysis.

CONSORT diagram.

Outcomes

The primary objective was to evaluate changes in patient-reported symptoms and overall HRQoL, durability of changes, and variability in symptoms with conversion between conventional high-flux HD and HDx therapy (MCO) using LEVIL, a dynamic PROM.

Dialysis Treatments

Dialysis treatments were delivered using Fresenius 5008 or Baxter Artis dialysis monitors (dependent on participating site). Mean treatment time was 3.7 ± 0.4 hours thrice weekly, dialysate temperature range was 35-36.5 °C, dialysate sodium ranged from 135-140 mmol/L, dialysate potassium was either 1.5, 2.0, or 3.0 mmol/L, dialysate calcium was 1.25 mmol/L, and bicarbonate ranged from 34-40mmol/L. Net ultrafiltration was calculated individually according to prescribed dry weight following unit protocols. Dialyzers (high-flux and MCO) were changed according to study protocol (maintaining surface area) with no additional changes to the dialysis prescription.

Dynamic PROM: LEVIL

The LEVIL application was installed onto a study-dedicated tablet. Anonymized study specific IDs were assigned to each participant and entered into the application by the study coordinators or researcher immediately before each LEVIL entry. The tablet was then handed to each participant to complete the questionnaire, with entries submitted in real-time. If a patient was not able to complete the survey independently completion (4 participants because of visual impairment), assistance was provided in a consistent manner at the most minimal level required to allow.

Using the visual analog scale, participants responded to questions rating the following:

Participants continued to enter symptoms using LEVIL (once to thrice weekly) throughout the entire study period (15 months).

LEVIL Data Outputs

Each LEVIL entry was transformed into a numeric output (not visible to user) between 0 (poor) and 100 (excellent) for each of the 9 visual analog questions. Overall HRQoL was calculated by combining all symptom domain scores together (automatically generated by LEVIL). Monthly scores were averaged for data analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics are reported as mean ± standard deviation or median as indicated for continuous variables as appropriate. Categorical variables are stated as frequency and percentages. Baseline scores (phase 1) were used to stratify groups (“LOW” = average LEVIL score <70, “HIGH” = average LEVIL score ≥70).

Repeated measures analysis of changes in mean scores for overall HRQoL and each symptom domain over time for the entire cohort and within stratified groups was conducted using one-way analysis of variance (Table S1). Symptom variability was evaluated by calculating the mean and using the standard deviation to assess the spread of data for each symptom over time. Percentage change of the mean was used to compare scores between phases of the study to evaluate effect size, durability, and carryover (Table S2). A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.0 (GraphPad Software).

Results

Baseline Characteristics of Study Population

The mean age of the study population was 62 ± 15.4 years (median 64.5 years) with mean HD vintage of 52 ± 52 months (range, 9-303 months). There were no differences between stratified groups (HIGH and LOW, both P = 0.85). Half of the population was woman, and 50% had type 2 diabetes mellitus. Common etiologies included diabetes mellitus (39%) and hypertension (47%) (Table 2).

LEVIL Input

On average, participants completed LEVIL 1.8 ± 0.8 times per week, requiring less <1 minute to complete, with no survey fatigue or refusal.

Stratification of Population Based on LEVIL Outputs

Participants were stratified into 2 groups: LOW (<70) and HIGH (≥70) LEVIL scores at baseline (based on user input). This stratification mirrored previous methods and was validated by a focus group who deemed a score of ≥70 to be acceptable for ‘living well’ with chronic kidney disease or a chronic disease.29

Dynamic PROM description of population

Fifty percent of the population experienced LOW HRQoL, with 7.1 symptom domains below the stratification threshold compared with only 1.8 symptom domains below threshold for those with HIGH HRQoL (P < 0.0001). Among those with LOW HRQoL, 13 participants had ≥7 domains below threshold, and 3 participants had all 9 symptom domains below threshold. The symptoms contributing to low HR-QoL include general wellbeing (100% of the stratified group), low energy (100%), poor sleep quality (94%), pruritus (78%), bodily pain (78%), poor appetite (78%), low mood (78%), and RLS (61%). Participants considered to have ‘HIGH’ HR-QoL were also affected by symptom of low energy (39%), poor sleep quality (39%), and pruritus (39%).

Phase 1: Baseline Characteristics (High-Flux HD)

At baseline, overall HRQoL, measured as the average of all 9 symptom domains, had a mean LEVIL score of 68.4 ± 9.1 (Table S1). In this phase, 50% of the participants were classified as having LOW HRQoL, with a mean score of 53.5 ± 13.5. For general wellbeing, the mean LEVIL score was 66.7 ± 21.4, with half of the population in the LOW group, scoring an average of 50.2 ± 13.7. In the energy domain, the LEVIL score was 58.4 ± 23.4, and 69% of patients in the LOW group had a mean score of 46 ± 15.4. Sleep quality showed a mean LEVIL score of 55.7 ± 30.2, with 67% of the patients in the LOW group having a mean score of 38.5 ± 20.4. For pruritus, the mean LEVIL score was 62.6 ± 27.9, and 58% of participants in the LOW category had a mean score of 41.6 ± 13.8. Bodily pain was characterized by a LEVIL score of 69.4 ± 25.1, with 47% of the patients in the LOW group scoring a mean of 47.7 ± 15.2. The RLS domain had a mean LEVIL score of 72.7 ± 29.4, and 39% of individuals in the LOW group had a mean score of 40.8 ± 19.6. Appetite was reported with a mean LEVIL score of 74.8 ± 23.6, with 42% of patients in the LOW group having a mean score of 50.5 ± 14. In terms of breathing, the LEVIL score was 83.3 ± 23.3, and 25% of patients in the LOW category had a mean score of 46.7 ± 13.7. Finally, for mood, the mean LEVIL score was 71.6 ± 22.7, with 47% of the participants in the LOW group showing a mean score of 51.5 ± 15. The mean recovery time at baseline was 5.7 ± 5.8 hours (Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4).

Phase 2: 3 Months of HDx Therapy

During phase 2, when patients received 3 months of HDx therapy, overall HRQoL improved by 9%, increasing from a baseline mean of 68.4 ± 9.1 to 74.5 ± 16.5. All symptom domains showed improvement with this intervention, and these enhancements were sustained throughout phase 2 when compared to baseline measurements. The most significant improvements were observed in the domains of sleep quality, which improved by 22% to a mean of 68 ± 26.2; pruritus, which improved by 18% to a mean score of 74.1 ± 22.1; energy, which improved by 17% to 58.5 ± 21.6; and general wellbeing, which improved by 12% to 74.7 ± 18.1. In contrast, bodily pain, RLS, appetite, and mood showed only minimal improvements, ranging between 2% and 7%. Additionally, post-HD recovery time improved by 16%, decreasing to an average of 4.8 ±5.7 hours. Variability in overall HRQoL improved (reduction) by 18% compared with baseline, with a reduction in symptom variability observed across most domains. Specifically, there were decreases in variability of general wellbeing (31%), energy (30%), bodily pain (24%), pruritus (21%), mood (16%), sleep quality (14%), and appetite (14%). Within the subset of participants classified as the LOW group, overall HRQoL improved by 18% compared with baseline. The most significant improvements among these patients were seen in pruritus (which improved by 53%), sleep quality (45%), RLS and breathing (both by 34%), general wellbeing and energy (each by 31%), bodily pain (26%), and appetite (17%) (Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4).

Phase 3: 2-Month Transition Back to High-Flux HD

During the 2-month wash-out period in phase 3, when patients transitioned from HDx therapy back to high-flux HD, LEVIL scores gradually worsened; however, they did not revert all the way back to baseline levels. The domains of sleep quality and pruritus were the most affected by the return to the high-flux membrane, worsening by 5% and 7%, respectively. Additionally, the post-HD recovery time increased by 4% during this phase. There was also an increase in variability during the wash-out period; variability in appetite increased by 11%, energy levels by 4%, and sleep quality by 2%. Within the LOW group, the return to high-flux HD led to notable worsening of symptoms including pruritus by 14%, sleep quality by 6%, and bodily pain by 5% (Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4).

Phase 4: 6-Month Return to HDx Therapy

On reintroduction of HDx therapy in phase 4, overall HRQoL once again improved by 10%, and sustained improvements in symptom domains were observed over time. The most pronounced effects were seen in pruritus, which improved by 18%; sleep quality, by 15%; bodily pain, by 13%; general wellbeing, by 10%; and RLS, by 10%. There were modest improvements in energy (9%), mood (8%), and appetite (5%). The post-HD recovery time also improved by 12% relative to baseline. Statistically significant improvements in both HRQoL and all symptom domains were evident between 8 and 12 weeks into this phase when compared with baseline data. Furthermore, during this phase, variability in symptom scores improved across most domains, with reductions ranging between 14% and 31%. The greatest reduction in variability was observed for pruritus (35%) and sleep quality (32%), followed by appetite (20%), mood (18%), energy (17%), RLS (14%), general wellbeing (13%), and bodily pain (8%). For the LOW group, improvements were notable, with overall HRQoL increasing by 18% and specific domains improving as follows: pruritus by 32%, bodily pain by 23%, RLS by 22%, sleep quality by 19%, general wellbeing by 18%, mood by 17%, energy by 14%, and appetite by 9% (Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4).

Phase 5: Final Return to High-flux HD (3 Months)

In the final phase, when patients returned to high-flux HD for a 3-month wash-out period, symptom scores worsened. Notably, sleep quality declined by 13%, pruritus by 11%, energy by 9%, and general wellbeing by 8%, whereas RLS and overall HRQoL each declined by 5%. In this phase, the post-HD recovery time was 16% longer, averaging 5.8 ± 7.7 hours (similar to baseline measures). Additionally, variability in symptom measures increased during the wash-out phase; overall HRQoL variability increased by 6%, pruritus variability by 47%, and sleep quality variability by 15%. Among participants in the LOW group, overall HRQoL worsened by 7% during the wash-out period, and there were additional declines in sleep quality by 13%, pruritus by 11%, RLS by 9%, energy by 8%, general wellbeing by 7%, bodily pain by 6%, appetite by 6%, and mood by 5% (Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4).

Discussion

This study successfully used a dynamic PROM tool to capture the subjective experience of HD patients over a 15-month period. We demonstrated that HDx therapy using MCO technology is associated with significant improvement in HRQoL and a variety of symptoms. The withdrawal and reintroduction results, coupled with effect requiring several weeks of therapy to become evident, are supportive of clearance-based benefit. Benefit was durable with continued therapy and variability in day-to-day fluctuations of distressing symptoms, and HRQoL measures were substantially reduced.

HD patients report experiencing ∼9 symptoms simultaneously, the most predominant being fatigue,26 likely to be a consequence of a combination or cluster of symptoms.1,26,27,31 Experts highlight the importance of improving systems and clinical pathways through an individual approach to achieve effective symptoms management.26 Traditional PROMs are not designed to represent a patient’s changing condition or response to treatment.28,29 Additionally, effective treatment strategies for the management of symptoms are lacking, fail to deliver, or lose effectiveness over time, resulting in interventions and medications being used with little to no benefit. Moreover, there is currently no readily available objective way to measure treatment efficacy and durability.

Two studies have assessed the impact of HDx therapy on patient-reported outcomes and found that cross-sectional use of the Kidney Disease Quality of Life 36-item survey and 12 months HDx improved disease effects and burden of disease as well as severity of RLS.19 Another study demonstrated that 12 weeks of HDx improved physical function and physical role with the added benefit of reducing pruritus and nocturnal scratching.17 An additional case report described the resolution of debilitating pruritus, healing vesicular skin lesions, and a reduction in soft tissue sodium accumulation as evidenced by Na23 magnetic resonance imaging with use of MCO membrane.30

HDx therapy has proven to be safe and effective, providing clearance of intermediate to large molecular weight uremic toxins within the footprint of routine conventional HD.9,10,16, 17, 18, 19 MCO membrane use has been associated with reductions in hospital admission rates and length of stay, cardiovascular events, medication requirements, and mortality, and has recently been shown to slow the loss of residual kidney function when compared to conventional high-flux-HD.23, 24, 25,32, 33, 34 Furthermore, HDx therapy effectively targets a range of commonly experienced symptoms that conventional high-flux HD manages inadequately.17,19,29,35 The introduction of a clearance-based effect profile (6-8 weeks) was initially introduced by our group using LEVIL29,30; however, durability of effect and impact on symptom variability has not been evaluated.

This study (supportive of previous research29) found that HDx therapy significantly improves HRQoL and symptom burden for all HD patients with no detrimental effects. Moreover, patients experiencing extreme burden (having ∼7 symptoms simultaneously) showed enhanced benefit with HDx therapy. Poor HRQoL was largely driven by low LEVIL scores in general wellbeing, energy, sleep quality, pruritus, bodily pain, and RLS. HDx therapy effectively impacted these distressing symptoms within ∼6-8 weeks, with durable improvement (with continued use of MCO) and gradual, similarly profiled loss of effect (which did not reach baseline levels after 3 months). Furthermore, HDx therapy had a remarkable impact on symptom variability that was equally durable, facilitating a more predictable life experience and enhanced life engagement.

Although this was designed as a multicenter trial, only 2 participating sites contributed (because of restrictions, priority shifts, and process delays related to the COVID-19 pandemic). We did not institute a blinded study design because of complexity and lack of available anonymized product; however, we incorporated wash-out and return to intervention to partially overcome this limitation (following Koch’s postulates for determining causality). Results derived from this study will inform future HDx therapy research focusing on establishing additional effects (eg, cognition, sexual desire, prolonged use) and guide clinical decision making related to effective symptoms management.

In conclusion, this study has contributed to the unanswered ‘what, when, and how’ of effective symptoms management in the HD population. The dynamic PROM used in this study, LEVIL, determined that HDx therapy is associated with clinically meaningful improvement, with durable effects, in HRQoL, distressing symptoms, and symptom variability, greatly benefiting those with increased suffering. LEVIL is sensitive to a patient’s changing conditions enabling individualized, real-time, focused care with the ability to measure efficacy of treatment. LEVIL can be easily integrated into routine clinical care, enabling targeted selection of patients most likely to benefit from HDx therapy and allowing intelligent utilization of limited resources.
