Endoscopic Features of Background Gastritis Associated With Remnant Gastric Cancer: A Multicenter Retrospective Study

ABSTRACT
PurposeWe identified the risk factors for remnant gastric cancer (RGC) based on remnant gastric mucosal characteristics and gastritis morphology in patients undergoing distal gastrectomy.Materials and MethodsThis multicenter retrospective study included 100 patients with RGC after distal gastrectomy and 550 patients without RGC treated between 2013 and 2020. Endoscopic findings, including anastomotic redness, red streaks, enlarged folds, bile reflux as anastomotic findings, as well as disappearance of the regular arrangement of collecting venules (RAC), atrophic gastritis, and intestinal metaplasia as background gastric mucosal findings, were evaluated. Disease risk score matching (1:1) was adjusted for baseline characteristics. Logistic regression analysis was used to develop a risk score model to stratify RGC risk into low, moderate, and high categories.ResultsAfter matching, 96 patients with RGC and 96 controls were analyzed. Anastomotic redness and red streaks, as well as the disappearance of RAC and atrophic gastritis, were significantly more frequent in the RGC group than in the control group, whereas enlarged folds and bile reflux showed no significant differences. Risk scores were assigned as follows: anastomotic redness, 2; red streaks, 3; disappearance of RAC, 7; and atrophic gastritis, 3. The total score stratified patients into high (≥15), moderate (7–14), and low risk (≤6). The positive and negative predictive values were 67.7% and 83.3%, respectively.ConclusionsThe endoscopic findings of anastomotic redness, red streaks, RAC disappearance, and atrophic gastritis were significantly associated with RGC development. The proposed risk-scoring model could serve as a stratification tool for RGC surveillance.Trial RegistrationUniversity Hospital Medical Information Network Identifier: UMIN000055884

INTRODUCTION

Gastric cancer remains a major global health concern, with over one million new cases and approximately 769,000 deaths in 2020, ranking fifth in incidence and fourth in mortality [1]. Its incidence has been reduced by the eradication of Helicobacter pylori [2]; however, the relative incidence of remnant gastric cancer (RGC) is increasing. A nationwide Japanese survey reported an RGC incidence of 2.4% in patients who underwent distal gastrectomy (DG) with >5 years of follow-up between 2003 and 2012 [3]. This is attributed to improved postoperative survival owing to advancements in chemotherapy and an increasing number of function-preserving gastrectomies [4].

RGC is associated with bile reflux and remnant gastritis following gastrectomy [56]. The risk of remnant gastritis and RGC varies depending on the reconstruction method used. As the Billroth I (B-I) and II (B-II) methods cause more bile reflux and remnant gastritis, a higher possibility of RGC occurring near the anastomosis exists [78]. Roux-en-Y (R-Y) reconstruction, recognized as a reconstruction method for total gastrectomy, has been widely adopted for DG to reduce bile/pancreatic juice reflux. R-Y reconstruction is superior to B-I and B-II reconstruction in preventing bile reflux and remnant gastritis [9]. However, depending on the reconstruction method, it is difficult to conduct a simple comparison of the occurrence of RGC because each method has a different historical background, such as the time period from initial surgery.

In 2014, the Kyoto classification of gastritis based on endoscopic characteristics of H. pylori infection-associated gastritis was developed by Haruma et al. [10], allowing the grading of endoscopically visible risk factors for gastric cancer development [101112]. This classification divides patients into 3 groups: H. pylori-negative patients (no gastritis), patients with current H. pylori infection (active gastritis), and patients previously infected with H. pylori (inactive gastritis). The scoring of 5 parameters of gastritis (atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, enlarged folds, nodularity, and diffuse redness) provides an estimate of the risk of gastric cancer. Subsequent large-cohort verification of 6,718 patients revealed that an endoscopy-based diagnosis of gastritis can stratify gastric cancer risks [13]. However, remnant gastritis is largely affected by bile reflux, and the Kyoto classification cannot be used directly to assess the risk of developing RGC. Therefore, classifying and grading the characteristics of remnant gastritis is necessary to estimate the risk of developing RGC.

This multicenter study identified the risk factors for RGC development based on background remnant gastric mucosa and remnant gastritis morphology in patients who underwent gastric resection, including endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study protocol

We retrospectively analyzed 108 patients who underwent radical resection, including ESD, for RGC after DG at Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine and 9 collaborating institutions between January 2013 and December 2020. In addition, we investigated 614 patients who remained free of RGC during postoperative surveillance after DG at the Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine during the same period. Patients with poor endoscopic observation, those within 1 year of the initial DG diagnosis, and those with unknown data were excluded (Fig. 1). Gastric cancer treatment was performed at all institutions in accordance with the Japanese Gastric Cancer Treatment Guidelines (7th edition) [14]. The term “remnant gastric cancer” in the Japanese Gastric Cancer Association’s “Japanese Classification of Gastric Carcinoma” is defined as all cancers arising from the remnant stomach after partial gastrectomy, regardless of the initial disease or surgery, including local recurrence in the gastric stump after partial gastrectomy for gastric cancer [15]. This study included patients who developed gastric cancer after DG for gastric cancer or ulceration. In cases in which DG was performed for gastric cancer, all patients underwent R0 resection.

Study flow chart. Of the 108 patients with RGC at 9 institutions, 100 were enrolled in the RGC (+) group after excluding 8 cases in which endoscopic observation was insufficient. Of the 614 patients without RGC after DG at Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine, 23 cases occurred within 1 year of the initial DG, 25 cases whose details of the initial DG were unknown, and 16 cases in which endoscopic observation was insufficient. A total of 550 patients were enrolled in the RGC (II) group. After disease score analysis with 1:1 matching, 96 patients from each group were included.

Data regarding clinicopathological findings, surgical findings, and postoperative clinical courses were obtained from the medical records and databases of each institution and described according to the Japanese Classification of Gastric Carcinoma (third edition) [15].

Endoscopy

Expert endoscopists performed the gastroduodenal endoscopy at each institution. Because endoscopic findings could change depending on the time elapsed after gastrectomy, patients within 1 year of the initial DG diagnosis were excluded, and endoscopic findings in patients with RGC were evaluated immediately before treatment. The most recent endoscopic findings were assessed in patients without RGCs. For all cases selected by disease risk score matching, endoscopic findings, including anastomotic redness, red streaks, enlarged folds, bile reflux, disappearance of regular arrangement of collection venules (RAC), atrophic gastritis, and intestinal metaplasia, were evaluated by expert endoscopists and surgeons (F.H. and O.T.), blinded to the patients’ backgrounds. Endoscopic findings were classified into 2 to 4 grades, and cut-off values were determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1). The correlation between the presence or absence of each endoscopic finding and RGC was evaluated.

Anastomotic redness

Anastomotic redness indicates circumferential redness of the anastomosis between the remnant stomach and the duodenum/small intestine. Severe redness was scored as 2 points, mild redness as 1 point, and no redness as 0 (Supplementary Fig. 1A). The cut-off was determined using the ROC analysis, with 1 and 2 points defined as positive and 0 as negative, respectively.

Red streaks

Red streaks indicate redness in the form of several stripes running longitudinally from the anastomosis and parallel to the longitudinal direction of the residual stomach. Red streaks are usually observed in H. pylori-uninfected mucosa without gastritis [16]. This is the redness on the surface that contacts the gastric juice and refluxed bile when the stomach contracts, and is thought to be caused by microvascular congestion (capillary network) on the mucosal surface. A red streak observed up to the fornix was scored as 3 points, one case observed up to the residual gastric body was scored as 2, one case observed near the anastomosis only was scored as 1, and one in which no red streaks were observed was scored as 0 (Supplementary Fig. 1B). The cut-off was determined using the ROC analysis, with 1, 2, and 3 points defined as positive, and 0 as negative.

Enlarged folds

Enlarged folds indicate folds of the greater curvature of the body that are not flattened or are only partially flattened due to stomach insufflation. The presence of enlarged folds was scored as 1 point (positive), and the absence was scored as 0 points (negative) (Supplementary Fig. 1C).

Bile reflux

However, the definition of bile reflux has not yet been established. In this study, patients with bile retention in the remnant stomach at the time of endoscopic insertion were classified as having bile reflux. Severe bile retention in the remnant stomach was scored as 2 points, mild bile retention as 1, and no retention as 0 (Supplementary Fig. 1D). The cut-off was determined using the ROC analysis, with 1 and 2 points defined as positive and 0 as negative, respectively.

Disappearance of the RAC

RAC is a condition in which collecting venules are arranged in the corpus, and is a typical endoscopic finding in normal gastric mucosa not infected with H. pylori [17]. Disappearance of the RAC was scored as 1 point (positive) or 0 points (negative) (Supplementary Fig. 1E).

Atrophic gastritis

Atrophic gastritis is characterized by the loss of folds in the lesser curvature of the gastric body and mottled yellowish-pale mucosa, accompanied by a visible capillary network due to atrophy. It is considered characteristic of gastritis caused by H. pylori infection [18]. Cases with atrophic gastritis ≥80% were scored as 2 points, <80% as 1, and no atrophy as 0 (Supplementary Fig. 1F). The cut-off was determined using the ROC analysis, with 2 points defined as positive and 0 and 1 as negative.

Intestinal metaplasia

Intestinal metaplasia refers to the replacement of the gastric mucosa by the intestinal epithelium due to different causes, such as chronic atrophic gastritis, decreased gastric acid secretion, and dietary and lifestyle factors. It typically appears grayish–white, with slightly elevated plaques surrounded by mixed patchy pink and pale mucosal areas, forming an irregular, uneven surface. Patients with intestinal metaplasia ≥80% were scored as 2 points, those with intestinal metaplasia <80% as 1, and those without intestinal metaplasia as 0 (Supplementary Fig. 1G). The cut-off was determined using the ROC analysis, with 1 and 2 points defined as positive and 0 as negative, respectively.

Diagnosis of H. pylori infection using endoscopic findings

As anastomotic findings are greatly affected by bile reflux, H. pylori infection was evaluated using 3 endoscopic findings in the background gastric mucosa: disappearance of RAC, diffuse atrophic gastritis, and extensive intestinal metaplasia. Diffuse atrophy and intestinal metaplasia have high specificity for the diagnosis of past and/or inactive H. pylori infection [19], whereas the disappearance of the RAC has a high sensitivity for non-infection [1720]. Endoscopic findings were compared between patients with and without RGC. The area under the curve maximized in the ROC curve was set as the cut-off value for each endoscopic finding score (Supplementary Fig. 1). Furthermore, we divided the endoscopic findings into anastomotic (anastomotic redness, red streaks, enlarged folds, and bile reflux) and background gastric mucosal findings (disappearance of RAC, atrophic gastritis, and intestinal metaplasia) and examined the relationship between different reconstruction methods and the presence or absence of bile reflux (Fig. 2).

Data analysis

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using JMP software (v.17; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All P-values were 2-sided, and the significance level was set at 0.05.

Disease risk score matching

We used logistic regression for the following factors in disease risk score calculations to balance the differences in baseline characteristics due to selection bias between RGC (+) and (−): age, sex, histology of primary disease (malignant vs. benign), and period from initial surgery. Patients were 1:1 matched using nearest neighbor matching with a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the disease risk score. Subsequently, endoscopic findings of the gastric remnant and clinicopathological factors were retrospectively compared.

RGC risk score creation

RGC risk scoring was based on parameter estimates derived from a multivariate logistic regression analysis of endoscopic findings that demonstrated a significant association with the occurrence of RGC. The estimated values were converted to integers while maintaining the ratio, and were used as RGC risk scores for each endoscopic finding. The total RGC risk score for patients with RGC (+) and (−) was calculated, and the value with the largest difference was used to classify the risk of developing RGC into 3 categories: low, moderate, and high risks.

To confirm the reliability of the RGC risk score, the patients were classified into low-, moderate-, and high-risk groups after matching using the risk score model, and the probability of developing RGC was calculated.

Ethical approval, human rights, and informed consent

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines for medical research on humans laid out in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was designed by the authors and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine (ERB-C-2137) and Institutional Review Boards of all collaborating institutions (Japanese Red Cross Society Kyoto Daiichi Hospital, 1339; Omihachiman Community Medical Center, R3-25; Kyoto Kizugawa Hospital, 2021-6; JCHO Kyoto Kuramaguchi Medical Center, 2021120801; Kyoto Saiseikai Hospital, R4-1; Fukuchiyama City Hospital, 3-31; North Medical Center Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine, ERB-C-2137; and Maizuru Medical Center, R3-19). Informed consent was obtained by providing an overview of the study on the hospital’s website along with an opportunity for participants to opt out. Participants who did not opt out were considered to have provided consent. This study was registered in the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN) Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000055884).

Comparison of endoscopic findings between RGC (+) and (−) groups

Gastritis was evaluated separately based on the anastomotic findings (anastomotic redness, red streaks, enlarged folds, and bile reflux) and background gastric mucosal findings (disappearance of the RAC, atrophic gastritis, and intestinal metaplasia).

No significant difference was observed between the RGC (+) and (−) groups regarding enlarged folds and bile reflux (P=0.647 and P=0.768, respectively). Among the 3 background gastric mucosal findings, the disappearance of RAC and atrophic gastritis was significantly higher in the RGC (+) group than in the RGC (−) group (P=0.001 and P=0.004, respectively). RGC risk scoring

Regarding the endoscopic findings demonstrating a significant correlation with RGC, the parameter estimates for each finding were calculated using a logistic regression model, resulting in values of 0.198 for anastomotic redness, 0.313 for red streaks, 0.663 for the disappearance of RAC, and 0.264 for atrophic gastritis. When these values were converted into integers by multiplying by 10 while maintaining the ratio, the RGC scores were 2, 3, 7, and 2 points for anastomotic redness, red streaks, RAC disappearance, and atrophic gastritis, respectively (Table 5). Furthermore, when the occurrence rate of RGC for each score was stratified into high, moderate, and low risks, it was classified into the following: high risk, 15 points; moderate, 7–14; and low, ≤6.

Reliability verification for the new RGC risk score

The occurrence rate of RGC was examined to verify the RGC reliability score. When the RGC risk score of each patient was calculated and divided into 3 groups (high, moderate, and low risks), the score of the RGC (+) group was significantly higher than that of the RGC (−) group (P<0.001), and 69.8% of cases in the of the RGC (+) group were classified as high risk. DISCUSSION

We retrospectively verified the risk of RGC development based on the background gastric mucosa and morphology of remnant gastritis. Our analysis identified 2 anastomotic findings (anastomotic redness and red streaks) and 2 background mucosal findings (disappearance of RAC and atrophic gastritis), which are characteristic RGC findings. In addition, bile reflux strongly correlated with anastomotic findings. Furthermore, we calculated the parameter estimates for each finding using a logistic regression model and created an RGC risk score model to evaluate these endoscopic findings, and found a correlation with RGC.

Several studies on the risk of developing RGC are available, suggesting the involvement of the type of initial gastrectomy and bile reflux [21]. Takeno et al. [7] reported that patients who underwent initial gastrectomy for gastric cancer were at risk of developing RGC, and that RGC tended to occur near the site of gastrojejunostomy in B-II. They mentioned that the reason for this was that B-II reconstruction caused more bile reflux. To address this, the Braun anastomosis was developed. However, the effectiveness of these methods remains limited [22]. Furthermore, certain studies compared R-Y with B-I in terms of postoperative endoscopic findings at least 1 year after surgery and reported that R-Y was associated with less bile reflux and remnant gastritis [232425]. In Japan, R-Y reconstruction after DG has been widely used to avoid bile reflux and the resulting residual gastritis, anastomotic ulcers, and RGC. A nationwide survey by the Japanese Society for the Study of Postoperative Morbidity after Gastrectomy involving 145 institutions reported that B-I was selected in 74% of the patients, whereas R-Y was chosen in 21% as the primary reconstruction method after DG [8]. Kubo et al. [26] proposed the residue, gastritis, and bile (RGB) classification in 2002. They classified the amount of residual food into 5 grades, the degree and extent of gastritis (redness and erosion) into 5 grades, and bile reflux into 2 grades. They used this classification to suggest that B-I reconstruction was associated with significantly more residual food and remnant gastritis than R-Y reconstruction. However, this classification does not consider the risk of developing RGC. In this study, bile reflux may not be a direct cause of RGC, as no difference was observed between RGC (+) and (−). However, because bile reflux demonstrated a strong correlation with anastomotic redness and red streaks, it is undoubtedly an indirect risk factor for RGC. Furthermore, because the R-Y group had a significantly lower bile reflex, our results may serve as the basis for selecting R-Y reconstruction after DG to prevent RGC by inhibiting bile reflux.

Considering the etiology of RGC, its relationship with H. pylori infection is essential. The relationship between gastric cancer and H. pylori infection is evident, and the diagnosis of H. pylori infection based on endoscopic findings is significant in evaluating the risk of gastric cancer. The Kyoto classification of gastritis provides a framework for classifying H. pylori infections based on endoscopic features and facilitates the histological diagnosis of gastritis [10111227]. RAC is a crucial characteristic endoscopic finding in patients without H. pylori infection and disappears after H. pylori infection. In addition, atrophic gastritis and intestinal metaplasia from H. pylori-induced chronic inflammation are major histological risk factors for gastric cancer, underscoring the need to prevent the occurrence of these precancerous conditions and reduce subsequent progression to cancer [282930]. Ohira et al. [6] proposed that atrophic gastritis and intestinal metaplasia may be risk factors for RGC, suggesting that H. pylori infection is associated with RGC development even after DG. Thus, H. pylori infection is an important factor in remnant gastritis; however, bile reflux is thought to have a significant effect. To address this, we developed a novel RGC risk score model incorporating both H. pylori- and anastomosis-related factors. Our RGC risk score revealed that patients classified as high risk (score=15) exhibited both H. pylori-associated and anastomosis-related findings. Approximately 70% of patients with RGC are at a high risk, indicating that although bile reflux has a substantial impact, H. pylori infection also plays a significant role. The need to establish an optimal endoscopic surveillance strategy after gastrectomy is proposed [6]. Another clinical application of this study is that it provides evidence supporting the need to undergo endoscopic surveillance more frequently and for a longer duration beyond the 5 years after the initial gastrectomy in patients estimated to have a high RGC risk score, as opposed to the recommendation in the Japanese guidelines, which state that endoscopic examination should be performed as needed if RGC is detected within 5 years [14].

Therefore, arguing that H. pylori eradication is necessary to prevent RGC is reasonable. A large cohort study demonstrated that H. pylori eradication after gastrectomy resulted in significant benefits in overall and gastric cancer-specific survival rates [313233]. Kim et al. [34] reported that H. pylori eradication within 1 year of gastric cancer treatment reduces the risk of developing metachronous gastric neoplasms compared to late eradication therapy in patients undergoing endoscopic resection and surgery. Furthermore, the Maastricht IV/Florence Consensus Report and Second Asia-Pacific Consensus Guidelines strongly recommend H. pylori eradication in patients with previous gastric neoplasia treated with endoscopic or subtotal gastric resection [3536]. However, the preventive effects of H. pylori eradication using medication on the occurrence of metachronous gastric cancer after gastrectomy remain controversial. Although many patients who undergo gastrectomy have H. pylori infection, several previous studies have reported that H. pylori eradication may not reduce the risk of metachronous gastric cancer in patients who have already developed atrophic gastritis and/or intestinal metaplasia [373839]. The latest Japanese gastric cancer treatment guidelines state that a clear recommendation cannot be made regarding H. pylori eradication after gastrectomy because it is unclear whether H. pylori eradication after gastrectomy suppresses the occurrence of metachronous gastric cancer and improves prognosis [14]. Another controversial point is that changes in H. pylori infection status after gastrectomy are associated with dramatic changes in bile reflux and acid secretion, which may inhibit the growth of H. pylori in the residual stomach [40414243]. However, in this study, the high-risk group included patients who exhibited anastomotic changes associated with bile reflux and simultaneously presented with H. pylori infection. This indicates that bile reflux does not necessarily lead to H. pylori eradication, and suggests that H. pylori eradication may significantly contribute to the prevention of RGC in this population. Given the previous reports and the results of our study, if the disappearance of RAC or atrophic gastritis is found on endoscopy after gastrectomy or if patients are determined to be at high risk of RGC, H. pylori testing should be performed. If positive, patients should undergo H. pylori eradication and endoscopic follow-up until RAC reappears and atrophic gastritis resolves.

This study had certain limitations. First, this was a retrospective study with a small number of patients with RGC using the disease risk score matching method, which may have caused selection bias. The number of patients may have been further reduced in the subgroup analysis for each reconstruction method, causing statistical bias. Moreover, the model did not undergo external or internal validation, limiting its generalizability and reliability. Second, the evaluation of the endoscopic findings was based on the subjective judgment of 2 physicians and thus lacked an objective assessment, which should be considered one of the limitations of this study. Although we attempted to standardize interpretation, future studies should incorporate blinded reviews or third-party validation to enhance objectivity. Third, regarding the reconstruction method, the results may differ depending on whether the Braun anastomosis was added to the B-II reconstruction, or whether the anterior or posterior colonic route was selected in the B-II or R-Y reconstruction. Fourth, because this was a retrospective study, several patients were not tested for H. pylori infection, and sufficient data were not obtained. Therefore, H. pylori infection status was diagnosed based on endoscopic findings according to the Kyoto classification of gastritis, which has inherent limitations in its diagnostic accuracy. Fifth, the RGC risk scoring system classifies the risk of developing RGC based on endoscopic findings in patients who are being followed up after gastrectomy. However, because this is a model created through a retrospective study, it cannot predict the timing of RGC occurrence from the initial surgery and RGC location. To resolve these limitations, a prospective, large-cohort, long-term time-to-event analysis is necessary to verify the relationship between this scoring system and the occurrence of RGC.

In conclusion, this multicenter study clarified that the background gastric mucosa and endoscopic findings of remnant gastritis, particularly anastomotic redness, red streaks, disappearance of the RAC, and atrophic gastritis, are significantly associated with the development of RGC. We developed that a new RGC risk score model created based on these endoscopic findings correlates with RGC. As this was a retrospective study, further prospective research is needed to validate the usefulness of this risk score model in predicting RGC development.
