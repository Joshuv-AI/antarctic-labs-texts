Global, Regional, and National Burden of Heart Failure Attributable to Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease From 1990 to 2021, With Projections to 2050
Authors: Xiaojing Deng, Huiqing Xu, Yeping Bian, Yunting Xu, Guofeng Ao, Luyan Yang, Ying Ji, Jian Xu, Fei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesHeart failure (HF), a common comorbidity of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), is associated with an increased risk of death among patients with COPD. However, no systematic assessment has quantified the population-level burden of comorbid COPD and HF, worldwide. This study assessed the global, regional, and national HF burden attributable to COPD from 1990 to 2021 and projected this burden to 2050.MethodsData on the non-fatal burden of COPD-attributable HF, including prevalence and years lived with disability (YLDs), were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021. Temporal trends in age-standardized rates (ASRs) for COPD-attributable HF from 1990 to 2021 were examined using joinpoint models. Bayesian age-period-cohort models were used to project ASRs to 2050.ResultsFrom 1990 to 2021, the absolute numbers of prevalent cases and YLDs for COPD-attributable HF increased globally, and the ASRs for prevalence and YLDs also increased. These upward trends were projected to continue to 2050. The burden of HF attributable to COPD was consistently higher in males than in females and higher in older adults than in younger individuals. By severity level, the burden of HF attributable to COPD was mainly driven by the treated and severe subtypes of HF. The burden varied substantially across regions, with low-socio-demographic index regions had the highest age-standardized burden.ConclusionsThe global non-fatal burden of HF attributable to COPD was substantial from 1990 to 2021 and may continue to increase to 2050. Public health interventions targeting HF attributable to COPD may help reduce this burden.Keywords: Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Heart failure, Global Burden of Disease, Population forecast

INTRODUCTION

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common chronic respiratory condition that imposes a substantial disease burden worldwide [1]. Globally, in 2019, the estimated prevalence of COPD was 10.3% among adults aged 30–79 years; COPD was also the third leading cause of death and was responsible for more than 3 million deaths [2,3]. COPD may also increase patients’ susceptibility to developing or experiencing worsening of other health conditions, including heart failure (HF) [4,5]. COPD and HF commonly coexist in clinical practice, particularly in respiratory medicine, because they share risk factors such as smoking and substantially overlap in pathophysiological processes, clinical signs, and symptoms [6,7].

On average, HF affects 17.7% of patients with COPD [8–10]. Conversely, COPD is the third leading driver of HF and affects approximately 30.5% of patients with HF [11]. Patients with HF attributable to COPD are at higher risk of adverse outcomes and all-cause hospitalization than patients with COPD or HF alone, as the 2 conditions can mutually worsen cardiac and respiratory function in a self-reinforcing cycle [12,13]. These findings suggest that HF attributable to COPD is not merely the coexistence of two common chronic conditions; rather, patients with HF attributable to COPD constitute a clinically distinct, high-risk population with a substantial disease burden. Accordingly, the importance of preventing and controlling cardiovascular events in patients with COPD was emphasized in the Global Strategy for the Diagnosis, Management, and Prevention of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease in 2013 and reinforced in subsequent COPD clinical management guidelines [14].

Although the burden of COPD and HF has been investigated separately, the burden associated with their comorbidity has not been adequately characterized in the global population. Estimates derived from COPD or HF alone may not capture the specific health loss, care needs, and resource demands attributable to the comorbidity. Separate assessment of HF attributable to COPD may therefore provide insights beyond single-disease burden analyses, particularly for identifying populations disproportionately affected by multimorbidity and informing integrated prevention and management strategies.

Given population aging and the increasing importance of multimorbidity in healthcare systems worldwide, quantifying the burden of HF attributable to COPD also has implications for healthcare planning, priority setting, and resource allocation. The Global Burden of Diseases (GBD), Injuries, and Risk Factors Study provides systematically modeled global data on disease and injury incidence, prevalence, and mortality [15], allowing the global burden of HF attributable to COPD to be assessed systematically. Using GBD 2021 data, this study investigated trends in the prevalence and disability burden of HF attributable to COPD from 1990 to 2021 and projected this burden to 2050, with analyses by sex, age group, and country-specific economic development status in the global population.

METHODS

Data Source and Participants

The data analyzed in this study were derived from the GBD 2021 study, which is managed by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation. Data were obtained from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation and are available at https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/; they were used with permission, and all rights are reserved. The general strategies and modeling framework of the GBD 2021 study have been described elsewhere [15,16]. The GBD 2021 study provided standardized estimates of the burden of 371 diseases and injuries from 1990 to 2021 at the global, regional, and national levels by age and sex. All estimates were reported with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). The study population comprised individuals aged ≥15 years from 1990 to 2021. Data on participants’ age and sex were also obtained from the GBD 2021 study [15].

Outcome Variables

COPD was identified using forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC) based on post-bronchodilator spirometry [17]. Individuals with an FEV1/FVC ratio <0.7 were defined as having COPD [17]. In the GBD study, COPD cases were also identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) version 9 and 10 codes: 491–492.9 and 496–499 from ICD-9, and J41–J44.9 from ICD-10 [18].

HF was regarded as an impairment condition, or a specific domain of functional health loss, and was diagnosed using structured criteria based on clinical signs and symptoms in the GBD 2021 study [15]. Specifically, stages C (symptomatic HF) and D (advanced HF) of the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association criteria were used to identify HF cases; these stages capture both individuals with current symptomatic HF and those diagnosed with HF who were asymptomatic at the time of assessment [15].

In this study, COPD-HF burden was regarded as the population-level burden of HF attributable to COPD within the GBD modeling framework, rather than the direct observation of individual-level coexistence of COPD and HF [15]. In GBD 2021 data, HF was modeled as a syndrome with mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive Level 3/4 underlying causes, among which COPD was explicitly included as a predefined cause. This framework allowed the burden of HF attributable to COPD to be estimated [15]. Briefly, the overall burden of HF was first estimated using DisMod-MR 2.1, the Bayesian meta-regression tool used in GBD. The burden of HF attributable to COPD was then calculated by constraining the proportion of HF attributable to COPD using proportion equation methods within the GBD framework. The outcome variable was defined as the burden of HF attributable to COPD, including prevalent cases, age-standardized prevalence rate, years lived with disability (YLDs), and age-standardized YLD rate. In the GBD 2021 dataset, prevalence and disability weights for HF were available, whereas incidence and mortality data were not available [15,19]. Therefore, based on data availability in the GBD study, YLDs—calculated as the product of COPD-attributable HF prevalence and its disability weight—were used to quantify the COPD-HF burden [15]. Disability weights were obtained from standard estimates recorded in the GBD dataset and were not specifically adjusted for comorbid conditions in this analysis. Additionally, based on the severity of HF clinical symptoms (mild, moderate, severe, and treated), HF attributable to COPD was classified into 4 subtypes for analysis.

Explanatory Variables

Several explanatory variables were analyzed in this study, including socio-demographic index (SDI), age, sex, location, and year. The SDI is an indicator of population-level development status and is calculated from income per capita, mean years of schooling among people aged ≥15 years, and fertility rate [20]. SDI scores range from 0 to 1, with higher scores indicating greater development status [20].

In the analysis, participants were classified as male or female. Because GBD studies collected data from individuals aged ≥15 years worldwide, participants were categorized into 17 age groups at 5-year intervals. The 204 countries and territories were stratified into 21 geographical regions based on the GBD regional classification system (Supplemental Material 1). These 204 countries and territories were also grouped into 5 quintiles based on SDI values to investigate the association between socioeconomic development status and HF burden attributable to COPD (Supplemental Material 1).

Statistical Analysis

First, absolute numbers and age-standardized rates (ASRs) were used to assess the global burden of HF attributable to COPD among all participants; the burden was then presented separately by geographical region, age, and sex. Rates were reported per 100 000 population with 95% UIs, according to the GBD algorithm. Next, Joinpoint version 5.0.2 (National Cancer Institute, Rockville, MD, USA) was used to calculate the average annual percentage change (AAPC) for each rate and examine temporal trends in the COPD-HF burden over the past 32 years. The AAPC was considered statistically significant if the 95% confidence interval (CI) did not include 0. A positive AAPC indicated an increasing trend in the COPD-HF burden, whereas a negative AAPC indicated a decreasing trend. A Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model in the “BAPC” package in version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) to predict ASRs for prevalence and YLDs for HF attributable to COPD from 2022 to 2050 [21,22]. Briefly, the model estimated expected rates under the assumption that the current demographic structure, risk factor patterns, and healthcare access would remain stable during the projection period [23]. In addition, the severity distribution of HF attributable to COPD was measured using the absolute numbers and ASRs of prevalence and YLDs. Finally, decomposition analysis was conducted to separate the total change in prevalent cases and YLDs of HF attributable to COPD into the contributions of aging, population growth, and epidemiological change [24,25]. Epidemiological change referred to the combined contributions of all factors other than aging and population growth [25].

Ethics Statement

Ethics approval for this study was waived by the Ethics Committee of Geriatric Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (2025/ 122-1) because all analyzed data were publicly available and contained no personal identifiers.

Figure 1 shows the numbers and ASRs for prevalence and YLDs for each subtype of HF attributable to COPD worldwide from 1990 to 2021. Overall, treated HF was the largest contributor to global prevalent cases of HF attributable to COPD, with 552 808 cases (95% UI, 409 791 to 720 651) in 1990 and 1 327 237 cases (95% UI, 966 174 to 1 793 225) in 2021. Severe HF was the second largest contributor to HF attributable to COPD, followed by mild and moderate HF. In contrast, severe HF was the largest contributor to global YLDs for HF attributable to COPD, followed by treated, moderate, and mild HF in both 1990 and 2021. Similarly, for ASRs, treated HF contributed most to prevalence, whereas severe HF contributed most to YLDs.

With regard to the global burden of HF attributable to COPD by sex from 1990 to 2021, upward trends in the numbers of prevalent cases and YLDs were observed in both male and female (Supplemental Material 1). However, the HF burden attributable to COPD differed by sex. The numbers and ASRs for both prevalence and YLDs were higher in male than in female throughout the 32-year study period. Increases in ASRs for prevalence and YLDs were identified in female but not in male.

For the projected burden of HF attributable to COPD to 2050, the estimated ASR for prevalence was projected to increase from 43.23 per 100 000 population in 2021 to 69.25 per 100 000 population in 2050 (Figure 2A). The ASR for YLDs was also projected to increase, from 3.84 per 100 000 people in 2021 to 5.83 per 100 000 people in 2050 (Figure 2B).

Global Burden of Heart Failure Attributable to Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease by Socio-demographic Index and Geographical Region From 1990 to 2021

Across the 5 SDI regions, the numbers of cases and YLDs for HF attributable to COPD increased from 1990 to 2021. The largest increase in the number of HF attributable to COPD cases occurred in the middle-SDI region over the 32-year study period (Table 1). The middle-SDI region also had the highest numbers of prevalent cases and YLDs for HF attributable to COPD in 2021. Notably, from 1990 to 2021, increases in the ASRs for both prevalence and YLDs for HF attributable to COPD were observed only in the high-SDI region, with AAPCs of 1.16 (95% CI, 1.11 to 1.21) and 1.16 (95% CI, 1.10 to 1.22), respectively. In 2021, however, the ASRs for prevalence and YLDs for HF attributable to COPD were lowest in the high-SDI region and highest in the low-SDI region.

Among the 21 geographical regions, 19 had increases in the numbers of prevalent cases and YLDs for HF attributable to COPD from 1990 to 2021; the largest increase occurred in East Asia, whereas decreases occurred in Central Asia and Eastern Europe (Table 1). In 2021, East Asia had the highest numbers of prevalent cases and YLDs for HF attributable to COPD, whereas Oceania had the lowest numbers. For ASRs of prevalence and YLDs, 10 of the 21 geographical regions showed increases from 1990 to 2021, with the largest increase observed in high-income North America. In 2021, East Asia had the highest ASRs for prevalence and YLDs, whereas Eastern Europe had the lowest ASRs.

Global Burden of Heart Failure Attributable to Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease by Age Group From 1990 To 2021

Figures 3 and Supplemental Material 1 illustrate the distribution of HF cases attributable to COPD across age groups at the global and SDI-region levels from 1990 to 2021. Globally, increasing trends in prevalent cases and YLDs for COPD-attributable HF were observed among participants aged ≥70 years from 1990 to 2021, with the highest numbers of both indicators observed among people aged 70–84 years. The increasing trends in prevalent cases and YLDs over the 32-year study period were likely attributable to the dominant effects of population growth and aging, which outweighed the counteracting effect of epidemiological change, as shown in the decomposition analysis (Supplemental Material 1). Moreover, the proportions of prevalent cases and YLDs for COPD-attributable HF were positively correlated with SDI quintile among populations aged ≥70 years over the 32-year study period. In 2021, the proportions of HF attributable to COPD prevalent cases and YLDs among those aged ≥70 years were 54.94% and 53.85%, respectively, in the low-SDI region and 76.88% and 76.52%, respectively, in the high-SDI region. Rising trends in HF attributable to COPD were also found among adults aged ≥70 years in both sexes and were more pronounced in female than in male. From 1990 to 2021, regardless of sex, prevalence and YLD rates for HF attributable to COPD showed strong increasing trends among populations aged ≥70 years globally (Figures 4, Supplemental Material 1). This trend was particularly evident among older female compared with older male and was considerably more pronounced in regions with higher SDI quintiles.

Global Burden of Heart Failure Attributable to Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease by Country or Territory From 1990 to 2021

At the national level, Norway had the largest increase in ASRs for prevalence and YLDs for HF attributable to COPD from 1990 to 2021, whereas Belarus had the largest decrease (Supplemental Material 1). In 1990, among the 204 countries and territories worldwide, the highest age-standardized prevalence rate of HF attributable to COPD occurred in China (109.64; 95% UI, 84.93 to 138.32); in 2021, it occurred in Papua New Guinea (83.09; 95% UI, 68.44 to 107.71). For the age-standardized YLD rate, China ranked first in 1990 (6.83; 95% UI, 6.30 to 13.57), whereas Papua New Guinea ranked first in 2021 (7.32; 95% UI, 4.53 to 10.33).

DISCUSSION

Patients with HF attributable to COPD generally have a poor prognosis [11]. However, the burden of HF attributable to COPD has not been adequately assessed. Using GBD 2021 data, this study systematically assessed the global, regional, and national non-fatal burden of HF attributable to COPD from 1990 to 2021 and projected this burden to 2050. In 2021, the estimated global numbers of prevalent cases and YLDs for HF attributable to COPD were 3.61 million and 0.32 million, respectively, and the global ASRs for prevalence and YLDs were 43.24 and 3.85 per 100 000 population, respectively. The numbers of prevalent cases and YLDs for HF attributable to COPD increased worldwide from 1990 to 2021. The ASRs for prevalence and YLDs also increased during this period, and these upward trends were projected to continue to 2050.

Globally, the most prevalent subtype of HF attributable to COPD was associated with treated HF, whereas severe HF was the largest contributor to YLDs. This finding suggests that much of the HF burden attributable to COPD was attributable to treated and severe HF, indicating the need for prevention and clinical care tailored to specific HF subtypes. However, the symptom-based classification approach may have increased the likelihood of categorizing patients with HF into more severe subtypes, although this HF classification method is widely used in guidelines and clinical practice [26,27].

A sex difference in the burden of HF attributable to COPD was observed in this study. Over the past 32 years, the numbers and ASRs for both prevalence and YLDs were consistently higher in male than in female, consistent with previous studies [28,29]. From 1990 to 2021, upward trends in the numbers of HF attributable to COPD cases and YLDs persisted in both sexes, whereas increases in ASRs for prevalence and YLDs were observed only in female. This sex disparity in HF attributable to COPD trends may be partly explained by increasing tobacco consumption among females and greater susceptibility among females to the adverse effects of smoking [30,31]. This finding underscores the importance of considering sex disparities in the prevention and management of HF attributable to COPD.

This study also found consistent disparities between age groups in the prevalence and YLDs of HF attributable to COPD. People aged ≥70 years consistently accounted for the largest proportion of HF attributable to COPD cases and YLDs globally, and this proportion increased from 1990 to 2021. Prevalence and YLD rates for HF attributable to COPD also showed strong increasing trends among populations aged ≥70 years worldwide. According to the decomposition analysis, the increasing trend in the crude HF attributable to COPD burden was largely attributable to population growth and aging. COPD and HF are both common among older adults, and this pattern may be explained by several mechanisms. First, chronic high-level inflammation and oxidative stress commonly occur in older adults [32,33]. Second, clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), a common age-related condition, is associated with both COPD and HF [34,35]. CHIP may trigger aberrant inflammatory responses in mutant hematopoietic cells and increase inflammatory cytokine levels in older adults, thereby increasing susceptibility to both COPD and HF.

Regional differences in HF attributable to COPD were also observed worldwide. The absolute burden of HF attributable to COPD was greater in regions with large populations, such as East Asia, and lower in regions with small populations, such as Oceania. This pattern may partly reflect population aging and the increasing prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors in East Asia over recent decades [36], but it should be interpreted with caution. Differences in healthcare system characteristics, including diagnostic capacity and disease registry completeness, may also influence the estimation and comparability of ASRs across regions. Moreover, this study showed large increases in ASRs for HF attributable to COPD prevalence and YLDs in countries within the high-SDI quintile from 1990 to 2021. This pattern in ASRs for prevalence and YLDs is unlikely to reflect a true increase in disease incidence; rather, it is more plausibly explained by earlier diagnosis, improved detection, longer survival, and better chronic disease management in high-SDI regions, which increase the number of individuals living with the condition and consequently elevate ASRs [37]. In contrast, in countries in the low-SDI quintile, such as Papua New Guinea, the prevalence and disability burden of HF attributable to COPD may be driven mainly by prevalent disease-associated risk factors, particularly smoking [38]. Access to and affordability of prevention programs and clinical medications are also limited in low-income countries, which may contribute to low rates of early identification and effective treatment [39].

This study investigated the global non-fatal burden of HF attributable to COPD and has several public health implications for population-level interventions. The findings may help researchers and policymakers better understand the prevalence and YLD burden of HF attributable to COPD and inform interventions targeting shared risk factors and healthcare resource allocation to reduce the specific non-fatal burden of HF attributable to COPD. Specifically, priority could be given to: (1) older adults, through bidirectional screening, including screening patients with COPD for HF and assessing pulmonary function in patients with HF; (2) integrated cardiopulmonary rehabilitation and multidisciplinary collaborative management; (3) strengthened tobacco control and early disease detection programs; and (4) improved medication access in low-income and middle-income regions.

This study has several strengths. First, to our knowledge, it is the first study to comprehensively assess the specific non-fatal burden of HF attributable to COPD in the overall population at global, regional, and national levels. Second, the non-fatal burden of HF attributable to COPD was estimated over more than 3 decades, allowing long-term trends to be examined. Third, the data were derived from the GBD 2021 study, a widely used dataset with strong scientific validity. In addition, definitions and measures of COPD and HF were adopted directly from the GBD 2021 study, supporting comparability with other GBD-based studies.

However, this study had several limitations. First, COPD and HF cases were identified based on GBD inclusion criteria, implying that this study shared the data-selection limitations of the GBD study. Second, because the GBD study did not record information on HF deaths or incidence, only selected non-fatal indicators of disease burden—prevalence and YLDs—were used to measure the burden of HF attributable to COPD in the present study. Third, GBD estimates depend on the quality and completeness of the underlying data, which may vary across countries. In low-income regions, diagnostic misclassification of COPD and HF may occur. Fourth, the COPD-HF burden estimates were derived from models within the GBD framework rather than from directly observed individual-level comorbidity data. Fifth, shared risk factors such as smoking could not be adjusted for in this ecological analysis, precluding causal inference. Moreover, ecological-level findings may not reflect individual-level associations. Sixth, projections to 2050 are subject to uncertainty related to model assumptions and future changes in socio-demographic characteristics and healthcare systems. Seventh, HF with reduced ejection fraction and HF with preserved ejection fraction could not be differentiated because these data were unavailable in the GBD study, although prevalence and mortality differ between these 2 HF subtypes [40]. Finally, because disability weights were based on GBD standard estimates rather than individual-level comorbidity adjustments, the potential additive disability effects of COPD and HF may not have been fully captured, possibly leading to underestimation of the overall burden.

In conclusion, the non-fatal burden of HF attributable to COPD was substantial worldwide and increased from 1990 to 2021. This upward trend may continue to 2050 globally. Sex, age, and regional disparities in the COPD-HF burden were observed over the 32-year study period. The largest absolute burden was observed in middle-SDI regions, the highest age-standardized burden in low-SDI regions, and the fastest increases in ASRs in high-SDI regions. These findings provide public health insights into the burden of HF attributable to COPD and epidemiological trends in its prevalence and YLDs. Prioritizing older adults through integrated surveillance, coordinated cardiopulmonary care, strengthened tobacco control, and early diagnosis and treatment may support the prevention and management of HF attributable to COPD. Improved access to essential medications in low- and middle-income regions may also help make interventions more precise and effective.
