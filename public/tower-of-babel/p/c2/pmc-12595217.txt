Nanoparticle-based approaches for wound healing: a comprehensive review of nanomaterials enhancing tissue regeneration
Authors: Pritam Paul, Sushruta S Hakkimane
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWound healing is a complex biological process involving multiple stages that require interactions between several cells, growth factors, and cytokines. The lack of a suitable environment, along with microbial infection, impairs the normal wound-healing process. Burns, trauma, diabetic injuries, and chronic ulcers are the most prevalent types of wounds on the skin. Conventional wound treatments, with their focus on a single aspect, often fail to consider the complex conditions fully surrounding the pathogens that cause infections. Nanotechnology, a rapidly developing and cutting-edge field, facilitates tissue engineering and drug delivery and has proven effective in tackling several wound healing challenges by delivering anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and angiogenic benefits. This review explores the wound healing process, chronic wounds, and current wound care challenges, highlighting how nanotechnology, through the use of metallic nanoparticles (such as silver, gold, and zinc oxide), polymeric systems, lipid-based carriers, and carbon-based materials, offers significant advantages and has become a crucial part of modern wound treatment. It further explores the role of nanotechnology in skin wound repair, its advantages over conventional treatment techniques, its biocompatibility, and it also highlights clinical trials using nanotech-based approaches and outlines future directions in wound healing research.Graphical abstract Keywords: Nanotechnology, Wound healing, Nanostructure, Tissue regeneration, Chronic wounds

Introduction

According to estimates, approximately 10 million people worldwide suffer from skin injuries each year, with an estimated 180,000 deaths each year specifically from burns, according to WHO statistics [1, 2]. Chronic wounds alone put an economic burden of 34.99 million euros over three years in the southern areas of Barcelona and Spain, and the US Medicare system spends an estimated 30 billion dollars per year on wound care [3, 4]. Regional variations in wound prevalence demonstrate major differences, with developing countries experiencing higher rates of traumatic wounds and infection-related complications, while developed countries face increasing challenges from aging-related chronic wounds and diabetic ulcers [2]. The skin serves as the body’s largest organ, weighing approximately 3.2 kg with a surface area of 2 m2 in adults. It comprises multiple layers and creates a strong partition between the “inside” and the “outside” of the organism, functioning as a strong biological barrier that protects the body from environmental threats Fig. 1 while producing antimicrobial peptides, neuropeptides, hormones, and cytokines that have both local and systemic biological effects [5, 6]. Since our skin constitutes the bulk of our body and is constantly exposed to the outside world, wounds, burns, infections, accidents, and skin trauma are inevitable.

Different layers of skin protecting body from external environment

Wound healing is a process that requires synchronized action from numerous cell lines and tissues. Along with inflammation and angiogenesis, it requires careful coordination of cell migration, proliferation, matrix deposition, and remodeling [7]. The healing process varies depending on the type of wound whether it is acute or chronic. Most acute wounds heal through normal physiological mechanisms within 4–12 weeks, some may progress to chronic wounds due to infection, inadequate circulation, underlying comorbidities, and compromised healing processes [8]. Although the bacteria that make up the skin’s bacterial microbiota help prevent other pathogens from colonizing and forming biofilms, if these bacteria reach a certain threshold at the site of a lesion, they may complicate the healing process, especially if biofilms are formed. The most prevalent aerobic bacteria found in biofilms include Staphylococcus and Enterococcus species, while Prevotella and Porphyromonas represent the dominant anaerobic bacteria. These biofilms create reactive-oxygen-dependent chronic infection that prevents re-epithelialization and represents a major barrier to healing [9, 10]. To date, both traditional and contemporary therapies have been developed for this purpose. Traditional wound dressings only offer a physical barrier, lacking active properties to restrict microbes or promote skin regeneration. In contrast, contemporary dressings regulate wound moisture and absorb excess exudates, creating an ideal healing microenvironment. [11]. However, nanotechnology-based wound dressings can locally alter the biochemical environment of the wound by focusing on bacterial load and elevated protease levels. When endogenous collagen is broken down by the proteolytic wound environment, it can also supply collagen matrices that are exogenous and act as a framework to support tissue growth [12, 13].

Nanotechnology represents a domain within the broader spectrum of technological advancements that modify the molecular scale of materials within the 1–1000 nm region, thereby rendering them applicable for use in biomedical contexts [14]. The intrinsic nanoscale properties of nanotechnology-based therapies directly address antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and angiogenic effects, offering targeted strategies to combat microbial infection and promote a conductive environment for healing. These interactions modulate one or more stages of wound healing by affecting specific cellular pathways and metabolic processes. The adaptability and tunability of this approach offer two critical advantages. Moreover, it facilitates the gradual and regulated release of bioactive substances or other therapeutic agents over extended periods, spanning days or weeks, which is occasionally unattainable with conventional therapeutic modalities [15–17]. Nanotechnology is a promising field for wound healing due to its adaptable physicochemical characteristics. Nanomaterials, including nanoparticles (NPs), have shown significant potential in accelerating wound healing by efficiently penetrating the body, improving drug concentration in the bloodstream, regulating release rates, and delivering medication directly to the wound site [18]. Wound healing is coordinated by complex networks of cytokines and growth factors (GFs) that regulate cell migration, proliferation, and diffusion. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), initiate the inflammatory response, while the anti-inflammatory response mediators, such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transformation growth factor-β (TGF-β), promote resolution and tissue repair [19]. Growth factors, including Fibroblast Growth Factor (FGF), Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF), Epidermal Growth Factor (EGF), and Platelet-Derived Growth Factor (PDGF), are essential for tissue repair during natural wound healing, which drives angiogenesis, epithelialization, and matrix deposition, but their efficacy is diminished by rapid degradation or dilution [15, 20]. Modified nanomaterials with pH, charge, and microenvironment effects improve drug targeting and bioavailability, reduce toxicity, and regulate the discharge rates of growth factors and medications. Therefore, nanotechnology not only maintains growth factors and biological activity but also their functional involvement in the wound-healing process [21–23]. Recently, the use of nanotechnology in the healing of wounds has increased. However, there are concerns regarding their toxicity compared to conventional wound healing therapies. Crucially, significant knowledge gaps persist regarding the long-term systemic effects and potential accumulation of nanoparticles in the body, necessitating a focus on safe design principles and rigorous long-term biocompatibility studies for their clinical translation [24]. However, environmentally synthesized nanoparticles demonstrate superior biocompatibility and reduced toxicity by using green formulations, leveraging natural compounds, and using suitable synthesis methods for nanoparticles, which are increasingly demonstrating benefits compared to chemically synthesized alternatives [25]. This comprehensive review aims to critically evaluate the current state of nanotechnology in wound healing, focusing on various nanoparticle systems, their mechanism of action, clinical application, and translational challenges. We provide an in-depth analysis of different nanomaterials, their biocompatibility profiles, and future directions for clinical implementation, with emphasis on evidence-based approaches to revolutionizing wound care practice.

Wound healing process

Wound healing is essential for restoring skin after injury, whether caused by accidental or medical procedures. It is a complex and dynamic process, involving multiple stages that occur sequentially and sometimes overlapping manner [7, 26]. Wounds range from minor skin epithelial interference to more significant wounds infiltrating the subcutaneous layer and causing damage to related structures, such as tendons, muscles, blood vessels, nerves, and even skeletal tissue [27]. Injuries are classified into two main categories: acute and chronic wounds, with acute wounds typically healing within four to twelve weeks, and chronic wounds, often associated with diabetes and bacterial infections, usually lasting longer than 12 weeks [8]. As shown in Fig. 2 the wound-healing process comprises four phases: (1) hemostasis, (2) inflammation, (3) proliferation, and (4) remodeling, all of which are controlled by a number of growth factors and cytokines [27, 28].

Hemostasis

Hemostasis is known to be the first step in the healing process, where it helps to stop bleeding and forms clot to seal the wound which begins immediately after a vascular injury. The process initiates with vasoconstriction, where endothelin from the injured endothelium drives vascular contraction, as do prostaglandins, catecholamines, and platelet-derived growth factors (PDGF), which affect muscle contraction but fail to control long-term flow and pressure [29, 30]. In the next phase (preliminary hemostasis phase) shows a cascade of coagulation marked by high platelet activity in the development of a clot, which is initiated by a variety of biochemical mediators [31, 32]. Exposure of the thrombogenic layer leads to platelet adhesion and platelets activation. This activation alters its morphology and increases adhesion and releasing numerous components which promotes adhesion and aggregation such as fibronectin, fibrinogen, von Willebrand factor (vWF) and collagen [7, 29, 33]. Platelet plugs are enriched in growth factors including transforming growth factor (TGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), insulin growth factor (IGF), and epidermal growth factor (EGF), which are crucial for the healing process [7].

Inflammation

In the inflammation phase, various immune cells such as neutrophils and monocytes act directly on the injured sites to counter infection and eliminate debris [34]. This process triggers damage signals, which are separated into two groups: damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), molecules that undergo necrosis, and pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), pathogen-specific molecules that are absent from the host, such as bacteria. [35]. These damage signals are recognized by pattern recognition receptors and Toll-like receptors (TLR). TLRs are exposed in host cells and stimulate various signaling pathways, including mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), which express several genes that trigger the inflammatory phase, including cytokines, chemokines, and antimicrobial peptides [36]. In response to chemokines, which result from bacterial breakdown, neutrophils are drawn from the bloodstream to the site of the lesion during the initial stages of inflammation. Neutrophil granules contain proteases that combat microorganisms and facilitate the movement of neutrophils from circulation to the lesion site, aiding tissue regeneration. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) is released by proteases to promote skin regeneration and wound healing [35, 37–39]. Uninvolved neutrophils must be cleared from a wound site; otherwise, this prolong presence can trigger inflammation, cytotoxicity, and increase the risk of chronic inflammation and scar formation. After their primary function, M1 macrophages change their phenotype to M2, entering the anti-inflammatory stage. In addition to promoting fibroblast differentiation into myofibroblasts and aiding angiogenesis and vascular genesis, M2 macrophages also boost the expression of collagen and smooth muscle actin(αSMA), all of which are necessary for tissue integrity. After fibrosis and angiogenesis, macrophages return to a phagocytic state and use phagocytosis to eliminate the cells and debris [38–40]. Concurrently, mast cells help in the initial phases of healing by releasing enzymes, such as histamine, which aids in modulating re-epithelization and wound contraction [41]. While not strictly necessary for wound healing dendritic cells are crucial for the rapid initiation and progression of healing processes immediately after injury. They are mostly antigen-presenting cells and are vital for initiating T-cell responses, emphasizing their role not only in immune defence but also in tissue repair, defence and regeneration [42, 43].

Proliferation

Following inflammation, proliferation plays a significant role in wound healing by covering the wound surface through granulation tissue growth, re-epithelization, neovascularization, and immunomodulation [7, 44]. Granulation tissue in wound healing serves as a temporary scaffold for cells that participate in the healing process. The temporary scaffold was composed of activated fibroblasts that lay down a temporary extracellular membrane (ECM). Granulation tissue is essential for several critical processes, including cell motility, oxygenation, and nutritional distribution. Revascularization begins with the activation of endothelial cells (EC) in response to growth stimuli in the wound, including basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and serine protease thrombin [44–46]. Fibroblasts are essential for the development of granulation tissue, which is prevalent in connective tissue and distributed throughout different organs, i.e., at the wound site, in response to the growth factors and cytokines, such as basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), transforming growth factor (TGF-β), and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) etc. Following their migration into the provisional wound matrix, fibroblasts multiply and produce proteinases such as matrix metalloproteins (MMPs) to break down the provisional matrix. Collagen is deposited along with other extracellular matrix (ECM) constituents, such as fibronectin, glycosaminoglycans, hyaluronic acid, and proteoglycans to form granulation tissues. After the wound has healed, these tissues will close the gap and serve as a scaffold for cell adhesion, growth, differentiation, and mitigation [44, 47, 48].

Remodeling

This is the last prolonged stage of wound healing, which can take anywhere from a few months to a few years. It begins at the conclusion of granulation tissue development, which is the production of fibers and the maturation of the healed tissue. The development of myofibroblasts, which express smooth muscle actin, is triggered by fibroblasts and cytokines such as TGF-β. The immune and parenchymal cells are constrained at this stage, and the remaining cells, including myofibroblasts, degrade. Collagen I rapidly replaces collagen III in the ECM to boost the tensile strength of the skin during repair or regeneration. Additionally, the repaired skin can regain approximately 80% of its initial strength [49, 50]. The wound-healing process, as discussed, is complex and multi-phased. Cytokines and growth factors regulate these overlapping stages. Immune and structural cells release signaling molecules. This intricate network ensures proper repair and prevents complications. Chronic wounds, wound care and challenges

While most acute wounds heal normally through the body’s natural repair mechanism, certain acute wounds may progress to chronic wounds. This inability to heal and restore anatomical and functional integrity is accompanied by several disruptions in the healing process [52, 53]. With a 5-year death rate of nearly 50% and a prevalence of 1–2%, chronic wounds are now recognized as a severe, life-threatening illness with significant financial burdens, similar to heart failure [4, 54]. They can be caused by various factors, including poor circulation, abnormal pressure, neuropathy, infection, and unresolved inflammation [2, 55, 56]. In addition to morbidity and death, wounds are the most costly skin condition, with the treatment of venous leg ulcers alone costing over $14 billion a year in the United States (US) [57]. Currently, chronic wounds are treated using various wound management techniques like debridement and diverse dressings, including hydrogel and nanoparticle-based dressings. Traditional methods, such as turmeric and aloe vera, are also used, alongside more advanced methods like growth hormones and bioengineered skin substitutes Table 2. Autologous skin grafts are required for more severe wounds. To address these multifaceted challenges in wound care, nanomedicine emerges as a promising solution, offering unique properties that can simultaneously target multiple aspects of wound healing while overcoming the limitations of conventional therapies [58–60]. Many organic and inorganic nanomaterials have been studied for their ability to reduce inflammation, manage microbial infections, and accelerate wound healing owing to their distinct physicochemical and biological characteristics. By increasing their solubility, half-life, stability, preventing degradation, and reducing potential toxicity, nanoparticles acting as carrier materials can increase the bioavailability of pharmaceuticals used in wound healing, such as growth factors, antibiotics, or anti-inflammatory agents [61–63]. While these established methods Table 2 offer foundational approaches to wound care, emerging trends in nanomedicine are actively addressing their limitations, particularly concerning targeted delivery, complex infection management, and enhanced regenerative capabilities, pushing the boundaries of what’s possible in chronic wound treatment.

Traditional and modern approaches used to accelerate wound healing

Nanotechnology in the remedy of wounds

Nanotechnology is a rapidly expanding and developing discipline. It includes the design, formulation, and synthesis of materials possessing nanoscale dimensions, which are then applied to various biological challenges, including therapeutic and diagnostic applications [92, 93]. Due to its unique properties, nanotechnology has been widely employed to cure many ailments. Various studies report its broad applications across diverse medical conditions and healing processes. [94, 95]. Wound healing is influenced by multiple factors, such as infection, stress, and obesity [94, 96]. Traditional wound care therapies often have limitations; they cannot deliver drugs or therapeutic agents to the wound site with precision. In contrast, nanotechnology offers the most favorable and ideal approach for delivering drugs or therapeutic agents to the wound site in a precise and efficient manner [97, 98]. The size and nanocarrier characteristics of these systems enable intercellular drug delivery of therapeutic agents or biomolecules. Nanocarriers also protect agents from degradation. They facilitate deeper penetration into wounds, offering controlled drug release to enhance healing. Furthermore, nanoparticles can promote the healing of both dermal and epidermal wounds [15]. Nanotechnology has improved the pharmacological effects of existing natural and pharmaceutical compounds. The application of nanoparticles within hydrogels and nanofibers specifically enhances medication delivery [99, 100]. Silver nanoparticles

Silver nanoparticles(AgNPs) are crucial for wound healing owing to their strong bactericidal properties, high conductivity, low cost, chemical stability, and catalytic activity [103]. Microorganisms that develop biofilms and are resistant to many drugs are effectively combatted by AgNPs [104]. The antibacterial efficacy of AgNPs is affected by their size, shape, and dosage. The antibacterial activity concurrently increases as the particle size falls [105]. Phosphorus-containing substances in microbial cells, such as DNA and sulfur-containing proteins anchored in the bacterial membrane, can interact with AgNPs. Their ability to combat respiratory cell death is the primary mechanism underlying their antimicrobial activities. Furthermore, the ejection of Ag + ions results in an increased bactericidal action [104, 106]. According to another study, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by silver ions that block the respiratory enzymes of bacterial cells have a bactericidal impact [107]. According to certain research, this can be achieved by lowering the expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and interleukin 6 (IL-6). AgNPs have demonstrated strong anti-inflammatory properties, are capable of inhibiting a wide range of pathogenic microorganisms, including fungi, bacteria, and viruses, and can also be utilized to prevent or lessen wound infection [108–110]. Similar to this, AgNPs promote wound contraction and hasten the healing of diabetic ulcers by stimulating keratinocyte migration and proliferation and assisting fibroblasts in differentiating into myofibroblasts Fig. 4 [111, 112]. AgNPs have been added to gels, including hyaluronic [113], PEG-Chitosan [114], polyacrylic acid [115] and foams [116], among others, which have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing the number of germs and treating diabetic wounds. CNF/AgNP-chitosan film [119], and AgNP coated with alginate, gelatine, and carboxymethyl cellulose on nonwoven fabrics [120] have been the most recent studies on the ability of AgNPs to accelerate wound healing by pathogen elimination. Shi et al. created hydrogels using thiolate chitosan impregnated with AgNPs and dextran grafted with maleic acid [121]. This hydrogel enhances the AgNP performance by acting as an antifouling substance. Through their antibacterial activity, inflammatory inhibition, and immune response modulation, they produce a gradual release of Ag + , which aids wound healing. Silver nanoparticle-embedded dressings, such as Acticoat, have demonstrated a controlled and sustained release of silver ions, which helps overcome the potential toxicity and inactivation of proteins in wound tissue [122]. Rigo et al. reported the safety of Acticoat Flex 3 (silver-nanoparticle-based wound dressing) using in vitro and in vivo analysis [123]. AgNPs may temporarily impair mitochondrial activity although they do not exert cytotoxic effects on cells. In human in vivo clinical research, the absence of necrosis or apoptosis in healed tissue indicated no harm. Antimicrobial medicines are frequently used immediately to prevent significant wound infections in burn injury cases. Thus, Zhang et al. examined the impact of postponed wound healing [124]. On the 0th, 3rd, and 5th days after burn, Cancio et al. applied AgNPs to the wounds. According to the results, a small delay on the third day of AgNP treatment for burn wounds accelerates wound healing by avoiding protracted inflammation, as opposed to the 0th and 5th days of treatment [125]. This study indicates that the effectiveness of wound healing is enhanced when antimicrobial drugs are applied to the wound site a little later. These publications reported that AgNPs can be used effectively in wound healing and antimicrobial applications, but there is a need for chronic wounds.

Gold nanoparticles

Gold nanoparticles(AuNPs) have special physiochemical properties, which make them popular in medical applications because AuNPs have antioxidant properties and can recover reactive oxygen species (ROS) [126]. Additionally, AuNPs have antibacterial properties that prevent ATP production by changing the potential of the cell membrane. This lowers the ATP levels, which causes energy metabolism to shift and ultimately leads to bacterial death [61]. The in vivo transcutaneous distribution of various medications was made easier by AuNPs, which penetrated the subcutaneous layer of the skin and extended across the epidermal layer [127]. When applied to open wounds, AuNPs promoted the production of granulation tissue and collagen deposition, re-epithelialization, and wound healing in Wistar Albino rats [128]. In a recent study, Zou et al. combined gelatin and KGM and loaded them with AuNPs and GS to create a wound dressing with strong antibacterial properties. When AuNPs were added, GS's antibacterial activity of the GS increased. KGM/Gelatin@AuNPs/GS efficiently eradicated bacteria with the highest zone of inhibition on gram-positive bacteria, such as S. aureus (26.3 mm), methicillin-resistant S. aureus (15.8 mm), and gram-negative bacteria, such as E. coli (18.9 mm). Additionally, cell culture tests revealed that KGM/gelatin @AuNPs/GS was not overtly cytotoxic. Histological analysis demonstrated that KGM/gelatin @AuNPs/GS stimulated epithelial cell regeneration and proliferation. After 14 days, an animal experiment demonstrated that it effectively stimulated wound healing. This study supports antimicrobial activity and promotes wound healing, as well as in methicillin-resistant S. aureus-affected wounds [129]. Recently, snail mucus was used for the first time to stimulate the production of AuNPs, which is both innovative and environmentally friendly. To assess the anti-inflammatory properties of AuNPs, they examined their effects on human keratinocytes. Additionally, research revealed that AuNP-SS accelerates wound closure, which is linked to elevated urokinase (uPAR) expression. uPAR directly regulates cell adhesion, differentiation, proliferation, and migration via a nonproteolytic method. Additionally, they noticed a sharp decline in the levels of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), which is necessary for macrophage activation and the subsequent release of cytokines. Although further research is needed, AuNP-SS can both encourage and possibly accelerate wound healing [130]. To address the issue of chemical-mediated AuNP synthesis, a green synthesis employing natural sources was developed, resulting in eco-friendly, greener AuNP synthesis using Acalypha indica extract. This study used histological staining to observe the wound-healing process of green-synthesized AuNPs at various time intervals (days 2, 7, and 15). The healing process improved on the second day, with somewhat better re-epithelialization than the control, followed by day 7. On day 15, there was a significant increase in neovascularization and complete re-epithelialization. It also has excellent antibacterial capabilities and could be a useful eco-friendly alternative to AuNPs for wound healing [131]. Zhu et al. created 4,6-diamino-2-pyrimidinethiol functionalized AuNPs and placed them onto a silk fibroin (SF) matrix film/membrane using a simple water evaporation approach to treat infectious wounds. The DAPT-AuNP-SF composite films effectively inhibited E. coli and MDR E. coli regardless of their susceptibility to antimicrobials. Additionally, it effectively treats and promotes wound healing in a rat model infected with clinically isolated MDR E. coli. According to this study, antimicrobial AuNPs can promote wound healing in drug-resistant bacterial infections [132]. AuNPs loaded into polymeric hydrogel [133], bioactive glass-AuNPs ointment [134], polymer poly-hexamethylene biguanide (PHMB) with AuNPs [135], Honey/chitosan nanofibers loaded with AuNPs [136], chitosan-synthesized AuNPs [137], and some of the green approaches for synthesizing AuNPs for wound healing include AuNPs synthesized from the insulin plant Chamaecostus cuspidatus [138] and garcinia pulp extract [139], which are a few of the most current research projects that scientists have undertaken to greatly enhance the healing of infected wounds.

Zinc oxide nanoparticles

Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs) are inorganic nanoparticles employed in a variety of biological applications. The biological activity of ZnO NPs varies with size. The biological application of ZnO stems from the formation of electron–hole pairs. It produces reactive oxygen species (ROS), has antibacterial properties, and promotes wound re-epithelialization. [140]. Azithromycin (AZM) absorption on the ZnONP surface has been studied, and this is the first publication [141]. ZnONPs, including AZM, offer a topical formulation that significantly enhances healing in infected wounds. The addition of AZM to the surface of the ZnONPs produced a healing effect that outperformed that of either medication alone. Higher AZM concentrations with a fixed ZnONP weight and temperature resulted in a drug-loading efficiency of 82.34 ± 2.52%. The in vivo and in vitro antibacterial and wound-healing activities of AZM-ZnONP-treated animals suggest that the proposed formulation may be a superior option for treating skin infections caused by resistant bacteria. Further research is needed to test the clinical efficacy and therapeutic efficacy of persistent diabetic wounds. Khan et al. developed a composite nanofiber (NF) membrane using a new combination of polymers and bioactive ZnONPs [142]. ZnO NPs were effectively encapsulated in nanofibers constructed from PLGA/SF polymers using electrospinning. Physiochemical evaluation demonstrated that NF membranes are suitable as wound-dressing materials owing to their good morphological properties, significant mechanisms, and thermal stability. Various biological tests have been performed on mice, including cytotoxicity, antioxidant, antibacterial, and in vivo wound-healing capabilities. Antibacterial activity was tested against gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, E. coli and S. aureus, and the results showed that a 3% ZnO concentration may inhibit both species. Another study on antimicrobial and wound bandages used cotton fibers doped with a wound-healing biopolymer made of chitosan, glycogen, and ZnO nanoparticles (CG@ZnONPs) that were previously synthesized using a green approach. Elastic compression straps support the composite, which is then secured with a tight-mesh polyester cloth. This bandage was intended to reduce the negative effects of regular bandages. The manufactured wound dressing also demonstrated excellent antibacterial action against P. aeruginosa, a gram-negative bacterium, Staphylococcus aureus, and Staphylococcus epidermidis, both gram-positive bacteria, and C. albicans, a pathogenic yeast. Furthermore, the bandage prototype resulted in nearly complete healing of the examined wounds with outstanding epithelialization, granulation, tissue formation, and condensed collagen deposition [143]. A recent study assessed the biological features of green-synthesized curcumin-mediated ZnO nanoparticles (CmZnONPs) in rats, including their antioxidant and wound healing properties. CmZnONPs blocked both DPPH and ABTS free radicals, indicating their antioxidant capabilities. Among the many forms of wound healing chemicals, CmZnONPs significantly decreased the wound surface of rat skin during the 14-day topical treatment course. CmZnONPs have strong wound-healing activity because they increase the number of fibroblasts and new capillaries, as evidenced by hyperplasia and angiogenesis in the epidermal layer of the wound. Overexpression of the VEGF and collagen genes confirmed angiogenesis and tissue regeneration, respectively. However, numerous in vivo studies must be performed to determine the potential mechanism of wound healing [144]. Some of the recent advancements that give efficient wound healing are ZnONPs [145], ZnONPs pre-impregnated with bacterial cellulose (BC) and betulin diphosphate (BDP) [146], ZnONPs embedded into alginate hydrogels [147], Electro spun Scaffold of Collagen and Polycaprolactone Containing ZnO Quantum Dots [148].

Lipid nanoparticles

Lipid nanoparticles(LNPs) are represented as solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) and nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs). Physiological lipids or lipid molecules, such as cholesterol and triglycerides (TG), are typically used to prepare lipid nanoparticles (NPs), and no potentially hazardous organic solvents are required during the preparation process. Some versatile LNPs have already hit the market; for example, Q10 is sold under the names Dr. Rimpler and Nanorepair Q10© [149, 150]. A new study on a successful method of treating diabetic wounds by delivering VEGF-A mRNA using lipid nanoparticles. An in vitro transcription technique was used to synthesize VEGF-A mRNA, which was then encapsulated using a lipid-mediated nanoparticle delivery system. By introducing VEGF-A mRNA into the body, they used a recently developed protein replacement treatment to promote the expression of the target protein and hasten diabetic wound healing. HUVECs treated with LNP/VEGF-A mRNA were used to measure the rate of cell proliferation. The cells showed an extraordinarily high rate of approximately 117.44% without any discernible toxicity, in contrast to the control that showed a viability of 110.26%. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that angiogenesis and cell migration contribute to vascularization and wound healing in diabetes. Following treatment with LNP/VGEF-A mRNA, histological analysis of mouse wound models has shown that it dramatically increases granulation tissue production, epidermal thickness, collagen deposition, and angiogenesis. Thus, as a protein replacement therapy, LNP/VEGF-A mRNA formulation promotes diabetic wound healing and may find practical use in the treatment of diabetic wounds [151]. Sandhu SK et al. created an optimal 15% of curcumin-loaded SLNs (CSLNs) using a high-pressure homogenization (HPH) process for wound healing, similar to an earlier study. Additionally, the authors asserted that a significant antibacterial effect and destruction of mature biofilms against Staphylococcus aureus 9144. Additionally, it was reported that wound healing was increased when FB and HaCaT cells grew and exposed to curcumin solution and CSLN at concentrations up to 0.1 mg/mL by lowering the chronic inflammatory response associated with oxidative stress and speeding up re-epithelialization, which demonstrated a 95.76 ± 7.85% decrease in angiogenesis and granulation tissue development after 11 days in female CD-1 mice [152]. The development of a Kolliphor-407 P-based gel with neomycin sulfate (NES) loaded into SLN is the goal of another investigation [153]. The QbD concept was incorporated into this study because it offers a time-and money-saving method to guarantee a high-quality outcome. Furthermore, the Box-Behnken design was implemented to effectively optimize the NES-SLN. With 0.467% stearic acid, 0.275% glyceryl monostearate, and 1.23% P-F68, this formulation provided the ideal NES-SLN preparation requirements. A Franz diffusion cell, which is a cellulose acetate membrane with a pore size of 0.45 µm, was used for ex vivo drug release. This indicates that within the first four hours, the drug was released in a burst. After the initial burst, the release rate was modulated, resulting in more controlled and prolonged drug release over time. Approximately 97.25% of the NES was released from the SLNs by the conclusion of the 24-h period, suggesting a high level of drug liberation over the study period. The antibacterial qualities of many gel formulations combining NES-SLN and NES are tested in this study against two common pathogens: Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus). The Kolliphor-407 P gel formulation with NES-SLN showed noticeably stronger antibacterial activity against S. aureus after a 24-h treatment period than the gel with NES alone. This implies that NES's antibacterial activity of NES is increased by SLN encapsulation, possibly because of greater drug stability, prolonged release, and bacterial cell barrier penetration [153].

Polymeric nanoparticles

Polymeric nanoparticles (NPs) are used in many pharmaceutical applications. The use of polymeric nanoparticles holds significant promise in several therapeutic holds significant promises in several therapeutic segments. They are used in different formulations for wound healing, including antibiotic-incorporated nanoparticles, growth factors-incorporated nanoparticles, gene delivery and stem cell delivery [154]. In chronic and diabetic wounds, there is an issue that is complicated to heal via a localised drug delivery system [155]. Most polymers are biomaterials that are typically used in the creation of vascular grafts, implanted devices, medical device coatings, and surgical instruments [156]. Although collagen, gelatin, hyaluronic acid, and DNA are naturally occurring polymers, synthetic polymers include polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), polystyrene (PS), polyvinyl chloride (PVC), polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA), polyamides (nylon), polysiloxanes (silicone), and polyurethanes (PU) [157–159]. For use in the wound cavity, Narisepalli et al. created an asiaticoside polymeric nanoparticle (AST PNP) encapsulated in a gelatine-based biodegradable hydrogel (15% w/v) to allow continuous release of AST and improve its therapeutic benefits.. In in vitro wound healing experiments, a wound was created using a cell scraper. Cell migration began 6 h after the wound cells were treated with free AST and AST PNP, and full wound closure was observed at 36 h. According to an analysis conducted using ImageJ, 96.88% of the group had AST PNP, followed by free AST (76.22%), blank PNP (51.07%), and control (51.03%). The results of the oxidative stress assay illustrate how AST PNP provides protection under stressful conditions. Additionally, an AST PNP-loaded gelatin scaffold was assessed in diabetic wound-bearing rats for in vivo wound healing. The findings revealed that the wounds in all treatment groups, including the control group that received PBS, displayed indications of healing. However, the AST PNP-treated wound closed more quickly after day 15 and completely closed on day 21. This demonstrates that AST PNP-loaded gelatin scaffolds have outstanding regenerative and healing qualities for diabetic wound care, including accelerated re-epithelialization, controlled collagen synthesis and deposition, and increased COL-1 synthesis, all of which are reported to improve the mechanical strength of regenerated tissue [160]. Additionally, a type of polymer nanoparticle, such as H10P10000NPs crosslinked with hydroquinone and polyethyleneimine, was proposed in this study to kill gram-positive bacteria with biocompatibility.. In this study, H10P10000NPs demonstrate selective efficiency against gram-positive bacteria, notably S. aureus, but showed limited activity against gram-negative bacteria. This selective antibacterial behaviour is attributed to the synergetic effects of the antibacterial properties of polyethyleneimine and hydroquinone. It also effectively disrupts biofilms, which are often resistant to conventional treatments, and contributes to resistance to bacterial infection. They also exhibit antioxidant properties and can scavenge free radicals, protect vital physiological metabolites in the wound area, and reduce inflammation. An interesting feature of H10P10000NPs is their green fluorescence, which enables the selective staining of gram-positive bacteria. This property could be particularly useful in medical diagnostics for distinguishing between gram-positive and gram-negative bacterial infections. In vivo, a mouse model with an S. aureus-infected wound demonstrated that H10P10000NPs accelerated wound healing without any notable biotoxicity or adverse side effects, highlighting their potential as a safe therapeutic option [161]. Another study of a hydrogel formulation loaded with polymeric nanoparticles (PoNPs) of simvastatin (SIM) for tropical wound healing applications. The results showed that the SIM-loaded PoNPs contained 86.4% of the intended drug content, indicating high drug-loading efficacy. Simultaneously, SIM-PoNPs-loaded hydrogel had a high drug content of about 97.25 ± 0.002%, which indicates an effective formulation that maintains drug integrity. Histopathological studies confirmed the efficacy of the SIMPoNP-loaded hydrogels in promoting wound healing. On day 11 post-wound creation, the formation of a normal epithelial layer was observed, indicating significant tissue regeneration and healing properties [162]. If left untreated, foot ulcers can result in partial or total limb amputation. To produce an optimal formulation based on size, zeta potential, and drug release in comparison with PLGA-based NPs, Maatouk et al. devised and optimized a method for generating Alg and AlgSulf/PCL double-emulsion NPs. When compared to the control, cytotoxicity studies showed that the NPs preserved the high viability of HaCaT cells. Additionally, they confirmed that biomimetic chemicals, such as AlgSulf, boost CTGF and IGF-I, which are involved in cellular migration, proliferation, and matrix deposition [163]. Another treatment for chronic diabetic wounds involves a new oxygen-generating system called HA-L-NB/PFE@cp, which uses a light-triggering hyaluronic acid-based (HAL-L-NB) hydrogel and biological photosystem chloroplast (cp)/conjugated polymer composite nanoparticles (PFE-1-NPs@cp). The hydrogel can be applied precisely because it is activated by light. Hydrogels contain conjugated polymers that specifically increase the electron transport rate in photosystem II (PS II) by overlapping their emission spectrum with photosynthetic organism’s absorption. As a result of this improvement, the rate of photosynthesis increases beyond what is possible with natural chloroplasts. To alleviate the hypoxic conditions commonly observed in diabetic wounds, accelerated photosynthesis results in higher oxygen release, and this locally elevated oxygen level encourages cell migration. To prevent infection, the system also includes components of photodynamic treatment, which efficiently eliminate microorganisms. Hydrogel technology efficiently reduces hypoxia, fights bacterial infection, and improves cellular activity according to both in vitro and in vivo validation experiments. Significantly faster wound healing results from the combined benefits of increased oxygen delivery, better cell migration, and potent antibacterial activity. Since there are few studies on burn wounds, a recent study focused on designing a composite therapy using advanced nanotechnology to effectively manage burn wounds (BW) [164]. Curcumin (CUR) and quercetin (QUE) co-loaded nanoparticles (HA-CUR-QUE-CSNPs) functionalized with hyaluronic acid (HA) were created. Compared to unfunctionalized CSNPs, HA-functionalized CUR-QUE-CSNPs exhibited regulated release kinetics and Fickian diffusion, according to in vivo drug release data. HA-functionalized CSNP appeared to have a greater capacity for tissue regeneration, as evidenced by a discernible increase in MC3T3-E1 cell proliferation over time (110% confluence on day 21). An in vivo study in Wistar rats confirmed that HA-functionalized CSNPs improved wound healing performance. The wound closure rate was the highest among all tested groups at 25% on day 7, 45% on day 14, 67% on day 21, and 98% on day 28. By demonstrating minimal inflammatory cell infiltration (which could impede the wound healing process), signs of re-epithelialization (wound closure), ECM formation, fibroblast infiltration at the wound site, granulation tissue formation, angiogenesis, and collagen deposition, the tissue regeneration potential of HA-functionalized CSNPs was validated by histological examination [165]. Scholars have also extensively employed a number of additional biomaterials, such as asiaticoside polymeric nanoparticles for effective diabetic wound healing [160], nano-enzyme-reinforced copper coordination polymer nanoparticles [166], and lipase-sensitive fusidic acid polymeric nanoparticles based hydrogel [167], etc.

Carbon nanotubes

The use of carbon nanotubes (CNTs), a quasi-one-dimensional allotrope of carbon, is increasing. Depending on their chirality, single-walled nanotubes (SWNTs), double-walled nanotubes (DWNTs), and multi-walled nanotubes (MWNTs) can be metallic, semimetallic, or semiconducting. Despite significant concerns regarding their toxicity, CNTs are increasingly being explored for biomedical applications [168]. Carbon nanotubes have bactericidal mechanisms that use oxidative stress and stress damage to kill germ cells. The antibacterial activity of SWCNTs was superior to that of DWNTs and MWCNTs. Its specific surface area increases as its size decreases, strengthening its bonding with the microbial cell wall or membrane [169]. The CNTs are cylindrical and composed of sp2-hybridized rolled graphene sheets. CNTs are frequently produced via chemical vapor deposition, laser ablation, or arc discharge [170]. One study focused on creating an advanced wound dressing using a hybrid composite of carboxylated multiwalled carbon nanotubes (CMWCNT) and curcumin (Cur) embedded in a nanofibrous matrix composed of polyamide 6 (PA6) and poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), called the PA6/PVA/CMWCNT-Cur nanofibrous composite. This type of dressing should have strong antimicrobial properties, reliable mechanical qualities, and the ability to retain moisture while absorbing exudate. The PA6 nanofibers' wettability, physical characteristics, and biodegradability were all enhanced by the addition of PVA and CMWCNT-Cur. The antibacterial properties of the released curcumin are attributed to this composite's high antibacterial activity, which inhibits the growth of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, S. aureus and E. coli, respectively. Using the L929 cell line, cell culture studies showed that the PA6/PVA/CMWCNT-Cur0.75 combination dramatically increased cell migration and proliferation, which are essential for efficient wound healing. In particular, the formulation containing 0.75% CMWCNT-Cur and the PA6/PVA/CMWCNT-Cur hybrid nanofibrous composites exhibited promise as multipurpose dressings for wound healing, deserving of additional study and possible clinical use. Bacterial biofilm formation can function as a barrier to wound healing by impeding the generation of granular cells, epithelialization, and other inflammatory processes [171]. Murugesan et al. and their group created multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) doped with nitrogen, fluorine, phosphorus, and boron (N, F, P/B), such as NFP-MWCNT and NFB-MWCNT, which can break down bacterial biofilms, a significant obstacle in wound healing. According to the results, doped MWCNTs—NFP-MWCNTs and NFB-MWCNTs—have better antibiofilm action against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, such as K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, E. coli, and B. subtilis. The microcolonies of the treated bacterial pathogens were destroyed by electrostatic contact and ROS production. In vivo testing of Wistar rats has demonstrated exceptional healing capabilities. The electronegativity difference between the heteroatoms caused the doping to produce a surface charge, which is crucial, in addition to the fact that the heteroatoms act as a mineral supply that promotes rat fibroblast granulation and regeneration. This study effectively created a method for producing heteroatom-doped MWCNTs that improved antibiofilm action and hastened wound healing at a reasonable cost [172].

Clinical trials performed on wound healing using nanotechnology

Recent developments in nanotechnology have significantly influenced the development of innovative wound-healing solutions. Clinical studies employing nanotechnological methods are crucial for assessing their effectiveness in promoting wound closure and accelerating the healing process. These trials evaluate both the potential negative effects and long term health consequences of nanotechnological therapies, in addition to their capacity to promote healing. Various nanomaterials, such as carbon-, metal-, and polymer-based nanoparticles have been utilized in these studies. However, standardizing outcome measures is a major obstacle in clinical studies of wound healing.

Standard outcome measures for assessing wound healing efficacy include (1) wound closure time and healing rate, refers to the duration until complete wound closed and re-epithelializes, expressed in days and weeks. It is typically evaluated by calculating the percentage area reduction (PAR) [196]. (2) The re-epithelialization rate is measured using various complementary methods, with histological assessment being the most reliable, which involves looking at tissue samples under a microscope after staining them with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to see how epithelial cells move and grow over the wound area [197]. Non-invasive imaging techniques like digital photography, stereophotogrammetry, and advanced imaging methods such as optical coherence tomography allows for real-time monitoring of how well the epithelial layer is covering the wound [198]. Additionally, in vitro, scratch wound assays measures how fast skin cells called keratinocyte move, usually checked every 24–48 h [199], (3) Granulation tissue formation and quality refers to assessing of the quality and quantity of new connective tissue that fills the wound bed, which is evaluated through tissue colour, texture, and vascular density. Evaluation systems checks granulation tissue using standardized scales that evaluate tissue color (red, pink, or pale), consistency (firm or friable), and coverage percentage [197]. To measure blood vessel density, morphology, and perfusion, angiogenesis quantification uses specialized imaging methods such as two-photon excited fluorescence microscopy (TPEF) and laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI) [200–202]. (4) Evaluation of collagen is an essential part of assessing wound healing, especially during the remodeling stage. Histochemical staining using Masson’s trichrome or picrosirius staining allows visualization and quantification of collagen deposition, and with the ability to differentiate between collagen type I and III [203], (5) Inflammatory markers and infection status are assessed through cytokine profiling from wound exudate or tissue samples, measuring key inflammatory mediators including TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 [204]. Matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activity serves as a biomarker for tissue remodeling and wound progression, with elevated MMP-9 levels having impaired healing [205]. Infection status is determined by bacterial load assessment using quantitative culture methods and molecular techniques; a bacterial count greater than 10^5 colony-forming units per gram of tissue indicates clinical infection [206], (6) There are various assessment tools for pain assessment, the most commonly used pain assessment tool is the visual analog scale (VAS) that typically employs a scale of 0–10 where patients rate their wound-related pain intensity [207]. Combining these different types of assessments gives a full picture of how the wound is healing, which lets doctors and researchers keep track of how well treatments are working or how long it will take the wound to heal and find the best treatment plans for each patient.

An important milestone in the development of nanotechnological solutions for wound healing is the shift from preclinical to clinical stages. To guarantee that the therapeutic potential of these technologies can be fully realized and safely implemented in clinical settings, extensive testing on human beings is required. Data gathered from randomized controlled trials (RCTs), which offer evidence-based efficacy and safety profiles for nanotechnological therapies, corroborate this shift. Clinical trials still face difficulties because of patient dropouts due to adverse events, endpoint failures related to insufficient efficacy, and challenges in patient recruitment and retention, particularly in chronic wound populations with multiple comorbidities, as well as the complexity of human pathophysiology, which may not be fully recreated in preclinical models, despite the enormous promise of nanotechnology in wound healing. These difficulties highlight the necessity of ongoing development and thorough testing of nanotechnological wound-care strategies [208]. Biocompatibility concerns and future perspectives

Regardless of the notable advancements in using nanotechnology for wound healing, numerous substantial hurdles and knowledge gaps must be resolved before translating it from the laboratory to clinical practice. The primary concern of nanotechnology-based wound healing therapy revolves around the long-term biocompatibility and safety of nanomaterials [209, 210]. Cytotoxicity and tissue accumulation are considered the most well-known problems, especially with metallic nanoparticles like silver (Ag) and zinc oxide (ZnO). These substances exhibit outstanding antibacterial qualities at therapeutic doses, but they tend to build up in tissue and stay in contact with cells for longer periods, which makes us wonder about their long-term safety profiles. Subsequently, understanding their systemic distribution and clearance mechanisms over an extended period of time is vital for maintaining patient safety and preventing unanticipated adverse effects [211, 212]. Another crutial area that needs to be addressed is the gaps in understanding nanoparticle-biomolecule interaction at fundamental levels [213, 214]. Protein corona formation is a fundamental yet poorly understood phenomenon where nanoparticles rapidly absorb proteins and biomolecules upon contact with the biological fluid [215, 216]. This dynamic protein corona significantly affects how cells uptake nanoparticles and their effectiveness as drugs, but it remains largely unpredictable for most combinations of nanomaterials and biomolecules [217].

Looking towards future trends, the field of personalized nanomedicine signifies a promising area wherein nanoparticle composition can be tailored to individual patient’s wound characteristics, genetic profiles, and response to therapy [218, 219]. This personalized approach could optimize therapeutic outcomes while minimizing adverse effects by precisely matching nanomaterial properties to patient needs. Another technological development is the creation of smart and responsive nanomaterials that precisely release therapeutic agents in response to specific wound microenvironments. This includes pH-sensitive nanoparticles that release drugs in response to infection related pH changes, enzyme-responsive systems that activate in the presence of specific wound enzymes, and temperature-responsive materials that adjust drug release on local inflammatory levels [220]. Such systems will enhance treatment specificity and reduce off-target effects. Additionally, combining machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) can speed up the development and refinement of new nanotherapies by optimizing treatment protocols and predicting therapeutic outcomes [221, 222]. AI can predict nanoparticle behavior, screen for potential cytotoxicity, and optimize synthesis parameters, leading to more effective cycles [223]. Lastly, to successfully overcome the current obstacles and bring these cutting-edge nanotechnologies from bench to bedside, it will be crucial to promote interdisciplinary collaborations among materials scientists, biologists, clinicians, and regulatory agencies.

Conclusion

This comprehensive review emphasizes how nanotechnology, specifically nanoparticles, has led to a transformative era in wound healing by providing therapeutic potential for promoting tissue regeneration. Advances in a various nanomaterial classes, including metallic nanoparticles (silver, gold, zinc oxide), polymeric systems, lipid-based carriers, and carbon-based materials, have produced significant advantages and established nanotechnology as a key component in wound treatment. These developments have progressively improved the physicochemical properties of nanomaterials, enabling them to more effectively mimic the complexity of the extracellular matrix and facilitate accelerated and more efficient healing processes [224]. These developments are driven by their distinct physicochemical characteristics, which include anti-inflammatory modulation, broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties, enhanced delivery of growth factors, promotion of cellular proliferation and collagen deposition. They are at the forefront of these advancements, challenging the conventional therapies that often fail to address them. However, there are still many issues in this field that need to be resolved before it can be widely used in clinical settings. Concerns such as nanoparticle accumulation, clearance mechanisms, and potential for organ toxicity require further investigation to bridge the gap between laboratory success and clinical standardization [210]. The complex interaction between the nanoparticles and biomolecules in a dynamic wound environment needs further research to optimize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing adverse effects [213]. Developing standardized characterization protocols for nanoparticle manufacturing and biocompatibility evolution is crucial to improving clinical stability [225]. Despite the promising preclinical evidence and early clinical successes, we need robust, well well-designed randomized controlled trials for comprehensive safety and efficacy profiles, which are necessary for widespread clinical adoption of nanotechnology-based wound therapies. These studies must include long-term follow-up assessments to fully characterize the safety profile of nanomaterial-based treatments. The field of wound healing using nanotechnology is at a crucial juncture where clinical demands and scientific advancements collide. The interaction of clinical medicine, biotechnology, and advanced material science offers unprecedented opportunities to address the worldwide burden of chronic wounds and enhance patient outcomes. It will take conventional research to successfully translate these technologies from lab to clinical settings, which will ultimately change primary care practice and enhance the lives of millions of patients globally.
