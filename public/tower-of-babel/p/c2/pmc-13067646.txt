Genetic variants in Ecuadorian families: unraveling the complexity of non-syndromic cleft lip and palate a case series
Authors: Darwin Luna-Chonata, Auki Guaillas-Japon, Nicolás Almeida, Vanessa I Romero, Benjamin Arias-Almeida, Iván Palacios
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCleft lip with or without cleft palate (CL ± P) is a common congenital anomaly with complex genetic origins.Case presentationThis study presents a genetic case series of three Ecuadorian families with non-syndromic cleft lip and/or palate analyzed using whole-exome sequencing (WES). We identified rare or novel variants in genes with established or emerging roles in craniofacial development. Bioinformatic analyses—while not supported by functional validation—helped prioritize several candidate variants, including a novel KIF7 variant.ConclusionThese findings provide exploratory genetic data from an underrepresented Latin American population and highlight the need to include diverse cohorts in genomic research to improve diagnosis and genetic counseling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-07796-8.Keywords: Non-syndromic cleft lip and palate, Genetics, FGFR1, KIF7, FRAS1, WES (Whole-exome sequencing)

Introduction

Cleft lip with or without cleft palate (CL ± P) is a common congenital malformation caused by incomplete fusion of facial structures during embryogenesis. This disruption typically occurs between the 4th and 12th weeks of gestation and results in a wide spectrum of anatomical presentations. The global prevalence of CL ± P varies by geography and ancestry, with rates in South America averaging around 1.5 per 1,000 live births, and higher estimates reported in Ecuador [1–3].

Non-syndromic CL ± P (NSCLP), which occurs in the absence of other congenital anomalies, accounts for approximately 70% of cases. It arises from complex interactions between genetic predisposition and environmental exposures [4]. Although multiple genes and pathways—such as WNT, SHH, BMP, TGF-β, and FGF—have been implicated, the genetic architecture of NSCLP remains poorly understood [5]. Syndromic forms of CL ± P often follow Mendelian inheritance patterns, whereas NSCLP exhibits complex, multifactorial inheritance [5, 6].

Despite extensive research in European and Asian populations, Latin American populations remain underrepresented in genetic studies of NSCLP. Ecuador presents a unique genetic context due to its population structure shaped by Indigenous, European, and African ancestry components. Investigating genetic variants in Ecuadorian families offers an opportunity to uncover novel or rare variants and contribute to a more globally inclusive understanding of NSCLP.

This study examines three Ecuadorian families with familial NSCLP using whole-exome sequencing (WES). This work is designed as a case series and is intended to be hypothesis-generating, providing exploratory observations rather than population-level conclusions. By examining a poorly studied population, this work offers insight into the genetic diversity underlying NSCLP and underscores the importance of including Latin American cohorts in genomic research [7, 8].

Case presentation

Patient selection and environmental exposure

Fundación Padre Carolo, an organization that provides surgical care for children with NSCLP, referred four families with affected siblings for this study. All families provided written consent for participation and publication of findings. This consent allowed the collection of clinical data and parental interviews about potential environmental exposures, including smoking, alcohol use, diabetes, obesity, antiepileptic medications, and possible contact with resins, paints, or pesticides, as well as parental occupations during pregnancy. One family was excluded as the patient presented with additional clinical findings.

Two families, residing in remote rural areas without telephone or cellular service, could not be contacted after initial communication. None of the families reported maternal obesity, diabetes, or use of antiepileptic drugs. Parental occupations—housewife, grocery store worker, and security guard—did not suggest occupational exposure risks. Families 2, living in the capital city, reported no known environmental exposures. In contrast, Families 1 and 3, residing in rural Amazonian towns, could not confirm or exclude such exposures. Mothers did not recall smoking or alcohol consumption during pregnancy and generally described inadequate prenatal care, with no available documentation confirming infectious disease testing. Since 2017, Ecuador has mandated the fortification of all commercially milled wheat flour with folic acid. However, access to prenatal folic acid supplementation remains limited across the country.

Whole exome sequencing analysis

We used a capture-based clinical exome sequencing approach, performed by a commercial provider (Mendelics). The target enrichment was based on a comprehensive clinical exome panel, which includes the exonic regions of approximately 4,800 disease-associated genes.

Coverage

The average sequencing depth was 100x, with > 98% of the targeted regions covered at ≥ 20x.

Variants analyzed

We focused exclusively on small variants—single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and short insertions/deletions (InDels). Copy number variants (CNVs) were not analyzed in this study. Only variants that passed quality filters (“PASS”) in the VCF files were considered for further interpretation.

Bioinformatics pipeline and versions

Raw reads were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA-MEM, version 0.7.17). Duplicates were marked, and files were sorted and merged using Picard tools (version 2.23.3). Variant calling was performed with GATK HaplotypeCaller (version 4.1.9.0). Variant annotation was conducted using ANNOVAR (2020–06–08 version), incorporating multiple population databases (e.g., gnomAD v2.1.1) and functional prediction tools including PolyPhen-2, SIFT and CADD (Combined Annotation-Dependent Depletion) scores into the annotation process to strengthen computational assessment of variant impact [9–11].

Variant prioritization and interpretation

Given the small cohort size and the case-series design, our analytical approach is exploratory and hypothesis-generating, not intended to support inferential or causal claims. For each family, we prioritized rare variants (MAF < 0.01) with functional consequences—such as non-synonymous, stop-gain, frameshift, or splice-site changes—and interpreted them according to the 2015 ACMG-AMP guidelines. We also considered variant conservation, predicted protein impact informed by PolyPhen-2, SIFT, and CADD scores, which together contributed to the computational evaluation of deleteriousness, and supporting literature. Where possible, we assessed segregation within families or evaluated consistency with the clinical phenotype. Prioritized variants are detailed in Supplementary Table 1 and a diagram of the bioinformatic analysis is summarized in Supplementary Figure S1.

Analytical approach

The analytical approach differed between families due to logistical and financial constraints. Singleton WES was used for Families 1, 2 and 3, followed by targeted Sanger confirmation in available relatives. We acknowledge that using singleton data limited our ability to distinguish inherited from de novo variants and increased the likelihood of identifying variants of uncertain relevance. Additionally, targeted Sanger sequencing provided partial segregation data for Families 2 and 3. We defined supportive segregation as presence of the variant in affected relatives and absence in unaffected relatives when samples were available. In Family 2, the FGFR1 variant was confirmed in heterozygous state in the affected sibling and in the asymptomatic father, suggesting incomplete penetrance or variable expressivity, both well-described in FGFR1-associated craniofacial phenotypes. In Family 3, the KIF7 variant was present in both affected siblings but absent in the unaffected siblings and in the mother. Although a paternal sample was not available, the observed segregation pattern suggests paternal inheritance (Supplementary Figure S2).

Both individuals experienced cleft palate (HP:0000175) accompanied by bilateral cleft lip, surgically corrected during childhood. No additional malformations were noted (Fig. 1a). Family 2

The second family includes a 15-year-old male and his 13-year-old brother, both of whom presented with bilateral cleft lip and palate. Both siblings were delivered at term. The older brother was born at 37 weeks to a 16-year-old mother. Both siblings underwent initial corrective surgeries during their first year of life, followed by additional procedures annually until age four. According to the mother, the main developmental challenges involved speech articulation difficulties (HP:0002167), and no additional malformations were reported.

Despite the absence of visible malformations in the parents of siblings, both reported a positive family history of other conditions. One of the mother’s sisters had a full-term pregnancy that ended in neonatal death due to an intestinal malformation, likely gastroschisis with imperforate anus based on the mother’s description. Another sister of the mother had a premature birth, with the neonate's cause of death remaining unknown.

On the paternal side, the father has nine siblings, one of whom had a child born with a cleft lip and palate. No signs of a specific syndrome were noted in the family, although additional relatives could not be contacted for evaluation due to family circumstances (Fig. 1b).

WES identified 849 rare variants predicted to alter protein function through missense, stop-gain, frameshift, or canonical splice-site changes; selected those with minor allele frequency (< 0.01) in various databases; and detected a heterozygous missense pathogenic variant NM_023110.2:c.1865C > T (p.Arg622Gln) in exon 1 of Fibroblast Growth Factor Receptor 1 (FGFR1) gene (Fig. 1b). Sanger sequencing confirmed the FGFR1 variant in heterozygous state in both affected siblings. Additionally, the variant was detected in the clinically unaffected father, consistent with reduced penetrance or subclinical expression of FGFR1-related phenotypes. The variant has a frequency of 0 in the Latin-American population in gnomAD. The variant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and is present in the dbsnp database (rs1815818452) (Table 1).

Family 3

The third case involves two siblings, aged 19 and 17, both born to the same parents and presenting with unilateral cleft lip and palate. Unfortunately, contact was lost during the investigation, precluding the gathering of any additional family details.

WES identified 775 rare variants predicted to affect protein function through missense, stop-gain, frameshift, or canonical splice-site changes; selected those with minor allele frequency (< 0.01) in various databases; and detected a novel heterozygous missense variant NM_198525.3:c.3257G > A (p.Ala1086Val) in exon 16 of the Kinesin Family Member 7 (KIF7) gene. The variant has no frequency in gnomAD. Algorithms developed to predict the effect of missense changes on protein structure and function result in the following: PolyPhen-2 = "Damaging"(1). The amino acid residue arginine is found in multiple animal species since zebrafish, suggesting that this missense negatively affects the function of the protein. Targeted Sanger segregation analysis showed that the KIF7 variant was present in both affected siblings and absent in the unaffected siblings and in the mother. Although paternal DNA was not available, these findings suggest that the variant was most likely inherited from the father (Table 1).

Discussion and conclusion

As a hypothesis-generating case series, our analysis identified genetic variants in genes previously implicated in NSCLP. These findings are exploratory and not intended to provide inferential evidence, but rather to document observations that may guide future research. Below is a brief review of the functions and relationship that these genes (FGFR1, KIF7 and FRAS1) have with NSCLP.

Interpretation of identified variants

WES of the three families revealed rare variants in three genes: FGFR1, KIF7, and FRAS1. Given the limited evidence supporting these gene–cleft associations in humans, these findings should be interpreted as potential candidate variants rather than confirmed contributors to NSCLP.

FGFR1 is essential for embryogenesis, influencing cell proliferation, migration, and tissue patterning [12].

KIF7 is a kinesin motor protein that regulates Hedgehog signaling and Gli transcription factors, both essential for neural and craniofacial development [13].

FRAS1 encodes an extracellular matrix protein involved in epidermal-dermal adhesion and has been linked to Fraser syndrome, which can include craniofacial anomalies [14, 15].

FRAS1 encodes an extracellular matrix protein involved in epidermal-dermal adhesion and has been linked to Fraser syndrome, which can include craniofacial anomalies [14, 15].

In Family 1, a missense variant c.7573C > T (p.Arg2525Cys) in FRAS1 was identified in affected siblings. While FRAS1 mutations are typically associated with autosomal recessive Fraser syndrome, no syndromic features were observed in this family. The rarity and predicted impact of this FRAS1 variant make it a potential NSCLP risk variant, although any contribution remains speculative and requires functional confirmation [14, 15].

Family 2 showed one heterozygous variant in FGFR1 (c.1865C > T), affecting amino acid (p.Arg622) in an evolutionarily conserved domain. FGFR1 is known to be expressed in orofacial tissues, and its disruption has been associated with clefting phenotypes; therefore, this variant represents a potential risk variant, but its relevance in this family remains preliminary and not sufficient for causal inference. Notably, this variant was absent in gnomAD’s Latin American dataset, highlighting the need for ancestry-informed variant interpretation [12, 16, 17].

In Family 3, we identified a novel missense variant c.3257G > A (p.Ala1086Val) in KIF7, absent from gnomAD. Although bioinformatic predictions suggest possible functional impact, there is no direct evidence linking this variant to clefting phenotypes [18]. Given that KIF7 has been implicated in craniofacial development through its role in Hedgehog signaling, We consider this a candidate NSCLP risk variant that remains hypothetical and requires functional validation before any causal relationship can be proposed [19].

Overall, our findings include known NSCLP-related gene (FGFR1) and potential NSCLP risk variants (KIF7, FRAS1), all of which require further validation and should be interpreted as exploratory.

Comparison with existing evidence

Our study on Ecuadorian families identifies variants in well-established NSCLP-associated genes—including FGFR1—as well as novel candidates such as KIF7 and FRAS1. The FGFR1 variant identified here further supports its established role in orofacial morphogenesis [12]. The detection of a novel KIF7 variant aligns with previous work in a Brazilian cohort [19], suggesting a possible involvement of this gene in NSCLP etiology. Finally, although the FRAS1 variant remains a VUS, its predicted damaging effect [14] and the known involvement of FRAS1 in craniofacial anomalies in Fraser syndrome [12] justify future functional studies to explore its potential relevance to NSCLP. Together, these findings highlight the genetic heterogeneity underlying this condition across global populations.

We had one additional family that underwent trio-WES analysis, but we excluded it because the proband showed additional clinical findings (e.g., intracranial cysts, hydrocephalus, developmental delay, and café-au-lait macules) suggestive of a complex or potentially syndromic presentation, despite a strong family history of clefting (Supplementary Figure S3a). Trio-WES identified a likely pathogenic IRF6 missense variant (NM_001206696:c.662 T > C; p.Leu221Pro/NM_006147:c.947 T > C; p.Leu316Pro), consistent with the established role of IRF6 in craniofacial development and NSCLP [20, 21]. The proband and mother also carried a JAG2 missense variant, and structural modeling suggested possible disruption of the EGF domain and Notch-related interactions (Supplementary Figures S3b–S4–S5) [14, 20–27]. Given that JAG2 and IRF6 interact in murine models, JAG2 may represent a potential modifier in the presence of IRF6 variation, although this remains speculative and requires functional validation [28–30].

Population-specific considerations

The genetic architecture of NSCLP is strongly shaped by ancestry and geography, with notable differences in prevalence and risk alleles across global populations [7]. Our focus on Ecuadorian families emphasizes the importance of expanding genetic studies to underrepresented regions, particularly within Latin America. Although databases such as gnomAD provide essential frequency estimates, the absence of the FGFR1 variant (rs1815818452) in the Latin-American subset illustrates how population-level resources may fail to capture variants enriched—or potentially unique—in specific regional or ancestral groups. Moreover, the higher prevalence of oral clefts reported in Ecuador compared with other South American countries [3] suggests a distinct interplay of genetic and environmental factors in this population. Prior work in Latin American cohorts has already demonstrated the value of studying these groups, including the discovery of population-specific susceptibility loci in Mexican and Brazilian populations [31] and unique allele frequency distributions for established NSCLP genes [32]. Investigating such diverse populations not only improves the precision of genetic counseling but also contributes to a more comprehensive and globally accurate understanding of NSCLP pathogenesis.

Limitations and environmental contributions

These limitations are consistent with the intended scope of a hypothesis-generating case series, which aims to report observations and potential NSCLP candidate variants rather than establish definitive genetic interpretations. We do not aim to define the broader genetic background of the Ecuadorian population or draw generalizable claims. Instead, we present family-level findings that underscore the value of targeted genomic investigations in understudied populations and offer preliminary gene–variant hypotheses for NSCLP.

This case series is limited by incomplete phenotyping in some families, particularly due to loss of follow-up. Family 2 reported congenital gastrointestinal anomalies in relatives. These factors may affect the accuracy of classifying all cases as non-syndromic and should be considered when interpreting the variants.

In addition, our interpretation of variant pathogenicity relies solely on in silico predictions, without functional validation, which limits the strength of these conclusions.

Environmental factors may also contribute to NSCLP risk [33]. Across families, mothers reported inadequate prenatal care and limited access to folic acid supplementation, with uncertain exposure histories in some rural cases. While no major maternal conditions or occupational risks were identified, these self-reported data are insufficient to support firm conclusions [34, 35]. As such, these observations should be considered exploratory. Larger, well-powered studies will be necessary to clarify gene–environment interactions in NSCLP.

Although several identified variants occur in genes with known roles in craniofacial development, others remain candidate variants whose relevance to NSCLP requires replication and functional validation. Future studies should include experimental assays to confirm the biological relevance of the prioritized variants and their mechanistic roles in craniofacial development.
