Integrative analysis identifies radiotherapy resistance-associated CAF subtypes shaping the tumor immune landscape in breast cancer
Authors: Yingyi Li, Zhihui Zhang, Xin Liu, Yixin Cheng, Kang Li, Weihong Zheng, Zhenyu He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) play a critical role in the tumor microenvironment (TME) of breast cancer (BC), influencing immune evasion and treatment resistance. This study aims to explore the heterogeneity of CAFs and their impact on prognosis and radiotherapy resistance.MethodsSingle-cell RNA sequencing was used to analyze CAF subsets in BC. Three major CAF subpopulations were identified, including inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs) and matrix-modulating CAFs (mmCAFs), which were enriched in Scissor⁺ cells associated with prognosis. A CAF-derived gene signature was constructed to stratify BC patients into transcriptome clusters with varying TME compositions. Survival analysis and multivariate regression were employed to assess the prognostic value of the gene signature and predict radiotherapy resistance. Validation was conducted using METABRIC and GEO cohorts.ResultsThe iCAF and mmCAF subsets exhibited high stemness potential and facilitated immune rejection through interactions with epithelial and immune cells. The CAF gene signature identified high-risk clusters with increased stromal infiltration, suppressed CD8⁺ T-cell responses, and activation of immune-suppressive pathways, including VEGF. These high-risk clusters were significantly associated with radiotherapy resistance. A five-gene CAF risk model was developed, which robustly predicted radiotherapy resistance and poor prognosis in multiple cohorts. Validation confirmed the expression of risk genes, such as ENO1, which correlates with poor survival, TP53 mutation, and resistance to radiotherapy.ConclusionsCAFs play a pivotal role in TME remodeling, immune evasion, and treatment resistance in BC. The CAF-derived gene signature offers a clinically applicable tool for risk stratification, predicting prognosis and radiotherapy resistance. Targeting CAFs may provide new therapeutic opportunities for improving breast cancer treatment outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15071-2.

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the malignant tumors with the highest incidence rate in the world, highly threatening women’s health. According to the statistics of the World Health Organization (WHO), about 2.3 million new cases of breast cancer are detected each year, accounting for 15.5 per cent of all new cancer cases among women worldwide [1]. Radiotherapy is one of the most effective treatments for breast cancer, especially for early stage localized breast cancer. Radiotherapy helps to eliminate residual tumor cells, especially as an adjuvant treatment after surgery [2]. Therefore, most of breast cancer patients receive radiotherapy to improve survival and reduce the chance of recurrence [3].

The tumor microenvironment (TME) has emerged as a key driver of BC heterogeneity and therapeutic failure [4, 5]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), the most abundant stromal component in solid tumors, play a central role in modulating immune infiltration, promoting extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and facilitating tumor invasion and metastasis [6]. CAFs are not a uniform population; instead, they exhibit significant transcriptional and functional heterogeneity, with different subtypes exerting either pro-tumor or immunomodulatory functions depending on context [7].

Recent single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) studies have uncovered diverse CAF states in breast cancer, including inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs), and antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs), each defined by distinct gene expression programs [8, 9]. However, in BC, the specific CAF subsets that contribute to disease aggressiveness and immune evasion remain largely undefined. Importantly, certain CAF subtypes have been shown to shape the immune landscape by excluding cytotoxic T cells [10], promoting regulatory T cell expansion, and secreting immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-6, CXCL12, and CD40 [7, 11].

Furthermore, studies in pancreatic and lung cancers have demonstrated that CAFs can directly modulate therapeutic response and tumor evolution by establishing immune-excluded or desmoplastic niches [12, 13]. Whether similar fibroblast-driven programs operate in BC, particularly in relation to prognosis, remains to be explored. Integrative approaches combining scRNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics, and clinical outcome data have provided new opportunities to link stromal cell states with disease behavior and patient survival.

In this study, we performed a comprehensive single-cell analysis of CAFs in BC using publicly available datasets. By integrating transcriptional profiling, prognostic modeling, and cell-cell communication inference, we identified a specific CAF subset that is strongly associated with poor prognosis and enriched in BC tumors resistant to radiotherapy. We constructed a CAF-derived gene signature, validated its predictive value across METABRIC RNA-seq datasets, and further confirmed the expression of key genes in clinical samples by IHC and qPCR. Our findings provide a novel framework for understanding CAF heterogeneity in BC and suggest potential targets for stroma-directed immunomodulation.

Materials and methods

Single-cell RNA sequencing data acquisition and preprocessing

Single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data from BC tumors were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number GSE176078. Raw gene expression matrices were processed using the Seurat R package (v4.3.0). Low-quality cells were excluded by filtering cells with < 200 genes, > 6,000 UMIs, or > 10% mitochondrial gene expression. Data normalization was performed using the “LogNormalize” method with a scale factor of 10,000. Highly variable genes were identified with FindVariableFeatures, and principal component analysis (PCA) was used for dimensionality reduction. Cell clustering was conducted using the Louvain algorithm (FindClusters, resolution = 0.5), and UMAP was used for visualization. Cell types were annotated based on canonical markers including CD79A/CD79B (B cells), ACKR1/PECAM1 (endothelial), EPCAM (epithelial), PDGFRB/ACTA2 (fibroblasts), CD3D/CD4 (CD4 + T cells), CD3D/CD8A (CD8 + T cells), RGS5/TAGLN(PVLs), CD79A/JCHAIN(PCs), and CD14/CD68 (myeloid).

CAF re-clustering and subtype identification

Fibroblast clusters were subset from the integrated Seurat object and re-clustered. The top 2,000 variable genes were used for PCA, followed by UMAP projection and Louvain clustering. CAF subtypes were annotated as: iCAFs: APOD+, MEG3+, CXCL12+; mmCAFs: ACTA2+, COL1A1+, POSTN+; apCAFs: HLA0-B+, HLA-DRB1+, HLA-DRA+. DEGs for each subtype were identified using FindAllMarkers with Wilcoxon rank-sum test (log2FC > 1, adjusted p < 0.05). Marker expression was visualized by heatmap.

Prognostic scoring using scissor

Scissor (v2.0) was used to link single-cell data with GEO bulk RNA-seq and survival data. GSE199633 FPKM data and survival metadata were used as input. In all clinical analyses, radioresistance was predefined as post-radiotherapy locoregional recurrence (LRR). Specifically, among patients who received adjuvant breast/chest-wall and regional nodal irradiation, those who developed ipsilateral breast tumor or regional nodal recurrence within 5 years after completion of RT were labeled radioresistant, whereas patients remaining LRR-free for ≥ 5 years were labeled radiosensitive. Patients who did not receive RT or had missing RT details were excluded from the primary resistance labeling. Scissor employs Cox regression to assign each single cell a label (Scissor + or Scissor–) based on its correlation with high- or low-risk patients. Input gene sets were normalized by z-score. Cells with positive Scissor coefficients were defined as high-risk associated. Cell type enrichment in Scissor + cells was visualized to identify risk-associated CAF subtypes.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis using Monocle2

To infer the differentiation hierarchy of CAFs, Monocle2 (v2.20.0) was applied. Fibroblasts were converted to Monocle CellDataSet via SeuratWrappers. Gene ordering was based on highly variable genes (dispersion > 1, mean expression > 0.1). Dimensionality was reduced via DDRTree, and cells were ordered along pseudotime using orderCells. The root node was defined in the iCAF cluster. Pseudotime plots were generated using plot_cell_trajectory. Gene dynamics were assessed via plot_genes_in_pseudotime. Branch-dependent expression changes were assessed using BEAM analysis and visualized via plot_genes_branched_heatmap.

Stemness Estimation using CytoTRACE

CAF differentiation potential was assessed using CytoTRACE (v0.3.3). The unnormalized fibroblast expression matrix was exported from Seurat and analyzed with CytoTRACE default settings. Higher CytoTRACE scores indicated higher stem-like potential. Scores were visualized by violin plots and UMAP. Group comparisons between CAF subtypes were performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Cell–cell communication analysis using cellchat

To characterize cell–cell signaling, CellChat (v1.6.1) was applied. The normalized expression matrix and Seurat-defined clusters were input. CellChat was used to compute communication probabilities based on a curated ligand-receptor database. The computeCommunProb and computeCommunProbPathway functions were used to calculate interaction strengths across pathways. Differential signaling networks between CAF-high and CAF-low groups were visualized using circle plots (netVisual_circle), interaction comparisons (compareInteractions) , overall pathway information flows (ranknet). Key CAF-related pathways (e.g., CD40, VEGF, PERIOSTIN, CXCL12) were extracted and compared.

Stromal and immune infiltration analysis using ESTIMATE

GSE199633 FPKM RNA-seq data were analyzed using the estimate R package (v1.0.13). The estimateScore function was used to calculate StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATEScore. Tumor purity was inferred from ESTIMATEScore. Scores were compared between CAF-defined molecular clusters using Wilcoxon tests. Correlation between CAF signature scores and ESTIMATE metrics was assessed using Spearman’s correlation.

Molecular subtyping and signature construction

Marker genes from CAFs were selected for molecular clustering. GSE199633 samples were clustered using ConsensusClusterPlus (v1.58.0) based on these genes. The optimal number of clusters was determined by cumulative distribution function (CDF) plots. CAF signature scores were calculated using the GSVA package (v1.44.2) and single-sample GSEA. Survival differences across clusters were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier plots and log-rank tests.

Prognostic model construction with LASSO regression and validated with the METABRIC database

Candidate genes from the CAF signature were screened using univariate Cox regression. Significant genes (p < 0.05) were input into LASSO Cox regression using the glmnet package (alpha = 1, 10-fold cross-validation). A five-gene prognostic model was established. Risk scores were computed for each patient as the sum of normalized gene expression × LASSO coefficient. Patients were divided into high- and low-risk groups based on median risk score. Survival was analyzed using survival and survminer, and predictive performance was evaluated using time-dependent ROC curves (timeROC). Finally, the validation data was sourced from 210 breast cancer patients who received radiotherapy in the METABRIC database.

BC patient sample collection

FFPE and fresh BC tumor tissues were collected from patients undergoing surgery at Sun Yat-sen university Cancer Center between 2022 and 2024. Inclusion criteria: age 18–70, histologically confirmed BC, radiotherapy. Tissue collection was approved by Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Tumor regions were pathologically evaluated, and CAF-rich areas were selected for further experiments.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining and Immunofluorescence (IF)

FFPE Sect. (5 μm) were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed using 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min. Sections were blocked in 5% BSA for 30 min, incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: ENO1 (1:500, Proteintech 11204-1-AP), CD24 (1:600, Proteintech 10600-1-AP), IGHG1 (1:1000, Abcam ab200699), TXDC17 (1:100, AffinitiY #DF15119) and P4HA1 (1:100, AffinitiY #DF12219).

For immunohistochemistry (IHC), after washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Dako) were applied for 1 h. DAB substrate was used for visualization, and hematoxylin was used for counterstaining.

For immunofluorescence (IF), sections were incubated with primary antibodies followed by DyLight 488- and Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen) for 45 min at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000) for 1 min, and images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope.

Sections were scored independently by two double-blind pathologists using a semiquantitative method (0 to 3+) based on staining intensity and percentage of positive stromal cells. For immunofluorescence, the percentage of double-positive cells (expressing both markers) was calculated by counting cells in six random fields of view at 20x magnification and expressed as a percentage of total cells.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Fresh BC tissues were dissociated with collagenase IV (1 mg/mL, 37 °C, 40 min) and filtered to obtain single-cell suspensions. CAFs were enriched by depleting CD45 + and EpCAM + cells using magnetic beads. RNA was extracted using RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen). Reverse transcription was done using PrimeScript RT Kit (Takara). qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara) on a Bio-Rad CFX96 system.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R v4.2.2. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used for pairwise comparisons, Kruskal–Wallis for multi-group comparisons. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were compared using log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazards regression was used for univariate and multivariate survival modeling. Time-dependent ROC curves were computed using the timeROC package. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All plots were generated using ggplot2, ComplexHeatmap, and survminer.

Results

Single-cell atlas of the tumor microenvironment(TME) in BC and cell type–specific enrichment scores

We analyzed a publicly available scRNA-seq dataset comprising 26 patient samples. Following batch correction and dimensionality reduction, UMAP visualization was performed for each sample individually (Fig. 1A), as well as for the merged dataset labeled by cluster ID (Fig. 1B). Based on canonical marker genes, we identified and annotated nine major cell types, including epithelial cells, fibroblasts, myeloid cells, endothelial cells, B cells, CD4⁺ T cells, CD8⁺ T cells, plasma cells (PCs), and PVLs (Fig. 1C–D).

To dissect fibroblast heterogeneity, we reclustered fibroblasts and identified three transcriptionally distinct CAF subsets: inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs), and matrix-modulating CAFs (mmCAFs) (Fig. 2C). These subsets exhibited clear spatial segregation in UMAP space, and a heatmap of representative genes highlighted their unique transcriptional profiles (Fig. 2D). CytoTRACE analysis showed that apCAFs displayed higher stemness potential compared to other CAF subsets (Fig. 2E), and pseudotime trajectory analysis placed them at the terminal end of CAF differentiation (Fig. 2F). Moreover, CellChat analysis of cell–cell communication networks revealed that apCAFs engaged in more frequent and stronger interactions with epithelial and immune cells (Fig. 2G–H), suggesting that this subset plays an active role in modulating the tumor microenvironment and promoting immune escape.

Prognostic relevance of CAF subsets and development of a CAF-based risk model

Applying Scissor to CAF subsets revealed that prognosis-associated (Scissor⁺) cells were predominantly enriched in iCAFs and mmCAFs (Fig. 3A–B), implicating these subsets as key contributors to poor prognosis.

Differential gene expression analysis between Scissor⁺ and Scissor⁻ fibroblasts revealed significantly upregulated and downregulated genes (Fig. 3C). KEGG enrichment analysis showed that upregulated genes were involved in cytoskeletal remodeling, metabolism, and tumor-related signaling pathways, while downregulated genes were associated with MAPK signaling, complement activation, and immune response (Fig. 3D).

A CAF risk score was then derived directly from genes enriched in Scissor⁺ CAFs. Applied to radiotherapy-treated BC cohorts (GSE199633 and TCGA), this simple signature was able to stratify patients into high- and low-risk groups, with the high-risk group showing significantly poorer survival outcomes (Fig. 3E–F). These results provide independent evidence that the Scissor-identified CAF population is clinically relevant and prognostically meaningful, thereby validating its role as a key determinant of patient outcome.

CAF-driven immunosuppressive networks remodel TIME and predict poor prognosis

CellChat analysis of ligand–receptor interactions showed that CAFs mediated extensive crosstalk with immune and epithelial cells, particularly in tumors with high CAF signature scores. Consensus clustering stratified patients into three groups (Fig. 4A). Among these, Cluster 1 displayed the highest CAF scores (Fig. 4B), an increased proportion of Scissor⁺ cells (Fig. 4C), and greater fibroblast and myeloid cell abundance (Figure S1 A-B). Consistently, Cluster 1 tumors exhibited the strongest intercellular communication (Figure S1 C), with CAFs serving as major hubs interacting with immune and epithelial compartments (Fig. 4D-F). Differential signaling analysis further revealed that CAF-driven communication in Cluster 1 was markedly enhanced through canonical immunomodulatory pathways, including CD40, VEGF, CXCL12, and PERIOSTIN (Figure S1D-E). These pathways are well known to promote stromal activation and suppress adaptive immunity, thereby contributing to tumor progression and immune exclusion [11, 14, 15].

To further elucidate the functional and clinical implications of the CAF signature, we performed unsupervised consensus clustering using CAF-related gene expression in the GSE199633 cohort, identifying three distinct transcriptomic clusters (Fig. 4G-H). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that Cluster 2 patients exhibited significantly worse overall survival than those in Clusters 1 and 3 (Fig. 4I), underscoring the prognostic relevance of the CAF program.

To investigate the biological basis of this poor prognosis, we next examined the tumor immune microenvironment of each cluster. Analysis of the tumor immune microenvironment revealed that Cluster 2 was characterized by a strongly immunosuppressive phenotype. CIBERSORTx showed enrichment of M2-like macrophages and Tregs, accompanied by a marked reduction of cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 4J). Consistently, ESTIMATE confirmed higher stromal and immune scores and lower tumor purity in Cluster 2 (Fig. 4K), indicating extensive non-malignant cell infiltration. Correlation analysis further demonstrated that CAF signature genes were positively associated with Treg and M2 macrophage abundance but negatively correlated with CD8⁺ T cell infiltration (Fig. 4L). Together, this immune-excluded phenotype aligns with the poor prognosis observed in Cluster 2, suggesting that CAF-driven TIME remodeling contributes to immune evasion and tumor progression in BC.

Construction and validation of a CAF-based prognostic model for radiotherapy resistance prediction in BC

To develop clinically relevant prognostic tools for radiotherapy resistance in breast cancer, we constructed a CAF-based gene signature model. Of 139 highly expressed genes associated with cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), 56 genes were significantly associated with radiotherapy resistance in univariate Cox. LASSO regression narrowed these to 9 candidates, and multivariate Cox regression finally identified 5 genes (ENO1, CD24, P4HA1, IGHG1, TXNDC17) that formed the basis of the risk score (Fig. 5A–C). The CAF-based five-gene risk model demonstrated strong prognostic performance in internal cohorts. Stratification of patients into high- and low-risk groups revealed that the high-risk group had markedly shorter recurrence-free survival (RFS) (Fig. 5D–E). The risk score remained an independent prognostic factor in both univariate and multivariate Cox analyses (Fig. 5G-H), and high-risk tumors consistently showed higher expression of CAF-related genes (Fig. 5A). Importantly, the model achieved excellent predictive accuracy, with a time-dependent ROC AUC of 0.957 at 1095 days (Fig. 5F) and a C-index of 0.8592. A nomogram incorporating the CAF risk score with clinical variables provided individualized recurrence prediction, and calibration curves showed close agreement between predicted and observed RFS (Fig. 5I-J).

External validation in the independent METABRIC cohort (n = 210) confirmed the model’s generalizability. High-risk patients experienced significantly worse overall survival (p = 0.018; Fig. 5K), and the risk score retained its status as an independent predictor in multivariate Cox analysis, unaffected by other clinical variables (Figure S2B-C). While the time-dependent ROC yielded a moderate AUC of 0.585 at 12 months (Fig. 5L), the biological consistency of the model was supported by the upregulation of CAF-related genes in high-risk patients (Figure S2A). Furthermore, a nomogram combining the risk score with clinical features showed strong calibration, underscoring the reliability of the model in external validation (Figure S2D-E).

Collectively, these results establish the CAF-based five-gene signature as a robust prognostic model that reliably predicts outcomes of radiotherapy-treated breast cancer patients and provides a clinically relevant tool for risk stratification and personalized management.

Experimental validation of the clinical relevance of the CAF prognostic model

To assess the real-world applicability of our CAF-based prognostic model, we analyzed tumor samples from 20 breast cancer (BC) patients who received radiotherapy. Based on the expression levels of five model genes measured by qPCR, we calculated a CAF risk score for each patient (Fig. 6A). Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups, and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed significantly worse recurrence-free survival (RFS) in the high-risk group (Fig. 6B). Consistently, immunohistochemistry (IHC) performed on paraffin-embedded tumor tissues from the same cohort (Fig. 6C) confirmed the differential expression of the signature genes at the protein level (Fig. 6D. Three of the model genes were predicted to be upregulated in high-risk patients (e.g., ENO1, CD24, and P4HA1), and their protein levels were indeed higher in CAF-rich stromal regions in the high-risk group. In contrast, the remaining two genes showed higher expression in low-risk patients, consistent with their negative coefficients in the model, thereby supporting the predictive accuracy of the signature.

Given that ENO1 had the highest regression coefficient in the model, we further examined its clinical significance. Analysis of TCGA datasets revealed that high ENO1 expression was associated with poor prognosis in breast cancer (Fig. 6E). Additionally, ENO1 was significantly enriched in TP53-mutant compared to non-mutant patients (Fig. 6F). Furthermore, immunofluorescence images showed that CAFs in the high-risk group exhibited higher ENO1 expression and stronger ENO1 intensity compared with those in the low-risk group, suggesting a potential functional role for ENO1 in CAF-driven tumor therapy resistance (Fig. 6G-H).

Discussion

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) have emerged as critical regulators of the tumor microenvironment (TME), beyond their classical roles in extracellular matrix remodeling [16]. In multiple cancers, including breast cancer (BC), CAFs have been implicated in immune evasion, therapeutic resistance, and metastatic progression [17]. In this study, we systematically identified a subset of radiotherapy-resistant-associated CAFs in BC using single-cell transcriptomic analysis, constructed a CAF-based prognostic gene signature, and validated its biological and clinical relevance through integrative bioinformatic and experimental approaches.

We found that CAFs were the predominant source of cells associated with poor clinical outcomes of radiotherapy, as revealed by Scissor analysis across all cell types. These CAFs were enriched in signaling pathways such as CD40, VEGF, PERIOSTIN and CXCL12, which have been previously reported to contribute to immune exclusion and stromal activation. The CellChat-based interaction network further demonstrated enhanced CAF–immune and CAF–epithelial communications in high-risk tumors, reinforcing their role in shaping a suppressive TME.

The heterogeneity within the CAF compartment was evident in our reclustering analysis, which recapitulated canonical CAF subtypes, including iCAFs, mmCAFs, and apCAFs. Of note, apCAFs were transcriptionally distinct and showed the strongest association with poor prognosis. CytoTRACE and pseudotime analyses suggested that apCAFs reside at the terminal end of the differentiation trajectory, possibly representing a functionally matured and immunosuppressive state. These findings align with previous work indicating that apCAFs may actively inhibit T cell infiltration and response, acting as an immunological “barrier” to effective antitumor immunity [18].

Clinically, we developed a CAF-derived prognostic model composed of five signature genes, three of which (ENO1, CD24, and P4HA1) were upregulated in high-risk patients. These genes have been previously associated with glycolytic reprogramming, stemness maintenance, and immune modulation [19–21]. In contrast, IGHG1 and TXNDC17 showed negative coefficients in our model, indicating inverse associations with adverse outcomes. IGHG1 is a plasma-cell–derived immunoglobulin heavy chain whose high expression, though associated with adverse features, marks an active humoral immune response that may counteract CAF-mediated immunosuppression and influence radiotherapy response [22]. TXNDC17 (TRP14) is a thioredoxin-related reductase sustaining cysteine/GSH-based redox buffering, potentially enabling CAF survival under irradiation-induced ROS and associating with radioresistance [23]. Among the five genes, ENO1 stood out as the most significant contributor to the model and was found to be highly expressed in breast cancer (BC) tumors with TP53 mutations. This is consistent with prior studies showing that ENO1 participates in both metabolic adaptation and immune escape in aggressive tumors [24]. Our qPCR and IHC validations in patient samples confirmed the expression patterns of these genes and supported the robustness of the model.

Despite the strength of our model, some limitations must be acknowledged. For example, GAPDH was retained as a feature gene due to statistical selection, but its ubiquitous expression questions its specificity for CAF-related biology. Another limitation is that while the CAF-derived signature achieved a high AUC in the internal cohort, its performance was more modest in the external validation set. This discrepancy is likely attributable to domain shift, as the external cohort differed in assay platform and preprocessing, endpoint definitions (e.g., RT-treated subset criteria and event window), and event prevalence, all of which can attenuate ROC performance relative to internal estimates. Future optimization of feature selection algorithms and the incorporation of spatial transcriptomics or lineage tracing may refine the specificity and interpretability of the model.

Moreover, although our results implicate CAFs as central mediators of immune suppression in radiotherapy-resistant BC, functional validation of specific CAF subtypes remains necessary. Whether targeting these CAFs or their downstream signaling pathways can enhance immunotherapeutic efficacy remains an open question. Recent efforts to combine CD24 blockade with immune checkpoint inhibitors offer a promising avenue for overcoming CAF-mediated immune exclusion [25], but additional preclinical and clinical studies are warranted.

In summary, our study provides a comprehensive framework for understanding CAF heterogeneity and its prognostic significance in BC. The CAF-based risk model not only serves as a potential clinical stratification tool but also highlights novel avenues for therapeutic intervention by targeting the stromal compartment to restore immune surveillance.
