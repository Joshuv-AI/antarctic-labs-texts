A high-performance detection model ISA-YOLO for eggplant pests and diseases
Authors: Haimin Luo, Jiawei Gao, Ruoqi Li, Qiyu Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Eggplant (Solanum melongena) is a major cash crop, yet field detection of its pests and diseases remains difficult because disease evidence is simultaneously occluded by foliage, blurred at lesion boundaries, and highly variable in scale. Fruit rot is especially challenging: the waxy epidermis and purple anthocyanin-rich surface reduce chromatic contrast, while infection spreads gradually from water-soaked tissue to necrotic tissue, producing diffuse borders between diseased and healthy regions. In this work, we reframe eggplant disease detection through a context-boundary-scale coupling principle, which states that accurate field detection should jointly model incomplete contextual cues, ambiguous lesion boundaries, and scale-varying symptom morphology rather than optimize these cues independently. ISA-YOLO is proposed as an implementation of this principle on top of YOLOv13 through coordinated context modeling, boundary-aware aggregation, and progressive multi-scale fusion. Experiments on two public datasets show that ISA-YOLO achieves 78.1 and 77.7% mAP at 30.66 and 31.74 FPS, outperforming mainstream detectors in overall trade-off between accuracy and speed. After pruning and quantization, inference speed increases to about 75 FPS while maintaining strong accuracy. These results indicate that the proposed principle provides an effective pathway for accurate and deployable eggplant pest and disease detection in smart agriculture.

Introduction

Agriculture is fundamental to food security and rural economic resilience, and stable crop production increasingly depends on accurate, timely, and scalable disease monitoring. Eggplant is an important vegetable crop in many Asian production systems, where the region contributes approximately 74% of global output [1]. Beyond its economic value, eggplant is also nutritionally important because it provides dietary fiber, minerals, and antioxidant compounds that support healthier diets [2]. However, reliable visual inspection of eggplant pests and diseases is considerably more difficult than for many other crops because the fruit surface, lesion morphology, and structure jointly reduce the separability of diseased and healthy regions in field images.

Fruit rot is a representative example of this difficulty. In an early-stage field scene, several tiny water-soaked lesions may emerge on the shaded side of a purple fruit, partially hidden by leaves and branches; in a later-stage scene, the same fruit may exhibit a larger irregular rotten area with diffuse borders that gradually merge into the healthy waxy epidermis. These illustrative examples show that eggplant disease analysis is not only a classification problem, but also a real-time localization problem under dense occlusion, ambiguous boundaries, and strong scale variation. As illustrated in Fig. 1, a generic detector can easily miss clustered small lesions, fragment the target region, or confuse disease symptoms with shadows and healthy reflective tissue.Fig. 1Illustrative example of detection difficulty in eggplant disease images, where occlusion, blurred lesion boundaries, and incomplete target responses can lead to missed or inaccurate detections

Motivation Manual scouting is labor-intensive, subjective, and difficult to scale in large planting areas. A deployable detector that can accurately locate pests and diseases in complex field scenes is therefore valuable for early warning, targeted spraying, residue reduction, and precision agriculture. This motivation is closely aligned with the broader research theme of intelligent agricultural vision systems that must be both accurate and deployable in real environments. The aim of this study is to develop a high-performance eggplant pest and disease detector that explicitly addresses contextual occlusion, boundary ambiguity, and multi-scale lesion variation. The specific objectives are to enhance long-range contextual modeling, improve lesion boundary localization, strengthen cross-scale feature interaction, and preserve real-time inference capability after model compression. This study focuses on RGB image-based detection of fruit rot, fruit borer, melon thrips, and healthy eggplant fruits on two public datasets (BISU and UTM), together with pruning and quantization analyses for edge-oriented deployment.

To achieve these goals, this study proposes ISA-YOLO, a detector tailored to the visual characteristics of eggplant pests and diseases. We define a context-boundary-scale coupling (CBSC) principle: for glossy fruit disease detection in field scenes, contextual incompleteness, lesion-boundary ambiguity, and symptom-scale variation should be modeled as a coupled phenomenon because they arise jointly from crop structure, fruit surface optics, and disease progression. Thus, the novelty of this study lies primarily in the formulation of a disease-specific detection principle and in its eggplant-oriented instantiation. ISA-YOLO serves as one concrete implementation of this principle. In this implementation, iRMB (Improved Residual Multi-Branch) [3] realizes context modeling in occluded scenes, SBA (Selective Boundary Aggregation) [4] realizes boundary-semantic coordination, and AFPN (Adaptive Feature Pyramid Network) [5] realizes robust multi-scale interaction. The main technical contributions are as follows:

(1) A context-boundary-scale coupling principle is proposed for eggplant fruit disease detection, shifting the contribution from a simple combination of existing operators to a biologically grounded design framework for modeling coupled disease evidence in field images. (2) A task-oriented detector architecture is designed as an instantiation of this principle, coupling context enhancement, boundary refinement, and multi-scale fusion within a unified YOLOv13-based framework. (3) Extensive experiments on two public datasets demonstrate that ISA-YOLO improves both accuracy and speed over the baseline model and remains competitive with strong contemporary detectors such as RT-DETR [6], Faster R-CNN [7], and RetinaNet [8]. (4) Ablation studies, visualization analysis, and compression experiments show not only that each module contributes to the final performance, but also that the resulting detector can be accelerated to about 75 FPS for practical deployment.

Related work

Traditional pest and disease identification relies on manual inspections, which are inefficient and constrained by subjective experience [9]. Therefore, developing a high-precision, high-efficiency pest and disease detection model capable of adapting to complex occlusion environments holds significant theoretical and practical value for precise pesticide application, reducing pesticide residues, and ensuring food security. Early research primarily relied on traditional machine learning methods, such as using KNN and Adaboost for pest and disease identification; however, these methods exhibit poor robustness in complex occlusion environments and depend on expert experience [10]. With the rapid advancement of computer vision technology, object detection algorithms based on neural networks, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have found widespread application in agricultural settings [11]. Maruthai et al. proposed Hybrid Vision Graph Neural Networks (HV-GNN) for coffee plantations. By combining CNN feature extraction with GNN graph relationship modeling, they achieved pest detection using 2850 annotated images [12]. Alharbi et al. proposed a plant disease classification model based on ConvLSTM-U-Net, employing bidirectional ConvLSTM and fully connected convolutional layers to identify bacterial leaf spot on the leaves of medicinal plants such as Bael [13]. Umirzakova et al. constructed the CMTL unified Transformer framework, which employs a Cross-Level Multi-Granular Encoder and a Multitask Self-Distilled Attention Fusion module to simultaneously perform cotton boll detection, pest segmentation, and phenological classification on UAV imagery [14]. Additionally, Sai Tejasree et al. employed ResNet50V2 transfer learning for fruit recognition [15]. Object detection technology not only identifies the presence of pests and diseases in images but also precisely locates their specific positions and extent, providing critical information for subsequent targeted pesticide application and precise pest control. Consequently, it has garnered significant attention in pest and disease detection research. In the evolution of object detection models, the YOLO series has become a representative algorithm in this field. Among them, YOLOv13 employs HyperACE and FullPAD modules [16] to mitigate the limited receptive field issue of traditional CNNs [17], but it still faces the three major challenges mentioned above.

To address this, this study proposes the ISA-YOLO model, specifically designed for eggplant pest and disease detection. This model incorporates improvements based on the unique biological characteristics of eggplants and the patterns of disease occurrence: through the three modules—Improved Residual Multi-Branch (iRMB), Selective Boundary Aggregation (SBA), and Adaptive Feature Pyramid Network (AFPN)—targeted optimizations are applied to the YOLOv13 backbone network, neck feature fusion, and detection head, respectively. These targeted improvements enable ISA-YOLO to outperform the original YOLOv13, as well as other models such as YOLOv10 [18], RT-DETR, Faster R-CNN, and RetinaNet, in terms of overall performance on two public datasets provided by Bohol Island State University and UTM. Furthermore, through model pruning and quantization techniques, ISA-YOLO achieves real-time inference at approximately 75 FPS on resource-constrained platforms while maintaining high accuracy, providing a intelligent prevention and control solution for eggplant, a critical crop.

A publicly available eggplant pest and disease detection dataset provided by Bohol Island State University (hereinafter referred to as the BISU dataset) [19]: Contains 3616 images, most of which are field-captured images of eggplants, covering natural pest and disease manifestations at various stages of maturity. The public eggplant pest and disease detection dataset provided by UTM (hereinafter referred to as the UTM dataset) [20] contains 4200 annotated images; appropriately increasing the dataset size helps capture subtle visual features of pests and diseases (such as textural differences in thrips feeding marks) and reduces model bias caused by small sample sizes. Overall design of the ISA-YOLO model

Guided by the CBSC principle introduced in the Introduction section, ISA-YOLO adapts the backbone, neck, and head of YOLOv13 through targeted modifications for eggplant pest and disease detection. Improved residual multi-branch (iRMB)

In image-based detection of eggplant pests and diseases, complex scenes—such as intertwined branches and leaves or occluded fruits—often result in incomplete target features, severely affecting detection accuracy. At the same time, global contextual information, such as the distribution and density of pests and diseases, is crucial for accurately identifying disease types and assessing severity. Although CNNs excel at extracting local patterns such as textures and edges through local convolutional kernels [21] and have achieved success in classifying various plant pests and diseases, their limited receptive fields make it difficult to capture long-range semantic relationships, resulting in insufficient feature inference capabilities for occluded regions. In contrast, ViT, leveraging its self-attention mechanism, is capable of global modeling [22]. However, its quadratic computational complexity results in significant resource consumption, making it difficult to deploy directly on resource-constrained devices. To address this trade-off, the iRMB module combines local convolutions with a global self-attention mechanism, successfully balancing detection performance and resource consumption while retaining global modeling capabilities. Channel expansion and attention computation: Input features are first processed via batch normalization (BatchNorm2d), then divided into fixed-size windows, and processed through two parallel 1× 1 convolutional branches: one maps the number of channels to twice the original value to generate query-key (QK) pairs, while the other projects the number of channels onto λ C to generate the value vector V. Within each window, the module employs a multi-head self-attention mechanism for information modeling. Unlike standard global attention, window attention divides the feature map into several fixed-size windows (parameters are configurable, with a default of 7× 7) and computes attention independently within each window. This design significantly reduces computational complexity, making it suitable for high-resolution pest and disease image processing. Notably, this module incorporates targeted improvements to the attention mechanism: both the query vector Q and the key vector K are generated via 1× 1 convolutions on the original channel dimension (C dimensions), with Q and K remaining in the original C-dimensional space; meanwhile, the value vector V is projected to λ C dimensions via 1× 1 convolutions. When λ < 1, the computational load of the V projection, the weighted attention output, and subsequent projections is reduced; when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda \approx 1$$\end{document} or λ > 1, the focus shifts toward preserving or enhancing representational capacity. This asymmetric design of the Q/K and V dimensions strikes a balance between efficiency and accuracy by adjusting λ .

Local feature enhancement: The attention output is subsequently processed via deep separable convolutions to capture local details such as lesion edges and textures. Deep separable convolutions perform spatial filtering on a per-channel basis; combined with batch normalization and the SiLU activation function, they effectively extract discriminative local features of pest and disease-affected areas. To further enhance inter-channel information interaction, a channel attention mechanism (SE module) can be introduced to adaptively adjust the importance weights of each channel.

Feature fusion and residual connections: Residual connections are employed within the module to fuse the attention features with the deep separable convolution features. The fused features first undergo Dropout regularization, then are downsampled to the original channel dimension via 1× 1 convolutions, and finally undergo residual addition with the module’s input through a DropPath random depth strategy to form the final output. Where, Dropout is used to prevent overfitting, while DropPath randomly discards entire paths during training to enhance the model’s generalization ability. The dual residual design (internal skip connections + external residual connections) ensures smooth gradient propagation, which is beneficial for the training and optimization of deep networks.Fig. 12Simplified diagram of iRMB

Selective boundary aggregation (SBA)

Furthermore, the detection of pests and diseases in eggplants faces the challenge of blurred lesion boundaries. The transition between diseased areas and healthy tissue is often gradual, particularly in the early stages of infection. This low-contrast characteristic makes traditional threshold segmentation or simple CNN methods highly prone to false positives and false negatives [23]. Most existing studies focus on overall classification accuracy or small-object detection, with relatively insufficient attention paid to the precise detection and localization of lesion boundaries. Therefore, this study introduce the Selective Boundary Aggregation (SBA) module. Designed with lightweight architecture in mind, this module utilizes the rich edge texture details in shallow-layer features to precisely outline lesion contours, while integrating high-level semantic information from deep-layer features to distinguish pest and disease-affected areas from easily confused backgrounds such as leaf veins and water stains. SBA uses an adaptive gated attention mechanism to bidirectionally fuse these two complementary types of information, enabling the model to accurately locate lesions and refine their boundaries while maintaining a lightweight architecture.Fig. 14SBA overall architecture diagram

SBA overall architecture diagram

The SBA module architecture is shown in Fig. 14. The SBA module takes two feature streams from different layers of the network as input. The shallow-layer features have high spatial resolution and can capture edge details and textural variations of lesions; although the deep-layer features have lower spatial resolution, they contain the abstract semantic information required for classifying pests and diseases. First, the module performs channel compression on the two input features using two 1× 1 convolutions, mapping them to a unified low-dimensional feature space (reducing the number of channels by half to C/2). This design not only facilitates subsequent cross-layer interactions but also reduces the computational load and parameter scale of subsequent operations by reducing the number of feature channels, which is useful to keeping the module lightweight. Subsequently, the module’s core—the element-wise Sigmoid gating mechanism—comes into play. This mechanism directly applies the Sigmoid function to the channel-compressed features, generating a confidence gating value between [0, 1] for each spatial location to assess the reliability of the feature at that position. Compared to complex approaches based on multi-layer perceptrons or self-attention, this lightweight element-wise Sigmoid gating has low computational complexity yet effectively evaluates the reliability of features at each location. Regions with clear edges and distinct textures in shallow-layer features receive higher confidence scores, while regions with clear semantic information and category-specific features in deep-layer features also receive high confidence scores. Based on the aforementioned confidence evaluation, the module employs a bidirectional aggregation strategy for feature fusion. Specifically, when the confidence of a shallow feature at a certain location is low (e.g., uniform regions within a lesion lacking textural details), the module automatically incorporates additional deep semantic information to supplement it; conversely, when boundary information at a certain location in the deep features is ambiguous, the module draws precise edge localization information from the shallow features. This adaptive information compensation mechanism ensures that the fused features possess both boundary precision and semantic accuracy.Fig. 15Simplified diagram of SBA

Adaptive feature pyramid network (AFPN)

In addition, eggplant diseases and pests exhibit multi-scale characteristics, ranging from tiny, indistinct early lesions to large-scale late-stage lesions, covering an extremely wide range. The classic FPN adopts a top-down path to transmit the rich semantic information of high-level features to lower levels, thereby enhancing its ability to represent targets at different scales. The Path Aggregation Network (PAFPN) extends the FPN by adding a bottom-up path, aiming to further supplement and refine spatial detail information [24]. However, these methods are constrained by their inter-layer fusion mechanisms, preventing direct interaction between non-adjacent layers (such as the top and bottom layers). This results in the loss of semantic and detailed information during transmission, thereby affecting detection accuracy [25]. To address this issue, this study introduces the Asymptotic Feature Pyramid Network (AFPN) to improve the head of YOLOv13. The design adopts a strategy of progressive cross-layer fusion with adaptive spatial weighting: first performing fusion among lower layers, then gradually introducing higher semantic layers, and finally completing joint fusion. By leveraging lightweight channel compression and efficient spatial weight calculation, this study achieves a better balance between semantic consistency and detail preservation.

Multi-level feature inputs and channel reduction: The detection head of this model receives inputs from four scale features of the backbone network, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{S_2,S_3,S_4,S_5\}$$\end{document}. To achieve efficient feature fusion, AFPN first uses 1× 1 convolutions to reduce the number of channels in each level’s features to one-quarter of the original. This preserves the key discriminative information of each level while reducing the computational load and the number of parameters in subsequent fusion modules. Channel compression enables the core computations of the entire AFPN module to be performed in a low-dimensional space, thereby reducing computational complexity.

Feature scale alignment: The upsampling operation is performed using a 1× 1 convolution combined with bilinear interpolation, which both adjusts the channel dimension and ensures a smooth increase in spatial resolution. Downsampling operations use convolution kernels of corresponding sizes based on the required scaling factor: 2× downsampling uses a 2× 2 convolution kernel with a stride of 2; 4× downsampling uses a 4× 4 convolution kernel with a stride of 4; and 8× downsampling uses an 8× 8 convolution kernel with a stride of 8. Compared to pooling operations, this design preserves more feature information. After each feature fusion is completed, four residual units are used to perform deep learning on the fused features. Progressive fusion: As shown in Fig. 17, the process begins by fusing the low-level features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_2$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_3$$\end{document}, followed by the addition of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_4$$\end{document}, and finally \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_5$$\end{document}, ultimately extracting a set of multi-scale fused features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{P_2,P_3,P_4,P_5\}$$\end{document}. After fusion, a 1× 1 convolution is applied to map the features from each level to a unified output channel dimension. AFPN first fuses low-level features, then deep-level features, and finally the top-level features, i.e., the most abstract features. The semantic gap between non-adjacent level features is greater than that between adjacent level features, especially between low-level and top-level features. This results in poor performance when directly fusing non-adjacent level features. Therefore, directly using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_2$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_3$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_4$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_5$$\end{document} for feature fusion is unreasonable. Since AFPN’s architecture is progressive, this brings the semantic information of features from different levels closer together during the progressive fusion process, thereby mitigating the aforementioned issue. For example, feature fusion between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_2$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_3$$\end{document} narrows the semantic gap between them. Since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_3$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_4$$\end{document} are features from adjacent levels, the semantic gap between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_2$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_4$$\end{document} is also narrowed. This progressive design allows the semantic gap to gradually narrow during the fusion process, effectively mitigating the semantic gap issue that arises from direct fusion between non-adjacent layers. At the same time, this progressive architecture achieves high-quality cross-level feature fusion without introducing additional complex attention modules, thereby maintaining high detection accuracy while avoiding a significant increase in model complexity.Fig. 18Adaptive spatial fusion operation. Structured pruning

To reduce model overhead and facilitate deployment on resource-constrained devices, this study adopts a deep structured pruning strategy based on channel importance evaluation. Unlike unstructured pruning, which produces irregular sparse matrices that are difficult to accelerate on general-purpose hardware [26], structured pruning removes redundancy at the level of convolutional kernels or channels. This operation directly reduces the number of channels between layers, effectively lowering the number of parameters and FLOPs [27]. More importantly, the pruned network retains a dense computation format, enabling seamless compatibility with mainstream frameworks such as PyTorch without requiring specialized sparse libraries. This allows the model to achieve substantial inference acceleration directly on standard CPUs, GPUs, and other devices. Therefore, this study employs an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_1$$\end{document}-norm-based structured channel pruning method to compress the ISA-YOLO eggplant pest and disease detection model. The specific pruning process is as follows:

For the i-th output channel in a convolutional layer, its importance score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_i$$\end{document} is calculated using the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_1$$\end{document} norm:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} S_i=\sum _{c=1}^{C_{\textrm{in}}}\sum _{h=1}^{H}\sum _{w=1}^{W}\left| W_{i,c,h,w}\right| \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_{i,c,h,w}$$\end{document} represents the convolution kernel weight corresponding to the i-th output channel, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{\textrm{in}}$$\end{document} denotes the number of input channels, and H and W denote the height and width of the convolution kernel, respectively. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_1$$\end{document} norm effectively measures the activation intensity of each channel; channels with lower importance scores contribute less to the model’s output and can be safely removed.

Given a target pruning rate ρ , the number of channels to retain, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\textrm{keep}}$$\end{document}, is calculated as follows:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} N_{\textrm{keep}}=\max \left( \left\lfloor \frac{N_{\textrm{orig}}(1-\rho )}{8}\right\rfloor \times 8, N_{\textrm{min}}\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\textrm{orig}}$$\end{document} represents the original number of channels, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\textrm{min}}$$\end{document} represents the minimum number of channels to be retained. The number of channels is aligned to a multiple of 8 to ensure hardware computational efficiency.

Furthermore, a fine-tuning strategy is employed to restore the model’s accuracy after pruning. Using the AdamW optimizer in conjunction with cosine-smoothed learning rate scheduling, fine-tuning on the original training data achieves a better balance between model lightweighting and performance preservation.

Structured pruning based on channel importance not only reduces the number of model parameters and computational complexity but has also become a widely favored model lightweighting technique in practice due to its excellent hardware and software compatibility.

TensorRT quantization

This study used the TensorRT inference engine to perform half-precision (FP16) quantization optimization on the ISA-YOLO eggplant pest and disease detection model, with the aim of achieving efficient, low-latency embedded deployment to meet the demands of real-time detection in the field. FP16 quantization converts model weights and activation values from 32-bit floating-point numbers (FP32) to 16-bit floating-point numbers, reducing memory consumption and computational overhead while maintaining high accuracy [28]. For the weight tensor W in the model, the FP16 quantization process can be expressed as:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} W_{\textrm{FP16}}=\textrm{cast}_{\textrm{FP32}\rightarrow \textrm{FP16}}(W_{\textrm{FP32}}) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The numerical range of FP16 is [-65,504, 65,504], which reduces storage space by 50% compared to FP32. This conversion fully leverages the hardware acceleration capabilities of NVIDIA GPU Tensor Cores, significantly improving inference throughput [29]. To balance accuracy and speed, this study opted for FP16 quantization over INT8 to avoid compromising the reliability of pest and disease detection due to excessive loss of precision [30]. Through TensorRT’s graph optimization and automatic kernel tuning, the ISA-YOLO model maintains a high mAP (mean accuracy) while reducing inference latency, enabling its practical deployment in real-time monitoring systems for eggplant pests and diseases and providing reliable technical support for precision agriculture.

Performance evaluation metrics

To comprehensively evaluate the model’s detection performance, this study adopted standard evaluation metrics in the field of object detection:

Precision (P): The proportion of samples correctly classified as positive by the model out of all samples that are actually positive; it measures the accuracy of positive predictions.8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P=\frac{\textrm{TP}}{\textrm{TP}+\textrm{FP}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{TP}$$\end{document} is the number of true positives and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{FP}$$\end{document} is the number of false positives.

Recall (R): The proportion of all true positive examples that the model correctly predicts as positive; this metric measures the model’s ability to identify true positive samples. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{FN}$$\end{document} is the number of false negative samples.9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} R=\frac{\textrm{TP}}{\textrm{TP}+\textrm{FN}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}F1-score (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_1$$\end{document}): The harmonic mean of precision and recall, which comprehensively reflects a model’s ability to balance accuracy and coverage.10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Mean average precision (mAP): This is the core metric for measuring the overall detection accuracy of the model across all classes.11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{mAP}=\frac{1}{N}\sum _{i=1}^{N}\textrm{AP}_{i} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, N is the number of classes, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{AP}_i$$\end{document} is the average precision for the i-th class.

Frame rate (FPS): The number of frames processed per second, used to measure the model’s real-time performance.12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{FPS}=\frac{\text {Total processed frames}}{\text {Total inference time}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Model size: The amount of storage space occupied by the model file, reflecting the storage and memory resources used by the model.

Number of model parameters: The total number of trainable parameters in the model, including weights and biases, reflecting the model’s computational complexity.

GFLOPs: Billions of floating-point operations, used here to characterize model computational complexity.

Discussion

The experimental trends can be understood more clearly from the biological characteristics of eggplant fruit rot. The visible boundary between diseased and healthy tissue is often blurred not because annotation is imprecise, but because the symptom itself is visually diffuse. Eggplant fruit has a smooth waxy cuticle that produces specular reflection, local glare, and unstable edge contrast under field illumination. At the same time, the purple epidermis is rich in anthocyanins, which partially mask or compress the chromatic difference between healthy tissue and early brown water-soaked lesions. As infection develops, tissue softening and subepidermal diffusion occur progressively rather than instantaneously, so the transition from glossy purple tissue to necrotic brown tissue is gradual. This explains why shallow texture cues alone are unreliable and why detectors that depend mainly on local edge sharpness tend to miss lesions or generate fragmented boxes.

This biological interpretation also clarifies the methodological significance of the CBSC principle. Context is needed because leaf occlusion and shadow often hide parts of the diseased fruit; boundary-semantic coordination is needed because the wax layer and anthocyanin-rich surface make lesion contours optically ambiguous; and scale-aware fusion is needed because the same disease can appear as punctate spots or large rotten regions at different stages. The present results extend these insights by showing that, for glossy fruit disease detection in the field, the key issue is not any single operator but the coordinated modeling of coupled symptom evidence. This is why the proposed method should be interpreted primarily as an implementation of a new detection principle rather than as a simple stack of attention, FPN, and boundary-fusion modules.

This setup increased the workload per inference run and reduced measurement bias caused by variations in system resources. Additionally, because the laptop used an NVIDIA 3050 GPU, the FlashAttention feature could not be enabled. Speed tests were then conducted based on the inference results, and P, R, F1, and mAP were calculated. Conclusion

This study presented a context-boundary-scale coupling principle for eggplant pest and disease detection and implemented it with the ISA-YOLO detector. The key idea is that occlusion, blurred lesion boundaries, and large symptom-scale variation are biologically and visually coupled, so they should be modeled jointly rather than treated as separate engineering issues. Guided by this principle, ISA-YOLO consistently outperformed the YOLOv13 baseline and remained competitive with heavier detectors on both BISU and UTM, achieving 87.9%/64.4%/73.6%/78.1%/30.66 and 89.0%/59.7%/70.5%/77.7%/31.74 in P/R/F1/mAP/FPS, respectively.At the same time, it also performs well on the Papaya and Tomato datasets. Visualization analysis confirmed tighter attention on symptomatic regions, and compression experiments showed that quantization-oriented deployment can raise throughput to about 75 FPS with limited accuracy loss. These findings indicate that the proposed principle offers a useful conceptual and practical framework for deployable agricultural detection. However, since ISA-YOLO relies primarily on computer vision, it may fail to detect objects in complete darkness; furthermore, it may be affected by extreme weather conditions.Future work will extend this framework to multimodal sensing, broader crop-disease coverage, and stronger cross-domain generalization under field weather and illumination shifts.
