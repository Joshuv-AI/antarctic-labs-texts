A bioactive magnesium alloy scaffold integrated with BMSCs-Loaded 3D microspheres synergistically promotes femoral head osteonecrosis repair by improving the osteogenic-angiogenic microenvironment
Authors: Minzheng Guo, Haojing Xu, Zijie Pei, Baochuang Qi, Chen Meng, Xusheng Chen, Huan Luo, Junxiao Ren, Zhihao Wang, Lin Wang, Qingyun Xue, Lei Shi, Yu Rao, Peng Wen, Chuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteonecrosis of the Femoral Head (ONFH) is primarily characterized by impaired osteogenesis and insufficient vascularization, leading to progressive structural collapse and limb dysfunction. To develop a targeted strategy for regulating the bone-vascular microenvironment in hip preserving treatment, we integrated a three-dimensionally (3D) printed microporous magnesium alloy scaffold with bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs)-loaded 3D microspheres into a composite regenerative system. We then evaluated its therapeutic efficacy and investigated its underlying mechanisms. In vitro, the composite system demonstrated favorable biocompatibility and promoted osteogenic and angiogenic differentiation. In a steroid-induced rabbit model of femoral head necrosis, micro-CT and histological analysis confirmed the system's significant interventional effect. The magnesium alloy scaffold provided adequate mechanical support and released Mg2+ ions to participate in microenvironmental regulation. The BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres were firmly integrated with the scaffold, which increased BMSCs loading capacity and provided a protective growth environment that shielded cells from mechanical damage. Further experiments indicated that the composite system modulated the interactions among BMSCs, osteoblasts, and vascular endothelial cells. By activating Extracellular Matrix organization, Focal adhesion, and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathways, it regulated the osteoblast and endothelial cell-related bone-vascular microenvironment to promote femoral head repair. This study demonstrates that combining a bioactive magnesium scaffold with BMSCs-loaded microspheres synergistically promotes femoral head repair, highlighting the therapeutic potential of integrating bioactive ion release with cellular paracrine regulation.

Introduction

Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is a progressive and refractory orthopedic disorder driven by ischemia-induced death of osteocytes and osteoblasts, ultimately leading to structural collapse of the femoral head and secondary osteoarthritis. The increasing incidence of ONFH in younger and more active populations has amplified its clinical and socioeconomic burden, as disease progression frequently culminates in total hip arthroplasty (THA), for which long term durability remains a major concern [1,2]. Despite diverse etiological factors, including corticosteroid exposure, alcohol abuse, trauma, and coagulation abnormalities, the pathophysiology of ONFH converges on a disrupted bone-vascular unit characterized by impaired angiogenesis, defective osteogenesis, and progressive deterioration of trabecular architecture. Current hip preserving interventions, such as core decompression and bone grafting, primarily address mechanical or structural deficits but fail to reestablish the regenerative microenvironment required for sustained bone repair. This limitation underscores a central challenge in ONFH treatment: the need to simultaneously restore mechanical support and reconstitute the osteogenic-angiogenic niche [3,4].

Magnesium based biomaterials have emerged as promising candidates for addressing this challenge due to their unique combination of mechanical compatibility and biological activity. Magnesium alloys possess an elastic modulus closer to native bone, thereby mitigating stress shielding, while their controlled degradation releases Mg2+ ions that actively participate in bone regeneration [5]. Increasing evidence indicates that Mg2+ ions promote osteogenic differentiation, enhance angiogenic signaling, and modulate the immune microenvironment. The magnesium degradation has also been implicated in regulating cell adhesion, cytoskeletal organization, and mechanotransductive signaling, suggesting a potential role in orchestrating cellular responses beyond passive structural support. However, magnesium scaffolds alone provide limited temporal and spatial control over the regenerative microenvironment, particularly under the ischemic and mechanically compromised conditions of ONFH [6,7].Cell-based strategies, especially those utilizing BMSCs, offer complementary advantages by providing both osteogenic potential and robust paracrine regulation [8]. In addition to their differentiation capacity, BMSCs secrete a broad spectrum of bioactive factors that coordinate angiogenesis, immunomodulation, and matrix remodeling. Nevertheless, the therapeutic efficacy of BMSCs is often hindered by poor cell retention, reduced survival in hostile microenvironments, and loss of functional organization following transplantation [9,10]. To overcome these limitations, advanced tissue engineering approaches have focused on integrating biomaterials with cell delivery systems to construct functional regenerative niches. In this context, we developed a composite system combining a WE43 magnesium alloy scaffold with BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres [11]. The 3D microspheres provide a porous and biomimetic microenvironment that supports cell viability, preserves cell to cell and cell to matrix interactions, and enables sustained paracrine signaling [12,13]. When coupled with magnesium scaffold, this system is designed to generate a dynamic bioactive system in which scaffold-derived ionic cues and effective support and cell-derived factors synergistically regulate tissue regeneration. Although these composite strategies exhibit considerable therapeutic potential, the molecular mechanisms responsible for their synergistic effects, particularly with respect to the regulation of osteoblast activity, remain poorly characterized [14,15]. Emerging evidence underscores the pivotal role of cell adhesion and mechanotransduction in bone regeneration. Integrin-mediated interactions with the extracellular matrix (ECM), coupled with downstream signaling cascades—including integrin-linked kinase (ILK) and the PI3K/Akt pathway—constitute a central regulatory axis that orchestrates cytoskeletal remodeling, mechanical force transmission, and extracellular matrix deposition in osteoblasts. Disruption of this mechanotransductive network is implicated in compromised bone regeneration in ONFH [16,17]. However, the extent to which magnesium-derived biochemical cues and BMSCs-mediated paracrine signaling converge upon, and functionally modulate, this axis remains incompletely defined and warrants systematic investigation [18,19]. In the present study, we aimed to address this gap by investigating the therapeutic efficacy and underlying mechanisms of the composite scaffold in ONFH. We evaluated its biocompatibility and osteogenic-angiogenic potential in vitro and assessed its reparative capacity in a rabbit model of ONFH [20]. Furthermore, through Transwell co-culture systems and transcriptomic analysis, we focused on initially explored the role of the Integrin-ILK-PI3K/Akt mechanotransduction axis in mediating the synergistic effects of magnesium ions and BMSC-derived signals on osteoblast function [21,22]. This work provides mechanistic insight into biomaterial–cell interactions and establishes a conceptual framework for the design of next-generation regenerative strategies for ONFH. Results and discussion

Characterization of the Multifunctional magnesium-based composite scaffold

WE43 scaffold with functionalized multilayer surface

WE43 scaffold was fabricated with a lattice gyroid design through Laser Powder Bed Fusion (LPBF) [23]. Benefiting from the high printability of the structure and subsequent chemical polishing, the scaffold exhibits a smooth surface and a precisely controlled porosity of 60% (Fig. 2A). The scaffold underwent a High Temperature Oxidation (HTO) process at 525 °C for 8 h, followed by a hydrothermal reaction at 120 °C for 6 h to obtain Mg-Al Layered Double Hydroxide (LDH) coating. Since 3D-printed Mg is more susceptible to corrosion compared to conventionally processed Mg, pre-oxidative passivation prior to the hydrothermal reaction is essential. Without this step, a large amount of loose Mg (OH)2 would form, significantly compromising the degradation resistance of the scaffold (Fig. S1). Fig. 2B revealed the surface microstructure after each treatment step. According to Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) result on Table S1, As-printed samples have a uniform elemental distribution, with elemental ratios similar to those of the original powders. Following the HTO process, large amounts of dense rare earth oxides (Nd、Gd and Y) were detected on the scaffold surface, consistent with the X-ray diffraction (XRD) results shown in Fig. 2C. Further insight into the effects of the HTO on 3D-printed WE43 can be found in our previous work [24,25].Fig. 2Coating characterization of WE43 scaffold. A) Process for coating preparation. A three-dimensional lamellar network was observed on the surface of the Mg-Al LDH scaffolds, with feature sizes ranging from 500 nm to 1 μm. EDX mapping (Fig. 2B) confirmed the presence of Al, while the characteristic crystal planes (003) and (006) of LDH were found in XRD results (Fig. 2C). The interlayer space of LDH was calculated to be d003 = 7.616 Å. Based on the molecular model of LDH containing CO32−, which suggests a molar ratio of H2O:CO32− is 3:1, the formula can be determined as [Mg6Al2(OH)16][(CO32−)·3H2O] [3]. Fig. 2D presents the Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra of the scaffolds surface, in which characteristic bands can be observed at 3479 cm−1 (stretching vibration of O-H), 2935 cm−1 (stretching vibration of CO32−-H2O), 1373 cm−1 (asymmetric stretching mode of C=O in carbonate) and 651 cm−1 (vibration of Mg-O), all of which correspond to the typical features of LDH. The surface roughness was evaluated with an atomic force microscope (Fig. 2E). Following the hydrothermal reaction, the surface roughness (Ra) of the scaffolds increased significantly to 95.4 ± 8.6 nm, which can be attributed to the formation of the three-dimensional LDH structure. In contrast, the HTO process didn't cause a substantial change in surface roughness. As shown in Fig. 2F, the HTO scaffolds exhibit a reduced water contact angle (57.0 ± 1.9°) due to the higher hydrophilicity of the surface oxides compared to the Mg matrix. Furthermore, the presence of hydrogen bonding in the LDH structure significantly enhances its affinity for water molecules, resulting in even greater hydrophilicity. (contact angle = 41.2 ± 2.7°).

In summary, the composite coating on Mg scaffolds was fabricated through a two-step process. The coating provided physical protection and endowed the scaffold with a rough, hydrophilic surface, which is more conducive to cell adhesion and 3D microspheres.

Composite coatings modulate magnesium degradation behavior through multifactorial optimization

In vitro degradation behavior of scaffolds was evaluated in Hank's simulated body fluids. As shown in Fig. 3A, both the oxide and LDH layers contribute positively to enhancing the degradation resistance of Mg scaffolds. Under the conditions of high specific surface area, the As-printed scaffolds exhibited pronounced corrosion pits on the first day and completely lost structural integrity after 3 days. In contrast, the HTO scaffolds didn't exhibit significant localized defects until the 7th day and remained largely intact in most regions. The attachment of the Mg-Al LDH coating further delayed this timeline to 14 days. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) was adopted to examine the microscopic morphology of the scaffolds surface after immersion (Fig. 3B). The As-printed scaffolds exhibited severe surface cracking at the day 1. The dense oxide layer on HTO scaffolds surface effectively protected the surface from cracking for 7 days. Additionally, the long-term degradation of the scaffolds was quantitatively evaluated by pH change, residual weight and Mg2+ ion release (Fig. 3C, D and E). As-printed sample reached its peak pH value (10.83 ± 0.01) after 2 days immersion. By the third day, only 26.42 ± 0.92% of weight remained. Magnesium ion release reached a high concentration of 1388.28 ± 37.37 mg/L by the 7th day. The HTO scaffold exhibited a weight residue ratio of 66.42 ± 12.25% at 7 days, with the pH reaching a maximum value of 10.63 ± 0.02 at the same time point. Magnesium ion release gradually accelerated over the 28-day period, reaching 1529.16 ± 98.18 mg/L at day 28, which was comparable to that of the as-printed scaffold (1536.61 ± 17.45 mg/L), indicating the near-complete degradation. In contrast, the Mg-Al LDH group exhibited a pH of 10.08 ± 0.22 and a weight residue of 84.94 ± 3.94% at 7 days. By day 28, the Mg2+ release reached only 848.86 ± 478.46 mg/L, demonstrating a more controlled degradation profile throughout the entire period. It is worth mentioning that the cumulative release of Al3+ of the Mg-Al LDH scaffold was only 0.50 ± 0.29 mg/L at 28 days, indicating no significant potential risk associated with Al3+ release [4].

According to the potentialiodynamic polarization (PDP) curves demonstrated in Fig. 3F, the corrosion current densities (Icorr) of As-printed, HTO and Mg-Al LDH supports were 1.46 ± 0.33∗10−4, 2.54 ± 0.17∗10−5 and 9.73 ± 2.18∗10−6 A/cm2 respectively. In the Bode plot (Fig. S2), the HTO and the Mg-Al LDH group exhibited higher low-frequency impedance, indicating the excellent passivation effect. These results are consistent with the initial degradation retardation of the coated scaffolds.

The composite coating effectively decreased the degradation rate of the 3D-printed WE43 scaffolds. The HTO process formed a dense passivation layer that served as a barrier against simulated body fluids. Additionally, the lamellar structure produced by the LDH process exhibited anion-exchange capabilities, enabling it to trap aggressive Cl− ions near the scaffold surface. Furthermore, CO32− ions embedded within the LDH layers contributed to chloride ion repulsion, which played a critical role in maintaining the long-term stability of the scaffolds [5].

Physicochemical properties characterization of the composite scaffold

To construct the composite scaffold, prefabricated BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres were integrated with the porous magnesium alloy scaffold through immersion and incubation, allowing spontaneous attachment of the microspheres to the scaffold surface. The 3D culture of BMSCs within the microspheres is illustrated in Fig. S3A. The 3D microspheres were initially stored in a tablet form and rapidly dispersed into numerous individual microspheres particles upon dissolution in neutral solutions (Figure S3B and S3C-D). The ECM imaging of the 3D microspheres is shown in Fig. 4D. The microsphere particles exhibited a relatively uniform spherical with an average diameter of approximately 200 μm. The microspheres were characterized by an interconnected porous architecture, which provided abundant internal space for cell adhesion and proliferation. Quantitative analysis of the ECM images revealed an average pore diameter of 17.78 ± 6.30 μm within the microsphere particles, a feature conducive to cellular infiltration and three-dimensional growth. As shown as in Fig. 4D, the microscopic observation revealed that the 3D microspheres were uniformly distributed throughout the interconnected pore network of the magnesium scaffold rather than being restricted to the external surface. The microspheres adhered stably to the scaffold struts and pore walls, forming multiple contact points. The attachment of microspheres did not obstruct the interconnected pore channels, which are essential for nutrient diffusion and tissue ingrowth. The microspheres conformed to the scaffold topology, suggesting that surface roughness and hydrophilicity of the coated magnesium scaffold facilitated microsphere anchoring. After incubation, the composite construct remained structurally stable during gentle handling and medium exchange, indicating sufficient interfacial adhesion between the microspheres and the scaffold[[27], [28], [29]]. These observations demonstrate that the composite scaffold can be readily assembled through a straightforward physical integration process, yielding a spatially organized hybrid system that combines the mechanical support of the magnesium scaffold with the biological functionality of BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres.

In vitro validation of the biocompatibility and osteogenic and angiogenic effects of the composite scaffold

The composite scaffold biocompatibility test

As a promising implant biomaterial, the composite scaffold needs excellent biocompatibility, and vascularization and osteogenesis depend heavily on the activity of BMSCs [30]. Thus, the biocompatibility of composite scaffold was detected by examining the flow cytometry analysis, skeleton staining, live-dead staining, CCK8 and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) of BMSCs. The grouping of this part of the experiment was Control, Mg, 3D cell, Mg/3D cell. The results of flow cytometry analysis are shown in Fig. 4A, throughout the entire culture period, the markers associated with BMSCs stemness and functional stability, CD105, CD73, and CD90, were consistently expressed at high levels, with positive rates exceeding 96% at all time points. In contrast, the markers, CD14 and CD34, indicative of inflammation and aberrant lineage differentiation associated with microenvironmental dysregulation remained at low expression levels (<1.5%). Flow cytometric analysis revealed no evident time-dependent loss of BMSC surface marker expression or induction of non-mesenchymal phenotypes during the culture period. Skeleton fluorescence staining of the actin cytoskeleton was performed to evaluate cell morphology and proliferative behavior of BMSCs under different culture conditions (Fig. 4B). In the Control and Mg groups, BMSCs cultured on two-dimensional substrates exhibited a typical spindle-shaped morphology with well-organized F-actin stress fibers as early as day 1. With prolonged culture, cells in both groups showed increased cell density and enhanced cytoskeletal organization, indicating continuous proliferation and stable adhesion. Notably, the Mg group displayed comparable cytoskeletal integrity and cell spreading to the Control group at all time points, suggesting that the magnesium alloy extract did not induce cytoskeletal disruption or morphological abnormalities. The BMSCs cultured on 3D microspheres (3D cell and Mg/3D cell groups) exhibited a distinct three-dimensional growth pattern. At day 1, cells adhered to the surface of the microspheres and displayed a rounded or partially spread morphology, accompanied by localized F-actin distribution. Over time, especially by days 4 and 7, cells in both 3D groups showed increased cell coverage on the microspheres and progressive cytoskeletal reorganization, forming interconnected actin networks consistent with active proliferation and adaptation to the three-dimensional microenvironment. The Mg/3D cell group demonstrated cytoskeletal features and cell density comparable to those of the 3D cell group throughout the culture period, indicating that the presence of magnesium alloy extract did not impair cell attachment, spreading, or growth in the 3D culture system. Live/dead fluorescence staining was performed on day 7 to evaluate the viability of BMSCs cultured within the 3D microsphere system in the presence of magnesium alloy extracts. As shown in Fig. 4C, the majority of cells exhibited strong green fluorescence corresponding to calcein-AM staining, indicating high levels of intracellular esterase activity and intact cell membranes. Only a minimal number of cells displayed red fluorescence from propidium iodide incorporation, suggesting a low proportion of dead or membrane-compromised cells. This pattern was consistently observed at both low and high magnifications, demonstrating homogeneous cell viability throughout the microsphere population. These findings confirm that the 3D microsphere-based culture system combined with magnesium alloy extracts exhibits excellent cytocompatibility and effectively supports the survival of BMSCs during extended in vitro culture. Cell viability and cytotoxicity of BMSCs under different culture conditions were further evaluated using CCK-8 and LDH assays. As shown in Fig. 4E, the CCK-8 assay demonstrated that there were no significant differences in OD450 values among the Control, Mg, 3D cell, and Mg/3D cell groups at both 24 h and 48 h (ns), indicating comparable cell metabolic activity across all groups. Moreover, cell viability increased from 24 h to 48 h in all groups, suggesting sustained cell proliferation without growth inhibition. LDH release analysis revealed no significant differences in LDH release rates among the four groups Fig. 4F, indicating that neither the magnesium alloy extract nor the 3D microspheres system induced detectable membrane damage or cytotoxic effects. These results confirm that the Mg extract, 3D microspheres, and their combination exhibit excellent cytocompatibility and do not adversely affect BMSC viability or membrane integrity[[31], [32], [33]].

Evaluation of the composite scaffold's ability to promote osteogenesis in vitro

To evaluate the osteogenic potential of the different treatments in vitro, ALP and Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining were performed on BMSCs cultured under osteogenic induction conditions at days 7 and 14. At day 7, ALP staining revealed a basal level of osteogenic differentiation in the control group, whereas both the Mg group and 3D cell group exhibited enhanced ALP-positive areas. Notably, the Mg/3D cell group showed the strongest ALP staining intensity and the largest positive area, indicating accelerated early osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 5A). Consistent with the ALP results, ARS staining demonstrated limited calcium deposition in the control group at day 7, while increased mineralized nodules were observed in the Mg and 3D cell groups (Fig. 5B). By day 14, calcium deposition was markedly enhanced in all experimental groups, with the Mg/3D cell group exhibiting the most extensive and dense mineralized matrix formation (Fig. 5B). To further investigate the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs at the protein level, Western blot analysis was performed after 7 and 21 days of osteogenic induction. The expression of osteogenic markers ALP, COL-1, OCN and Runx2 was evaluated. At day 7, the Mg and 3D cell groups exhibited increased expression of these osteogenic markers compared with the control group, indicating enhanced early osteogenic commitment. Notably, the Mg/3D cell group showed the highest protein levels than other groups (P < 0.01), suggesting a synergistic effect on the initiation of osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 5C and E). By day 21, the expression of these osteogenic markers was markedly upregulated in all experimental groups, with the Mg/3D cell group displaying the strongest protein expression among all groups. In contrast, the control group exhibited relatively lower levels of mature osteogenic markers (Fig. 5D and F). Immunofluorescence staining was performed to quantitatively evaluate the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs by assessing the expression of ALP and COL-1 at days 7 and 21. As shown in Fig. 6A and C, ALP fluorescence intensity at day 7 exhibited a modest increase in the Mg and 3D cell groups compared with the control group, although no significant difference was observed between the Mg and 3D cell groups. In contrast, the Mg/3D cell group showed a significantly higher ALP fluorescence intensity than all other groups (P < 0.01), indicating enhanced early osteogenic activity [34]. At day 21, ALP expression was further elevated in all experimental groups. The Mg/3D cell group exhibited the highest ALP fluorescence intensity, which was significantly greater than that of the Mg and 3D cell groups (P < 0.01), suggesting a sustained and synergistic promotion of osteogenic differentiation. Consistent with the ALP results, COL-1 expression showed a time-dependent increase across all groups (Fig. 6B and D). At day 7, COL-1 fluorescence intensity was significantly higher in the Mg/3D cell groups compared with other group (P < 0.01), whereas no significant difference was observed between the Mg and 3D cell groups. By day 21, COL-1 expression was markedly enhanced in all treated groups, with the Mg/3D cell group exhibiting the strongest fluorescence signal, significantly exceeding that of the Mg and 3D cell groups (P < 0.01). In vitro angiogenic potential evaluated by VEGF immunofluorescence and tube formation experiments

To evaluate the pro-angiogenic potential of the composite scaffold in vitro, immunofluorescence (Fig. 7A) and tube formation experiments (Fig. 7B) were selected. VEGF expression in HUVECs was assessed by immunofluorescence staining at 7 and 21 days under different culture conditions. At day 7, quantitative analysis of relative fluorescence density revealed that the Mg/3D cell group exhibited the highest VEGF expression, which was significantly greater than that of all other groups (P < 0.001). In addition, the 3D cell group showed significantly higher VEGF fluorescence intensity compared with the Mg group (P < 0.001), while the Mg group also demonstrated a moderate but significant increase relative to the control group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 7A and C). The enhanced VEGF expression observed in the Mg group at this early stage suggests that magnesium alloy extracts exert a transient pro-angiogenic effect, potentially through Mg2+-mediated activation of endothelial cells and the early induction of angiogenic signaling pathways [38]. At day 21, VEGF expression remained markedly elevated in the Mg/3D cell group, which again showed significantly higher fluorescence intensity than all other groups (P < 0.001). The 3D cell group continued to exhibit significantly higher VEGF expression than the Mg group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 7B and D), whereas no significant difference was observed between the Mg and control groups at this later time point. This temporal attenuation of the Mg-only effect indicates that the angiogenic stimulation provided by magnesium ions alone is insufficient to sustain long-term VEGF production [39]. A plausible reason is that, in the absence of cellular support, the pro-angiogenic influence of Mg2+ is limited to early-stage endothelial activation and diminishes as the culture system reaches a new equilibrium [11]. In the tube formation experiments, our findings showed that the HUVECs generated sparse, short, and poorly interconnected tubular structures with limited branching and few closed meshes in the control group at 3 h time point, reflecting a low basal angiogenic activity under unstimulated conditions. In the Mg group, exposure to magnesium extract resulted in visibly elongated tubes and moderately increased branching complexity, indicating that Mg2+ alone exerted a pro-angiogenic effect, likely through modulation of endothelial migration and cytoskeletal organization. The 3D cell group exhibited further enhancement of vascular patterning compared with the Mg group. Tubular structures in this condition were more continuous and interconnected, with a higher density of junctions and a greater number of polygonal networks. These findings suggest that BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres released paracrine factors that actively supported endothelial sprouting, collective alignment, and spatial organization, even in the absence of exogenous magnesium stimulation. The Mg/3D cell group displayed the most mature and extensive vascular-like networks among all conditions even at the shorter time of 3 h. Endothelial structures formed dense, well-organized lattices characterized by long, continuous tubes, numerous branch points, and a significantly expanded mesh area covering the Matrigel surface. Compared with both single-factor groups, this condition showed more uniform spatial distribution and higher structural integrity of the capillary-like network, indicating a clear synergistic interaction between magnesium-derived ionic signals and BMSCs-derived paracrine regulation. The above trend is more evident at 6 h time point. The quantitative analysis also substantiated these morphological observations (Fig. 7E). These results suggest that magnesium extract sensitizes endothelial cells to pro-angiogenic cues, potentially by activating Mg2+-responsive signaling pathways that facilitate cytoskeletal remodeling and sprouting behavior. In parallel, BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres likely provide a sustained source of angiogenic cytokines and extracellular matrix components that reinforce endothelial adhesion, alignment, and intercellular coordination. Recent studies have further clarified that the pro-angiogenic effects of BMSCs are largely mediated through paracrine signaling and extracellular vesicle–dependent intercellular communication. BMSC-derived exosomes have been shown to deliver functional microRNAs and regulatory proteins to endothelial cells, thereby enhancing endothelial proliferation, migration, and tube formation. These exosomal cargos can activate key angiogenic pathways, including PI3K–Akt and eNOS signaling, by suppressing anti-angiogenic regulators such as PTEN and upregulating VEGF and angiopoietin expression, ultimately promoting vascular network formation [40]. Recent studies have demonstrated that BMSC-derived exosomal miRNAs, such as miR-486 and miR-150-5p, can directly regulate endothelial cell metabolic activity and signaling cascades, thereby enhancing angiogenic capacity and facilitating the formation of specialized angiogenic vessel subtypes closely associated with osteogenesis [41]. In addition, hypoxia-responsive pathways, particularly HIF-1α-mediated signaling, further amplify the secretion of pro-angiogenic factors and improve endothelial cell survival and vascular regeneration under regenerative microenvironment conditions [42]. These findings indicate that BMSCs function as key regulators of the regenerative microenvironment by modulating endothelial cell behavior through sustained paracrine signaling and exosome-mediated molecular transfer, thereby promoting angiogenesis and contributing to osteogenic–angiogenic coupling during bone regeneration.

In vivo evaluation of bone regeneration and angiogenesis induced by the composite scaffold

Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) analysis

Micro-CT was employed at 8 and 16 weeks post-treatment to comprehensively assess femoral head structural integrity and bone regeneration across experimental groups. Representative sagittal, coronal and transverse sections, together with three-dimensional reconstructions, revealed substantial differences in trabecular architecture and defect repair among groups (Fig. 8A and Fig. S5). In the ONFH group, progressive deterioration of femoral head structure was observed over time, characterized by expansion of necrotic regions, marked trabecular rarefaction or complete loss in localized areas, and pronounced cystic degeneration in the subchondral bone. These features are hallmarks of advanced osteonecrosis and reflect the inability of the necrotic femoral head to undergo effective structural self-repair in the absence of intervention (Fig. S5) [43]. Consistent with these morphological changes, quantitative micro-CT analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular number (Tb.N), accompanied by an increase in trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), in the ONFH group relative to normal controls (Fig. 8B–E). The Mg/3D cell group displayed a markedly denser and more uniformly distributed trabecular structure at 8 weeks, closely resembling normal cancellous bone morphology (Fig. 8A). Quantitatively, this group exhibited significantly increased BV/TV, Tb.Th and Tb.N, together with a pronounced reduction in Tb.Sp compared with the ONFH group (P < 0.05; Fig. 8B–E). Hematoxylin and eosin, Masson, goldner staining

To further evaluate the therapeutic effect of the composite scaffold on osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH), histomorphometric analyses were performed using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson's trichrome, and Goldner's trichrome staining.

As shown in Fig. 9A, the femoral heads of the ONFH group exhibited classical pathological features of osteonecrosis, including a high number of empty osteocyte lacunae, disrupted and fragmented trabecular structures, and a sparse and disorganized trabecular arrangement. In addition, extensive adipose tissue infiltration into the marrow cavity was observed, reflecting impaired bone remodeling and degeneration of the marrow microenvironment. These pathological alterations are characteristic indicators of osteonecrotic progression and are closely associated with compromised mechanical integrity of the femoral head. In contrast, the Mg/3D cell group demonstrated markedly improved histological architecture. The trabecular network appeared denser and more regularly organized, with significantly fewer empty lacunae and minimal fat cell infiltration. These findings suggest that the composite scaffold effectively restored bone microstructure and promoted active bone remodeling within the necrotic region. The 3D cell group and Mg group showed partial improvement compared with the ONFH group, but their trabecular architecture remained less organized and more discontinuous than that observed in the Mg/3D cell group, indicating that the combined treatment exerted a more pronounced therapeutic effect than either component alone. Quantitative analysis of the empty lacunae rate further supported these observations (Fig. 9B). The Mg/3D cell group exhibited the lowest percentage of empty osteocyte lacunae among all groups, indicating improved osteocyte viability and recovery of bone tissue homeostasis. According to the histopathological diagnostic criteria of ONFH, trabecular bone is considered necrotic when the empty lacunae rate exceeds 50%, typically accompanied by extensive trabecular involvement and partial bone marrow necrosis. In the present study, the ONFH group clearly fulfilled these pathological criteria, whereas the Mg/3D cell group demonstrated a substantial reduction in lacunar emptiness after 16 weeks of treatment (Fig. 9A), suggesting effective reversal of osteonecrotic degeneration [45]. As shown in Fig. 9A, collagen fibers were stained blue, whereas mature bone tissue appeared red. The control and Mg/3D cell groups displayed extensive blue-stained regions, indicating abundant collagen deposition and active osteogenesis. In contrast, the ONFH group was dominated by red-stained regions corresponding to degenerative or residual bone tissue, with markedly reduced collagen-rich areas. Co-culture study

Transcriptomic evidence of adhesion and mechanotransduction pathway activation in osteoblasts

To preliminarily investigate the potential molecular mechanisms by which the magnesium ion-enriched microenvironment combined with BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres promotes osteonecrosis repair, transcriptomic sequencing was performed on human osteoblasts derived from the co-culture system. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were subsequently identified, and bioinformatic analyses were conducted to compare gene expression profiles and associated signaling pathways between the two groups.

Unsupervised clustering analysis of all detected genes was performed, and a gene expression heatmap was generated (Fig. 11A). The results revealed a clear separation in global gene expression patterns between the two groups, with the Osteoblast-3DCM and Osteoblast-C groups forming two distinct transcriptional profiles. This finding indicates that the composite co-culture system markedly alters the transcriptional landscape of human osteoblasts. It further suggests that microenvironmental changes mediated by the magnesium ion milieu and the BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres may regulate osteogenesis-related gene expression at the molecular level. Based on these observations, DEGs between the two groups were identified and visualized using a volcano plot (Fig. 11B). Compared with the Osteoblast-C group, a total of 8850 DEGs were identified in the Osteoblast-3DCM group. These genes exhibited distinct expression trends, with a subset significantly upregulated under stimulation by the composite system, while others were downregulated. This bidirectional regulation suggests that the magnesium-associated microenvironment may modulate osteoblast function through coordinated regulation of multiple gene networks [53]. To further elucidate the biological functions associated with these DEGs, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed. The results demonstrated that, compared with the Osteoblast-C group, multiple signaling pathways related to cell adhesion and extracellular matrix (ECM) interactions were significantly upregulated in the Osteoblast-3DCM group. These primarily included the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, ECM-receptor interaction, and focal adhesion pathways. Further analysis revealed that 85 genes were significantly upregulated within the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. This pathway is widely recognized as a central regulator of cell survival and regeneration during osteonecrosis repair, playing critical roles in regulating cell survival, osteogenic differentiation, angiogenesis, and anti-apoptotic responses across multiple stages of the repair process. In addition, 32 genes were significantly upregulated in the ECM-receptor interaction pathway, which primarily describes the interactions between extracellular matrix components and cell surface receptors. This pathway regulates key biological processes such as cell adhesion, migration, differentiation, and signal transduction, thereby playing a pivotal role in bone tissue repair and remodeling. Meanwhile, 66 genes were significantly upregulated in the focal adhesion pathway, which serves as a critical signaling nexus linking the extracellular matrix to the cytoskeletal system. During osteonecrosis repair, this pathway plays essential roles in regulating cellular mechanotransduction and cell fate determination, thereby contributing to both bone regeneration and vascular reconstruction [54]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the extracellular matrix microenvironment plays a crucial role in osteonecrosis repair. The ECM not only provides structural support for cells but also mediates intracellular signaling through membrane receptors such as integrins, thereby regulating a variety of biological processes including cell adhesion, migration, and differentiation [31,55]. Extensive evidence has established the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway as a central regulatory axis in osteonecrosis repair, orchestrating cell survival, osteogenic differentiation, angiogenesis, and anti-apoptotic responses throughout the repair process. The ECM–receptor interaction pathway primarily reflects the interplay between ECM components and cell surface receptors, whereas focal adhesion represents a key signaling node linking the ECM to the cytoskeleton. These two pathways are fundamental to cellular mechanotransduction. Importantly, these signaling pathways do not function in isolation but instead form an interconnected regulatory network, in which the PI3K-Akt pathway is often considered a critical downstream effector of adhesion-mediated signaling. The findings of the present study are largely consistent with previous reports, further supporting the notion that cell adhesion and mechanotransduction constitute essential molecular foundations for regulating osteoblast function during osteonecrosis repair [56].

Validation of key signaling pathways at the protein level and mechanistic integration

To further validate the expression changes of key proteins involved in the signaling pathways identified by transcriptomic analysis, Western blot assays were performed to examine representative proteins in the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, ECM-receptor interaction pathway, and focal adhesion pathway. The Western blot results are presented in Fig. 11I–K, and the quantitative analyses are shown in Fig. 11L–N. The results demonstrated that the expression level of CREB3, a key regulatory protein in the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, was significantly higher in the Osteoblast-3DCM group than in the Osteoblast-C group, with statistical significance. This finding suggests that the magnesium ion microenvironment combined with BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres activates PI3K-Akt-related signal transduction. In the ECM-receptor interaction pathway, the expression levels of key regulatory proteins, including COL4A1, Thrombospondin 2, ITGA7, and LAMB3, were also significantly elevated in the Osteoblast-3DCM group compared with the Osteoblast-C group, indicating that the composite system enhances the expression of extracellular matrix-related proteins in osteoblasts and may thereby strengthen cell-ECM interactions. Furthermore, within the focal adhesion pathway, the expression levels of ILK, MYL12A, and phospho-MYL12A remained significantly higher in the Osteoblast-3DCM group than in the Osteoblast-C group, suggesting that the composite system may promote cellular mechanotransduction by enhancing adhesion structures and cytoskeletal remodeling.

Based on these findings, we propose a preliminary mechanistic model in which osteoblasts exposed to the magnesium ion microenvironment and BMSCs-loaded 3D microsphere composite system first exhibit activation of the upstream PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, leading to upregulation of the downstream transcriptional regulator CREB3. Activation of PI3K-Akt signaling further modulates the ECM-receptor interaction pathway by upregulating key structural proteins, including COL4A1, Thrombospondin 2, ITGA7, and LAMB3, thereby enhancing interactions between the extracellular matrix and cell surface receptors. Concurrently, integrin-associated signaling activates the focal adhesion pathway, promoting increased ILK expression and enhanced phosphorylation of MYL12A, which in turn regulates cytoskeletal remodeling and cellular mechanotransduction. Through this cascade of signaling events, osteoblast adhesion capacity, cytoskeletal organization, and osteogenic activity may ultimately be enhanced, contributing to osteonecrosis repair [57].

The findings of this section are of considerable significance for elucidating the mechanism by which the magnesium ion microenvironment combined with BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres facilitates osteonecrosis repair. At the transcriptomic level, this study systematically identified key molecular pathways through which magnesium ions and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells regulate osteoblast function, and proposed a potential signaling regulatory axis, namely “PI3K-Akt-ECM-receptor interaction-focal adhesion,” which was further preliminarily validated at the protein expression level. These results provide new experimental evidence for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying the synergistic effects of magnesium-based biomaterials and seed cells in promoting bone tissue regeneration. Moreover, by integrating the magnesium ion microenvironment with a BMSCs-loaded 3D microsphere system, this study elucidates the molecular basis of osteonecrosis repair from the perspective of microenvironmental regulation, thereby enriching current knowledge regarding the mechanisms of action of bioactive materials in bone tissue engineering [58,59].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the composite scaffold comprising a microporous magnesium alloy scaffold integrated with BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres markedly promotes structural restoration and bone regeneration in osteonecrosis of the femoral head. Through the synergistic effects of controlled magnesium ion release and BMSC-derived paracrine signaling, the scaffold enhances osteoblast adhesion, cytoskeletal organization, and mechanotransductive signaling, primarily via activation of the PI3K-Akt-ECM-receptor interaction-focal adhesion axis. These findings provide mechanistic insight into how bioactive composite scaffolds facilitate osteogenic repair and highlight their potential as a therapeutic strategy for the treatment of femoral head osteonecrosis.

Materials and methods

Preparation of the composite scaffold

Fabrication of WE43 scaffolds by 3D printing and surface acid etching, and deposition of a layered double hydroxide (LDH) coating via high temperature oxidation (HTO) processing

The composition of WE43 powder (Weihao, Tangshan, China) is 1.12% Gd, 3.41% Y, 0.19% Zn, 0.34% Zr, 1.12% Nd and balanced Mg. The scaffolds were fabricated by a laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) machine (BLT S210, Xi'an, China) with a 70 μm laser spot diameter. The parameters employed in this study were as follows: laser power of 100 W, scanning speed of 1000 mm/s, layer thickness of 40 μm, and hatch space of 100 μm. Before printing, the substrate was preheated to 200 °C, and a zig-zag exposure pattern with a 67° rotation (α) between successive layers was employed to promote a uniform microstructure with minimal defects. Cylindrical contoured lattice gyroid scaffolds with a diameter of 3 mm, height of 26 mm, 500 mm2 and 60% porosity were fabricated for degradation and implantation tests. Additionally, cylindrical discs with a diameter of 10 mm and height of 2 mm were printed for coating property evaluation. Following 3D printing, the scaffolds were immersed in anhydrous ethanol containing 5% HCl and 5% HNO3 for chemical etching, with continuous stirring for 100 s. Subsequently, the samples were ultrasonically cleaned in anhydrous ethanol for 120 s and dried at 50 °C for 90 min, resulting in As-printed scaffolds with a metallic sheen on the surface.

The As-printed scaffolds were placed in a muffle furnace and heated to 525 °C at a constant rate of 10 °C/min in an air atmosphere, where they were held for 8 h. After that, the scaffolds were quenched in room-temperature water, resulting in HTO scaffolds with a surface passivation layer. The preparation of LDH coatings was carried out using the HTO scaffolds. 0.0005 mol Na2CO3 was added to 50 mL of 0.02 M Al(NO3)3 solution under stirring, followed by the dropwise addition of 10 M NaOH until the pH reached 12. The HTO scaffolds and the solution were placed together in a hydrothermal reactor, and the hydrothermal reaction was conducted at 120 °C for 6 h to form the LDH coating. The resulting scaffolds were sequentially cleaned with deionized water and anhydrous ethanol via ultrasonic treatment for 120 s, followed by drying at 50 °C. The final scaffolds were called Mg-Al LDH scaffolds [60].

Characterization of materials, in vitro degradation and electrochemical tests

The surface morphology was observed using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, Zeiss Gemini-300, Germany) at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV, while the elemental distribution was analyzed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. The phase composition was further analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance, USA) with Cu Kα radiation (40 kV, 40 mA). The diffraction patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 10-85°. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR, Vertex 70, Bruker, Germany) was used to detect characteristic bands in the LDH layers. Spectra were recorded in the range of 4000 cm−1 to 600 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1 using the attenuated total reflectance (ATR) method. Surface topography and roughness were characterized using atomic force microscopy (AFM, Bruker Dimension Icon, USA) with tapping mode. Before testing, sample surfaces were mechanically polished with aluminum oxide suspension to reveal the effect of the coating on surface morphology. The hydrophilicity of the coating and substrate was evaluated using a surface contact angle goniometer (Data Physics OCA 20, Germany).

In vitro immersion test was conducted using Hanks' solution (containing calcium and magnesium, without phenol red) as a simulated body fluid. The immersion was carried out at 37 °C for up to 28 days. The pH value of the immersion solution was measured daily (every two days after day14) to monitor the degradation rate. Solutions and scaffolds were collected at immersion time points of 1, 3, 7, 14, and 28 days to analysis the ion concentration and residual weight. Mg2+ ion concentration was determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Agilent 5110, USA) after dissolving residues in the solution with HNO3. Residual weight was calculated after removing corrosion products on scaffolds through a CrO3 solution. A three-electrode electrochemical workstation was employed to evaluate the electrochemical properties. The sample was immersed in Hanks’ solution at 37 °C for 60 min to stabilize the open circuit potential (OCP). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was then performed at a frequency range of 10−2 to 105 Hz with an amplitude of 10 mV. Corrosion potential (Ecorr) and corrosion current density (Icorr) were determined from the potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) curves using the Tafel extrapolation method [61].

Preparation of BMSCs-laden 3D microspheres

The 3D microspheres used in this study were provided by the School of Medicine, Tsinghua University. The 3D microspheres are primarily composed of polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA) [62,63]. The microspheres were stored in tablet form (Fig. S3B), referred to as 3D microcarrier tablets. Five microcarrier tablets (20 mg per tablet) were placed into a sterile magnetic rotary culture vessel, followed by the addition of 20 mL of complete culture medium to allow complete dissolution of the tablets. Subsequently, 1 mL of a human BMSC suspension containing 2.5 × 106 cells was slowly introduced along the inner wall of the vessel, after which an additional 30 mL of complete medium was added. The magnetic rotary culture vessel, containing a total initial culture volume of 50 mL, was placed on a magnetic stirring platform inside a cell incubator and subjected to intermittent rotation at 35 rpm for 5 min, followed by 0 rpm for 1 h, for a total of 24 cycles. The incubator was maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. On the following day, the rotation speed was adjusted to a constant 40 rpm, and an additional 25 mL of complete medium was supplemented. Thereafter, the medium was replaced every 3 days, and the culture was maintained for up to 21 days, resulting in the successful preparation of BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres [19].

Preparation of the magnesium-based composite scaffold incorporating BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres

The composite scaffold was assembled by integrating prefabricated BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres with the magnesium alloy scaffold through a physical adsorption-based approach. BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres were harvested after in vitro culture and resuspended in complete culture medium to obtain a homogeneous microsphere suspension. The suspension was ensured to contain at least 1 × 105 cells by counting. Magnesium alloy scaffolds were sterilized and preconditioned in culture medium to improve surface wettability. Each scaffold was then immersed in the microsphere suspension and incubated under static conditions at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 for a defined period, allowing the microspheres to settle and attach to the scaffold surface. During incubation, the microsphere suspension was gently agitated at predetermined intervals to facilitate uniform distribution and infiltration of microspheres into the interconnected pore network of the scaffold. After incubation, the scaffolds were carefully removed and gently rinsed with PBS while preserving those stably adsorbed onto the scaffold struts and pore walls. The resulting constructs were defined as composite scaffolds and were immediately used for subsequent experimental animal surgery. This assembly strategy enabled the homogeneous spatial integration of cell-laden microspheres within the magnesium scaffold while maintaining the intrinsic porous architecture and mechanical integrity of the scaffold.

In vitro study

In vitro biocompatibility test

Flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry analysis was employed to evaluate the biocompatibility of the composite scaffold and its effects on the phenotypic stability of BMSCs. BMSCs were cultured in a three-dimensional configuration using 3D microspheres, the magnesium alloy extract prepared in advance was used to replace the medium. The preparation method of the extract is detailed in the Supplementary file 1. And cells were harvested for analysis on days 0, 3, 7, and 14. At each time point, the 3D microspheres were first lysed, followed by cell collection; the lysis procedure has been described in detail in our previous study. After collection, cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and resuspended in staining buffer. The cells were then incubated, according to the manufacturer's instructions, with fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies against human CD105, CD73, CD90, CD14, and CD34. Appropriate isotype-matched control antibodies were included to determine background fluorescence. Following incubation, cells were washed, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (BioTek, America). A minimum of 10,000 events per sample were acquired. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version X), and the percentages of marker-positive cells were calculated based on gated live cell populations. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results are presented as the proportion of cells expressing each surface marker at the indicated time points [64].

Cytoskeleton staining and morphological assessment of BMSCs

To assess cell morphology, cytoskeletal organization, and proliferation behavior under different culture conditions, actin filament staining was performed using phalloidin. BMSCs were assigned to four experimental groups: control (cells cultured in standard growth medium), Mg (cells cultured in growth medium supplemented with magnesium alloy extracts), 3D cell (BMSCs seeded on 3D microspheres), and Mg/3D cell (BMSCs seeded on 3D microspheres and cultured in the presence of magnesium alloy extracts).

Cells were cultured under standard conditions and collected for cytoskeletal staining on days 1, 4, and 7. At each time point, samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and incubated with phalloidin conjugated to a fluorescent dye to label filamentous actin (F-actin). Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. After washing, samples were mounted and imaged using a fluorescence or confocal microscope. Representative images were acquired to qualitatively evaluate cell spreading, actin cytoskeleton organization, and changes in cell density over time. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and imaging parameters were kept constant across all groups to ensure comparability [65,66].

Live/dead staining

In the three-dimensional (3D) culture system containing magnesium alloy extracts, suspensions of BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres were collected on day 7 for live/dead viability staining. According to the manufacturer's instructions, aliquots of the culture suspension were transferred to a 96-well plate at 100 μL per well. After removal of the supernatant, calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI) (Wako, Japan) were added. The plate was then incubated at room temperature in the dark for 20 min. Subsequently, the supernatant was discarded, and the samples were washed twice with PBS. The stained samples were observed under a fluorescence microscope to assess cell viability [39].

Cell viability and cytotoxicity assays

Cell viability and cytotoxicity were evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay, respectively. BMSCs were divided into four experimental groups: control (cells cultured in standard growth medium), Mg (cells cultured in growth medium supplemented with magnesium alloy extracts), 3D cell (BMSCs seeded on 3D microspheres), and Mg/3D cell (BMSCs seeded on 3D microspheres and cultured in the presence of magnesium alloy extracts). For the CCK-8 assay, cells were seeded under the indicated conditions and cultured for predetermined time points. At each time point, CCK-8 reagent was added to the culture medium according to the manufacturer's instructions, followed by incubation at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and cell viability was expressed as optical density values normalized to the control group. For the LDH assay, culture supernatants were collected at corresponding time points to assess membrane integrity-associated cytotoxicity. LDH activity in the supernatants was quantified using a commercial LDH detection kit following the manufacturer's protocol. Absorbance was measured at the recommended wavelength, and LDH release was calculated relative to the control group. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. These assays were used in combination to comprehensively evaluate the biocompatibility of the Mg-based composite scaffold by simultaneously assessing cellular metabolic activity and cytotoxic responses [67].

Evaluation of osteogenic capacity in vitro

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining

ALP staining was performed to assess early osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs under different culture conditions. Cells were assigned to four experimental groups: control (BMSCs cultured on tissue culture plates and induced with osteogenic differentiation medium), Mg (BMSCs cultured on tissue culture plates and induced with osteogenic differentiation medium supplemented with magnesium alloy extracts), 3D cell (BMSCs seeded on 3D microspheres and induced with osteogenic differentiation medium), and Mg/3D cell (BMSCs seeded on 3D microspheres and induced with osteogenic differentiation medium supplemented with magnesium alloy extracts). Cells were cultured in osteogenic differentiation medium and harvested for ALP staining on days 7 and 14. At each time point, samples were gently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. ALP staining was then performed using a commercial ALP staining kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. After staining, samples were rinsed with distilled water and imaged using a light microscope. Representative images were acquired under identical imaging conditions for all groups. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and ALP staining was used as an indicator of early-stage osteogenic differentiation [68].

Alizarin red S (ARS) staining

ARS staining was performed to evaluate extracellular matrix mineralization as a marker of late-stage osteogenic differentiation. The experimental grouping was identical to that used for ALP staining. The BMSCs were cultured either on conventional tissue culture plates or on 3D microspheres, and induced with standard osteogenic differentiation medium in the absence or presence of magnesium alloy extracts. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2, and the osteogenic medium was refreshed every 2-3 days to ensure a stable induction environment. At predetermined time points (day 7 and 14), samples were collected for mineralization assessment. Culture media were removed, and cells were gently washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) before fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15-20 min at room temperature. After fixation, samples were rinsed with PBS and incubated with 2% (w/v) Alizarin Red S solution (pH 4.1-4.3) for 20-30 min at room temperature. Excess dye was removed by repeated washing with distilled water until the supernatant became clear. Particular care was taken during washing steps in the 3D culture groups to prevent microsphere loss. Stained samples were air-dried and imaged using a light microscope under identical acquisition settings across all groups. For each sample, five randomly selected fields were recorded, and representative images were used for analysis. ARS-positive mineralized nodules, indicated by orange-red staining, were considered evidence of calcium deposition and late-stage osteogenic differentiation. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and reproducibility was confirmed across independent repeats [69].

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis was performed to evaluate osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs under different coculture conditions. A transwell coculture system was established using 24-well plates fitted with transwell inserts (pore size 0.4 μm; Corning). In all experimental groups, BMSCs were seeded in the lower chamber at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured in osteogenic induction medium. Experimental grouping of the upper chamber was identical to that used for immunofluorescence analysis. The upper chamber volume was 200 μL, while the lower chamber contained 600 μL of osteogenic induction medium. Culture medium was refreshed every 2-3 days. At days 7 and 21, BMSCs from the lower chamber were harvested for Western blot. Cells were washed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were collected and clarified by centrifugation at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Equal amounts of protein (20-30 μg per sample) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against osteogenic markers, including alkaline phosphatase (ALP), collagen type I (COL-1), osteocalcin (OCN), and runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2). After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies and visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system. Protein band intensities were quantified by densitometric analysis and normalized to β-actin. All experiments were performed in triplicate [70].

Immunofluorescence analysis

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to evaluate osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs under different coculture conditions. A transwell coculture system was established using 24-well plates equipped with transwell inserts (pore size 0.4 μm; Corning), allowing paracrine interactions while preventing direct cell contact between compartments. In all experimental groups, BMSCs were seeded in the lower chamber at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured in osteogenic induction medium. Four experimental groups were designed according to the conditions applied to the upper chamber: control group, in which the upper chamber contained osteogenic induction medium alone; Mg group, in which magnesium alloy extracts were added to the upper chamber at a predefined concentration; 3D cell group, in which a suspension of BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres was placed in the upper chamber; and Mg/3D cell group, in which BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres were combined with magnesium alloy extracts in the upper chamber. The volume of medium or suspension added to the upper chamber was 200 μL, while the lower chamber contained 600 μL of osteogenic induction medium. Culture medium was refreshed every 2-3 days. BMSCs in the lower chamber were collected at 7 and 21 days for immunofluorescence analysis. Cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin, samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ALP (Abcam, ab224335) and collagen type I (COL-1) (Abcam, ab96723). Following washing, cells were incubated with appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature in the dark. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope under identical imaging parameters for all groups. Immunofluorescence staining for ALP and COL-1 was used to assess osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs under different coculture conditions. All experiments were performed in triplicate [67].

Immunofluorescence analysis of angiogenic differentiation in vitro

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to evaluate angiogenic activity of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) under different coculture conditions. A transwell coculture system was established using 24-well plates equipped with transwell inserts (pore size 0.4 μm; Corning), which allowed paracrine signaling between compartments without direct cell contact. In all experimental groups, HUVECs were seeded in the lower chamber at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured in endothelial cell growth medium. The experimental groups in the upper chamber were identical to those used in the osteogenic coculture experiments. Briefly, the control group received endothelial cell growth medium alone; the Mg group received magnesium alloy extracts; the 3D cell group received a suspension of BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres; and the Mg/3D cell group received a combination of BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres and magnesium alloy extracts. The volume of medium or suspension added to the upper chamber was 200 μL, while 600 μL of endothelial cell growth medium was added to the lower chamber. Culture medium was refreshed every 2-3 days. At days 7 and 21, HUVECs in the lower chamber were harvested for immunofluorescence analysis. Cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). After washing, samples were incubated with appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature in the dark. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope under identical imaging parameters for all groups. Immunofluorescence staining of VEGF was used to assess angiogenic responses of HUVECs under different coculture conditions. All experiments were performed in triplicate [67,68].

The tube formation experiment

The angiogenic response of HUVECs was evaluated using a transwell-based co-culture system combined with a Matrigel tube formation assay. HUVECs at passages 3-6 were cultured in endothelial growth medium (EGM-2, Lonza, CC-3162) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, 10099-141), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, 15140-122), and endothelial growth supplements according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 and used at 70 the 3D microspheres had previously been fabricated under sterile conditions and suspended in serum-free α-MEM prior to use. For tube formation, the lower chamber of a 24-well transwell system (Corning Costar, 3470; polycarbonate membrane, 8 μm pore size) was pre-coated with 150 μl growth factor-reduced Matrigel (Corning, 356231) per well and polymerized at 37 °C for 30 min. HUVECs were then seeded onto the Matrigel-coated membrane at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells per well in 500 μl of EGM-2 medium. The upper chamber of each transwell groups were same as immunofluorescence experiment. The transwell plates were incubated for 3 h and 6 h at 37 °C to allow diffusion of soluble factors and ions across the membrane while preventing direct cell-cell contact or microsphere migration. After incubation, the lower chamber was gently washed twice with warm PBS, and tube-like structures formed by HUVECs on the Matrigel were imaged using an inverted phase-contrast microscope (Nikon Ti2) equipped with a digital camera. For each well, at least five randomly selected non-overlapping fields were captured at 10 × magnification. Quantitative analysis of angiogenic network formation was performed using ImageJ (NIH) with the Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin. The following parameters were extracted: number of tubes. All experiments were conducted in biological triplicate with three technical replicates per group. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test.

In vivo study

Establishment of a steroid-induced femoral head osteonecrosis model in New Zealand white rabbits

A rabbit model of ONFH was established using a combined lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and methylprednisolone (MPS) protocol. The animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines set by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUC), and approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the 920th Hospital of the People's Liberation Army Joint Logistic Support Force (Approval No. 2022-039-01). A total of 40 healthy New Zealand white rabbits (male, body weight approximately 3.0 kg, aged 6 months to 1 year) were used in this study. All animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions with free access to food and water. To induce ONFH, all rabbits received an intravenous injection of LPS (Sigma-Aldrich) at a dose of 10 μg/kg. Twenty-four hours after LPS administration, methylprednisolone (Pfizer, USA) was administered by intramuscular injection at a dose of 20 mg/kg. The MPS injection was repeated every 24 h for a total of three consecutive doses, with injections alternated between the left and right hind limbs. To prevent infection during the steroid administration period, penicillin (100,000 U) was administered intramuscularly at each MPS injection. Following drug administration, animals were maintained for 6 weeks to allow development of osteonecrotic lesions. Model establishment was evaluated by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) using a 7.0 T scanner. Ten rabbits were randomly selected for in vivo bilateral femoral head imaging to assess the presence of osteonecrotic changes and determine the success rate of model induction [71].

Surgical procedure

Prior to surgery, the operative area of each rabbit was shaved using an electric razor. Animals were anesthetized by inhalation of isoflurane, and adequate anesthesia was confirmed before positioning the animals in the lateral decubitus position. The surgical field was routinely disinfected with povidone-iodine solution and draped under sterile conditions. The anatomical location and orientation of the femur were identified by palpation. A longitudinal skin incision was made along the femoral neck axis, followed by blunt dissection of the subcutaneous tissue and muscles to fully expose the femoral neck. A 0.8-mm Kirschner wire was then inserted at an angle of approximately 30° below the greater trochanter and advanced along the longitudinal axis of the femoral neck toward the distal femoral head to a depth of approximately 1 cm. The position and trajectory of the Kirschner wire were confirmed by X-ray imaging (Fig. S4A). After confirmation of proper positioning, a 3.5-mm cannulated drill was used to enlarge the medullary canal along the Kirschner wire to create a bone tunnel (Fig. S4B). The bone tunnel was thoroughly irrigated with sterile saline to remove residual bone debris. Subsequently, animals received different treatments according to their group allocation. In the Mg group, a magnesium alloy scaffold was implanted into the bone tunnel. In the 3D cell group, a suspension of BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres was delivered into the tunnel. In the Mg/3D cell group, a composite scaffold consisting of a magnesium alloy scaffold combined with BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres was implanted (Fig. S4C). After implantation, the bone tunnel was sealed with bone wax, and the incision was closed in layers. Animals were monitored until full recovery from anesthesia, and once vital signs were stable, they were returned to the animal facility for postoperative care.

Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) analysis

The femoral heads were scanned using micro-CT (Hiscan, China), with voltage of 80 kV and current of 100 μA. The single exposure time is 50 ms, the scanning resolution is 25 μm, and the scanning angle interval is 0.5°. The selection of threshold depends on the comparison of gray value between binary image and original image, and femoral heads between different groups performed the same threshold range. The scanning software has a pixel size of 0.1 × 0.1 × 0.1 mm, a scanning accuracy of micrometers, and a scanning range of 10 cm from the femoral head to the middle of the femur. A 5 mm wide area below the epiphyseal line of femoral head with a total of 150 consecutive 2D slices was drawn as a region of interest (ROI). After scanning, 3D images were reconstructed by Recon software and the BV/TV, Tb.N, Tb.Th, and Tb.Sp of ROI were calculated by Cruiser software [72].

Histological staining and histomorphometric analysis

At 8 and 16 weeks after treatment, rabbits were euthanized and femoral head specimens were harvested for histological evaluation. The samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin. Serial sections with a thickness of 5 μm were prepared for histological staining. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to assess general tissue morphology, trabecular integrity, and the presence of osteonecrotic features. Masson's trichrome staining was used to evaluate collagen deposition and fibrotic tissue formation, while Goldner's trichrome staining was conducted to distinguish mineralized bone, osteoid, and soft tissue components. All staining procedures were carried out according to standard protocols.

Stained sections were imaged using a light microscope under identical imaging conditions for all groups. Representative images were obtained for qualitative analysis. For quantitative histomorphometric assessment, multiple randomly selected fields from each section were analyzed using ImageJ software to evaluate parameters related to bone structure and tissue composition. All analyses were performed in a blinded manner to minimize bias [47].

Immunohistochemical staining analysis

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to evaluate osteogenic and angiogenic marker expression in femoral head samples at 8 and 16 weeks after treatment. Paraffin-embedded sections (5 μm) were first deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was carried out by heating sections in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) using a microwave oven. After cooling to room temperature, sections were rinsed with PBS and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min.

Non-specific binding was blocked by incubating sections with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against OCN and CD34, which were used as markers of osteogenesis and angiogenesis, respectively. After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. OCN was visualized using a green fluorescent signal, while CD34 was visualized using a yellow fluorescent signal. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Stained sections were examined and imaged using a fluorescence microscope under identical exposure settings for all groups. Representative images were acquired from multiple randomly selected fields within each section. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity and positive area was performed using ImageJ software. All image acquisition and analyses were conducted in a blinded manner [73].

Co-culture study preliminary study on the mechanism of the composite scaffold in promoting the repair of femoral head necrosis

Transwell co-culture

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the pro-regenerative effects of the composite scaffold on osteonecrosis of the femoral head, a Transwell-based indirect co-culture system was established. Human osteoblasts were seeded into the lower chambers of 6-well Transwell plates (0.4 μm pore size; Corning, USA) at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured in complete osteogenic medium (Human osteoblasts were provided by OriCell® (HUXMA-01001)). The upper chambers were subjected to different intervention conditions, generating three experimental groups: the Osteoblast group, in which the upper chamber contained standard complete culture medium only; the Osteoblast-C group, in which a suspension of BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres was added to the upper chamber; and the Osteoblast-3DCM group, in which BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres were combined with magnesium alloy extracts in the upper chamber. The volume of medium in both chambers was maintained at 2 ml per well, and the culture medium was refreshed every 3 days throughout the experiment [21].

RNA sequencing analysis

After 7 days of co-culture, cells from the lower chambers were harvested for transcriptomic analysis and immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using the Qiagen RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using the Qubit® RNA Assay Kit on a Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, CA, USA). For each sample, 3 μg of total RNA was used for library construction with the TruSeq RNA Library Prep Kit v2 (Illumina, CA, USA). High-throughput sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq X platform (Illumina, CA, USA), generating approximately 6 Gb of paired-end sequencing data per sample. Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control using Trimmomatic v0.39 to remove adaptor sequences and low-quality bases. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2 v2.0.5. Gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) using StringTie2 v2.0.4. Differential gene expression analysis was performed with DESeq v1.10.1, and genes with an adjusted P value < 0.05, calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, and an absolute fold change≥1.5 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Functional enrichment analysis of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways was conducted for the identified differentially expressed genes using the R package clusterProfiler, with enriched pathways defined by adjusted P values < 0.05 (significant) or < 0.01 (highly significant) [74].

Western blot analysis of osteoblasts in transwell co-culture system

To validate the transcriptomic alterations identified in the Transwell co-culture system at the protein level, Western blot analysis was performed on osteoblasts harvested from the lower chamber at day 7. Human osteoblasts were seeded into the lower compartment of 6-well Transwell plates (Corning, USA; pore size 0.4 μm) at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured in complete osteoblast growth medium. The upper chambers were assigned to three experimental conditions: Osteoblast-C group, containing suspensions of BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres; and Osteoblast-3DCM group, containing suspensions of BMSCs-loaded 3D microspheres supplemented with magnesium alloy extracts. The co-culture system was maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2, and the culture medium was refreshed every 3 days. At day 7, osteoblasts from the lower chambers were gently washed twice with cold PBS and lysed using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Cell lysates were collected and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove debris. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of total protein (30 μg per sample) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting key components of the ECM-integrin-cytoskeletal signaling axis, including collagen type IV alpha 1 (COL4A1), thrombospondin-2 (THBS2), integrin alpha-7 (ITGA7), laminin subunit beta-3 (LAMB3), integrin-linked kinase (ILK), CREB3, phosphorylated myosin light chain 12A (p-MYL12A), total MYL12A, and β-actin as an internal loading control. Following incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents and imaged with a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software, and protein expression levels were normalized to β-actin.

Statistical analyses

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test was applied for normally distributed data, whereas the Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way or two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's or Bonferroni's post hoc test, as appropriate. For experiments involving multiple time points, two-way ANOVA was employed to evaluate the effects of treatment and time. Quantitative analyses of histological staining, immunofluorescence intensity, and positive area were performed using ImageJ software based on multiple randomly selected fields per section. All image acquisition and data analyses were conducted in a blinded manner. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
