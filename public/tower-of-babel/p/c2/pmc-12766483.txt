Feasibility of Valve in Valve After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement With SAPIEN 3 Valves and Surgical Aortic Valve Replacement
Authors: Tej Sheth, Warkaa Al-Shamkani, Ahmed Makhdoum, Jorge Chavarria Viquez, Muhammad Suleman, Alexander Dick, Janarthanan Sathananthan, David Wood, Neil Fam, Hatim AlRaddadi, Madhu K. Natarajan, James L. Velianou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe feasibility of transcatheter intervention after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) and surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) is important for the lifetime management of aortic stenosis.MethodsA multicenter study was conducted of 4 patient groups: (1) TAVR using SAPIEN 3 at ≤90% implant depth, (2) TAVR using SAPIEN 3 at >90% implant depth, (3) SAVR, and (4) SAVR with aortic root enlargement (ARE) who underwent post-treatment computed tomography. Future TAVR was classified as challenging if the simulated neo-skirt was at or above the coronary ostia and <4 mm away from the coronary ostia and/or <2 mm from the sinotubular junction or aortic wall.ResultsA total of 245 patients were evaluated (n = 117 TAVR using SAPIEN 3 at ≤90% implant depth, n = 66 TAVR using SAPIEN 3 at >90% implant depth, n = 31 SAVR, n = 31 SAVR with ARE). The proportion of patients where the simulated neo-skirt was below both coronary arteries was the highest in TAVR with implantation depth ≤90% (47.0%), intermediate in TAVR using SAPIEN 3 at >90% implant depth (22.7%) and SAVR (12.9%), and the lowest in SAVR with ARE (0%), (p < 0.001). Challenging anatomy for future TAVR was identified in 9.8% of TAVR at ≤90% implant depth, 28.8% of TAVR at >90% implant depth, 22.6% of SAVR, and 29.0% of SAVR with ARE (p < 0.001).ConclusionsBased on post-treatment computed tomography analysis, TAVR with SAPIEN 3 at implant depth ≤90% was the most repeatable initial intervention whereas SAVR with ARE did not improve the feasibility of future TAVR.

Introduction

In patients undergoing treatment for aortic stenosis, the ability to perform future transcatheter intervention after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) and surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) is an important consideration for lifetime management. Feasibility of TAVR in prior transcatheter or surgical valves is influenced by the height of the neo-skirt formed by leaflets of the initial valve and its relationship to the coronary ostia and the sinotubular junction (STJ).1,2 For TAVR, depth of valve implantation has a major impact on repeatability of TAVR with short frame valves such as SAPIEN 33 having an advantage over tall frame valves due to a shorter neo-skirt height.1,4 For SAVR, there are limited data on the feasibility of TAVR after SAVR using computed tomography (CT) modeling, with 1 study evaluating patients after aortic root enlargement (ARE), suggesting that the increase in coronary sinus dimensions achieved with ARE is counterbalanced by a reduction in coronary heights from the surgical annulus.5 The comparative performance of initial TAVR and SAVR for feasibility of future TAVR has not been studied previously. We, therefore, performed an analysis of the feasibility of TAVR using post-treatment CT analysis in 4 patient groups: (1) TAVR with SAPIEN 3 and implant depth ≤90% aortic, (2) TAVR with SAPIEN 3 and implant depth >90% aortic, (3) SAVR, and (4) SAVR with ARE.

Methodology

Study Design and Population Cohort

The study was a retrospective multicentre observational study of patients who underwent TAVR with a SAPIEN 3 valve at four high volume tertiary centers in Canada (Hamilton Health Sciences, Vancouver General Hospital, St. Michaels Hospital/Unity Health, and University of Ottawa Heart Institute) or SAVR at Hamilton Health Sciences. Participants included in the study were those who had undergone successful valve replacement and had available post-treatment CT scans of sufficient image quality to evaluate valve placement relative to anatomical landmarks. The study protocol received approval from the local research ethics boards.

CT Analysis Methodology

CT measurements were performed using a dedicated workstation (3mensio Structural Heart; Pie Medical Imaging BV, Maastricht, Netherlands). Preprocedural CT measurements included annular area, area derived diameter, and left ventricular outflow tract area measured 2 mm below the annular plane. Coronary heights were measured from the annular plane to the inferior border of each coronary ostium, using a straight-line measurement on a multiplanar reconstruction. The sinus of Valsalva (SOV) diameter was measured as a straight line from commissure to the opposite cusp and the mean value was used. STJ height was measured from the annular plane to the STJ using a straight-line measurement on a multiplanar reconstruction.

On post-treatment CT images, coronary heights and SOV widths were measured. For TAVR treated patients, coronary heights were measured in relation of the native annulus and did not change compared to preimplant CT measures. For surgical patients, coronary heights were measured relative to the annulus of the SAVR valve. The neo-skirt plane was defined at the top of the commissural posts and adjusted for each valve type. The virtual TAVR valve used for modeling of feasibility was the same as the index SAPIEN valve size for TAVR and a SAPIEN valve size equivalent to the internal diameter of the SAVR valve or the next largest available. The proportion of cases in which the neo-skirt was below both coronary arteries was determined. For cases in which the neo-skirt plane was at or above at least 1 coronary artery, measurements of the neo-skirt to coronary artery distance (VTC) were performed. For cases in which the neo-skirt was above the coronary arteries, the neo-skirt to STJ or aortic wall (VTSTJ/VTA) distance was measured. Valve-in-Valve Repeatability Criteria

The repeatability criteria were adapted from previous studies of valve in valve feasibility.3,4,6 A future procedure using a SAPIEN 3 valve was considered feasible under the following conditions (1) the neo-skirt plane was below both coronary ostia or (2) the neo-skirt plane was at or above any of the coronary ostia and the distance from the neo-skirt plane to the coronary ostia (VTC) was ≥4 mm and the distance from VTSTJ or VTA was ≥2 mm. All other cases were classified as having challenging future TAVR implantation.

Data Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means and SDs and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. For continuous variables, comparisons were made across the four groups using a one-way analysis of variance to assess differences in group means. When a significant difference was observed, t-tests were used for pairwise comparisons. For categorical variables, a chi-squared test was used to compare proportions across groups. When a significant difference was observed, two-proportion z-tests were used for pairwise comparisons. A p value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated pretreatment and post-treatment CT to assess the feasibility of future TAVR in patients undergoing initial TAVR and SAVR. We observed that (1) coronary heights are reduced in SAVR and SAVR + ARE patients, (2) the frequency of the neo-skirt being below the coronary ostia was the highest in TAVR with implantation ≤90% aortic, and (3) overall feasibility of future TAVR was also the highest in TAVR with implantation ≤90% aortic.

This study demonstrated substantial changes in the aortic root anatomy after SAVR and SAVR with ARE. In both surgical approaches, the height of the coronary arteries above the surgical annulus was significantly lower than the height of the coronary arteries above the native annular plane before surgery. This can be attributed to the placement of the surgical valve above the native annular plane, a phenomenon that may be exaggerated in root enlargement surgery where a larger prosthesis relative to the annulus is used. In this study, the mean diameter of valve prosthesis was larger than the mean area derived annular diameter in the SAVR + ARE but not in the SAVR group. These results are consistent with the typical valve upsizing seen with most root enlargement techniques.10,11 SAVR with ARE resulted in significant increases in sinus dimensions for all three coronary sinuses. The favorable impact of large sinuses on repeatability was, however, modified in some cases by tilting of the valve toward the left and noncoronary sinus to accommodate a larger size. Overall, the findings of this analyses suggest that ARE does not lead to higher feasibility of future valve in valve TAVR compared to SAVR alone.

In contrast to surgery, aortic root anatomy does not change after TAVR. A major determinant of feasibility of redo-TAVR is the depth of implantation. Although a higher implant depth can help to reduce conduction disturbances and the need for pacemakers,12 it also results in a greater chance of the neo-skirt being at or above the level of the coronary ostia. Implanting a short frame valve such as SAPIEN 3 minimizes neo-skirt height. However, the findings of this study highlight the importance of individualizing the implant depth decisions even with SAPIEN 3 valves. In younger patients with low coronaries and/or narrow sinus dimensions, the feasibility of repeat TAVR may be greatly enhanced by a lower implantation. In this study, the proportion of valves with neo-skirt below both coronary arteries was nearly 50% and these cases were repeatable regardless of other anatomic constraints.

Current guidelines recommend consideration of TAVR and SAVR for patients aged 65 to 80 years.13 Younger patients treated with either treatment approach may experience bioprosthetic valve failure in the future requiring repeat intervention when they are substantially older.14 Therefore, the heart team needs to optimize the initial intervention for future treatment options. This planning is best facilitated if all patients being considered for a bioprosthetic AVR undergo an aortic valve CT study and have a heart team review to develop a plan for initial and subsequent valve interventions if needed.15

Limitations

The cutoffs used to adjudicate feasibility of future TAVR for the valve to coronary distance and valve to STJ/aorta distance were derived from TAVR in SAVR registries. Although they have not been prospectively validated, they have been extensively used in the literature to assess suitability for valve-in-valve intervention.3,4,6 These analyses do not account for commissural alignment and the ability to perform leaflet modification to facilitate future valve-in-valve procedures. Prior studies have shown that commissural alignment sufficient to make leaflet modification techniques viable is obtained in approximately 20% of SAPIEN implants.4 They also do not account for he potential protective effect of concomitant bypass grafting or coronary protection with chimney stenting. This analysis was limited to short frame TAVR valves and are not necessarily applicable to other TAVR valve designs.

Conclusion

In this study, based on post-treatment CT evaluation of patients treated with TAVR and SAVR, TAVR with SAPIEN 3 at implant depth ≤90% had the highest likelihood of feasible reintervention. ARE which was performed in smaller anatomy did not lead to higher feasibility of future valve in valve TAVR compared to TAVR or SAVR alone.

Choice of implant depth for TAVR and surgical technique for SAVR should be considered by the heart team to optimize feasibility of future TAVR.
