Long-Term Management of Polypoidal Choroidal Vasculopathy: A Narrative Review
Authors: Adrian Koh, Giovanni Staurenghi, Jay Chhablani, Georgios D Panos, Colin S Tan, Taffeta Ching Ning Yamaguchi, Theo Empeslidis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV) is a subtype of neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), characterised by polypoidal lesions. Prevalence data indicate that PCV is more common in people of Asian ethnicity, although it also occurs in non-Asian individuals. However, because of a lack of globally agreed diagnostic criteria and routine screening programmes, PCV is likely to be under-diagnosed in some populations. Recent consensus suggests that PCV is a form of type 1 macular neovascularisation and should be classified as a subtype of nAMD. Anti–vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) monotherapy has demonstrated short-term efficacy in terms of visual acuity in PCV, and outcomes may be further improved by combining anti-VEGF therapy with photodynamic therapy. However, data on the long-term (≥ 5 years) efficacy of these treatments are limited and often conflicting. Although some people with PCV can achieve long-term remission following initial treatment (21.7–51.5%), eventual lesion reactivation is reported for approximately four-fifths of patients (81.3%). The socioeconomic impact of long-term PCV management could be reduced by identifying and characterising the subpopulations least likely to require chronic treatment. Large, global, randomised, placebo-controlled studies with long-term follow-up periods are needed in addition to real-world evidence to allow clinicians to reliably stratify people with PCV and support the stopping of anti-VEGF therapy in people at low risk of disease reactivation.Keywords: Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor, Long-term, Neovascular age-related macular degeneration, Photodynamic therapy, Polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy, Narrative review

Key Summary Points

Polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV) is a subtype of neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) that is more common in Asian populations.PCV is often underdiagnosed due to a lack of globally agreed diagnostic criteria and routine screening.Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy has demonstrated short-term efficacy in PCV, but long-term data are inconclusive.Large, global, randomised, placebo-controlled studies with long-term follow-up are needed to accurately stratify populations and support better identification of treatment needs.

Introduction

Polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV) is currently classified as a subtype of neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD); however, this classification is not universally supported [1–7]. Some cases of PCV show distinct vascular abnormalities of the choroidal vessels that differ from those observed in nAMD [8]. In addition, disease progression and treatment response can vary between people with nAMD and those with PCV [8]. In the clinic, in cases where PCV is located in the pre- or mid-periphery without evidence of significant exudation in the form of subretinal fluid or impairments in best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), treatment is at the physician’s discretion; patients are often observed for signs of disease progression before any treatment decisions are made.

Prevalence of PCV is reportedly higher in Asian populations (22.3–61.6% of patients with nAMD) than in White (4.0–16.8% of patients with nAMD) and Black (33.3% of patients with nAMD) populations; Table 1 [9–18]. However, the lack of globally agreed diagnostic criteria for PCV and the higher rate of PCV among ageing populations confound meaningful comparison of prevalence across regions [9, 13, 14, 19, 20]. Furthermore, diagnostic tests for PCV (e.g., indocyanine green angiography [ICGA], optical coherence tomography [OCT], fundus fluorescein angiography, colour fundus photography and OCT angiography [OCTA] [19, 21]) are not uniformly indicated or routinely performed across the world, which may contribute to the under-diagnosis of PCV in people of non-Asian ethnicities [14, 22]. When non-invasive tests with enhanced sensitivity and specificity in diagnosing PCV relative to ICGA, such as swept-source OCTA [21], become more widely available, the geographical differences in reported PCV prevalence rates are expected to become less pronounced. Consequently, the number of people with PCV in Europe is predicted to increase by 32% between 2022 and 2040 [9]. There is also variation in the phenotypic presentation of PCV across populations; for example, disease is driven by a pachychoroid-related mechanism in Asian patients but is more often associated with subretinal haemorrhage in Black patients and with soft drusen in White patients [13, 14]. Physicians should be aware of these different signs of PCV to consistently diagnose different patient populations.

The overall socioeconomic burden of PCV will increase as diagnoses increase, making the need for effective, evidence-based treatments paramount. Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) monotherapy has demonstrated efficacy in terms of visual acuity in PCV [12, 23]. However, anti-VEGF therapy carries a high treatment burden and is associated with variable long-term outcomes, substantial non-response rates, and high recurrence rates when treatment is stopped [12]. As such, there remains a large and growing unmet need for novel treatments that can reduce treatment burden, increase the rate of polyp regression, and minimise the risk of disease recurrence during the long-term management of PCV [12, 24]. This article reviews the current literature on the long-term management of PCV, to identify the critical unmet needs that remain to be addressed by new treatments and treatment strategies. For the purposes of this review, long-term studies have been defined as those with at least 5 years of follow-up, in line with previous research on long-term PCV outcomes [25, 26]. PubMed literature searches were performed on 26 May 2025 with no time constraints using the search strategies ‘(polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy) AND (long term)’ and ‘polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy’ (filtered by ‘Clinical Trial’ and hand searched for studies with follow-up ≥ 5 years and effects of treatment on visual acuity and/or disease regression/recurrence as key outcomes); additional studies were identified through review of the bibliographies of papers identified in the searches. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Defining PCV

Although the disease process now known as PCV was first described over 4 decades ago [2], our understanding of its pathology remains limited. Results of early studies in PCV were difficult to interpret, as they were based on ICGA imaging, which lacks the ability to resolve depth [27]. PCV was initially described as a bilateral, peripapillary disorder predominantly affecting middle-aged Black women [28]. However, recent advances in imaging allow for the features of PCV (subretinal, vascular lesions with terminal, polypoidal dilatations and a branching vascular or neovascular network; Figs. 1 and 2) to be characterised in more detail [27]. These features can translate to a variety of clinical manifestations, including asymptomatic, quiescent or pulsatile, subretinal, orange nodules; serous fluid (with similar appearance to central serous chorioretinopathy); subretinal haemorrhage; focal choroidal excavation (also seen in people with AMD and macular neovascularisation [MNV]); and retinal pigment epithelial detachments (PEDs) [4, 29, 30]. It is important to distinguish PCV from nAMD because treatment response to anti-VEGF therapy may be lower in individuals with PCV, including poor control of subretinal/intraretinal fluid; combination therapy with photodynamic therapy (PDT) may be necessary to optimise outcomes [12]. Further, the potential for polyps to bleed adds to an increased risk of haemorrhage, macular degeneration, and vision loss in patients with PCV; submacular haemorrhage is often present at diagnosis [31]. Polyp clusters have particularly high risk for severe vision loss [31]. Multimodal imaging remains essential in the diagnosis of PCV, particularly for distinguishing it from other forms of MNV such as typical nAMD. The cases illustrated in Figs. 1 and 2 represent very typical examples of PCV as encountered in routine clinical practice, showing hallmark features including sharp-peaked PEDs and subretinal orange nodules on colour fundus photography, double-layer signs on OCT, and the presence of polypoidal lesions and branching vascular networks on ICGA. These patterns are widely recognised by retina specialists and serve as practical diagnostic anchors. However, PCV can occasionally masquerade as typical nAMD, particularly when polyps are small or obscured. In such cases, ICGA remains the gold standard for identifying polypoidal lesions [20], while OCTA may assist in delineating the vascular network component [32]. Swept-source OCTA is the newest iteration of OCTA technology with particular promise for monitoring PCV and guiding treatment [33]. A high index of suspicion and the use of multimodal imaging are therefore critical in patients with treatment resistance, atypical fluid patterns, or disproportionately severe haemorrhage relative to lesion size. This reinforces the importance of maintaining ICGA capability, especially in regions with a high prevalence of PCV.

The overlap in the clinical features of PCV and other retinal diseases such as type 1 choroidal neovascularisation (CNV)—alongside the lack of consistent nomenclature and classification of MNV types beginning in clinical trials, the requirement for specific tests, and the lack of uniformly accepted diagnostic criteria—complicates the diagnosis of PCV. As such, PCV is sometimes overlooked in favour of a broader, better-recognised retinal disease such as AMD, MNV or central serous chorioretinopathy [34–36]. If standardised diagnostic protocols such as those described in the EVEREST and EVEREST II studies [19, 37, 38] were universally adopted, greater consistency in the clinical identification of PCV and standardisation of clinical trial inclusion criteria could follow; this could facilitate more uniform and robust assessments and comparisons of long-term treatment efficacy. As noted by the EVEREST investigators, ‘earlier studies used a variety of clinical and ICGA features to diagnose PCV and differentiate it from neovascular AMD, these features were highly variable between studies and the characteristic appearance of a polyp was often not well-defined’ [19]. However, these imaging protocols require ICGA, which may not be readily available or uniformly adopted across countries. Additionally, we note that HCP experience in diagnosis and interpretation of signs may affect the findings [19, 37, 38]. The most recent guidance from the Asia–Pacific Vitreo-retina Society (APVRS) notes that less-invasive imaging using modalities such as OCT and OCTA are also useful (100% consensus). The 2025 APVRS guidelines note that OCT features can be considered as major diagnostic criteria for a PCV diagnosis (e.g., sub-retinal pigment epithelium [RPE] ring-like lesions, en face OCT complex RPE elevation, and sharp-peaked) [4]. Use of SD OCT alone has been shown to correctly identify approximately 95% of PCV lesions; therefore, this may be used for successful PCV diagnosis where ICGA is not available [39].

Past and Present Therapeutic Landscape

Currently, decisions to initiate treatment for PCV are primarily based on BCVA and the location and activity of the PCV lesion [40]. Prior to the availability of anti-VEGF therapies, PDT was the main treatment option for active PCV [23]. Although PDT monotherapy could preserve the BCVA of people with PCV for up to 5 years, the introduction of anti-VEGF therapies enabled prolonged maintenance of visual outcomes [41–43]. In one retrospective South Korean study of 261 patients with PCV spanning the years 2005–2018, data from the earlier part of this time period showed that initial BCVA was sustained for about 1 year on treatment; however, after 2007 and the introduction of anti-VEGF agents, initial BCVA was sustained notably longer, for approximately 4 years [41]. Several randomised controlled trials have reported on the promising short-term safety and efficacy of anti-VEGF monotherapy in people with PCV (Table 2) [37, 38, 44–52].

Even better outcomes have been reported following PDT in combination with anti-VEGF therapy (Table 2) [25, 37]. In the EVEREST study, significantly more participants who received PDT plus ranibizumab achieved complete polyp regression at 6 months versus those who received ranibizumab alone (p < 0.01) [37]. Similarly, in the larger EVEREST II study, 24-month adjusted mean BCVA gains and the complete polyp regression rate were significantly greater in participants who received PDT plus ranibizumab compared with those who received ranibizumab monotherapy (p = 0.005 and p < 0.001, respectively) [38]. It is worth noting that the long-term outcomes of clinical trials may be affected by factors such as different PCV diagnostic criteria, patient population, use of different anti-VEGF drugs and regimens and PCV treatment frequency.

The timing of combination treatment might also impact outcomes: In the Fujisan study, fewer additional treatments with ranibizumab were required when PDT was administered initially compared with when PDT was delayed [49]. However, in the PLANET study, there was no difference in BCVA between the group who received rescue PDT compared with the group who received sham rescue PDT [53]. Based on the available data, a panel of retinal specialists from China with expertise in PCV have recommended anti-VEGF monotherapy or combination anti-VEGF therapy and PDT over PDT monotherapy for PCV, and noted that PDT may be more useful if initiated later as rescue therapy [54]. Conversely, the APVRS guidelines and the European ATLANTIC study have concluded that, to date, there has been no strong evidence for the use of PDT as part of a treat-and-extend anti-VEGF regimen [45, 55]. The conflicting global guidelines suggest that more evidence is required to optimise the utilisation of anti-VEGF therapy plus PDT for PCV.

Long-Term Efficacy of Current PCV Treatment Options

Although anti-VEGF therapies can reliably improve visual acuity during the first and second years of treatment [56], the initial increase in BCVA has been reported to regress towards baseline values upon long-term administration [36, 41, 57]. Despite this, data on the long-term, real-world outcomes of current treatments are limited and often conflicting (Table 3) [25, 26, 41–43, 56–69]. Although visual outcomes of people with PCV treated with anti-VEGF injections, with or without PDT, were reported in one study to deteriorate over 10 years despite treatment [41], no significant change or improvement in BCVA was seen over 5–7 years in people treated with PDT monotherapy [58, 66], anti-VEGF monotherapy [57, 62] or combination therapy [58, 65] in other studies.

Some people with PCV can achieve long-term (≥ 5 years) remission following initial treatment (21.7–51.5%) [26, 58]; however, many people experience incomplete polyp regression [12, 38], which can result in lesion reactivation [63, 68]. The mean time to PCV recurrence following anti-VEGF and PDT combination therapy has been reported as 28.6 ± 23.1 months (95% confidence interval: 21.5–35.7; median 18.0) [65], with the number of re-treatments required following anti-VEGF or PDT monotherapy peaking in year 1 and declining thereafter [63, 68]. Year 6 follow-up data from the EVEREST II study revealed that approximately 75% of patients required additional treatment(s) after the 2-year randomised treatment period ended, regardless of initial treatment arm assignment (ranibizumab plus PDT or ranibizumab monotherapy); however, participants in the combination study arm received significantly fewer anti-VEGF injections during follow-up than did those who initially received ranibizumab monotherapy (mean, 6.7 versus 8.6; p < 0.01) [59]. Importantly, both anti-VEGF monotherapy and combination therapy are associated with substantial costs; monotherapy requires a higher number of injections in the long-term, and combination therapy incurs a substantial cost up front due to PDT [70].

Notably, lesion reactivation is more frequent among people with PCV than people with typical nAMD, resulting in an increased treatment burden in PCV versus nAMD [63, 68]. One consistent feature of long-term quiescence of PCV is the ability to achieve complete closure of polypoidal lesions [59, 71], and therefore there is the potential to achieve better long-term vision improvements than in patients with nAMD. In clinical practice, we find that these data support a chronic disease approach, whereby HCPs set expectations for long-term monitoring and re-treatment, favouring treat-and-extend regimens where appropriate.

Long-term treatment outcomes may be affected by factors such as lesion location, intraretinal changes, fibrosis, RPE atrophy, and haemorrhage [12, 72–74]. Additionally, a suboptimal treatment response to long-term anti-VEGF monotherapy has been associated with increased subfoveal choroidal thickness [75, 76], but this is not uniformly reported [62]. It is possible that other, yet unidentified, factors may influence long-term treatment outcomes in PCV; therefore, further research is required to characterise these.

The long-term treatment burden of PCV may be reduced by reliable identification phenotype using ICGA to identify people most likely to experience complete polyp regression. One of the factors that influence the rate of complete polyp regression is the treatment received. In a subset of randomly selected patients from the EVEREST II study, complete lesion regression was more frequent and disease recurrence was lower in the group who received combination therapy (PDT and ranibizumab) compared with the group who received ranibizumab monotherapy [77]. Further, despite the upfront cost and time investment required for combination therapy, an economic evaluation of data from the EVEREST II study found that it remained cost-saving or cost-effective in all lifetime scenarios modelled [78]. Combination therapy generated more quality-adjusted life-years (7.87) than ranibizumab monotherapy (7.85) at a comparable cost (savings of US $32), over a lifetime horizon [78]. These findings were supported by a real-world analysis of clinical data, which confirmed that combination therapy (PDT + anti-VEGF injections) remained highly cost-effective, with gross domestic product per capita as the willingness-to-pay threshold [70]. However, combination therapy was not cost-effective over a shorter time horizon (2 years), indicating that this treatment may not be suitable for very elderly populations [70]. Additionally, lack of access to PDT in low-resource settings may necessitate treatment with anti-VEGF monotherapy, which requires more injections and therefore results in increased costs over time [40, 70].

However, anti-VEGF monotherapy remains effective in some populations, as indicated by a subanalysis of data from the LUMINOUS study which found that ranibizumab monotherapy may be more effective in South Korean participants compared with the global population [79]. Genetic factors may help to identify the best treatment approach for different populations. For example, in a genome-wide association study, rs429608 (an allele of C2-CFB-SKIV2L) was associated with visual improvement in people with AMD and PCV after 1 year of treatment (p = 0.003) [80]. It should be noted that although genetic factors that contribute to PCV treatment outcomes may help to identify individuals who could benefit from stopping therapy earlier, further investigation in randomised controlled trials is needed before any definitive conclusions can be made.

To date, the longest randomised, placebo-controlled trials conducted in people with PCV had follow-up periods of up to 2 years [38, 44, 46, 51, 52]. This research gap is particularly large outside of Asia, where PCV is less widely recognised. Randomised controlled trials with longer follow-up periods will help to further improve our understanding of the long-term efficacy of current therapeutic approaches for PCV and will help to inform future management strategies. For example, if such trials showed that current therapies do not demonstrate long-term efficacy, this should incite a pivot in treatment approaches with a renewed exploration of combination therapies and sequencing. Further, there may be investigation of higher-durability, dual-pathway or mechanism-diverse agents, innovations in delivery and adjunctive modalities. To support these investigations, we would advocate for pragmatic, multicentre, randomised controlled trials and platform trials using OCT/OCTA biomarkers and endpoints relevant to PCV.

We also recommend the collection of more real-world data via robust, multicentre studies and analyses of registries with standardised imaging and endpoints. This real-world evidence will test the external validity of endpoints, inform further cost-effectiveness analyses, capture ethnic or geographical variations in treatment outcomes and identify phenotypes that may indicate that subgroups of patients will benefit from alternative sequencing or combination therapy. However, in order to expand the evidence base, PCV requires greater global recognition.

Greater global recognition of PCV could be achieved primarily through continued education of HCPs, including those practising in areas with a low prevalence of PCV. Education of HCPs may be supported by the establishment of a multinational PCV registry linking tertiary centres with a shared data dictionary and local data control; this would capture a core dataset including elements such as demographics, visual acuity, OCT metrics, treatment regimens, adverse events and patient-reported outcomes. Additional data could include ICGA/OCTA metrics and genotype. Relevant prespecified treatment outcomes may also be captured, e.g., haemorrhage-free survival, sustained polyp/branching vascular network (BVN) inactivation, visual acuity area under the curve and safety. This registry would benefit from the standardisation of imaging protocols, structured reports and periodic central reads to improve consistency across datasets. The capture of diverse data may also support equity analyses, and the quality of the dataset could be maintained via regular audits and automated checks.

PCV Remission and Reactivation

As mentioned above, people with PCV can achieve remission following initial treatment. Although only approximately 19% of people achieve long-term remission [64], lesion reactivation is infrequent among those who do [26]. Lesion reactivation has been observed in approximately four-fifths (81.3%) of people with nAMD or PCV treated with anti-VEGF monotherapy (N = 192), most frequently reported during the first year of follow-up (p < 0.001) and in people with PCV (p = 0.021) [63]. In line with these findings, over 50% of people with PCV have been reported to experience lesion recurrence within 18 months of disease remission [24]. In treatment-naïve participants, PCV recurrence has been associated with older age of PCV onset (p = 0.001), lower choroidal vascularity index (p = 0.019) and the absence of choroidal vascular hyperpermeability (p = 0.037) [64]. The need for re-treatment has also been associated with the presence of the T allele of the ARMS2 A69S gene (hazard ratio 2.18; 95% confidence interval: 1.47–3.24) [80]. In a previous study, medical records showed that a significantly smaller proportion of participants who entered long-term remission had the T allele of the ARMS2 A69S gene (p = 0.029) than those who experienced lesion reactivation [26].

The frequency of the T allele of the ARMS2 A69S gene has also been reported to be lower in eyes with pachydrusen [81], which is associated with PCV (p < 0.001) but not with AMD or central serous chorioretinopathy [82, 83]. As such, the presence of the T allele of the ARMS2 A69S gene might account for the increased rate of lesion reactivation observed in people with PCV [63]. Upon investigating the strength of this association further in large-scale prospective genome-wide association studies, the variants of ARMS2 and CFH genes may become imperative in helping physicians predict disease recurrence [80].

PCV Progression

In eyes with particular fundus clinical features (reddish-orange nodule alone or nodules with a small subretinal haemorrhage) or the absence of hard exudates and fibrin, PCV is likely to take a benign or stable course [84]. Similarly, the presence of polypoidal lesions and cystoid macular oedema is associated with maintenance of baseline BCVA over 5 years in people with PCV [36]. Evaluating the individual risk of disease progression will allow for appropriate allocation of resources for the long-term management of PCV. For example, people with low risk of progression may sustain positive treatment outcomes despite the introduction of longer visit-free or treatment-free intervals [84] or from stopping therapy earlier, whereas those at high risk of progression may benefit from additional treatment and monitoring.

Many of the clinical trials conducted to date did not isolate individuals with different types of PCV or those with PCV from those with typical AMD, which may have introduced a potential bias in the reported results. The differences in patient populations and treatment regimens between studies make identification of risk factors for PCV lesion progression challenging. For example, one potential risk factor is the occurrence of PEDs. The absence of PEDs at baseline has been associated with better vision outcomes [85], whereas the presence of PEDs has been associated with RPE tears and fellow eye progression [86–88]. However, although PEDs are more frequently observed in people with PCV than in those with nAMD [30], PCV itself is associated with lower rates of fellow eye exudation than nAMD [87]. The interaction between PED, PCV and disease progression is therefore unclear, and further research is needed to allow clinicians to stratify people with PCV according to PCV progression risk.

New and Emerging Strategies for the Long-Term Management of PCV

To help offset the high acquisition costs of anti-VEGF therapies, efforts to develop biosimilars are ongoing [89–91]. Positive data for one of the ranibizumab biosimilars have been reported in PCV [91] and are awaited for aflibercept biosimilars in related indications [92–94]. Additionally, treat-and-extend regimens are now recommended for PCV, whereby treatment is initiated with ≥ 3 consecutive monthly injections until disease remission, after which treatment intervals are gradually extended in increments of 2–4 weeks for a maximum of 3 months [54, 55, 95]. Treat-and-extend regimens have been shown to optimise visual outcomes of anti-VEGF therapy and reduce the number of clinic visits [44, 95, 96]. Anti-VEGF monotherapy administered in a treat-and-extend regimen is generally well-tolerated by people with PCV [45], and achieves visual outcomes similar to pro re nata aflibercept but with fewer injections [57]. However, there is currently insufficient evidence in the literature to support stopping anti-VEGF therapy in people at low risk of disease reactivation [55]. Therefore, further studies are needed to fully characterise the long-term effectiveness of treat-and-extend regimens of all the available anti-VEGF therapies in PCV [55].

Other approaches to reduce treatment burden include novel formulations that require less frequent administration, or the use of a refillable ocular implant that allows for continuous passive diffusion of the drug into the vitreous (i.e., a port delivery system). One port delivery system entered development for the anti-VEGF ranibizumab; however, to date, it has not been studied in people with PCV [12], and manufacturing concerns raised in 2022 led to its withdrawal from the market [97]. This port delivery system has now re-entered the US market for nAMD following US Food and Drug Administration approval of updates in July 2024 [97]. Such novel delivery systems have the potential to reduce the burden of intravitreal injections; however, dedicated studies are required to better assess their effectiveness.

Other drugs with improved pharmacokinetic profiles relative to current anti-VEGF therapies are also in development [98, 99]. In the TENAYA and LUCERNE trials, the 2-year efficacy of faricimab 6 mg administered every 16 weeks and aflibercept 2 mg administered every 8 weeks was comparable in people with nAMD [98]. Further, in-depth analyses on the efficacy, durability and safety of faricimab in PCV are being conducted in the ongoing phase IIIb/IV SALWEEN study [100]. A multicentre, retrospective study of 1-year outcomes observed complete polypoidal regression in 52% of eyes with PCV [101]. Similarly, administration of brolucizumab with a treatment interval of 12 weeks was shown to be feasible: non-inferiority versus aflibercept every 8 weeks was observed, although the dosing frequency was increased to every 8 weeks in around half of the people with nAMD who received brolucizumab [99]. Intravitreal brolucizumab has demonstrated significant improvements in BCVA, central macular thickness and central choroidal thickness across multiple studies of people with PCV [102–106], with a high incidence of polypoidal regression (78.9–93.3%) [102, 103, 105]. A substantial letter gain and reduction in macular thickness with brolucizumab were also sustained for up to 96 weeks [104]. Finally, investigational efforts to achieve the ultimate therapeutic goal of long-term remission in PCV also include dual-targeted approaches. VEGF and angiopoietin 2 (Ang2) receptors are both involved in the maintenance of choroidal homeostasis [107, 108]. Therefore, dual anti-VEGF/Ang2 inhibition is expected to have a greater therapeutic effect on the choroid than antagonism of VEGF receptors alone [109]. In people with nAMD or PCV, the effects of dual anti-VEGF/Ang2 inhibition on the choroid are similar to those of anti-VEGF monotherapy [110–112]. Although the lack of clear differences in the effects of faricimab, aflibercept and brolucizumab on the choroid could be attributed to population differences between the studies, or the relatively short study duration, long-term specific comparative studies are needed to confirm the benefits of Ang2 inhibition on the choroid of people with PCV.

There are several other therapeutic approaches that are in the early stages of clinical investigation for treatment of PCV. The use of subthreshold micropulse laser therapy has been shown to alleviate visual loss in people with intermediate AMD [113], and at least one case report noted a dramatic response within 12 weeks; prospective studies using this technique would be of great interest in PCV due to its ability to stimulate the repair of the inner blood–retinal barrier without causing damage to photoreceptors [114]. Gene therapy is also an emerging area of therapeutic potential. More promising findings include the identification of several genetic polymorphisms that are risk factors for certain PCV phenotypes [115] or associated with treatment response [116]. One additional field of research in neovascular ocular conditions involves tyrosine kinase inhibitors; studies to date have focused more on nAMD and diabetic macular oedema rather than on PCV but, if successful, would likely expand to PCV as well [117].

Advances in artificial intelligence may help to identify more novel candidate therapeutic targets (such as plasma kallikrein) [118] for the development of efficacious therapies in PCV. Further, artificial intelligence may improve image analysis, help monitor treatment responses, inform personalised treatment plans and allow more accurate detection of PCV and reliable differentiation from nAMD [119, 120].

This array of potential new treatment options and scope of research activity in the field of PCV is encouraging, and short-term data will provide initial indicators of clinical usefulness. However, findings from a clinical trial situation are not always immediately translatable to real-world clinical practice given differences in patient populations and accessibility barriers to treatment related to cost or other factors. Further, given the length of time required for full clinical investigation and the need for PCV-specific evidence, most of these novel approaches are unlikely to enter routine clinical practice in the near future. Therefore, the immediate priority for researchers is to rigorously establish the effectiveness and durability of currently available anti-VEGF therapies in real-world settings.

Discussion

Despite ongoing advancements in awareness of PCV and its acute management, there remain significant gaps with regard to universally standardised criteria for diagnosis, long-term monitoring and the understanding of long-term treatment strategies and implications. The long-term data reviewed herein reveal evidence of the potential for significant improvements in visual acuity over the initial years of treatment but with gains generally dissipating over time. Much remains to be learned about optimal treatment strategies for durable long-term efficacy using strategies that are cost-effective and widely accessible. One yet unanswered question pertains to which anti-VEGF is the most effective for first-line treatment. PCV is a complex disease, and there remains a knowledge gap regarding differences between patients’ initial response rates and response durability.

Looking ahead, gene therapy could be particularly valuable in PCV where lesion reactivation, persistent BVN activity and haemorrhagic events drive long-term morbidity [12, 121]. A durable, single-dose approach that suppresses pathogenic signalling (e.g., VEGF/Ang–Tie pathways) or remodels the choroidal milieu could reduce recurrence, mitigate haemorrhage risk and lessen injection burden. However, the introduction of gene therapy into the PCV treatment landscape would require PCV-specific trials, investigation of endpoints beyond VA (e.g., haemorrhage-free survival, sustained polyp/BVN inactivation) and careful safety evaluation. Further, as understanding of PCV improves, treatments may be developed to address new therapeutic targets based on pathophysiological mechanisms that are not yet well understood. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors may also be useful, pending further investigation in PCV specifically. Thus, trial results for all the described treatment modalities are highly anticipated.

Although recent efforts, particularly from the APVRS, have helped clarify diagnostic protocols, global adoption remains incomplete, making it difficult to achieve consistent patient stratification and treatment outcomes [6]. PCV is widely recognised in Asian populations, where the use of ICGA is commonplace; however, the cost of ICGA may limit its availability in some regions [9–11, 122]. Under-diagnosis of PCV in regions that do not perform routine ICGA testing suggests that PCV may be more widespread than previously thought [14, 22]. As our appreciation of the global burden of this condition rises, so does the urgency for greater recognition and more precise diagnostic tools. Going forward, ensuring global implementation of a multimodal diagnostic approach composed of OCT-B imaging, followed by referral to more specialised hubs with ICGA capabilities could reduce variability in PCV diagnosis and improve treatment outcomes across different patient populations.

Recent advancements in imaging technologies may play a pivotal role in improving the diagnosis and long-term monitoring of PCV. Although ICGA remains the gold standard for visualising polypoidal lesions, OCTA has emerged as a promising non-invasive tool, particularly for detecting the branching vascular network. OCTA can provide detailed, depth-resolved visualisation of flow signals within the choroid, allowing for longitudinal assessment of disease activity without the need for dye-based angiography [123]. However, its limited sensitivity for detecting polyps compared with ICGA remains a challenge. Continued improvements in image resolution, segmentation algorithms and machine learning–based interpretation may enhance the utility of OCTA for PCV diagnosis and re-treatment planning. For example, variable interscan time analysis (VISTA) is a recently introduced OCTA-based algorithm that allows visualisation of relative blood flow speeds within polypoidal lesions—potentially useful information for both PCV diagnosis and assessment of therapeutic response [124].

There are several other important practical barriers to diagnostic improvements. Units may rely on older SD-OCT equipment without enhanced imaging and therefore increase disparities in access to OCT imaging. Further constraints include cost and the requirement for adequate staffing and monitoring, with a level of expertise that will allow consistent and accurate diagnostic inferences. Despite this, incorporation of such imaging advancements into clinical trials and real-world care could reduce the reliance on ICGA, increase diagnostic accessibility and enable more individualised treatment strategies. Thus, standardisation of diagnostic imaging techniques should facilitate more accurate analyses of the burden of PCV across countries. Further, there remains variation in the features used to diagnose PCV; for example, the EVEREST II investigators noted that in earlier studies of PCV, polyps have been described as ‘polypoidal choroidal vascular dilatations,’ ‘focal vascular dilations,’ and ‘polypoidal lesions’ [19]. There is substantial room for interpretation of these terms; therefore, standardised imaging protocols are also important to understand the full burden of disease [19]. Creating a standardised definition of PCV was beyond the remit of this publication, and inferences drawn in the current review are therefore limited by the variation in diagnostic processes and the lack of a standardised definition of PCV across captured studies.

Much of the current clinical trial evidence in PCV has been derived from retrospective studies with heterogeneous protocols and predominantly from Asian populations, limiting the generalisability of the data. Additionally, almost all long-term evidence of PCV is derived from Asian populations because PCV is particularly prevalent in these individuals. However, there is increasing research into PCV in non-Asian populations, which suggests that presentation of disease may vary substantially [13, 14, 17, 35]. Therefore, it is important to note that the conclusions drawn from this review may not be generalisable to non-Asian populations where PCV exhibits different clinical characteristics [13, 14, 17, 35]. Additionally, it should be noted that the present review excluded any studies with fewer than 5 years’ follow-up potentially excluding relevant studies with only 3–4 years’ follow-up.

Several emerging therapies have demonstrated durability or non-inferiority in typical nAMD, but PCV remains underrepresented in, or excluded from, these programmes [97–99]. Given the unique disease profile of PCV, extrapolation of findings from these nAMD trials is not often appropriate. The ultimate treatment goal is the resolution of subretinal haemorrhages associated with PCV to reduce the need for chronic treatment. Addition of PDT to anti-VEGF therapy shows some promise in this regard [25, 37]; however, evidence is insufficient to recommend broad adoption, and therefore, guidelines regarding the use of this combination therapy in PCV are conflicting. There is an urgent need for prospective, longitudinal, multicentre studies with standardised outcome definitions to evaluate the benefits and durability of different treatment strategies beyond 5 years. Such studies should compare treatment groups for relevant functional outcomes such as BCVA, anatomical outcomes such as fluid control, fluid resolution rates, PED changes, polyp closure rates and changes to the branching neovascular network and rates of severe complications such as macular haemorrhage, RPE tears/rips, macular atrophy and fibrosis. These studies should also focus on refining patient stratification, optimising treatment regimens and determining the appropriate conditions under which the use of anti-VEGF therapy can be safely reduced or stopped in individuals at low risk of disease reactivation. Emerging therapies that may specifically target polypoidal lesions, including biosimilars and extended-release delivery systems, hold promise for reducing treatment burdens; however, these need further investigation specifically in PCV, and findings from this research will likely not be available in the near future.

Moreover, re-treatment criteria and follow-up intervals remain inconsistently applied in clinical practice, particularly in patients with persistent branching vascular networks but inactive polyps. The role of imaging biomarkers—such as OCTA-detected flow in branching vascular networks or persistent subretinal fluid—as predictors of recurrence remains to be clearly defined. Additionally, long-term treatment burden, cost-effectiveness and patient-reported outcomes are under-reported in the literature. Addressing these additional evidence gaps will be crucial for optimising care in a chronic disease where patients may require monitoring and treatment over decades.

The socioeconomic impact of currently available treatments for long-term PCV management is considerable due to the chronic nature of the disease and the need for frequent, long-term treatment with anti-VEGF therapy (with or without concomitant PDT) [12]. In routine care, the treatment burden for PCV is at least comparable with, and in many cases greater than, that of typical nAMD [12, 31]. This is due to the higher rate of reactivation and greater risk of submacular haemorrhage, in addition to the time and equipment required for diagnosis and monitoring. This translates into a substantial economic burden for healthcare systems and highlights the need for a focus on PCV-specific treatments [12, 31]. One way to reduce the treatment burden of long-term PCV management is the development of novel treatments with greater durability and lower treatment burdens. Identification and characterisation of patient subgroups that are less likely to require long-term treatment may also lead to significant improvements in patients’ quality of life and reductions in healthcare costs related to PCV.

Conclusions

Ultimately, global collaboration will be crucial to advancing the management of PCV. Future studies should include under-represented populations, particularly from Western and African regions, to assess potential ethnic and geographic differences in lesion behaviour and treatment response. By bridging gaps in PCV diagnosis, treatment and regulation, we can move towards a future where the long-term care of this sight-threatening disease is more effective, personalised and accessible.
