The Impact of Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria on Sleep—A Systematic Review
Authors: Kenan Kherallah, Claire S. Chung, Raveena Ghanshani, Thomas Issa, Sharon Ontiveros, Vivian Y. Shi, Katrina H. Lee, Jennifer L. Hsiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sleep disturbance is increasingly recognized as an important dimension of morbidity in chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU), yet existing evidence remains fragmented across clinical, psychosocial, and physiologic domains. We conducted a systematic review of studies identified through PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, CENTRAL, and ClinicalTrials.gov to evaluate the prevalence, associated factors, and biologic correlates of sleep dysfunction in CSU, including subjective and objective sleep measures, sleep-disordered breathing (SDB), and potential biomarkers. Across cohorts, sleep disturbance was consistently reported and was more common in patients with CSU than in controls. Patients with CSU generally demonstrated worse global sleep quality, shorter sleep duration, longer sleep latency, and greater insomnia severity. Poor sleep was commonly associated with higher disease activity (UAS7), lower disease control (UCT), and worse quality of life (QoL) outcomes. Sleep impairment frequently co-occurred with anxiety, depression, fatigue, headache, and additional systemic symptom burden. Emerging biomarker data suggested possible circadian-immune dysregulation, including altered melatonin and orexin signaling in patients with CSU. SDB, including increased obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) risk and higher apnea–hypopnea indices, was reported in select cohorts and was generally more frequent among patients with greater urticaria activity. Limitations include heterogeneity in study design and the predominance of cross-sectional data, with limited objective sleep testing and biomarker assessment across cohorts. Despite these considerations, the available evidence indicates that sleep dysfunction in CSU is common, multifactorial, and clinically meaningful, supporting routine sleep assessment to improve comprehensive disease management.

Introduction

Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is characterized by spontaneous pruritic wheals, angioedema, or both, persisting for longer than 6 weeks in the absence of an identifiable external trigger [1]. CSU affects approximately 1% of the global population and frequently follows a relapsing and unpredictable clinical course [2], with studies suggesting that more than 60% of patients may continue experiencing symptoms even after 3 years [3]. It predominantly affects women and typically presents between 20 and 40 years of age [4]. The pathogenesis of CSU is incompletely understood but is thought to be driven primarily by aberrant mast cell and basophil activation, resulting in the release of histamine and other proinflammatory mediators, including prostaglandins, cysteinyl leukotrienes, and vasoactive factors, which drive wheals, angioedema, and pruritus [5].

In clinical practice, the impact of CSU extends well beyond the skin, with substantial impacts on daily functioning and well-being due to the severe burden of itch as well as the chronic and unpredictable nature of the disease. Prior studies have demonstrated significant associations between CSU and impaired health-related quality of life (QoL), reduced work productivity, and increased healthcare utilization [6, 7]. Psychological comorbidity is also common, including anxiety and depression, reflecting the cumulative weight of persistent symptoms and disease unpredictability [8].

Sleep disturbance represents an important yet often underrecognized component of disease burden in inflammatory dermatoses. It encompasses difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, reduced sleep duration, and clinically defined sleep disorders such as insomnia, restless legs syndrome (RLS), and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). In children with atopic dermatitis (AD), sleep disturbance was reported in approximately two thirds of patients and was associated with increased depression, anxiety, fatigue, and impaired attention [9]. In adult AD cohorts, sleep loss was also linked to reduced work productivity [10]. Together, these findings indicate that sleep dysfunction can also inherently contribute to psychological morbidity, cognitive impairment, and economic burden. Given the shared features of chronic pruritus, inflammation, and psychosocial impact, sleep disturbance may represent a similarly meaningful dimension of morbidity in CSU.

Supporting this, in a comprehensive review of sleep outcomes across inflammatory dermatoses, Kaaz et al. reported that CSU was associated with increased risk of poorer sleep quality and noted that higher urticaria activity was associated with worse sleep outcomes, with improvement in sleep parameters observed alongside effective disease control [11]. More recently, Wasim et al. synthesized mechanistic and clinical evidence linking CSU with worsened sleep, emphasizing the roles of nocturnal pruritus, inflammatory mediators, and psychological factors in shaping sleep disruption [12].

Although these reviews establish sleep dysfunction as an important component of CSU burden, existing evidence remains dispersed across multiple study types and outcome domains. Prior reviews have characterized sleep impairment in CSU, but have either addressed CSU within a broader dermatosis-focused framework without incorporating emerging evidence on sleep-disordered breathing (SDB) and biomarker correlates of sleep disruption [11], or provided mechanistic narrative synthesis without systematic methods to comprehensively integrate the full evidence base [12]. Accordingly, this systematic review aims to summarize and critically evaluate literature on the impact of CSU on sleep, including patient-reported sleep outcomes, SDB, and biologic correlates associated with sleep disturbance.

Methods

Search Strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and ClinicalTrials.gov from database inception through the start date of the study on 28 November 2024. Searches were performed using database-specific controlled vocabulary and free-text terms related to CSU and sleep (Table 1). All records retrieved from the databases were imported into Covidence for deduplication and screening. Titles and abstracts were independently reviewed by three reviewers (RG, TI, SO), and studies were advanced to full-text review on the basis of prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria. Full-text articles were then assessed independently by three reviewers (KK, TI, SO) to determine final inclusion. Discrepancies at any stage were resolved through discussion, with adjudication by a senior reviewer (JLH) when consensus could not be reached. Reference lists of included studies were also manually screened to identify additional eligible articles.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they were human studies involving participants with a diagnosis of CSU and evaluated sleep dysfunction, including sleep disturbance, sleep quality, sleep duration, or SDB, in relation to CSU. Only articles published in English with full-text availability were considered. Studies were excluded if they were animal or in vitro investigations, focused on urticaria subtypes without inclusion of CSU, mentioned sleep only peripherally or did not evaluate sleep-related outcomes, primarily assessed treatment effects on sleep rather than sleep dysfunction inherent to CSU, attributed sleep disturbance predominantly to medication use or non-CSU comorbidities, or were non–peer-reviewed publications, including reviews, editorials, commentaries, abstracts, or conference proceedings. Studies using the broader term “chronic urticaria” (CU) were included if the study population was consistent with a CSU diagnosis or if patients with CSU comprised a substantial or majority proportion of the study population.

Data Extraction

Data were independently extracted by three reviewers (KK, TI, SO) using a standardized data extraction form. Extracted information included study design and setting, participant characteristics, sleep assessment instruments, biomarkers related to sleep, sleep-related outcomes, and associations with CSU disease activity measures, as well as any other notable features. Disagreements were resolved by a fourth reviewer (JLH).

Study Quality Assessment

Methodologic quality and risk of bias were assessed independently by two reviewers (KK, CSC) using the appropriate Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklists based on study design, with disagreements resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (JLH). This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. This systematic review was registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD42024613788). The review is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies involving human participants or animals performed by the authors.

Results

PRISMA

A total of 1014 records were identified through database searching, including Embase (n = 645), Web of Science (n = 192), PubMed (n = 141), ClinicalTrials.gov (n = 31), and CENTRAL (n = 5). After the removal of 284 duplicate records, 730 studies remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 546 records were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. One study could not be retrieved, leaving 183 full-text articles assessed for eligibility. Following full-text review, 135 studies were excluded for the following reasons: abstract-only publication (n = 64), primary evaluation of treatment effects on sleep dysfunction in CSU (n = 32), wrong outcomes (n = 15), wrong study design (n = 10), wrong patient population (n = 8), or review-type publications (n = 6). Study Quality Assessments

Risk of bias was assessed using the JBI critical appraisal checklists, selected on the basis of each study design. Overall study quality was categorized by the proportion of checklist items rated “Yes,” with good defined as ≥ 80%, fair as 60–79%, and poor as ≤ 59%. Using these thresholds, 34/48 studies were rated good quality, 11/48 fair, and 3/48 poor.

Sleep Measures Used Across Studies

Across the included studies, sleep was assessed using a combination of subjective questionnaires and objective physiologic measures, encompassing both validated and non-validated instruments. Validated subjective sleep measures most frequently included the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI; n = 16), which evaluates sleep quality across multiple domains over a 1-month time interval; Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS; n = 6), which measures daytime sleepiness; and the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI; n = 4), which quantifies insomnia severity. Additional validated subjective instruments used less frequently included the Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire (MEQ; n = 2), Medical Outcomes Study Sleep Scale (MOS; n = 2), Jenkins Sleep Scale (JSS; n = 1), Sleep Hygiene Index (SHI; n = 1), General Sleep Disturbance Scale (GSDS; n = 1), International Restless Legs Syndrome Rating Scale (IRLS; n = 1), and the STOP-Bang questionnaire for OSA risk screening (n = 1). Studies also utilized sleep-related items that are found within broader validated quality-of-life instruments, including the Chronic Urticaria Quality of Life Questionnaire (CU-Q2oL; n = 16), the Urticaria Patient Daily Diary (UPDD; n = 3), and Nottingham Health Profile (n = 1). One study used DSM-5-based sleep disorder assessments (n = 1). Detailed descriptions of the sleep assessment instruments, including the domains assessed and score interpretation, are provided in Table 2. In contrast, nonvalidated sleep assessments included self-reported sleep difficulties (n = 9), generic questionnaires (n = 1), and visual analog scales for sleep disturbance (n = 1). Summary of Included Studies

A high prevalence of sleep disturbance was reported across several studies as demonstrated in Tables 3 and 4. Abdel-Meguid et al. reported worse PSQI scores in CSU versus controls, including shorter sleep duration, prolonged sleep latency, and reduced sleep efficiency [13]. In large population-based surveys, Balp et al. and Vietri et al. found that 50% or more of patients with CSU endorsed sleep difficulties, with higher prevalence than matched controls (including psoriasis comparators) [40–42, 49]. Across validated instruments, multiple studies reported impaired sleep quality and higher PSQI or ISI scores in CSU, with worse sleep outcomes in patients with higher Weekly Urticaria Activity Scores (UAS7) and poorer disease control [13, 15, 16, 19, 23, 30, 32, 37]. Additionally, Weekly Sleep Interference Scores (SIS7) were found to increase across CSU severity categories, stratified into categories using UAS7, in one study [14]. Qualitative and diary-based studies described nocturnal awakenings, difficulty initiating sleep, and itch as a predominant symptom in patients with CSU [25, 26]. Increased sleep disturbance was also described in adolescent and young patient cohorts [18, 22, 26, 34, 48]. Sleep disturbance was also reported alongside psychological outcomes, including positive correlations between sleep difficulty and depressive symptoms and stress, associations between impaired sleep and higher anxiety scores, and mediation of CSU-related anxiety/depression by itch and sleep disruption [22, 32, 43]. Sleep impairment was found to be associated with headache, nocturnal itch, non-skin-related symptoms (NSRS), RLS, sexual dysfunction in women, type D personality trait, refractory disease, and stigmatization [18, 21, 30, 31, 33, 35, 37, 38].

Sleep-related biomarkers were evaluated in CSU as outlined in Table 5. Bešlić et al. reported reduced salivary melatonin levels in patients with CSU compared with controls, with lower melatonin levels correlating with poorer overall QoL but not with PSQI scores, while Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) and PSQI were positively correlated with each other [51]. Lower serum melatonin levels were also observed in patients with CSU compared with controls, with reduced melatonin correlating with worse CU-Q2oL sleep-related items and higher systemic inflammatory markers [52]. Additionally, a separate study found elevated orexin-A levels, a wake-promoting neuropeptide, in patients with CSU, which correlated with poorer sleep quality and greater daytime sleepiness as measured by PSQI and ESS, respectively [53]. Discussion

Sleep Outcomes and Clinical Correlates in CSU

Sleep dysfunction is a well-established component of CSU morbidity. Sleep–wake disorders such as insomnia have been reported in 36.7% of patients with CSU, and nocturnal pruritus has been identified as a major contributor to impaired sleep initiation and maintenance, with more than half of patients with CSU reporting itch-related sleep disturbance [6, 60]. Across included studies in this systematic review, sleep impairment was consistently documented across various assessment methods. For example, among eight studies that reported mean PSQI values for CSU and control patients, scores for CSU ranged from 4.20 to 15.12, compared with 0.72 to 8.12 in control populations. Importantly, these findings extended across diverse populations as sleep disturbance was reported in CSU cohorts from the USA, Europe, India, Korea, Australia, Sri Lanka, and Brazil [29, 39–43, 49]. Sleep impairment was also described in children and adolescent cohorts, as well as young adult cohorts, with consistent reports of substantial sleep disturbance and impaired sleep-related functioning in association with CSU in these populations [18, 22, 26, 34, 48].

However, potential demographic modifiers are less consistent. While Etgü and Önder observed poorer sleep outcomes among female patients [16], larger datasets such as Sorour et al. and Stepaniuk et al. did not demonstrate significant sex differences [36, 47], suggesting that sex-based vulnerability may be context-dependent rather than a uniform finding. Comparative evidence also places CSU-related sleep burden within a broader dermatologic context, as one study reported sleep problems in 50% of patients with CSU compared with 38–47% across psoriasis severity strata, supporting sleep dysfunction as a prominent contributor to CSU morbidity [46].

A recent review has demonstrated that nocturnal itch is frequently implicated as a key driver of sleep disruption in CSU [60]. Several studies included in Table 3 supports the role of pruritus in sleep disruption. For example, one study found that patients with nocturnal itch and greater itch severity were more likely to experience impaired sleep than those without nocturnal itch and with lower itch severity [35]. Other studies similarly found that itching was associated with worse CU-Q2oL sleep subscale scores, worse PSQI scores, and that greater itch intensity predicted sleep disturbance [28, 34, 37]. However, several studies also suggest that sleep disturbance in CSU extends beyond itch alone and may cluster with specific comorbid symptom profiles. Filiz et al. reported more pronounced sleep impairment among patients with CSU with comorbid headache, with higher sleep disturbance scores than those without headache despite no significant between-group differences in UAS7 [18]. In addition, in a large cohort, NSRS, including fever, malaise, and musculoskeletal pain, were associated with higher odds of sleep disturbance, supporting a broader systemic inflammatory phenotype [30], with a separate study linking poorer sleep to elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) [16]. Moreover, the increased frequency of RLS and worse RLS severity reported in CSU populations than in controls suggests that neurologic and sensory comorbidities may further exacerbate insomnia symptoms and nocturnal fragmentation [37]. Sleep dysfunction may also intersect with broader psychosocial domains, as studies examining sexual dysfunction and personality traits described greater sleep impairment among affected subgroups [31, 33].

The relationship between CSU severity and sleep impairment appears complex. In a 2019 review of sleep outcomes across chronic dermatoses, Kaaz et al. reported that greater urticaria activity, as assessed by UAS7, was associated with worse sleep quality [11], a pattern that is similarly observed across multiple studies included in the current review [13, 15, 16, 19, 23, 30, 37]. However, this association is not universal. One included study did not identify a significant relationship between ISI and either UAS7 or UCT, suggesting that sleep impairment may persist despite relatively controlled CSU in a subset of patients [24]. Other clinical variables also showed mixed associations with sleep outcomes. Relationships between disease duration and sleep impairment varied across studies, with one study reporting worse sleep with longer CSU duration (≥ 5 years) [16], while another found no clear relationship [47]. Findings were likewise inconsistent for angioedema, with one study reporting worse sleep among patients with angioedema [16], whereas others found sleep outcomes to be similar regardless of angioedema status and more closely related to pruritus burden [28, 47].

Taken together, these findings highlight that sleep disturbance in CSU is both prevalent and multifactorial, reflecting contributions from disease activity, symptom burden, and comorbid conditions.

QoL and Psychological Comorbidity in CSU

Sleep disturbance in CSU also has important implications for QoL and psychological health. While CSU is well recognized to impair health-related QoL [6, 7], findings from this review indicate that sleep disturbance contributes to this broader functional burden. Across studies, poorer sleep quality and greater insomnia severity were associated with worse overall disease burden, including findings by Etgü and Önder showing that higher PSQI and ISI scores positively correlated with higher CU-Q2oL scores [16]. This is consistent with broader evidence of substantial QoL impairment in CSU, as approximately 30% of patients were reported to have substantial DLQI impact (21–30) [44], and this effect on QoL was typically shown to be positively correlating with urticaria severity [23].

Psychological comorbidity is also common in CSU, with one review reporting anxiety prevalence of 10–31% and depression of 7–29% [6]. Several studies in this review further indicate that sleep dysfunction intersects with anxiety and depressive symptoms, including evidence that itch and sleep disturbance mediated most of CSU’s association with adolescent anxiety (95.7%) and depression (116.7%) (p < 0.001) [22], supporting a model in which pruritus and sleep disruption may represent a key link between inflammatory symptom burden and downstream mental health outcomes. Additionally, anxiety and depression themselves are known to cause significant impairments on sleep [61]. Sleep complaints in CSU should therefore prompt the active consideration of underlying anxiety and depressive symptoms, as unrecognized psychological comorbidity may meaningfully contribute to persistent functional impairment.

Biomarkers and SDB

Emerging evidence suggests that inflammatory, biologic, and physiologic mechanisms contribute to sleep dysfunction in CSU. Altered orexin-A and melatonin levels have been associated with impaired sleep, supporting a role for circadian–immune dysregulation beyond symptom-driven nocturnal disruption [51–53]. Elevated levels of orexin-A were associated with worse sleep quality (PSQI domain) and ESS scores in patients with CSU, supporting orexin-A as a potential biomarker for future study in CSU-related sleep disruption [53]. Additionally, melatonin has been characterized in allergic disease as sleep-promoting and immunomodulatory [62], and melatonin receptors within the skin support a potential role for cutaneous melatoninergic signaling in maintaining skin homeostasis under inflammatory stress [63]. In this context, reduced melatonin availability may represent a biologically relevant disturbance, as lower melatonin levels in CSU were associated with higher systemic inflammatory markers [52]. Drawing from pediatric AD, one study reported impaired sleep quality alongside lower melatonin levels in patients with AD compared with controls [64], and separate work demonstrated improved disease severity and shorter sleep onset latency following melatonin supplementation [65]. Future CSU studies should continue to evaluate melatonin and other sleep-related biomarker levels, including the potential role of melatonin as an adjunctive intervention in carefully designed trials.

Evidence on SDB suggests that physiologic sleep pathology may coexist with CSU-related sleep impairment. Several studies in this review identified higher rates of OSA and OSAS, as well as increased SDB risk and abnormal AHI among CSU cohorts, reinforcing the clinical relevance of screening beyond subjective insomnia symptoms [55–59]. Importantly, one study reported negative correlations between total CU-Q2oL scores and objective SDB indices such as AHI, longest apnea duration, and respiratory events, highlighting heterogeneity across individual studies [56]. Interestingly, large-scale data from He et al. points to a bidirectional relationship, showing that preexisting sleep disorders, particularly sleep apnea and chronic insomnia, are associated with increased CSU risk, consistent with the possibility that sleep disorders may trigger CSU through inflammatory pathway activation and immune dysregulation [66]. The bidirectional relationship between sleep disorders and CSU warrants further investigation. Future studies should evaluate whether treatment of SDB or insomnia in patients with CSU could result in improvement in urticaria activity along with sleep outcomes. Collectively, these findings support screening and early referral for OSA and related breathing disorders in patients with CSU with prominent sleep complaints.

Pathogenesis of Sleep Disruption in CSU

Collectively, the available evidence suggests that sleep disruption in CSU is likely multifactorial, arising from multiple interacting mechanisms, including persistent symptoms, neuroimmune inflammation, psychosocial stress, and treatment-related influences, as summarized in a recent 2025 narrative review [12]. Nocturnal pruritus appears to be a dominant proximal driver, with multiple included studies describing itch as the primary symptom disrupting sleep, while associations between angioedema and sleep outcomes were less consistent [16, 25, 26, 28, 34, 35, 47]. Mechanistically, CSU can be conceptualized as a mast cell-driven process in which histamine contributes to local cutaneous manifestations such as wheals and pruritus that disrupt sleep processes, while also exerting broader effects on sleep–wake regulation given histamine’s recognized wake-promoting role and its potential interplay with melatonin systemically in circadian signaling [5, 67, 68].

CSU has also been linked to systemic inflammatory activation and stress-axis dysregulation. This is supported by case–control evidence demonstrating higher serum interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha levels and altered cortisol in CSU compared with healthy controls, with CSU severity correlating positively with cortisol levels, consistent with heightened physiologic arousal and wakefulness [69]. These pathways align with findings in this review linking poorer sleep and lower melatonin levels in CSU to systemic inflammatory burden [16, 52].Treatment-related effects may also contribute, as first-generation antihistamines can cause daytime somnolence, and systemic corticosteroids, sometimes used during severe flares, may increase restlessness and delay sleep onset, emphasizing that sleep dysfunction in CSU often reflects overlapping disease- and therapy-related factors [70, 71].

Clinical Implications for Sleep Assessment in CSU

Considering these findings, practical integration of sleep assessment into clinical care becomes an important consideration. The available evidence supports sleep dysfunction as a clinically relevant component of CSU burden that warrants proactive assessment in routine care. Brief validated tools such as the PSQI or ISI, or targeted questions regarding difficulty initiating sleep, nocturnal awakenings, and daytime somnolence, may help identify clinically meaningful sleep disruption, particularly in patients with nocturnal pruritus, fatigue, or refractory symptoms. When sleep disturbance is identified, several practical strategies may help improve sleep quality. First and foremost, optimal management of CSU that addresses the patient’s itch and hive burden is needed. When up to four-times standard dosing of antihistamines is intolerable or inadequate to control a patient’s symptoms, a discussion regarding a long-term non-antihistamine option including the three FDA-approved options, omalizumab, dupilumab, or remibrutinib, is indicated [1]. Adjunctive measures to improve nighttime sleep include optimizing antihistamine timing with evening dosing when appropriate, avoiding sedating first-generation antihistamines during daytime hours, and minimizing nighttime dosing of medications that may impair sleep initiation, such as systemic corticosteroids. If sleep complaints persist despite adequate CSU management, dermatologists should consider referral to sleep medicine to evaluate for SDB such as OSA. The interconnectedness between sleep disturbance and mental health also supports concomitant screening for comorbid depression or anxiety, as addressing these psychiatric comorbidities may also improve sleep and the overall health of the patient with CSU.

Limitations and Conclusions

This systematic review should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. Included literature was variable in study design and population characteristics, and many studies were cross-sectional, limiting causal inference and restricting conclusions regarding directionality between CSU activity and sleep impairment. Objective sleep testing and biomarker assessment were reported in a minority of studies, and potential confounders such as psychiatric comorbidity, obesity, medication exposure, and baseline sleep disorders were inconsistently reported across studies.

In conclusion, given the high prevalence and multifactorial nature of sleep disturbance in CSU, routine sleep assessment should be integrated into CSU clinic visits. In parallel, clinical trials should incorporate validated sleep quality instruments to enable a more comprehensive evaluation of overall disease burden and therapeutic efficacy. Finally, future research is warranted to elucidate the bidirectional relationship between sleep disorders and CSU.
