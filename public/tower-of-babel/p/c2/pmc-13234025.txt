Genetic architecture of lumbar spinal stenosis

ABSTRACT
Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) affects over 100 million people globally, with an increasing incidence due to an ageing population. While LSS is known to be heritable, its genetic basis remains poorly understood. We conduct a genome-wide meta-analysis of LSS in 40,303 cases and 741,469 controls. We identify 73 previously unreported loci in addition to 15 known loci, and highlight spinal degeneration as a key pathogenic mechanism. In 12,784 surgically treated cases, we discover five loci specifically associated with severe disease. Age-of-onset analyses show that most variants influence risk after midlife, but some confer susceptibility as early as age 34. Mendelian randomization further demonstrates causal effects of higher body mass and fat-free mass on LSS risk. Overall, our findings expand knowledge of the genetic background of LSS and inform future translational research.

Introduction

Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) narrows the lumbar central spinal canal or foramina, causing symptoms mainly in the buttocks and lower extremities1,2. The term central stenosis is used if the whole central spinal canal is compressed, and foraminal or lateral stenosis if the single nerve root exiting the neural foramina is compressed1. LSS usually develops with aging when lumbar structures degenerate, including changes in intervertebral discs, facet joints, ligamentum flavum, and vertebrae. In addition, spondylolisthesis can cause or aggravate LSS1. Even though the majority of LSS cases have a degenerative origin, in a smaller subgroup of patients, LSS arises from developmental or congenital factors2,3. Naturally, developmental LSS can predispose individuals to degenerative LSS as they age3. Developmental causes include a congenitally small central canal and Paget disease, which leads to atypical bone growth1,3.

In the general population, the majority of radiological LSS cases are asymptomatic. The prevalence of diagnostic LSS is around 11%, increasing significantly with age1,4. LSS becomes clinically significant when it causes symptoms affecting daily life. The main symptoms include discomfort, pain, fatigue, paresthesia, and/or numbness in the lower extremities or the buttocks, which exacerbate during activities such as walking, frequently called neurological claudication1,2,4. Low back pain can be present or absent2. The symptoms are typically relieved during sitting or forward flexion1,2. Diagnosis is based on patient history and symptoms, and clinical examination, with confirmation through imaging, usually magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT)1. Non-surgical management, such as physiotherapy, oral medication, and activity modification, is usually the first-line treatment. Surgical treatment is warranted if the symptoms are severe or non-surgical care does not improve the symptoms enough1,2. A previous MRI-based twin study5, estimated the heritability of LSS to be 0.67, indicating a substantial genetic contribution to LSS risk. Further research is needed to fully understand the hereditary background of LSS, which is currently poorly characterized. Using data from FinnGen, Estonian Biobank, and UK Biobank, we perform a genome-wide meta-analysis and identify previously unreported loci. We also show that examining LSS by utilizing surgical procedure codes provides further information regarding a more severe form of the disease that requires surgical treatment.

Results and discussion

88 Loci associated with LSS risk in meta-analysis

In this study, we used the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 code M48.0 to characterize LSS in the genome-wide meta-analysis. Data from FinnGen, the Estonian Biobank, and the UK Biobank were analyzed, comprising 40,303 cases and 741,468 controls (Supplementary Data 1). We can presume that diagnoses are symptomatic since they are clinically determined. Depending on the study cohort, the prevalence of LSS in our study varied from 2.5 to 8.4%; in the meta-analysis, it was 5.2%. A total of 88 loci with at least one genome-wide significant (P < 5 × 10−8) variant were associated with LSS in the meta-analysis. Seventy-three of the loci were previously unreported (Figs. S1–S73, Supplementary Data 2). We also successfully replicated fifteen loci previously associated with LSS, while 5 loci did not replicate (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Data 2–4, Fig. S74)6–9. Given the observed heterogeneity in some observed effects, we also performed random-effect meta-analysis as a sensitivity analysis (Fig. S74, Supplementary Data 4). In the conditional analysis, we also detected secondary signals at five LSS-associated loci (Supplementary Data 2). In addition, we fine-mapped all loci associated with LSS in the meta-analysis (Supplementary Data 5). In eight loci, there was only one variant in each credible set. Depending on the study dataset used, the LD score regression-derived SNP-based heritability on the liability scale ranged between 0.1969 and 0.2499, indicating that genetic factors explain 19.7–25% of the common variation in LSS risk (Supplementary Data 6). The genomic inflation factor lambda GC varied between 1.14 and 1.54 and suggested inflation in the test statistics. Considering the observed intercept values varied between 1.00 and 1.14, the inflation is likely attributed to polygenic signals10. Our heritability estimates were higher compared to the previous SNP-based heritability estimates, which have estimated that 10.1–14.9% of the common variation in LSS risk originates from the genetic factors (https://nealelab.github.io/UKBB_ldsc/h2_summary_M13_SPINSTENOSIS.html)6.

Meta-analysis focusing on LSS that requires surgery

The sensitivity meta-analysis conducted in FinnGen and the Estonian Biobank focused on LSS patients who required surgery and identified 32 genome-wide significant loci (Fig. 1A, Figs. S75, S76, Supplementary Data 7, Supplementary Data 8). The surgical cases were required to have procedure codes of Nomesco v1.15: ABC36, ABC56, ABC66, ABC99, NAG61, NAG62, NAG63, NAG66, NAG67, and NAG99. The surgical meta-analysis consisted of 12,784 cases and 418,246 controls (Supplementary Data 1). We compared the original meta-analysis with the sensitivity meta-analysis on LSS patients requiring surgery and observed differences in the effect estimates for six variants, indicating larger effect estimates in the surgical cohort (Fig. 1B, Fig. S75, Supplementary Data 9).

LSS-associated loci observed near genes related to degenerative changes in the spine

LSS is suggested to be caused by degenerative changes in the spine11, and we identified several potential genes that may account for these degenerative changes. Several loci near genes that function in biological pathways, such as TGF-β, BMP, or WNT-signaling pathways, which are known to play a role in musculoskeletal disorders, were identified (Supplementary Data 3). Some of the observed loci have also been associated with other musculoskeletal disorders, such as osteoarthritis12, lumbar disc herniation13,14, or back pain15,16. The same was observed for some loci in our PheWAS analysis, where we found that many LSS-associated lead variants were also associated with other musculoskeletal disorders. On the other hand, some of the associations appear to be LSS-specific based on our results (Supplementary Data 3). Of particular interest was the locus close to BMP6 (bone morphogenetic protein 6), the effect estimate of which was also significantly larger in the LSS surgical meta-analysis compared to the primary LSS meta-analysis (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Data 9). BMP6 has previously been associated with spondylosis17 and osteoarthritis18 and has been found to play an important role in bone and cartilage formation and repair19 and can also induce angiogenesis20. Interestingly, in our study, we also detected other loci in the close proximity of angiogenesis-related genes, such as ANGPT1 (angiopoietin 1), which has a role in vascular development and angiogenesis21. Vascular changes are often observed in association with spinal degeneration; examples of these changes include the association of angiogenesis with osteogenesis and thus a possible impact on bone homeostasis22, and vascular ingrowth in intervertebral discs23. Another interesting LSS-associated locus was observed near CCDC91 (coiled-coil domain containing 91), which has previously been associated with the ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL)24,25. OPLL is characterized by ectopic bone formation within the posterior longitudinal ligament in the spinal canal. OPLL may also contribute to the pathogenesis of LSS, as an ossified ligament can compress the spinal cord or nerve roots26,27. In addition, we identified and replicated previously identified loci near genes related to the nervous system, such as DCC (DCC netrin 1 receptor) and NGF (nerve growth factor), the latter of which is also already a known potential medical target for pain28. The identification of nervous system-related genes suggests that, in addition to structural changes, nervous system-related pathways are potentially involved in the underlying symptomatic LSS and may thus explain the differences in symptoms experienced by patients. Similar findings have also been made in GWAS studies investigating the genetic background of LDH13 and chronic back pain29. The loci we observed in the surgical meta-analysis largely overlapped with those observed in the meta-analysis. We also observed five loci that were specific to the surgical GWAS; however, the effects of these variants were in the same direction in the original meta-analysis (Fig. S76, Supplementary Data 7). Furthermore, these associated loci potentially play a role in the same biological pathways as those we observed in the LSS meta-analysis (Supplementary Data 3).

Functional annotations and colocalizations provide further information about LSS

Twenty-five genes that we identified as potential candidate genes at the novel LSS-associated loci were also highlighted by the GCTA-fastBAT analysis30 and the MAGMA31 gene-based test (Supplementary Data 2, Fig. 2A, Supplementary Data 10 and 11). The pathway responsible for neurotrophin receptor binding showed the most significant enrichments in the MAGMA gene-set analysis (Fig. 2B), which used the pFDR<0.05 significance threshold. Gene-set analysis also highlighted pathways associated with ossification, pathways controlling biosynthetic processes and differentiation, as well as pathways related to vasculature development (Fig. S77). Additionally, enrichments were found in many gene-sets related to the nervous system, such as the pathway related to the binding of neurotrophin receptors. There were no tissues identified in the MAGMA tissue expression analysis (Fig. S77) that exhibited a positive correlation between associations with LSS and tissue-specific gene expression profiles. In total, we detected 758 colocalizations (posterior probability [PP] for the shared variant ≥0.8) between LSS GWAS and 322 different resources that contained numerous QTL datasets and other FinnGen R12 endpoints. Unsurprisingly, several colocalizations were observed with other back-related disorders in FinnGen. For example, LSS association signals on chr12 near gene SOX5 (SRY-box transcription factor 5) and chr15 near SMAD3 (SMAD family member 3) were colocalized with association signals of several back-related disorders (Supplementary Data 12). These findings, together with our PheWAS findings, suggest that some of the biological pathways involved in the pathogenesis of LSS overlap with other back-related disorders. The LSS association signal on chr2 near GFPT1 (glutamine-fructose-6-phosphate transaminase 1) and chr7 near IL6 (interleukin 6) colocalized specifically with several immune-related eQTLs. For both genes, colocalizations were observed in monocytes and macrophages, and also with fibroblasts and tibial artery (Supplementary Data 12).

Disease onset-modifying variants were discovered in survival analyses

LSS usually manifests later in life1. Before our study, an age-of-onset analysis for genetic variants was not possible since the genetic background of the LSS has not been investigated extensively, and there have been limitations in the availability of large, longitudinal datasets. At each locus associated with LSS, we examined the differences in the cumulative incidences between homozygotes for the lead variants and calculated the cumulative incidence of LSS diagnoses. Around age 40, the number of LSS diagnoses started to accumulate, with the majority of diagnoses occurring between the ages of 50 and 70. All lead variants showed a similar trend in terms of the accumulation of LSS diagnoses, with the lead variant genotypes showing only slight variation and cumulative morbidities following the sample prevalence value (6.88% for LSS in FinnGen, Supplementary Data 13). Interestingly, we observed that for LSS-associated variant near the OTUD7B (OTU deubiquitinase 7B), the cumulative incidence differed between homozygotes already at the age of 34 (p = 0.013, Fig. S78, Supplementary Data 13). With the majority of the other variants, the difference in the cumulative LSS diagnoses between homozygotes manifested at a later age, but with some variants, no significant difference was observed at all (Supplementary Data 13). We carried out the same analysis in the Estonian Biobank and discovered that diagnoses were accumulating for the variant in a similar manner to that observed in FinnGen (Fig. S78). We also performed genome-wide survival association analysis in FinnGen, with the aim of identifying genetic variants associated with LSS age of onset. In this GATE analysis32, we identified 24 loci associated with the age of onset of LSS, 22 of which were loci we identified in GWAS (Supplementary Data 14). One of the identified variants was the same rs11205303 variant near OTUD7B, further validating its potential role in LSS onset. Two loci specific to age of onset were found near genes NCOA1 (nuclear receptor coactivator 1, rs1041837006, p = 8.17 × 10−9) and APOE (apolipoprotein E, rs429358, p = 2.43 × 10−11). While our findings related to OTUD7B are interesting, based on our PheWAS and colocalization findings (Supplementary Data 3, Supplementary Data 12), the variant would be a poor potential target for translational research, as the rs11205303 variant appears to protect from LSS while increasing the risk of cardiovascular disorders and cancer.

Potential causal relationships observed between anthropometric factors and LSS

Prior research has shown associations between anthropometric traits and LSS2,33,34; however, the causal relationships between these factors have not been thoroughly examined. With the help of Mendelian randomization, we uncovered that there are also potential causal relationships between several anthropometric traits and LSS (Fig. 4A, Figs. S79–S82, Supplementary Data 16, Supplementary Data 17). The most significant potential causal relationships were observed between whole body fat-free mass and higher LSS risk (OR = 1.724, pFDR = 1.11 × 10−20, Fig. S79) and between body mass index (BMI) and higher LSS risk (OR = 1.553, pFDR = 2.12 × 10−24, Fig. S80). The only outcome for which we found LSS to be potentially causal was back pain (OR = 1.027, pFDR = 5.92 × 10−13, Fig. 4B, Fig. S83, Supplementary Data 16). However, the sensitivity analysis with MR Egger (OR = 1.025, pFDR = 0.3156) was not statistically significant. The fact that we did not observe significant directional pleiotropy (Supplementary Data 16) suggests that the null result we obtained with MR Egger could be the consequence of the known reduced statistical power of the method compared with IVW. Although pleiotropy was not observed, some causal estimates were heterogeneous (Supplementary Data 16), and thus, these results should be interpreted with caution. Due to the observed heterogeneity for some causal estimates, we conducted further sensitivity analyses and used the weighted median method to derive causal estimates resilient to the presence of some invalid instruments and leave-one-out analysis to assess whether any single variant disproportionately influenced the results (Fig. 4A, B, Supplementary Data 18). These results aligned with our primary analysis performed with IVW. MRPRESSO36 was also applied to obtain outlier-corrected causal estimates (Fig. 4A, B); the results were consistent with the IVW estimates (Supplementary Data 16). We also investigated the possibility of reverse causality, mapping potential causal relationships for the same traits in the other direction as well. Back pain (OR = 88.140, pFDR = 1.94 × 10−13) was the only trait for which we observed a statistically significant potential causal relationship in both directions (Supplementary Data 16). The sensitivity analysis using MR Egger (OR = 8.311, pFDR = 0.459), however, did not reach statistical significance. This observed potential two-way causal relationship is intriguing and may be explained by the fact that those suffering from back pain usually seek treatment and are diagnosed2.

Discussion

By identifying a significant number of novel loci and performing multiple downstream analyses, we were able to expand the current understanding of the genetic basis of LSS. Our findings highlighted the significance of spinal degeneration as a major risk factor for LSS. Since we found that the number of diagnoses for LSS increased only after the age of 50, our study’s age-of-onset analyses lend credence to the notion that LSS is an age-related condition. However, our analysis of cumulative LSS incidence patterns revealed variants that significantly impact the risk of LSS morbidity as early as 34 years of age. Additionally, numerous loci associated with LSS requiring surgery underlined pathways that can lead to a more severe LSS.

Our study has strengths and limitations. Multiple genome-wide significant associations with LSS were discovered, facilitated by integrating data from large biobanks, which resulted in a substantial sample size. Divergences in the proportional frequency of LSS cases among the sample groups incorporated in the meta-analysis indicate possible inconsistencies in the ability of biobanks to recognize patients with LSS. It is also important to note that only people with European ancestry were included in our study. Both the frequency of LSS surgical treatment and the type of surgery performed can differ greatly between countries2. In our research, we have used surgical codes that are used in Finland for the surgical treatment of LSS patients37. The eQTL analyses would benefit from incorporating bone and cartilage data, which are crucial tissues in LSS pathogenesis. Since our work only uses computational methods, future functional studies would enhance our results. Taken together, our work contributes to our understanding of the genetic origins of LSS and may open the door to future research into novel therapies and preventative strategies for LSS.

Study populations

FinnGen

FinnGen strives to advance our understanding of disease mechanisms and the efficacy of medical care. The study’s aim is to identify links between individual genetic differences and diseases. The FinnGen project has the necessary ethical and prior permits for biobank research (Supplementary Note), and all persons who have provided a research sample are aware of the intended use of the samples and have given their written consent to biobank research either in connection with sample donation or when participating in older research projects, the materials of which have been transferred to Finnish biobanks with the written consent of Fimea. We used The International Classification of Diseases Tenth Revision (ICD-10) code M48.0 and Ninth Revision (ICD-9) codes 7230A, 7240A, and 7240B (Supplementary Data 1) to characterize cases in FinnGen. Patients without a record of these ICD codes were classified as controls, and participants who were diagnosed with other dorsopathies were excluded (M40-M54, M76). Patient data were obtained from the Hospital Discharge Registry and the Cause of Death Registry; however, patients whose registration data were only available in the primary care registry were excluded from the analysis. The FinnGen (R12-version) data used in the study included 26,090 LSS cases and 353,224 controls.

Estonian Biobank (EstBB)

The Estonian Biobank cohort is a volunteer-based sample of the Estonian resident adult population (aged ≥ 18 years)38. Estonians represent 83%, Russians 14%, and other nationalities 3% of all participants. The current number of participants is >205,000 and represents a large proportion, >15% of the Estonian adult population, making it ideally suited to population-based studies. General practitioners (GPs) and medical personnel in the special recruitment offices have recruited participants throughout the country. At baseline, the GPs performed a standardized health examination of the participants, who also donated blood samples for DNA, white blood cells, and plasma tests and filled out a 16-module questionnaire on health-related topics such as lifestyle, diet, and clinical diagnoses described in WHO ICD-10. A significant part of the cohort has whole genome sequencing (3000), whole exome sequencing (2500), genome-wide single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) array data (200,000), and/or NMR metabolome data (200,000) available. In the meta-analysis, 71,277 participants from the Estonian Biobank were included; 6019 of these were LSS cases, and 65,258 were controls.

UK Biobank (UKBB)

The UK biobank39 cohort comprises of samples acquired between 2006 and 2010. Hundreds of thousands of people aged 40–69 from across Britain provided samples. In our research, we used a subset of European ancestry, consisting of 8194 LSS patients and 322,986 controls from the UK biobank, more precisely, summary statistics from the PanUKBB project (https://pan.ukbb.broadinstitute.org/).

Genotyping, imputation, and quality control

FinnGen

Genotyping was performed using Illumina and Affymetrix DNA microarrays. Genotype data were quality controlled to exclude variants with a low Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) p-value (<1 × 10−6), minor allele count (MAC) below three, and high missingness (cut-off 2%), as well as individuals with high genotype missingness (cut-off 5%), high levels of heterozygosity (±4 SD), non-Finnish ancestry, and individuals whose sex did not match the genotype data. Eagle 2.3.5 was used in prephasing, were 20,000 was used as a threshold of conditioning haplotypes. Genotype imputation was done with Beagle 4.1 (version 08Jun17.d8b), and Finnish SISu v4.2 was used as a reference panel. The whole imputation protocol has been described at https://docs.finngen.fi/finngen-data-specifics/red-library-data-individual-level-data/genotype-data/imputation-panel/sisu-v4.2-reference-panel. For post-imputation quality control, we excluded variants with imputation information less than 0.6.

EstBB

Illumina Human CoreExome, OmniExpress, 370CNV BeadChip, and GSA arrays were used for genotyping. Individuals were excluded from the analysis if their call-rate was <95% or sex defined using X chromosome heterozygosity estimates did not match phenotypic data. Quality control included filtering on the basis of sample call rate (<98%), heterozygosity (>mean ± 3 SD), genotype and phenotype sex discordance, cryptic relatedness (IBD > 20%), and outliers from the European descent based on the MDS plot in comparison with HapMap reference samples. SNP quality filtering included call rate (<99%), MAF (<1%), and extreme deviation from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (p < 1 × 10−4). SHAPEIT2 was used for prephasing, while the imputation was performed with the Estonian-specific reference panel40 and IMPUTE241 with default settings. Variants with call-rate <95%, MAF < 1%, or HWE p-value < 1e−4 (autosomal variants only) and indels were excluded. For post-imputation quality control, we excluded variants with imputation information less than 0.6.

UKBB

In UKBB, Applied Biosystems UK BiLEVE Axiom Array by Affymetrix and Applied Biosystems UK Biobank Axiom Arrays were used for genotyping. Individuals who were identified as outliers for heterozygosity and missing rate, and individuals whose sex did not match the genotype data, were removed. Phasing on the autosomes was carried out using SHAPEIT342, with the 1000 Genomes phase 3 dataset43 used as a reference panel. For imputation, the Haplotype Reference Consortium (HRC) dataset44 was used as the main imputation reference panel, but also merged UK10K and 1000 Genomes phase 345 reference panels were utilized. Imputation was carried out with the IMPUTE4 program (https://jmarchini.org/software/), which is a re-coded version of the haploid imputation functionality implemented in IMPUTE241. Variants with INFO scores >0.8 and with an allele count of at least 20 were retained. Also, variants that had a greater than 5% overall missing rate and had a MAF of less than 0.0001 were removed.

GWAS

The Regenie (version 2.2.4) program46 was used to conduct GWAS using an additive genetic model in FinnGen and EstBB, modifying each phenotype for age, sex, the first 10 genetic principal components, and genotyping arrays. The sensitivity analysis was performed using Regenie and the same covariates as above. In UKBB, GWAS was performed using an additive genetic model and the SAIGE pipeline, modifying each phenotype for age, sex, and the first 10 genetic principal components. A more detailed description of the GWAS pipeline and implementation can be found here: https://github.com/atgu/ukbb_pan_ancestry/wiki/Batch-pipeline.

Sensitivity analysis: surgical GWAS

Additionally, we conducted sensitivity analysis in FinnGen and EstBB, with the aim of identifying genomic regions associated with more severe LSS that required surgical treatment. The sensitivity analysis was done using patients with LSS who had undergone surgery (NOMESCO version 1.15, ABC36, ABC56, ABC66, ABC99, NAG61, NAG62, NAG63, NAG66, NAG67, and NAG99)37, with the control group defined similarly to the meta-analysis. A total of 11,747 cases and 352,997 controls were included in this analysis. LSS cases that lacked documentation of studied surgical codes were not included in the analysis. The same policy was followed in cases of patients who underwent surgery but did not have an LSS diagnosis because it is likely that these patients had an acute injury. The same approach was utilized in the Estonian Biobank. A total of 1037 cases and 65,249 controls were included in the analysis.

LSS genome-wide meta-analysis (FinnGen, EstBB, UKBB)

An inverse variance-weighted fixed-effect meta-analysis of the GWAS results from FinnGen, EstBB, and UK Biobank was conducted using the METAL software47. Variant data from the Estonian and UK Biobanks were converted from hg19 to hg38 before being meta-analyzed using the Picard liftover (http://broadinstitute.io/picard). Meta-analysis data consists only of SNPs that have been observed in at least two of the datasets, totaling 15,144,278 SNPs. In total, there were 781,771 participants in the meta-analysis, of which there were 40,303 cases and 741,468 controls.

Random effect meta-analysis

In order to account for heterogeneity that was observed for some lead variants, we additionally conducted a random-effect meta-analysis. The lead variants observed in the main meta-analysis were used in the analysis that was conducted in the same cohorts as our main meta-analysis. The analysis was conducted using the metafor R package using the “REML” model48.

Surgical meta-analysis

The METAL software47 was used to do an inverse variance-weighted fixed-effect meta-analysis of the surgical GWAS results from FinnGen and EstBB. Prior to the meta-analysis, variant data from the Estonian Biobank were converted from the hg19 to the hg38 prior to the meta-analysis. The meta-analysis only included SNPs that were present in both datasets, totaling 13,994,491 SNPs. There were 12,784 cases and 418,246 controls among the 431,030 participants in the surgical meta-analysis.

Candidate gene characterization

A locus was defined as a window of 2MB (±1,000,000 bases) that contains at least one variant associated with LSS at p < 5 × 10−8. Using literature and databases (Genbank49, UniProt50, GTEx-Portal51) and identified a potential candidate gene with a relevant biological function for the loci that had not been reported in association with LSS in previous studies.

Fine mapping and conditional analyses

We applied the FinnGen fine-mapping pipeline (available at https://github.com/FINNGEN/finemapping-pipeline) with default parameters after first extracting the summary statistics of each locus associated in the meta-analysis. An exception was made for variants in the HLA region that were left out. The pipeline uses FinnGen samples to compute linkage disequilibrium within the regions of interest using LDstore252, creates 99% credible sets using the SUm of Single Effects (SuSiE)53, and summarizes the findings. Additionally, with the help of GCTA software (1.93.0 beta Linux)54, we performed conditional analyses for the loci to identify possible secondary signals. We used lead variants observed from the loci as a covariate and utilized FinnGen as the reference sample to estimate linkage disequilibrium (LD) corrections. The associations were first conditioned on the most significant variant at each locus using the --cojo-cond option with the default settings. For those loci where secondary signals were detected, the analysis was repeated using the first conditional analysis results. Conditioning was continued until no variant reached genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10−8).

Heritability

The LD score regression-derived SNP-based heritability estimate was computed using LDSC software (version 1.0.1)10. Heritability estimation was performed using the liability scale, with sample prevalence in each study population (Supplementary Data 1) and population prevalence estimated at 0.11, as estimated by Jensen et al.4. For heritability estimation, we used the HapMap3, a European-only reference panel provided by Alkes Price’s group (https://alkesgroup.broadinstitute.org/LDSCORE/).

PheWAS

In FinnGen, we also conducted a PheWAS analysis for lead variants associated with LSS. The PheWAS analysis excluded lead variants that were located in the HLA region. In the analysis, we used 124 clinician-curated core endpoints to test the association of lead variants, and results with p < 5 × 10−8 were considered significant. All endpoints and endpoint definitions are available here: https://www.finngen.fi/en/researchers/clinical-endpoints. The whole PheWAS pipeline has been described here: https://github.com/FINNGEN/pheweb/.

Functional annotations

FUMA55 was used for the functional annotations that were performed based on the meta-analysis summary statistics. We selected that the analysis would use functional information for the mapping. Positional mapping was also carried out, with SNP markers chosen from regions of exons or introns that affect post-transcriptional modifications and are involved in gene regulation. Gene expression data were also used for mapping, as well as eQTL mapping. We used whole blood, cultured fibroblasts, tibial nerve, and cervical spinal cord (GTEx v8) tissues in the analysis, and focused on the protein-coding genes only. MAGMA analysis31, a functional association test, was also performed; it focuses on gene-level information, unlike GWAS, where associations are reported at the variant level. MAGMA uses curated gene sets and GO annotations from MSigDB56 in the analyses. We used a 10 kb gene window and selected GTEx v8 tissue variants in the analysis. We also used fastBAT from the GCTA software package (1.93.0 beta Linux)54 for gene prioritization. FastBAT calculates the association p-value for a set of SNPs from an approximated distribution of the sum of χ2-statistics over the SNPs at gene regions using LD correlations between SNPs from a reference sample with individual-level genotypes and summary data from GWAS30. We utilized FinnGen as a LD reference and used 2MB window for the loci. For LD pruning, a 0.9 r2 threshold was used alongside the default methods. We considered results with pfastBAT < 5 × 10−8 and pGWAS < 5 × 10−8 significant30. Lead variants that are located in the HLA region were left out from the annotation analyses.

Colocalizations

We investigated the colocalizations of LSS association signals using various QTL data and other FinnGen R12 endpoints. For this, the FinnGen SuSiE colocalization pipeline was utilized (https://github.com/FINNGEN/coloc.susie.direct). Colocalizations between 571 resources are carried out by this pipeline, including all GWAS endpoints from UKBB, FinnGen, and the eQTL catalog, the Generisk project, and the proteomics study from INTERVAL, UKBB, and FinnGen. We utilized our SuSiE fine mapping results as input for the analysis. Colocalizations with a posterior probability of ≥ 0.8 for the variant were deemed significant57.

Survival analyses

Our aim was to observe how the LSS diagnoses accumulate for different variants according to age and to evaluate whether there are early onset variants by exploring the cumulative events. We determined the diagnosis age in years when the curve of the effect allele (EA) homozygotes statistically differed from the curve of other allele (OA) homozygotes of the same variant, of the variants where significant differences in the accumulation of diagnoses were observed. The calculation was performed with the two-tailed z-test by using the survival rates and survival rate standard errors obtained with the ‘survfit’ function, which is part of the ‘survival’ (https://github.com/therneau/survival, version 3.2-7) R library. The equation used was z = (‘survival rate EA/EA’-‘survival rate OA/OA’)/sqrt(‘se_survival_rate_EA/EA2’+’se_survival_rate_OA/OA2’), p = 2*(1-diff). A significant difference was defined as a p-value < 0.05. Furthermore, we calculated cumulative morbidity for each variant to determine whether some variants result in more diagnoses. In addition, we conducted a GATE analysis32 in FinnGen, which examines the association between the variants and the time-to-event rather than the presence of the disease. In the analysis, we attempted to detect variants associated with LSS disease onset by extracting diagnosis ages for LSS cases and ages at the end of the follow-up or at death for controls. Sex and the first 4 genetic principal components were used as covariates in the analysis.

Genetic correlations

Genetic correlations were calculated between meta- analyzed LSS summary statistics and 849 other phenotypes extracted from the GWAS database provided by the MRC Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU) (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). These calculations were performed using the LDSC software (version 1.0.1)10,58. We set the threshold for significant correlations at a false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected p-value (pFDR) < 0.05. Genetic correlations were performed using the HapMap3 European-only reference panel provided by Alkes Price’s group (https://alkesgroup.broadinstitute.org/LDSCORE/). For genetic correlations, we used our LSS summary statistics and the GWAS summary statistics that were similar to those that have been previously available in LDhub (https://ldsc.broadinstitute.org).

Mendelian randomization

In order to investigate the causal relationships between LSS and its associated risk factors, we used the Two-Sample MR (version 0.6.17) R library to perform a Mendelian randomization. Risk factors related to body composition and pain were included in the analysis (Supplementary Data 17), and we were able to assess if LSS is causal for risk factors and, conversely, if risk factors are causal for LSS due to a bi-directional study approach. To avoid sample overlap, since many of the GWAS data provided by the MRC-IEU are UKBB-based, we derived LSS instruments from a meta-analysis of FinnGen and EstBB summary statistics. As above, variant data from EstBB were first converted from hg19 to hg38, and we then conducted inverse variance-weighted fixed effect meta-analysis using the METAL software47. This meta-analysis comprised 450,591 participants, including 32,109 cases and 418,482 controls. Variants that were correlated were removed from the data, leaving only independent variants for analysis. We used the default clumping settings in the analysis (clumping window 1000 kb, r2 = 0.001). The principal analysis method we employed was the inverse variance weighted (IVW) model. In the sensitivity analyses, we obtained MR Egger estimates and performed Cochran’s q-test and the MR Egger intercept test to evaluate the heterogeneity and pleiotropy of the instruments. Additionally, as a sensitivity analysis, we also used a weighted median model and obtained outlier-corrected causal estimates by using MRPRESSO with default settings (NbDistribution = 10,000, p-value threshold = 0.05)36. Also, in order to determine whether a particular SNP is responsible for a potentially detectable causal relationship, a leave-one-out analysis was also carried out.
