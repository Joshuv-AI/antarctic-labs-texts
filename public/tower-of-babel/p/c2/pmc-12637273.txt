Molecular detection and characterization of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus pseudintermedius (MRSP) ST2361 in a healthcare setting
Authors: Reza Besharati, Ali Haghbin, Seyed Ahmad Hashemi, Ahmad Vosoughi-Motlagh, Amir Azimian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStaphylococcus pseudintermedius, a commensal in dogs, is an emerging zoonotic pathogen with increasing methicillin-resistant (MRSP) strains. This study investigated MRSP isolates from hospital environments and healthcare workers in Bojnurd, Iran, to assess antimicrobial resistance and zoonotic potential.MethodsNasal swabs from healthcare workers (n = 40) and hospital supplies (n = 35) were cultured. Phenotypic and genotypic analyses included antimicrobial susceptibility testing (Kirby-Bauer), PCR for resistance (mecA, vanA, cfr) and virulence genes (pvl, hla, icaA/D), SCCmec typing, and multilocus sequence typing (MLST).ResultsTwo MRSP isolates (ST2361) were identified, one from a nasal swab and another from hospital supplies. Both exhibited resistance to β-lactams (cefoxitin, penicillin), fluoroquinolones, macrolides, and sulfonamide-trimethoprim, but susceptibility to tetracyclines, gentamicin, linezolid, and vancomycin. PCR confirmed mecA and virulence genes (pvl, hla, icaA/D). A SCCmec pattern consistent with type IV and identical ST2361 profiles suggested potential clonal relatedness.ConclusionThe presence of MRSP ST2361 in humans and hospital environments highlights its zoonotic and nosocomial transmission risks. The isolates' multidrug resistance and virulence genes underscore the need for improved diagnostics, antimicrobial stewardship, and infection control measures in healthcare settings.

Introduction

Staphylococcus pseudintermedius represents a commensal organism that colonizes the cutaneous and mucosal surfaces of dogs, with epidemiological studies indicating carriage rates exceeding 75 % in canine populations [1,2]. While less prevalent in felines, this bacterium has also been isolated from cats. Within veterinary medicine, S. pseudintermedius is recognized as the predominant etiological agent responsible for pyoderma cases and has been increasingly associated with urinary tract infections in companion animals [3]. Alarmingly, recent surveillance data suggest that methicillin-resistant S. pseudintermedius (MRSP) now accounts for approximately 50 % of clinical isolates from these hosts [3]. The resistance profile of MRSP strains extends beyond β-lactam antibiotics (mediated by the mecA gene) to include multiple drug classes such as aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, tetracyclines, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole combinations [4]. This multidrug-resistant phenotype has significantly complicated therapeutic management of animal infections, creating an urgent need to investigate alternative antimicrobial options beyond currently approved veterinary medications [5].

The clinical significance of S. pseudintermedius extends beyond veterinary practice. Due to its phenotypic similarity to S. aureus and S. intermedius, this pathogen has been frequently misidentified in human clinical microbiology laboratories, leading to underreporting of human infections [6]. However, the implementation of advanced diagnostic technologies, including matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS), polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based assays, and whole-genome sequencing, has improved detection accuracy. These methods have uncovered S. pseudintermedius as an emerging opportunistic pathogen in human medicine, particularly associated with wound infections, respiratory tract colonization, bacteremia, and skin/soft tissue infections in immunocompromised hosts [7,8].

Geographical distribution studies have documented human S. pseudintermedius infections across multiple countries, strongly suggesting zoonotic transmission from companion animals to their owners or veterinary personnel [1,8,9]. While molecular epidemiological investigations have identified region-specific sequence type (ST) distributions among animal-derived isolates [9], the ST patterns of human clinical isolates remain poorly characterized, leaving questions about potential animal-human transmission pathways unanswered. Current understanding of the mechanisms facilitating S. pseudintermedius transmission between companion animals and humans, especially regarding MRSP strains, remains limited by insufficient epidemiological evidence. A systematic literature search of PubMed and Scopus databases from 2020 to 2025 using the keywords ‘Staphylococcus pseudintermedius Iran’ and ‘MRSP Iran’ revealed no prior reports of human clinical or colonization isolates with molecular characterization, highlighting the novelty of our findings in the Iranian context.

To address these knowledge gaps, our study examined the antimicrobial resistance profiles and molecular characteristics of S. pseudintermedius isolates (MRSP strains) obtained from both Hospital supplies and staff nose in Iran. This dual approach aimed to: [1] establish current resistance trends among circulating strains and [2] perform comparative genomic analysis between animal- and human-derived MRSP isolates to evaluate the zoonotic transmission potential of this multidrug-resistant pathogen.

Methods

Study design and sample collection

A cross-sectional study was conducted between September and December 2024, at Imam Reza Hospital in Bojnurd, Iran. A total of 75 samples were collected, comprising 40 nasal swabs from healthcare workers (HCWs) and 35 samples from hospital environmental surfaces. HCWs from various wards (including Pediatrics, ICU, and Surgery) were randomly selected. Participants completed a brief questionnaire documenting demographic data and potential exposure to companion animals. Exclusion criteria included the use of systemic or intranasal antimicrobials within the two weeks preceding sample collection. Nasal swabs were collected from both anterior nares using sterile transport swabs with Amies medium. Environmental samples were collected from frequently touched surfaces, including computer keyboards, bed rails, infusion pump buttons, and nurses' station counters, using pre-moistened sterile swabs. All samples were transported to the laboratory within 2 h for processing.

Bacterial isolation and phenotypic identification

Swabs were inoculated onto Mannitol Salt Agar (MSA) and Blood Agar plates and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24–48 h. Presumptive Staphylococcus colonies were sub-cultured for purity. Phenotypic identification involved Gram staining, catalase test, tube coagulase test with rabbit plasma, DNase test, and mannitol fermentation assessment.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST)

The antibiotic resistance profiles of Staphylococcus spp. isolates were determined using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method on Mueller-Hinton agar, according to CLSI guidelines. For S. pseudintermedius isolates, interpretation was based on the CLSI VET08 document. The following antibiotic discs (MAST DISKS™, UK) were used: cefoxitin (30 μg), penicillin (10 μg), tetracycline (30 μg), erythromycin (15 μg), levofloxacin (5 μg), moxifloxacin (5 μg), clindamycin (2 μg), gentamicin (10 μg), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (1.25 μg), minocycline (30 μg), rifampicin (5 μg), linezolid (10 μg), mupirocin (30 μg). Vancomycin and linezolid susceptibility were confirmed using the E-test method to determine Minimum Inhibitory Concentrations (MICs). Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 was used as the quality control strain. Inducible clindamycin resistance was detected by the D-test. Multidrug-resistant (MDR) phenotype was defined as non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories, as defined by Magiorakos et al. (2012).

Genomic DNA extraction

Bacterial DNA was isolated using a commercial Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (KR-2000, GENET BIO, Korea). The lysis buffer was supplemented with lysostaphin at a final concentration of 20 μg/ml, following the manufacturer's instructions for bacterial DNA purification. DNA quality was verified using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer to confirm the presence of double-stranded DNA.

Molecular identification of coagulase-positive Staphylococcus spp

Isolates were confirmed at the species level using a multiplex PCR protocol as described by Sasaki et al. [10], which targets the part of nuc gene for each Coagulase positive staphylococci. The product sizes for each strain are specific and different with others. The amplicon size for staphylococcus aureus is 359 bp, Staphylococcus intermedius 430 bp, Staphylococcus schleiferi 526 bp, Staphylococcus delphini 661 bp, Staphylococcus hyicus 793 bp and Staphylococcus pseudintermedius is 926 bp.

PCR detection of resistance and virulence genes

Conventional PCR was used to detect the methicillin, vancomycin and linezolid resistance genes (mecA, vanA, cfr). Virulence genes associated with leucocidin (pvl), hemolysin (hla), and biofilm formation (icaA, icaD) were also targeted [11,12]. The primer sequences are listed in Table 1. Multiplex PCR for SCCmec typing

SCCmec typing was performed using a multiplex PCR protocol initially designed for MRSA [13,14], which has been used in other studies for preliminary typing of MRSP. We acknowledge that this method may not be optimal for all MRSP lineages.

Multi-locus sequence typing

MLST for MRSP isolates was carried out by amplifying and sequencing internal fragments of seven housekeeping genes (ack, cpn60, fdh, pta, purA, sar, and tuf) as described by Solyman et al. [15]. The sequence type (ST) was assigned via the S. pseudintermedius PubMLST database (pubmlst.org).

Results

Sample

A total of 75 samples were processed. From the 40 HCW nasal swabs, 15 presumptive Staphylococcus spp. isolates were recovered. From the 35 environmental samples, nine presumptive Staphylococcus spp. isolates were obtained. Among these 24 isolates, 22 were phenotypically identified as S. aureus (coagulase and mannitol fermentation positive). The two remaining isolates from a HCW and a hospital supply (a computer keyboard) were coagulase-positive but mannitol fermentation negative, prompting molecular identification [Fig. 1].Fig. 1Flowchart of Sample Collection and Bacterial Isolation.Fig. 1

Molecular confirmation of MRSP and antimicrobial resistance

Species-specific PCR targeting the 926 bp part of nuc gene confirmed both isolates as S. pseudintermedius. Both isolates were resistant to cefoxitin (MRSP) and carried the mecA gene and also were negative for vanA and cfr genes. The antibiogram revealed a multidrug-resistant profile, with resistance to β-lactams (cefoxitin, penicillin), fluoroquinolones (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin), macrolides (erythromycin), lincosamides (clindamycin; D-test negative), and sulfonamide-trimethoprim. Both isolates remained susceptible to tetracyclines (tetracycline, minocycline), gentamicin, linezolid and vancomycin. Virulence and molecular typing

PCR confirmed the presence of the pvl and hla genes, as well as the biofilm-associated genes icaA and icaD in both isolates. SCCmec typing revealed a pattern consistent with type IV. MLST analysis identified both isolates as Sequence Type 2361. The allelic profile was ack [1], cpn60 [8], fdh [1], pta [1], purA [5], sar [5], tuf [1]. Discussion

The findings of this study provide evidence for the presence of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus pseudintermedius (MRSP) sequence type (ST) 2361 in both the hospital environment and the nasal cavities of healthcare workers in Bojnurd, Iran. This dual detection is particularly significant within a One Health framework, as it suggests potential zoonotic transmission and nosocomial persistence of this multidrug-resistant pathogen [1,8]. The convergence of human, animal, and environmental health is critical in understanding the epidemiology of emerging pathogens like MRSP.

The genetic characterization of our isolates revealed a SCCmec pattern consistent with type IV and ST2361. While common in MRSA, SCCmec type IV is less frequently the dominant type in global MRSP populations, which often feature types III and V [[16], [17], [18]]. The detection of a pattern consistent with type IV in our setting may indicate the acquisition of more mobile genetic elements, potentially facilitating spread. More notably, the assignment of our isolates to ST2361 is a key finding. This sequence type has been previously reported in companion animals, particularly in European and Asian studies [[19], [20], [21]], but to our knowledge, this is among the first reports of its isolation from human colonization and a hospital environment in Iran. A recent study from a neighboring region identified ST2361 in clinical infections from dogs [22], strengthening the possibility of a regional clone with zoonotic capability. The identical profiles of our two isolates, despite their different sources, provide preliminary evidence of clonal relatedness and potential for nosocomial persistence, underscoring the need for heightened surveillance.

The isolates exhibited a broad-spectrum resistance profile, showing resistance to β-lactams, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, and sulfonamide-trimethoprim. This multidrug-resistant phenotype aligns with global reports of MRSP as a repository of resistance genes, severely limiting therapeutic options [4,5]. The retention of susceptibility to tetracyclines and aminoglycosides like gentamicin offers potential alternative treatment regimens, a finding consistent with some regional reports in veterinary medicine [20]. The presence of the mecA gene confirms the basis for β-lactam resistance, a hallmark of MRSP.

Beyond resistance, the isolates harbored key virulence determinants. The detection of the pvl (Panton-Valentine leukocidin) and hla (alpha-hemolysin) genes is of particular clinical relevance. PVL is a potent cytotoxin associated with neutrophil lysis and tissue necrosis, and while its role is well-established in severe S. aureus infections like necrotizing pneumonia and skin abscesses [23], its presence in MRSP signifies a concerning pathogenic potential. Alpha-hemolysin is a key pore-forming toxin that contributes to tissue damage and immune evasion. The combination of multidrug resistance and these potent virulence factors creates a scenario where infections could be both severe and difficult to treat, posing a significant challenge in clinical management [24]. Furthermore, the presence of biofilm-associated genes (icaA, icaD) suggests an enhanced capacity for these strains to adhere to medical devices and mucosal surfaces, facilitating persistent colonization and environmental survival [25].

This study has several limitations that must be acknowledged, the most significant being the absence of parallel sampling from companion animals in the same region. Without comparative genomic data from animal-derived MRSP isolates, we cannot definitively conclude the direction of zoonotic transmission. The presence of MRSP in our hospital could be due to colonization of healthcare workers who are pet owners, or through direct contamination from assistance animals. The small sample size also restricts the generalizability of our findings. Future research must integrate human, animal, and environmental surveillance within a rigorous One Health framework. Interdisciplinary collaboration between human and veterinary medicine is essential to trace transmission routes, monitor the evolution of clones like ST2361, and implement coordinated infection control strategies. Public health initiatives should prioritize antimicrobial stewardship and education for both healthcare workers and pet owners to mitigate the spread of this emerging pathogen.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reports the isolation and molecular characterization of MRSP ST2361 from both hospital environments and healthcare workers, highlighting its multidrug-resistant phenotype, virulence potential, and potential for nosocomial persistence. The convergence of resistance and pathogenicity factors in these isolates underscores the emerging threat of MRSP as a zoonotic and healthcare-associated pathogen. Clinicians and microbiologists should consider S. pseudintermedius in cases of staphylococcal infections, particularly when conventional diagnostics yield ambiguous results. Interdisciplinary collaboration between human and veterinary medicine is essential to monitor MRSP transmission, optimize antimicrobial use, and implement evidence-based infection control strategies. Public health initiatives must prioritize antimicrobial stewardship, environmental decontamination, and education for healthcare workers and pet owners to mitigate the spread of this pathogen. As MRSP continues to evolve, proactive surveillance and research will be critical in preventing its establishment as a persistent public health concern. To fully understand the transmission ecology of MRSP, future research must integrate human, animal, and environmental surveillance within a One Health framework. Interdisciplinary collaboration between human and veterinary medicine is essential to monitor MRSP transmission, optimize antimicrobial use, and implement evidence-based infection control strategies.
