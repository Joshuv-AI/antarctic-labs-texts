New insights into the evolution and metabolism of the bacterial phylum Candidatus Acidulodesulfobacteriota through metagenomics
Authors: Yanhu Ren, Yue-Hong Wu, Jun Chen, Zhen-Hao Luo, Xue-Wei Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCandidatus Acidulodesulfobacterales, a formerly proposed bacterial order within the Deltaproteobacteria lineage, represents an ecologically significant group in sulfur-rich environments. Their diversity and functional potential in artificial acid mine drainage (AMD) ecosystems have been well studied; however, their distribution and ecological role in marine hydrothermal sulfides remain poorly understood. Here we integrated publicly available metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) with a newly reconstructed MAG from hydrothermal sulfides to perform comprehensive phylogenetic, metabolic, and host-virus interaction analyses. Phylogenomic and 16S rRNA gene analyses indicated that this lineage represents a distinct phylum-level clade, leading us to propose the designation Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota. Metabolic reconstructions indicated a versatile lifestyle, encompassing pathways for carbon fixation, nitrogen fixation, sulfur metabolism, iron oxidation, and hydrogen oxidation. Notably, the concatenated DsrAB protein phylogeny and the mixed enzyme types involved in Dsr-dependent dissimilatory sulfur metabolism suggest that Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota may represent a transitional lineage in the evolutionary shift from reductive to oxidative Dsr metabolism. Viral auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) associated with this phylum were predicted to modulate host metabolic pathways, including folate biosynthesis and sulfur metabolism, highlighting intricate host-virus interactions. These findings advance our understanding of the evolution, metabolic potential, and ecological roles of Ca. Introduction

The Deltaproteobacteria are an ecologically and metabolically diverse group of bacteria that play essential roles in global carbon, sulfur, and nutrient cycling [27]. Historically classified as classes within the phylum Proteobacteria, Deltaproteobacteria and Oligoflexia were reclassified in 2020 into four novel phylum-level lineages. Specifically, Deltaproteobacteria were divided into three distinct phyla: Desulfobacterota, Myxococcota, and SAR324, while Oligoflexia was reassigned to the newly proposed phylum Bdellovibrionota. [50]. In 2019, four metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) retrieved from an artificial acid mine drainage (AMD) system were identified and proposed as a new order within the Deltaproteobacteria, designated as Candidatus Acidulodesulfobacterales [46]. However, Ca. Acidulodesulfobacterales were not incorporated into the reclassification of Deltaproteobacteria, possibly due to the absence of cultured representatives and the limited availability of MAGs at that time.

Reconstructed metabolic pathways and gene expression profiles from the pioneering research suggested that Ca. Acidulodesulfobacterales were likely facultatively anaerobic autotrophs capable of nitrogen fixation, dissimilatory sulfate reduction, and iron oxidation [46]. Additionally, one MAG affiliated with this lineage was recovered in a study investigating microbial communities in sulfur-rich hydrothermal sediments [57]. This MAG was designated as SZUA-79, which was subsequently used as the phylum-level placeholder name for Ca. Acidulodesulfobacterales in the Genome Database Taxonomy (GTDB) [41]. Thereafter, as research on AMD ecosystems has expanded, an increasing number of MAGs belonging to Ca. Acidulodesulfobacterales have been progressively identified [12, 32, 51]. However, studies on this lineage in marine ecosystems remain scarce, limiting our understanding of its ecological distribution and metabolic potential. Given the dramatic shifts in the phylogeny of Deltaproteobacteria and the growing availability of MAGs for Ca. Acidulodesulfobacterales, further investigations are needed to elucidate the evolutionary history and functional capabilities of this lineage.

In this context, we collected available genomes of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacterales from public databases and reconstructed a novel MAG of this lineage from a hydrothermal black smoker sample. Through comprehensive phylogenetic analyses and comparative genomics, our study confirmed that Ca. Acidulodesulfobacterales constitutes a monophyletic, phylum-level bacterial lineage. Accordingly, we propose the designation Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota for this phylum, offering new insights into its taxonomy, host–virus interactions, and the evolutionary history of dissimilatory sulfite reductase (Dsr).

Materials and methods

A total of 167 publicly available Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota MAGs were downloaded from NCBI and GTDB in June 2024. In addition, one MAG of this phylum was recovered in this study from deep-sea hydrothermal massive sulfides. The methods used to produce this MAG and the genome analysis approaches employed in this study are described below.

Sample collection, DNA extraction, and metagenomic sequencing

Marine hydrothermal massive sulfides were retrieved from a hydrothermal field at a depth of 2,750 m in the Southwest Indian Ridge during the DY52 expedition aboard the R/V Dayang Yihao in May 2019. Massive sulfides were collected using a deep-sea television grab, and immediately stored at -80 °C upon retrieval on deck for subsequent metagenomic sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted using the PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit (MP Biomedicals, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The DNA concentration was measured using NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). The total community DNA was used for library preparation with the NEB Next® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England Biolabs, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The library was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (150-bp paired-end reads) to obtain metagenomic data for the sequenced sample.

Metagenome assembly, genome binning, and taxonomic assignment

Raw metagenomic reads from this study were quality filtered and trimmed using Trimmomatic (version 0.39) [5] with the following parameters: LEADING:3 TRAILING:3 SLIDINGWINDOW:5:20 MINLEN:50. Then quality-controlled reads were de novo assembled using MEGAHIT (version 1.2.9) (D. [29]) with the parameters -k-min 31, -k-max 111, -k-step 20, and -min-contig 1000. The assemblies were then processed using the MetaWRAP pipeline (version 1.3) [49] for genomic binning with default parameters. Briefly, initial binning was conducted using CONCOCT (version 1.1.0) [2], MaxBin2 (version 2.2.5) [52], and metaBAT2 (version 2.12.1) [23] in the BINNING module, and then the MAGs generated above were consolidated and refined using Binning_refiner (version 1.2) [44] in the BIN_REFINEMENT module. Taxonomy was assigned to the refined MAGs based on the GTDB (version r220) using GTDB-Tk (version 2.4.0) [8]. Quality-controlled reads were then mapped back to the refined MAGs to calculate their relative abundance by CoverM (version 0.7; https://github.com/wwood/CoverM) with default parameters.

Dereplication and annotation of genomes

The completeness and contamination of all available Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota MAGs, including the one from this study, were assessed using CheckM (version 1.1.3) [42]. Only MAGs with completeness > 50% and contamination < 5% were retained for subsequent analysis. Then the MAGs were de-replicated using dRep (version 3.4.2) [38] with a 95% average nucleotide identity (ANI) threshold, resulting in a non-redundant representative genome set for this phylum (Table S1). FastAAI (version 0.0.1) [14] was used to estimate the average amino acid identity (AAI) among representative genomes. The optimal growth temperatures (OGTs) of these MAGs were calculated using Tome (version 1.00) (G. [30]). For metabolic function analyses, open reading frames (ORFs) of de-replicated MAGs were determined using Prodigal (version 2.6.3) [21] with the parameters: -f gff, -g 11, -p single, -q, and -c. The resulting proteins were initially annotated by BLASTP searches against the KEGG [22] and eggNOG databases (version 5.0.0) [20] using a threshold of E-value < 1e-5. To improve annotation accuracy and coverage, functional predictions were further refined using the MetaCerberus pipeline (version 1.4.0) [10], applying a more stringent E-value cutoff of < 1e-9. Especially, genomic proteins were annotated using DiSCo (version 1.0) [37] to identify protein sequences and enzyme types associated with Dsr-dependent dissimilatory sulfur metabolism. Gene categories involved in iron metabolism were annotated using FeGenie (version 1.2) [13]. The genes encoding hydrogenases were identified with the reference sequences from the previous study [15]. Carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) were identified using the dbCAN2 (v3.0.2) analysis pipeline [56].

Phylogenetic and host-virus interaction analyses

The phylogenomic tree was constructed using a concatenated set of 120 universal marker proteins (bac120) identified by GTDB-Tk (version 2.4.0) [8]. Sequence alignments were performed using MUSCLE5 (version 5.2) [9], and poorly aligned regions were removed using trimAl (version 1.4) [7]. A maximum-likelihood tree was then inferred using IQ-TREE 2 (version 2.3.4) [33] with the parameters set as follows: -alrt 100, -B 1000, and -m MFP. For 16S rRNA gene phylogeny, sequences were extracted using Barrnap (version 3.0) (https://github.com/tseemann/barrnap) and aligned with MUSCLE5. DsrAB protein reference sequences were retrieved from previous studies [3, 34] and aligned similarly. All alignments were filtered using trimAl to remove columns with ≥ 95% gaps. Gene trees were constructed using IQ-TREE 2 with the same parameters as above. All trees were uploaded to iTOL (Version 7) [28] for visualization and formatting.

To explore Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota viruses and the auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) they encode, publicly available metagenomic datasets, from which MAGs of this phylum were reconstructed, were downloaded from the NCBI SRA database (Table S2). The metagenomes were then processed using the ViWrap (version 1.3.0) [58] pipeline to identify, bin, classify, and predict viral-host relationships for viruses. Briefly, metagenomic clean reads and assemblies were used as input to identify viral scaffolds through the combined use of VIBRANT (version 1.2.1) [25] and VirSorter2 (version 2.2.3) [16]. vRhyme (version 1.1.0) [24] was then used to bin viral genomes based on scaffold coverage, and CheckV (version 1.0.1) [35] was employed to evaluate the genome quality of all identified viruses. Viral genomes were dereplicated into genus-level and species-level clusters using vConTACT2 (version 0.11.0) [4] and dRep (version 3.4.0) [38], respectively. Taxonomic classification of viral genomes was performed within the ViWrap framework using its built-in modules, which incorporate protein-level searches against the NCBI RefSeq viral protein database and HMM marker proteins from the VOG database. Subsequently, viral ORFs were functionally annotated through combined searches against the Pfam, KEGG, and VOG databases to identify AMGs. Finally, iPHoP (version 1.3.3) [43] was employed to predict hosts of the viruses, and those with Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota as host were selected for further analysis (Table S2).

Results and discussion

Discovery and phylogenetic characterization of "Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota"

In this study, approximately 35 Gbp of metagenomic data were generated for the massive sulfides sample. Metagenome assembly and genome binning yielded several prokaryotic MAGs, among which the lineage Ca. Acidulodesulfobacterales (H_13), with a relative abundance of 19.1% (Fig. S1), was selected for further analysis. Combined with 167 MAGs retrieved from public databases, a total of 45 non-redundant representative genomes of this lineage were obtained based on the 95% ANI threshold (Fig. 1a). Phylogenomic analysis based on 120 conserved single-copy marker genes revealed that the forty-five MAGs formed a well-supported clade, with other newly proposed phyla as sister lineages (Fig. 1b). Additionally, 16S rRNA gene phylogenetic analysis also demonstrated that they constitute a monophyletic unit distinct from other phyla (Fig. S2). Therefore, we suggest that they should not be classified within the Deltaproteobacteria but instead represent a distinct phylum-level bacterial lineage. In line with recent changes in prokaryotic nomenclature rules [39], we propose the name “Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota” for this phylum, derived from the genus Ca. Acidulodesulfobacterium [40, 46].

A recent study demonstrated that well-sampled phyla exhibit AAI thresholds of approximately 45 and 50% for delineating different classes and orders, respectively [14]. To further resolve the internal phylogenetic structure of Ca Acidulodesulfobacteriota, we performed pairwise AAI comparisons among the 45 MAGs. The resulting AAI matrix revealed a minimum pairwise identity of 47.33%, with the genomes clustering into six distinct groups separated by AAI values around 50% (Fig. S3). Notably, these six groups corresponded closely to the six phylogenetic clades identified in the phylogenomic tree. Based on these results, we propose that Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota currently comprises a single class, Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriia, and six distinct orders. Four of these are designated as Ca. Acididesulfobacterales, Ca. Acidulodesulfobacterales (originally proposed by Tan et al. [46]), Ca. Hydrothermoacidobacterales, and Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriales. The remaining two orders remain unnamed, as each is represented by only a single MAG (Table S4; Fig. 1b). All proposed names at the phylum, class, and order levels have been submitted to the SeqCode Registry (https://seqco.de/; [18]) to facilitate future validation and taxonomic indexing of these names in association with their corresponding MAGs.

General features of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota MAGs

The basic genomic characteristics of the 45 representative genomes of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota were summarized in Table S1. Their genome sizes ranged from 1.0 to 3.0 Mb, with estimated completeness between 54.7 and 93.7% and contamination levels below 4.4%. The GC content of these MAGs ranged from 29.0 to 43.5% and nearly half of the MAGs contained detectable 16S rRNA genes. The ANI pairwise comparison values between these MAGs were calculated, ranging from 73.9 to 94.2% (Fig. S4). Of the 45 MAGs, the majority were reconstructed from metagenomes associated with acid mine drainage (AMD) environments, while only two were derived from metagenomes of inactive hydrothermal vents (Fig. 1). Despite their small number, these hydrothermal vent-associated MAGs formed a distinct phylogenetic order, suggesting a unique evolutionary trajectory for lineages inhabiting hydrothermal environments. The predicted OGTs of the 45 MAGs ranged from 37.9 to 47.2 °C (Table S5), indicating that this lineage was mesophilic. These findings are consistent with previous studies that revealed the widespread presence and high abundance of this uncultivated lineage in 16S rRNA gene clone libraries from AMD ecosystems and inactive hydrothermal sulfide chimneys in the deep sea [6, 45, 54], which are known as sulfur-rich environments. These observations suggest that this lineage occupies a specialized ecological niche adapted to these environments.

Metabolic potential of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota

The metabolic repertoires of the 45 representative genomes of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota were detailed in Fig. 2 and Table S6. Consistent with the findings of the previous study [46], most MAGs across the six proposed orders of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota encoded nearly complete pathways for carbon fixation (rTCA), glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, and pyruvate metabolism. In addition, these genomes possessed most of the genes required for oxidative phosphorylation, lactate and acetate fermentation, as well as a diverse array of carbohydrate-active enzymes (Table S6). For ethanol fermentation, the order Ca. Acididesulfobacterales harbored a complete pathway, encoding both alcohol dehydrogenase (adhP) and aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH). In contrast, order_3 lacked this pathway entirely, while most MAGs from the remaining orders possessed only partial pathways, predominantly encoding alcohol dehydrogenase (adhP) alone. Order_3 lacked the genetic potential for nitrogen fixation, as no key nitrogenase structural genes (nifHDK) were detected. In contrast, all five remaining orders harbored a complete set of these genes, indicating that nitrogen fixation is likely a conserved and ecologically significant trait across most lineages of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota. Urea metabolism, however, was detected in only a single MAG from each of Ca. Acididesulfobacterales and Ca. Acidulodesulfobacterales, suggesting it represents a strain-specific rather than lineage-wide feature. The co-occurrence of Ni–Fe hydrogenases and iron oxidation pathways across multiple orders suggested an adaptive strategy for energy acquisition, allowing Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota to flexibly exploit alternative electron donors under dynamic environmental conditions. Heavy metal resistance in this phylum was primarily mediated through metal ion efflux systems, including arsenic (arsB), copper (cusAB), and cobalt-zinc-cadmium (czcD) transporters, as well as reduction-based detoxification pathways, such as arsenate reductase (arsC) and mercuric reductase (merA). These genes were broadly distributed across most orders, except order_1, which lacked arsC and cusAB. Collectively, these findings suggest that Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota may adopt a mixotrophic lifestyle, which could enable these microorganisms to persist under nutrient-limited conditions and withstand heavy metal stress [19].

Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota exhibited metabolic versatility in sulfur metabolism (Figs. 2, 3; Table S6), potentially representing an adaptive strategy for thriving in sulfur-rich environments. The orders Ca. Acididesulfobacterales, Ca. Hydrothermoacidobacterales, and Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriales harbored key genes associated with a partial assimilatory sulfate reduction pathway, supporting their potential to reduce sulfate to sulfite. Moreover, the presence of soxA, soxB, soxY, and soxZ genes in the orders Ca. Acididesulfobacterales, Ca. Acidulodesulfobacterales, and Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriales suggested a potential for thiosulfate oxidation to elemental sulfur via a partial SOX system lacking soxCD genes [17]. In contrast, the orders Ca. Hydrothermoacidobacterales, order_1, and order_3 possessed only a subset or entirely lack the aforementioned key genes, indicating a likely loss or absence of this metabolic capability in these lineages. Furthermore, the key genes dsrAB, associated with the dissimilatory sulfite/sulfate reduction pathway, were present across all six proposed orders of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota. Interestingly, the concatenated DsrAB protein tree revealed that the dsrAB genes from Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota and Desulfurella, as sister lineages, formed a well-supported monophyletic evolutionary clade (Fig. 3a). Correspondingly, enzyme types associated with Dsr-dependent dissimilatory sulfur metabolism, as predicted by DiSCo (version 1.0) [37], indicated that the DsrAB proteins of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota and Desulfurella were of the reductive disproportionation type (Fig. 3b, Fig. S5). This finding aligned with a previous study that demonstrated some isolated Desulfurella species were capable of disproportionating sulfur compounds [11]. Additionally, our DsrAB protein phylogeny was consistent with previous studies showing that known sulfur-disproportionating microorganisms (e.g., Desulfurivibrio alkaliphilus) could not be phylogenetically distinguished from bacteria harboring reductive-type DsrAB proteins. [31, 48]. Therefore, we proposed that Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota might represent a lineage capable of sulfur disproportionation, and labeled their DsrAB proteins as the reductive bacterial disproportionation type. Since the mechanism and the operative direction of the Dsr pathway in known sulfur-disproportionating microbes is still not fully understood [36, 48], future efforts, such as isolating pure cultures of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota, are needed to further validate and elucidate its sulfur disproportionation mechanism. Moreover, a recent study demonstrated that microorganisms encoding both reductive and oxidative Dsr proteins were constrained to a few phyla, including SAR324, Nitrospinota, Nitrospirota, Actinomycetota, and CG2-30–53-67. In these lineages, DsrAB sequences were phylogenetically positioned at the interface between canonical oxidative and reductive bacterial clades, and were thought to be associated with the evolution and origins of Dsr-mediated sulfur oxidation [26]. In the present study, the newly proposed phylum Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota exhibited a comparable mixed Dsr pathway, combining components of both reductive-type proteins (Sat-AprAB-Qmo cascade & DsrD) and oxidative-type proteins (DsrCMKEFH). Phylogenetic analyses further revealed that DsrAB proteins from this lineage also occupied an intermediate position between well-defined oxidative and reductive clades. Taken together, these findings suggested that Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota, like the aforementioned phyla, might represent a transitional lineage in the evolution from reductive to oxidative Dsr metabolism.

Potential contribution of AMGs to the biogeochemical cycling of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota

AMGs are virus-encoded genes acquired from their hosts via horizontal gene transfer, and are thought to enhance the metabolism of infected host cells [1, 47]. To explore the ecological roles of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota viruses, 16 publicly available metagenomic datasets from AMD ecosystems were used to identify viral contigs and analyze AMGs (Table S2). From the assembled contigs, 160 viral scaffolds or genomes (VSGs) with Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota as the host were recovered. Taxonomically, 124 VSGs were assigned to Caudoviricetes within Duplodnaviria, while 5 and 7 VSGs were assigned to Tectiliviricetes and Megaviricetes within Varidnaviria, respectively. However, 24 VSGs could not be taxonomically classified, which may be due to both the genetic novelty of these viruses and the current limitations of viral genomic databases and classification tools, especially for viruses recovered from under-explored environments such as AMD. Then, the above VSGs were screened for AMGs that might influence the metabolism of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota. Overall, 64 ORFs encoded by 31 VSGs were identified as AMGs and categorized into five main specific KEGG metabolic pathways: carbohydrate metabolism (10), energy metabolism (2), lipid metabolism (2), amino acid metabolism (23), and metabolism of cofactors and vitamins (27) (Fig. 4 and Table S7).

AMGs involved in cysteine and methionine metabolism, as well as folate biosynthesis, were the most common, each comprising 15 annotated genes (Fig. 4b). The most prevalent AMGs involved in cysteine and methionine metabolism were those encoding DNA methyltransferase (DNMT1), which were identified in 13 VSGs. DNMT1 plays a critical role in viral immune evasion by methylating viral DNA to bypass bacterial restriction-modification (RM) systems [53]. Its high prevalence suggests a widespread viral anti-defense mechanism in the interaction between viruses and their host, Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota. Besides, previous studies have demonstrated that folate biosynthesis shares a common step with the de novo synthesis pathway of queuosine (Que) during tRNA biogenesis, and AMGs encoding folE and Que biosynthesis proteins (queC, queD, queE, and queF) may contribute to tRNA biogenesis by producing hypermodified guanosine incorporated into tRNAs [55]. Caudoviricetes bin_93 contained a partial Que biosynthesis pathway, encoding folE, queD, queE, and queC (Fig. 4a), suggesting its potential regulatory role in the tRNA biogenesis of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota. Moreover, AMGs were predicted to modulate the biochemical cycling of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota. For instance, a virus (bin_17) classified within the class Tectiliviricetes carried the cysD gene, which may reprogram the assimilatory sulfate reduction of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota.

Conclusions

Our study presents a comprehensive investigation into the evolutionary and functional landscape of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota. By combining newly reconstructed and publicly available genomes, we established this lineage as a phylum-level clade, currently comprising one class and six orders. Key metabolic pathways, including carbon fixation, nitrogen fixation, and sulfur metabolism, underscore the ecological significance of this lineage in sulfur-rich environments. The evolutionary history of dissimilatory sulfite reductase and the associated enzyme types suggest that this lineage may be associated with the evolution and origins of Dsr-mediated sulfur oxidation. Additionally, the discovery of diverse viral auxiliary metabolic genes highlights the role of viruses in shaping the metabolic capabilities of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota, particularly in five specific KEGG metabolic pathways: carbohydrate metabolism, energy metabolism, lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and the metabolism of cofactors and vitamins. Future efforts, such as isolating pure cultures and experimentally validating metabolic functions, are crucial for further elucidating the ecological and evolutionary dynamics of Ca. Acidulodesulfobacteriota.
