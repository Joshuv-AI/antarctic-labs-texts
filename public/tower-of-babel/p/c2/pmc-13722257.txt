Recurrent giant cell tumor of the right distal tibia: A case report
Authors: Nisha D. Barole, Kuntal P. Godde, Vaishnavi D. Rajurkar, Shubhangi P. Raut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGiant cell tumors (GCTs) of the bone are neoplasms that are characterized by benign, locally aggressive behavior and occur most commonly in the young population. It is usually located at epiphyseal areas of bones, especially distal femur and proximal tibia. This paper presents a 20-year-old female, who presented with a malignant recurrence of GCT in the right distal tibia which had initially been treated by curettage with cement and bone grafting. The patient managed in August 2021 and came back in 2023 with the symptoms of recurrence manifested by swelling, pain, and functional limitation of the right ankle. Curettage and cement augmentation surgery were accomplished successfully. There were no complications in the postoperative course, and the patient reported symptom improvement and increased functional abilities at follow-up. It discusses the complexities of the management of recurrent GCTs, while stressing the importance of detailed planning of surgeries, intraoperative and postoperative multidisciplinary management, and long-term follow-up. It also addresses modern management styles: Adjuvants and the role of imaging in detecting recurrences. If it is handled well, those challenges can help enhance the end result in patients with recurrent GCTs, especially in sensitive areas such as the distal tibia.

Introduction

Giant cell tumors (GCTs) of the bone are rare tumors, constituting 4–5% of all primary bone tumors and 20% of benign bone tumors. These tumors are more common in individuals between the ages of 20 and 40 years, tended toward females. Similar to some other histologic types of cancer, GCTs are characterized by their locally invasive nature and the ability to metastasize and destroy bones and soft tissues.[1] GCT usually affects the physical growth plates of long bones: the distal femur, proximal tibia, and distal radius. GCT involvement of the distal tibia is a relatively rare area affected; it comprises less than 4% of all GCTs.[2] The clinical manifestation of GCTs includes local tenderness, mass, and functional limitation of the affected area. Some of the very complex may present complications such as involvement of joints, fractures, or the infiltration of other soft tissues.[3] GCTs may be simply defined by the presence of multinucleated giant cells and mononuclear stromal cells. The outcome analysis clearly shows that the chances of recurrence are measured in as 15–50% with variations influenced by the surgical techniques plus adjuvants employed.[4] Current management of GCTs is also by surgical removal through curettage, possibly with phenol or freezing treatment or cementation.[5] The cause of recurrence may be attributed to residual tumor, absence of adjuvant treatment, and high tumor aggressiveness. Recurrence is likely to require further surgical intervention, a procedure usually fraught with greater technical challenges, higher mortality, and more postoperative complications.[6]

Case History

A 20-year-old female came to the clinic complaining of right-side ankle pain and swelling that affected her ability to walk for the past 1 year. From the record, she had been diagnosed with a GCT of the distal tibia in August of the previous year. The patient continued to demonstrate signs of the disease in response to the treatment throughout follow-up visits, including complaints of pain and inability to use the affected limb adequately.

In the follow-up, due to persistent pain and upon subsequent imaging, recurrent tumor growth became apparent, and implant removal surgery was performed in October 2023, around 2 years after the implantation. Biochemical and hematological profiling performed before the surgery showed renal function to be within normal limits with serum creatinine at 0.55mg/dL. LFT, including total bilirubin, and SGOT/SGPT were also normal, and blood grouping revealed B-positive; two units of PRC were on standby for possible intraoperative transfusion. MR imaging of the right ankle and foot in the enhanced mode revealed suspicion of a recurrent lesion in the distal tibia. The patient had the gross pathological tissue [shown in Figure 1a] removed by curettage along with the recurrence of the lesion, bone grafting, and plating for structural reinforcement. The purpose of the surgery was to remove the tumor in its entirety while leaving functionality of the limb intact. During surgery [surgical site shown in Figure 1b], great care was taken to remove all the unhealthy tissue around the lesion to reduce the chances of further tumor reoccurrence. Surgical management was carried out as planned, and the patient had a smooth course of this operative procedure. Patients were observed for postoperative recovery and management of the operated site to prevent infection and promote limb function.

(a) Open surgical site with soft tissue manipulation for repair. (b) Metallic screw, indicating an orthopedic procedure

Patient counseling was done in detail to explain the patient her condition, possibility of relapse, and need for her to make follow-up visits. While being seen on her last follow-up, she had better mobility; there were no clinical features or radiological findings of any recurrence. She is still followed up every now and then with a view to stabilizing her disease and achieving optimal functioning.

Discussion

Recurrent GCT of the bone is one of the periosteal tumors of STS histological subtypes, which is characterized by its locally invasive nature and high propensity to recur locally, especially when occurring in the distal tibia.[6] This made it to be recurrent and needed to be treated through curettage, bone grafting, and cementing in other sessions, thus underlining the fact that single-stage treatment could not completely solve the issue with this type of tumor.[7] The combination of bone grafting with cementing offered stability as a primary stiffener in form entry while offering a theoretical possibility of graft integration into the host bone.[8] This case also demonstrates the need for a follow-up in as much as it shows that recurrences are usually detected early, the presence of the recurrence was tackled immediately, and, therefore, there would be no other problems. A previously unrecognized minimal recurrent disease that might have otherwise have been overlooked at conventional investigations.[9]
