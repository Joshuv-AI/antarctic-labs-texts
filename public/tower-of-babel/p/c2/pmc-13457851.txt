Clustering metabolic and behavioral risk factors for hypertension: evidence from a longitudinal cohort in western Iran
Authors: Maryam Dezhkameh, Mehdi Moradinazar, Farid Najafi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypertension (HTN) is a major modifiable risk factor for non-communicable diseases, shaped by both metabolic and behavioral determinants. This study investigated the combined impact of key lifestyle and clinical variables on HTN incidence using a clustering approach within the Ravansar Non-Communicable Disease (RaNCD) Cohort. A total of 8082 adults aged 35–65 years from the RaNCD follow-up phase were included. Cluster analysis was performed using four variables body mass index (BMI), dyslipidemia, dietary inflammatory index (DII), and smoking status to identify distinct risk profiles. Associations between clusters and HTN incidence were assessed primarily using logistic regression. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by age and sex. Nelson–Aalen cumulative hazard estimates were calculated. Over a median follow-up of 9.3 (IQR 8.6–9.8) years, totaling 70977.024 person-years 1171 new cases of HTN were identified corresponding to an incidence rate of 16.50 per 1000 person- years. Three clusters emerged: Cluster 1 (Low Risk), Cluster 2 (Smokers), and Cluster 3 (Dyslipidemia–Overweight). Adjusted models revealed significantly higher odds of HTN in Cluster 3 (OR = 1.17; 95% ci 1.02–1.35) and Cluster 2 (OR = 1.27; 95% CI 1.01–1.59) compared to Cluster 1. Age-stratified sensitivity analysis confirmed the stability of these associations, while gender-stratified results showed significance only among men. Individuals with clustered metabolic and behavioral risk factors particularly smoking, overweight, and dyslipidemia are at elevated risk for developing HTN whereas DII distribution did not meaningfully differentiate risk across clusters. Introduction

Hypertension (HTN) is a common chronic condition and a major risk factor for cardiovascular diseases1. The global prevalence of HTN has doubled from 650 million in 1990 to 1.3 billion in 20192. It is estimated that HTN is responsible for 10.8 million deaths, and contributes to 9.3% of Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs)3. Although its prevalence has decreased in high-income countries, it is increasing in low- and middle-income countries, which account for more than two-thirds of global cases4,5.

HTN is largely influenced by lifestyle behaviors such as diet, physical activity, smoking, alcohol consumption and obesity. The relationship between each of these health-related behaviors and the incidence of HTN has been examined separately in previous studies. For example, a meta-analysis showed that adhering to a Mediterranean diet for at least one year lowered systolic blood pressure (SBP) by 1.44 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) by 0.7 mmHg6. Another study reported that each 5-unit increase in body mass index (BMI) was associated with a 1.27-fold higher risk of developing HTN7. Although such studies provide important evidence, most studies have been conducted in Western populations or have focused on predefined risk constructs, and limited evidence exists regarding the combined effects of metabolic and behavioral determinants in specific ethnic and regional populations, particularly among the Kurdish population in western Iran8.

Statistical methods such as cluster analysis offer a suitable approach to classify individuals into distinct groups based on common behavioral and clinical patterns, thereby identifying specific risk patterns.

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to investigate the association between a set of metabolic and behavioral risk factors, including Body Mass Index (BMI), dyslipidemia, dietary inflammatory index (DII), and smoking, with HTN using a clustering method to identify groups at risk of developing HTN. Although other factors also play a role in the development of HTN, these variables were selected because they represent distinct and complementary biological pathways in the development of HTN, including mechanisms related to obesity, metabolic disorders, diet-induced inflammation, and the direct vascular effects of smoking. Other lifestyle factors such as physical activity and alcohol consumption, although important determinants of hypertension, were not included in the clustering method due to potential measurement error, cultural underreporting (especially for alcohol consumption), and overlap with the selected metabolic indices, which could reduce cluster stability. This approach allowed the identification of meaningful and interpretable risk profiles related to the sociocultural context of western Iran.

Methods

Study design and populations

The data for the present study were obtained from the RaNCD cohort, a subset of the PERSIAN (Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran) project. This is the first cohort study in the Kurdish population of Ravansar province, western Iran, which began in 2014 with 10,047 adults aged 35–65 years9,10. Further details about the cohort study have been published elsewhere11.

Data on the incidence of HTN were collected during the follow-up phase, which began in 2016 and is ongoing, with the eighth phase now in 2025. The median follow-up time per participant was 9.3 (IQR 8.6–9.8), yielding a total of 70,977.024 person-years of follow-up.

Non-attendance at follow-up was anticipated due to participants’ unwillingness to continue, migration from the study area, unavailability for follow-up, inability to accurately recall information, or providing inaccurate self-reports. To minimize these issues, the cohort team employed several strategies, summarized as follows:

Counsel participants on the benefits of continued participation, including access to medical services, early detection and management of chronic diseases, and preventive recommendations for events such as cardiovascular disease.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants who were missing data on variables used for clustering or key covariates (approximately 2.49%) were excluded before applying the predefined exclusion criteria. Beyond the initial inclusion and exclusion criteria, individuals with a history of HTN or those receiving antihypertensive treatment (n = 1515), patients diagnosed with cancer (n = 65), and pregnant women (n = 135) were excluded. Ultimately, 8082 eligible subjects were included in the final analysis. The flow diagram of the study participants is shown in Fig. 1.

Quality control and data collection

All data collection procedures in the RaNCD cohort were conducted under strict quality control protocols to ensure validity, reliability, and reproducibility. Trained personnel underwent standardized instruction and certification prior to fieldwork, and periodic retraining sessions were held to minimize inter-observer variability. Data were collected using digital checklists and structured questionnaires. All data were entered electronically with built-in logic checks and range validations. A random 10% of records were re-audited by independent supervisors to ensure data integrity. Clustering was performed on participants with complete data for the four selected clustering variables (BMI, DII, dyslipidemia, and smoking), which had no missing values. Variables removed during preprocessing (e.g.,T2DM, SES) and exclusion criteria were applied prior to clustering. Although no formal comparison of baseline characteristics was made between included and excluded participants, the low proportion of missing data suggests that the impact on our results is likely to be minimal.

Follow‑up measurements

According to the cohort protocol, during the follow-up phase, all participants were interviewed by telephone annually and also following the occurrence of the desired outcome. Follow-up was conducted in two ways: active and passive. In active follow-up, each participant is contacted annually, and if necessary, a physician from the study center conducts a visit. Passive follow-up involves receiving self-reports (whenever participants visit the center to report an event) and annual reports from the disease and mortality registry. Finally, confirmation of the desired outcome is achieved by reviewing medical records and verifying with two physicians at the cohort center11.

Selection rationale for clustering variables

To ensure meaningful and interpretable clustering, we selected variables based on their established relevance to HTN risk in prior epidemiological studies. The chosen variables represent both metabolic and behavioral domains, capturing multidimensional aspects of lifestyle and physiological health. This approach allows for the identification of distinct risk profiles rather than isolated predictors. Variables were screened for completeness, distribution, and clinical significance, ensuring that each contributed uniquely to the clustering structure without redundancy. We selected four key variables representing both metabolic and behavioral domains:

BMI: Height and weight were measured using calibrated digital instruments, and BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²), serving as a standard indicator of adiposity13.

Dietary inflammatory index (DII): dietary inflammatory index (DII) calculation

The DII was calculated using the standardized 118-item food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) of the RaNCD study, which assessed participants’ usual dietary intake over the past year. The calculation followed the method described by Shivappa et al., in which 45 nutrients, including macronutrients, micronutrients, and flavonoids, were linked to inflammatory markers such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, and CRP. In the present study, data were available for 31 of the 45 nutrients.

To calculate the DII, each participant’s intake of a nutrient was subtracted from the global mean intake of that nutrient and divided by its global standard deviation to generate a z-score. To reduce right skewness, the z-score was converted to a percentile score, which was then centered by multiplying by 2 and subtracting 1. The centered percentile score was multiplied by the inflammatory effect score of each nutrient. Finally, the total DII score for each participant was obtained by summing the scores across all available nutrients. Higher DII scores indicate a more pro-inflammatory diet, whereas lower (negative) scores indicate a more anti-inflammatory diet.

The DII was calculated as the energy-adjusted version (E-DII) using the residual method, standardized to a global reference database. Participants were then categorized into quartiles (Q1–Q4) for analysis14,15.

Dyslipidemia: Dyslipidemia was defined based on standard cut-offs commonly used in epidemiological studies (e.g., LDL-C ≥ 160 mg/dL, TG ≥ 200 mg/dL, TC ≥ 240 mg/dL, HDL-C < 40 mg/dL or use of lipid-lowering medication16,17.

Smoking status

Participants who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime were considered smokers; those who had smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes or were only exposed to passive smoking were considered non-smokers., following RaNCD protocol definitions18.

Outcome and confounder variables

New cases of HTN were identified through both passive and active follow-up procedures. In passive follow-up, participants were contacted and asked about a history of hypertension. Participants who reported having hypertension were subsequently invited to the cohort center for further evaluation and were asked to provide relevant medical documents for verification of the diagnosis.

In active follow-up, participants attended the cohort center in person. Blood pressure was measured according to the cohort protocol, using the same procedure as at baseline. After at least 10 min of seated rest, systolic and diastolic blood pressure were measured using a Richter sphygmomanometer in both the left and right arms, and the mean of the measurements was recorded. Participants with systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg underwent further clinical evaluation.

In both follow-up approaches, the final diagnosis of hypertension was confirmed through review of medical records and additional examinations conducted by two internal medicine specialists, based on ICD-10 and ICD-11 codes11,19.

According to the RaNCD study protocol, individuals with a history of hospitalization, treatment due to conditions such as stroke, myocardial infarction, coronary artery disease, or use of CVD medications were considered patients with CVD16.

Individuals with a fast plasma glucose test 126 ≥ mg/dl or taking blood sugar-lowering medications at the beginning of the study were considered to have diabetes after confirmation by the RaNCD center physicians20.

Fatty liver was assessed using the Fatty Liver Index (FLI). This index was calculated by Bedogni et al. in 200621. The range of this index is between 0 and 100, and accordingly, FLI less than 60 is considered as not having fatty liver, and FLI higher than 60 is considered as having fatty liver22.

SES status was examined using three items, including education level, place of residence, and amenities, using the Principal component analysis method (PCA). The variables TV, freezer, smartphone, vacuum cleaner, dishwashing machine, motorcycle, car, laptop, per capita room, housing area, kind of house ownership, and type of kitchen were entered into the PCA method. Based on this method, a weight was assigned to each variable, and then based on the sum of the weights of each variable, the final score of SES status was calculated for each participant. Finally, SES level was classified into 5 groups (the poorest, poor, middle, rich, and the richest)23,24.

Statistical analysis

To explore the relationship between clustered risk profiles and HTN incidence, we employed a multi-step analytical approach. First, quantitative variables (BMI and DII) were standardized using z-scores to account for differences in scale and distribution, while binary variables (dyslipidemia and smoking) were coded as 0 and 1. Clustering was performed using the k-means algorithm, a distance-based method that assigns individuals to groups based on Euclidean proximity to cluster centroids25,26. K-means clustering was performed using random initialization with 25 random starts (k-means function in R, nstart = 25). A fixed random seed (set.seed (123)) was used to ensure full repeatability.

The optimal number of clusters was initially assessed using the silhouette coefficient and within-cluster sum of squares (WSS; elbow method) plots in R. To further ensure the optimal number of clusters, the NbClust package in R was used, which evaluates multiple clustering indices and suggested the same solution within the range of k = 2 to 8. All methods consistently indicated the same optimal number of clusters.

The validity of the final clustering solution was assessed using the silhouette coefficient (0.38), Calinski–Harabasz index (3718.046), and Davies–Bouldin index (1.090). Robustness was evaluated by repeating the clustering on a 50% random subsample using the same number of clusters and variable scaling, yielding a high Adjusted Rand Index (ARI = 0.9796).

Associations between clusters and incident HTN were examined using both crude and adjusted logistic regression models. Adjustments included demographic and clinical covariates: age, gender, residence type, marital status, education level, SES, T2DM, and fatty liver status. T2DM and fatty liver were considered potential mediators on the causal pathway between metabolic/behavioral clusters and HTN; adjusting for them allows estimation of the direct effect of clusters on HTN, while other covariates were included to control for confounding. The stability of the association of clusters with the incidence of HTN was examined using sensitivity analysis based on age and gender Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. To assess time-to-event data, Cox proportional hazards models were applied, with study entry defined as the start of follow-up and HTN onset as the event. The proportional hazards (PH) assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals (Stata stphtest). Results showed that PH assumption was satisfied for the main exposure (clusters; p = 0.668), whereas some covariates (age and residence type) violated the assumption (p < 0.05). Therefore, hazard ratios for clusters can be interpreted as valid over time, while results for these covariates should be interpreted with caution. Predicted values base on cox model were generated across the observed age range separately for each cluster. These analyses were intended as sensitivity and descriptive assessments rather than formal interaction testing. Cumulative risk was illustrated using Nelson–Aalen cumulative hazard plots. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.0.

Ethical consideration: The current study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the commencement of the study. The Ethics Committee of Kermanshah University, within the Department of Research and Technology, approved this study (KUMS.REC.1394.318).

Result: A total of 8082 participants were included in the final analysis. During the follow-up, 1171 participants developed HTN, yielding an incidence rate of 16.50 per 1000 person-years of follow-up. Most participants were female (51.3%), aged 35–45 years (52.5%), married (89.9%), urban residents (59.9%), and of rich socioeconomic status (21.03%), with a notable illiteracy rate of 42.18%. Cluster analysis identified three distinct subgroups based on combined metabolic and behavioral risk profiles. Participants in Cluster 1 (Low-risk) were characterized by a mean BMI of 27.1, no smokers, no dyslipidemia, and a mean DII of −2.42, with the lowest exposure to modifiable risk factors and an incidence of HTN 13.65%. Cluster 2 (Smokers) had a mean BMI of 25.4, all participants were smokers, approximately 56% had dyslipidemia, and a mean DII of −2.22, with a slightly lower incidence of HTN (13.24%). Cluster 3 (Dyslipidemia–Overweight) exhibited a mean BMI of 28.2, no smokers, 100% dyslipidemia, and a mean DII of −2.20, with the highest incidence of HTN (16.12%) (Table 1).

Sensitivity analyses stratified by age and sex supported the robustness of the main findings. In sex-stratified logistic regression analyses, cluster membership was significantly associated with incident HTN among men, with higher odds observed for Cluster 2 (OR = 1.36; 95% CI 1.04–1.78) and Cluster 3 (OR = 1.34; 95% CI 1.05–1.71) compared with the low-risk cluster. In contrast, no statistically significant associations were observed among women (Supplementary Table 1). In age-stratified analyses, Cluster 3 (dyslipidemia–overweight pattern) consistently showed the highest odds of HTN across age categories, indicating a persistent metabolic risk pattern (Supplementary Tables 1, 2).

An additional sensitivity analysis, Cox proportional hazards models were applied to incorporate time-to-event information and evaluate whether the associations between clusters and incident HTN were sensitive to the choice of statistical model. In the Cox models, cluster membership was not significantly associated with HTN after accounting for variables, suggesting that the associations observed in logistic regression primarily reflect differences in cumulative risk rather than differences in the timing of HTN onset (Supplementary Table 3). Discussion

In the present study, we examined the association of combined metabolic and behavioral risk factors, including BMI, dyslipidemia, DII, and smoking, with the incidence of HTN using a clustering approach. Three distinct clusters were identified. In the primary analysis based on multiple logistic regression, Cluster 2 characterized by overweight, moderate dyslipidemia, low DII score, and smoking showed the highest odds of incident HTN compared with the low-risk cluster, followed by Cluster 3, which was characterized by overweight and high dyslipidemia with a low DII score but without smoking. Age-stratified sensitivity analyses yielded associations consistent with the main findings, whereas sex-stratified analyses indicated that statistically significant associations were observed mainly among men. Nelson–Aalen cumulative hazard curves were used to descriptively illustrate time-to-event patterns across clusters; however, Cox proportional hazards models did not show statistically significant differences between clusters. Therefore, these survival patterns should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory.

These findings are consistent with previous research. Cherfan et al. reported that a combination of high-risk behaviors like heavy drinking, physical inactivity, poor diet, and overweight/obesity increases the risk of developing HTN27. Similarly, Mohseni et al. found that the interaction between dyslipidemia and obesity is associated with higher HTN risk28.

These findings align with established pathophysiological pathways of risk factors linked to HTN. Obesity contribute to HTN through mechanisms involving the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS). Increased adipose tissue, especially in the visceral region, leads to SNS activation, resulting in heightened sodium reabsorption from the kidneys. Conversely, in the RAAS, angiotensin II, a vasoconstrictor peptide, narrows blood vessels and stimulates the hormone aldosterone, which increases renal blood flow and enhances the return of blood to the heart. Consequently, the heart pumps blood at a faster rate, leading to increased HTN29–31. Therefore, given the strong link between overweight, obesity, and HTN, it is crucial to focus on its prevention.

Another significant clustered risk factor is dyslipidemia, which is influenced by dietary patterns and BMI. Dyslipidemia, characterized by an imbalance of blood serum lipids, poses a serious risk for CVD alongside HTN. This condition, coupled with endothelial damage, results in decreased nitric oxide secretion, leading to increased vasoconstriction, reduced vascular elasticity, and ultimately elevated blood pressure32. Numerous studies have established a connection between lipid profiles and the incidence of HTN. For instance, the findings of the study by Otsuka et al. indicated that higher levels of TC, LDLC, and non-HDLC correlate with an increased risk of HTN33. In our study, only cluster 3 exhibited high levels of dyslipidemia, while other factors remained at normal levels, except for BMI. This underscores the synergistic significance of dyslipidemia and overweight/obesity in the development of HTN.

Another important risk factor is smoking, which is linked to many diseases, including respiratory diseases, CVD, diabetes, and cancers34. According to studies, nicotine in cigarettes can contribute to increased HTN by damaging the endothelium and resulting in decreased nitric acid, increased vasoconstriction, and increased peripheral resistance35.

Evidence for a direct link between smoking and the incidence of HTN is conflicting. For example, Kaplan’s study showed that current smokers with a 5-pack-year history of smoking had higher risk of developing HTN compared with nonsmokers35. While Eun Park et al. reported in their study that there was no significant difference in the risk of developing HTN between current smokers and non-smokers36. In our study, only participants in cluster 2 were smokers and exhibited a higher risk for HTN, this pattern suggests that smoking may lead to a high-risk HTN phenotype even when other risk factors, such as dyslipidemia and BMI, are at moderate levels.

The results indicated a significant association between increasing age and a higher risk of HTN. Previous studies have indicated that SBP rises with age while DBP declines with the onset of middle age. These changes are clinically significant because HTN at an older age can lead to more severe complications than in younger individuals. The underlying mechanism for age-related changes in HTN is linked to alterations in the vascular structure of the heart. As people age, the collagen structure of the vessels replaces the elastic structure, leading to arterial stiffening and ultimately HTN37,38. Therefore, adherence to healthy behaviors from an earlier age is advised.

In the current study, the results of the sensitivity analysis in men were consistent regarding the occurrence of HTN in clusters39. Previous studies have also highlighted gender differences in the incidence of HTN. Kumar and colleagues reported that before the age of 50, the risk of developing HTN is similar for both sexes; however, after that age, women are at a higher risk, potentially due to hormonal changes and the decline in estrogen levels in postmenopausal women40,41. Also, the risk factor of smoking is higher in men, as in our study, the majority of participants in cluster 2 with a higher risk of developing HTN were male and smokers. This can be explained by differences in the prevalence and amount of smoking in men, working conditions, differences in awareness, differences in access to diagnostic and treatment services. Women also had a smaller share in the Cluster 2 population, and this may affect the statistical power and contribute to the lack of statistical significance. In addition, because clustering was performed on a mixed sample, the cluster structure may reflect a male pattern, which can partially explain this finding. Therefore, it is recommended that gender differences be taken into account in educational, screening, and treatment programs.

Strengths and limitations

Our study had several strengths, including a prospective design, a large sample size, simultaneous examination of modifiable behaviors associated with the outcome using a robust method and, to our knowledge, for the first time, and finally identification of distinct groups of behavioral and metabolic risk factors associated with HTN. Our study had several limitations. We examined only four variables (BMI, dyslipidemia, DII, and smoking) in the clusters; other potential risk factors such as physical activity, neuropsychiatric disorders, alcohol consumption, and medication adherence were not assessed. Risk factors were measured once at baseline, so individuals’ lifestyle and metabolic conditions may have changed over the follow-up. Clustering assigns individuals to groups based on similarity and cannot establish causality. Some variables, like smoking, were self-reported, which may introduce information bias. Findings are based on the Kurdish population in western Iran and may not be generalizable to other regions or populations.

Conclusion

Using a robust clustering approach based on BMI, dyslipidemia, dietary inflammatory index, and smoking, we identified three distinct clusters of individuals at risk of HTN: Cluster 1 (Low Risk), Cluster 2 (Smokers), and Cluster 3 (Dyslipidemia–Overweight). During the follow-up period, certain combinations of modifiable risk factors were associated with a higher likelihood of developing HTN, although survival analyses did not demonstrate statistically significant differences between clusters. HTN remains an important risk factor for CVD and contributes to premature death and disability; therefore, public health strategies promoting healthy behaviors, particularly from early life, may be beneficial. These findings may help policymakers identify high-risk groups and inform targeted prevention and screening programs.
