From establishment to network consolidation: Registrant engagement and collaborative development in the international Consortium for Research in Eating Disorders (CoRe-ED)
Authors: Gemma Sharp, Hao Hu, Peter Wigley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEating disorders are a global health concern, yet research in this field has historically been underfunded and sometimes perceived as “niche”. To attempt to address these challenges, the international charity Consortium for Research in Eating Disorders (CoRe-ED) was launched in September 2024. CoRe-ED aims to promote innovations in eating disorders research by empowering all voices and supporting the development of new therapies. The present study examined the characteristics of individuals who joined CoRe-ED over the first ~ 15 months, their engagement with consortium initiatives and their expected benefits and experiences.MethodsBetween 25 September 2024 and 31 December 2025, CoRe-ED registrants completed an online registration form and consented to the use of deidentified, aggregated data for research. Data were analysed for registrant characteristics, including primary country of residence and role(s) (e.g., researcher, health professional, lived experience), and for perceived expectations and experiences, using inductive thematic analysis. Survey feedback from ten CoRe-ED events, engagement with collaborative CoRe-ED initiatives, and an overarching survey capturing overall registrant experiences were also analysed.ResultsA total of 960 individuals from 37 countries across five continents registered with CoRe-ED, representing researchers, health professionals, individuals with lived experience, advocates, not-for-profit representatives and industry professionals. Registrants’ expectations included networking and community building, research contribution and collaboration, learning and professional development, advocacy, facilitation of innovation, integration of lived experience into research, global collaboration and mentorship. CoRe-ED also implemented a structured “Next Big Research Idea” initiative, which involved 18 internationally distributed multidisciplinary teams across 20 countries in collaborative research proposal development. Event-specific and overall feedback indicated high satisfaction with CoRe-ED activities, particularly valuing the diversity of presenters, global perspectives, inclusive environment and opportunities to learn and connect.ConclusionsOver the first ~ 15 months, CoRe-ED engaged a diverse, international registrant group and developed activities consistent with early registrant expectations. The consortium implemented initiatives intended to support cross-disciplinary research collaboration, knowledge exchange and innovation, while expanding international representation. Future research should examine longer-term experiences, including impacts on research outputs, mentorship, co-design and policy translation, to better understand how the consortium evolves in response to the needs of its international community.Keywords: Eating disorders, Consortium, Collaboration, Research, Education, Global, Lived experience, Partners

Background

Eating disorders are complex conditions with far-reaching impacts on individuals, families and communities. Despite this, research in eating disorders has historically been underfunded and often perceived as a “niche” area within psychiatry and the health sector in general [1–4]. Moreover, research initiatives historically included the engagement of only a limited range of groups, with people with lived experience and certain professional sectors often excluded from the design, interpretation and dissemination of research [5]. Although there have been concerted efforts to purposefully involve people with lived experience in eating disorders research in more recent times [6], these gaps have hindered both innovation and the real-world applicability of research findings.

In an effort to attempt to address some of these challenges, the international registered charity Consortium for Research in Eating Disorders (CoRe-ED) was established and officially launched on 25 September 2024 in Melbourne, Australia [7]. CoRe-ED has a global mission to “promote innovations in eating disorders research by empowering all voices and ultimately creating new therapies for all people experiencing eating disorders” [8]. Registration is free of charge and open to researchers, clinicians, advocates, individuals with lived and living experience, industry representatives and partner organisations worldwide. As of the time of publication, partnerships have been established with 51 academic institutions, clinical and healthcare organisations, advocacy groups and technology-focused organisations [8], and promotion has been supported via both mainstream and social media channels.

In an initial 3-month study, we reported on early registrants of CoRe-ED (N = 252) and their expectations, showing engagement across 20 countries (with Australia being the most commonly represented country) and five continents, and also roles, including researchers, clinicians, advocates, individuals with lived experience and industry representatives [9]. Registrants expressed a range of anticipated benefits, including networking and community building, contributing to and collaborating on research, learning and professional development, raising awareness and advocacy, supporting innovation in practice and policy, sharing lived experience, and gaining international perspectives and inspiration. These early findings informed the design and expansion of subsequent CoRe-ED initiatives, including an extensive educational event and networking program for 2025 focused on an “all voices” multidisciplinary approach with cross-country collaborations. Furthermore, we also launched our inaugural “Next Big Research Idea” initiative in July 2025 which allowed CoRe-ED registrants to form multidisciplinary teams, which included lived experience and early career representation, and propose novel research ideas which the teams anticipated could have major impacts on the field of eating disorders research and treatment [8]. CoRe-ED announced the winning team in October 2025 and supported the team with a dedicated research fundraising campaign in early 2026.

The present study expands on the 3-month findings and further progression of CoRe-ED by examining ~ 15 months of data following CoRe-ED’s launch. Beyond exploring who registered and their motivations, we aimed to investigate registrants’ experiences and engagement with multiple CoRe-ED initiatives over this period, including the “Next Big Research Idea” initiative, alongside exploring insights from an overarching feedback survey capturing their overall experiences with the consortium. The study also describes registrant engagement in relation to expectations identified in the earlier 3-month study, including networking, knowledge sharing and international collaboration. Examining engagement and feedback over a longer timeframe may provide insights into the implementation of an international, multidisciplinary consortium model in eating disorders research.

Method

Participants and procedure

Registration

Registrants were people aged 18 and over, located anywhere in the world, who registered for CoRe-ED (free of charge) from 25 September 2024 to 31 December 2025 (~ 15 months of CoRe-ED establishment). Individuals completed a brief online registration form [10], providing a display name, email, primary country of residence, organisation, role (e.g., researcher, clinician, lived experience, where the registrant could nominate more than one role), and their expected experiences or benefits from involvement in CoRe-ED. Only the display name and email were compulsory to access the CoRe-ED registrant portal [11]. Registrants were informed that optional information could be publicly visible to others. As part of registration, registrants also agreed to the CoRe-ED Registrant Code of Conduct and Privacy Policy, which included consent for deidentified, aggregated data to be used for research and publication [10]. Online written agreement to this clause was deemed informed consent for all data collected through CoRe-ED (including event and overall evaluation surveys - see below) through our ethics approval from a Bellberry Limited Human Research Ethics Committee (ID: 2025-01-043).

Event evaluation and overall evaluation

Immediately following each CoRe-ED event (which were free of charge), individuals who had registered for that event were emailed a weblink to complete an anonymous feedback survey in English, hosted on Google Forms. There were ten CoRe-ED hosted events in the study time period: two in-person events in Australia and eight webinars co-hosted with partners in South Korea, Japan, Australia, New Zealand, US, UK, Italy and a global research showcase with Australia, Canada, New Zealand, Italy, Japan, Denmark, South Korea, Costa Rica, Ireland, Mexico and US-based partners involved. Noting that the presenters at each of the ten events/webinars were given the option to present in their native language (if not English) with English translation provided. The vast majority of presenters across the ten events elected to present in English. The feedback survey included the same questions for all ten events and was completed anonymously. The survey collected demographic information (country of residence, role) and quantitative items (overall satisfaction with the event and likelihood of recommending the event) and open-ended questions regarding aspects of the event/webinar registrants liked and disliked.

An online survey in English language (also hosted by Google Forms) requesting feedback on all aspects of CoRe-ED experiences was emailed to all CoRe-ED registrants in the December 2025 newsletter (dated 11 December 2025). The survey collected demographic information (country of residence, role) and quantitative items (overall satisfaction with being part of the CoRe-ED community and likelihood of recommending CoRe-ED) and open-ended questions regarding aspects of the CoRe-ED community they liked and disliked. Respondents were not reimbursed for completing event-focused or the overall CoRe-ED surveys.

For clarity, three related but distinct groups are referred to throughout this manuscript [1]. CoRe-ED registrants refer to individuals who completed the CoRe-ED registration form and joined the registry database [2]. Event registrants refer to individuals who registered for specific CoRe-ED events or webinars; event registration links were publicly accessible and therefore event registrants may include both CoRe-ED registrants and non-registrants [3]. Survey respondents refer to individuals who completed anonymous event evaluation or overall consortium feedback surveys. Because all surveys were completed anonymously, it was not possible to link responses across events or to the registry database.

“Next Big Research Idea” initiative

CoRe-ED implemented a structured “Next Big Research Idea” initiative during the study period. This initiative was only available to individuals who had registered to join the consortium. CoRe-ED registrants formed teams of up to 6 members, which had to include lived experience and early career representation as well as team members from at least 2 different countries. Teams submitted collaborative research proposals between 15 July and 17 September 2025. These proposals underwent a staged review process by CoRe-ED’s scientific committee involving selection of 5 finalist teams on 24 September 2025. The finalist teams then prepared short videos outlining their proposals for a CoRe-ED registrant community online vote which, together with CoRe-ED’s scientific committee review, informed selection of a single winning proposal (28 October 2025). The winning team subsequently entered a fundraising and planning phase in partnership with CoRe-ED in early 2026 (outside the current study period). This initiative is described as part of the consortium’s engagement activities and analysed using descriptive counts of participation and progression.

Data analysis

Registration data and survey data, where all responses were provided in English language, were analysed for “primary country of residence” and “role” which were categorised respectively and frequency data calculated to provide insights into registrant and survey respondent characteristics. Event-specific survey responses were aggregated across the ten CoRe-ED events for descriptive analysis because review of the event-level data indicated highly consistent quantitative response patterns and overlapping qualitative themes across events. The purpose of aggregation was to provide an overall evaluation of CoRe-ED engagement and experiences across the first ~ 15 months rather than to compare individual events. As surveys were anonymous, responses could not be linked across events and may include repeat respondents. In the event and overall surveys, responses to quantitative measures were analysed for frequency data. Open-ended question responses in the registration and survey data were examined using a codebook-driven thematic analysis approach [9, 12–14]. An inductive process was employed to analyse responses, whereby codes were generated from the data and similar ideas were grouped together. These codes were then organised into broader themes by the team, with Gemma Sharp serving as the primary coder and Peter Wigley and Hao Hu along with a research assistant providing secondary coding. The very occasional discrepancies between coders were resolved through discussion, which also supported a deeper understanding of the data. All authors reached consensus on the naming and description of the themes, and Gemma Sharp supervised the overall data analysis process. Participation in the “Next Big Research Idea” initiative was summarised using descriptive counts of submissions and progression through the selection stages.

Reflexivity statement

I, Gemma Sharp, conducted the analyses and recognise that my perspectives and experiences may have influenced the research process. In particular, my background as a clinical psychologist, previous research in the field, and role as Founding Director of CoRe-ED may have shaped how I approached the study. Throughout, I made efforts to ensure that these factors did not restrict the scope of the research or limit the variety of insights provided by respondents. To support analytic rigour and interpretation of responses, secondary coding was undertaken by Peter Wigley and Hao Hu, both CoRe-ED Directors, as well as a research assistant external to the CoRe-ED leadership team who contributed to coding and theme development. The involvement of multiple coders with differing levels of clinical and research experience was intended to enhance reflexivity and provide multiple interpretive perspectives within an internally conducted program evaluation. All co-authors had varying levels of experience in eating disorder research, and none of the co-authors involved in secondary coding had clinical practice roles.

Results

CoRe-ED registrant characteristics

In the first ~ 15 months since CoRe-ED was launched, 960 registrants joined. The most common role registrants nominated was researcher (n = 290, 30.2%), followed by health professional (n = 260, 27.1%), lived experience of an eating disorder either personally or supporting another (n = 141, 14.7%), working for a not-for-profit/advocacy role (n = 86, 9.0%), and working in industry (n = 62, 6.5%). One hundred and fifty-four registrants (16.0%) did not provide an answer to this item.

For country representation, registrants were most commonly based in Australia (n = 454, 47.3%), followed by US (n = 118, 12.3%), UK (n = 60, 6.3%), Canada (n = 50, 5.2%), Malaysia (n = 37, 3.9%), New Zealand (n = 21, 2.2%), Italy (n = 19, 2.0%), Mexico (n = 14, 1.5%) and Ireland (n = 13, 1.4%). The following countries/regions were all represented at < 1.0%: Costa Rica, Netherlands, Singapore, Brazil, France, Japan, Germany, South Korea, Indonesia, Spain, Argentina, Denmark, Czech Republic, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Norway, Honduras, Saudi Arabia, Peru, Turkey, United Arab Emirates, Chile, Israel, Sweden, Switzerland, Belgium, India and China. Ninety-seven registrants (10.1%) did not provide an answer to this item.

Expectations for CoRe-ED registrant experiences and benefits

The majority of registrants (n = 552, 57.5%) provided a response to their expected experiences and benefits from CoRe-ED involvement over the ~ 15-month period. The most frequently endorsed themes were research contribution and collaboration (n = 261, 47.3%), learning and professional development (n = 231, 41.8%), and networking and community building (n = 171, 31.0%). Registrants also identified advocacy and awareness raising (n = 102, 18.5%), sharing lived experience (n = 66, 12.0%), facilitating innovation in practice and policy (n = 48, 8.7%), and gaining international perspectives as important motivations (n = 21, 3.8%). The detailed thematic descriptions presented below focus on eight additional themes identified within the dataset, reflecting perspectives on prevention, innovation and novel interventions, specialised clinical areas, research translation and implementation, global collaboration, equity and inclusion, mentorship, and lived experience integration.

Theme: Prevention and public health focus

Registrants (n = 35, 6.3%) described interest in prevention, health promotion, early intervention and community- or school-based education initiatives, emphasising the adoption of public health perspectives to address eating disorders, reduce risk factors and implement prevention strategies for youth and vulnerable populations. One registrant explained, “Establishing or learning more about eating disorder prevention through a health promotion/public health lens” (Educator, US), while another shared, “Interested in prevention, particularly school nutrition education, weight stigma, and body image in schools” (Health Professional, Canada).

Theme: Innovation, novel interventions and emerging treatments

Innovation in prevention and treatment was reported by 32 registrants (5.8%), who expected CoRe-ED to support the development, dissemination and critical discussion of novel approaches. This included interest in digital mental health interventions, pharmacological developments such as Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 (GLP-1) medications, novel therapeutic models, single session interventions and the commercialisation or translation of research into practice. Registrants framed innovation not only as scientific advancement but also as responsiveness to gaps in existing care systems. One registrant described an interest in “…potential of GLP-1s to treat eating disorders…” (Lived Experience, US) and “…innovative digital treatment approaches that serve youth with eating disorders in this modern world” (Researcher and Health Professional, Australia).

Theme: Specialised clinical focus

Registrants (n = 29, 5.3%) highlighted attention to specialised clinical populations and complex presentations, including neurodiversity, perimenopause/menopause, chronic illness and co-morbidities within eating disorders. They described the need for tailored interventions, individualised care approaches and recognition of intersecting clinical needs. One registrant noted, “…to contribute to and amplify a critical mass of advocacy specifically for support for neurodivergent people with lived experience of eating distress or disorders” (Researcher, UK), while another stated, “My goal is to devise a prevention plan for the public and tackle this problem effectively on the ground (muscularity-oriented EDs)” (Researcher, Malaysia).

Theme: Research translation and implementation

While research contribution and collaboration were dominant themes, a subset of registrants (n = 28, 5.1%) reported the importance of moving beyond knowledge generation toward tangible implementation, translation and systems change. Responses indicated expectations that CoRe-ED would support the integration of research findings into clinical care, education systems and policy reform, including harmonised data capture, benchmarking of evidence-based modalities and influencing governmental or institutional guidelines. One registrant noted an interest in “…collaborating with other researchers to develop harmonised data-capture strategies and novel interventions…” (Researcher, Australia) and another wanted to “…facilitate the translation of research findings into clinical practice and system change” (Health Professional, US).

Theme: Global collaboration and international knowledge exchange

Registrants (n = 23, 4.2%) emphasised the value of cross-country partnerships, learning from global experts, understanding culturally specific prevalence and practices and contributing to reform in under-resourced regions. Some explicitly identified Latin America, Southeast Asia, rural contexts and Indigenous communities as areas where knowledge exchange was needed. Responses positioned CoRe-ED as a mechanism for global capacity-building and bidirectional learning rather than unidirectional dissemination. One registrant reflected, they wanted “…to learn more about how other cultures view and experience eating disorders” (Researcher, New Zealand), and “I look forward to exchanging ideas, learning from international colleagues, and contributing to a global effort to enhance the quality and reach of eating disorder services” (Health Professional and Advocate, US).

Theme: Equity, diversity and inclusion in research and treatment

Culturally-informed practice and systemic equity in eating disorder research and care were reported by 20 registrants (3.6%). They expressed the importance of addressing marginalised populations, embedding cultural safety and decolonising interventions to ensure inclusive and relevant research and clinical practice. One registrant reflected, “I have consistently used my lived experience and professional position to advocate…to contribute to systemic change that recognises eating disorders as shaped by culture, identity, colonisation, and structural inequity” (Health Professional and Lived Experience Advocate, Australia), and another added, “Contributing to research and development of culturally pertinent interventions” (Researcher, Mexico).

Theme: Early career and mentorship opportunities

Registrants (n = 18, 3.3%) described expectations for mentorship, academic guidance and early career development. They highlighted the desire to gain insights on PhD and postdoctoral opportunities, research independence, grants and leadership in the field of eating disorders, emphasising the value of connecting with experienced researchers and academic mentors to shape their research trajectory. For example, one registrant stated, “…hope to build meaningful connections with fellow researchers, explore opportunities for future collaboration, and gain insights that will help shape my research direction and academic pathway” (Researcher, Indonesia). Another noted, “Would love a mentoring relationship to pursue research…” (Researcher and Health Professional with Lived Experience, Australia).

Theme: Consumer-researcher co-design and lived experience integration

Integration of lived experience into research governance, co-design and leadership was emphasised by 18 registrants (3.3%), reflecting a desire to move beyond sharing personal experiences toward participatory and co-produced research models. They described embedding lived experience voices into protocol development, advisory boards, prevention planning and research prioritisation, including amplifying neurodivergent perspectives and ensuring carers contribute to reform efforts. One registrant explained, “I want to ensure lived experience informs research priorities and prevention planning, particularly for neurodivergent populations” (Lived Experience, Australia), while another reported, “Integrating carers and bereaved families into the research process can strengthen interventions and ensure they are relevant to those directly affected” (Not-for-Profit and Lived Experience, New Zealand).

Experiences with CoRe-ED: “Next Big Research Idea” initiative

The CoRe-ED “Next Big Research Idea” initiative resulted in 18 internationally distributed multidisciplinary teams submitting collaborative research proposals, all of which included lived experience and early career representation. Across the 18 teams, 79 CoRe-ED registrants participated, with no overlap of individuals between teams, representing 20 countries. Five finalist teams were selected from the 18 submissions, with one winning team announced at the conclusion of the initiative.

Discussion

The present study examined ~ 15 months registrant engagement following the launch of CoRe-ED, expanding on earlier findings from the initial three months [9]. In this longer-term registrant pool, 960 individuals from 37 countries across five continents registered for the consortium. These findings represented a 281% growth in total registrants and 85% increase in numbers of countries involved compared to the three-month study [9]. The registrants were researchers, health professionals, individuals with lived experience, not-for-profit and industry representatives. As in the earlier investigation [9], researchers and health professionals remained the most common roles for registrants. However, lived experience representation increased to become the third most frequent role, potentially reflecting broader multi-group engagement and reduced barriers to participation, including the decision to make registration free of charge. This is consistent with previous research showing that accessibility can support broader engagement in health research initiatives [5, 6].

Expectations for CoRe-ED involvement reported at registration were largely consistent with those identified in the first three months, particularly the desire for networking, collaboration, learning and community building [9]. Over the ~ 15-month period, unique additional themes were found, reflecting registrants’ interest in innovation, research translation, prevention, specialised clinical focus, equity and inclusion, mentorship, global collaboration and structural integration of lived experience. These findings suggest the diverse priorities of the consortium’s registrants and the value of an inclusive approach to research design, reflecting recent calls for participatory and co-designed research in mental health and eating disorders [15]. Comparing early registrant expectations with ~ 15-month engagement suggests CoRe-ED implemented initiatives aligned with registrant feedback from the earliest stages, including multi-country webinars, networking opportunities and international knowledge exchange events (e.g., global research showcase in October 2025 [16]). This iterative, registrant-informed approach appears to have supported engagement, as reflected in the generally high satisfaction ratings observed across CoRe-ED events and overall consortium feedback.

A key feature of CoRe-ED’s early implementation was the “Next Big Research Idea” initiative, which aimed to facilitate structured international collaboration and the co-development of research proposals. During the study period, the initiative engaged 18 internationally distributed teams across 20 countries in collaborative research idea generation, with a staged selection process resulting in five finalist teams and one winning proposal. Importantly, the initiative was not designed to produce immediate scientific outputs within the timeframe of this study (e.g., funded trials or publications), but rather to support pathways from ideation to collaborative development and early-stage translation. The progression of the winning team to a fundraising and implementation phase within CoRe-ED provides one example of this intended process in practice. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence supporting the feasibility of structured consortium-based approaches to international research collaboration, rather than evidence of downstream research impact.

Event-specific feedback from ten CoRe-ED initiatives revealed that respondents valued the diversity of presenters, friendly and inclusive atmospheres and unique global perspectives. Suggestions for improvement primarily focused on increasing opportunities for interaction and discussion. These suggestions have subsequently been addressed in our educational webinar program in 2026 by ensuring at least a third of event/webinar length (e.g., 20 min of a 60-minute webinar) is dedicated to discussion/interaction.

Overall feedback from the broader registrant survey further supported these findings, highlighting the importance of webinars and events for learning, being part of a global network and the consortium’s specific focus on eating disorders. Respondents appreciated free and inclusive access, regular communication via multiple channels (email newsletters, online portal, social media) and opportunities to engage with a wide range of partnerships as well as the “Next Big Research Idea” initiative. A small number of respondents reported that they wanted to be more involved in CoRe-ED but were not sure how and also asked for specific educational topics to be addressed. As a result, we have included more calls for volunteer support in our monthly newsletters, such as nominating resources for our global eating disorders resources database (Connections Initiative [8]), as well as addressing nominated educational topics, including the influence of trauma on eating disorder experiences for the webinar series in 2026 [8]. Collectively, these results suggest that CoRe-ED may support opportunities for cross-disciplinary knowledge sharing, collaboration and professional development, echoing the importance of building trust and community across diverse groups in international health research [17, 18]. It is possible, however, that the largely positive feedback may also be influenced by the absence of financial cost to registrants, potentially reducing the likelihood of more critical evaluation.

Limitations and future directions

These findings should be interpreted with limitations in mind. The online registration form intentionally collected minimal demographic data to reduce barriers to joining the consortium, limiting insight into variables such as age, gender identity and specific research interests. Although CoRe-ED engaged registrants from 37 countries, involvement was weighted towards Australia, reflecting the consortium’s origin and initial implementation context. This may limit the generalisability of findings to more evenly distributed international populations. Ongoing efforts are focused on increasing engagement across underrepresented regions to further strengthen the global balance of involvement and enhance the generalisability of future findings. Furthermore, despite response rates being similar to or exceeding expected rates for online surveys [19], many registrants did not provide any feedback and open-text items were left unanswered by a proportion of individuals, which may limit representativeness. Owing to event evaluation surveys being anonymous and not capturing identifiers, it was not possible to determine whether some respondents completed surveys for multiple events. Consequently, analyses reflect aggregated anonymous survey responses rather than unique individual-level observations, and responses were treated as independent for descriptive analysis purposes. In addition, as the feedback surveys were voluntary, survey respondents may not have fully represented the broader CoRe-ED registrant cohort, and it was possible that individuals with more positive engagement experiences were more likely to respond. Moreover, providing feedback surveys in English language only may have deterred some attendees from providing feedback, particularly if English was not their first language. Online language translation tools should be embedded within the surveys, rather than respondents having to source their own translations, to make providing feedback as simple as possible for the CoRe-ED community [20]. The CoRe-ED online portal [11], particularly the noticeboard, has successfully catered for multi-language posts from registrants. Furthermore, this study represents an internally conducted program evaluation led by members of the CoRe-ED leadership team, which may introduce potential for interpretive bias despite the use of multi-coder reflexive analysis and inclusion of an independent research assistant in the analytic process. Finally, longer-term follow-up is needed to examine whether registrants’ evolving expectations are met, particularly regarding mentorship, co-design and research translation. Future research should also explore how CoRe-ED initiatives influence tangible outcomes in research outputs, policy and clinical practice.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the ~ 15-month data suggest that CoRe-ED has engaged a diverse, international group of 960 registrants from 37 countries and made efforts to respond to their early feedback by implementing initiatives aligned with their expectations. The consortium has employed strategies intended to support networking, collaboration, innovation and inclusive participation while expanding representation across countries and key groups. These findings suggest the potential of a registrant-informed, international research consortium to advance innovation and knowledge exchange in eating disorders and provide a foundation for future efforts to evaluate the longer-term impact of such collaborative, inclusive models.
