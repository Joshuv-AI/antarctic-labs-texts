Active Cerebrospinal Fluid Exchange Versus External Ventricular Drainage in IVH and SAH: A Meta-Analysis
Authors: Amir Reza Bahadori, Albin Röblom, Mika Niemelä, Saif D. Salman, Adnan Siddiqui, Rabih G. Tawk, Babak Jahromi, William D. Freeman, Sepide Kashefiolasl, Reza Dashti, Behnam Rezai Jahromi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Intraventricular hemorrhage and subarachnoid hemorrhage are associated with neuroinflammation and increased morbidity and mortality. Rapid clearance of blood from the cerebrospinal fluid space may mitigate neuroinflammatory cascades and improve clinical outcomes. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare the clinical effectiveness of active cerebrospinal fluid exchange, including neuroendoscopic lavage and IRRAflow systems, versus external ventricular drainage in patients with intraventricular hemorrhage or subarachnoid hemorrhage.METHODS:The study followed Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines and thoroughly assessed databases, including PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane, and Web of Science, up to September 18, 2025. Statistical analysis, both fixed-effect and random-effect model analyses, was performed by the Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software. In addition, the Cochran Q test was conducted to determine the statistical heterogeneity. In terms of low heterogeneity, the fixed-effect model is reported.RESULTS:This review included 7 studies with a total sample size of 834. Our analysis showed that active cerebrospinal fluid exchange was associated with significantly higher hematoma clearance (odds ratio, 2.72 [95% CI, 1.22–6.08]; P=0.015), better functional outcomes (odds ratio, 1.66 [95% CI, 1.12–2.48]; P=0.012), lower shunt dependency (odds ratio, 0.22 [95% CI, 0.10–0.50]; P<0.001), and reduced infection rates (odds ratio, 0.22 [95% CI, 0.12–0.42]; P<0.001). Subgroup analysis showed that neuroendoscopic lavage was most effective for hematoma clearance, while IRRAflow reduced infection rates. Also, younger patients had better neurological recovery.CONCLUSIONS:Active cerebrospinal fluid exchange techniques demonstrated superior outcomes over external ventricular drainage in managing intraventricular hemorrhage and subarachnoid hemorrhage, particularly in clot resolution and neurological recovery. Large-scale trials are warranted.

CLINICAL PERSPECTIVE

What Is New?

This is the first meta-analysis to compare active cerebrospinal fluid exchange (ACE), including neuroendoscopic lavage and IRRAflow, with standard external ventricular drainage in intraventricular hemorrhage and subarachnoid hemorrhage, showing that ACE significantly improves hematoma clearance, reduces shunt dependency and infection rates, and leads to better functional outcomes (modified Rankin Scale score ≤3) with moderate-certainty evidence.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) and subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) are serious life-threatening neurocritical conditions, with an overall mortality rate of 50% to 80%1,2 and 30% to 40%,3 respectively.

Blood and clots impair cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation and may obstruct ventricular passages, causing acute hydrocephalus.4,5 CSF drainage is essential for increased intracranial pressure (ICP) control, and timely hematoma evacuation minimizes secondary injury.6 Current standard treatment is careful ICP monitoring and CSF drainage via an external ventricular drain (EVD) orchestrated by neurointensivists and neurosurgeons.7

Novel techniques involving active CSF exchange (ACE) have emerged, such as neuroendoscopic hematoma evacuation combined with lavage, and active ventricular irrigation with devices such as the IRRAflow dual-lumen catheter. Lavage systems are dual-lumen catheters that allow for simultaneous drainage and irrigation, which dilutes CSF and removes clots. Also, the IRRAflow systems are equipped with ICP monitoring, and automatic ICP control is integrated into the device by setting upper and lower ICP limits. The flow rate is adjusted at the physician’s discretion.8

There is a lack of consensus regarding the superiority of these new techniques and standard EVD. This systematic review and meta-analysis were initiated to synthesize evidence to guide clinical decision-making. This review aims to clarify the comparative effectiveness of ACE and EVD, potentially informing future neurocritical care strategies and improving patient outcomes. ACE may improve early recovery and reduce long-term disability, offering a promising advance in neurocritical care.

Methods

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

The study followed PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses)9 and MOOSE (Meta-Analyses of Observational Studies in Epidemiology)10 guidelines, aligned with Cochrane recommendations for meta-analysis, and was registered in PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews; Unique identifier: CRD420251145794). This review was conducted as a study-level meta-analysis based on aggregated data reported in the included studies.

Search Strategy and Eligibility Criteria

We performed a comprehensive systematic search across multiple databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane. We used a combination of MeSH terms and keywords, such as lavage, irrigation, IRRAflow, active CSF exchange, ICH, IVH, SAH, external ventricular drainage, and treatment outcome, in conjunction with appropriate Boolean operators of AND and OR. The search included all published articles from the inception of the abovementioned databases up to September 18, 2025, without any restrictions on language and publication dates (S1 in the Supplemental Material).

Inclusion Criteria: Adult Studies Comparing ACE Versus EVD for IVH and SAH

The exclusion criteria included pediatrics, no comparison between ACE and EVD, animal studies, conference abstracts, editorials, trial registries, case reports, and reviews.

Both endoscopic lavage and IRRAflow were classified as ACE because each involves active removal or exchange of intraventricular blood/CSF compared with passive drainage by EVD. The primary exposure was the type of intervention, defined as ACE (endoscopic lavage and IRRAflow) versus EVD. The prespecified outcomes for this meta-analysis included hematoma clearance, shunt dependency, functional recovery measured by modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score, infection rate, rebleeding, duration of intensive care unit (ICU) stay, and mortality.

Study Selection, Data Extraction, and Quality Assessment

During the study selection process, all articles were imported into EndNote V.21. After the removal of duplicates, 2 independent reviewers assessed the relevance of the titles and abstracts and then full-text screening. Discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer.

Two independent reviewers extracted data. Data included (1) study details: author, country, design, and intervention; (2) participants’ demographic data: sample size, sex, age, diagnosis, and severity score of IVH/SAH; and (3) clinical outcomes of both ACE and EVD groups post-operation: mRS score, hematoma clearance, shunt dependency, infection rate, length of ICU stay, rebleeding, and mortality. When multiple time points were reported for outcomes, the longest available follow-up was used for consistency across studies.

To assess methodological quality, we applied the Risk of Bias Tool for Randomized Trials (RoB 2)11 and the nonrandomized studies of interventions (Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions [ROBINS-I]) tool,12 following Cochrane Collaboration guidelines. The risk of bias for each study was solely assessed by 2 researchers. We use the RobVis tool for plotting.13

Statistical Analysis

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the guidelines outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions.14 All analyses were performed using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software, version 3. Due to limited randomized clinical trials (RCTs) and low heterogeneity, mixed designs were pooled, consistent with Cochrane guidance on combining study types in meta-analysis.15,16

For dichotomous outcomes, data were entered as event rates and sample sizes for each group, and odds ratios (ORs) were calculated. For continuous outcomes reported as mean and SD, groupwise data were analyzed using the standardized mean difference.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q test, and the degree of inconsistency was quantified by the I2 statistic. An I2 value below 50% was considered low heterogeneity, while values >50% indicated high heterogeneity. Both fixed-effect and random-effects models were used to calculate OR and 95% CIs. In cases of low heterogeneity, results from the fixed-effects model were reported.

Publication bias was assessed using the funnel plot, the Egger regression test, and the trim-and-fill analysis for outcomes with significant asymmetry. Metaregression assessed the influence of continuous moderators. For studies reporting dichotomous outcomes, subgroup analysis was performed. Prespecified subgroup analyses included diagnostic heterogeneity, patient age, and ACE modality. Interaction between subgroups was tested using omnibus P values. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of pooled estimates by comparing results from RCTs and nonrandomized controlled trials (NRCTs).

Certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation framework,17 evaluating risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

Results

Following a comprehensive review of the specified databases, a total of 499 articles were initially identified. After removing duplicates and screening, 27 articles remained for full-text assessment. Finally, 7 studies were included in our study8,18–23 (Figure 1).

Baseline Characteristics

The current meta-analysis consists of 834 participants (262ACE/572 EVD). Two ACE studies used neuroendoscopic lavage18,19; others used IRRAflow. Studies from China, the United States, Denmark, Finland, and Germany support global relevance. Among included studies, Carrera et al8 reported that IRRAflow patients received irrigation with saline or alteplase, using a pharmacy-approved protocol (4 mg in 500-mL saline at 30 mL/h, ≈5.76 mg/d), while EVD patients received intermittent boluses of 2 to 3 mg every 8 to 12 h (≈6 mg/d). They also noted that 89% of IRRAflow patients received irrigation with tPA (tissue-type plasminogen activator) compared with 33% in the EVD group. Field et al20 described 3 IRRAflow cases with fibrinolytic therapy. Rezai Jahromi et al23 noted ACE irrigation with saline or Ringer solutions, with or without tPA. Other studies did not specify irrigation type or infusion rate. The Table summarizes study characteristics, and S2 in the Supplemental Material depicts cumulative data of included studies.

Meta-Analysis

The meta-analysis included a total of 7 studies (2 RCTs and 5 NRCTs). Despite differences in study design, all studies were pooled together based on comparable populations, interventions, and outcome measures, allowing for a unified synthesis of effect estimates.24 Pooling mixed designs was justified by low heterogeneity and outcome similarity, consistent with Cochrane guidance on combining study types in meta-analysis.15,16

In this study, the fixed-effect model revealed significant superiority of ACE in hematoma clearance rate, lower incidence of shunt dependency, lower incidence of infectious rate, and superiority of favorable neurological status of patients (mRS score ≤3; OR, 2.719 [95% CI, 1.217–6.075]; P=0.015; OR, 0.223 [95% CI, 0.099–0.501]; P<0.001; OR, 0.220 [95% CI, 0.115–0.423]; P<0.001; and OR, 1.663 [95% CI, 1.117–2.476]; P=0.012, respectively; Figure 2). Moreover, our included studies had low heterogeneity in terms of hematoma clearance rate, incidence of shunt dependency, incidence of infection rate, and mRS score (Q=5.180, P=0.159, I2=42.086%; Q=3.189, P=0.203, I2=37.284%; Q=3.320, P=0.506, I2=0%; and Q=4.337, P=0.227, I2=30.823%, respectively). In contrast, the fixed-effect model did not illustrate and statistical differences between ACE group and EVD group for mortality, possibility of rebleeding, and duration of ICU stay (OR, 1.089 [95% CI, 0.687–1.726]; P=0.716; Q=5.716, P=0.221, I2=30.019%; OR, 0.565 [95% CI, 0.211–1.515]; P=0.257; Q=3.143, P=0.543, I2=0%; and standardized mean difference, −0.206 [95% CI, −0.737 to 0.325]; P=0.447; Q=4.739, P=0.094, I2=57.794%, respectively; Figure 3). In addition, owing to the funnel plot and the Egger test, publication bias did not show in any of the variables except mortality (hematoma clearance rate: P=0.863, incidence of shunt dependency: P=0.464, infectious rate: P=0.477, mRS score: P=0.385, rebleeding rate: P=0.646, ICU stay: P=0.945, and mortality: P=0.018; S3 and S4 in the Supplemental Material). In addition, for mortality, the trim-and-fill analysis did not impute any missing studies, and the adjusted fixed-effect estimate remained nonsignificant, suggesting minimal impact on the pooled result.

To account for diagnostic heterogeneity, we performed subgroup analysis comparing IVH-only studies to those including SAH/IVH mixed populations. Both IVH and mixed population demonstrated significantly lower infectious rate in ACE (OR, 0.181 [95% CI, 0.055–0.590]; P=0.005; OR, 0.240 [95% CI, 0.110–0.525]; P<0.001, respectively).

Metaregression and subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the influence of age on treatment outcomes. Metaregression showed age was not a significant moderator for hematoma clearance, mRS score ≤3, and shunt dependency (β=−1.73, P=0.081; β=−0.72, P=0.097; and β=−0.950, P=0.343, respectively; S5 in the Supplemental Material). Age subgroup analysis (<60 versus ≥60 years) showed younger patients had better outcomes in hematoma clearance rate (OR, 4.14 [95% CI, 1.67–10.23]; P=0.002; n=4 studies) and functional recovery (OR, 2.80 [95% CI, 1.32–5.97]; P=0.007; n=4 studies), while older patients showed no significant benefit (P=0.530 and P=0.199, respectively). For shunt dependency (n=3 studies), both age groups showed nonsignificant results (≥60: OR, 0.43 [95% CI, 0.04–4.48]; P=0.479; <60: OR, 0.26 [95% CI, 0.01–5.63]; P=0.394), and between-group heterogeneity was not significant (P=0.807). Although subgroup analysis showed a significant benefit in younger patients, metaregression did not identify age as a significant moderator.

In addition, we performed a further subgroup analysis to assess which type of ACE has a better effect on patients’ outcomes. The fixed-effects model analysis yielded statistically significant findings indicating hematoma clearance (n=4 studies) in lavage (OR, 3.695 [95% CI, 1.375–9.930]; P=0.010) with low heterogeneity (I2=0%). Furthermore, the fixed-effects model for low incidence of infectious (n=5 studies) depicted significances among lavage and IRRAflow systems with superiority of IRRAflow (OR, 0.169 [95% CI, 0.047–0.610]; P=0.007; OR, 0.242 [95% CI, 0.113–0.515]; P<0.001, respectively) with low heterogeneity (I2=0% and I2=29.389%, respectively). Due to insufficient data on sex and comorbidities, further subgroup analyses were not feasible. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by study design (RCT versus NRCT) to assess the robustness of pooled estimates.

Complications

Haldrup et al21 reported a significantly higher rate of catheter occlusions in the ACE group compared with EVD, longer procedure times, and one case of catheter tract hemorrhage. Of 21 serious adverse events in the ACE group, 16 were intervention-related, including rebleeding, catheter displacements, infections, and catheter occlusions. Notably, procedure times were substantially longer for IRRAflow insertion compared with EVD (median [interquartile range], 55.3 [33–75] versus 12 [4–20] minutes), which is probably related to operator experience and technical challenges in device placement. Other included studies did not report catheter tract hemorrhage or worsened IVH.

Quality Assessment

Two independent reviewers assessed study quality using RoB 211 and ROBINS-I.12 Two studies assessed by RoB 218,21 had a low risk. For the remaining studies, we performed ROBINS-I. Except for 2 studies,20,23 the remaining have a low overall risk. The average risk of all included studies was low (Figure 5).

Discussion

The current systematic review and meta-analysis provide a comparative evaluation of ACE versus EVD in neurocritical care patients with IVH and SAH. A total of 7 studies were included (2 RCTs and 5 NRCTs). Despite differences in design and regional protocols, the pooled analysis revealed consistent trends favoring ACE for hematoma clearance rate, incidence of shunt dependency, incidence of infection rate, and functional outcomes. The low heterogeneity across outcomes and the absence of publication bias strengthen the reliability of these results. Moreover, due to limited RCTs and low heterogeneity across outcomes, we pooled randomized and nonrandomized studies, consistent with accepted meta-analytic frameworks.15,16 Also, the risk of bias stemmed mainly from incomplete outcome reporting rather than methodological flaws.

The limited number of studies may reflect ACE’s relative novelty as a treatment for IVH and SAH. However, other factors such as publication bias, prohibitive costs, or resource-intensive requirements may also contribute to the scarcity of available data.

Sensitivity analysis by study type showed that ACE was significantly more effective in hematoma clearance and infection reduction in NRCTs, while RCTs demonstrated a significant benefit only in reducing rebleeding. Although the core analysis indicated consistent advantages of ACE across key outcomes, these subgroup findings likely reflect differences in sample size and study design. Given the low heterogeneity across pooled outcomes, combining study types remains methodologically sound; however, larger, high-quality RCTs are warranted to validate these results.

Treatment Strategy

Patients with IVH and SAH need immediate treatment to reduce mortality.25–27 The primary causes of IVH-related death include (1) ICP, which activates inflammatory cascades in the brain and might be accompanied by cerebral vasospasm19,28; (2) obstructive hydrocephalus can occur owing to the compression of the third and fourth ventricles, potentially leading to central cerebral herniation29,30; and (3) water and electrolyte imbalance resulting from hematoma-induced hypothalamic dysfunction.19,25 As well, rebleeding,31 cerebral vasospasm and delayed cerebral ischemia,32 and acute hydrocephalus and ICP are the primary causes of SAH-related mortality.26,33 Therefore, early treatment and evacuation are crucial. EVD, a passive form of CSF diversion, is the most common initial strategy. It may relieve ICP and facilitate CSF outflow. Alternatively, ACE offers enhanced clot clearance and reduced inflammation, potentially improving clinical outcomes.25,34 In SAH, hematoma evacuation is typically considered after securing the aneurysm via endovascular coiling or microsurgical clipping.35 In selected cases, cisternal or ventricular irrigation may be used to reduce vasospasm and improve neurological outcomes.25 Neuroendoscopy enhances clot removal, especially with ACE systems. Adjunctive therapies such as thrombolytics and ICP monitoring may support evacuation in both IVH23 and SAH.35 ACE may improve neurological outcomes by promoting clot removal and regulating CSF.36 These findings align with the rationale behind the ongoing ARCH trial (Active Removal of Intracerebral Hemorrhage via Active Irrigation; Unique identifier: NCT05118997), which aims to evaluate active irrigation techniques for hematoma evacuation and may further validate ACE’s role in neurocritical care.

Clinical Outcomes

Hematoma Clearance

Hematoma clearance critically influences neurological recovery in patients.25,37 Qu et al18 noted that neuroendoscopy lavage effectively removes hematoma and enhances treatment outcomes, which is related to a clear visual field, which improves visualization and CSF flow.19,38 Also, several studies mentioned that the IRRAflow systems can clear a hematoma more quickly than EVD, especially when combined with tPA administration; therefore, it may improve the efficacy of the recovery phase.8,20 However, Haldrup et al’s study did not show any superiority between the 2 techniques.21 The prolonged duration of EVD use and its lower hematoma clearance rate may be partly attributed to the site of catheter insertion. In many cases, EVDs are placed in the contralateral ventricle, which, especially in the presence of midline shift, can increase the risk of herniation and reduce the effectiveness of blood evacuation.7,8,39,40

Our meta-analysis showed that the hematoma clearance rate is significantly higher among ACE versus EVD. Subgroup analysis showed that neuroendoscopic lavage has greater efficacy in clot resolution. Improved clearance was associated with better postoperative GCS scores, suggesting that more rapid and aggressive clot removal may help mitigate secondary brain injury. These findings support the hypothesis that ACE techniques may offer superior outcomes in the neurocritical care management of IVH and SAH.

Shunt Dependency

The ACE group showed a low rate of shunt dependency, which may be attributed to the fast removal of debris from the subarachnoid space and ventricles, reducing the incidence of hydrocephalus.41 The underlying mechanisms are probably related to dilution of macromolecules, enhancement of CSF dynamic flow, and accelerated clearance of physical obstruction.29,42 Despite the result of our analysis, Haldrup et al21 mentioned that shunt dependency in the ACE group is higher, which is related to procedure complexity, high initial hemorrhage burden,43 frequent catheter occlusions, longer procedure time,44 ICU stay,43 and early trial termination.

Subgroup and metaregression showed age did not affect shunt dependency in IVH and SAH. Li et al45 found age was not independently linked to shunt dependency; hemorrhage volume and procedural factors were more influential.46

Functional Outcome (mRS Score ≤3)

For assessing functional outcome, studies evaluated the mRS score. Carrera et al8 defined the mRS score ≤3 as Good outcome and reported data accordingly; consequently, we assessed the analysis due to their regard. Our findings suggest that the ACE has a greater impact on the mRS score, and subgroup analysis highlights the superiority of younger patients in achieving an mRS score of ≤3. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating improved functional outcomes with ACE that may benefit from enhanced CSF clearance and reduced secondary injury.8,18,21,22 Several studies show that younger patients have better outcomes in CSF evacuation, which is related to greater neuroplasticity, faster recovery phase, and better tolerance to surgical procedures.47,48

Mortality

Mortality remains a critical outcome in the management of IVH and SAH, reflecting both the severity of patients’ initial condition and the effectiveness of treatment.49,50 Notably, in Haldrup et al’s study, despite a higher complication rate associated with IRRAflow, the lower mortality observed suggests that surgical technique and operator expertise are critical to achieving favorable outcomes.21 Moreover, ACE systems maintain continuous irrigation, potentially minimizing clot burden and improving CSF dynamics more effectively than EVD. Although findings were not statistically significant, the trend of lower mortality in the ACE versus EVD demonstrates the minimal superiority of ACE. ACE’s irrigation and clot clearance may reduce long-term disability and improve neurocritical care outcomes. In addition, despite the Egger test suggesting potential publication bias for mortality, trim-and-fill analysis did not impute any missing studies, and adjusted estimates remained consistent with the original findings. This supports the stability of the mortality outcome despite limited study numbers.

Complications and ICU Course

Infectious Rate

ACE and EVD carry infection risks. ACE infection risks include CSF exchange frequency, duration, aseptic technique, type, and sterility of flushing solution.51,52 Moreover, for EVD, it is related to duration of catheter placement, frequency of CSF sampling, and closed versus open drainage systems.52,53

The pooled data show that the infection rate in ACE is significantly lower than in EVD. Subgroup analysis by ACE type demonstrated that both lavage and IRRAflow systems reduce infection rates, with IRRAflow showing greater benefit. In addition, subgroup analysis based on patient diagnosis showed that ACE significantly reduced infection rates in both IVH-only and SAH/IVH mixed populations, supporting its effectiveness across varied clinical presentations.

Rebleeding

In the current meta-analysis, the pooled ES (0.565) indicates no statistically significant difference in the risk of rebleeding. The low heterogeneity (I2=0%) supports the consistency of this finding. Rebleeding risk may stem from mechanisms such as (1) rapid ICP changes: ACE may cause a sudden shift in ICP, leading to disruption of fragile clot formations at bleeding sites; (2) mechanical disruption: infusion and drainage may disturb clots and tissue, increasing rebleeding risk44; (3) catheter placement trauma22,54,55; and (4) fundamental vascular pathology, such as aneurysms.56

ICU Stay

Between ACE and EVD, the pooled data did not reveal any statistically significant differences in the course of the ICU stay. According to current literature, reasons for ICU admission following evacuation include (1) the risk of intracerebral hemorrhage and hydrocephalus owing to CSF pathway obstruction,34,47,57 (2) the possibility of infection and device-related complications,21,58 and (3) the need for continuous neurological monitoring.33,59

Safety and Complications

While ACE demonstrated superior outcomes in hematoma clearance, infection reduction, and functional recovery, complication profiles require careful consideration. Haldrup et al21 documented higher catheter-related risks in ACE compared with EVD, including occlusions, displacements, infections, and one tract hemorrhage. Importantly, the markedly longer procedure times for IRRAflow insertion suggest that operator inexperience and technical challenges in catheter placement may have contributed to the observed complication rates. Other studies provided limited complication data, with no reports of worsened IVH. These findings highlight the need for standardized complication reporting in future trials to enable meaningful safety comparisons and guide device optimization.

Factors Influencing Outcomes in IVH/SAH

Age

Chen et al60 noted that the age of 60 years is commonly used as a cutoff in stroke and intracerebral hemorrhage research due to its alignment with epidemiological trends and clinical outcomes, often marking a shift in risk profiles and recovery potential. We used this threshold for subgroup analysis. Our findings illustrated that younger patients had better outcomes in hematoma clearance and functional recovery; however, it did not influence the shunt dependency. To support our findings, Hidaka et al61 presented that subarachnoid space volumes decrease with age, which leads to slower clearance of blood and a higher risk of hydrocephalus among older patients. Also, age-related changes in brain and CSF physiology may prolong ICU stays and reduce functional outcomes.62

Risk-Benefit Analysis

To clarify the clinical relevance of our findings, a focused risk-benefit analysis was conducted using 3 studies8,21,22 that concurrently reported both mRS score ≤3 and shunt dependency rates. The pooled OR for achieving a favorable neurological outcome with ACE was 2.72, while the pooled OR for shunt dependency was ≈0.20, indicating a meaningfully lower risk in the ACE group. This yielded a risk-benefit ratio of 13.6, suggesting that, for each unit of reduced shunt risk, ACE confers a 13.6-fold increase in the likelihood of favorable functional recovery. These findings underscore the clinical advantage of ACE over EVD in neurocritical care. Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation ratings17 support moderate-certainty evidence for key outcomes, reinforcing the strength of these findings. Low-certainty ratings for mortality and ICU stay highlight the need for further high-powered studies.

Limitations

This study is, to our knowledge, the first meta-analysis of ACE with EVD in IVH and SAH. However, several limitations should be considered. First, the number of eligible studies was small, which reflects the novelty of ACE and the limited available evidence in this area. Only 2 randomized controlled trials were included, and most of the remaining studies were nonrandomized. Second, the included studies were clinically heterogeneous, particularly in patient population, intervention strategy, and the use of intraventricular tPA. In addition, important procedural details, such as irrigation method, infusion rate, catheter type, and frequency of tPA use, were not consistently reported. Some outcomes, including ICP and hydrocephalus, were also incompletely reported, which limited subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Although pooling studies with different designs was supported by low statistical heterogeneity and similar outcome measures, this approach may still introduce clinical heterogeneity. Finally, the evidence does not allow firm conclusions about the relative effect of each specific ACE strategy, and larger prospective studies with standardized reporting are needed.

Conclusions

This study illustrates that ACE, an automated irrigation and drainage system, offers superior outcomes over passive EVD in managing IVH and SAH. ACE significantly improves hematoma clearance, reduces infection rates, decreases shunt dependency, and enhances functional recovery. No significant differences were observed in mortality, ICU stay, or rebleeding. ACE appears promising as a neurocritical care strategy, warranting further validation through large-scale, high-quality trials. Its automated CSF management and clot clearance may reduce ICU burden and streamline workflow. The ongoing ARCH trial may provide definitive evidence to support ACE’s integration into neurocritical care protocols.
