Leveraging randomized response frameworks to enhance sensitive data acquisition: implications for data regarding public health
Authors: Faiza Shah, Li Honghe, Muhammad Imran Shahid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traditional survey methods make it difficult to collect sensitive data, as respondents provide false information or deliberately refuse to answer. The current investigation presents innovative methods utilizing multi-stage randomized response models (MRDRMs) to tackle challenges in precisely quantifying sensitive numerical variables while safeguarding respondent anonymity. The MRDRMs framework, comprising two as well as three-stage mathematical models. To employ two randomized response mechanisms designed to improve privacy protection and estimation efficiency to address perceptions like social desirability and over-estimation, which are prevalent in sensitive public health data collection. The main problem addressed in this study is the difficulty of obtaining precise estimates of sensitive quantitative variables because respondents often hesitate to provide truthful answers due to privacy concerns, fear, and social desirability bias. The incorporation of substantial randomization stages enables the models to provide unbiased and precise estimates of means and sensitivity levels, thereby ensuring the reliability of the data while reducing the psychological strain on participants. Theoretical analysis, particularly simulations conducted using Monte Carlo methods, suggests that MRDRMs, in both two-stage and three-stage formats, significantly improve the precision and relative efficiency of estimates compared to conventional randomized response strategies. The empirical validation carried out via a cross-sectional survey in the districts of Faisalabad, Lahore, Multan, and Rawalpindi in Punjab, Pakistan, examined not sufficiently reported COVID-19 cases along with vaccine hesitancy, thereby affirming the practical significance of the MRDRMs methods. The findings demonstrate a substantial improvement in estimation accuracy accompanied by reduced variance compared to conventional approaches. The results highlighting the effectiveness of MRDRM-I and MRDRM-II as robust methods for sensitive data collection. The findings from this research highlight the potential of MRDRMs for enhancing the acquisition and evaluation regarding sensitive data, strengthening consistent, ethical, and tangible results in research related to public health. These improvements strengthen privacy protection and enhance the validity of responses in sensitive surveys, offering meaningful contributions to public health monitoring and evidence-based policy development at the global level. The proposed models can be applied in public health surveillance, epidemiological studies, social science research, and policy-sensitive domains requiring accurate and privacy-preserving data collection.

Introduction

In the course of sample surveys, conducting surveys on sensitive traits such as cheating, undeclared income, tax evasion, and induced abortions presents a unique challenge due to the prevalence of high refusal rates and inaccurate reporting. To mitigate these issues and obtain reliable data, Warner1 introduced the randomized response model (RRM). Originally developed for qualitative variables, Warner later adapted the method for quantitative variables by incorporating additive noise to ensure respondent privacy. The primary goal of RRM is to estimate the proportion of individuals exhibiting stigmatizing characteristics. This method has proven highly effective in assessing sensitive behaviors, including smoking prevalence, truancy, extramarital affairs, alcohol consumption, substance use, and violations of public health laws. Numerous researchers have proposed improvements to the qualitative RRM to enhance estimation efficiency. For example, Horvitz et al.2 extended Warner’s model by replacing one sensitive question with a non-sensitive question unrelated to the study variable. Greenberg et al.3 further advanced this approach by analyzing cases with both known and unknown proportions of unrelated traits. Moors4 demonstrated that a variant of Greenberg’s model exhibited enhanced efficiency. Folsom et al.5 introduced a model for cases where the population proportion of the non-sensitive characteristic was unknown. Leysieffer and Warner6 emphasized that the choice of a randomized response model should not be based solely on variance reduction but also on the level of privacy protection provided. Kim and Warde7 explored Warner’s RRM under stratified random sampling with optimal allocation to improve precision, and similar quantitative extensions were examined by Gupta and Shabbir8 and Kim and Elam9.

Inspired by the seminal work, the estimation of sensitive quantitative variables has evolved significantly since Warner’s pioneering work10. Greenberg et al.11 expanded on this by introducing a method that employed two quantitative questions one sensitive and the other non-sensitive to achieve unbiased estimation. This led to numerous advancements in randomized response models, with key contributions from Eichhorn and Hayre12, Gupta et al.13, and Pollock and Bek14. Gupta and Thornton15 proposed a model where a predetermined proportion of respondents provided true answers, while others scrambled their responses to improve privacy protection. Gupta and Shabbir16 highlighted that certain approximations were made in estimating the variances of the estimators. Gjestvang and Singh17 introduced a forced quantitative randomized response model, and Huang18 developed a model to estimate both the mean and sensitivity levels of stigmatizing variables. Later, Gjestvang and Singh19 proposed an innovative additive model in which respondents scrambled their answers for both assertions, leading to improved accuracy. Other contributions from Gupta et al.20,21, Huang22, Hussain and Khan23, Mehta et al.24, and Odumade and Singh25 further enhanced methodologies for estimating the mean and sensitivity of sensitive quantitative variables. Shahid et al.26 proposed two advanced randomized response models for the efficient estimation of mean and sensitivity in stigmatizing variables. A full list of abbreviations and symbols in conjunction with the contributions.

Following the contributions of Kumar and Singh27, notable advances have been achieved in tackling non-response bias through randomized scrambling techniques. Gupta et al.28 introduced an estimator for population variance based on the randomization strategy originally proposed by Diana and Perri29. Saleem et al.30 presented enhanced estimators for population means within randomized response frameworks. Zapata et al.31 proposed improvements to Warner’s classical technique, while Azeem et al.32 presented a comparative study of randomized response techniques using separate and combined metrics of efficiency and privacy. Additional contributions to randomized response methodologies have been made by Bhat33, Gupta34, Jenum et al.35, and Zahid et al.36, among others. Lovig et al.37 studied misleading reactions in RRT models, and Azeem et al.38,39 proposed a sample-size-independent evaluation metric.

The COVID-19 pandemic, which began in 2019, has had a profound impact on the quality of life in China40. It caused significant disruptions to both physical and mental health, creating complex interactions between various health determinants. Post-infection, physical symptoms were primarily characterized by respiratory distress, which, in severe cases, led to complications such as organ failure and death, thereby worsening adverse physical health outcomes41,42. Long-term sequelae reported globally included a range of symptoms such as sleep disturbances, anosmia, ageusia, persistent chest tightness, hair loss, fatigue, and cognitive decline43. A comprehensive global analysis revealed that the cumulative prevalence of long-term sequelae among COVID-19 survivors ranged44, placing an immense burden on healthcare systems substantially greater than that associated with comparable post-viral syndromes 45–51.

Although randomized response methodologies have been advanced by Hussain et al.52, Bhat33, Gupta34, Lovig et al.37, and Azeem et al.38,39, these studies primarily emphasize partial improvements in efficiency, behavioral response patterns, or evaluation criteria. The present study moves beyond these contributions by proposing MRDRM-I and MRDRM-II as more comprehensive frameworks that jointly estimate the population mean and sensitivity level of a sensitive quantitative variable under privacy protection. Their applicability to real-life public health data further strengthens the practical relevance of the proposed models and marks an important advancement over existing approaches.

Existing randomized response studies have largely focused on privacy protection and efficient estimation, with less emphasis on their practical applicability and real-life implications. In the modern era, where data-driven decisions shape public health and social policy, the need for precise and privacy-preserving methods for handling sensitive quantitative information has become more important than ever. The present study addresses the difficulty of obtaining precise estimates when respondents hesitate to provide truthful answers because of privacy concerns, fear, and social desirability bias, especially in real-life data settings. To overcome this problem, MRDRM-I and MRDRM-II are proposed as improved frameworks for accurate and privacy-protective estimation.

Acknowledging the complexity of this challenge, we sought innovative solutions through the development of MRDRMs. Drawing inspiration from Hussain et al.52, who addressed low respondent cooperation in sensitive surveys by introducing two- and three-stage optional randomized response models to improve participation, we propose two novel MRDRMs designed to obtain reliable information while ensuring respondent anonymity. The innovation in this study lies in the application of MRDRMs to estimate both the mean and sensitivity levels through a two-fold response approach, where respondents use two scrambling variables, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Y_{i}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Z_{i}}$$\end{document}, for each response. A key advantage of this methodology is that collecting the second response incurs no additional costs. To empirically validate the MRDRM-I and MRDRM-II models, we conducted a cross-sectional survey in districts of Faisalabad, Lahore, Multan, and Rawalpindi in Punjab, Pakistan, from June to August 2021, using structured questionnaires to assess underreported COVID-19 cases and vaccine hesitancy. Ensuring anonymity facilitates ethical data collection, aids public health authorities in designing targeted and cost-effective interventions, and ultimately enhances public health monitoring and policy development.

Strategic frameworks for reliable and confidential estimations

This section presents the theoretical foundation of the proposed strategies and derives the expressions for the unbiased estimators and their variances. Two additive and subtractive MRDRMs are proposed to estimate the population mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _X$$\end{document} and sensitivity level \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W$$\end{document} of the stigmatizing variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X$$\end{document} using a double-response mechanism. In this mechanism, the scrambling variables \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z_i$$\end{document} are added to, or subtracted from, the true response \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X$$\end{document} in two separate response stages. The use of both additive and subtractive scrambling helps balance possible over-reporting and under-reporting tendencies, thereby reducing response bias in sensitive data collection. At the same time, the randomized structure protects respondent anonymity and improves the reliability of the collected information. To reduce the burden of providing two responses, respondents were informed in advance that their answers would remain anonymous and that parameter estimation would be conducted only at the aggregate level, without tracing any response back to an individual participant.

The simulation parameters were chosen to represent realistic conditions commonly encountered in sensitive health survey research. Three sample sizes, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 100, 500,$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1000$$\end{document}, were considered to examine estimator performance under small, moderate, and large sample settings. The parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T$$\end{document} were selected to reflect different levels of scrambled and truthful reporting, respectively, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W$$\end{document} represented the sensitivity level of the study variable. For each scenario, 10,000 Monte Carlo replications were performed to ensure stable simulation outcomes and to provide a reliable assessment of estimator accuracy, variance reduction, and relative efficiency.

In both MRDRM-I and MRDRM-II, even if a respondent falls in the truthful-response category, the interviewer does not observe the underlying response mode that generated the reported pair, and the responses are interpreted only through the mixed probabilistic design at the aggregate level. Moreover, in the empirical implementation, privacy was further strengthened through individualized randomization tables, confidentiality procedures, and shredding of calculation sheets after use. Therefore, the proposed models preserve respondent anonymity and confidentiality in practice.

The Exponential distribution was chosen because its strictly positive support is fully compatible with our additive and subtractive mechanism, so \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X+Y_{1}+Z_{1}$$\end{document} remains an upper response and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X-Y_{2}-Z_{2}$$\end{document} remains a lower response, thereby preserving the intended interval-valued structure. If a Normal distribution were used directly, negative realizations could occur, causing the additive response to fall below X and the subtractive response to exceed X, which may destroy the ordered interval interpretation and even produce implausible values for sensitive quantitative data. Therefore, the use of the Exponential distribution is deliberate and methodologically appropriate for the proposed MRDRM-I and MRDRM-II designs.

The MRDRM-II model is introduced to overcome the limitations of the two-stage MRDRM-I by integrating a three-stage response mechanism. This enhancement improves respondent privacy and provides more flexible reporting probabilities with predetermined proportions. As a result, it reduces response bias and enhances estimator efficiency, making MRDRM-II more suitable for complex survey environments with varying sensitivity levels. The three-stage response mechanism enables more refined control over the information shared, offering a higher degree of anonymity and increasing the reliability of the collected data. Additionally, the flexibility in reporting allows for better accommodation of different types of sensitive information, resulting in more accurate and meaningful responses. By incorporating these improvements, MRDRM-II contributes to more robust and reliable statistical analysis in areas requiring high confidentiality.

Implementation of MRDRMs on COVID-19 data

The survey examined self-reported COVID-19 infection status and vaccine hesitancy, both of which are sensitive variables that are likely to be underreported. The dual-scrambling mechanism of the MRDRM protects respondent anonymity and supports more reliable estimation. This improves the integrity of sensitive public health data and makes the resulting estimates more useful for evidence-based decision-making. The results validate MRDRMs in generating accurate estimates of underreported health parameters, essential for effective pandemic management. The growing use of randomized response techniques in epidemiology45–51 further demonstrates the potential of MRDRMs to refine public health governance. This method increases cooperation and reduces bias. Informed consent was obtained from all eligible and interested participants before their participation in the study. They were approached and asked about their willingness to take part. Participants were provided with comprehensive information about the study’s background, objectives, potential benefits, procedures, and confidentiality measures.

The questionnaire employed in this study was derived for it, which applied survey within randomized response framework for sensitive public health data collection. The questionnaire was designed and validated to safeguard respondent privacy while ensuring data reliability. In the present study, the questionnaire was used in its original form without any modification. It also established methodological rigor for the current research context.

Strengths and limitations

This study introduces a rigorous and innovative MRDRM framework. The framework improves estimator precision while preserving respondent anonymity. By integrating additive and subtractive scrambling within a dual-response structure, the proposed models reduce social desirability bias and underreporting in sensitive numerical surveys. The theoretical derivations confirm the unbiasedness and efficiency of the estimators. These findings are further supported by extensive Monte Carlo simulations, which show substantial efficiency improvements over conventional randomized response techniques. The empirical application also demonstrates the robustness and practical relevance of the proposed models for public health governance and sensitive data estimation. The staged randomization design strengthens the statistical stability of the estimators across different sensitivity levels. The models provide unbiased and consistent parameter estimates under clearly defined assumptions, which reinforces their methodological soundness. The structured formulation of MRDRM-I and MRDRM-II also allows the models to be adapted to other sensitive quantitative research domains. However, the present study uses cross-sectional survey data from a defined geographical context. This provides strong internal validation, but broader cross-cultural examination is still needed. Although the proposed MRDRMs are statistically efficient, their application requires careful survey implementation and appropriate statistical expertise. The estimator performance is derived under theoretical distributional assumptions, which may vary across real-world settings. Therefore, practical implementation may require pilot calibration of the scrambling parameters to achieve optimal efficiency across different populations. Future studies using large-scale national datasets would further strengthen the external generalizability of the proposed framework.

Conclusion

This study proposes two innovative multi-stage randomized response models (MRDRMs), including a two-stage model and a three-stage model. Both models use additive and subtractive scrambling techniques to collect sensitive quantitative information while preserving respondent anonymity. The proposed frameworks provide unbiased and consistent estimators for the mean and sensitivity level of stigmatized quantitative variables. At the same time, they reduce disclosure risk and improve privacy protection. By incorporating double scrambling into the MRDRM structure, the proposed designs directly address major response biases, particularly social desirability bias and underreporting. This improves both the confidentiality of the response process and the statistical quality of the resulting estimates. The study therefore addresses an important problem in sensitive survey research: obtaining precise estimates when respondents may be unwilling to provide truthful answers due to privacy concerns, fear, or social pressure. A detailed examination of MRDRM-I and MRDRM-II clarifies their operational structures and relative estimation efficiencies. The results show that combining staged randomization with dual scrambling can reduce biased reporting and improve estimator precision across a wide range of parameter settings. Theoretical derivations, variance analysis, Monte Carlo simulations, and empirical application collectively demonstrate the methodological strength and practical usefulness of the proposed models. results supported by extensive Monte Carlo confirm that MRDRMs consistently outperform conventional randomized response procedures in terms of efficiency and accuracy, especially under higher sensitivity and stronger misreporting behavior. Beyond methodological contributions, the practical value of the proposed frameworks was validated through a cross sectional survey on under reported COVID 19 cases and vaccine hesitancy in Faisalabad, Lahore, Multan, and Rawalpindi (Punjab, Pakistan), showing that MRDRMs remain stable and reliable in real field conditions. The empirical application is based on cross-sectional data from selected districts of Punjab; therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution when considering broader generalization to other regions or populations. The results demonstrate the practical usefulness of the proposed models in real-world public health settings. Future research may extend these models to larger populations, other geographical contexts, and longitudinal survey designs. Overall, the proposed MRDRMs offer a flexible and implementable class of privacy preserving survey designs for sensitive numerical data, and they can be directly adapted for public health surveillance and policy relevant measurement problems where truthful reporting is difficult but high quality estimation is essential.
