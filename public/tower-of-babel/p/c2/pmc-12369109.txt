Pregnancy outcomes following hysteroscopic resection of uterine septum: does the size matter? a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Hadel Watad, Raanan Meyer, Michal Axelrod, Roy Mashiach, David Stockheim, Aya Mohr-Sasson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe benefits of surgical treatment of uterine septum malformation have recently been questioned. The aim of our study is to compare pregnancy outcomes following hysteroscopic resection of minor versus major septum.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted at a single tertiary university-affiliated medical center. All women who underwent hysteroscopic resection of uterine septum followed by a documented pregnancy between March 2010 to March 2019, were included. The septum size was assessed by ultrasound or during operative hysteroscopy by the surgeons. Two groups were defined based on septum size: 1. minor (< 50% uterine cavity); 2. major (≥ 50% uterine cavity). Cases with additional uterine malformations were excluded from the study. Data were compared between the groups before and following septum resection. A univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted.ResultsDuring the study period, 57 women met inclusion criteria, of these, 19 (33.3%) and 38 (66.7%) with minor and major septum, respectively. Baseline parameters were comparable between the groups. The rates of composite pregnancy complications before and following septum resection were also comparable between the two groups. Logistic regression analysis did not find septum size to be associated with pregnancy complications; however, comparing composite pregnancy complications prior and following septum resection revealed a trend of rate reduction in composite complications in the minor group (36.8% and 21.1% respectively) and a trend of increase in the major group (23.7% and 44.7% respectively).ConclusionsPregnancy outcomes following hysteroscopic resection of uterine septum were comparable between minor and major septum groups. Our results do not support septum removal to reduce pregnancy complications. However, due to the small sample size, further studies are required to strengthen our findings.Keywords: Hysteroscopy, Metroplasty, Pregnancy complications, Septostomy, Uterine septum

Introduction

Septate uterus is the most common Müllerian anomaly. The estimated prevalence is 0.2–2.3% in women of reproductive age, depending on the diagnostic method and classification system which differs considerably [1–3]. Moreover, it is the anomaly most amenable to surgical correction [4]. Multiple classification systems for Müllerian congenital malformations had been proposed, however, the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) classification is the most popular and has received the most acceptance in recent years [2].

Although many women diagnosed with septate uterus do not experience any reproductive difficulties [5], adverse reproductive outcomes have been attributed to septate uterus, including infertility, pregnancy loss, and poor obstetrical outcomes, such as malpresentation and preterm delivery [1, 6–11]. As such, the impact of a uterine septum on reproductive outcome is one of the most important and challenging dilemmas [12]. The lack of uniformity in diagnosis and treatment makes it difficult to interpret existing data and create treatment guidelines. Therefore, it's not surprising that there is no specific answer to the questions whether and when intervention is recommended in cases of septate uterus.

Several observational studies indicate that hysteroscopic septum resection (metroplasty) is associated with improved clinical pregnancy rates in women with infertility [8, 13–16], however, resection of the septum to improve pregnancy outcomes remains a subject of debate [6]. There is no convincing evidence concerning its effectiveness and safety. In 2021, Rikken et al. [17] published a randomized controlled trial comparing results following septum resection versus expectant management, and no improvement in reproductive outcomes was observed. Furthermore, there is insufficient evidence to conclude that obstetric outcomes are different when comparing the size as defined by length or width of uterine septa [6, 16, 18–22].

Due to the aforementioned reasons, we aimed in this study to compare pregnancy outcomes before and following hysteroscopic resection of minor (< 50% of the uterine cavity) and major (≥ 50% of the uterine cavity) uterine septum.

Materials and methods

This is a retrospective cohort study conducted at a tertiary, university affiliated medical center. All women who underwent hysteroscopic resection of uterine septum between March 2011 and March 2019, and who subsequently had at least one documented pregnancy following the resection, were included in the study. Cases were retrieved using electronic based search and included the words"septum","septate uterus","metroplasty"or"septum resection". The medical records including surgical report were thoroughly reviewed, and only appropriate cases were included in the study.

Uterine septa were diagnosed by ultrasound, hysterosalpingogram or hysteroscopy before the procedure.

Analysis was based on the assessment of septum size during hysteroscopy by the surgeon performing the procedures. In cases where septum size was not reported during hysteroscopy, ultrasonographic evaluation was used. Since the size of corpus uterus differs between patients, we believed that the ideal cut-off between small and large septum should depend on individual uterine cavity length. Therefore, in our study a cut-off of 50% of uterine cavity was arbitrarily chosen; and women were assigned to one of two groups: minor septum (< 50% uterine cavity) and major septum (≥ 50% uterine cavity). All procedures were performed by a single surgical team consisting of six highly skilled surgeons, all of whom are specialists in minimally invasive surgery.

Different hysteroscopic techniques for septum resection were used, including resection with cold scissors, unipolar or bipolar cautery. The decision was based on the surgeon's preference.

Demographic parameters including age, body mass index (BMI), medical history, parity, gravidity, abortions, ectopic pregnancies, past obstetric history prior and following septum resection including preterm delivery, preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), intrauterine growth retardation (IUGR), intrauterine fetal demise (IUFD), past medical history and surgeries were retrieved. Data from the surgical intervention including indication for evaluation, septum size, full vs. partial resection, confirmatory diagnostic hysteroscopic following septum resection and number of operative hysteroscopies were collected as well.

Miscarriage was defined as any nonviable pregnancy occurring up to 20 weeks gestation. Stillbirth was defined as any fetal death occurring after 20 weeks of pregnancy [23].

Primary outcome was defined as composite obstetric outcome composed of the occurrence of any of the following: IUGR, placental abruption, preterm labor before 37 weeks of gestation, PPROM before 37 weeks of gestation or missed abortion after 10 weeks of gestation.

The manuscript was written and edited according to the STROBE statement [24].

The study received approval from the Helsinki Institutional Review Board (reference number 6072–19-SMC), waiving the requirement for informed consent.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk or Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Data are presented as median and inter-quartile range (IQR) for categorical parameters. The chi-square and Fisher's exact tests were used for comparison between categorical variables. Logistic multivariate regression analysis was used to determine which factors were significantly and independently predictors for pregnancy complications following septum resection adjusted for potential confounding variables, including septum size, age, pregnancy complications prior to septum resection. Significance was accepted at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using the IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM SPSS v.22; IBM Corporation Inc, Armonk, NY, USA).

One case had a uterine rupture during labor. In this case, a uterine septum was diagnosed during infertility evaluation and removed. Following septum removal, the woman conceived spontaneously and underwent an elective cesarean delivery at term due to malpresentation; two years later, during her second delivery, she underwent an urgent cesarean section due to failed vacuum extraction during a trial of labor. The rates of composite pregnancy complications following septum resection were comparable (21.1% vs. 44.7%, respectively; p = 0.081). Comparing composite pregnancy complications prior and following septum resection revealed a trend toward a reduction in composite complications in the minor group (36.8% and 21.1%, respectively) and a trend toward an increase in the major group (23.7% and 44.7%, respectively).

Using a t-test to compare composite pregnancy outcomes in relation to septum size, did not find any correlation between septum size and pregnancy complications (p = 0.18).

Discussion

The main findings of the study are: 1. Adverse pregnancy outcomes were comparable between the minor and major septum group before and following septum resection. 2. No correlation between septum size and pregnancy complication was found.

Hysteroscopic septum resection is considered a worldwide standard procedure for women with a septate uterus. The positive impact of hysteroscopic metroplasty for uterine anomalies, especially a septate uterus, has been addressed in previous studies, and the main impact reported was the prevention of pregnancy loss [25, 26]; However, as previously mentioned, this issue remains debatable among gynecologists and even among patients [27].

Moreover, the benefit of hysteroscopic septum resection in improving pregnancy outcomes remains controversial. According to a previously published study, hysteroscopic metroplasty for a septate uterus was not found to be a risk factor for adverse obstetric outcomes [28]. In another study [29], septum removal was not associated with an increased risk of placental complications compared to other hysteroscopic procedures. However, these studies did not assess non-placental pregnancy complications and did not compare outcomes before and after septum resection. The lack of uniform diagnostic criteria and the absence of a standardized surgical approach are important confounders in interpreting of the existing data. In the TRUST (The randomized uterine septum transection) trial [17] there was no evidence of a difference in clinical pregnancy, ongoing pregnancy, pregnancy loss or preterm birth rates between women who underwent septum resection and those who received expectant management. However, the effect of septum size was not addressed in this trial.

The suggested pathogenetic mechanisms by which uterine septa cause obstetric complications and/or reproductive failure are diverse [30], including inadequate uterine vascularization [31], uncoordinated uterine contractions [32, 33], altered composition of the fibroelastic connective tissue within the septum [33] and/or an irregular sensitivity to hormonal changes in the septal endometrium [34]. Considering the proposed mechanisms, it is logical to hypothesize that the size of the septum may affect adverse pregnancy outcomes. Our study was designed to evaluate this hypothesis.

Adverse pregnancy outcomes were found comparable between the minor and major septum group. There was a trend forward a decrease in composite pregnancy complications after septum resection in the minor septum group, whereas a trend toward an increase was observed in the major septum group. However, none of these trends were statistically significant. The rate of spontaneous abortion after septum resection did not differ from the rate prior to septum resection. This finding is in contrast to a previous study [30]. Paradisi et al. conducted a retrospective study that included 112 non-parous women who underwent septum removal. In that study reproductive outcomes were compared before and after the procedure. Hysteroscopic resection of uterine septum was found to significantly improve reproductive outcomes irrespective of the septum size (small septum defined as a septum length ≤ 2.5 cm and large septum > 2.5 cm).

The difference between the current study and the previous publication can be explained by the fact that all women in the previous study were non-parous with infertility or abortion history, whereas in our study we had a heterogenous group of women including history of poor pregnancy outcomes. This suggests that the removal of uterine septum may prove effective in addressing reproductive issues, but not necessarily adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Clinical implications

Based on our findings, it appears that adverse pregnancy outcomes after septum resection may not be directly related to the size of the septum. This suggests that making decisions about whether to operate based on septum size should be approached with caution, and the decision to intervene should not be based primarily on septum size as the key factor in decision-making. Although it is pathophysiologically plausible that septum removal can improve pregnancy outcomes by enhancing the uterine cavity—an effect studied in fertility treatments [35–37]—our results did not demonstrate this positive effect. However, the sample size and study design are major limitations of our study.

Strengths and Limitations: Our study has several important limitations that warrant mention. First, the sample size is relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, the retrospective design of our study may introduce selection bias, potentially influencing the results. Attempts were made to minimize inaccuracies were made by manual validation of individual medical files and by the preliminary excluding of patients with missing or incomplete data regarding septum parameters. Moreover, septum size assessment was not—standardized and was based upon provider’s opinion. Additional limitation is the fact that data about subsequent pregnancies were retrieved from our medical records, and it might be reasonable to assume that some patients received follow-up care at other facilities, thus possibly affecting our results. However, we should mention that our medical center is a tertiary referral center. Thus, complex cases are usually treated at our facility. Another limitation is the fact that our study was not designed to investigate the efficacy of surgical treatment in infertile patients. At last, second-look hysteroscopy was not performed in all cases.

Our study has several strengths; we investigated the impact of different septum size on reproductive outcomes, a poorly studied parameter in previous works. We had complete data about septum size which helped stratification of the minor and major septum group. We also had a follow up data about subsequent pregnancies and complications which made it possible to compare pre and post procedure composite pregnancy outcomes.

Conclusions

According to our study, pregnancy complications were not associated with uterine septum size. The conclusions from this study are limited by its small sample size and possible lack of generalizability. Further randomized controlled trials are required to strengthen our findings.
