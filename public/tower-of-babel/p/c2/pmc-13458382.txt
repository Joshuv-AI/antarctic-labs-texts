Impact of an Acute Kidney Injury Prevention Bundle in High-risk Intensive Care Unit Patients: A Single Center Before–After Study
Authors: Vittobaraju Muralidharan, Anusha Cherian, Muthapillai Senthilnathan, Pankaj Kundra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsAcute kidney injury (AKI) is a frequent and serious complication in critically ill patients and is associated with increased morbidity, mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and significant healthcare costs. We evaluated the impact of an AKI prevention bundle on the incidence, severity, and outcomes in patients at high-risk of developing AKI in the intensive care unit (ICU).Patients and methodsIn this single-center before-and-after study, high-risk patients were identified using a prediction score. A bundle comprising daily assessments, risk factor modification, nephrotoxin avoidance, hemodynamic optimization, and renal dose adjustment was implemented after a 2 months education period. Preimplementation lasted 6 months, followed by 8 months of bundle application. Outcomes including AKI incidence and severity, need for renal replacement therapy (RRT), length of ventilation days and ICU stay, and mortality were compared between pre- and post-implementation groups.ResultsA total of 374 patients were included, 187 in the pre and 187 in the post implementation phase. The primary outcome, incidence of AKI within 72 hours of ICU admission, was lower in the post-bundle implementation group than pre-bundle implementation group (21.9 vs 27.8%); however, the difference was not statistically significant. Overall incidence of AKI was lower in the post-bundle group than in the pre-bundle group (28.9 vs 37.9%); while between 72 hours and 1week, incidences were 6.9 vs 10.1%, which were not statistically significant. Importantly, moderate-to-severe AKI was significantly reduced post-bundle implementation (13.9 vs 25.6%, p = 0.042). This group also showed significantly shorter ICU stays (6.17 vs 7.25 days, p = 0.03) and reduced mechanical ventilation duration (4.34 vs 5.30 days, p = 0.02). Renal replacement therapy requirement, 30 days mortality, and persistent renal dysfunction were lower post-bundle implementation but without statistical significance.ConclusionWhile the AKI prevention bundle did not significantly affect overall AKI incidence in high-risk ICU patients, it was associated with reductions in moderate-to-severe AKI, ICU stay, and duration of mechanical ventilation. A simple clinical risk score can identify patients for early intervention, and larger multicenter studies are warranted to confirm these findings.Keywords: Acute kidney injury, AKI prevention bundle, Bundle care, Critically ill patients, Renal replacement therapy

Highlights

All patients admitted to intensive care unit (ICU) should be assessed for the risk of developing acute kidney injury (AKI). Implementing an AKI prevention bundle especially in this group of patients may help to reduce overall AKI incidence and lower moderate to severe AKI, ventilation duration, and ICU stay, with nonsignificant reductions in renal replacement therapy (RRT) use, mortality, and persistent renal dysfunction.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common and serious condition, affecting nearly 57% of critically ill patients in the intensive care unit (ICU).1 It is associated with prolonged ICU and hospital stays, high morbidity and mortality, and increased healthcare costs.2 Even mild AKI is associated with short-term complications such as acid–base disturbances, electrolyte abnormalities, and fluid imbalance, as well as adverse long-term outcomes.3 Most patients recover renal function, however, there is increasing evidence showing that it strongly influences prognosis and risk of progression to chronic kidney disease (CKD), major cardiovascular events, and mortality after discharge.4,5 The risk increases with greater severity and longer duration of AKI. Those with subclinical AKI and those who showed an initial recovery also are at increased risk of progression of kidney disease.6 Hence, early recognition, real-time monitoring, and preventive measures are essential to prevent further renal damage in a timely manner. In 2012, kidney disease: Improving global outcomes (KDIGO) recommended the implementation of “AKI care bundle” in order to prevent and manage AKI. The bundle includes optimization of fluids and hemodynamics, avoiding nephrotoxins, and hyperglycemia.7 However, there are no standards specified for the component and their targets and there is a lack of evidence for benefit. Hence, their implementation remains inconsistent. Care bundles have been successfully applied in ICU settings for conditions such as ventilator-associated pneumonia, catheter-related infections, and sepsis, improving patient outcomes. Acute kidney injury care bundled approach for AKI prevention has shown variable success in cardiac and surgical populations.8 However, limited data exist in critically ill patients at high risk of AKI especially from the Asian population. The incidence is the highest in low and low middle income countries.9 Further, many studies have not shown a benefit of implementing such a bundle.10 This may be due to the fact these studies included patients irrespective of their baseline risk for developing AKI. Hence, we hypothesized that by implementing an AKI care bundle in those patients who are at a high risk of developing AKI in the ICU, the incidence of AKI and their severity can be reduced.

The primary objective of this study was to compare the incidence of AKI of any stage before and after implementation of the AKI care bundle in patients at high risk of developing AKI within the first 72 hours of ICU admission. The secondary objectives were to evaluate the incidence of AKI between 72 hours and 1 week of ICU admission, assess compliance with the bundle, and compare outcomes between groups, including the need for renal replacement therapy (RRT), 30 days mortality, incidence of persistent renal dysfunction at 30 days, ICU length of stay (LOS), and duration of mechanical ventilation.

Patients and Methods

After obtaining approval from the institutional review board, the study was registered in Clinical Trial Registry-India (CTRI number: CTRI/2023/01/048650).

This clinical study was designed as a before and after implementation study and was conducted between January 2023 and May 2024.

Since it is a quality improvement project and was done at the departmental level, all eligible patients were included in the study and waiver of consent was obtained from the institutional ethics committee.

All consecutive critically ill patients over the age of 18 years admitted in the ICU, at an increased risk of developing AKI based on the risk prediction score (Table 1) were included in this study. This also included medical and post-surgical patients (Fig. 1).

Patients with a score >5 were classified as high risk for AKI based on the previously validated AKI prediction model described by Malhotra et al. and were included in our study.11

Patients already initiated on RRT or with admission creatinine >1.5 mg/dL and patients who were readmitted were excluded from the trial.

The study was conducted in three phases.

Phase I: Pre-bundle (control) group baseline data collection: During the first 6 months, patients were treated according to usual care.

Phase II (education phase, 2 months) involved educating all ICU personnel on the AKI prevention bundle through presentations, discussions, pamphlets, daily reinforcement during rounds, and a trial implementation. Acute kidney injury prevention care bundle consist of 10 key elements as shown in Table 2. Fluid was optimized based on fluid balance, lactate levels, presence of shock, and hypervolemia, hourly urine output. Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was maintained above 65 mm Hg with fluids or vasopressors. Hyperglycemia avoidance was not specified as an intervention, as glycemic control is part of our standard ICU care for all patients.

Acute kidney injury prevention bundle

Phase III (post-bundle implementation phase, 8 months) involved incorporating the AKI prevention care bundle into daily ICU workflow with data collection from included patients, bundle checklist completed once every 24 hours during morning rounds. The bundle components were assessed daily for the whole length of ICU stay of the patient. Risk for AKI was reviewed every day, fluid status and balance were checked every day and further fluids planned for next 24 hours, nephrotoxic drugs were reviewed, creatinine was checked every day. Mean arterial pressure of >65 mm Hg was maintained at all times for the patients.

Acute kidney injury was defined according to the KDIGO criteria based on daily serum creatinine measurements. Urine output criteria were not applied because reliable and standardized measurement was not feasible in the study setting.

Persistent renal dysfunction was defined as when creatinine levels remained high after 2 weeks of initial diagnosis of AKI. Acute kidney injury recovery was defined as return of serum creatinine levels to <1.5 times baseline, no more RRT requirement and serum creatinine to within 25% of baseline creatinine. The patients were followed-up at 30 days in hospital or contacted telephonically at 30 days if they were discharged to find out about mortality and RRT requirement.

Baseline data including age, gender, admission type, acute physiology and chronic health evaluation (APACHE) II score, presence of comorbidities, and AKI prediction score were collected form all patients. Fluid balance, compliance of bundles, mortality at 30 days, the need for RRT up to 30 days, the occurrence and severity of AKI and the length of ICU stay and the duration of mechanical ventilation was recorded. The incidence and severity of AKI was assessed according to the creatinine levels in each patient in accordance to the KDIGO definition guidelines.

Primary outcome was to compare the incidence of AKI of any stage before and after implementation of the AKI care bundle in patients at high risk of developing AKI within the first 72 hours of ICU admission.

Secondary outcomes were to evaluate the incidence of AKI between 72 hours and 1 week of ICU admission, assess compliance with the bundle, and compare outcomes between groups, including the need for RRT, 30 days mortality, incidence of persistent renal dysfunction at 30 days, ICU LOS, and duration of mechanical ventilation.

In a study conducted by Hoste et al., the incidence of AKI was found to be 57%.1 We expected 15% reduction in incidence of AKI after implementation of AKI CARE bundle. To achieve a power of 80% and level of significance of 5%, sample was calculated as 174 in each group. To compensate for the dropouts, additional 10% of sample size has been added and final sample size was calculated as 374 with 187 patients in each group.

The continuous variables were expressed as mean with standard deviation and compared using a student's t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test, depending on the distribution. Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of data. The categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using a Chi-square test. The data were analyzed using the statistical software Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 19.0 (IBM, Armonk, New york, United States of America).

Results

Of the 420 patients screened, 46 were excluded and a total of 374 patients who were at high risk of developing AKI upon ICU admission were included in the study. One hundred eighty-seven patients were included prior to bundle implementation (pre-bundle group) and 187 patients were studied post-bundle implementation (post-bundle group).

The primary outcome, the occurrence of AKI within 72 hours of ICU admission according to the KDIGO classification system, was observed in 52 patients (27.8%) in the pre-bundle group and in 41 (21.9%) post-bundle group [p = 0.19; OR, 0.73 (95% CI, 0.454–1.17); absolute risk reduction (ARR), 5.88% (95% CI, −2.8 to 14.6%)], the difference was not statistically significant, possibly due to limited power of the study.

Development of AKI between 72 hours and 1 week of ICU admission was observed in 19 patients (10.1%) in the pre-bundle group and 13 (6.9%) in the post-bundle group [p = 0.27; OR, 0.66 (95% CI, 0.316–1.38); ARR, 3.21% (95% CI, −2.4 to 8.8%)] (Table 4).

The AKI prevention care bundle was carried out on 1,061 instances out of the total 1,154 expected instances. The bundle compliance was 91.9%. Renal dose adjustment and discontinuation of nephrotoxic agents following the identification of AKI represented the most challenging aspect of adherence to the care bundle.

Discussion

We conducted a before-and-after study in 374 high-risk ICU patients (187 pre-bundle, 187 post-bundle) to assess the impact of a bundled approach targeted at preventing AKI in patients who were expected to have a high risk of developing AKI in the ICU. Although the incidence of AKI was lower in the post-bundle (28.9 vs 37.9%), it was not statistically significant despite a high bundle compliance rate (91.9%). We observed a significant reduction in the rates of moderate to severe AKI (stages 2–3).

The absence of a statistically significant reduction in overall AKI incidence may have several explanations. First, the study may have been underpowered to detect modest differences in AKI incidence. Second, preventive bundles may be more effective in limiting AKI progression rather than preventing early stage AKI, which could explain the observed reduction in stages 2–3 AKI.

Among the bundle components, avoidance of nephrotoxic drugs posed the greatest challenge in implementing the bundle. This likely reflects the clinical complexity of ICU patients, particularly those with sepsis who require broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy or other potentially nephrotoxic agents.

Current studies reporting implementation of AKI bundles show varying results. Göcze et al. and Zarbock et al., demonstrated that bundle implementation reduced moderate to severe AKI in surgical patients enriched by a biomarker, though overall AKI incidence did not always reach significance.12,13 Similarly, Meersch et al. and Couturier et al. showed significant reductions in AKI with bundle use in biomarker-identified high-risk cardiac surgical patients while Halmy et al. found no significant reduction in overall AKI after major non-cardiac surgery but did report fewer severe cases and shorter ICU stays.14–16 Our study is in line with most of the studies that we did not find a significant decrease in AKI before and after implementation of the bundles.

In a recently published narrative review exploring the endpoints of studies involving AKI, it was observed that as association between stage 1 AKI and hospital mortality was not found to be significant for critically ill patients, stages 2–3 AKI might be a more appropriate endpoint especially for interventions that carry some risk.17 Additionally, a recent study reported that compared to biomarker-negative stage 1 AKI in septic patients, biomarker-positive stage 1 had a much higher risk of death.18 Thus, preventing stages 2–3 as well as biomarker-positive stage 1 might be a better endpoint. The review concluded that trials reporting complications of AKI in hierarchical composites maybe of greater clinical relevance. In our study, predictive enrichment was applied to identify patients at risk of AKI and to target the implementation of the AKI prevention bundle. We did not use a biomarker as this would increase the cost and reduce the feasibility in resource limited settings.

In our cohort, RRT requirement was reduced (OR = 0.61) but not significant, consistent with findings by Göcze et al. and Schanz et al.12,19 However, like Halmy et al., we observed fewer RRT days following implementation of the bundle.16 Importantly, bundle implementation significantly shortened mechanical ventilation and ICU stay. These outcomes were not consistently achieved in earlier trials. Mortality and persistent renal dysfunction were both lower in post-bundle group but not significant, in line with previous studies. Overall, our results suggest that while AKI bundles may not consistently reduce overall AKI incidence, they appear effective in lowering moderate to severe AKI and improving ICU outcomes, comparable to prior evidence.

A significantly lower cumulative positive fluid balance was observed on day 7 in the post-bundle implementation group, irrespective of the development of AKI. The reduction in positive fluid balance observed in the post-bundle group may reflect improved attention to fluid stewardship, which is a key component of most AKI prevention bundles.

Strengths and Limitations

The principal strength of our study lies in the inclusion of all high-risk patients admitted to the ICU, which enhances the external validity and generalizability of our findings compared with prior studies that were restricted to specific patient cohorts. In addition, the study specifically focused on patients at high risk of developing AKI during their ICU stay. Targeting this population is clinically relevant, as interventions are more likely to yield a meaningful reduction in AKI incidence and associated morbidity when applied to patients with an elevated baseline risk.

Our study is limited by its single-center, non-blinded design, and small sample size. The before–after study design is susceptible to bias from secular trends and evolving ICU practices over time. In addition, the post-implementation phase may have been susceptible to a Hawthorne effect, as awareness of observation and participation in a quality-improvement initiative may itself have improved clinician behavior and adherence to supportive ICU practices independent of the intervention bundle.

Interrupted time-series analysis was not performed; therefore, temporal changes independent of the intervention could not be fully accounted for, limiting causal inference. In addition, while baseline characteristics between groups were comparable, we did not perform any adjustment to control for potential confounders. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Furthermore, AKI diagnosis was based solely on serum creatinine criteria, as urine output measurements were not reliably standardized in our setting. This may have resulted in underdiagnosis of AKI, particularly early or transient stage 1 AKI cases. Sensitivity analyses were not performed to assess the robustness of the findings under different analytical assumptions. Although, bundle compliance was high overall, we did not analyze compliance for individual bundle components or contribution of each component to the prevention of AKI separately. Hence it remains unclear which specific elements of the bundle contributed most to the observed effects.

The observed effect sizes were modest, and therefore, the clinical impact of bundle implementation should be interpreted cautiously until validated in larger multicenter studies.

While the AKI prevention bundle did not significantly affect overall AKI incidence in high-risk ICU patients, it was associated with reductions in moderate-to-severe AKI, ICU stay, and duration of mechanical ventilation. These findings highlight the potential value of early risk stratification using clinical scoring systems and targeted preventive strategies. Further large multicenter studies are required to confirm these results and determine their generalizability across diverse critical care settings.

Our study differs from previous investigations in that it was conducted in a general ICU population rather than a specific subgroup of critically ill patients. In addition, risk enrichment was based on a simple clinical risk score, which is more feasible and scalable in resource-limited settings compared with biomarker-based strategies.

These findings support the feasibility of implementing pragmatic AKI prevention strategies in resource-limited ICU settings.
