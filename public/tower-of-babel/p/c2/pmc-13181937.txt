Severe baclofen intoxication managed with hemodialysis: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Soufiane Abdouh, Othmane Benlenda, Hicham Nassik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBaclofen, a γ-aminobutyric acid-B (GABAB) receptor agonist commonly prescribed for muscle spasticity, is eliminated primarily by the kidneys; therefore, toxicity is more likely and more prolonged in patients with renal impairment. Acute baclofen overdose can cause profound central nervous system depression, leading to respiratory failure and coma. Management relies mainly on supportive care, and extracorporeal treatments such as hemodialysis remain controversial.Case presentationWe report the case of a 16-year-old Moroccan female who developed coma and respiratory depression following a suspected baclofen overdose, with an estimated ingestion of 300 mg. She required endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation. Due to profound coma with respiratory depression, hemodialysis was initiated in addition to supportive care, despite preserved renal function and the unavailability of baclofen measurements. Neurological recovery occurred gradually over 72 h without apparent long-term complications.ConclusionsThis case underscores the importance of recognizing baclofen toxicity and prioritizing supportive care consistent with the Extracorporeal Treatments In Poisoning (EXTRIP) Workgroup recommendations against routine hemodialysis in preserved renal function. Although hemodialysis was instituted due to profound presentation, its efficacy is unproven, and recovery is attributable to spontaneous clearance rather than dialytic effect.

Introduction

Baclofen is a γ-aminobutyric acid receptor agonist widely prescribed for the management of spasticity, particularly in patients with spinal cord and other neurological disorders [1, 2]. Although generally safe at therapeutic doses, baclofen intoxication can cause profound central nervous system (CNS) depression, leading to respiratory failure and coma. Management of acute baclofen poisoning is primarily supportive, and the role of extracorporeal treatments such as hemodialysis (HD) remains controversial, especially in patients with preserved renal function. Recent reviews and Extracorporeal Treatments In Poisoning (EXTRIP) guidance emphasize that although baclofen is pharmacokinetically dialyzable, extracorporeal treatment has not been shown to improve clinical outcomes in patients with preserved kidney function [3–6]. In this context, we report a 16-year-old female with severe baclofen intoxication, in whom HD was initiated despite normal renal function; the case illustrates the challenges of decision-making and interpretation in the absence of serum baclofen measurements.

Case presentation

A 16-year-old Moroccan female presented to the emergency department after being found unconscious at home. On initial examination, the patient was unresponsive, with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 3/15, hypotonia, and miosis, without fever or signs of head trauma. Vital signs revealed a blood pressure of 100/70 mmHg, heart rate of 51 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 14 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 94% on room air. Lung auscultation demonstrated diffuse ronchi with airway obstruction due to secretions; therefore, the patient was promptly intubated, placed on mechanical ventilation, and transferred to the medical intensive care unit (MICU). Capillary blood glucose was elevated at 1.48 g/L. Pressure sores on the back and heels were noted, suggesting prolonged immobilization. Cardiac auscultation was normal, and the remainder of the physical examination was unremarkable. Laboratory investigations revealed elevated muscle enzyme levels, attributed to rhabdomyolysis secondary to prolonged immobilization, while liver function tests, complete blood count, serum electrolytes, and renal function were within normal limits (Table 1). Head computed tomography showed no evidence of cerebral edema, stroke, or intracranial hemorrhage. Additional investigations, including blood and urine cultures, a urine toxicology screen, and lumbar puncture, were negative. Electrocardiography findings were normal.

Metabolic encephalopathy was considered in the differential diagnosis; however, laboratory investigations revealed no significant metabolic abnormalities, making this diagnosis unlikely. The diagnosis became clearer shortly after admission when a family member brought an empty bottle of baclofen 5-mg tablets and reported that the patient’s father used baclofen for a spinal cord injury. Based on the clinical presentation, we made a presumptive diagnosis of baclofen intoxication, with an estimated ingested dose of 300 mg. Because serum baclofen level testing was unavailable, management included aggressive intravenous (IV) hydration and urinary alkalinization to mitigate the risk of acute kidney injury associated with rhabdomyolysis, and a single session of intermittent HD using high-efficiency, high-flux membranes. Hemodialysis parameters included a blood flow rate of 200–250 mL/min, a dialysate flow rate of 500 mL/min, and a dialysate composition of 3 mEq/L potassium and 1.50 mEq/L calcium.

The patient demonstrated marked neurological recovery, achieving a GCS of 13/15 at 48 h and progressing to complete neurological recovery with GCS 15/15 by 72 h, with concurrent normalization of elevated muscle enzyme levels. She was successfully extubated on day 3 and discharged from the MICU after 1 week in baseline functional status. Upon recovery, she confirmed ingestion of 60 tablets of baclofen 5 mg (total dose 300 mg) and was assessed by the child and adolescent mental health team, which diagnosed a depressive disorder with suicidal intent. Safeguarding measures were implemented, including family counseling, restriction of access to medications at home, notification of social services, and arrangement of close outpatient psychiatric follow-up (Table 2). Discussion

Baclofen, a selective GABAB receptor agonist, exerts its inhibitory effects on monosynaptic and polysynaptic reflexes within the medulla [7]. While initially synthesized in 1962 by Heinrich Keberle and investigated for epilepsy treatment in 1972, it proved ineffective for this purpose [8]. Its therapeutic role evolved, becoming a mainstay in managing spasticity in adults with spinal cord injury or multiple sclerosis [9], and in children with cerebral palsy [10].

Like all medications, baclofen carries the risk of adverse effects, particularly with oral administration. Due to its limited blood–brain barrier penetration, higher doses are often required to achieve therapeutic levels, increasing the risk of sedation, weakness, orthostatic hypotension, confusion, respiratory depression, cardiac conduction abnormalities [11], and even coma [12, 13]. Careful monitoring of respiratory and cardiovascular function is crucial, especially in patients with pre-existing cardiac, pulmonary, or neuromuscular conditions.

Following oral administration, baclofen is efficiently absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, with maximal plasma concentrations typically achieved within 1–4 h [5, 14–17], and is renally eliminated unchanged via glomerular filtration [14]. Dosage adjustments are necessary in patients with renal insufficiency to mitigate neurotoxicity and the elevated risk of respiratory depression [15, 18].

In overdose, however, baclofen elimination becomes prolonged, likely due to saturation of renal clearance pathways and delayed redistribution from peripheral and CNS compartments [19–21]. Pharmacokinetic studies consistently demonstrate that baclofen is efficiently cleared by HD, with a marked shortening of the apparent elimination half-life, particularly in patients with impaired renal function [22, 23]. Baclofen’s low protein binding (approximately 30%) and low volume of distribution (0.83 L/Kg) contribute to its dialyzability [23]. However, clinical recovery lags behind biochemical clearance due to delayed redistribution from the CNS, as baclofen diffuses slowly across the blood–brain barrier. This explains why serum elimination half-life may not reflect the slower elimination rate from the CNS [21–24].

While baclofen is dialyzable, expert consensus underscores the lack of compelling evidence supporting routine HD in patients with normal renal function [4, 5]. Current clinical evidence does not demonstrate that HD shortens duration of coma, reduces mechanical ventilation time, or improves neurological sequelae in this population. Dialyzability alone does not guarantee improved patient-centered outcomes [5].

A review of reported cases of baclofen intoxication (Table 3), including the present one, shows that severe toxicity can occur in both healthy individuals with normal renal function and in patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD), but at markedly different dose ranges. In patients with preserved renal function, clinically significant baclofen toxicity typically follows a single, acute oral ingestion of several hundred milligrams or more; however, our review identified 60 mg as the lowest reported dose. In contrast, in ESRD, toxicity occurs after smaller total exposures, with doses reported as low as 20 mg. This pattern reflects baclofen’s dependence on glomerular filtration for elimination and its prolonged half-life in renal failure [19, 25–28].

When cases are stratified by renal status, management patterns and recovery trajectories diverge. Among patients with normal renal function, most reports describe recovery with supportive care alone, including airway protection, mechanical ventilation when indicated, hemodynamic support, temperature control, and seizure management. With optimized supportive care, awakening commonly occurs within 24–72 h, and fatal outcomes or persistent neurological sequelae appear uncommon in the available literature [29, 30]. In contrast, patients with ESRD or markedly reduced glomerular filtration may develop more prolonged central nervous system depression after relatively small baclofen exposures; in this subgroup, clinicians more often rely on hemodialysis to enhance clearance of circulating baclofen and potentially shorten the clinical course.

The EXTRIP Workgroup recommends against routine extracorporeal treatment for acute baclofen poisoning in patients with normal renal function, citing very low-quality evidence for clinical benefit. Hemodialysis is suggested only for selected patients with severe toxicity and impaired renal clearance [5].

Hemodialysis was initiated in our patient despite preserved renal function because of the severity of presentation and the anticipated need for prolonged organ support. This decision represents an escalation beyond guideline-supported indications, rather than evidence supporting broader use of HD in patients with preserved renal function. A key limitation is the absence of serum baclofen measurements before, during, and after hemodialysis. This represents a fundamental constraint that precludes any mechanistic evaluation of extracorporeal removal, dialytic clearance rates, or pharmacokinetic modeling. Accordingly, the efficacy of hemodialysis cannot be inferred, and the temporal association with awakening remains compatible with spontaneous recovery and CNS redistribution.

Hemodialysis also carries procedural and physiologic risks, particularly in hemodynamically unstable, intubated patients. Potential complications include intradialytic hypotension, vascular access bleeding or thrombosis, catheter-related infection and electrolyte disturbances [52–55]. Because supportive care alone is usually sufficient and outcomes are generally favorable in patients with normal renal function, exposing patients to these procedural risks without proven benefit is difficult to justify outside highly selected rescue scenarios.

Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of recognizing baclofen toxicity and prioritizing supportive care as the cornerstone of management in patients with preserved renal function consistent with the EXTRIP Workgroup recommendations. Although hemodialysis was initiated due to profound coma and respiratory depression, its efficacy is unproven, recovery is attributable to spontaneous clearance rather than dialytic effect, and this case should not be interpreted as supporting routine hemodialysis use.
