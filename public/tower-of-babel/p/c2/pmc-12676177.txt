Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) programs are compared on short-term outcomes, so robust risk adjustment is essential. In the United States, this relies on the American College of Cardiology National Cardiovascular Data Registry (NCDR) CathPCI Registry and its validated in-hospital PCI mortality models (predicted risk of mortality [PROM]).1,2 Health systems are exploring whether social risk should inform quality improvement. The Distressed Communities Index (DCI) is a residential ZIP code–level composite from the Economic Innovation Group based on 7 indicators (no high school diploma, adults not working, poverty rate, median income ratio to the state, housing vacancy, change in employment, change in business establishments), scored from 0 to 100. Higher values indicate greater distress, with data often grouped into deciles or quintiles and linked using the ZIP code of the patient's residence at admission.3

Because DCI captures place-based context rather than bedside clinical severity, its ideal role in PCI quality measurement remains unsettled, whether for individual risk adjustment or population-level equity planning.4Take-Home Messages•Keep PROM for short-term mortality benchmarking.•Use DCI to target equity interventions, not to recalibrate individual risk predictions.

Project Rationale

This hypothesis-driven study tested whether neighborhood socioeconomic distress, as measured by the DCI, provides incremental discrimination beyond the CathPCI PROM for in-hospital PCI mortality. Our center serves a heterogeneous catchment that spans highly distressed and less distressed ZIP codes, and stakeholders are evaluating the role of social risk in quality measurement.

Project Description

We conducted a single-center study at Barnes-Jewish Hospital using NCDR CathPCI data from November 2022 to December 2024. The dataset contained 1,847 PCI admissions. We excluded 107 admissions that were not mortality-risk eligible or could not be linked to DCI (missing or invalid residence ZIP code), leaving 1,740 eligible admissions. Because some patients had multiple admissions, we randomly selected 1 index admission per patient to avoid within-patient correlation, yielding a final cohort of 1,551 unique patients. The outcome was in-hospital mortality. We fit 3 logistic regression models (PROM only, DCI only, PROM + DCI). Discrimination was assessed with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with differences tested by the nonparametric DeLong test. Differences in DCI across subgroups were assessed with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Kruskal-Wallis test. A 2-sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Project Deliverables

The project yielded 3 outputs: a defined cohort with 1 index PCI per patient and ZIP code–to-DCI linkage; AUCs for PROM, DCI, and PROM + DCI; and a 1-page summary of DCI associations with race, sex, insurance type, PCI indication, cardiogenic shock, in-hospital mortality, and out-of-hospital cardiac arrest.

Project Outcome

In-hospital mortality occurred in 54 of the 1,551 patients (3.5%). PROM alone achieved an AUC of 0.93; DCI alone, 0.61; and PROM + DCI, 0.88 (Figure 1). Discussion

In this single-center quality improvement analysis, the CathPCI PROM retained excellent discrimination for in-hospital mortality, whereas DCI had poor standalone discrimination and did not improve prediction when added to PROM. This pattern reinforces a well-described principle in PCI benchmarking: short-term mortality after PCI is dominated by acute clinical severity and procedural factors already embedded in contemporary registry models.5

These results do not diminish the importance of social determinants of health. In our cohort, DCI was associated with multiple patient and presentation characteristics, and external literature links community socioeconomic distress with worse downstream outcomes and readmissions over longer horizons. Our project was not designed to evaluate postdischarge endpoints, so the effect of DCI on longer term risk in our PCI population remains an open question for future work.6, 7, 8

Practically, the findings suggest complementary roles for clinical and social data: Retain PROM for fair in-hospital risk adjustment, and use DCI to inform equity-focused planning (eg, navigation, transportation support, cardiac rehabilitation outreach) for communities facing structural barriers. This is consistent with evidence showing that targeted cardiac rehabilitation strategies can mitigate downstream risk yet remain underutilized after PCI.9

There are a number of social determinants (eg, readmission, mortality) that affect outcomes in cardiovascular care. The use of DCI to capture the impact of some of these social determinants in patients undergoing PCI can potentially be performed in real time. If its use is associated with other long-term outcomes, then adding it to traditional risk models will be helpful to guide awareness of postprocedural monitoring in order to prevent negative outcomes.

Finally, implementation choices around social risk in quality measurement require care. Policymakers and professional groups continue to debate when and how to account for social risk to avoid penalizing safety-net providers while not obscuring true performance differences. Within that context, our results argue against adding DCI to in-hospital PCI mortality risk adjustment at this time, while supporting its use to guide population-level equity efforts and to motivate follow-up analyses of 30- and 90-day outcomes.10

Conclusions

PROM remains the preferred model for short-term mortality benchmarking.
