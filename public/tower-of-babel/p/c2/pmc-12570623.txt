Bayesian joint spatiotemporal modelling of primary infection and reinfection of HFMD at county level in Jiangsu, China, 2009–2023
Authors: Weijia Wang, Hong Ji, Yifan Tang, Hongfei Zhu, Wendong Liu, Kai Wang, Liguo Zhu, Chengxiu Ling, Changjun Bao, Ying Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Over the past decade, multiple outbreaks of hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD) have occurred in East Asia, especially in China. It is crucial to understand the distribution pattern and risk factors of HFMD while also studying the corresponding characteristics of reinfections. This paper aims to jointly analyze the spatiotemporal distribution and influential factors of primary infection and reinfection of HFMD in Jiangsu province, China, under the Bayesian framework. Using county-level monthly HFMD counts from 2009 to 2023, we proposed four spatiotemporal hierarchical models with latent effects shared in the reinfection sub-model to evaluate the influence of air pollution, meteorological factors, and demographic characteristics on primary infection and reinfection of HFMD. The integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA) approach estimated model parameters and quantified the spatial and temporal random effects. Scattered status and critical primary infection have a significant positive effect on primary infection and reinfection. Positive sharing coefficients revealed similar spatiotemporal patterns of both types of infection. Introduction

Hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD) is a contagious viral infection caused by enteroviruses, primarily affecting children under five years of age. Transmission occurs through contact with infected individuals or contaminated objects [1]. Symptoms typically appear after an incubation period of 3 to 7 days, including fever, oral ulcers, and rashes on hands and feet, with most patients recovering within a week [2]. While most cases are self-limited, severe infections can lead to neurological complications and even death [3]. HFMD was classified as one of the notifiable infectious diseases after a large outbreak in 2008 that originated in Fuyang city, Anhui province [4, 5]. Since 2009, the annual incidence of HFMD has never been less than 100 per 100,000 and has caused hundreds of deaths each year in China. As one of China’s most developed and densely populated provinces, Jiangsu reports an average annual HFMD incidence of 145.39 per 100,000 and ranks among the top three nationwide in reported case numbers [6], making it a representative and meaningful region for studying the disease’s spatiotemporal patterns.

Numerous studies have identified various external factors influencing the incidence of HFMD, including meteorological conditions [7], socioeconomic factors [8], and air pollution [9]. For instance, higher temperatures generally increase the risk of HFMD, while air pressure negatively affects incidence [10]. Socioeconomic variables, such as urbanization rate, gross domestic product (GDP), and population density, further shape disease dynamics by influencing population behavior and transmission patterns [11]. Air pollution has also been shown to play a critical role, with elevated NO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2}$$\end{document} levels linked to higher HFMD risk, while O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{3}$$\end{document} exposure appears to reduce risk [7]. Interestingly, the relationships between these factors and HFMD incidence are often non-linear and context-dependent. For example, temperature impacts can vary by region. Research by [12] showed HFMD risk increased with temperature below 8.1 °C, but the effect became negative above this threshold. In contrast, in regions identified as cold spots, HFMD incidence remained stable or even decreased at temperatures exceeding 25 °C [13]. Similarly, air pollutants like PM\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{10}$$\end{document} exhibited non-linear effects too, with HFMD risk peaking at moderate concentrations before declining [7].

Reinfection with HFMD is common, disrupting patients’ daily activities and increasing the risk of further transmission, which has been a focus of epidemiological research [14]. Studies have explored reinfection risk factors, virus strains, and outbreak patterns. For instance, [15] analyzed the epidemiological and virological features of HFMD reinfection, applying the Kaplan-Meier method to estimate recurrence with different virus types and revealing risk factors associated with severe reinfection cases. Similarly, [16] used a Cox proportional hazards model and found elevated reinfection risks among individuals initially infected with CV-A16 or during CV-A16 predominant years. However, these studies mainly focused on observable covariates and neglected the possible dependence structure in residual spatial and temporal random effects shared between primary infection and reinfection. Although Bayesian models have been used to explore the spatiotemporal distribution of overall HFMD incidence [17, 18], their application to jointly analyze the spatiotemporal patterns of primary infection and reinfection has been lacking; this study addresses that gap.

Many epidemiological studies have used spatiotemporal models under the Bayesian framework to capture the spatiotemporal variability in disease occurrence [19]. This approach enhances statistical inference by incorporating prior knowledge with observed data. It allows for specifying spatial, temporal, and spatiotemporal random effects to study disease transmission and risk factors [20, 21]. The joint modelling approach effectively captures interdependencies and spatiotemporal dynamics by sharing latent effects between the two factors of interest [22]. This framework has already been applied in environmental research [23], and here we innovatively extend it to study the primary infection and reinfection of HFMD.

Using surveillance data from Jiangsu province, China, from 2009 to 2023, this study jointly analyzed primary infections and reinfections of HFMD, examined their spatiotemporal distribution patterns and risk factors, including meteorological variables, air pollution, and demographic characteristics. These variables were modeled as fixed effects in four joint Bayesian hierarchical models with varying spatial and temporal random effects. The joint modelling accounted for the nonlinear relationships of meteorological and air pollution factors. The optimal model was selected based on multiple Bayesian inference criteria, and comparing standard deviations of coefficients highlighted the benefits of joint modelling by capturing the spatiotemporal dependence of HFMD incidence and exploring connections between primary infection and reinfection.

Materials

Study area and population

We obtained data on HFMD cases in Jiangsu from the National Infectious Diseases Reporting System (NIDRS), which has covered all the cases in Jiangsu province since 2009. Jiangsu is an eastern-central province of China (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$116^\circ 21'-121^\circ 56' \text {E}, 30^\circ 45'-35^\circ 08' \text {N}$$\end{document}) and covers an area of 107.2 thousand square kilometers and 85.26 million residents by the end of 2023 [24]. The southern part of Jiangsu is mainly characterized by a humid subtropical monsoon climate, gradually transitioning to a cool temperature continental monsoon climate toward the north. The original dataset contained a total of 1,578,702 infection cases reported from 2009 to 2023 in Jiangsu province, including patients’ names, ages, sex, dates of birth, home address, onset date, diagnostic date, an indicator for critically ill, etc. After excluding invalid reinfection cases (repeated primary infection, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n=$$\end{document} 7,615, 0.48%), cases of patients with addresses outside of Jiangsu province (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n=$$\end{document} 44,653, 2.83%), and cases from patients with unspecified personal addresses (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n=$$\end{document} 786, 0.05%), we identified a total of 1,525,648 cases of HFMD who lived in Jiangsu province from 2009 to 2023.

Outcomes

We aimed to analyze primary infections and reinfections by categorizing cases accordingly. First, patients’ names were combined with dates of birth to create a unique identifier for each individual. Then, cases with multiple occurrences were filtered as potential reinfection candidates. Finally, reinfection was defined as an infection occurring at least 21 days after the previous one, consistent with criteria used in previous studies [15, 25, 26].

Among 1,525,648 cases (covering all age groups), 1, 477, 294 were classified as primary infections and 48,354 as valid reinfections. The address codes from the dataset were cross-checked with the county codes in the shapefile to ensure they match the current administrative divisions. Finally, we aggregated the data at the monthly and county levels, forming the spatial and temporal resolution for the study. Yearly population data for each county were obtained from the Statistics Yearbook published by each prefecture’s Bureau of Statistics and are recorded in units of 100, 000.

Meteorological variables were obtained from the ERA5-Land hourly data from 1950 to the present [29]. Daily averages were computed by averaging 24 hourly values for each day from 2009 to 2023. Subsequently, monthly averages were obtained by averaging the daily values within each month. The original spatial resolution was 9 km \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 9 km. We selected air pollution variables, including PM\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2.5}$$\end{document} [30, 31], O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{3}$$\end{document} [32], and NO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2}$$\end{document} [33, 34], from the Global High Air Pollutants (CHAP) dataset, which provides monthly data at a 1 km \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 1 km resolution. To harmonize these datasets at the county level, we processed each dataset separately by assigning grid points to counties based on whether their centroid coordinates fall within the county administrative boundaries. The values of all grid points assigned to each county were then averaged to obtain county-level estimates.

The two demographic explanatory variables include the proportion of critically ill individuals (patients diagnosed as critically ill for their first infection or reinfection in each county each month) [26] and scattered individuals (those not attending kindergarten or school in their county each month) [14]. Considering social isolation and potential under-reporting due to intense COVID-19 transmission from 2020 to 2022, we introduced an indicator variable for the COVID period (1 = COVID, 0 = non-COVID). This COVID period spans three years, from January 2020, covering the early outbreak and lockdown phases, to December 2022, when reopening transitions occurred, as detailed in relevant reports [35].

Methods

Spatiotemporal joint models

We developed a joint Bayesian spatiotemporal model to analyze the primary infection and reinfection of HFMD and their associated influencing factors, simultaneously fitting two sub-models, incorporating shared random effects, and capturing spatiotemporal dependencies between the two infection types.

We conducted a dispersion test for the generalized linear model on primary infection and reinfection count data, which revealed significant overdispersion in the response. Additionally, 49.6% of the reinfection data are zero, indicating the necessity to properly deal with excess zeros in the model [36, 37]. Model 3 combines Models 1 and 2 above, taking both spatial and temporal random effects into account\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} & f_0(s, t) = \gamma ^m_0(t)+\gamma ^y_0(t)+b(s),\\ & f_1(s,t)= \beta ^*_4\gamma ^m_0(t)+\beta ^*_5\gamma ^y_0(t)+\beta ^*_6 b(s). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Model 4: Spatiotemporal interaction. Finally, we considered increasing Model 3 with an additional independent spatiotemporal interaction term representing different monthly variations at different locations:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} & f_0(s,t) = \gamma ^m_0(t)+\gamma ^y_0(t)+b(s)+\epsilon _0(s)\times \gamma ^m_0(t),\\ & f_1(s,t)= \beta ^*_7\gamma ^m_0(t)+\beta ^*_8\gamma ^y_0(t)+\beta ^*_9 b(s)+\epsilon _1(s)\times \gamma ^m_1(t). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Here, we included two independent interactions of the unstructured spatial effect \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon _j(s)$$\end{document} and the monthly random effect \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma ^m_j(t)$$\end{document} in the two submodels (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$j=0,1$$\end{document} for the NB and truncated ZINB models), by assuming that the monthly variation at each location is independent of other locations.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1), with hyperparameters following the integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA) default prior distributions.

Non-linear analysis

In our joint modelling framework, we considered the nonlinear effects of each air pollutant (PM\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2.5}$$\end{document}, O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}, NO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}) and meteorological variable (temperature, precipitation, relative humidity, wind speed, and solar radiation) on both primary infection and reinfection of HFMD. Specifically, the average number of primary infections \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _0(s,t)$$\end{document} and the reinfection cases \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _1(s,t)$$\end{document} are modeled as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \log \mu _0(s,t) & = \log (N(s,t)) + \beta _{00} + \varvec{x}_0^{\top }(s,t)\varvec{\beta }_0 + g_{0}(\varvec{z}_{0k}(s,t)) + f_0(s,t), \\ \log \mu _1(s,t) & = \log (N(s,t)) + \beta _{10} + \varvec{x}_1^{\top }(s,t)\varvec{\beta }_1 + g_{1}(\varvec{z}_{1k}(s,t)) + f_1(s,t). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

We incorporated function terms \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{0}(\cdot )$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{1}(\cdot )$$\end{document} to flexibly capture the complex nonlinear relationships between selected environmental factors and the two infection outcomes. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{z}_{0k}(s,t)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{z}_{1k}(s,t)$$\end{document} represent the kth environmental variable with a nonlinear effect at location s and time t, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z_{0k}, z_{1k}, k = 1, \dots , 8$$\end{document} correspond to PM\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2.5}$$\end{document}, O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}, NO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} temperature, precipitation, relative humidity, wind speed, and solar radiation, while all other covariates in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} In our implementation using the INLA framework, we specified a second-order random walk (RW2) model for each of these environmental variables on its domain to model their potential nonlinear effects.

Although frequentist spline methods and Bayesian latent Gaussian models differ fundamentally, with one relying on maximum likelihood estimation and the other on stochastic process modelling [40], a conceptual ’equivalence’ exists between ordinary cubic splines and the RW2 process model. The RW2 model details are provided in Appendix A.3.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted two sensitivity tests to examine the robustness of our primary findings. First, for the spatial and temporal model parameters, we employed the penalized complexity (PC) priors [38] that penalize the complex models to avoid overfitness. We set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Prob}(\tau _{\textrm{RW}} < 1)=0.01$$\end{document} for the RW1 model, which means the probability of the standard deviation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _{\textrm{RW}}=\tau ^{-0.5}_{\textrm{RW}}$$\end{document} larger than 1 is very low. Second, for the BYM2 model, we used \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Prob}(\tau _{\textrm{BYM2}} < 1.6)=0.01$$\end{document} to penalize model with large variance and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Prob}(\phi < 0.1)=0.80$$\end{document}.

Results

Descriptive analysis

Figure 2a demonstrates the temporal variation of primary infection and reinfection incidence from 2009 to 2023. Since 2011, both infections displayed similar seasonal patterns, peaking in April–June and October–December. These semiannual peaks are consistent with patterns observed in southern China and may be attributed to climatic conditions that favor enterovirus transmission, as well as the seasonal accumulation of susceptible individuals [41]. Incidence levels generally increased from 2009 to 2018, declined from 2019 to 2022 during COVID-19, and rebounded in 2023. Figures 2b and c display the spatial distribution of average incidence between 2009 and 2023 for primary infection and reinfection, respectively. In general, both types exhibit similar spatial patterns, with higher incidences in southern counties, where primary infection and reinfection incidences approach 20 and 1 per 100,000, respectively. In contrast, northern counties show lower incidences, with primary infection and reinfection incidences below 5 and 0.25 per 100,000, respectively. Additionally, we presented the annual spatiotemporal distribution of primary and reinfection infections across the 95 counties in Appendix Figures A.1 and A.2. Discussion

We proposed a joint spatiotemporal framework allowing for sharing random effects between primary infection and reinfection of HFMD. This framework offers three-fold contributions: a) It captured the dependence structure in residual spatial and temporal random effects shared between two infections, providing a clearer understanding of their underlying transmission mechanisms. b) It identified common risk factors influencing both primary infection and reinfection, offering a more comprehensive assessment of their impacts. c) By sharing latent components, it improved estimation accuracy, leveraging the interdependencies to provide more precise estimates of the spatiotemporal patterns and risk factors associated with HFMD.

By employing the joint model, we identified similarities in the spatial distribution and temporal trends (monthly and yearly) of both infection types, with the most significant variation occurring at the yearly level. This aligns with the idea that smaller time units exhibit more significant variability and flexibility, reducing the likelihood of two infection patterns matching. Utilizing detailed county-level data from 95 counties in Jiangsu province, China, we conducted an analysis of primary infection and reinfection of HFMD, allowing for greater spatial variability to be detected and reflected. To validate the joint model, we compared the standard deviations of the fixed effects between the joint and independent models. Our findings were consistent with those by [44], demonstrating that one strength of joint modelling on infectious disease is the reduced uncertainty and estimation bias, particularly when there is a possibility of under-reporting.

All three air pollution variables exhibited a significant linear association with HFMD risk in the two sub-models. PM\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2.5}$$\end{document} and O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{3}$$\end{document} were identified as suppressive factors of HFMD transmission, which is also reflected in the research by [6], showing that higher infections occur with better air quality. While some existing literature has suggested that higher concentrations of air pollution particles like PM\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2.5}$$\end{document} can facilitate viral transmission in the air, leading to increased disease incidence [45], the non-linear associations of PM\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2.5}$$\end{document} reported by [9] and [46], and O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{3}$$\end{document} reported by [47] and [48], showed RRs less than 1 in most cases, which corresponds to the negative linear association and non-linear trend displayed in our study. As PM\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2.5}$$\end{document} and O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{3}$$\end{document} concentrations increase, individuals tend to adopt protective behaviors, such as wearing masks or limiting outdoor activities, as evidenced by [49], which observed reduced park visitation under higher air pollution levels in China. While these actions primarily reduced exposure to harmful pollutants, they may have indirectly helped reduce the overall burden on the immune system, potentially lowering susceptibility to infections like HFMD [50]. In contrast, NO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2}$$\end{document} was positively related to HFMD incidence, consistent with the research by [51]. Studies by [46] and [47] demonstrated the non-linear trend of the influence of NO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2}$$\end{document} with RRs greater than 1 in most cases, aligning with our result. Higher concentrations of NO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2}$$\end{document} can damage children’s respiratory systems by causing airway inflammation, impairing lung function, and weakening immune responses, which may increase their susceptibility to HFMD [52].

Regarding average temperature, we observed opposite trends for primary infection and reinfection, with a cut-off point at 27 °C. Specifically, rising temperatures were generally associated with higher primary incidence until reaching 27 °C, after which higher temperatures became less conducive to virus survival and transmission [43]. For reinfection, the effect was negative below 27 °C and positive above 27 °C, aligning with findings from related studies in Guangdong province by [48] and [53]. Such distinctions may be attributed to different types of virus-infected individuals in primary infection and reinfection cases and their differing responses to temperature changes [54]. A study by [15] reported different probabilities of reinfection caused by different virus types, with primary infection mainly caused by EV-A71 and CV-A16. Additionally, [55] indicated a much higher risk related to EV-A71 on cold days compared to other virus serotypes. Further research could be conducted to identify the specific effects of temperature or other meteorological factors on different serotypes.

Meanwhile, non-linear analysis suggested that temperature had a more substantial effect on primary incidence than on reinfection, though these differences require further verification in future studies. Wind speed, relative humidity, and solar radiation were suggested to influence both primary infection and reinfection positively. To some extent, higher wind speed may boost virus transmission and increase the risk of infection [56]. Similarly, higher relative humidity increases virus accumulation and may restrict body metabolism, making children more susceptible to HFMD [57]. The effect of solar radiation on HFMD had not been fully understood, but our findings correspond with [58], suggesting that exposure to ultraviolet radiation may damage the immune system and weaken resistance to infections [59]. On the other hand, precipitation was negatively associated with HFMD incidence, as higher rainfall may reduce virus transmission by decreasing airborne viral concentration, limiting outdoor activities, and promoting environmental cleaning [60].

Our findings suggested that reinfection is significantly associated with the severity of the first infection, as severe initial infections may cause cardiac dysfunction and neurological damage [61], increasing the likelihood of reinfection. Additionally, lifestyle factors, such as frequent exposure to crowded environments or poor hygiene practices [62], further elevated the risk of reinfection, especially in individuals with a compromised immune system. A higher incidence of primary infection was found among scattered children as indicated by [63]. This was probably due to the younger age group of scattered children, as HFMD most commonly affects young children whose immune systems are still developing and thus less capable of effectively combating the virus [1]. Finally, the incidence of both types of infections was significantly lower during COVID-19. On the one hand, COVID-19 restrictions, including national lockdowns and quarantine policies, may have reduced the probability of contracting a transmission source [64]. On the other hand, the government’s focus on COVID-19 control likely diverted resources from monitoring HFMD, leading to under-reporting [65]. Factors included delayed detection, reduced public attention, parents avoiding healthcare due to COVID-19 fears, and changes in reporting systems prioritizing COVID-19 cases.

Our shared-component modelling framework enabled us to quantify the extent to which HFMD reinfections mirrored the underlying spatiotemporal dynamics of primary infections. The notably high yearly trend sharing coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _8^{*}$$\end{document} = 5.774) suggested that long-term influences, such as evolving population immunity, changes in healthcare policies, or broader environmental shifts [66], had a parallel impact on both types of infections. This alignment may also imply a potential cascading effect, where changes affecting primary infection risks progressively influence reinfection dynamics over time. Meanwhile, the more moderate shared monthly coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _7^{*}$$\end{document} = 1.823) reflected partial seasonal alignment, potentially shaped by overlapping viral circulation periods or behavioral and climatic factors [26]. Spatially, the shared spatial coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _9^{*}$$\end{document} = 0.99) suggested a spillover effect, as both infection types exhibited highly consistent geographical patterns [67], suggesting that areas with outbreaks of primary HFMD cases are also likely to experience clusters of reinfections. These findings underscored the importance of heightened surveillance in these regions, not only to control the initial outbreak but also to identify and manage potential secondary cases, which might have presented with more severe clinical outcomes or reflected the underlying vulnerability in the population [68].

This study has several limitations. First, while it primarily focuses on the environmental impact on HFMD incidence, factors such as breastfeeding status [69], vaccination [70], and virus type [71], which may influence susceptibility, were not analyzed due to data constraints. Second, this study explores risk factors and associations between primary infection and reinfection at the population level, but it cannot capture potential biases arising from individual-level heterogeneity. Third, the relatively coarse spatial resolution of meteorological data may result in exposure misclassification; further study can be considered using simulated high-resolution exposure surfaces (e.g., 100 meters) based on monitoring station data to improve accuracy [72].

In light of our findings, several policy implications emerge from the joint Bayesian spatiotemporal framework. First, dynamic monitoring and early-warning systems should incorporate meteorological and air pollution indicators to anticipate seasonal peaks of HFMD, supporting timely allocation of healthcare resources [73, 74]. Second, targeted interventions must focus on scattered children and patients with critical first infections, who are the most vulnerable to primary infection and reinfection, respectively, requiring stronger community-based health education and routine follow-up [75]. Third, the strong shared spatiotemporal patterns between the two types of infection highlight the need for integrated surveillance platforms so that regions facing primary outbreaks are simultaneously reinforced with reinfection-prevention measures. Unlike previous studies that mainly offered broad recommendations based on overall incidence, our joint analysis provides differentiated and actionable guidance by addressing distinct vulnerabilities and shared dynamics, thereby offering more precise evidence for public health decision-making.
