Whole genome sequencing in oesophageal adenocarcinoma unmasks potential precision therapies
Authors: Sowmya Sharma, Ho Yi Wong, Rebecca L Johnston, Lambros T Koufariotis, Scott Wood, Georgina Hollway, Lauren G Aoude, Aimee L Davidson, John V Pearson, Frank P Lin, Andrew P Barbour, Nicola Waddell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOesophageal adenocarcinoma (OAC) incidence continues to rise in developed countries. Despite advances in deciphering cancer genomes and oncogenic pathways, precision therapies for OAC remain limited. An expansion of precision therapy repertoire in OAC is urgently required.MethodsWe assembled 109 actionable and/or potentially actionable genes based on pan-cancer drug information from open-source compendia and clinical trials. To discover approved treatments and potentially targetable alterations (PTAs), we re-analysed whole genome sequencing data of 217 OAC patients and clinically evaluated somatic mutations in these genes using international cancer curation guidelines. Additionally, matching RNA-seq from 210 of the patients was interrogated for select immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) targets (n = 59), with expression findings validated in an independent OAC cohort (n = 114).ResultsApproved actionable targets were revealed in 23% of patients; including HER-2 and MET antagonists, and immunotherapy directed by microsatellite instability and high tumour mutation burden. PTAs were noted in a further 72.4% of patients, although the highest frequency was in TP53 which is a challenging target for treatment. Other PTAs were noted in APC, ARID1A, BRCA1, BRCA2, CCNE1, CDKN2A, EGFR, KRAS, MDM2, PIK3CA, PTEN, SMARCB1 and TSC2. Candidate co-occurring alterations included co-occurrence of PTEN loss and ERBB2 amplification and co-occurring variants in KRAS and TP53. For the remaining 4.6% of patients, no PTAs were detected. We identified and validated high expression of four genes encoding candidate ICI targets for OAC: CD24, VEGFA, PVR, and SLC3A2.ConclusionThese PTAs and ICI targets will help to inform future clinical trials to facilitate precision therapy for patients with OAC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16044-9.Keywords: Oesophageal adenocarcinoma (OAC), Targeted therapy, Immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) targets, Genomics, Personalized medicine

Introduction

The incidence of oesophageal adenocarcinoma (OAC) is rising in developed countries [1, 2]. Overall survival of OAC remains grim, with approximately 20% of patients surviving 5 years [3, 4] and 12% surviving 10 years [5]. Treatment options for primary tumours are generally limited to radiotherapy ± chemotherapy ± surgical resection; only select patients receive treatment combinations with HER-2 antagonists or immunotherapy against PD-1 [6–9].

Precision therapy in cancer has been enabled by large consortia such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the International Cancer Genome Consortia (ICGC) [10], which used sequencing-based approaches to define the mutation landscape of many cancer types. Over 30 different cancers can now be effectively treated with genomic guided therapies [11]. However, although comprehensive genomic profiling provides valuable insight into tumorigenesis, the vast majority of genomic alterations are not currently actionable [12, 13].

In OAC, the anti-HER-2 monoclonal antibody, Trastuzumab in combination with chemotherapy was approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) based on improved overall survival compared to standard chemotherapy alone in HER-2 positive OAC [14]. Similarly, the MET blocker Savolitinib is showing promising results in phase 2 clinical trials [15]. Both these drugs were repurposed from other tumour types. Pan-cancer, biomarker-directed indications also include immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) targeted therapies across solid tumours, including OAC (NCT02872116). Even though high tumour mutation burden (TMB) [16] and/or microsatellite instability (MSI) [16] have been approved for solid tumour immunotherapy, specific studies demonstrating the efficacy in OAC are unavailable. Moreover, approved ICI targets in cancer generally, and in gastro-oesophageal carcinoma specifically, are largely limited to drug targets against the PD1-PDL1 axis, CTLA4 and LAG3 [17, 18]. New ICI targets under investigation include TIGIT [19] and VEGFR/FGFR/PDGFR/c-kit [20].

In this study, we undertook a comprehensive analysis to identify candidate treatment opportunities in OAC. We assembled a list of pan-cancer actionable and potentially actionable genes. Using whole genome sequencing (WGS) data from 217 OAC tumours, we curated somatic genomic alterations in select genes to identify known and potential treatment targets. Only somatic genomic alterations were investigated which included somatic single nucleotide variants (SNVs)/indels (referred to in this publication as “variants”) and copy number alterations (CNAs). Germline variants were not explored. Further, using matched RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from the same individuals, we explored immunotherapy targets by assessing ICI target expression, TMB and MSI. Our findings propose candidate treatment targets in OAC and inform future clinical trials to confer personalized therapeutic options for patients with OAC.

Methods

This is a research study conducted at QIMR Berghofer. Data access was approved by QIMR Berghofer Human Research Ethics Committee (P2095). WGS with matching RNA-seq data for n = 234 OAC cases (including gastro-oesophageal adenocarcinoma cases) was accessed from the ICGC via the European Genome Archive (EGA, accession numbers EGAD00001004417, EGAD00001004029, EGAD00001007785 and EGAD00001007496 for WGS and EGAD00001005388, EGAD00001004423, EGAD00001007496 and EGAD00001007809 for RNA-seq). We performed sample quality control (QC) of the ICGC OAC WGS and RNA-seq data to exclude cases and individual samples with poor data quality. This resulted in n = 217 cases for WGS analysis and n = 210 cases for RNA-seq analysis, with n = 210 OAC cases having matching WGS and RNA-seq data. An independent OAC RNA-seq cohort was utilised (available in EGA under accession number EGAS00001002864). This dataset included 98 OAC cases with n = 114 samples (16 cases had two samples). Quality information and exclusion criteria are provided in detail in Supplementary Table 1.

WGS analysis to call somatic variants

Whole genome data was accessed as BAM files, which were converted to FASTQ using samtools v1.9 [21]. Sequence adaptors were trimmed with Cutadapt (v1.9) using samtools v1.9 [22] and reads were mapped to human reference genome GRCh38 using BWA-MEM (v0.7.15) [23] and SAMtools (v1.9) [21]. Duplicated reads were marked with Picard MarkDuplicates (v2.8.15; https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). The mean coverage was estimated for samples using qCoverage tool (v0.7) (https://github.com/AdamaJava/adamajava). To be included in the study, required samples needed to have a minimum sequence coverage for normal ( = > 20 average read depth) and tumour ( = > 50 average read depth). In addition, > 80% somatic variants identified needed to be absent from dbSNP. All quality information is provided in Supplementary Table 1, including sample exclusion reason.

Whole genome data was aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 and somatic mutations called. SNVs were called using a dual calling approach via consensus of two distinct tools, qSNP [24] (v2.1.4) and GATK HaplotypeCaller [25] (v4.0.4.0). Indel calling was performed using GATK [25] (v4.0.4.0) and variant annotation using gene consequences was implemented using SnpEff (v2.1.2) [26]. CNAs including ploidy were identified using ascatNgs [27]. Gene-specific CNAs were determined by annotation with Ensembl known genes (version 75). To be conservative in the analysis we only considered genes which were amplified or deleted. Amplifications were defined using the criteria from COSMIC [28]: (i) total DNA segment copy number ≥ 5 for tumours with average ploidy ≤ 2.7 as inferred by ascatNgs or (ii) total DNA segment CN ≥ 9 for tumours with average ploidy > 2.7.

Tumour mutation burden (TMB), homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) and microsatellite instability (MSI)

TMB was determined by counting non-synonymous SNVs, indels and multiple nucleotide variants (MNVs) per megabase of the coding region. A TMB of > 10 mutations per MB was considered actionable for ICI targeted treatment [29]. MSI for each case was estimated using MSIsensor [30]. Scores > =3.5 were considered MSI and eligible for ICI treatment, while scores < 3.5 were microsatellite stable (MSS). HRD for each patient was calculated using Classifier of HOmologous Recombination Deficiency (CHORD) [31]. Scores > 0.5 were considered HRD.

Assembling candidate actionable genes for curation

To compile a list of potentially actionable genes, we used two open access compendia for SNV/indel curation: (1) Topograph [32], which aims to assess individual therapies and their targets, was accessed on 19 March 2024 (Supplementary Table 2); and (2) OncoKB [33], which focusses on variant assessment and linked therapies, was procured from the webpage (https://www.oncokb.org) on 22 March 2024 (Supplementary Table 3). For CNAs, in addition to Topograph and OncoKB, we accessed OAC clinical trials in August 2024 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/search?cond=Oesophageal%20Adenocarcinoma).

The focus of this study is current and potential genomic-guided treatments in OAC, therefore genes that were diagnostic or prognostic were excluded. Levels of actionability differ between Topograph and OncoKB, as Topograph considers local context through approvals from the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) of Australia. Actionability in Topograph is classified as: Tier 1 (TGA-approved), Tier 2 (standard of care, but not approved by TGA, may be approved by other international jurisdiction e.g. FDA), Tier 3 (clinical evidence of anti-tumour activity), Tier 4 (preclinical or early clinical evidence), Tier R1 (standard of care resistance biomarker) and Tier R2 (suggesting resistance). Actionability in OncoKB is classified as: Level 1 (FDA-approved drugs), Level 2 (standard of care), Level 3A (compelling clinical evidence), Level 3B (standard of care in another indication), Level 4 (biological evidence), Level R1 (standard of care resistance biomarker), and Level R2 (compelling clinical evidence suggesting resistance).

Only genes listed singly were included for curation based on the actionable evidence of variants (Tiers in Topograph and Levels in OncoKB). For SNV and indels, genes in Topograph annotated with Tiers 1 and 1b (combined Tier 1), 2, 3 and R1; or in OncoKB, genes with Levels 1, 2, 3 and R1, were selected for curation. For CNA curation, genes that were annotated as “amplified” or “deleted” within Topograph or OncoKB were included. In Topograph, genes with CNA events listed as Tiers 1 and 1b (combined Tier 1), 2, 3 and R1, and in OncoKB, genes with CNA events listed as Levels 1, 2, 3 and R1 were selected for curation. Finally, we derived a gene list from OAC targeted therapy clinical trials in which loss-of-function of certain genes (analogous to deletion) was one of the patient selection criteria: ARID1A (NCT05396833), BRCA1 and BRCA2 [34], CDKN2A (NCT04966481), FH, TP53 (NCT01748825) and TSC2 [32]. These genes were compiled into the final CNA gene list. Actionability terminologies within Topograph and OncoKB that were not associated with a single gene name were excluded: two or more gene combinations, TMB, MSI, HRD score, MMR, LOH score, CD274 (PDL1), HPVgenotype, and Immunoscore (IC). The resulting gene lists from Topograph, OncoKB and OAC clinical trials were compiled and duplicate genes removed. This resulted in 108 genes selected for SNV/indel curation and 17 genes selected for curation of CNAs (Supplementary Table 4).

Curation of somatic variants

Somatic SNVs, indels, amplifications and deletions were identified and curated. Coding SNVs and indels (missense, in frame and frameshift mutations, start-losses, stop-gain/loss, splice-site acceptor, splice-site donor and splice region [defined as the region either within 1–3 bases of the exon or 3–8 bases of the intron] variants and premature start gains) were selected for curation to assess oncogenicity and actionability. Variant Validator [35] was used to validate the nomenclature of each variant. When a variant was annotated to multiple transcripts, the MANE SELECT transcript was selected for curation. When the variant was noted on an alternative transcript, we selected the transcript associated with the most deleterious protein change and, therefore the worst functional consequence. Oncogenicity score was calculated using the oncogenicity guidelines for classification of variants in cancer by the Joint recommendations of Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen), Cancer Genomics Consortium (CGC), and Variant Interpretation for Cancer Consortium (VICC) [36]. The sources used to classify oncogenicity included: gnomad (version 4) [37], computational predictors including CADD (version 1.7) [38], CScape [39], Bayesdel (versions 4.9, with and without AF) [40], Revel [41], MetaL [42] and dbscSNV [43], as well as the resources Franklin [44] and Varsome premium [45]. Where applicable, Grantham scores were used to determine the degree of damaging effect of an amino acid substitution [46] in line with the definition of OM4 as per recommendations [36]. Hotspot (specific, recurrent and functional variants in cancer) scores [47, 48] were determined and variant analysis was performed by interrogating ClinVar [49], COSMIC [50], UniProt [51] and IARC TP53 database [52].

Actionability for SNVs and CNAs was determined by applying AMP/ASCO/CAP guidelines [53]. Actionability categories were awarded to genomic changes based on the following: Targets for drugs approved by PBS and/or FDA were classified as category 1A; targets for drugs not approved by PBS and/or FDA but with evidence of clinical efficacy against esophago-gastric cancer in clinical trials (phases 4, 3 or basket phase 2 trials) were classified as category 1B, targets for drugs not approved by PBS and/or FDA for OAC but with evidence of clinical efficacy against different cancers were classified as category 2C. Targets for drugs not approved by PBS and/or FDA but with possible and/or early evidence of clinical efficacy in preclinical, case reports, phase 1 and early phase 2 trials for any cancer were classified category 2D. Variants of uncertain significance without evidence of clinical efficacy were classified as Tier 3 and LB/B variants were classified as Tier 4. Evidence used for actionability assessment included Topograph [32], OncoKB [33], JAX-CKB [54], cBioPortal [55] the US clinical trial databases [56] and the literature (Pubmed and Google search for scholarly articles containing the genomic alteration).

Assembling candidate ICI targets in OAC

A list of known and potential, pan-cancer ICI targets (n = 59) were compiled from the literature (Supplementary Table 5, compiled May 2024). PDL1 is the only TGA and FDA approved ICI target approved in combination with chemotherapy for HER-2 negative OAC [57] Each pan-cancer marker was interrogated against FDA/Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) lists. A marker present in at least one of the FDA/PBS lists for any cancer was assigned a clinical score of 4. If the marker was absent in both FDA and PBS lists, it was interrogated for inclusion criteria for patient selection in clinical trials [56]. Markers identified in phases IV and III clinical trials were assigned a clinical score of 3 and markers in phases II or I clinical trial a clinical score of 2.

RNA-seq data

Expression of the genes encoding ICI targets was assessed using two RNA-seq datasets. The first dataset was the ICGC OAC (n = 210) (accession numbers EGAD00001005388, EGAD00001004423, EGAD00001007496 and EGAD00001007809), with findings confirmed in a second independent OAC cohort (n = 114, EGAD00001015476). Of these 114 cases, 57 cases underwent RNA-seq for the current study. RNA was extracted from fresh tissues collected in RNAlater using the Qiagen AllPrep kit and RNA-seq was performed using Illumina’s TruSeq Stranded mRNA kit and sequenced with 100 bp paired-end reads.

RNA-seq analysis

RNA-seq data from the ICGC cohort and the independent validation were aligned to the human GRCh38 using STAR [58] (v2.7.10a). Sequencing adapters were trimmed using Cutadapt [22] (v1.9). Sequence read groups for each sample were merged using samtools (v1.9). Gene annotation, transcript and exon features of Ensembl GRCh38 (release 110) were used to compute counts of individual genes for all RNA-seq samples. Quality Control (QC) was performed using RNA-SeQC (v1.1.8). For the ICGC OAC dataset, to pass sample QC, samples must obtain > 4 million mapped read pairs and an intragenic rate > 30%. Seven samples failed sample QC. For the ICGC cohort, the median number of mapped read pairs was 49.66 million (range 4.10–168.76 million), and for the independent OAC cohort, the median number of mapped read pairs was 42.16 million (range 15.76–133.55 million). Gene expression was estimated using RSEM (v1.3.1) [59]. Downstream analysis was performed in R version 4.2.0 [60] using the expected gene count matrices from RSEM. Gene filtering and normalisation was performed using edgeR (v3.40.2) [61]. First, a DGEList object was created, then only protein-coding genes with sufficiently large counts were retained for further analysis using the edgeR function filterByExpr.

To normalise RNA-seq data for visualization of a single gene, counts were first filtered using edgeR’s “filterByExpr” function. Scaling factors were calculated using edgeR’s “calcNormFactors” function, to convert observed library sizes into effective library sizes. To calculate log2 counts per million (CPM), representing counts normalised by the effective library sizes, we used edgeR’s “cpm” function with log set to TRUE. MTAP RNA expression (log2 CPM) differences across all CDKN2A copy number groups (0, 1 and 2+) were evaluated using a Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test with the function “kruskal.test”. Where significant, post hoc pairwise Wilcoxon rank sum tests with Benjamini-Hochberg multiple testing correction were performed with the function “pairwise.wilcox.test” to identify specific group differences. The ggplot2 package was used to create box and whisker plots and jitter plots.

To normalise RNA-seq data for visualisation using heatmaps, we note that moderated counts-per-million (CPM, calculated using the edgeR function cpm) aren’t ideal to compare expression between ICI gene targets, as CPM aren’t corrected for gene length. Transcripts-per-million (TPM) do correct for gene length, however by default these values cannot be compared between samples as they aren’t corrected for RNA composition bias. To overcome this issue, we therefore calculated TPM values using TMM scaling factors. First, gene lengths were calculated using Ensembl gene details with the formula: gene end – gene start + 1. Then scaling factors were calculated using the edgeR function calcNormFactors. RPKM were calculated using the edgeR function rpkm, which incorporates the scaling factors by default. TPM values using TMM scaling factors were calculated using the formula: t(t(counts_rpkm)/colSums(counts_rpkm)) * 1e6. Then log2 TPM values using TMM scaling factors were calculated: log2(counts_tpm_tmm + 0.01). Genes were then subset to the selected ICI target genes for exploratory analysis, where some target genes did not pass the minimum expression filter (i.e. edgeR’s filterByExpr function). An ICI target gene was considered to have high expression if > 5% of cases in the cohort obtained expression values for that gene in the top 5% of expression values across all genes. In other words, for each ICI gene target that passed the minimum expression filter, we determined the number of cases with expression values > 95th percentile (based on R’s quantile function) across the n = 210 ICGC OAC cohort, where the 95th percentile corresponded to a log2 TPM value of 6.545401, and if the number of cases was > 5% of the cohort size, the gene was considered to have high expression. The RNA-seq analysis was repeated on the independent OAC cohort (n = 114). Within the independent cohort, the 95th percentile corresponded to a log2 TPM value of 4.950242. Heatmaps were generated using ComplexHeatmap version 2.14.0 [62] with log2 TPM values using TMM scaling factors as input.

Results

Actionable genes in OAC

To explore potentially targetable alterations (PTAs) in OAC, specifically SNVs, indels and CNAs (amplifications and deletions), we used two compendia that list pan-cancer actionable and potentially targetable genes, Topograph and Oncokb. Topograph contains actionable information for 231 genes, with actionability represented in Tiers (Tier 1, 1b [combined Tier 1], 2, 3, 4, R1 and R2) [32]. OncoKB contains actionable information for 76 genes, with actionability represented as Levels (Level 1, 2, 3, 4, R1 and R2) [33]. We identified relevant genes for SNV/indel curation by selecting genes with SNV/indel/oncogenic mutations listed as Tier 1, 2, 3 or R1 in Topograph (n = 87) and/or as Level 1, 2, 3, or R1 in OncoKB (n = 65). This resulted in 108 genes selected for curation of SNVs and indels (Fig. 1a). To identify genes for CNA curation, we first selected genes marked with amplifications and/or deletions and listed as Tier 1, 2, 3 or R1 in Topograph (n = 10) and/or as Level 1, 2, 3, or R1 in OncoKB (n = 6). Second, we reviewed OAC clinical trials to identify loss-of-function alterations (analogous to deletion) in certain genes (n = 7). Together we identified 17 unique genes for curation of copy number events. This resulted 109 genes selected for curation, 92 of which were selected for SNV/indel curation, 16 for SNV/indel and CNAs curation, and one gene (MDM2) selected for CNA curation (Fig. 1a).

We next compared the potentially targetable gene list (n = 109) to a list of 76, previously reported, significantly mutated genes (SMGs) in OAC [63]. Only 15 (19.7%) of the SMGs were identified as potentially targetable in Topograph and/or OncoKB, indicating that the majority of SMGs in OAC are not yet treatment candidates (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Table 4). Finally, we compared the gene lists derived from Topograph and OncoKB by Tier/Level. Within actionability categories, 1, 2, 3 and R1, several genes overlapped: 30.80%, 46.60%, 30.70% and 70% respectively (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Table 4). This suggests that OncoKB may be more conservative than Topograph, or that there may be subtle differences in categorization of respective Levels and Tiers of the two compendia.

Identification of genes with targetable SNV/indel variants within OAC patients

Within the 217 ICGC OAC samples with WGS data, a total of 5,944,266 somatic variants (range: 792–225,652) were detected and 3,212,960 CNAs were present (range: 0–28786) (Supplementary Table 6). The mean TMB was 5.1 mutations per mega base (range: 0.03–69.6) (Supplementary Table 6). All cases contained at least two mutations in genes previously described as SMGs in OAC [63] (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Of the 108 genes selected from Topograph and OncoKB for SNV/indel curation, only 88 genes contained a candidate targetable somatic variant across the ICGC OAC samples. This resulted in a total number of 594 candidate somatic variants (SNV and indels) which underwent clinical curation (variants are listed in Supplementary Table 7). Of these variants, 295 were classified as oncogenic/likely oncogenic (O/LO), 291 as variants of uncertain significance (VUS) and eight as likely benign/benign (LB/B) (Fig. 2a). TP53 was the most frequently mutated gene, with 170 of 217 patients (78%) harbouring a TP53 somatic change. The TP53 changes comprised 93 unique variants and accounted for 30% of all curated variants (178 of 594). In support of TP53 being a tumour suppressor and the most recurrent SMG in OAC [63], most variants in this gene were classified as O/LO (n = 170) and the gene accounted for 57.6% of all O/LO variants in the OAC cohort. Actionability assessment of the 594 variants revealed categories 2C (n = 284), 2D (n = 9), 3 (n = 293) and 4 (n = 8) (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, TP53 harboured the most PTAs classified as 2C/2D (n = 171). TP53:p.Arg248Gln was the most common, found in 14 cases (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Table 7). Other recurrent variants in TP53 included p.Arg175His (n = 11), p.Arg273His (n = 9), p.Arg248Trp (n = 7), p.Arg282Trp (n = 5), p.Arg213* (n = 5), p.Gly245Asp (n = 4), p.Gly245Ser (n = 4), p.Cys135Phe (n = 4) and p.Trp146* (n = 4). The high number of recurrent variants makes TP53 a prime candidate for future OAC clinical trials.

Several other genes contained PTAs, but none as frequent as TP53 (Fig. 2c): CDKN2A (n = 26), ARID1A (n = 24), APC (n = 12), PIK3CA (n = 11) and KRAS (n = 11) (Supplementary Fig. 2). These genes also contained recurrent variants. In terms of resistance markers, 11 patients harboured oncogenic, category 2C potentially actionable, recurrent somatic variants in KRAS (p.G12 n = 6 or p.G13 n = 5, Supplementary Fig. 2). These changes are known to confer resistance to anti-EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibition (TKIs) [64, 65] when they co-occur with oncogenic EGFR variants. Similarly, when these KRAS variants co-occur with ERBB2 amplification, they are associated with resistance to HER-2 blockers [66]. However, EGFR variants that co-occurred with KRAS variants did not pass the threshold of actionability for anti-EGFR TKIs. Similarly, patients with KRAS variants did not harbour ERBB2 amplification. Therefore, no resistance markers were identified in the ICGC OAC cohort.

Identification of genes with targetable CNAs and homologous recombination repair deficiency (HRD) within OAC patients

We assembled a list of 17 genes for curation of amplifications and deletions (including loss of function) linked to therapy, based on information from Topograph, OncoKB and OAC clinical trials (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 8). Of the 217 OAC cases, 155 (71.43%) contained a copy number event within the 17 genes. These included 454 CNA events, with n = 336 deletions and n = 118 amplifications (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 9). After curation based on AMP/ASCO/CAP guidelines [53], the CNA events were assigned a category from the range actionability categories: 1A (n = 35), 1B (n = 8), 2 C (n = 301), 2D (n = 16) and 3 (n = 94) (Fig. 3b). Category 1A events included OACs harboring ERBB2 amplification (n = 35), whilst category 1B events included OACs with MET amplifications (n = 8); these OACs can be treated with HER-2 inhibitors and TKIs, respectively. Other genes with amplifications were EGFR (n = 17) and CCNE1 (n = 21) curated as category 2C. EGFR amplifications have been proposed as a resistance mechanism to TKIs in non-small cell lung cancer [67], however in this OAC cohort only 1 sample contained a co-occurrence of EGFR and MET amplification (Supplementary Table 9). Therefore, CNAs potentially able to confer treatment resistance were rare.

Recurrent deletions were noted in a number of genes and curated as 2C, including CDKN2A (n = 87), TP53 (n = 39), SMARCB1 (n = 31), ARID1A (n = 31), TSC2 (n = 26) and PTEN (n = 25). CDKN2A homozygous deletions have previously been associated with decreased MTAP expression in several tumour types [68, 69]. The MTAP gene is located near CDKN2A on chromosome 9. As such, of the 87 cases with CDKN2A deletion 79 had MTAP copy number loss, and there was a significant association with CDKN2A copy number loss and lower MTAP gene expression (p = 5.54e-15 Kruskal-Wallis test, Supplementary Fig. 3). Other genes that contained recurrent CNA events included the DNA repair genes BRCA1 (3 amplification, 10 deletions) and BRCA2 (9 amplifications, 8 deletions). If cells are unable to repair DNA double strand breaks it may result in HRD, a phenotype associated with BRCA1/2 loss of function mutations. We predicted two of 217 OAC tumours as HRD (Supplementary Table 6) and assigned an actionability category of 2C. Tumours with HRD phenotype may benefit from poly ADP ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibition approved for treatment of other tumours hormone driven solid tumours [70]. These findings suggest that although genome instability occurs frequently in OAC [71], HRD is rare.

Prevalence of genomic and gene markers of immunotherapy response in OAC

MSI [30] and a high TMB (> 10 mutations per mega base) [29] are markers of response to ICI targets in solid tumours including gastro-oesophageal adenocarcinomas [32, 33]. We identified 12 of 217 OAC cases with a high TMB (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 6) and were thus assigned an actionability category of 1B. MSIsensor detected seven of 217 cases with MSI (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 6) and were assigned an actionability category of 1A. All MSI cases also showed high TMB. Altogether this resulted in 12 of 217 (5.5%) OAC patients that may be eligible for ICI targeted therapy.

To explore if patients may benefit from ICI targeted treatment, we examined the expression of select ICI target genes (n = 59, Supplementary Table 5). Within the ICGC cohort, 210 OAC cases had RNA-seq data, and 44 of the 59 selected ICI targets passed the minimum expression filter (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 10). We considered a gene to have high expression if > 5% of the cases (i.e. more than 10 cases for ICGC OAC cohort) obtained expression values for that gene in the top 5% of expression values across all genes. This resulted in five ICI target genes with high expression: CD24 n = 207 cases, VEGFA n = 174, PVR n = 24, CD47 n = 14 and SLC3A2 n = 11. We then assessed RNA-seq expression an independent OAC cohort (n = 114), and found that 48 of the 59 selected ICI targets passed the minimum expression filter (Supplementary Tables 11 and Supplementary Fig. 4). We identified six ICI target genes with high expression (CD24 n = 113 cases, VEGFA n = 77, SLC3A2 n = 16, VSIR n = 14, PVR n = 7 and TNFRSF4 n = 6). Therefore, CD24, VEGFA, PVR and SLC3A2 showed consistently high expression across both OAC cohorts.

Comprehensive approach to precision therapy in OAC

To assess novel treatment strategies for OAC patients, we integrated the results for gene targets and ICI targets determined by WGS and RNA-seq respectively. Within the ICGC cohort (n = 217) a potential precision therapy approach (Tier 1A, 1B, 2C or 2D) was found for 95.4% OAC patients (Fig. 5a). For 23% (n = 50) of OAC patients, we identified a Category 1A/1B targeted genomic alteration or approved genomic biomarker of immunotherapy (MSI or high TMB). All of these cases also harbored PTAs classified as 2C. An additional, 72.4% (n = 157) of patients harbored Category 2C and/or 2D PTAs that could be investigated in future clinical trials for OAC. Only 10 cases (4.6%) contained no PTAs.

Next, we explored whether combination therapy could be considered in OAC. We found that 82% (n = 177) of cases contained two or more PTAs. Of note, three patients (1.4%) demonstrated simultaneous PTEN loss and ERBB2 amplifications (Fig. 3a). These may be amenable to combination therapy with Everolimus and Traztuzumab, analogous to the promise shown in breast cancer [72]. Others included co-occurring KRAS and TP53 oncogenic variants in 5 OAC cases (2.3%, Supplementary Table 7). Co-occurrence of TP53 and RAS oncogenic variants in metastatic colorectal cancer has been implicated in patient response to WEE1 inhibition [73]. Whilst numerous other potential combinations are possible within ICGC OAC, currently approved precision therapies do not encompass these combinations. These were noted in patients who qualified for category 1A/1B targeted therapies, as all these patients also contained variants for therapies classified as 2C/2D. In addition, all patients that qualified for immunotherapy (TMB high and/or MSI) also qualified for targeted therapy with a 2C agent.

In terms of ICI targets, genes encoding four ICI targets (CD24, VEGFA, PVR, and SLC3A2) were validated as the top highly expressed ICI targets (Fig. 5b). These targets are integral components of the tumour microenvironment (Fig. 5c). Interestingly, of the 12 cases with matching WGS and RNA-seq data that contained high TMB according to WGS, 11 cases showed high gene expression of CD24 and VEGF, which further strengthens CD24 and VEGFA as promising candidate ICI targets for OAC that could be considered in future immunotherapy clinical trials. Finally, although IDO1 and CD276 did not qualify above the threshold of 5% cases, high expression was noted in certain cases in both cohorts. This suggests that in such cases where further personalisation of therapy is required, IDO1 and CD276 may also be considered as candidate ICI targets.

Discussion

WGS has been greatly successful in deciphering oncogenesis pathways in OAC, with 76 significantly mutated driver genes elucidated [63]. However, only one of these genes is decisively implicated in contemporary precision therapy with FDA approvals, ERBB2 which encodes the HER-2 protein. Furthermore, only about 10% of drugs developed and investigated via clinical trials successfully acquire FDA approvals [74]. Lack of specificity in matching individual patients to drugs within clinical trials for both targeted [75–77] and ICI [78] therapies appears to be an important cause of inconsistent and contradictory results. Curation of mined cancer drug-targets, specifically oncogenicity and actionability characteristics, to determine patient selection, may provide superior insights into patient specific candidate agents and improve the success rate of drug discovery and approvals.

Large-scale studies increasingly use comprehensive genomic panels targeting 300–500 genes [79, 80]. However, this may limit the discovery of new treatment targets and identification of non-coding PTAs, including deep intronic mutations which may impact splicing or regulatory gene regions that influence gene expression. This in turn limits patient enrolment into clinical trials when such variants occur in isolation. WGS also provides a permanent comprehensive record of an individual’s the entire cancer genome. This is available for future interrogation as new drug targets are discovered. It also serves as a baseline, against which genomes of metastatic lesions could be compared, potentially revealing resistance markers, genomic pathways of progression and inform therapy selection. Additionally, RNA-seq reveals the expression of the genes encoding full-spectrum of approved and experimental ICI targets. This could provide insights into the scope of candidate ICI targets for the patient’s specific cancer.

This work contains several limitations and assumptions within the methods. For example, we only assessed mutations in selected genes which were not based on a comprehensive search of the literature and represent a snapshot in time that will become outdated as new treatment targets are described. Additionally, the definition of high expression for ICI targets was arbitrary and would need to be studied further if they were to be targeted in clinical trials. Another limitation is that we did not consider clinical data. Within the ICGC oesophageal adenocarcinoma cohort patient age, sex, tumour stage are available for analysis, however we did not have access to other clinical data including clinicopathological features including anatomical location, surgical modality, prevalence of metastatic disease, histopathological subtype, EBV and HPV status. Future studies could explore whether certain clinical groups of patients are enriched for therapy targets.

We interrogated WGS and matched RNA-seq in OACs from the ICGC cohort to explore if genes/genomic changes implicated in precision therapy for other cancers can be repurposed to expand OAC precision targets. Within the contemporary scope of pan-cancer precision therapies, 22.1% of ICGC OAC patients could be offered treatments with FDA approved agents, with a further 72.8% harboring PTAs. TP53 is the most commonly mutated gene in OAC [81] and is a candidate gene for precision therapy. TP53 variants accounted for almost 38% of PTAs, including a number of known hotspot variants [47, 48] which have been defined as DNA-binding (R248, R273), protein folding (R175, G245, R282) and Zinc binding (C176, C242 and H179) [79]. Early phase clinical trials are underway employing various strategies including restoration of wild p53 phenotype with CP-31,398 [82], RITA [83], Chetomin [84], APR-246 (NCT03268382, NCT02999893), arsenic trioxide (NCT04695223, NCT04869475); degradation of mutant p53 with ganetespib (NCT02012192), Atorvastatin (NCT04767984), SAHA (NCT02042989); and synergistic lethal therapy with AZD1775 (NCT02688907, NCT02448329). However, targeting TP53 mutated tumours has been notoriously difficult, but remains an active area of exploration [85]. Recently, drugs that target specific p53 protein mutations have been developed. The dp53m-RA [85] selectively degrades the p53 mutation R175H, while having no effect on wild-type P53 protein. While the p53 reactivor, rezatapopt, that targets the Y220C variant has demonstrated promise in a phase 1 clinical trial that included multiple solid tumour types [86]. Our study strongly supports the inclusion of patients with OAC within future clinical trials targeting oncogenic PTAs in TP53.

Several other genes with PTAs in OAC also represent candidate targets as they are already being investigated in clinical trials for solid tumours. Patient selection criteria for these trials include: oncogenic mutations in ARID1A (NCT03297424), APC (NCT06005974, NCT05919264), CDKN2A (NCT02540876), PIK3CA [87](NCT06132932); loss of PTEN (NCT02449538), SMARCB1 (NCT05286801), BRCA1 or BRCA2 (NCT06614751, NCT05787587); and amplified CCNE1 (NCT02656849, NCT03253679), EGFR (NCT03065387), MDM2 (NCT05012397) and TSC2 (NCT05103358). Targeting CDKN2A loss is challenging, therefore approaches to target the consequence of CDKN2A loss have been pursued. CDKN2A loss contributes to cancer development by causing an increase in CDK4/6 which regulate the cell cycle. This has led to the development of CDK4/6 inhibitors which have been used to treat some cancer types, in particular breast cancer [88, 89], however resistance to these inhibitors often develops. Recently, the use of Proteolysis Targeting Chimeras (PROTACs) to selectively degrade CDK4/6 are a promising approach for breast cancer treatment [90] and may have relevance in OAC.

Given the presence of each of the PTAs within OAC is rare, clinical trials testing these would be challenging requiring multi-center or national level enrolment. However, many of the trials for these PTAs are tissue pathology agnostic, therefore we propose that patients with OAC tumours containing oncogenic PTAs in these genes could be considered within these and future clinical trials.

Outside of the selected gene list we noted that CDKN2A loss was associated with low expression of MTAP. It has been proposed that MTAP immunostaining may be a proxy for CDKN2A homozygous deletion in several tumour types [68, 69], while in another study it has been reported as not associated with CDKN2A homozygous deletion in mesothelioma [91]. Our findings support that low expression of the MTAP gene is associated with CDKN2A deletion. The frequent loss of MTAP expression in OAC may have clinical significance. Loss of MTAP expression may confer sensitivity to protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) inhibition [92, 93] and as such there are clinical trials evaluating PRMT5 inhibitors in solid cancers with loss of MTAP (including NCT05094336, NCT05245500). Therefore, due to the prevalence of MTAP loss in OAC, screening of MTAP deletion could occur in OAC patients to determine eligibility for clinical trials.

Genes with recurrent PTAs can be considered ‘low-hanging fruit’ to expedite expansion of OAC precision targets; e.g. recurrent KRAS mutations at G12 and G13 noted in our study (KRAS:pGly12Asp, KRAS:pGly12Ser, KRAS:pGly12Cys and KRAS:pGly13Asp). KRAS:p.Gly12Cys can be targeted by FDA approved, Sotorasib in NSCLC [94], advanced solid tumours [95] and in combination with EGFR TKI for colorectal carcinomas (CRCs); while clinical trials for pancreatic cancer are underway [96]. Furthermore, although KRAS:p.G12D confers a dismal prognosis in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, it can be selectively inhibited by MRTX1133 [97]. ASP3082, a KRAS:p.G12D degrader, has been successfully employed against advanced CRC, NSCLC and pancreatic adenocarcinomas in a phase 1 clinical trial [98]. A Phase 2 clinical trial employing MEK inhibition shows promise against histiocytic tumours that harbor KRAS:p.G13C [99]. KRAS:pG13D, previously identified in CRC responds to certain EGFR TKIs like cetuximab due to constitutive downstream RAS activation [100]. CRCs harbouring oncogenic TP53 and KRAS G13 variants can be targeted with Adavosertib [73]. We observed identical combinations of TP53 and KRAS G13 variants in two OAC patients. Hence, KRAS hotspots [47] at G12 and G13 [32] positions together with TP53 oncogenic variants are promising candidates for combination therapy clinical trials in OAC.

We assessed HRD status as a candidate predictive biomarker for PARP inhibitor response. While HRD is approved in high-grade serous ovarian cancer, bioinformatic analysis of WGS offers an opportunity for streamlining clinical testing and could extend therapeutic options to OAC where companion diagnostics do not exist. In ovarian cancer, multiple pivotal trials have established the clinical utility of PARP inhibitors in HRD genomic instability score positive (GIS-positive) populations: the PRIMA trial demonstrated Niraparib efficacy as maintenance therapy in platinum-sensitive disease, particularly in HRD patients [101, 102]; the ATHENA-MONO trial revealed significantly prolonged PFS with Rucaparib in GIS-defined HRD-positive populations [103]; and Olaparib plus bevacizumab maintenance was approved with a companion GIS diagnostic based on PAOLA-1 results [104]. Importantly, however, the ARIEL3 trial showed genomic LOH was not completely predictive, as some BRCA wild-type and low-LOH patients responded to Rucaparib [105]. We show that in OAC the presence of HRD is rare. While bioinformatics analyses represent significant potential to define synthetic lethality treatment strategies in OAC subsets, a critical evidence gap exists requiring prospective trials to determine optimal thresholds and examine whether PARP inhibitor selection benefits these rare OAC populations.

Classical PD1-PDL1 immunotherapy has had limited success in OAC (KEYNOTE-012: NCT01848834, KEYNOTE-059: NCT02335411). Solid tumors with high TMB of > 10 mutations/ megabase were approved for Pembrolizumab based on Keynote-158 (NCT02628067), a single-arm, phase II multi-cohort study. Key critiques of this trial include agnostic nature of the study towards tumour type [106], specific ICI targets [107], assay types [108, 109] and absence of MSI status of the solid tumour as a determinant for patient selection [110]. In our study only 7 OAC cases demonstrated MSI and all were accompanied by high TMB, qualifying them for Pembrolizumab immunotherapy. However, the majority of OAC were not eligible for ICI immunotherapy. To address the scope of ICIs, we assessed the gene expression of 60 known and candidate ICI targets. ICI targets currently approved for the treatment of solid cancers did not show high gene expression in both OAC cohorts assessed herein. Four candidate genes, CD24, VEGFA, PVR, and SLC3A2 were identified in the ICGC cohort and validated in the independent cohort. Early (phase 1) clinical trials for CD24 (NCT05985083) and VEGFA (NCT04777994) are being explored together with PDL1-PD1 agents. Specifically, high expression of CD24 in cancers of the breast [111] and the uterine cervix [112] confers a poor prognosis and may confer chemoradiotherapy resistance in pancreatic [113], ovarian [114], oral [115] cancers and melanomas [116]. Therefore, targeting CD24 together with chemoradiotherapy has been proposed to overcome treatment resistance in advanced cancers [117]. However, we present a word of caution: immunotherapy drugs are commonly monoclonal antibodies against protein targets. In this analysis we used the RNA-seq data as PDL-1 status was not available. Therefore, translational validation studies to measure protein expression, employing clinically applicable diagnostic tests like immunohistochemistry, would be prudent before exploration in future clinical trials.

Conclusion

The current FDA approved drugs for OAC include Tislelizumab for PDL-1 positive, HER2 negative OAC tumours, Zolbetuximab for CLDN18.2 positive HER-2 negative tumours, Trastuzumab deruxtecan for HER-2 positive tumours, Nivolumab for PDL1 positive tumours, Pembrolizumab with Trastuzumab and chemotherapy for PDL1 and HER2 positive tumours. Our study forms the basis for design of future clinical trials in precision therapy for OAC. Routine diagnostic comprehensive genomic profiling will enable real-world implementation and realisation of personalised oncology for all OAC patients in the future.
