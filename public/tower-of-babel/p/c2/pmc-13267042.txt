Molecular mechanisms underlying the inhibition of Sparassis latifolia mycelial growth by cycloleucine: a comprehensive transcriptomic and m6A methylome analysis
Authors: Chi Yang, Dandan Wu, Donglai Xiao, Xiaoling Jiang, Hui Lin, Xin Wang, Yanquan Lin, Lu Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCycloleucine, a non-proteinogenic amino acid, has been identified as a potent inhibitor of fungal growth; however, its molecular mechanisms of action against the edible mushroom Sparassis latifolia remain inadequately understood. In this study, we integrated transcriptomic and m6A methylome analyses to elucidate the inhibitory effects of cycloleucine on S. latifolia. Our findings demonstrate that cycloleucine treatment suppresses hyphal growth and biomass accumulation in S. latifolia, which are associated with a significant reduction in global RNA m6A levels. Through meRIP-seq analysis, we identified 67 differentially m6A-modified genes, including aif1 (apoptosis-inducing factor 1) and mfs1 (major facilitator superfamily transporter 1), suggesting their involvement in growth regulation. Genome-wide screening and expression detection revealed that EVM0008471-mediated m6A demethylation plays a potential role in controlling hyphal growth dynamics. This study provides the first evidence of m6A-dependent growth regulation in S. latifolia, advancing the understanding of fungal epitranscriptomics and offering potential targets for optimizing industrial mushroom cultivation.KEYWORDS: Sparassis latifolia, cycloleucine, m6A, growth, meRIP-seq

Introduction

Sparassis latifolia Y.C. Dai et Z. Wang (Sparassidaceae, Polyporales, and Agaricomycetes), also known as the cauliflower mushroom, is a rare edible and medicinal mushroom that has garnered attention for its potential health benefits and culinary uses. Its fruiting bodies are rich in nutrients, have a delicious taste, and are abundant in active polysaccharides, which have specific effects on enhancing the body’s immune system and exhibiting anti-cancer properties (Nishioka et al. 2020; Nowacka-Jechalke et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2022; Qiao et al. 2022; Abedpoor et al. 2024; Jin et al. 2025). The cultivation of S. latifolia, however, presents certain challenges, particularly due to its slow mycelial growth rate (Ma et al. 2016; Yang et al. 2024b). This prolonged cycle not only increases production costs but also limits the supply-chain flexibility, hindering widespread adoption and market penetration. Therefore, there is an urgent need to delve deeper into the biological mechanisms underlying the growth and development of S. latifolia.

In recent years, significant progress has been made in the research on S. latifolia. Ma et al. (2016) optimized the submerged fermentation medium for producing S. latifolia liquid spawn, achieving a high biomass yield of 11.96 g/L, highlighting its potential for industrial application. High levels of oxalic acid secreted by S. latifolia during saprophytic growth was found to significantly inhibit its own mycelial growth (Shu et al. 2022a) and the transcriptomic response of S. latifolia to excessive oxalic acid revealed that growth inhibition is primarily mediated by the down-regulation of key genes involved in carbohydrate utilization, energy metabolism, and wood-degrading enzymes (Qiu et al. 2022). To better understand the genetic basis of S. latifolia, a comprehensive genome sequencing of S. latifolia was conducted (Xiao et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2021). Primordium formation is a complex and critical developmental event in fruiting body development for S. latifolia cultivation. A de novo transcriptome assembly highlighted key pathways involved in the development of the fruiting body, including the MAPK and mTOR signaling pathways (Shu et al. 2022b). Our research also identified 30 key genes involved in light-induced primordia formation of S. latifolia by integrative analysis of ATAC-seq (Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin with high-throughput sequencing) and RNA-seq (Yang et al. 2019). Fresh pine sawdust is most frequently used for commercial cultivation of S. latifolia (Ma et al. 2016; Ghafoor and Niazi 2023), it is necessary to study the molecular mechanism of its degradation. Our previous study constructed the gene expression profile of S. latifolia cultured on fresh pine sawdust at different time points (Yang et al. 2022). Compared with Wolfiporia hoelen (Fr.) Y.C. Dai & V. Papp, another edible fungus that can grow on fresh pine sawdust, S. latifolia has a slower mycelial growth rate (Li et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2024b). Comparative genomics analysis identified the similarities and differences of their coding genes, and there were 645 common orthogroups that showed significant enrichment in 12 pathways, including steroid biosynthesis, biosynthesis of antibiotics, and tyrosine metabolism (Yang et al. 2024b). These findings provide a solid foundation for further exploring the molecular mechanisms underlying mycelial growth and offer new avenues for improving the cultivation efficiency of S. latifolia. However, our understanding of the mycelial growth mechanism of S. latifolia is still limited.

Epigenetics refers to heritable changes in gene expression mediated by DNA methylation, histone modifications (e.g., methylation, acetylation), and non-coding RNAs, which regulate chromatin structure and gene activity without altering the DNA sequence (Holliday 2006). Epigenetic modifications are crucial for fungal growth and development, and large-scale sequencing technology has become an important research strategy and approach (Li et al. 2025; Qi et al. 2025). N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification, which is the most abundant internal epigenetic modification in eukaryotic mRNAs, plays key roles in RNA metabolism, including stability, splicing, translation, and subcellular localization (Alarcón et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2015; Chen et al. 2022; Qiao et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023). In fungi, the yeast m6A methyltransferase complex (MTC) plays a key regulatory role in meiosis by influencing mRNA translation and decay, thereby promoting yeast as a powerful model for studying the fundamental mechanisms and regulatory significance of m6A (Albihlal et al. 2024). The yeast homolog KAR4 of mammalian mRNA m6A methyltransferase METTL14 is necessary for two different developmental programs, mating and meiosis, in brewing yeast (Park et al. 2023b). Analysis of yeast m6A reading protein Pho92/Mrb1 revealed that Pho92 is co-transcriptionally loaded to promote protein synthesis and facilitate meiosis (Varier et al. 2022). In addition to yeast, the molecular mechanisms underlying m6A regulation of the invasion structure and function in the rice blast fungus, Magnaporthe oryzae, were employed, and a protein MTA1, which is homologous to human METTL4, was identified (Ren et al. 2022). The production of aflatoxin in Aspergillus flavus was entirely inhibited following treatment with m6A inhibitor cycloleucine (Liang et al. 2024a), and the m6A methyltransferase, AflIme4, regulated the growth, development, and aflatoxin production of A. flavus (Liang et al. 2024b). Our previous study showed significant changes in 12 RNA modifications in A. flavus when incubated at 29 °C versus 37 °C, and we screened for transcripts with differential methylation and expression by methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq) and RNA sequencing (Yang et al. 2024a). To gain a comprehensive understanding of the evolutionary trajectories of m6A machinery, the global phylogenies for potential m6A writers (methyltransferases), erasers (demethylases), and readers (binding proteins that recognize m6A) within fungal species were constructed, and comprehensive functional analyses were performed on the m6A factor in the plant pathogenic fungus Fusarium graminearum (Kim et al. 2024). What’s more, both the m6A methyltransferase CpMTA1 and demethylase CpALKBH regulated the development and virulence of Cryphonectria parasitica (Murr.) Barr (Zhao et al. 2024, 2025). However, the regulatory mechanisms underlying reversible RNA methylation modification in S. latifolia have yet to be elucidated.

Given the significance of m6A modification in fungal biology and the pressing need to enhance the cultivation efficiency of S. latifolia, this study aims to contribute to the expanding knowledge base on fungal m6A biology and facilitate the sustainable and efficient cultivation of S. latifolia, ultimately promoting its broader utilization and societal benefits.

Materials and methods

Fungal strain and culture conditions

Sparassis latifolia (strain SP-C) was preserved at the Institute of Edible Mushroom, Fujian Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Fuzhou, China). Mycelia were cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA; Difco) supplemented with 0, 5, 10, 15, or 20 mmol/L cycloleucine (Sigma-Aldrich) at 25 °C in the dark. The EC50 value, indicating the concentration needed to inhibit 50% of mycelial growth, was calculated by plotting inhibition percentage against the logarithm of sample concentration. Biomass quantification was performed using a gravimetric method (Ma et al. 2016), and colony diameter was measured after 14 days of culture.

Detection of RNA modifications

The levels of RNA modifications were measured following the protocol outlined in a previous study (Yang et al. 2024a). The total RNA was extracted using TRIZOL and digested with S1 nuclease, phosphodiesterase I, and alkaline phosphatase at 37 °C. Following the identification of RNA modifications through MRM, Analyst 1.6.3 software (Sciex) was used to collect the data, and Multiquant 3.0.3 software was employed for metabolite quantification. The screening criteria for differential RNA modification were that the |log2FC| > 1 and FDR < 0.05.

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-Seq)

Genedenovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) provided high‐throughput RNA m6A MeRIP‐sequencing. Briefly, 100 μg of RNA was fragmented (~100 nt) and divided into two parts. RNA treated with EpiMark® N6-Methyladenosine Enrichment Kit [E1610S, New England Biolabs (NEB), USA] was used as IP, while untreated RNA was used as input. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, RNA libraries were constructed from the input and m6A immunoprecipitated RNAs using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, America). RNA using an Illumina NovaSeq instrument, library sequencing was carried out with 150 bp paired-end reads. To obtain clean reads, quality control of raw reads was conducted with Fastp (Chen et al. 2018), removing low-quality data. Using HISAT2 version 2.4 (Kim et al. 2019), clean reads were aligned to the reference genome of S. latifolia (Yang et al. 2021) with default settings, which is a chromosome-scale genome assembly. m6A peaks were called using exomePeak2 (v1.6.1) (Meng et al. 2014) under default parameter settings with p value < 1e-5. Motif analysis was conducted via the Hypergeometric Optimization of Motif EnRichment (HOMER) tool. The significant differential peaks were analyzed using DiffBind (Bressan et al. 2024) (version 2.8) software (|log2FC| > 1, p < 0.05). The gene expression analysis utilized input sequencing reads derived from MeRIP-Seq. The assembly of mapped reads for each sample was performed using StringTie v1.3.1 (Pertea et al. 2016). The FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) value was determined to measure gene expression levels utilizing RSEM (Li and Dewey 2011) software. Using DESeq2 (Love et al. 2014) software, gene differential expression analysis was carried out using the RSEM raw counts, with |log2FC| > 1, FDR < 0.05 as the criteria for identifying differentially expressed genes. Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment were analyzed. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. The co-regulatory relationships between gene expression and m6A methylation were described in a four-quadrant diagram.

Genome-wide identification of m6A-related proteins in S. latifolia

The previously published whole genome reference and annotated protein sequences of S. latifolia were used as the local protein database (Yang et al. 2021). The Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profile of MT-A70 family (PF05063), WTAP family (PF17098), RNA methyltransferase (PF05971), 2OG-Fe(II) oxygenase superfamily (PF13532), and YTH domain (PF04146) were downloaded from PFAM database (http://pfam.xfam.org/, accessed on 10 March 2025) and used as the query to search against local protein database using the simple HMM search with the threshold of E-value < 1 × 10−5 by TBtools (Chen et al. 2023). The presence and completeness of conserved domains were confirmed by the NCBI-CDD (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/, accessed on 10 March 2025) database. The physicochemical parameters of these proteins were examined using the ProtParam tool in ExPASy online software (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). WoLF PSORT with fungal setting (https://www.genscript.com/wolf-psort.html) was used to determine subcellular localization. The neighbour joining method embedded in MEGA 11.0 software (Tamura et al. 2021) was used to construct phylogenetic tree with the Boostrap parameter set to 1000.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Following the previously described protocol (Yang et al. 2017), qRT-PCR analyses were carried out to analyze gene expression levels using the advanced QuantStudio 1 Plus real-time PCR system from Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA), coupled with the high-sensitivity SupRealQ Purple Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (U+). Prior to qRT-PCR, total RNA was meticulously extracted utilizing the FreeZol reagent (Vazyme, China), ensuring optimal RNA quality for downstream analyses. For cDNA synthesis, the HiScript IV All-in-One Ultra RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, China) was employed, facilitating efficient and accurate reverse transcription. The specific primers utilized in this study are detailed in Table S1. The relative quantification of gene expression was conducted by the robust 2−ΔΔCT method, with normalization to the housekeeping gene gapdh as a stable reference.

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation-qPCR (MeRIP-qPCR)

MeRIP-qPCR analysis was performed with riboMeRIPTM m6A Transcriptome Profiling Kit (No. C11051, RiboBIO, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Mycelia were collected after culturing in potato dextrose broth (PDB; Difco) supplemented with 0 or 10 mmol/L cycloleucine (Sigma-Aldrich) at 25 °C in the dark for 7 d. After fragmentation with RNA Fragmentation Buffer, RNA was immunoprecipitated with anti-m6A magnetic beads A/G in immunoprecipitation buffer and eluted with free m6A. Enrichment levels were quantified by qPCR.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The differences between two independent groups were evaluated using Student’s t‐test (unpaired, two‐tailed). All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates (n ≥ 3). A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Cycloleucine inhibits mycelial growth and biomass production of S. latifolia

The inhibitory effects of cycloleucine on the growth of S. latifolia, were systematically evaluated under varying concentrations (0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 mmol/L) after 14 d cultivation. As shown in Figure 1a, cycloleucine exhibited concentration-dependent suppression of radial growth. At 5 mmol/L, cycloleucine reduced mycelial growth by 30.26% compared to the control (p < 0.001), while higher concentrations (10 to 20 mmol/L) caused 48.23% to 53.94% inhibition (EC50 = 15.17 mmol/L). Notably, the inhibition rate of 10 mmol/L was close to 50%, while the inhibition rate of higher concentrations of cycloleucine on the mycelial growth of S. latifolia was not much improved (Figure 1b). Additionally, the biomass production of S. latifolia cultured in PDB with 0 or 10 mmol/L cycloleucine for 7 d cultivation was measured. The results showed that the biomass production of S. latifolia in 10 mmol/L cycloleucine group was significantly reduced compared to the control group (p < 0.001, Figure 1c). These results demonstrate that cycloleucine significantly suppressed both mycelial growth and biomass accumulation in S. latifolia. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying cycloleucine’s inhibitory effects, we selected an effective concentration of 10 mmol/L for subsequent experiments.

Cycloleucine inhibits RNA methylation of S. latifolia

Cycloleucine is a competitive inhibitor of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) transferase and inhibits the SAM-mediated nucleic acid methylation (Zhang et al. 2022; Liang et al. 2024a). The levels of RNA 5-methylcytidine (m5C), N6-methyladenosine (m6A), 2’-O-methyluridine (Um), 2’-O-methyladenosine (Am), 2’-O-methylguanosine (Gm), and 2’-O-methylcytidine (Cm) of S. latifolia cultured in PDB with 0 mmol/L (PDB) or 10 mmol/L (PDB-C) cycloleucine for 7 d cultivation were detected using LC-MS/MS. The overlap of the total ion chromatogram (TIC) in mass spectrometry shown in Supplementary Figure S1a demonstrated the instrument’s high stability, ensuring data repeatability and reliability. The calibration curves for nucleoside standards had high linearity, indicated by coefficients of determination (R2) over 0.98 (Table S2). With more than 80% of substances in QC (Quality Control) samples showing a CV (coefficient of variation) value less than 0.2, the experimental data is considered very stable (Figure S1b). Differential modification level analysis showed that only the content of m6A was significantly changed among several detected RNA modifications (|log2FC| > 1 and FDR < 0.05, Figure 1d and Table S3).

Global changes in the m6A RNA methylome of S. latifolia induced by cycloleucine

To elucidate the epigenetic regulation of S. latifolia under cycloleucine treatment, we next performed an m6A MeRIP‐seq assay on S. latifolia mycelia treated with 0 mmol/L and 10 mmol/L cycloleucine. The details of the MeRIP sequencing were listed in Table S4. At least 4.20 million clean reads were obtained for each sample and over 70% of clean reads were unique mapped to the S. latifolia genome. The distribution of m6A peak on S. latifolia chromosomes was shown in Figure 2a,b. As illustrated in Figure 2c,d, among the two groups, the m6a methylation of mRNAs is principally present in the start codon and coding sequences region (CDSs), which accounted for 34.61% and 31.93% in PDB group and 36.71% and 30.7% in PDB-C group, respectively. The statistics of the number of peaks on genes showed that the majority (> 85%) of genes contained no more than three m6A peaks (Figure 2e,f). Based on the analysis of peaks (100 bp on both sides of the vertex) in both groups, it was anchored to a core motif sequence of 5’-GGACA-3’(p = 1e-1429, Figure 2g), which is the well-known DRACH (D = G/A/U, R= G/A, H = A/U/C) type m6A binding motif. There were 14,463 peaks in the PDB-C group and 14,937 peaks in PDB group with 7962 and 8011 related genes, respectively (Figure 2h).

Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated clear separation between the PDB and PDB-C groups (Figure 3a), with the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) accounting for 64.20% and 24.00% of the total variance, respectively. To assess sample reproducibility, we calculated pair-wise Spearman correlation coefficients based on peaks identified across all MeRIP-seq samples. As shown in Figure 3b, high correlations were observed among biological replicates within each treatment group. By screening with p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1, 1751 differentially methylated peaks (DMPs) were identified, of which 722 were hypermethylated and 1029 were hypomethylated (Figure 3c and Table S5).

To further analyze the functional role of m6A methylation modification in S. latifolia, GO enrichment analysis was performed for the differentially methylated peak-related genes (DMGs) and 728 GO terms were identified (p < 0.05) (Figure 3d and Table S6). The most strikingly enriched GO terms in the biological process category were DNA conformation change (GO:0071103), DNA packaging (GO:0006323), and chromosome organization (GO:0051276). In the molecular function category, ATP binding (GO:0005524), adenyl ribonucleotide binding (GO:0032559), and adenyl nucleotide binding (GO:0030554) were the most significantly enriched terms. Whereas terms associated with nuclear chromosome (GO:0000228), chromosome (GO:0005694), and CORVET complex (GO:0033263) were significantly enriched in the cellular component category. KEGG analysis revealed that these DMGs were enriched in the Cell cycle-yeast (ko04111), Glycerophospholipid metabolism (ko00564), and Autophagy-yeast (ko04138) (Figure 3e and Table S7).

Transcriptional changes of gene expression in S. latifolia induced by cycloleucine

To further explore the functional significance of m6A methylation in S. latifolia, we analyzed the transcriptome profiles using RNA-seq data (MeRIP-Seq input library). PCA results indicated that the samples were clustered based on the PDB and PDB-C groups with PC1 accounting for 97.60% and PC2 for 1.40% (Figure 4a). As shown in Figure 4b, the results of correlation for the three samples in each group were very close to each other. By screening with FDR < 0.05, |log2FC| > 1, 436 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, of which 283 were up-regulated and 153 were down-regulated (Figure 4c and Table S8). These DEGs were enriched in 401 GO terms (p < 0.05) (Figure 4d and Table S9). GO terms pertaining to biological processes were notably enriched in organic acid metabolic process (GO:0006082), carboxylic acid metabolic process (GO:0019752), and oxoacid metabolic process (GO:0043436). In the cellular component category, calcium channel complex (GO:0034704), presynaptic active zone (GO:0048786), and uniplex complex (GO:1990246) were the most significantly enriched terms. In the molecular function category, significant enrichment was observed for terms related to oxidoreductase activity (GO:0016491), catalytic activity (GO:0003824), and maleylacetoacetate isomerase activity (GO:0016034). Additionally, these DEGs were enriched in 16 KEGG pathways (p < 0.05), including Biosynthesis of amino acids (ko01230), Metabolic pathways (ko01100), and valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis (ko00290) (Figure 4e and Table S10).

Conjoint analysis of m6A methylation and gene expression

According to the joint analysis of the m6A methylation and gene expression, we identified a total of 67 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) harboring differential m6A peaks (Figure 5a and Table S11). Among these genes, 18 genes exhibited both m6A hypomethylation and up-regulated mRNA expression, while 13 showed m6A hypomethylation coupled with down-regulated mRNA expression. Conversely, 27 genes displayed m6A hypermethylation with up-regulated mRNA expression, and 21 genes showed m6A hypermethylation and down-regulated mRNA expression (Figure 5b). These genes were enriched in 183 GO terms (p < 0.05) (Figure 5c and Table S12). GO terms pertaining to biological processes were notably enriched in lipid metabolic process (GO:0006629), glycosyl compound metabolic process (GO:1901657), and mycothiol metabolic process (GO:0010126). In the cellular component category, integral component of plasma membrane (GO:0005887), intrinsic component of plasma membrane (GO:0031226), and glutamate synthase complex (NADPH) (GO:0009342) were the most significantly enriched terms. Whereas terms associated with mycothione reductase activity (GO:0050627), transmembrane transporter activity (GO:0022857), and carbon-oxygen lyase activity (GO:0016835) were significantly enriched in the molecular function category. Additionally, these DEGs were enriched in 2 KEGG pathways (p < 0.05), including Valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis (ko00290) and Vitamin B6 metabolism (ko00750) (Figure 5d and Table S13).

To further confirm the results of MeRIP-seq, the m6A methylation and gene expression alterations of EVM0007546 and EVM0009161 were validated. The changes in m6A methylation and gene expression of the two genes were analyzed using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV). As shown in Figure 5e,f, the EVM0007546 gene was m6A hypomethylated and mRNA expression down-regulated, while EVM0007546 gene was m6A hypomethylated and mRNA expression up-regulated. The gene expression of these two genes was validated by qRT-PCR. The results revealed that the mRNA levels of EVM0007546 gene were down-regulated in the PDB-C group compared to the PDB group, while the mRNA levels of EVM0009161 gene were up-regulated (Figure 5g). Further, the m6A methylation alterations of EVM0007546 and EVM0009161 were detected using MeRIP-qPCR. The results showed significantly increased m6A levels of both two genes (Figure 5h). The results were consistent in MeRIP-seq and MeRIP-qPCR.

Genome-wide identification of m6A related genes in S. latifolia

Through a genome-wide search, a total of 4 m6A writers, 3 m6A readers, and 6 m6A erasers were detected in S. latifolia genome (Table S14). The prediction accuracy was supported by the unique conserved domains displayed by each class (Figure 6a). Chromosome distribution analysis revealed that these genes were located on 8 out of 13 chromosomes as well as contig00018 (Figure 6b). Lachesis_group3 harbored the highest number of m6A-related genes (n = 3). Analysis of physicochemical properties demonstrated that the amino acid length varied among different protein categories: writers (365–640 aa), erasers (260–1311 aa), and readers (764–886 aa) (Table 1). The relative molecular weight (Mw) and theoretical pI ranged from 28.86 to 146.09 kDa and 4.66 to 9.82, respectively. Subcellular localization prediction displayed that most of S. latifolia m6A regulatory proteins were located in the nucleus. Phylogenetic tree analysis confirmed that S. latifolia EVM0007073.1 was close to S. cerevisiae NP011323.1, while S. latifolia EVM0008434.1 was far from other reported MT-A70 domain proteins in fungi (Figure S2a). The homology trees based on the multiple alignment of 21 ALKBH protein sequences of A. thaliana (14 members), C. parasitica (1 member), and S. latifolia (7 members), show that all alkbh genes of A. thaliana can be divided into 7 categories: ALKBH1, ALKBH2, ALKBH6, ALKBH7, ALKBH8, ALKBH9, and ALKBH10, but S. latifolia has only 5 categories: ALKBH1, ALKBH2, ALKBH6, ALKBH7, and ALKBH8, respectively (Figure S2b).

To further investigate their potential functions, the expression of S. latifolia m6A regulatory genes was analyzed according to the RNA-seq data described above. The results showed that there were no significant differences in the expression of all these genes in S. latifolia induced by cycloleucine (|log2FC| > 1, FDR < 0.05). As shown in Figure 5c, it was significantly up-regulated under cycloleucine treatment compared to the control.

Discussion

Cycloleucine inhibits the growth and m6A methylation of S. latifolia

Cycloleucine, a nonmetabolizable amino acid, could inhibit the methylation level of RNA in different species (Kang et al. 2018; Chen et al. 2019a, 2019b; Liang et al. 2024a). In A. flavus, one of the most economically devastating filamentous fungi globally, 20 mmol/L cyloleucine treatment significantly reduced RNA methylation modification levels (Liang et al. 2024a). In our result, cycloleucine specifically inhibited the level of RNA m6A in S. latifolia, but had no significant effect on the other 5 kinds of RNA methylation modifications (Figure 1 and Table 1). These results suggest that cycloleucine can be applied to the functional study of m6A modification in mushroom-forming fungi.

Cycloleucine can inhibit the growth and development of different species by inhibiting of RNA m6A modification. Cycloleucine impaired meiosis of oocytes and early embryonic development of porcine via RNA m6A methylation and altering the expression of metabolism-related genes in blastocysts (Yu et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2022). Cycloleucine greatly reduced A. flavus growth, conidia formation, mycelia dry weight, and contamination in peanut and corn seeds (Liang et al. 2024a). It also effectively inhibited AFB1 production, significantly altering total RNA methylation levels in cells. In our results, cycloleucine not only inhibited mycelial growth and biomass, but also decreased the content of m6A in RNA in S. latifolia (Figure 1 and Table 1). Cycloleucine also significantly inhibited plant growth of Solanum melongena L. (Niu et al. 2023), although the content of m6A was not determined. These indicate that cycloleucine can be used to explore the role of RNA m6A modification in biological growth and development.

m6A modification has important functions in mushroom-forming fungi

m6A represents a crucial RNA modification that has been extensively characterized across diverse organisms, including fungi. In yeast, numerous studies have elucidated the roles of m6A in various biological processes (Yadav and Rajasekharan 2017, 2018; Yadav et al. 2018; Varier et al. 2022; Ensinck et al. 2023; Park et al. 2023a, 2023b; Albihlal et al. 2024). Similarly, m6A has been investigated in several pathogenic fungi, including M. oryzae (Ren et al. 2022), A. flavus (Liang et al. 2024a, 2024b; Yang et al. 2024a), F. graminearum (Kim et al. 2024), and C. parasitica (Zhao et al. 2024, 2025). Research has shown that there are differences in m6A methylation across fungal species. In early diverging fungal lineages, abundant m6A is associated with transcriptionally active genes and up to 2.8% of all adenines were methylated in early diverging fungi, far exceeding levels in other eukaryotes and more derived fungi (Mondo et al. 2017). However, so far, there is no report on the function of m6A modification in mushroom-forming fungi.

S. latifolia, often referred to as cauliflower mushroom, possesses both medicinal and edible values (Qiu et al. 2025). Although cauliflower mushrooms are becoming increasingly economically significant and in high demand, their cultivation remains limited. One primary factor is the slow growth rate of S. latifolia mycelium, resulting in an extended cultivation cycle. Investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying mycelium growth is of considerable importance for advancing industrial development. Our findings demonstrated a significant correlation between the level of m6A modification and the growth of S. latifolia mycelium. This discovery not only establishes a foundational basis for future investigations into its underlying molecular mechanisms but also substantiates the critical role of m6A modification in mushroom-forming fungi.

Molecular mechanism underlying mycelial growth of S. latifolia

Research on the molecular mechanism underlying mycelial growth has been carried out to promote the cultivation of S. latifolia. Different media and substrates have been explored for its growth. For example, in the cultivation of S. latifolia from Pakistan, maximum cultural growth was observed on pine needles extract agar medium at 24 °C (Ghafoor and Niazi 2023). Pine sawdust serves as a primary substrate for the cultivation of S. latifolia. In our prior study, we employed comparative transcriptomics to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the degradation of fresh pine sawdust by S. latifolia (Yang et al. 2022). Through the analysis of organic acid content during the growth and development of S. latifolia, it has been observed that tartaric acid and L-pyroglutamic acid significantly enhance mycelial growth within the concentration range of 0.02 to 0.20 g/L. In contrast, citric acid and malic acid are effective in promoting mycelial growth at higher concentrations (Liu et al. 2024). Aspartic acid and glutamic acid markedly enhanced mycelial growth in comparison to ammonium sulfate and fish meal peptone and RNA-seq technology was employed to investigate the DEGs of S. latifolia when induced by various nitrogen sources (Liu et al. 2023). The secretion of excessive oxalic acid by S. latifolia inhibits mycelial growth during its saprophytic process (Shu et al. 2022a) and the mechanisms were explored by transcriptomic analysis (Qiu et al. 2022). In our transcriptome data, DEGs induced by cycloleucine were enriched in organic acid metabolic process (GO:0006082), organic acid biosynthetic process (GO:0016053), and organic acid catabolic process (GO:0016054). This suggests that cycloleucine may inhibit the hyphal growth of S. latifolia by affecting the metabolism and synthesis of organic acids.

Mycelial growth is a complex and fascinating biological process. Filamentous fungi, a large and ancient group of microorganisms, owe their ecological success in diverse niches largely to the elongate hypha, a chain of cells separated by septa. Hyphae grow through polarized exocytosis at the apex. This process involves the establishment of a growth site, maintenance of the growth axis, and transport of growth supplies like membranes and proteins along the cytoskeleton to the hyphal tip (Steinberg et al. 2017). In our transcriptome data (Table S6), there are multiple pathways enriched in DEGs that are closely related to the membrane, including iron ion import across plasma membrane (GO:0098711), integral component of plasma membrane (GO:0005887), transmembrane transporter complex (GO:1902495), mitochondrial calcium ion transmembrane transport (GO:0006851), sarcoplasmic reticulum membrane (GO:0033017), iron ion transmembrane transport (GO:0034755), and transition metal ion transmembrane transporter activity (GO:0046915). Meanwhile, there are also many GO terms related to amino acid synthesis processes and synthase activity, such as branched-chain amino acid biosynthetic process (GO:0009082), isoleucine biosynthetic process (GO:0009097), alpha-amino acid biosynthetic process (GO:1901607), cellular amino acid biosynthetic process (GO:0008652), valine biosynthetic process (GO:0009099), aspartate family amino acid biosynthetic process (GO:0009067), sulfur amino acid biosynthetic process (GO:0000097), leucine biosynthetic process (GO:0009098), L-asparagine biosynthetic process (GO:0070981), glutamine family amino acid biosynthetic process (GO:0009084), pyruvate family amino acid metabolic process (GO:0009078), threonine synthase activity (GO:0004795), tryptophan synthase activity (GO:0004834), glutamate synthase (NADPH) activity (GO:0004355). DEGs are also enriched in KEGG pathways involved in the synthesis and metabolism of some amino acids, including biosynthesis of amino acids (ko01230), valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis (ko00290), glutathione metabolism (ko00480), glycine, serine and threonine metabolism (ko00260), phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis (ko00400), beta-Alanine metabolism (ko00410), and lysine biosynthesis (ko00300) (Table S7). The valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis pathway was also enriched by the DEGs in A. flavus induced by cycloleucine (Liang et al. 2024a). All of these indicate that cycloleucine may inhibit the mycelial growth of S. latifolia by regulating protein synthesis and membrane function.

Apoptosis is regarded as an essential mechanism involved in numerous biological processes, such as normal cellular turnover, the appropriate development and functioning of the immune system, hormone-dependent atrophy, embryogenesis, and chemically induced cell death (Elmore 2007). In the F. graminearum, apoptosis-inducing factor 1 gene was significantly upregulated in the ΔFgmdm33 mutants, which exhibited a significant reduction in mycelial growth (Lv et al. 2024). Through our comprehensive analysis of m6A modifications and gene expression levels, we observed a significant upregulation in both the expression of the EVM0009161 gene, which encodes Apoptosis Inducing Factor 1 (AIF1), and the levels of m6A modifications (Figure 5). Autophagy is another genetically regulated, evolutionarily conserved process that regulates cell fate. Study showed that the m6A methylation genes encoded autophagy-related proteins are important for the growth and virulence of M. oryzae (Ren et al. 2022). In our results, two autophagy related GO terms were enriched by the DEGs, including autophagy of nucleus (GO:0044804) and piecemeal microautophagy of the nucleus (GO:0034727) (Table S4). The findings indicate that cycloleucine may modulate the hyphal growth of S. latifolia via mechanisms involving apoptosis and autophagy pathways.

m6A-modification factors in S. latifolia

The m6A modification is dynamically regulated by three classes of proteins: writers, erasers, and readers. While extensively studied in mammals and plants, the understanding of m6A regulatory mechanisms in fungi has rapidly expanded in recent years, revealing its essential roles in growth, development, and virulence. The m6A methyltransferases were identified in yeast (Park et al. 2023a, 2023b), A. flavus (Liang et al. 2024b), C. parasitica (Zhao et al. 2024), and F. graminearum (Kim et al. 2024), while the function of m6A demethylases was only explored in C. parasitica (Zhao et al. 2024). The global phylogenetic relationships of potential m6A writers, readers, and erasers in fungi have been reconstructed (Kim et al. 2024). Nonetheless, the complete set of m6A modification factors has not yet been identified in the selected species. In the case of Sparassis spp., they identified only two proteins containing the YTH domain and one MT-A70 domain containing protein. In contrast, our comprehensive genome-wide screening revealed a total of three proteins with the YTH domain and two MT-A70 domain containing proteins (Figure 6). Notably, no m6A demethylases were identified in Sparassis spp., whereas our research has identified a total of six potential m6A demethylases. Among the m6A modification factors identified in S. latifolia, only the EVM0008471 gene, which encodes a 2OG-Fe(II) oxygenase superfamily domain-containing m6A demethylase, was found to be upregulated under cycloleucine induction, as evidenced by both RNA-seq and qRT-PCR analyses (Figure 6). This indicates that cycloleucine may reduce the level of m6A modification in S. latifolia through the upregulation of EVM0008471 gene expression.

Conclusions

The industrial cultivation of S. latifolia is critically dependent on precise hyphal growth regulation. Our study demonstrates that cycloleucine treatment significantly inhibits hyphal growth and biomass accumulation, concomitant with a global reduction in RNA m6A methylation levels. Through meRIP-seq analysis, we identified 67 differentially m6A-modified genes (e.g., aif1 and mfs1), which are functionally enriched in apoptosis and nutrient transport pathways, suggesting their potential roles in growth suppression.

Notably, genome-wide screening revealed EVM0008471, encoding a conserved m6A demethylase, as a potential regulator of this process. Expression profiling confirmed that EVM0008471-mediated m6A erasure modulates the transcriptomic landscape to restrict hyphal development. To our knowledge, this is the first report linking m6A epitranscriptomic mechanisms to fungal growth control in edible basidiomycetes.

These findings not only advance our understanding of post-transcriptional regulation in fungal biology but also propose m6A modification as a potential target for strain optimization in industrial mushroom cultivation. Future work should explore the translational applications of m6A modulation to enhance fungal yield and stress resilience.
