High-intensity running exercise promotes knee meniscal damage via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis
Authors: Juncheng Yao, Haolin Ke, Guangxin Huang, Xuan Wang, Zhe Li, Yan Shao, Huabin Chen, Jianying Pan, Daozhang Cai, Chun Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimsTo examine cellular and molecular mechanisms of meniscal degeneration, including matrix degradation and inflammation, and identify therapeutic targets for osteoarthritis interventions.MethodsMeniscal tissue samples were collected from a total of ten patients. Using a small animal running platform, a mouse model of meniscal injury was established via running training, and the extent of the damage was assessed using histological and immunohistochemical staining. Human meniscal cells and tissues obtained from ten patients undergoing arthroscopic surgery or joint replacement arthroplasty were exposed to mechanical stimuli, mimicking the forces experienced during human running activity using a cyclic stretching system. Western blot and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analyses were performed to evaluate the responses. Transcriptome sequencing was conducted to determine gene expression changes associated with meniscal injuries, and functional alterations were analyzed using the Metascape platform. Rapamycin was administered via knee injections to evaluate its therapeutic effects on meniscal damage.ResultsHistological staining showed progressive meniscal tissue degeneration over time in the high-intensity running group. Transcriptome analysis revealed that the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) pathway was markedly enriched in mechanically overstressed meniscal cells. Downstream activation of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway was confirmed via western blot and immunofluorescence, consistent with the results of the mouse chronic overload model. The subsequent intra-articular administration of rapamycin, a well-established mTOR inhibitor, substantially mitigated meniscal degeneration in mice, a finding corroborated in experiments using human meniscal cells.ConclusionThese observations demonstrate that prolonged and excessive mechanical stress may contribute to meniscal damage or degeneration through activation of the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B/mammalian target of rapamycin (PI3K/AKT/mTOR) axis.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2025;14(11):969–983.

Introduction

Musculoskeletal injuries associated with running are common, particularly among long-distance athletes such as marathon runners, and the incidence rate is 28.1%.1,2 Excessive biomechanical stress on the joints is a substantial contributing factor to the development of OA.3,4 Additionally, injuries to the meniscus, a key structure involved in mechanical load transmission within the knee joint, play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of OA, leading to severe knee pain and functional impairment.5,6 The meniscus is essential for load distribution and shock absorption, and excessive or prolonged mechanical stress from repetitive loading may contribute to meniscal degeneration, particularly in chronic conditions associated with factors such as higher BMI or malalignment.7 These observations suggest that excessive mechanical stimulation may result in meniscal injury and degeneration as a secondary effect.

The meniscus is a fibrocartilaginous tissue essential for load transmission and distribution within the knee joint.8,9 Meniscal tears are among the most common knee injuries, with an annual prevalence of 60 to 70 cases per 100,000 individuals. These injuries are intricately linked to the development of knee arthritis.10,11 Therefore, preventing meniscal tears is particularly important for young individuals. The unique characteristics of the meniscus could be attributed to its complex geometry, ultrastructure, and tissue composition. The meniscal tissue comprises a hydrated extracellular matrix that primarily contains collagen. While type 2 collagen is predominantly present in the endochondral region, type 1 collagen is mainly found in the outer vascularized region.12,13 Prior studies have shown that collagen and proteoglycan loss occurs in degenerated menisci, accompanied by the overexpression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs 4 (ADAMTS4).14,15 Evidence for the crucial role of mechanical stress in the pathophysiology of meniscal degeneration is accumulating.16 Killian et al17 observed that excessive mechanical stress could considerably decrease lunate cell viability and increase proinflammatory mediator levels. Nonetheless, their research did not examine the specific mechanical factors and molecular mechanisms underlying meniscal degeneration.

Some studies have indicated that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway plays a key role in inflammation, metabolism, and apoptosis.18,19 Regulation of this pathway is essential for the development and progression of various conditions, including cancer, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases. Furthermore, a recent investigation has uncovered the involvement of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in the development of OA.20 Nonetheless, its specific role in meniscal degeneration is yet to be elucidated.

This study aimed to examine the effects of varying running intensities and durations on meniscal damage in a mouse treadmill model and to investigate the impact of mechanical stimuli on human meniscal cells. We used transcriptome sequencing to explore the molecular mechanisms underlying meniscal damage induced by excessive mechanical stress. We hypothesized that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway may play a central role in this process and that modulating this pathway could mitigate meniscal injury.

Methods

Animal study design

All animal care and experimental procedures were approved by the Laboratory Animal Committee of Southern Medical University (approval number: SMUL202308012). This research adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines, and no animals were excluded due to accidental death. An ARRIVE checklist is included in the Supplementary Material to show that the guidelines were adhered to in this study. A total of 50 male C57BL/6 mice (aged 8 to 10 weeks, weighing 20 to 25 g) were used in this study. Initially, all mice underwent a one-week treadmill adaptation period, with sessions lasting 30 minutes per day. The details of the training speed and treadmill incline are shown in Table I. Following adaptation, 40 mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 10 per group), and the animals in each group were subjected to treadmill training at varying speeds, following a schedule of five training days per week with two days of rest. The treadmill incline was kept at 10°, and the training duration was four or eight weeks, as outlined in Table II. An additional ten mice (n = 5 per group) received both pharmacological intervention and treadmill training, with the treadmill set to 15 m/min and a 10° incline. Mice in the treatment group were administered 5 µl of 10 µM rapamycin solution intra-articularly on a daily basis. In contrast, those in the control group received an equal volume of the rapamycin solvent, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Knee joint specimens were acquired after four or eight weeks, as applicable, for histological and immunohistochemical analysis.

Isolation and culturing of human meniscal cells

The Ethics Committee of The Third Affiliated Hospital, Southern Medical University approved this study. Meniscal tissue samples were collected from five patients who underwent partial medial meniscectomy (mean age 27 years (22 to 32); three males and two females), to prepare paraffin sections and cell extraction for subsequent experiments. Additionally, meniscal tissue samples were collected from five patients who underwent total knee arthroplasty (mean age 68 years (65 to 72); three males and two females), to prepare paraffin sections for further analysis. Meniscal cells were extracted using the method of Sun et al,21 briefly involving mincing the tissue, enzymatic digestion with collagenase, and culturing in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum.

Establishment of a stress-induced meniscus degeneration model

A Flexcell tension system was used to establish a model of meniscal degeneration. This system used a vacuum to apply strain to human meniscal cells adhered to a flexible silicone membrane (BioFlex plate; Flexcell International Corporation, USA). The setup was created as previously described.22,23 Meniscal cells were stretched at mechanical strains of 5%, 10%, and 20% (0.5 Hz) for six hours and 24 hours using the computer-controlled Flexcell FX-5000 unit. Total cellular proteins were extracted for Western blot analysis, and RNA was collected for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis. In the rapamycin treatment studies, cells in the experimental group were pre-treated with the drug at 10 ng/ml for one hour prior to stretching, whereas the control group received an equal volume of DMSO (as the solvent). Both groups were subjected to 20% mechanical strain, and total cellular proteins and RNA were extracted after 24 hours of stretching.

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed as reported previously.24 Subsequently, the relative messenger RNA (mRNA) expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used are listed in Table III.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed as described previously.25 Briefly, proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% non-fat milk, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and ECL detection. Densitometric analysis of the images was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.53; National Institutes of Health (NIH), USA). Mouse anti-glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (1:10,000, ab8245; Abcam, UK), rabbit anti-MMP13 (1:2,000, 18165-1-AP; Proteintech, USA), rabbit anti-A disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs 5 (ADAMTS5) (1:1,000, A2836; Abclonal, USA), rabbit anti-collagen type I (COL1) (1:2,000, 14695-1-AP; Proteintech), rabbit anti-Aggrecan (1:1,000, A11691; Abclonal), rabbit anti-phospho-PI3K p85 (1:1,000, YP0224; ImmunoWay, USA), rabbit anti-phospho-AKT (Thr308) (1:500, PC2720; Abmart, China), rabbit anti-phospho-S6 (S240/S244) (1:500, AP0537; Abclonal), rabbit anti-PI3K (1:500, A17433; Abclonal), rabbit anti-AKT (1:1,000, ab192623; Abcam), and rabbit anti-S6 (1:500, A6058; Abclonal) served as the primary antibodies used.

Phalloidin staining

Cells cultured on stretch plates were subjected to 24 hours of stretching at varying intensities. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, the cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 and then incubated in a fluorescein isothiocyanate-labelled phalloidin working solution (50 μg/ml for 30 minutes at room temperature) containing 1% bovine serum albumin. DAPI (4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) was used for nuclear counterstaining, and fluorescent images were acquired to observe cytoskeletal changes in meniscal cells after stretching. ImageJ software was used to quantify the nuclear spread area.

Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining

The expressions of phospho-PI3K, phospho-AKT, and phospho-S6 in meniscal tissues excised from human patients and from mouse knee joints were evaluated using immunohistochemical staining to determine the activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Additionally, the expressions of MMP13 and Aggrecan, markers of meniscal cartilage degeneration and damage in mouse knees, were detected as previously documented,26 briefly involving antigen retrieval, blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin, incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, and visualization with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAB substrate. The same groups of mice were used to assess these and related indicators in serial sections of each mouse’s knee joint.

Histological evaluation score

Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Safranin O-Fast Green staining were performed on knee joints from mice and human meniscal tissue samples obtained from a total of ten individuals. 27-29 Meniscal samples from mice and humans were assessed using the scoring systems proposed by Pauli et al,27 Kwok et al,28 and Glasson SS et al, respectively, whereas the cartilage surfaces of the mice were evaluated using the scoring system of the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI).29 Histological scoring was performed by three independent reviewers (JY, HK, HC) who were blinded to the group assignments. Intraobserver and interobserver reliability were determined using the intraclass correlation coefficient. The anterior and posterior menisci of mice were scored based on structural, cellular, and matrix staining criteria; the total score ranged from 0 to 25. Human menisci were scored based on surface properties, cellularity, matrix and collagen disorganization, and Safranin O-Fast Green staining intensity; the total score ranged from 0 to 18. The cartilage surfaces in mice were graded from 0 to 6 based on the severity of cartilage degeneration and defects, with higher scores indicating more severe meniscal and cartilage damage or deterioration.

RNA sequencing and analysis of differentially expressed genes

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 1 µg of RNA was collected from each sample for library construction. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform (Illumina, USA), generating 150-bp paired-end reads. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq (2012) R package (developed by Simon Anders and Wolfgang Huber, Germany), with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as those with |log2 fold change| > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. All sequencing and analyses were performed by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (China). Clustering and enrichment analyses of the DEGs were conducted using the Metascape online platform (USA),30 developed by Zhou et al.31

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data analysis between groups was conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, while comparisons between two groups were performed using independent-samples t-test. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. If assumptions were violated, non-parametric tests including the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test were applied. Data were presented as the mean (SD). For functional enrichment analysis, p-values were calculated using Fisher’s exact test. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 20.0; IBM, USA), and the data were visualized using GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Results

Histological assessment of cartilage and meniscal changes in the knee joints of mice

The anterior and posterior horns of the meniscus and the articular cartilage surfaces were assessed in different groups of mice using histological staining (Figure 1a). After four weeks of running training, the mice in the moderate- and high-intensity groups showed signs of meniscal degeneration, including reduced Safranin O staining and cavitation. This degeneration became more pronounced at the eight-week timepoint, particularly in the long-term high-intensity running group in which more severe meniscal degeneration and early signs of OA on the cartilage surfaces were observed. Histological scores of the menisci were elevated at both timepoints in the fast-running group, indicating meniscal damage, which was exacerbated at eight weeks (Figure 1b). In addition, defects in the articular cartilage layer were seen in the fast-running group, which worsened at the eight-week mark (Figure 1c). Overall, some mice in the moderate- and high-intensity running groups displayed decreased cartilage thickness and calcification of the cartilage surface after eight weeks. These results indicate that long-term high-intensity running can result in meniscal degeneration and varying degrees of wear on the articular cartilage.

Response of human meniscal cells to different mechanical stimuli

To further examine the response of human meniscal cells to mechanical stimulation, they were subjected to varying intensities of strain using the Flexcell loading device (Figure 2a). In this study, 5%, 10%, and 20% mechanical strains were applied. Western blot analysis revealed that at 20% stimulation for 24 hours, the expression levels of COL1 and Aggrecan decreased, whereas those of MMP13 and ADAMTS5 increased (Figure 2b). After 24 hours of 20% stimulation, the mRNA expression levels of MMP13 and ADAMTS5 were elevated, whereas that of ACAN was reduced. Although COL1A1 mRNA expression showed a reduction, it was not statistically significant compared with the control group (p > 0.05, independent-samples t-test) (Figure 2c). These changes show that excessive mechanical stimulation affects gene expression in human meniscal cells. Subsequently, phalloidin staining was used to visualize the meniscal cytoskeleton. This study showed that 20% of stimulation induced considerable alterations in meniscal cell morphology. The nuclear spread area was then measured (Figures 2d to 2e). These observations point to prolonged and excessive mechanical stimulation leading to changes in cellular structure in the meniscus.

Excessive mechanical stimulation damages meniscal tissues and cells

Additional experiments were performed to validate the conclusion that prolonged excessive mechanical strain aggravates meniscal damage. Eight-week-old C57BL/6 mice were selected, and the expressions of MMP13 and Aggrecan were evaluated using immunohistochemical staining (Figures 3a and 3b). In the high-intensity group, MMP13 expression was increased, whereas that of Aggrecan was decreased in the meniscus. These changes were particularly evident in the anterior horn. Furthermore, the posterior horn exhibited a decrease in Aggrecan and an increase in MMP13 expression. However, the differences were less pronounced than those in the anterior horn. Prolonged low-intensity mechanical stimulation led to increased Aggrecan expression and decreased MMP13 expression in the anterior horn of the meniscus compared with control mice of the same age group. These findings suggest the potential protective effect of low-intensity mechanical stimulation on the meniscus. Moreover, human meniscal cells were extracted and subjected to 20% stretch for 24 hours (Figure 3c). Immunofluorescence staining revealed a decrease in Aggrecan-positive cells and an increase in MMP13- and ADAMTS5-positive cells (Figure 3d). These findings indicate that prolonged excessive mechanical stimulation contributes to meniscal damage.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis of gene expression changes in meniscal cells

To elucidate the specific mechanisms by which excessive mechanical stimulation exacerbates meniscal injury, primary human meniscal cells were extracted and subjected to 20% mechanical stretch for 24 hours, followed by transcriptome sequencing analysis. The heatmap indicates that 130 genes were upregulated and 94 were downregulated in the mechanically stimulated group compared with the control group (Figure 4a). Furthermore, the volcano plot highlighted the 15 most significantly differentially expressed genes between the upregulated and downregulated groups, with red indicating upregulated genes and blue indicating downregulated genes (Figure 4b). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses illustrate the functional changes in meniscal cells subjected to an excessive mechanical load (Supplementary Figures a and b). Subsequently, the functions of these DEGs were examined using the Metascape gene function enrichment platform. This study established that the upregulated genes were predominantly linked to long-term potentiation, wound healing, and the PI3K-AKT signalling pathway (Figure 4c). In contrast, the downregulated genes were mainly involved in cell division, cell cycle, and regulation of mitotic nuclear division (Figure 4d). The PI3K-AKT pathway showed the highest enrichment among the upregulated genes, implying its crucial role in the response to excessive mechanical stimulation.

Overactivation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in damaged or degenerated meniscal tissues and cells

The role of the PI3K/AKT pathway in meniscal degeneration was further validated. The Metascape gene function enrichment analysis showed that PI3K/AKT-related genes were associated with multiple functions, asserting the significance of this pathway in the enrichment network (Figure 5a). The mTOR pathway is a critical downstream component of the PI3K/AKT pathway.32 Investigations were performed to determine whether excessive mechanical stimulation worsens meniscal injury by activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Human meniscal tissues were analyzed for this purpose (Figure 5b). Histological scoring of the human meniscus revealed significantly higher scores in the OA group than the control group, indicating severe damage and a considerable increase in the expressions of phosphorylated proteins such as P-PI3K, P-AKT, and P-S6 (Figure 5c). In addition, the meniscal cells were subjected to 20% mechanical strain for 24 hours using the Flexcell loading device. Western blot analysis indicated increased P-PI3K, P-AKT, and P-S6 levels, suggesting activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway (Figure 5d). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed an increase in cells positive for P-PI3K, P-AKT, and P-S6 (Figure 5e), and statistical analysis showed significant differences (Figure 5f).

In vivo, a mouse model of prolonged high-intensity running in eight-week-old C57BL/6 mice was constructed. Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated that levels of P-PI3K, P-AKT, and P-S6 were elevated in the anterior and posterior horns of the meniscus and the articular surface compared with the control group (Figure 5g). These differences were statistically significant (Figure 5h), indicating the activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in the mouse model. Thus, activating this pathway may serve as a key target in the process by which excessive mechanical stimulation leads to meniscal degeneration.

Inhibition of the mTOR pathway alleviates meniscal damage and degeneration

To confirm the findings, rapamycin, a classical mTOR pathway inhibitor, was administered via intra-articular injections in eight-week-old mice, followed by eight weeks of treadmill training. The therapeutic effects of the drug were evaluated using histological staining (Figure 6a). Histological scoring indicated that the rapamycin-treated group exhibited significantly lower meniscal and articular cartilage scores (Figures 6b and 6c), with a statistically significant difference. Immunohistochemical analysis of Aggrecan and MMP13 expressions (Figure 6e) indicated that MMP13 was significantly downregulated and Aggrecan was significantly upregulated in the rapamycin-treated group (Figure 6f). These findings indicate that rapamycin exerts a therapeutic effect on meniscal degeneration by inhibiting mTOR.

In vitro, human meniscal cells were extracted, subjected to 20% mechanical strain for 24 hours, and treated with rapamycin. qRT-PCR analysis showed downregulation of MMP13 and ADAMTS5 expressions and upregulation of COL1A1 and ACAN expressions (Figure 6d). Immunofluorescence staining revealed a reduction in MMP13- and ADAMTS5-positive cells and an increase in Aggrecan-positive cells in the rapamycin-treated group compared to controls (Figure 6g), and the differences were statistically significant (Figure 6h). These results indicate that rapamycin protects the meniscus by inhibiting the mTOR pathway (Figure 7).

Chronic excessive running induced meniscal degeneration and mild osteoarthritis in mice. Similarly, excessive mechanical loading induced damage and degeneration in human meniscal cells, along with activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Rapamycin injections inhibited the activation of the mTOR pathway, which delayed the onset of meniscal degeneration and damage induced by excessive mechanical loading.

Discussion

This study examined the effects of various parameters on meniscal injury using a treadmill model in mice. Distinct responses of primary human meniscal cells to mechanical stimulation were observed, demonstrating that excessive mechanical stress harms the meniscus. While moderate- or high-intensity running caused meniscal damage and degeneration in mice, low-intensity running did not induce such damage. Consistent with these observations, earlier investigations have suggested that mechanical signals are crucial in regulating cellular differentiation and gene expression during processes such as development, morphogenesis, and wound healing.33 Moreover, excessive mechanical stimulation damaged and degenerated human meniscal cells, indicating that the meniscus exhibits similar mechanical properties to those observed in the mouse model. These findings provide key insights into the recommended exercise intensity during daily running activities to prevent meniscal damage.

The incidence of meniscal injuries has been rising annually, with over one million patients undergoing surgical repair or meniscectomy each year in the USA alone.34 Partial or total meniscectomy is often needed owing to irreparable meniscal damage. Nonetheless, meniscectomy considerably enhances the risk of developing OA later in life by elevating joint contact stress. Approximately 50% of patients with meniscal injuries develop OA within ten to 20 years post-injury.5,35 However, long-term clinical follow-up studies have revealed that various surgical interventions for OA are often associated with postoperative complications, such as infection, implant loosening, and joint stiffness.36-38 Considering the profound psychological and socioeconomic burdens associated with these complications for patients, preventing OA represents an urgent public health priority.39-41 Meniscal tears are hypothesized to be associated with meniscal degeneration, implying that meniscal injury can occur even without applying large forces.7 Previous studies have shown that increased expression levels of genes encoding MMPs promote collagen fibre degradation, leading to meniscal tears, with these proteolytic activities concurrently triggering inflammatory pathways implicated in pain and OA development.42 Moreover, Col1a expression is reduced in meniscal tears.8,43,44 The degradation and reduction of type I collagen fibres negatively impact meniscal health and may contribute to collagen tissue disorders,45 which are notable factors in meniscal degeneration.

This research focused on the effects of excessive mechanical stress on meniscal injury. The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is upregulated in OA and has been reported to be closely linked to cellular autophagy and energy metabolism. Knocking down the mTOR gene in transgenic mice effectively alleviated the development of OA. The overactivation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway has been strongly associated with subchondral bone sclerosis. In addition, a PI3K/AKT inhibitor (LY294002) has been documented to alleviate subchondral osteosclerosis and simultaneously attenuate cartilage degeneration by inhibiting osteogenesis in OA mice.21 In the present study, pathway enrichment analysis of the upregulated genes identified that PI3K/AKT was the central component of the regulatory network. The downstream mTOR pathway was markedly activated, as confirmed using western blot and immunohistochemistry. Thus, mechanical stress may lead to meniscal damage and degeneration via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling axis. Furthermore, blocking the mTOR pathway with rapamycin both in vitro and in vivo mitigated meniscal degeneration under excessive mechanical stimulation, supporting the hypothesis that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway mediates meniscal degeneration under mechanical stress.

The biological response of meniscal cells to mechanical stimulation is vital for the physiology, pathology, and repair processes of the meniscus.46 Mikic et al47 demonstrated that immobilizing the limbs in late-stage chicken embryos inhibited meniscal development, emphasizing the pertinent role of mechanical stress in meniscus formation. Similarly, in a canine model of internal meniscal injury, collagen content was reduced in the injured area of immobilized animals compared with those that resumed movement immediately after the surgery.48 Magnan et al45 observed that excessive stress in a rabbit model substantially reduced lunate cell viability and increased the levels of proinflammatory mediators such as nitric oxide (NO). Applying 10% strain to meniscus explants for two hours at 1 Hz inhibited NO release and enhanced proteoglycan gene expression.49,50 In contrast, high strain amplitudes (20%) induced pathological responses, including the upregulation of MMP1, MMP3, MMP13, ADAMTS4, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and interleukin-1α, resulting in augmented NO release and proteoglycan degradation.50-52 Similar findings have been noted in a 2D cell cycle tensile strain model, where uniaxial cyclic stretching at 0.5 Hz for 24 hours upregulated type II collagen and SOX9 mRNA expressions in meniscal cells in vitro.53 Therefore, appropriate mechanical stimulation appears necessary for normal meniscal physiological activity. Nevertheless, excessive stress may lead to meniscal cell degeneration. In the present study, 20% mechanical strain increased the expressions of MMP13 and ADAMTS5 in meniscal cells, demonstrating that excessive mechanical stress may contribute to meniscal injury and degeneration.

Despite these promising findings, there are certain limitations. First, the biomechanical environment of the knee joint in small animals differs from that in humans. Therefore, the treadmill model in mice may not completely replicate the biomechanical conditions experienced by the meniscus and articular cartilage during human running. Second, all meniscal cells used in this study were derived from clinical meniscal specimens injured at different timepoints and underwent arthroscopic resection. Third, other pathways downstream of PI3K/AKT that may be relevant to meniscal injuries were not explored. Hence, these drawbacks should be addressed in further studies.

However, this study established the effects of excessive running exercise on meniscal injuries. The findings suggest that mTOR pathway inhibitors can serve as protective agents, offering a potential strategy to prevent meniscal injuries in the future.
