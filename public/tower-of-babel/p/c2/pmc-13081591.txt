Introduction

High-grade dysplastic spondylolisthesis (HGDS) is a rare but severe pediatric spinal deformity, characterized by pronounced lumbosacral dysplasia, vertebral translation exceeding 50%, and progressive sagittal malalignment, often causing persistent low back pain, neurological symptoms, or functional impairment [1–3]. If left untreated or inappropriately managed, HGDS can progress rapidly, leading to worsening neurological deficits and irreversible global sagittal imbalance [2, 4–6]. Although in situ fusion and partial reduction are commonly performed in children due to concerns about neurological complications, there is still no consensus on the optimal surgical strategy for HGDS [4, 5, 7–9]. Incomplete reduction and suboptimal prior management have been associated with abnormal compensatory changes and persistent sagittal imbalance, as illustrated by a representative referral case treated at our institution (Fig. 1).

Early efforts toward complete anatomical reduction were pioneered by Ruf et al. (2006) [10], who demonstrated that temporary L4 instrumentation combined with L5–S1 fusion could achieve near-anatomical alignment through controlled distraction and ligamentotaxis. This landmark work established the feasibility of full reduction while preserving motion at L4–5, representing a major conceptual advance over traditional in situ fusion. However, the technique relied on upper-level lever distraction and temporary fixation at L4, which may increase the risk of L5 nerve traction and impose mechanical stress on the adjacent L4–5 segment. Subsequent studies have highlighted the technical challenge of achieving full reduction while maintaining neural safety and long-term lumbosacral stability, particularly in skeletally immature patients. Building upon this foundation, we refined the concept by adopting a purely posterior approach incorporating circumferential (360°) soft-tissue release and sacral dome osteotomy to facilitate low-tension, direct anatomical realignment without temporary upper-level fixation. This modification aims to achieve complete reduction and restoration of lumbosacral alignment while minimizing neurological risk and preserving spinal mobility [11]. Here, we present the first multicenter series reporting outcomes of sacral dome osteotomy, complete L5 reduction, and single-level posterior L5–S1 fusion in pediatric HGDS, and we further explore factors associated with residual sagittal imbalance at final follow-up.

Patients and methods

Study design and patients

We conducted a retrospective review of patients younger than 18 years diagnosed with spondylolisthesis who underwent posterior spinal fusion between January 2008 and December 2023 at two tertiary hospitals experienced in spinal deformity care. All surgeries were performed under senior surgeon supervision with assistance from spine specialists. Informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians.

During the study period, all consecutive patients with L5 high-grade dysplastic spondylolisthesis treated using sacral dome osteotomy, complete L5 reduction, and posterior single-level L5–S1 fusion were included, provided that a minimum follow-up of 2 years with complete standing whole-spine radiographs was available. Low-grade dysplastic spondylolisthesis (Grade I–II), when present, was managed conservatively or with alternative surgical strategies and was therefore not included in the current analysis.

Inclusion criteria were: age under 18 years, radiographically confirmed L5 HGDS, treatment with sacral dome osteotomy, complete L5 reduction, posterior single-level fusion at L5–S1, and a minimum of 2 years of follow-up with complete standing whole-spine radiographs obtained every 6 months.

Exclusion criteria included any construct not limited to single-level L5–S1 instrumentation and fusion (e.g., extension of instrumentation to L4, or alternative constructs that bypass L5 fixation such as L4–S1 instrumentation), concomitant scoliosis requiring surgical intervention (typically defined as a Cobb angle ≥ 40°), in situ or partial L5 reduction, prior spinal surgery, or incomplete clinical or imaging data despite repeated follow-up attempts.

All enrolled patients presented with symptoms such as low back pain, lower extremity pain, lower extremity numbness, neurogenic claudication, or abnormal gait. One patient exhibited urinary incontinence and preoperative lower extremity weakness (Grade IV), while two others also had lower extremity weakness (Grade IV).

Surgical procedure

After general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the prone position. A standard midline incision was made to expose the posterior structures of the spine, and pedicle screw fixation was performed bilaterally from L5 to S1. Decompression was performed by resecting the spinous process, lamina, and inferior articular process of L5 using an ultrasonic bone scalpel. Partial removal of the epidural fat and surrounding tissue was performed to expose and protect the bilateral L5 nerve roots and dural sac, further decompressing the L5 nerve roots. Following this, a discectomy of L5/S1 was performed. Next, the superior endplate of S1 (S1 dome) was smoothed using an ultrasonic bone scalpel, proceeding bilaterally in a posterior-to-anterior direction. The soft and bony tissues around the L5 and S1 nerve roots were resected in all directions for complete release prior to reduction, specifically a 360° release. Two rods were then connected to the bilateral pedicle screws at S1 and secured. L5 reduction was accomplished by connecting and locking the proximal rods to the bilateral proximal screws. Reduction was achieved using a standard rod–screw reduction maneuver with progressive locking of the L5 set screws after sacral dome resection and smoothing, following a 360° release, under continuous neuromonitoring, without using reduction screws. Cages were bilaterally inserted into the L5/S1 intervertebral space, with supplemental allograft and autograft bone used for interbody fusion. The procedure was verified using C-arm fluoroscopy. After the reduction and interbody fusion, the nerve roots were re-explored to ensure there was no compression or traction. The system of intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring was implemented in all our surgeries, with motor evoked potentials (MEP) and somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEP) being the major monitoring metrics. If neuromonitoring changes suggested potential neurological compromise, the reduction would be relaxed and reassessed, and an intraoperative wake-up test would be considered when appropriate. Postoperative ambulation began on the second day after surgery. Patients were instructed to wear protective braces during ambulation for the first three months after surgery.

Evaluation of radiographic parameters

Full-length standing radiographs were obtained preoperatively, postoperatively, and at the latest follow-up for patients without revision surgery. For those who underwent revision, the most recent radiographs obtained prior to revision were used as the final follow-up assessment. The superior endplate of S1 was identified using the method described by Labelle et al. [12]. Radiographic measurements were conducted with Surgimap software (Nemaris, New York, NY, USA), and standard parameters such as slip angle [13], Dubousset’s lumbosacral angle (Dub-LSA) [14], L5 incidence [15] as previously described.

Radiographic parameters and their definitions are summarized in Table 1, and the measurement techniques are illustrated in Fig. 2. All radiographic measurements were independently performed by two experienced spine surgeons, and the mean values were used for statistical analysis.

An SVA greater than 5.0 cm was considered indicative of global sagittal imbalance. A PT/SS ratio greater than 1 was defined as an unbalanced pelvis, reflecting pelvic decompensation and regional sagittal malalignment. According to Hresko et al. [16], patients with an unbalanced pelvis demonstrate sagittal alignment patterns distinct from those with balanced pelvises or normal controls, suggesting increased mechanical stress across the lumbosacral junction.

In this study, sagittal imbalance was defined as meeting either of the following criteria: global sagittal imbalance (SVA > 5.0 cm) or regional sagittal malalignment (PT/SS > 1). Accordingly, the primary outcome, residual sagittal imbalance at the latest follow-up, was recorded when either criterion was present.

Evaluation of complications and revision surgery

Complications were categorized as neurological, implant-related, wound-related, or radiographic adjacent segment degeneration (R-ASD). R-ASD was defined on standing full-length anteroposterior and lateral radiographs as anterior or posterior vertebral translation greater than 3 mm, or posterior disc space opening exceeding 5° [17].All complications and subsequent revision surgeries were systematically recorded. Radiographic progression was evaluated by comparing parameters measured at 6 months postoperatively with those obtained at the final follow-up.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Depending on data distribution, independent t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used for group comparisons. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test or Pearson’s chi-square test, as appropriate.

Univariable logistic regression was performed to explore associations between selected clinical and radiographic variables and residual sagittal imbalance. Given the limited sample size and number of outcome events, multivariable logistic regression was conducted in an exploratory manner with a restricted number of covariates, selected based on clinical relevance and event-per-variable considerations rather than solely on statistical significance, to minimize the risk of overfitting.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was applied in an exploratory context to assess the discriminatory ability of preoperative sagittal vertical axis for residual sagittal imbalance within this cohort. Cutoff values were identified descriptively based on Youden’s index. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p value < 0.05, without adjustment for multiple comparisons. All analyses were verified by an independent biostatistician.

Result

Patient demographics and surgical characteristics

Thirty-one pediatric patients (30 females and 1 male) were included in this study, with a mean age at surgery of 9.66 ± 2.27 years (range, 5–15 years). The mean operative time was 243.77 ± 70.90 min (range, 75–420 min), and the mean estimated blood loss was 392.26 ± 258.81 mL (range, 100–1100 mL). The mean follow-up duration was 3.05 ± 2.01 years (range, 2–10 years) (Table 2).

Radiological outcomes

Radiographic outcomes are summarized in Table 3. The Slip% significantly decreased from 61.0 ± 11.0 preoperatively to 13.0 ± 10.0 postoperatively (p < 0.001) and remained stable at 9.0 ± 12.0 at the latest follow-up (p = 0.064). The Dub-LSA increased from 79.25 ± 14.06° to 95.87 ± 11.03° (p < 0.001) and was maintained at 95.81 ± 9.03°. The PI–LL mismatch improved from 36.12 ± 21.08° to 18.82 ± 15.67° postoperatively (p < 0.001) and further to 15.48 ± 10.69° at follow-up. Other spinopelvic parameters demonstrated similar trends toward restored sagittal alignment. L5 incidence and PT decreased postoperatively, accompanied by an increase in SS and lumbar lordosis. Thoracic kyphosis increased postoperatively, indicating a return toward physiological curvature, while thoracolumbar kyphosis, upper instrumented vertebral angle (UIVA), and segmental L5–S1 lordosis remained stable throughout follow-up. Sagittal vertical axis (SVA) improved from 2.7 ± 4.3 cm to 1.0 ± 2.8 cm (p = 0.014) and remained balanced at 1.4 ± 2.4 cm (Fig. 3).

At the latest follow-up, 7 patients (22.6%) met the defined unfavorable sagittal outcome (SVA > 5.0 cm or PT/SS > 1), indicating residual or recurrent spinopelvic imbalance.

Exploratory analyses of residual sagittal imbalance

Exploratory analyses examining factors associated with residual sagittal imbalance are summarized in Tables 4 and 5. In univariable logistic regression analyses, preoperative pelvic tilt, preoperative sagittal vertical axis (SVA), and postoperative PI–LL mismatch were associated with residual sagittal imbalance. Other variables, including age, sex, and additional postoperative parameters, did not demonstrate significant associations. In exploratory multivariable analysis with a limited number of covariates, preoperative SVA remained associated with residual sagittal imbalance (OR 1.04, 95% CI 1.00–1.08, p = 0.037), whereas the associations for preoperative pelvic tilt and postoperative PI–LL were attenuated (Table 5). Exploratory ROC curve analysis was performed to assess the discriminatory ability of preoperative SVA for residual sagittal imbalance within this cohort. The area under the curve was 0.869 (p = 0.002). A preoperative SVA value of approximately 4.0 cm demonstrated high sensitivity (100%) and moderate specificity (75%) for identifying patients with postoperative sagittal imbalance (Fig. 4).

Complications and revision surgery

Complications observed in this cohort included neurological-related events and R-ASD, while no implant- or wound-related issues were noted (Table 6). Four patients (4/31, 12.9%) experienced neurological-related complications, all of which were new-onset and transient. No patient developed new postoperative motor weakness. Three patients reported transient sensory/radicular symptoms in the lower extremities (L5-distribution pain provoked by leg extension in one patient; postoperative numbness in two patients), with improvement within postoperative day (POD) 2–3 and marked improvement by POD 5–6. One patient sustained an intraoperative dural tear, managed conservatively; the drain was removed on POD 5, and the patient was discharged at 2 weeks postoperatively without neurological deterioration. No patient with preoperative neurological deficits experienced postoperative worsening. The patient who presented preoperatively with lower extremity weakness and urinary incontinence regained full muscle strength before discharge and demonstrated improved bladder control at 2.5-year follow-up.

Seven patients (7 of 31, 22.6%) had residual spinopelvic imbalance at final follow-up. Preoperatively, these patients presented with low back pain, gait abnormality, and or lower-extremity sensory or radicular symptoms. During follow-up, one patient in this subgroup developed R-ASD with symptomatic L4 spondylolisthesis and underwent revision with extension of instrumentation to L4.

Overall, three patients (3 of 31, 9.7%) developed R-ASD, and all three subsequently underwent revision surgery with extension of instrumentation to L4. One of these three R-ASD cases overlapped with the residual spinopelvic imbalance subgroup and developed newly symptomatic Grade I L4 spondylolisthesis during follow-up at 2.7 years postoperatively. The other two R-ASD cases included one patient who developed newly symptomatic Grade I L4 spondylolisthesis associated with lumbar instability (Fig. 5) and progressive lower-limb symptoms at 2 years, and one patient with Grade II L4 spondylolisthesis detected at 2 years without prominent clinical symptoms. Across the revision subgroup, sagittal parameters improved after revision, including SVA, spinopelvic parameters, lumbar lordosis, and slip percentage. Consequently, the incidence of R-ASD and the overall revision rate in this cohort were both 9.7%.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first multicenter series evaluating sacral dome osteotomy with complete L5 reduction and posterior L5–S1 fusion in children with high-grade dysplastic spondylolisthesis (HGDS). In this cohort, the procedure was associated with a relatively low incidence of transient neurological complications (12.9%) and acceptable rates of radiographic adjacent segment degeneration and revision (9.7%), comparable to those reported in previous studies, supporting its feasibility in this challenging pediatric population.

These findings suggest that complete reduction can be achieved in a consistent manner using this technique and may be associated with improved spinopelvic alignment and less residual sagittal malalignment in this cohort.

Traditionally, in situ fusion has been considered a safer approach for adolescents with spondylolisthesis [18–20]. However, recent studies have shown no significant difference in the rate of neurological complications between the reduction and in situ fusion groups [21, 22]. Both in situ fusion and partial reduction may fail to adequately restore sagittal and coronal alignment in patients with HGDS, limiting their ability to correct lumbosacral kyphosis and prevent abnormal compensatory changes [4, 21]. By contrast, complete reduction has been proposed to directly decompress neural structures, restore lumbosacral alignment, and reduce abnormal tension across the lumbosacral junction, which may facilitate fusion by decreasing local shear forces associated with the deformity [23, 24].

Early attempts at complete anatomical reduction for high-grade dysplastic spondylolisthesis were described by Ruf et al. (2006), who utilized an upper-level lever through L4–S1 distraction combined with compression to achieve L5 reduction via ligamentotaxis. In their series, a neurological complication rate of 22% (6/27) was reported, consisting predominantly of L5 nerve root symptoms, with one patient experiencing persistent deficits. Adjacent segment changes at L4–5 were observed in a subset of patients, with 4 of 27 cases demonstrating radiographic decompensation, while only a limited number required subsequent surgical intervention. Temporary bilateral L4 pedicle screw placement was employed as part of the reduction strategy; however, this approach may have been associated with an increased risk of neurological complications related to upper-level lever distraction and necessitated additional procedures for implant removal in some patients [10]. Similarly, Kan et al. (2011) described sacral dome resection with single-stage posterior reduction in adolescents and young adults, using partial release and L4 lever distraction. However, fixation extended to L4–S1, sacrificing lumbar mobility, and neurological complications occurred in 27% (4/15) of patients [25]. More recently, Štulík et al. (2022) described complete reduction with single-level circumferential fusion in children with HGDS. Some patients underwent staged procedures or additional anterior interbody fusion, and only a subset received sacral dome resection or partial discectomy. Despite a low incidence of transient new neurological injury (5.6%, 1/18), the rate of adjacent segment L4–L5 olisthesis was high (44%), likely reflecting incomplete local correction and residual pelvic malalignment [24]. Guo et al. (2025) further demonstrated that posterior L5–S1 fixation carried a higher risk of adjacent segment spondylolisthesis or instability compared with L4–S1 fixation (38%, 6/16 vs. 0%, 0/23). Notably, not all patients in the L5–S1 group underwent complete reduction, which likely contributed to residual local sagittal imbalance and increased the risk of adjacent segment complications [26]. Importantly, our cohort demonstrated a low incidence of neurological complications, all of which were transient, even lower than rates reported in most previous studies. This favorable outcome may be related to the use of extensive S1 sacral dome resection combined with circumferential soft-tissue release, which allows L5 reduction under relatively low-tension conditions. Similarly, the incidence of R-ASD and revision surgery was comparatively low, likely reflecting the effective correction of local deformity achieved with complete reduction. Thus, even with single-level L5–S1 fixation, the need for subsequent procedures appeared to be limited in our cohort. In addition to the favorable safety profile and low incidence of revision, our procedure achieved substantial radiographic improvements in both coronal and sagittal planes. Coronal alignment was improved postoperatively, with the main curve Cobb angle decreasing from 13.95 ± 7.13° preoperatively to 9.26 ± 8.27° postoperatively (p = 0.009), and further reduced to 7.51 ± 6.69° at the latest follow-up (p = 0.097). Previous research has shown that the incidence of scoliosis associated with dysplastic spondylolisthesis is 58% among young patients [27]. In our cohort, the main curve showed substantial postoperative correction that persisted through follow-up, indicating results consistent with those observed in prior studies [28, 29]. Postoperatively, Slip % and slip angle were significantly reduced (p < 0.001) and maintained at follow-up, reflecting durable restoration of lumbosacral alignment. Key sagittal parameters [4, 6, 12, 30–32], including Dub-LSA, L5 incidence, PT, SS, LL, PI–LL mismatch, L4–S1 lordosis, L4–S1/LL ratio, L5–S1 lordosis, PT/SS ratio, and SVA, all showed significant improvement (p < 0.05), supporting effective correction of regional and global sagittal imbalance. Thoracic kyphosis (TK) also increased significantly (p = 0.003), further contributing to overall sagittal balance. Most parameters remained stable during follow-up. These radiographic outcomes are comparable to those reported in previous studies [10, 24, 25, 33]. An interesting finding in our study was the postoperative decrease in PI. PI is generally considered a fixed anatomical parameter, and previous studies involving sacral dome osteotomy have reported no significant change in PI after surgery [24, 25]. In our cohort, PI was slightly lower postoperatively than preoperatively (66.48 ± 10.19° vs. 63.16 ± 9.81°, p = 0.035), although the absolute difference was small. Because PI measurement on standing lateral radiographs is sensitive to consistent identification of the S1 endplate and pelvic positioning, variability in radiographic acquisition conditions and measurement landmarks may contribute to this observed difference. In addition, postoperative changes around the sacral dome (resection and remodeling) could potentially influence the radiographic landmarks used to define the S1 endplate plane. To avoid overinterpretation, we report this as an observational finding, and its clinical significance warrants confirmation in larger cohorts.

At the latest follow-up, 22.6% (7/31) of patients demonstrated residual sagittal imbalance. Among them, 2 patients had SVA > 5.0 cm, and 5 patients had an unbalanced pelvis (PT/SS > 1), with one patient requiring revision surgery due to R-ASD. The remaining patients with residual imbalance who did not undergo revision warrant careful longitudinal observation, as persistent sagittal malalignment has been associated with an increased risk of implant failure or adjacent segment degeneration in previous reports.

This finding suggests that greater preoperative SVA values are associated with a higher likelihood of residual sagittal imbalance after surgery. In our cohort, a preoperative SVA of approximately 4.0 cm demonstrated high sensitivity for identifying patients with postoperative imbalance. These observations highlight the importance of individualized preoperative counseling and structured postoperative follow-up, particularly in patients presenting with marked preoperative sagittal malalignment. Although an SVA of less than 5.0 cm is generally considered acceptable, this threshold reflects a static radiographic definition rather than a binary indicator of sagittal balance. In pediatric patients with high-grade dysplastic spondylolisthesis, an SVA approaching this upper limit may indicate limited remaining compensatory capacity. Furthermore, a larger preoperative SVA in this population likely reflects a more advanced and structurally established sagittal deformity involving both the lumbosacral junction and pelvic compensation. Consequently, even after technically adequate reduction and fusion, complete restoration of global sagittal alignment or pelvic balance may not be fully achievable, predisposing these patients to residual postoperative imbalance.

Although postoperative PI–LL was not independently associated with residual sagittal imbalance in the multivariable analysis, it demonstrated an association in univariable analysis. This observation indicates that incomplete restoration of lumbar lordosis relative to pelvic incidence may be related to postoperative sagittal alignment patterns. While reference thresholds for PI–LL mismatch have been established in adults [34] but not in pediatric patients, our findings suggest that optimizing the restoration of lumbar lordosis relative to pelvic morphology may be relevant in mitigating postoperative sagittal decompensation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design inherently carries the risk of selection bias and incomplete data capture. Second, the marked sex imbalance (30 females and 1 male) may limit the generalizability of our findings; however, this distribution is consistent with the known epidemiology of pediatric high-grade dysplastic spondylolisthesis. Third, despite the procedure having been performed at our centers for many years, HGDS remains a rare condition, and the overall sample size was modest. In addition, some early cases lacked standardized full-spine radiographs, reflecting changes in imaging protocols over time and resulting in incomplete sagittal and coronal assessments. Fourth, because many patients underwent surgery at a very young age, pre- and postoperative patient-reported outcome measures, such as SRS-22 scores, were unavailable, limiting the evaluation of health-related quality of life. Finally, although the follow-up duration was sufficient to assess radiographic outcomes, longer-term follow-up is needed to evaluate the durability of correction and the risk of late complications, including adjacent segment degeneration. Future research should focus on prospective, multicenter studies with larger cohorts, standardized imaging protocols, and comprehensive patient-reported outcomes to further evaluate this procedure in pediatric HGDS.

Conclusion

Sacral dome osteotomy combined with complete L5 reduction and posterior L5–S1 single-level fusion achieved reliable restoration of lumbosacral alignment in children with high-grade dysplastic spondylolisthesis. Durable correction of sagittal and coronal deformities was observed, with rates of transient neurological complications and adjacent segment degeneration comparable to those reported in previous studies.

Residual postoperative sagittal imbalance occurred in a subset of patients and was associated with greater preoperative sagittal malalignment. These findings underscore the importance of careful preoperative assessment and structured postoperative surveillance, particularly in patients presenting with marked sagittal imbalance. Overall, this surgical technique represents a feasible treatment option for pediatric HGDS, allowing effective deformity correction while preserving lumbar motion.
