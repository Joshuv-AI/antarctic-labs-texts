Probing the Roles of Temperature and Cooperative Effects in Chirality-Induced Spin Selectivity: Photoelectron Spin Polarization in Helical Tetrapyrroles
Authors: Paul V. Möllers, Adrian J. Urban, Steven De Feyter, Hiroshi M. Yamamoto, Helmut Zacharias
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We investigate the roles of molecular vibrations and intermolecular interactions in the mechanism underlying chirality-induced spin selectivity (CISS) in monolayers of helical tetrapyrrole (TPBT) molecules. The spin polarization of photoelectrons emitted from TPBT-functionalized Cu(111) surfaces was measured as a function of the temperature and the surface coverage. We employed DFT calculations to determine the energy and temperature-dependent population of vibrational modes which vary either the molecular pitch and/or the molecular radius. In combination, the data demonstrate that molecular vibrations do not play a significant role for CISS in the TPBT layers. Submonolayer coverages were created by gradual thermal desorption of the molecules from the surface during the spin polarization measurements. While the spin polarization scales nonlinearly with the surface coverage, this behavior can be rationalized entirely through changes of the photoelectron yield upon surface functionalization, and therefore represents no evidence for cooperative effects involved in CISS.

In the past decade, electron spin filtering in monolayers of chiral organic molecules adsorbed onto various substrates has evolved into an established effect, termed chirality-induced spin selectivity (CISS), and into a field of intense research. Phenomena related to CISS have been observed in a variety of experiments, ranging from photoemission,1−3 conductive AFM4 and voltammetry5 to the inverse spin Hall effect.6 It has been established that the direction of the longitudinal spin orientation is coupled to the spatial structure of the enantiomers, which corroborated that the spin selectivity arises from the overall helical geometry of the molecule, that is, its global properties.7,8 CISS has successfully been exploited to enhance the selectivity and efficiency of chemical reactions,9 for enantiomer separation,10 and to fabricate new spintronic devices.11,12 Nevertheless, a consensus, even on a qualitative understanding of the mechanisms underlying CISS, remains elusive.

Despite the lack of any consensus, different theoretical approaches to model the effect have been proposed. These include continuum13−17 and tight-binding18−24 model calculations based on simplified helical geometries resembling the molecules, combined with calculations of the spin-selective transport properties. Such calculations reproduce, e.g., the hallmark correlation between molecular chirality and the sign of the spin polarization but require unrealistically strong spin–orbit coupling in the light atomic constituents such as carbon to reproduce the experimentally observed magnitude of the effects. More recently, ab initio calculations7,25,26 became more common which, however, similarly fail to match the order of magnitude of the spin polarization. Currently, it is argued that multielectron and temperature effects need to be included into the descriptions to obtain a more realistic picture, and to account for the robustness of the CISS effect.27−32 However, opposing claims can as well be found.33

On the experimental side, controversial results are reported in literature. The vast majority of experiments is carried out at room temperature, and it is another hallmark of the CISS effect that large spin polarization values are measured under these conditions. In experiments with various molecular entities, any dependence of the spin polarization on the temperature has been reported in the past, from a decrease with increasing temperature,34−37 independence of temperature,4,38 to experiments which favor an increase of the absolute spin polarization value with temperature.39−42 The different accounts are not necessarily inconsistent. Díaz et al. pointed out24 that the prevailing charge transport regime, i.e., coherent or hopping transport, depends on both the temperature and the molecular length. For example, in short π-conjugated molecules, coherent transport likely prevails, and varying temperatures hardly affect the charge transfer. For longer molecules, temperature-activated hopping transport quickly becomes predominant. Disorder, as introduced by molecular vibrations, leads to a localization of the molecular eigenstates. This affects the charge transport differently depending on whether it is mostly coherent or already incoherent. Simultaneously, the transport regime itself is affected by whether the molecular length is below or above the length scale at which the eigenstates are localized. Depending on the specifics of the experimental setup, the temperature dependence of the measured spin selectivity does not necessarily reflect the behavior of the CISS effect inside the chiral molecules. Kumar et al.43 assigned a decrease of the spin selectivity with temperature to Elliot–Yafet scattering by phonons inside the electrode rather than inside the molecule, an often-unaddressed problem of conduction measurements with multiple interfaces.44

These considerations emphasize that the individual features of CISS, such as the dependence on the temperature, on the molecular length and on the electronic structure (e.g., the degree of conjugation) are interdependent properties which cannot be discussed as separate issues. Nevertheless, more experimental data on, e.g., the temperature dependence of the spin polarization, can allow to test some models if the respective other properties, such as the nature of the electronic structure, are known.

Most measurements, including magnetic conducting probe AFM (mcp-AFM) experiments, investigated ordered monolayers of organic molecules whose helical axes were oriented roughly along the normal of the substrate surface. Above all, the methods mentioned average over a large number of molecules. The question arises whether the observed spin selectivity reflects the properties of the individual molecular entities or whether cooperative effects among the molecules contribute to enhance the observed spin polarization or magnetization dependence of the conductivity. Intermolecular interactions could generally affect CISS either directly or indirectly by modifying the ordering within the molecular layer.

In early experiments by Naaman and co-workers, it was found that even a small admixture of the opposite enantiomer led to a significant decrease, if not complete vanishing, of the spin polarization,1 supporting the notion that cooperative effects may be important. STM studies of polyalanin molecules on magnetic substrates by Tegenkamp and co-workers45 suggest that the spin selectivity is enhanced if the molecules are covalently bonded to the surface and situated within a well-ordered film. Compared to heptahelicene molecules, which have a height of 0.22 nm when adsorbed onto a metal surface,46 these molecules have a much greater length of about 5 nm.47 More recently, it was reported that the rates of adsorption of chiral molecules onto magnetic substrates can depend on the combination of the substrate magnetization direction and the molecular enantiomer.10 Consistent with the previous claims of cooperative effects, Cuniberti and co-workers showed in a first-principles study48 that such behavior can arise from intermolecular exchange interactions which are present only in dense arrays of helical molecules. Recent break junction experiments, in contrast, demonstrated that the CISS effect occurs also in individual molecules, specifically, spin crossover complexes49 and α-helical polypeptides.50 Consistently, STM measurements by Bürgler and co-workers demonstrated that the CISS effect occurs in single heptahelicene molecules adsorbed onto spontaneously magnetized Co islands on Cu(111), and at very low temperatures.51,52

In this work, we report results concerning the roles of both the temperature and intermolecular interactions. We have chosen a new molecular system, a transition metal complex based on a tetrapyrrole (19-benzoyl-5,10,15-triphenyl-bilatrien-1-one, H2TPBT), which has previously been proposed as a model system for systematic studies of CISS.53 Its molecular structure, shown in Figure 1(a), is simple enough that first-principle calculations can be carried out for this system. The molecule essentially represents a “cut-open” metal porphyrin which makes one-and-a-quarter helical turns, and, in this sense, is similar to heptahelicene.

We first demonstrate that monolayers of these molecules generate a considerable spin polarization, as expected based on the current understanding of CISS. Subsequently, we measure the spin polarization as a function of the sample temperature. We employ DFT calculations to determine the energy and temperature-dependent population of those vibrational modes that vary either the molecular pitch and/or the molecular radius. In combination, the data demonstrate that molecular vibrations do not play a significant role in the mechanism underlying CISS in these molecules. Lastly, we thermally desorb the molecules from the surface during the spin polarization measurements to create decreasing submonolayer coverages, and investigate the role of intermolecular interactions. While the spin polarization is found to scale nonlinearly with the surface coverage, we demonstrate that this behavior can be explained entirely through the changes in the photoelectron yield introduced by the surface functionalization, and that it hence provides no evidence for cooperative effects involved in CISS in this system.

A detailed description of the synthesis of Ni(TPBT) was published earlier.53,54 The central metal ion inside the complexes may be changed to implement different functionalities and catalytic activities. Here, we chose to work with complexes that feature a central Ni ion; these molecules show a superior stability against racemization as opposed to, e.g., the respective copper and zinc complexes or the free-base molecules.53,55,56 Despite containing nickel, the complex was shown to be ESR-silent so that any observed spin polarization would be caused solely by the molecule’s chirality.57 Different functionalizations may be introduced to interconnect with neighboring molecules. In the present case, we used four phenyl units attached to the methine carbons bridging the four pyrrole rings, based on the ease of synthesis of the tetraphenyl-porphyrin precursor.58,59 Because the crystal structure shows no π–π stacking, we can be confident that the molecules do not interact with each other when adsorbed onto the substrate surface.53 Optical CD measurements of both enantiomers in solution are shown in Figure 1(b). Both enantiomers show CD spectra that are in good approximation mirror images of each other, indicating that both enantiomers are indeed chiral and well separated. DFT calculations, detailed in sections S5 to S7 of the SI, indicate that the molecules are adsorbed onto the substrate with the lactam ring oriented closely to the surface. For the present study, the molecules were adsorbed onto a sputter-cleaned and well annealed single-crystalline Cu(111) surface via sublimation at 443 K under ultrahigh vacuum conditions (p ≤ 5 · 10–10 mbar).

During the deposition process, the substrate surface was held at an elevated temperature of T = 508 K (235 °C) to prevent the deposition of multilayers. The adsorption process was monitored through vacuum ultraviolet (VUV) photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) using a photon energy of hν = 10.5 eV by measuring the sample work function and the intensity of the copper surface state. The surface was exposed to the molecules in consecutive steps. Adsorption of a monolayer of the molecules decreases the work function Φ of the surface by a specific value, ΔΦML, (see section S1 in the SI) and the intensity of the surface state, which is a feature of the bare Cu(111) crystal surface. Since the Cu(111) substrate surface has a low number of step edges and other defects, the work function scales approximately linearly with the surface coverage.60−64 The change ΔΦ/ΔΦML in the work function, normalized to the work function change ΔΦML ≈ – 120 meV measured on a full monolayer, is therefore employed as a measure of the surface coverage in the following.

For addressing the questions regarding a possible influence of cooperative interactions and regarding the influence of temperature effects, it is beneficial when the full monolayer shows a high spin polarization. We first established that this is the case for the Ni(TPBT) complexes investigated here. After deposition of the full monolayer, photoelectrons were excited from the sample by irradiation with linearly polarized deep-UV laser pulses at a wavelength of λ = 213 nm (hν = 5.83 eV) and at a repetition rate of 20 kHz. The pulse duration and energy were 200 to 300 ps and 10 to 100 pJ, respectively. The photon energy was chosen such that it exceeded the sample work function while lying below the ionization energy of the molecules. The photoelectrons were hence emitted only from the metal substrate. The average spin polarization of the photoelectrons was measured using a Mott polarimeter, in a procedure detailed previously2,3,7−9 and in section S3 of the SI. The experimental geometry is depicted in Figure 2(a). The light impinged onto the sample surface along the surface normal, and the photoelectrons were as well collected along this direction. The geometry of the experiment is designed such that the longitudinal spin polarization is measured; the spin polarization quantization axis coincides with the surface normal and is antiparallel to the wave vector k of the light. A positive spin polarization value indicates that the average spin orientation is aligned parallel to the electron momentum directly after emission. Throughout the measurement procedure, the spin polarization is averaged over time intervals of 13 s, corresponding to roughly 2.6 · 105 laser pulses and 1.7 · 104 photoelectrons. The data are presented as histograms of the spin polarization values determined for these intervals. The histograms typically comprise about 100 of these 13 s long measurement intervals, i.e., 106 photoelectrons. An average spin polarization value and its confidence interval are extracted as the expectation value and the fwhm of a Gaussian distribution fitted to the histogram. Data measured on a monolayer of (P)-Ni(TPBT) are shown in Figure 2(b). At room temperature, the sample emits photoelectrons with a spin polarization of P = (33.0 ± 3.5)% after functionalization with a monolayer of the (P) enantiomer. The (M) enantiomer generates a negative spin polarization, whereas the bare substrate emits unpolarized photoelectrons, see section S4 in the SI. The high spin polarization value allows to measure statistically significant changes in the spin polarization, introduced by, i.e., variation of the sample temperature.

Recently, Plenio and co-workers argued that either a longitudinal stretching of the helix or a vibrational increase of its diameter could lead to an enhanced spin polarization.31 However, since the authors considered only a model system, it is not known whether such vibrations are excited at realistic temperatures in the present Ni(TBPT) molecules. Other theoretical approaches do not specify the vibrational mode, and generally conclude that with increasing temperature above about 50 K the spin polarization generated in helical molecules is expected to increase.24,41,42 Here, we directly measure the spin polarization generated by CISS as a function of the temperature and identify the relevant vibrational modes. Starting with a full surface coverage of Ni(TBPT) molecules, the sample was heated very slowly, and the spin polarization was measured simultaneously. These data are shown in Figure 3. Up to about T = 623 K (350 °C) the spin polarization remained constant at P ≈ +30% within the margin of error. Clearly, no temperature dependence of the electron spin polarization is observed.

To assess whether these data allow for conclusions regarding the influence of molecular vibrations on the CISS effect, it is necessary to evaluate how the strength of the coupling of the low-energy electrons to the molecular vibrations varies with temperature. In the framework of the Eliashberg theory,65 the total strength of the electron–phonon interaction is quantified by a dimensionless parameter λ ∼ ∫0∞α2(ν)F(ν)ν–1 dν. Here, F(ν) is the vibrational spectrum and the weighting α2(ν) expresses the coupling strength to different vibrational modes as a function of the vibrational frequency ν. The frequency dependence of α2(ν) is typically weak66 and hence neglected in the following. The product α2(ν)F(ν) can be calculated by the Eliashberg function.65,66 The averaged coupling strength λ is a temperature-independent parameter in this description; the strength of the coupling to vibrations hence changes with temperature only insofar as the spectrum of occupied vibrational modes changes.67,68 To obtain an estimate of the strength of the electron coupling to the molecular vibrations, it is therefore sufficient to determine how the population of the vibrational modes of Ni(TPBT) changes upon heating from 300 to 623 K.

We employed density function theory (DFT) to determine the energy, Ei, of each vibrational mode i, and to identify those vibrations which change either the radius of the molecular helix and/or its pitch. We tested several combinations of density functionals (B3LYP,69 CAM-B3LYP,70 M06-L,71 PBE072 and ωB97X73 and basis sets (6–31G*,74 def2-SVP,75,76 and found ωB97X/def2-SVP to be the most suitable combination, based on the agreement of the predicted IR spectrum with an experimental one, see section S5 in the SI. The measured FTIR spectrum, recorded from a CsI pellet between ν̃ = 500 cm–1 and 2,000 cm–1, is shown in Figure S7 of the SI.

Section S6 of the SI includes a table of the internal energies Ei = hcν̃i of all individual vibrational modes i below a vibrational frequency of ν̃i = 500 cm–1. These modes were visualized using the Avogadro software77 to understand the nature of the vibration, i.e., its impact on the helical geometry of the molecule. Animated renderings are available online as part of the SI. We identified 20 vibrations which change the diameter of the molecular Ni(TBPT) helix (breathing vibrations) and 19 vibrations which change the pitch. These vibrations are correspondingly marked in the table in section S6. The breathing modes are in the low-frequency range from about ν̃ = 27 cm–1 to about 460 cm–1; the pitch-changing vibrations have frequencies between about ν̃ = 46 cm–1 and about 287 cm–1. We then determined the relative populations in all vibrations of either type with frequencies ν̃i from zero up to 1,500 cm–1. To that end, Bose–Einstein distributions1were calculated individually for each of the 7440 modes obtained from DFT in this energy interval. The ensemble-averaged absolute population of each of the individual modes increases monotonically with the temperature T, see the plots of Ni(T) in section S8 in the SI. However, the total number of different modes that can be excited increases with the temperature, as well, decreasing the probablility of each specific mode i to be excited. The temperature-dependent occupation probability of an individual mode, pvibi(T) = Ni(T)/Ntotal(T), is therefore obtained only after normalization of the respective Bose–Einstein distribution, Ni(T), to the partition function2which corresponds to the sum of the Bose–Einstein distributions of all vibrational modes. We discuss these normalized occupation probablities rather than the absolute number of excited modes because we consider only a fraction of the available modes, i.e., those modes which affect the helical geometry. For the breathing and pitch-changing modes, the calculated frequencies, ν̃i, and the normalized populations, pvibi(T), are each given in Figure 4(a) and Figure 4(b), respectively.

Besides the fundamental vibrations, we took the overtones and binary combination vibrations into account, both of which contribute predominantly at higher internal energies. While the modes affecting the overall helical molecular geometry were found in the low-energy regime of the vibrational spectrum, higher-energy modes correspond mostly to vibrations of partial groups of the molecule. Furthermore, it is evident from the insets in Figure 4(a) and (b) that higher-energetic modes above ν̃ = 500 cm–1 will contribute only weakly to the overall spectrum. Circles indicate the maxima of the temperature-dependent normalized population of the investigated modes. The relative populations of the three lowest-energy breathing and pitch vibrations peak at 29 K, 35 K, and 62 K and at 38 and 43 K, respectively. The pitch vibrations with second- and third-lowest energies have very similar energies and peak temperatures, see Figure 4(b) and table S6 in the SI. The data reveal that all dominant modes have the highest occupation probability below room temperature. Therefore, the normalized population pvibi(T) of all modes decreases monotonically above 300 K, with a slight exception for the highest-energy breathing modes. The modes whose populations peak above 300 K contribute only very weakly to the overall spectrum, with normalized populations below approximately 6 · 10–4.

The maxima of both distributions are at very low temperatures of about 33 and 51 K, respectively. Beyond the maximum, the overall population of these structure-changing vibrations decreases monotonically, in particular in the temperature range from 300 to 623 K which we assessed experimentally in this work. This is a general behavior for low-frequency vibrations, like breathing and pitch-changing modes, in any larger molecular system.

The experimental data and the estimation of the population in structure-changing vibrations thus suggest that the spin coupling to molecular vibrations is of less importance in the present molecular system. Indeed, the Ni(TPBT) molecules have a completely conjugated π system and a length of only 2.5 nm, which suggests that coherent electron transport prevails over a phonon-mediated hopping transport. These features make it reasonable to assume that molecular vibrations have no major impact on the charge transport and its spin selectivity in this system.

If the spin polarization had increased with temperature, this would have indicated that the spin preferentially couples to vibrations with high internal energy. If it couples only to low-lying vibrations, a decrease of the spin polarization with temperature should be expected when increasing the temperature further over 300 K. For larger molecules in which hopping transport prevails, a more pronounced temperature dependence may be expected.

As mentioned above, the simple evaluation presented here does not differentiate between different coupling strengths of the electron spin to different vibrations. It is thus possible that a more refined theoretical analysis yields modified coupling strengths of certain vibrational motions. However, the general conclusion will probably be modified only slightly. In the present study, heating the sample to T = 623 K induced changes on the order of a factor of 2 to 3 in both the absolute and the normalized population of the relevant vibrational modes. Stronger changes, especially in the absolute populations, Ni(T), could be achieved by additional cooling of the sample. Compared to T = 623 K, the populations of the normalized (absolute) population are increased (decreased) by a factor of about 5 (one to two orders of magnitude) at T = 100 K.

Above about 623 K, the spin polarization begins to decrease, see Figure 3(a). Changes in the work function, shown in green in Figure 3(a), indicate that the desorption of Ni(TBPT) molecules from Cu(111) sets in. For a more rapid desorption, we increased the temperature further up to 740 K which was kept constant. Once this temperature is reached, the decrease in the spin polarization is described well by an exponential function, shown in red in Figure 3. An exponential decrease of the surface coverage with time is expected, assuming that a constant fraction of the remaining coverage is desorbed in each time interval.

Similar as during the molecule adsorption process, changes ΔΦ in the work function were measured via UPS throughout the heating (desorption) procedure, and the relative work function difference, ΔΦ/ΔΦML, is employed as a measure of the surface coverage. The raw spectra can be found in Figure S1 of the SI. Approximately one hour after the desorption sets in, ΔΦ reaches zero, indicating that the work function is completely reverted to the value measured on the bare Cu(111) surface. This observation leads to the conclusion that the spin polarization indeed vanishes in the moment the Ni(TBPT) adsorbate molecules are no longer available on the sample surface. Consistent with this interpretation, the surface state is as prominent as on a freshly sputter-cleaned surface after heating the functionalized surface to 740 K. In a separate control experiment, we verified that Ni(TBPT) molecules appear in the gas phase at this temperature by detecting molecular fragments from the loss of a benzoyl group with a quadrupole mass spectrometer (QMS). The maximum partial pressure is reached when the work function decreases fastest. The QMS data can be found in section S2 of the SI. In conclusion, the reversal of the adsorption-induced changes of the work function and of the surface state intensity, along with the desorption flux measured by the mass spectrometer, corroborate the interpretation that the decrease of the spin polarization is a consequence of the decreasing surface coverage.

While the data shown in Figure 3 clearly suggest that the spin polarization is directly related to the surface coverage, assessing the role of cooperative effects in the mechanism generating the spin polarization requires a more quantitative evaluation of the data.

For a fully covered surface, all photoelectrons are transmitted through the adsorbed molecular layer. However, for a partially covered surface, schematically sketched in Figure 7, the overall photoelectron yield includes both electrons transmitted through the layer and those emitted from pristine areas of the substrate. Therefore, at intermediate surface coverages, the measured photoelectron spin polarization is an incoherent superposition of the spin polarization from the bare substrate areas and the spin polarization from the functionalized substrate areas. In the present case, the substrate emits unpolarized photoelectrons and the spin polarization from the functionalized surface regions is that generated by the CISS effect.

Schematic representation of the photoemission measurement on a partially covered substrate surface. The incident laser light is indicated in blue.

In absence of cooperative effects in the CISS mechanism, the spin polarization measured from the surface, averaged over a macroscopically large area, increases linearly with the relative photoelectron contributions from the functionalized surface regions. If, in contrast, the spin polarization is generated through a mechanism that involves cooperative effects, its magnitude should decrease disproportionately once the surface coverage decreases below a full monolayer. However, the scaling of the spin polarization with varying surface coverage does not directly reveal the cooperative or single-molecule nature of CISS. The relative photoelectron contributions not necessarily scale linearly with the coverage because the photoelectron yield changes upon functionalization of the surface. Consequently, the scaling of the spin polarization with the coverage will generally be nonlinear even in absence of intermolecular interactions. When the photon energy is below the molecular ionization energy, a variation in the photoelectron yield upon functionalization can be attributed to changes in the work function of the sample. With Nfunc and Nsub designating the number of electrons emitted from the functionalized and the bare surface regions, the measured spin polarization, Pmeas, can be written as the average3of the spin polarization values Pfunc and Psub measured on a full monolayer and the bare substrate surface, respectively. Nfunc and Nsub can be expressed as Nfunc = Yfunc · cfunc and Nsub = Ysub · csub, where Y denotes the photoelectron yield and c the relative surface coverage when only a single adsorbate species is prevalent. To calculate the behavior of the measured spin polarization with the coverage expected for the present system, the relative photoelectron yield from the surface in both its bare (sputter-cleaned) and functionalized (full monolayer) state was measured at a fixed laser intensity and on multiple freshly prepared monolayers. The photoelectron yield varied considerably, probably because a small surface area of about 0.13 mm2 was probed, but the functionalized surface consistently emitted significantly more photoelectrons. Yield ratios in the range of Yfunc/Ysub ≈ 2.0 to 9.4 (i.e., Yfunc ≈ 66% to 90% of the total yield) were measured. The spin polarization as a function of the surface coverage should therefore lie inside the light green-shaded area in Figure 8(b). The expectation based on the standard deviation (±1σ/√n) interval of the individual photoelectron intensities, Yfunc/Ysub ≈ 4.2 to 7.2, is shaded in Figure 8(b) in a darker green color. Shown in blue are the measured data points; the line corresponds to the slightly smoothed curve shown in Figure 3. As can be seen in Figure 8(b), the data indeed lie in the expected regime within the experimental uncertainty. The nonlinear scaling of the spin polarization with the estimated surface coverage can hence be explained solely through the changes in the photoelectron yield introduced by the functionalization of the surface with the TPBT molecules. The data indicate that cooperative effects are not important for the generation of spin polarization in the Ni(TBPT)/Cu(111) system studied here. This observation supports the previous conclusions drawn by Bürgler and co-workers51,52 who reported CISS in individual heptahelicene molecules. Similar to the Ni(TPBT) complex investigated in the present study, heptahelicene is a small molecule which possesses a π-conjugated electronic valence system.

Lastly, we point out that the arguments above may hold true for previously published studies in which possible variations in the electron yield or in the molecular conductivity of different constituents in a monolayer were neglected. A large body of data on CISS is provided by AFM-based measurements of the magnetization-dependent conductivity of chiral molecules. These mcp-AFM measurements address a considerably smaller surface area than, e.g., the photoemission experiments. But in contrast to true single-molecule measurements such as performed by Bürgler and co-workers,51,52 mcp-AFM usually probes molecules that are situated within a dense monolayer and, consequently, measures the current through an ensemble of molecules.

Some studies investigated CISS in supramolecular structures comprising chiral molecular building blocks that were diluted with achiral molecules, and observed a steep, nonlinear increase of the spin selectivity with the relative content of chiral molecules. This behavior was explained by means of the “sergeants-and-soldiers” model in which the chirality of the chiral “sergeant” molecules induces the chirality of the molecular “soldiers”. Groups of individual molecules forming an ordered but noncovalent arrangement, similar to molecules within a dense self-assembled monolayer, were probed in these experiments. The two types of molecules will, in general, differ in their electric resistance. Hence, a correction for the different contributions to the total electron current needs to be performed, similar to the considerations presented above. The specific scaling of the spin polarization with the relative number of chiral and achiral molecules could potentially be rationalized through the differences in the molecular conductivities if the chiral molecules exhibited a larger conductivity than the achiral molecules. Indeed, it was pointed out earlier78,79 that CISS itself enhances the efficiency of the charge transport through chiral molecules as compared to their achiral counterparts by suppressing backscattering of the charge carriers.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that Cu(111) surfaces functionalized with monolayers of helical Ni(TPBT) complexes emit significantly spin-polarized electrons. Consistent with previous studies, the spin polarization, which reached values of up to P = (33.0 ± 3.5)%, is assigned to the chirality-induced spin selectivity effect. This interpretation is supported by the correlation between the sign of the spin polarization and the helical sense of the molecules. Right-handed molecules were found to generate positively spin-polarized electrons, as is the case with heptahelicene, α-helical bacteriorhodopsin and dsPNA duplexes.

The temperature dependence of the CISS effect was probed by slowly heating the sample while continuously measuring the photoelectron spin polarization. Within the error margins, the spin polarization was found to remain constant up to about 623 K. DFT calculations were performed to determine the energies of the molecular vibrations and to identify those vibrational modes which alter the pitch and/or the radius of the molecules. It was shown that the population of the modes which alter the helical parameters of the molecules monotonously decreases upon heating in the probed temperature range, down to approximately 58% of the room temperature values at 623 K. Since this did not affect the measured spin polarization, a CISS mechanism that involves strong electron coupling to those modes appears unlikely in the Ni(TPBT) molecules. The molecules represent fully conjugated π systems in which tunnelling charge transport presumably prevails; the conclusion may thus not hold for larger molecular systems such as dsDNA. A potentially rewarding future endeavor would be to compare the results presented here with data from larger molecules in which a phonon-mediated hopping transport prevails.

The role of intermolecular interactions in the generation of the spin polarization was investigated by gradually desorbing the molecules during the measurement. While the spin polarization was found to scale nonlinearly with the surface coverage, this behavior could be explained completely through the differences in the photoelectron yield from the bare and functionalized surface regions. Therefore, no evidence for cooperative effects in CISS was found. We point out that these previously neglected variations in the photoelectron yield might allow for a reinterpretation of previously published data.
