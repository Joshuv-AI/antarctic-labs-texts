Reprogramming macrophage immunometabolism via glutamine antagonism potentiates colorectal cancer therapy in mice
Authors: Renming Fan, Xintong Lu, Bingjie Zhang, Zhuang Hou, Ting Zhao, Yongrui Hai, Ye Chen, Wenhui Wang, Yue Chen, Qiu Sun, Lei Liang, Le Yang, Gaofei Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer (CRC) often exhibits a suppressive tumor microenvironment that is associated with elevated glutamine metabolism and induction of immunosuppressive macrophages. Glutamine antagonists such as 6-diazo-5-oxo-L-norleucine (DON) and its prodrug JHU-083 can limit tumor growth, but their toxicity and immune cell selectivity remain suboptimal. Here, we conjugate DON with the macrophage-targeting moiety artesunate to develop WGF-T17 (T17), which blocks glutamine metabolism in macrophages. In vitro, T17 rewires macrophage metabolism toward glycolysis, inducing lactate buildup and histone lactylation, enhancing mitochondrial fission and phagocytic activity, and thereby reprogramming macrophages to an inflammatory state. In vivo, T17 controls tumor growth better than JHU-083 via macrophage-dependent pathways, and also increases the efficacy of immunotherapy, chemotherapy and anti-angiogenic therapy in female mice carrying subcutaneous CRC tumors. Our findings thus hint T17 as a promising treatment strategy for CRC by targeting macrophage glutamine metabolism to reverse immune suppression.Subject terms: Drug development, Cancer immunotherapy, Monocytes and macrophages, Colorectal cancer

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains one of the most prevalent and lethal malignancies worldwide1,2. Despite the availability of chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, only a subset of patients derives meaningful and durable clinical benefits3. Increasing evidence implicates tumor-intrinsic metabolic reprogramming and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) as major contributors to treatment resistance4. Among various metabolic adaptations, glutamine metabolism plays a central role in supporting both tumor progression and immune evasion5–7. Glutamine is a vital nutrient for nucleotide and amino acid biosynthesis, TCA cycle cataplerosis, and redox homeostasis7–9. Beyond its role in cancer cells, glutamine availability within the TME profoundly influences immune cell function by modulating dendritic cell antigen presentation, sustaining T cell activity, and promoting the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAM) toward an immunosuppressive state10–12.

6-diazo-5-oxo-L-norleucine (DON), a broad-spectrum glutamine antagonist, has shown promising antitumor efficacy by disrupting glutamine-dependent enzymatic pathways and relieving metabolic competition between tumor and immune cells13,14. However, its clinical translation has been hindered by gastrointestinal toxicity15,16. Its prodrug JHU-083 has partially addressed these concerns by improving drug stability and tumor accumulation17,18. Nevertheless, challenges remain in achieving immune-selective activation and avoiding off-target toxicity. An unmet need persists for next-generation glutamine-targeting agents that combine tumor specificity with immune system reprogramming to potentiate therapeutic responses.

Among the diverse immune components within the TME, TAM represent a metabolically adaptable and immunologically dominant population, highly responsive to glutamine availability and central to orchestrating tumor immunity12,19. In CRC, TAM are often skewed toward an anti-inflammatory state, contributing to immune suppression, angiogenesis, and resistance to therapy20,21. Recent evidence suggests that metabolic reprogramming of TAM can unlock potent antitumor immunity, positioning macrophages as both sensors and effectors of immunometabolic interventions22,23. Artesunate (ART), a sesquiterpene lactone with reported macrophage-targeting properties as TREM2 ligand, provides a potential scaffold for improving immune-cell-directed delivery of glutamine antagonists24,25.

In this study, we investigate whether macrophage-directed glutamine antagonism can overcome the immunosuppressive microenvironment of CRC. We develop WGF-T17 (T17), a DON-ART Immune-Metabolic Drug Conjugate (IMDC), and show that it preferentially acts on TAM, suppresses CRC growth more effectively than JHU-083, and limits gastrointestinal toxicity. Mechanistically, T17 shifts macrophage metabolism toward glycolysis and links lactate accumulation to H4K12 lactylation, DRP-1-associated mitochondrial fission, and enhanced phagocytosis. These findings identify macrophage glutamine metabolism as a tractable node for reshaping antitumor immunity and support T17 as a broadly applicable immunometabolic sensitizer for combination CRC therapy.

Results

Targeting glutamine metabolism regulates macrophage polarization in colorectal cancer

Glutamine is a critical nutrient that sustains tumor cell proliferation by fueling biosynthetic and bioenergetic pathways26. As the rate-limiting enzyme catalyzing the first step in glutamine catabolism, GLS1 plays a central role in this metabolic process27. To assess the clinical relevance of GLS1 in CRC, we analyzed its mRNA expression using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset. GLS1 expression was significantly elevated in CRC tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal mucosa (Fig. 1a), consistent with enhanced glutamine utilization in malignant tissue. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis further demonstrated that patients with high GLS expression exhibited significantly reduced overall survival relative to those with low GLS expression (Fig. 1b). Together, these findings link elevated GLS expression to unfavorable clinical outcomes in CRC and underscore the potential clinical significance of dysregulated glutamine metabolism in CRC.

To assess glutamine metabolism in CRC tissues, we obtained 50 samples of tumor and matched normal tissues from CRC patients (Fig. 1c and Supplementary table 1). Tumor tissues contained much higher levels of glutamine than normal tissues (Fig. 1d). Moreover, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated elevated GLS1 protein expression in tumor samples, as evidenced by both qualitative imaging and quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity (Fig. 1e–g). These results suggested that glutamine metabolism in CRC is robust, and targeting glutamine metabolism in CRC is expected to inhibit the proliferation of tumor cells.

Beyond sustaining tumor proliferation, glutamine metabolism also plays a critical role in modulating immune cell infiltration within the TME. Previous studies have demonstrated that modulation of glutamine metabolism can influence the activity of multiple immune populations, including T cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages, thereby shaping antitumor immune responses10,11,28. To explore the macrophages relevance of GLS1 in CRC, we performed immune correlation analyses using TCGA datasets. GLS1 expression displayed a strong positive association with anti-inflammatory macrophage-associated markers, including CD163 and ARG1, while showing an inverse correlation with pro-inflammatory markers, including CD86 and NOS2 (Fig. 1h).

Subsequently, patient-derived tumor samples were stratified according to GLS1 immunofluorescence intensity, with the top 15 samples ranked by mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) defined as the GLS1high group and the bottom 15 samples defined as the GLS1low group. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that the GLS1high group exhibited increased expression of green fluorescence labeled CD163, accompanied by a reduced number of Cy3 labeled CD86+ cells, diminished infiltration of pro-inflammatory macrophages (Fig. 1i, j). In contrast, tumors in the GLS1low group displayed a higher abundance of CD86+ pro-inflammatory macrophages, consistent with the trends observed in the TCGA-based correlation analyses. Collectively, these data indicate that GLS1 expression is closely associated with macrophage polarization within the TME, and further suggest that targeting glutamine metabolism may represent an effective strategy to modulate macrophage phenotypes and suppress tumor progression.

Synthetic characterization and pharmacological screening of DON-based IMDC

Given the association between GLS1 overexpression and immunosuppressive macrophage polarization in CRC, we aimed to determine whether pharmacologic glutamine blockade could reprogram TAM. Although DON, a classical glutamine antagonist, shows antitumor activity by irreversibly inhibiting glutamine-dependent enzymes, its clinical application is hindered by severe gastrointestinal toxicity. The prodrug JHU-083 improves the pharmacokinetic profile of DON. However, systemic toxicity, particularly intestinal side effects, remains a major concern.

To overcome these limitations and enhance macrophage-targeted delivery, we rationally designed a series of DON-based IMDC by covalently conjugating DON to ART, a sesquiterpene lactone with well-established tropism for macrophages. This dual-targeting strategy aimed to (i) achieve selective activation within tumors and macrophage-enriched compartments, and (ii) improve the chemical stability compared to earlier-generation analogs such as JHU-083. Moreover, to fine-tune the steric and hydrolytic properties of the drug, we systematically modified the alkyl group within the carbamate moiety of JHU-083, replacing the ethyl substituent with methyl, ethyl, isopropyl, or tert-butyl groups. This dual-point modification yielded four structurally distinct DON-ART conjugates, WGF-T17, WGF-T27, WGF-T37, and WGF-T47 (Supplementary Fig. 1a, and Supplementary Figs. 14–17). These compounds provided a platform to investigate how drug design influences chemical stability, enzymatic activation, and immunoregulatory capacity within the TME. Among them, WGF-T17 (T17) showed the best antitumor efficacy in the MC38 model (Fig. 2a), with an 80% tumor inhibition rate and minimal weight loss (Supplementary Fig. 1b–e). Immunofluorescence confirmed that T17 specifically inhibited GLS1 in tumors (Supplementary Fig. 1f), supporting effective inhibition of glutamine metabolism at the tumor site. To further assess the cellular targeting properties conferred by the ART moiety, we employed a fluorescently labeled artesunate analog (ART-7ACC1, Supplementary Fig. 18) to compare compound uptake between tumor cells and macrophages in a co-culture system. ART-7ACC1 exhibited limited intracellular accumulation in MC38 tumor cells, whereas robust intracellular fluorescence was observed in bone marrow–derived macrophages, indicating preferential uptake by macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 1g). Dose-finding studies in MC38-bearing mice showed that 1 mg/kg achieved strong tumor control with minimal toxicity, whereas higher doses increased mortality (Supplementary Fig. 1h–j).

In vitro stability assays demonstrated that T17 remained stable in PBS but underwent esterase-mediated hydrolysis to release DON and ART (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). To further characterize the behavior of T17 in vivo, we performed pharmacokinetic and tissue-distribution analyses. Levels of released DON were quantified in plasma, tumor, and gastrointestinal (GI) tissues at 10, 20, and 30 min, and 1, 3, and 5 h following administration. JHU-083 showed rapid degradation in plasma and substantial accumulation in GI tissues at early time. In contrast, T17 displayed greater stability in the gastrointestinal tract and achieved higher and more sustained exposure within tumor tissues, indicating a more favorable biodistribution profile than JHU-083 (Supplementary Fig. 2c).

WGF-T17 suppresses colorectal tumor growth in vivo

We further tested T17 (1 mg/kg) in MC38 and CT26 models against JHU-083, ART, and their combination (Fig. 2b). T17 significantly reduced tumor burden in both models and outperformed all comparators in efficacy and safety (Fig. 2c–f, and Supplementary Fig. 3a–d). While JHU-083+ART also suppressed tumor growth, it caused more weight loss and higher toxicity. In contrast, T17 achieved superior therapeutic efficacy while maintaining better overall health, as evidenced by the lowest mortality rate observed in the T17 group across both models (Fig. 2g, and Supplementary Fig. 3e). H&E and Ki67 staining revealed increased necrosis and decreased proliferation in T17-treated tumors (Fig. 2h, and Supplementary Fig. 3f). The tumor tissues showed an increase of necrotic tissue and a significant decrease in the expression of the proliferation marker Ki67 after T17 treatment. This was consistent with the results acquired from the tumor growth curves, indicating that T17 possesses a superior anti-tumor ability to JHU-083.

To evaluate the antitumor efficacy of T17 in a more clinically relevant colorectal cancer model, we established an orthotopic MC38-luc tumor model in mice (Fig. 2i). Bioluminescence imaging revealed progressive tumor growth in PBS, JHU-083, and ART treated mice, whereas T17 treatment resulted in a marked and sustained suppression of tumor-derived luminescent signals (Fig. 2j). Quantitative analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in total flux and terminal tumor weight in the T17 group compared with all control groups, while body weight remained stable throughout the treatment period (Fig. 2k–m). Consistent with these findings, histological examination of orthotopic tumor tissues showed increased necrotic areas and substantially decreased Ki67 expression following T17 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 3h), further supporting its robust antitumor activity in a clinically relevant model.

We also performed H&E staining of the main organs to evaluate systemic toxicity. No histopathological abnormalities were observed in mice treated with T17 at 1 mg/kg (Supplementary Fig. 3g). However, during drug treatment, we observed that JHU-083 decreased the body weight of mice in the pre-treatment period (Fig. 2c, m and Supplementary Fig. 3a), which could be due to gastrointestinal toxicity. To validate our hypothesis, we performed H&E staining on mouse GI tissues (Supplementary Fig. 3i). The results showed that the JHU-083 treatment group exhibited damage to the intestinal villi and a thinned mucosal layer, whereas these morphological changes were not observed following T17 treatment. These findings suggest that T17 has comparatively lower toxicity and holds greater potential for clinical application.

The potent antitumor effects of WGF-T17 depend on macrophages

Given the observed association between elevated GLS1 expression and immunosuppressive TAM in CRC, we next evaluated whether T17 exerts its antitumor effects via macrophage reprogramming (Fig. 3a). Previous studies have implicated α-KG, a key glutamine metabolite, as a regulator of macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory phenotype28,29. Based on this, we hypothesized that T17, by disrupting glutamine metabolism, could modulate macrophage polarization in the TME. Flow cytometry revealed a marked increase in CD86+ pro-inflammatory TAM in T17-treated tumors (56.8%) compared to JHU-083 (30.2%) and PBS (11.8%). Conversely, the proportion of CD206+ anti-inflammatory TAM decreased to 36.3% following T17 treatment, compared to 67.0% and 59.5% in the JHU-083 and PBS groups, respectively (Fig. 3b–d and Supplementary Fig. 4a). Further multiparametric flow cytometric analysis of anti-inflammatory macrophage subsets revealed that the reduction in CD206+ TAM was primarily attributable to significant decreases in both CD206+Arg1+ and CD206+VEGF+ macrophage subsets following T17 treatment. (Supplementary Fig. 5a–f). These results indicate that T17 effectively reprograms TAM toward a pro-inflammatory state. Besides, T17 also increased CD8+ T cells and dendritic cells (DC) infiltration similarly to JHU-083, indicating that the superior antitumor efficacy of T17 may not stem from enhanced T cell immunity (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c).

We also assessed cytokine levels in tumor lysates and serum to confirm the immunological effects of this phenotypic change. T17 treatment significantly elevated proinflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor (TNF), which increased from 11.97 pg/mg in PBS-treated tumors to 39.61 pg/mg in T17-treated ones. In contrast, the anti-inflammatory cytokines TGF-β and IL-10 were highest in the JHU-083 group and decreased in the T17 group (Fig. 3e–l). These findings indicate that T17 promotes macrophage reprogramming and reshapes the cytokine toward a pro-inflammatory and antitumor profile.

To assess the necessity of macrophages in T17’s activity, we depleted TAM using clodronate liposomes (CLD-Lp) (Fig. 3m). Flow cytometry and immunostaining confirmed effective macrophage depletion (Fig. 3p–s). Tumor growth curve analysis revealed complete abrogation of T17-mediated tumor suppression in macrophage-depleted group, with final tumor volumes reaching 499.2 ± 205 mm³ (T17 + CLD-Lp) versus 82.6 ± 74 mm³ (T17 alone) (Fig. 3n, and Supplementary Fig. 4d). A similar loss of efficacy was observed in the CT26 model (Fig. 3o), confirming that TAM are required for T17-mediated tumor suppression. Next, we performed an ex vivo co-implantation study to further examine whether macrophage activation alone is sufficient to confer tumor control (Fig. 3t). T17-treated bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) significantly inhibited tumor growth (p = 0.0119), and 2/5 mice achieved complete tumor clearance, demonstrating that reprogrammed macrophages are sufficient to confer substantial tumor control (Fig. 3u, v and Supplementary Fig. 4e, f).

In parallel, we evaluated the relative contribution of CD8+ T cells to T17 efficacy (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Depletion of CD8+ T cells led to a mild attenuation of T17 efficacy attributable to the compromised immune system, but this reduction did not reach statistical significance, indicating that cytotoxic T cells make an auxiliary contribution to T17-mediated tumor therapy. In contrast, macrophage depletion resulted in a profound and significant loss of therapeutic benefit (Supplementary Fig. 6b–f). Furthermore, T17 retained robust antitumor activity in the HCT116 xenograft model established in nude mice, whereas the efficacy of the T cell dependent agent JHU-083 was largely restricted (Supplementary Fig. 6g–l). Collectively, these data demonstrate that TAM reprogramming constitutes the principal mechanism underlying T17 therapeutic activity.

Interestingly, we observed body weight loss of JHU-083 in the pharmacodynamic model (Fig. 2c). Given the established role of macrophages in promoting gastrointestinal inflammation, we hypothesized that the intestinal toxicity associated with JHU-083 might be mediated by aberrant activation of proinflammatory macrophages in the gut. Indeed, flow cytometry and immunofluorescence analyses revealed a marked increase in CD86+ macrophages in the small intestine following JHU-083 treatment, whereas no such effect was observed in T17-treated mice (Fig. 3w and Supplementary Fig. 7a). This difference is likely attributable to the distinct biodistribution and stability profiles of the two compounds, as T17 exhibited reduced accumulation and degradation in the small intestine, thereby limiting its impact on gastrointestinal macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Importantly, depletion of macrophages using clodronate liposomes partially rescued intestinal villus damage induced by JHU-083 (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Consistently, the expression of tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Claudin-1 was restored upon macrophage depletion, indicating improved intestinal barrier integrity (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). Together, these findings suggest that the gastrointestinal toxicity of JHU-083 is partly caused by inflammatory responses triggered by intestinal macrophages, whereas the improved stability and tumor-selective activation of T17 mitigate this adverse effect.

WGF-T17 promotes a pro-inflammatory macrophage state

Based on the observed macrophage-dependent antitumor effects of T17 in vivo, we further examined its role in macrophage polarization in vitro. Compared to JHU-083, T17 exhibited lower cytotoxicity in RAW264.7 cells and M0 BMDMs, maintaining viability and reducing apoptosis, a prerequisite for effective reprogramming (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). Flow cytometry demonstrated that T17 robustly increased CD86+ pro-inflammatory macrophages and decreased CD206+ anti-inflammatory macrophages in both RAW264.7 and BMDMs, surpassing JHU-083 in efficacy. Particularly in BMDMs, T17 treatment achieved a 64.1% CD11b+ CD86+ cell population, representing an approximate 30% increase compared to JHU-083 (Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Fig. 8c, e). This effect was time-dependent, with a progressive rise in CD86+ cells observed over 12-36 hours (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Western blot and immunofluorescence confirmed elevated pro-inflammatory markers, including CD86, iNOS, and IL-1β after T17 treatment (Fig. 4c, d and Supplementary Fig. 8f–h). ELISA further showed increased pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF secretion from both RAW264.7 and BMDMs (Fig. 4e, f). Taken together, these in vitro findings provide preliminary evidence that the IMDC T17 promotes polarization toward a pro-inflammatory macrophage state, thereby enhancing their antitumor capacity.

However, most tumors are characterized by an immunosuppressive microenvironment dominated by anti-inflammatory macrophages30. To model this state in vitro, BMDMs were pre-treated with IL-4 to induce an anti-inflammatory phenotype. T17 significantly reprogrammed anti-inflammatory macrophages, raising CD86+ cells to 50.8% and reducing CD206+ cells to 3.85%, whereas JHU-083 achieved only partial conversion (Fig. 4g–i). Consistent with these findings, western blot and immunofluorescence analyses demonstrated that T17-treated BMDMs and RAW264.7 cells exhibited the most pronounced reduction in CD206 protein expression and the highest CD86 protein levels compared to other groups. This was corroborated by protein-level analyses, which showed higher CD86 and lower CD206 expression in T17-treated cells (Fig. 4j–l, and Supplementary Fig. 8i–l). Importantly, T17 was less cytotoxic to anti-inflammatory macrophages than JHU-083, indicating that its immunomodulatory effects were not due to cell elimination but rather functional reprogramming (Supplementary Fig. 8m).

Transcriptomic analysis of T17 treated TAM and BMDMs by RNA-seq further substantiated its macrophage reprogramming activity. Consistent with the phenotypic and functional data, T17 markedly upregulated pro-inflammatory macrophage-associated genes, including Nos2, Cd80, Cd86, and Tnf, while concomitantly suppressing canonical anti-inflammatory macrophage-associated markers such as Tgfb1, Arg1, Il10, Mrc1, and Cd36, in both TAM and BMDMs (Fig. 4m). In contrast, JHU-083 treatment preferentially maintained or enhanced the expression of multiple anti-inflammatory associated transcripts, indicating an incomplete or biased macrophage reprogramming profile. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that T17-responsive genes were significantly enriched in pathways related to innate immune response, inflammatory response, and positive regulation of phagocytosis, whereas pathways associated with negative regulation of immune response, and glutamate metabolic regulation were comparatively suppressed (Fig. 4n). These transcriptional signatures indicate that T17 drives a coordinated shift toward a proinflammatory, immune-activating macrophage state at the molecular level.

WGF-T17 regulates macrophage metabolic processes increasing lactate accumulation

Given that T17 is an IMDC of the glutamine antagonist DON, and that glutamine metabolism constitutes a central metabolic hub linking the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, redox homeostasis, and macrophage functional states, we next investigated how T17 modulates macrophage metabolic programs. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of transcriptomic profiles from treated macrophages revealed that T17 significantly upregulated pathways related to glucose metabolism and pyruvate metabolism, while concomitantly suppressing oxidative phosphorylation, compared with both JHU-083 and PBS control groups (Fig. 5a, b). Consistently, T17 markedly induced the expression of key glycolytic genes, including Slc2a1, Hk2, and Pfkb, while downregulating genes associated with mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, such as Tfam, Sdha, and Ndufa3 (Fig. 5c). These transcriptional changes were further validated at the protein level by western blot and immunofluorescence analyses, which demonstrated a pronounced increase in hexokinase 2 (HK2) expression in both BMDMs and RAW264.7 cells following T17 treatment (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 9a–c).

To further characterize T17-driven metabolic remodeling, we performed untargeted metabolomics in BMDMs after 36 h of treatment. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct metabolic clustering between T17-treated macrophages and controls, indicating significant T17-driven metabolic remodeling (Fig. 5e). Among the 294 metabolites detected, hierarchical clustering identified differentially regulated pathways, including pyrimidine nucleotide metabolism, organooxygen compounds, and carboxylic acid derivatives (Fig. 5f, g).

In physiological settings, macrophages metabolize glucose through glycolysis and the TCA cycle to support lipid and cholesterol biosynthesis (Fig. 5h). T17 significantly decreased intracellular glutamate (50.9%) levels, leading to reduced α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), a key intermediate of the TCA cycle (Fig. 5i). This reduction likely impaired TCA cycle activity, consistent with transcriptomic suppression of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation genes. Despite reduced glutaminolysis, macrophage glucose uptake was not decreased but increased, and the intracellular glucose content after T17 treatment rose up to 2.3-fold compared with that in the Control group. Meanwhile, lipid and cholesterol biosynthesis remained unchanged, intermediates in the pentose phosphate pathway (Ribulose-5-phosphate, GMP) were significantly reduced. However, only the pyruvate and lactate were significantly increased in T17-treated macrophages, suggesting preferential shunting of glucose through the glycolytic pathway.

To validate this observation, we directly assessed glucose uptake capacity and intracellular lactate levels in BMDMs following T17 treatment. After 36 hours of exposure, T17-treated BMDMs exhibited significantly increased uptake of the fluorescent glucose analog 2-NBDG compared with both control and JHU-083 treated cells (Supplementary Fig. 9d). In parallel, intracellular lactate levels were markedly elevated upon T17 treatment, consistent with enhanced glycolytic flux (Fig. 5j and Supplementary Fig. 9e). Combined with the significant upregulation of the glycolytic pathway found by RNA-seq, we propose that the large amount of glucose ingested by macrophages after T17 action is converted to lactate via the glycolytic pathway to accumulate in large quantities within the cell and affect macrophage polarization and function (Fig. 5k).

WGF-T17 enhances macrophage phagocytosis by histone lactylation

Intracellular lactate accumulation following T17 treatment is not merely a metabolic byproduct but also serves as a substrate for histone lysine lactylation31–33. To explore this, we assessed global lactylation changes in BMDMs and RAW264.7 cells. The lactylation level of macrophages increased significantly after 36 hours of T17 treatment (Fig. 6a, b and Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). We next screened a total of four common histone lactylation sites and found that T17 selectively enhanced H4K12 lactylation, but not H3K9, H3K14, or H4K16 (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 10c–e). Notably, H4K12 lactylation increased in a time- and concentration-dependent manner, peaking at 36 h, suggesting T17 specifically drives this modification (Supplementary Fig. 10f).

To explore the functional consequences of H4K12la, we performed CUT&Tag sequencing in T17-treated BMDMs34,35. T17 treatment enriched H4K12la signals in gene promoter regions, with GO analysis showing strong association with mitochondrial organization and membrane pathways (Fig. 6d, e and Supplementary Fig. 10g, h). IGV analysis confirmed H4K12la signal enrichment at promoters of mitochondrial dynamics regulators, such as Dnm1l (encoding DRP-1), Mfn1, and Mff (Fig. 6f). Consistently, western blot and immunofluorescence staining showed T17 upregulated DRP-1, a key mediator of mitochondrial fission, while mitochondrial fusion proteins MFF remained unchanged (Fig. 6g, h and Supplementary Fig. 10i, j)36,37. Importantly, the DRP-1 inhibitor Mdivi-1 abrogated the DRP-1 increase and mitochondrial fission (Fig. 6i). In vivo, Mdivi-1 also reversed T17’s antitumor effects, confirming the role of DRP-1 dependent mitochondrial fission in mediating its function (Supplementary Fig. 11a–d).

Furthermore, transmission electron microscopy revealed T17-treated macrophages exhibited extensive mitochondrial fission, characterized by the transformation of elongated tubular mitochondria into fragmented punctate structures (Fig. 6j). Given the link between mitochondrial fission and phagocytosis38,39, we assessed phagocytic function using co-culture of T17-treated RAW264.7-mCherry macrophages with CFSE-labeled MC38 tumor cells. T17 significantly increased phagocytosis, with 13.5% more tumor cell uptake compared to controls (Fig. 6k and Supplementary Fig. 11e). In contrast, expression of the antigen presentation marker MHC II remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 11f, g), suggesting that T17 preferentially augments phagocytic function rather than antigen presentation.

Together, these findings indicate that T17 promotes histone lactylation-dependent mitochondrial fission via DRP-1 activation, thereby enhancing the phagocytic activity of macrophages and contributing to its antitumor effects.

WGF-T17 improves the efficacy of clinical treatments

Given T17’s ability to reprogram macrophage metabolism and promote pro-inflammatory polarization via H4K12 lactylation, we next evaluated its translational potential in combination with clinically relevant therapies. We established a MC38 tumor model in C57BL/6 mice and conducted a series of pharmacodynamic studies combining T17 with established therapeutic regimens (Fig. 7a). Specifically, we selected immune checkpoint inhibitors (αPD-L1 and αCTLA-4), the chemotherapeutic agent 5-FU, and the VEGFR-targeting monoclonal antibody DC10140,41.

Although immune checkpoint inhibitors such as αPD-L1 and αCTLA-4 have demonstrated clinical efficacy in various malignancies, their therapeutic benefit in CRC remains limited to a small subset of patients with high microsatellite instability42. In our model, monotherapy with αPD-L1 or αCTLA-4 showed limited efficacy in some mice due to suboptimal immune responsiveness. However, combination with T17 markedly enhanced therapeutic outcomes, attributed to its ability to reprogram macrophages and reverse immunosuppressive features of the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 7b).

Similarly, although 5-FU remains a frontline chemotherapeutic in CRC, its effectiveness is often hampered by systemic toxicity and resistance43. In our study, mice receiving 5-FU monotherapy exhibited significant weight loss, with mortality observed as early as day 17 (Fig. 7b). However, co-administration with T17 improved the overall quality of life and prolonged survival in mice. Although the mortality rate remained at 50% in the late treatment phase, it is noteworthy that this combination effectively suppressed tumor growth and prevented regrowth after treatment withdrawal. These findings suggest that T17 has the potential to mitigate the clinical limitations associated with 5-FU treatment.

Inhibition of VEGF-VEGFR signaling is a well-established strategy in CRC therapy, often employed to inhibit angiogenesis and deprive tumors of essential blood supply44,45. However, anti-angiogenic therapies are often compromised by localized tumor hypoxia, and impaired immune cell infiltration46,47. Combination treatment with DC101 and T17 improved tumor perfusion in the short term, thereby providing a more favorable structural basis for immune cell trafficking into the tumor parenchyma. Concurrently, the tumor immune microenvironment was activated, as evidenced by increased infiltration of pro-inflammatory macrophages, which significantly enhanced antitumor therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 7b). This enhanced efficacy was accompanied by marked immune remodeling within the tumor microenvironment. Immunofluorescence staining revealed a pronounced increase in CD86+ macrophage infiltration across all combination treatment groups compared with monotherapies, indicating effective macrophage reprogramming by T17 (Supplementary Fig. 11h). Notably, increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells was also observed in tumors receiving combined treatment, consistent with a more permissive and immunostimulatory tumor microenvironment.

Overall, these results demonstrate that T17, as a metabolically active immunomodulatory small molecule, exhibits broad potential for combination therapy. Our findings provide experimental evidence supporting its application in overcoming resistance to immunotherapy and chemotherapy, offering potential avenues for future mechanistic studies and clinical translation.

Discussion

CRC is marked by metabolic heterogeneity and an immunosuppressive TME, which limit the efficacy of existing therapies1,2. Among dysregulated metabolic pathways, glutamine metabolism plays a central role in fueling tumor growth and shaping immune suppression11,26. Integrative analysis of clinical CRC samples revealed that high GLS1 expression correlates with advanced disease, poor prognosis, and infiltration of anti-inflammatory, supporting a mechanistic link between glutamine metabolism and immunosuppressive TME remodeling (Fig. 1).

Although glutamine antagonists like DON and JHU-083 exhibit therapeutic potential, their clinical application is hindered by systemic toxicity and poor immune cell selectivity13,17. To address this, we developed T17, an IMDC of DON conjugated to artesunate. T17 not only inhibits tumor growth but also reprograms the TME by promoting pro-inflammatory of TAM (Figs. 2–4). Mechanistically, T17 shifts macrophage metabolism toward glycolysis, leading to lactate accumulation and histone H4K12 lactylation. This epigenetic mark upregulates DRP-1, a key regulator of mitochondrial fission, enhancing macrophage phagocytosis and antitumor activity (Figs. 5, 6). This “glutamine-lactate-H4K12la-mitochondria” axis highlights how metabolic interventions can rewire innate immune cells.

Notably, although T17 and JHU-083 share the same active moiety (DON), T17 consistently exhibited stronger effects in multiple in vitro assays. As pharmacokinetic advantages are not applicable under these conditions, this difference likely reflects intrinsic cellular mechanisms. We speculate that the ART moiety enhances intracellular delivery, retention, or subcellular distribution of the active compound in macrophages, thereby amplifying metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming. In addition, the conjugate structure of T17 may improve effective DON release dynamics within cells, contributing to its enhanced potency compared to JHU-083.

Compared to JHU-083, T17 shows superior immune selectivity and reduced toxicity. While JHU-083 induces systemic inflammation, T17 achieves spatially restricted activation within the TME, likely due to its esterase-sensitive linker and ART-mediated macrophage targeting (Fig. 3w and Supplementary Fig. 3i). Macrophage depletion and co-implantation assays confirmed that T17 as a macrophage-centric immunometabolic modulator, distinct from traditional immune checkpoint inhibitors that primarily act on T cells. Importantly, we also observed that ART alone recapitulates part of the biological effects of T17, including modulation of macrophage polarization, increased lactylation, and enhanced phagocytic activity. These findings suggest that ART may not function solely as an inert targeting moiety, but rather contributes to the overall biological activity of T17. Therefore, the therapeutic efficacy of T17 is likely driven by a combinatorial mechanism involving both DON-mediated glutamine antagonism and ART-mediated immunomodulation.

Furthermore, T17 improves the therapeutic efficacy of multiple clinically relevant treatments (Fig. 7). These findings support the translational value of T17 not only as a monotherapy but also as an immunometabolic sensitizer that enhances standard treatment efficacy. The ability to potentiate multiple therapeutic modalities underscores the broad versatility and clinical adaptability of this approach.

In conclusion, T17 represents a macrophage-targeted glutamine antagonist that integrates metabolic, epigenetic, and mitochondrial remodeling to reshape macrophage function and promote durable antitumor immunity (Fig. 7c). Rather than acting as a simple targeted drug, T17 functions as a dual-component immunometabolic regulator, combining glutamine blockade with macrophage-directed modulation. By defining this immunometabolic axis, this study expands therapeutic strategies for reprogramming macrophages. These findings offer insights into immunometabolic therapy and support further development of T17 as a promising candidate for clinical translation in colorectal cancer and potentially other solid tumors with high glutamine dependency.

Methods

Ethics statement

The tumor and adjacent nontumor tissues used in this research were obtained written informed consent from the patients in the Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center. All samples in this research were collected at Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center and stored at -80 °C or 4% paraformaldehyde. These experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (approval number: 2407-ZZK-123). All animal experiments were conducted under the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Northwestern Polytechnical University (approval number: 202301188).

Clinical tissue samples

The tumor and adjacent nontumor tissues used in this research were obtained written informed consent from the patients in the Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center. All samples in this research were all collected at Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center and stored at −80 °C or 4% paraformaldehyde. These experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (approval number: 2407-ZZK-123). Clinical information, including sex, was obtained from medical records provided by the hospital. Due to the limited sample size, sex-based analyses were not performed in this study.

Animals

Female 6-8-week-old C57BL/6 mice (stock no. FH202506155) and BALB/c mice (stock no. FH202509076) were purchased from Shaanxi Pharmaceutical and Medical Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Female 6-week-old BALB/c-nu mice (stock no. FH202511447) were purchased from Beijing SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) barrier facility under controlled conditions of 21 °C–24 °C, 40–70% relative humidity, and a 12 h light/dark cycle. Animals were purchased from commercial vendors and were not bred in-house. Mice of the same strain were co-housed before randomization and then housed by treatment group after randomization. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Northwestern Polytechnical University (approval number: 202301188). Only female mice were used in all animal experiments.

C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously injected with 5 × 105 MC38 cells, and BALB/c mice were subcutaneously injected with 5 × 105 CT26 cells, to establish syngeneic colorectal cancer models. BALB/c-nu mice were subcutaneously injected with 2 × 106 HCT116 cells to establish a xenograft tumor model. Tumor volume was measured every other day using calipers and calculated as (length × width²)/2. Body weight and general health status were monitored every other day throughout the treatment course. When tumor volume reached approximately 80 mm³, mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups and treated via intragastric administration according to the indicated protocols. PBS, JHU-083, ART, and T17 were administered at 1 mg/kg by intragastric administration (i.g.).

The maximum tumor size permitted by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee was 1500 mm³, and this limit was not exceeded in this study. Mice were euthanized before the planned endpoint if they met any predefined humane endpoint criteria, including tumor volume approaching the approved maximum size, tumor ulceration or necrosis, impaired mobility, severe distress, or body weight loss exceeding 20% of baseline body weight. At the experimental endpoint or when humane endpoint criteria were met, mice were euthanized by overdose of isoflurane anesthesia followed by cervical dislocation before tissue collection.

Cells

CT26 (CL-0071), MC38 (CL-0972), and HCT116 (CL-0096) cells were purchased from the Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. MC38-luc (YC-A002-Luc-P) was purchased from Ubigene Biosciences. All cells were cultured under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2) in the DMEM (E600003-0500, Sangon Biotech) or RPMI-1640 (E600028-0500, Sangon Biotech) culture medium with 10% FBS (FSP500, Excell) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (E607011-0100, Sangon Biotech). None of the cells were contaminated by mycoplasma.

BMDMs acquisition and treatment

The bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were extracted from the femur and tibia of female C57BL/6 mice aged 6-8 weeks. Mouse bone marrow cells were flushed from femurs and tibias with PBS and then cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 20 ng/ml M-CSF (51112-MNAH, Center for Bioprocessing) for 6 days. Then, BMDMs were collected and prepared for subsequent stimulation.

On day 7, differentiated BMDMs were treated with JHU-083 (10 μM), artesunate (10 μM), or T17 (10 μM) and subsequently collected for downstream analyses.

Pharmacokinetic and tissue distribution analysis

For in vivo pharmacokinetic and biodistribution studies, tumor-bearing mice were administered T17 or JHU-083 as indicated. Blood, tumor tissue, and gastrointestinal samples were collected at 10, 20, and 30 min, and at 1, 3, and 5 h post-administration. Plasma was isolated by centrifugation, and tissues were homogenized in appropriate extraction buffer. The levels of released DON were quantified by LC-MS/MS using validated analytical methods. Concentration-time profiles were generated to compare systemic exposure and tissue distribution between T17 and JHU-083.

Glutamine content measurement

Glutamine levels in tumor and normal tissues were quantified using a commercial Glutamine Colorimetric Assay Kit (E-BC-K853-M, Elabscience) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 0.1 g of frozen tissue was homogenized in 0.9 mL of physiological saline solution. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. Glutamine concentration was determined by measuring absorbance at 450 nm and normalized to tissue weight.

Immunofluorescence

Tissue sections or cell-containing coverslips treated with drugs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20–30 min, followed by permeabilization using 0.1–0.25% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 10 min. Subsequently, samples were blocked with 5% BSA in PBST blocking solution at room temperature for 1 hour. Primary antibodies (dilution ratios specified in Supplementary Table S2) were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After thorough washing with 0.05% PBST for three times, secondary antibodies were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Cell nuclei were marked with Hoechst 33342 (C1022, Beyotime), and all fluorescence-related procedures were performed in the dark. Images were finally acquired using a Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (OLYMPUS--OLYMPUS FV3000).

H&E Staining

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a descending ethanol series (100%, 95%, 80%, 70%), and rinsed in distilled water. Slides were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, washed in running tap water, and differentiated in 1% acid alcohol for a few seconds. After rinsing, sections were counterstained with eosin for 1 min. Slides were then dehydrated in ascending alcohols, cleared in xylene, and mounted with coverslips using neutral resin. Images were acquired using the Olympus bright-field microscope.

Immunohistochemical staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Briefly, sections were incubated in xylene, followed by sequential immersion in 100%, 95%, 80%, and 70% ethanol, and then rinsed in distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections at 95 °C–100 °C in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After cooling to room temperature, sections were washed with PBS and incubated with 5% BSA for 30 min at room temperature to block non-specific binding.

Primary antibodies (dilution ratios specified in Supplementary Table S2) were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Followed by washing with PBST and incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 30 minutes at room temperature. Signal was developed using DAB (3,3’-diaminobenzidine) substrate solution until the desired color intensity was achieved. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. After dehydration and mounting, stained sections were imaged using the Olympus bright-field microscope.

Flow cytometry

For cultured cell samples, cells were harvested after drug treatment and resuspended in flow cytometry staining buffer. Surface marker staining was performed at 4 °C for 1 h using antibodies diluted according to the ratios specified in Supplementary Table S2. After incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS to remove unbound antibodies. For intracellular marker detection, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the FoxP3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Kit (IC001, Multisciences Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s protocol prior to intracellular staining. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a BD FACSCelesta™ flow cytometer, and data were analyzed with FlowJo software.

For spleen samples, freshly isolated spleens were mechanically dissociated by gently pressing through a 70 μm cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension. Red blood cells were lysed using a standard RBC lysis buffer (RT122-02, TIANGEN), and 1 × 10⁶ nucleated cells were used for subsequent staining. The staining procedure was identical to that used for cultured cell samples.

For tumor tissue samples, excised tumors were minced into small fragments and digested in tissue dissociation buffer containing 2 mg/mL collagenase I (LS004196, Worthington) and 2 mg/mL collagenase IV (LS004188, Worthington) at 37 °C with gentle shaking for 1 h. The enzymatic reaction was quenched with PBS containing 5% FBS, and the resulting suspension was filtered through a 70 μm strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension. Cells were washed with PBS, counted, and approximately 1 × 10⁶ cells were used for each staining reaction. Tumor-derived cells were resuspended in flow cytometry staining buffer and incubated with surface marker antibodies at 4 °C for 1 h. After washing twice with PBS, intracellular staining was performed using the FoxP3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Kit when required. Stained cells were washed, resuspended in staining buffer, and analyzed using a BD FACSCelesta™ flow cytometer48,49.

The gating strategies for cell sorting are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 12 and 13.

ELISA

The levels of TNF, TGF-β, IL-12, and IL-10 in mouse serum and tumor tissues were quantified using uncoated ELISA kits (88-7324-88, 88-8350-22, 88-7121-88, 88-7105-88, Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For serum collection, whole blood was obtained via retro-orbital bleeding, allowed to clot at room temperature for an hour, and centrifuged at 3000 g for 20 min to isolate the serum. For tumor homogenates, approximately 100 mg of tumor tissue was homogenized in PBS containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant.

Each cytokine-specific ELISA was performed by coating 96-well high-binding plates with the corresponding capture antibody. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves and normalized to sample volume or tissue weight as appropriate.

Macrophage depletion in mouse model

To investigate the role of macrophages in T17-mediated antitumor activity, macrophages were selectively depleted in vivo using clodronate liposomes (40337ES10, YEASEN). Tumor-bearing mice received an intraperitoneal injection of 200 μL clodronate liposomes on Day 6, one day prior to the initiation of drug treatment. Thereafter, clodronate liposomes were administered every three days to maintain macrophage depletion. Control groups received equivalent volumes of PBS-containing liposomes. The efficacy of macrophage depletion was assessed by flow cytometry in tumor tissues.

Ex vivo co-implantation assay

To evaluate the functional contribution of T17-primed macrophages to tumor control, an ex vivo co-implantation model was established. BMDMs were isolated from female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) and differentiated in vitro with M-CSF for 7 days. On Day 7, BMDMs were treated with T17 or vehicle control for 36 hours. The pretreated BMDMs were then mixed with MC38 tumor cells at a 1:1 ratio (5 × 10⁵ each) in PBS, and the mixture was subcutaneously injected into the flanks of naïve syngeneic C57BL/6 mice. Tumor growth was monitored over time to assess the impact of T17-conditioned macrophages on tumor progression in vivo.

RNA-seq

Total RNA was isolated from BMDMs or TAM using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed with the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system using the RNA 6000 Nano LabChip Kit (5067-1511, Agilent Technologies). Only high-quality RNA samples with an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) greater than 7.0 were used for library preparation. RNA sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform (LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) following the standard protocol provided by the manufacturer.

Differential gene expression analysis was carried out using the DESeq2 package. Significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses. Enrichment was determined based on the hypergeometric distribution to identify biologically relevant GO terms and signaling pathways.

Cell viability

Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. Briefly, RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured until approximately 50% confluence. The medium was then replaced with drug-containing media at various concentrations, and cells were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C. After treatment, MTT (ST316, Beyotime) reagent was added to each well, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader to determine cell viability.

Western blot

Total cellular protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with PMSF and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay kit. Samples were mixed with 5 × SDS loading buffer and denatured at 100 °C for 10 min. Equal amounts of protein were resolved by SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore) using methanol-based transfer buffer. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat dry milk diluted in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Antibody details and dilutions are in Table S2). After washing, membranes were incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system and imaged with a digital imaging system.

Assessment of apoptosis by flow cytometry

Macrophage apoptosis was performed using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (40302ES60, Yeasen Biotechnology). After drug treatment, 1 × 10⁶ RAW264.7 cells were collected and washed twice with cold PBS. Cells were resuspended in 100 μL of binding buffer and stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) at room temperature for 15 minutes in the dark. Samples were immediately analyzed by flow cytometry using a BD FACSCelesta™ system. Apoptotic cells were quantified as Annexin V+/PI− (early apoptosis) and Annexin V+/PI+ (late apoptosis or necrosis).

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Untargeted metabolomic profiling was conducted in collaboration with LipidALL Technologies (Shanghai, China). Cellular metabolites were extracted using methanol-based solvents and analyzed by ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC–MS/MS) using a Sciex 5600 + quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer. Both hydrophilic interaction and reverse-phase liquid chromatography columns were applied for metabolite separation. Metabolite identities were annotated by comparing MS/MS spectra to standard databases including HMDB and internal spectral libraries. A panel of isotopically labeled internal standards was spiked into each sample for normalization and relative quantification. Data processing and statistical analyses were performed using MarkerView, PeakView, and proprietary algorithms provided by the service platform.

Lactate assay

To assess intracellular lactate accumulation, BMDMs and RAW264.7 cells were collected after drug treatment and lysed in 200 μL of pre-chilled PBS using sonication. Cell lysates were centrifuged at 4 °C, and the supernatant was used for lactate quantification using an L-lactate colorimetric assay kit (A019-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute). Absorbance was measured at 530 nm using a microplate reader.

Cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag)

CUT&Tag was performed to investigate histone modifications following T17 treatment. Library construction was conducted using the Hieff NGS® Multi-CUT&Tag Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (12592ES12, Yeasen Biotechnology). Briefly, BMDMs treated with T17 or vehicle were immobilized on concanavalin A-coated magnetic beads and incubated with anti-H4K12la primary antibody, followed by pA-Tn5 transposase binding. After tagmentation and DNA isolation, sequencing libraries were amplified and purified. Library quality was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and Qubit Fluorometer. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Comparisons between two groups were made using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, while one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test was used for comparisons among multiple groups. Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise indicated. Sample sizes (n ≥ 3) and number of independent experiments are specified in the figure legends. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Exact p-values are provided in figure panels and corresponding source data files.

All experiments were repeated independently at least three times with similar results, unless otherwise stated. Representative micrographs, immunofluorescence images, immunohistochemistry images, western blots, and flow cytometry plots shown in the figures were selected from independent experiments that yielded similar results. For animal studies, the number of mice per group is indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Animal experiments were performed as independent biological experiments as stated in the figure legends.
