Association between dietary inflammatory index, empirical dietary inflammatory patterns and the risk of Long COVID: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Yue Zhang, Daihai Mo, Yang Cai, Hongyao Lv, Minjing Wei, Fengyang Jing, Jiang Xu, Yihan Ou, Yuan Yuan, Ziqi Liu, Xiaoyu Fang, Wenhui Wu, Xiangyu Ma, Xiangyu Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInflammatory responses and immune dysregulation are considered core mechanisms of Long COVID. Given that diet can modulate systemic inflammation, we evaluated the associations between two indices reflecting dietary inflammatory potential—the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) and the Empirical Dietary Inflammatory Pattern (EDIP)—and the risk of Long COVID in a prospective cohort of 69,070 participants from the UK Biobank.MethodThe DII and EDIP were calculated and categorized into quartiles based on Dietary information obtained from the Oxford WebQ online system. Outcome information was obtained from hospital admission records (HESIN, ICD-10) linked to the UK Biobank. Cox proportional hazards models were used to calculate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Restricted cubic splines were employed to evaluate the nonlinearity.ResultsAfter full adjustment, the highest EDIP quartile (most pro-inflammatory) was associated with increased Long COVID risk (HR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.08–1.22, p < 0.001), with no significant association for DII. Yet, both indices demonstrated significant associations with risks for specific organ system conditions—particularly affecting the cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, kidney, and mental health systems—among the 10 predefined categories. Both indices showed significant non-linear associations with Long COVID risk (p for nonlinearity < 0.05), with a U-shaped pattern for DII and a J-shaped pattern for EDIP.ConclusionIn this prospective observational study, a pro-inflammatory dietary pattern was associated with a higher risk of Long COVID-related outcomes and multi-organ sequelae. These findings suggest that dietary inflammatory potential may be relevant to post-COVID-19 health and warrant further investigation as a potentially modifiable factor.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28519-2.Keywords: Dietary Inflammation Index, Empirical Dietary Inflammatory Pattern, Diet, Long COVID

Background

COVID-19 is an acute respiratory infectious disease caused by infection with the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). Since the outbreak first emerged in December 2019, multiple waves of infections have occurred worldwide, with over 100 million confirmed cases and over 2 million deaths reported to date [1, 2]. Although the incidence and mortality rates of COVID-19 have decreased, long-term health issues caused by SARS-CoV-2 infection—known as Long COVID—are emerging as a new global public health challenge [3, 4]. Long COVID, a common chronic condition and health issue following SARS-CoV-2 infection, is characterized by symptoms lasting longer than three months, including but not limited to: fatigue, cognitive dysfunction, respiratory distress, and multisystem dysfunction [5]. This syndrome not only severely affects patients’ quality of life and social functioning but also imposes a sustained disease burden on public health systems [5, 6]. Epidemiological studies have shown that during the 6 to 12 months following SARS-CoV-2 infection, there is an increased risk and disease burden of cardiovascular, pulmonary, neuropsychiatric, and metabolic disorders [7, 8]. Notably, for some diseases, this elevated risk persists for up to two years after infection [9]. Despite advancements in acute-phase management through vaccination and antivirals [10], effective preventive strategies against Long COVID remain elusive [11].

Chronic systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation are considered central to the pathogenesis of Long COVID [12]. This highlights the potential of anti-inflammatory interventions as a preventive strategy. Diet represents a key modifiable lifestyle factor with established immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties [13]. Its inflammatory potential can be quantified using tools such as the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII), which is literature-derived and nutrient-focused, and the Empirical Dietary Inflammatory Pattern (EDIP), which is data-driven and based on food groups [14, 15]. The DII is an index that quantifies the effects of various dietary components on inflammatory markers (such as IL-6, CRP, TNF-α) based on a review of the literature [14]. The EDIP, on the other hand, is a dietary inflammatory index derived from specific populations through statistical methods (such as reduced-rank regression), focusing on the relationship between food groups and inflammatory markers [15]. Compared to the DII, the EDIP places greater emphasis on the overall effects of food groups rather than the impact of individual foods or nutrients [13]. Studies have shown that both indices are consistently associated with the risk of chronic inflammatory diseases (such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes) [16–20], and have been linked to the severity and outcomes of acute COVID-19 [21], while anti-inflammatory diet may improve health outcomes by reducing systemic inflammation [22–24]. Notably, a pro-inflammatory diet may exacerbate the risk of long-term sequelae after acute infection by exacerbating cytokine storms, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction [17, 21].

However, prospective evidence on the association between pro-inflammatory diets, as measured by DII or EDIP, and Long COVID risk remains limited. Addressing this gap may provide useful insights into the potential relevance of dietary inflammatory potential for post-COVID-19 health and future dietary research.

Therefore, this study aimed to systematically evaluate the prospective associations of DII and EDIP with Long COVID-related outcomes in a large cohort. By examining dietary inflammatory potential in relation to post-COVID-19 multisystem sequelae, we sought to provide epidemiological evidence on the potential relevance of diet-related inflammation to post-COVID-19 health and to inform future research on modifiable lifestyle factors.

Methods

Study participants

The UK Biobank is a large prospective cohort study containing phenotypic and genomic data on over 500,000 participants aged 40–69 years (recruited 2006–2010) [25]. At baseline, researchers collected individual demographics, lifestyle, health-related factors, physical measurements and blood samples [25]. Follow-up information were obtained by linking national medical records (including primary/secondary care, disease and death registries [26]) and were validated for reliability, accuracy and completeness [27], with detailed information available elsewhere [25]. COVID-19 cases in this study were confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or antibody testing (from the UK Public Health Second Generation Surveillance System) [28].

For the current analysis, we selected 174,878 participants who had PCR- or antibody-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection between March 1, 2020 (date of the first recorded case) and October 31, 2022, from a total of 502,128 UK Biobank members. The date of the first infection was considered the baseline date (T0). After applying the exclusion criteria—including incident cases on or after April 30, 2022 (to ensure ≥ 6 months of follow-up for all participants), those who experienced the outcome event or died within 30 days post-T0, and those with a history of the outcome within one year prior to T0—a total of 138,775 participants were included. Thereafter, individuals lacking baseline dietary data or with implausible total energy intake (defined as < 500 or > 3,600 kcal/day for women, and < 800 or > 4,200 kcal/day for men) were further excluded, yielding a final analytical cohort of 69,070 participants. Assessment of diet

Dietary information were collected via an Oxford WebQ online questionnaire covering intake of 206 foods and 32 drinks over the last 24 h [29]. Information were collected in 5 cycles: including baseline (2009.4-2010.9) and 4 subsequent rounds (2011.2-4, 2011.6-9, 2011.10–12, 2012.4-6) [30]. The median [IQR] duration between baseline assessment of dietary and the date of infection was 12.10 [11.48–12.81] years. In each collection cycle, participants were asked to complete a 24-hour dietary recall, and then the researcher derived the intake of each food or beverage based on calculating the product of standard portion size and consumption [31]. After completing the questionnaire, the Oxford WebQ online system automatically generated the total energy intake (kJ) for each individual. In this analyses, we used the average of multiple assessments as the food/beverage consumption to improve accuracy [31]. Based on previous studies and the availability of dietary variables in the UK Biobank, 31 food parameters and 18 food groups were selected from the database to calculate the DII and EDIP scores, respectively [14, 15]. Details are shown in the Supplementary Tables 1–2.

Ascertainment of outcomes

In the current study, the Long COVID phenotype was defined as a composite outcome encompassing 85 symptoms and disorders across 10 organ systems (cardiovascular, coagulation, metabolic, gastrointestinal, kidney, mental health, musculoskeletal, neurological, respiratory, and fatigue/malaise), based on established evidence on post-COVID-19 sequelae [9, 32–35]. This endpoint was not based on a single ICD-10 code for clinically diagnosed Long COVID. Participants with relevant medical history within 1 year before the index date (T0, date of SARS-CoV-2 infection) were excluded, and outcomes were ascertained from 30 days to 2 years after infection. The primary outcome was defined as the first occurrence of any condition included in the predefined multisystem sequelae list, whereas secondary outcomes were defined as the first occurrence of symptoms or disorders within each individual organ system. Multisystem sequelae information was obtained from hospital admission records linked to the UK Biobank (HESIN, ICD-10 coding), with data available until October 31, 2022. To ensure a minimum follow-up period of 6 months for all participants, the study cutoff was set at April 30, 2022. The detailed definitions and the complete list of ICD-10 codes used in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Assessment of covariates

Based on literature and prior knowledge [35–37], we pre-identified several covariates for adjustment, including sociodemographic characteristics such as age at infection, sex, education (university/college or other), the Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI), and ethnicity (White/other); as well as lifestyle factors: mean total energy intake across available dietary assessments (kcal/day), smoking status (never/former/current), BMI (normal/underweight/overweight or obesity), and physical activity (meeting the 2017 UK Physical Activity Guidelines [38]: ≥150 min of walking or moderate-intensity activity per week, or ≥ 75 min of vigorous-intensity activity). Missing covariate data were handled as follows: (1) Participants with missing ethnicity information (< 0.1%) and those who selected “none of the above” or “prefer not to answer” were categorized as “other.” (2) Participants with missing education level information (< 0.1%) were categorized as “other,” which also included “none of the above” and “prefer not to answer.” (3) Missing data for smoking status (0.3%) were imputed using the mode.

Statistical analysis

Assessment of dietary inflammatory potential

The DII index was constructed based on literature from 1950 to 2010. Researchers first screened articles on the effects of diet on six validated biomarkers (IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and CRP), scored the pro-inflammatory/anti-inflammatory effects of food parameters, and weighted them according to the study design of the included articles. Next, a database was constructed using dietary information from 11 countries. Individual intake levels were standardized to percentile values relative to the global average, then multiplied by the corresponding inflammatory effect scores and summed to obtain the DII score reflecting dietary inflammatory potential. Detailed explanations are provided in the original article [14].

The EDIP was developed and validated by Tabung et al. based on three prospective cohorts in the United States (NHS, NHS-II, HPFS). It assesses the inflammatory potential of the diet by weighting and summing the intake of 18 key food groups (9 anti-inflammatory and 9 pro-inflammatory) and dividing by 1000. The 9 anti-inflammatory food groups include beer, wine, tea, coffee, dark yellow vegetables, fruit juice, green leafy vegetables, snacks, and pizza. The 9 pro-inflammatory food groups include red meat, processed meat, organ meat, high-energy beverages, other [non-dark-colored meat] fish, other vegetables [including cruciferous vegetables, deep yellow vegetables, or green leafy vegetables], refined grains, low-energy beverages, and tomatoes. For detailed construction methods, please refer to the original literature [15].

In this study, participants were included if they had completed at least one dietary assessment cycle. For those who engaged in multiple assessment, the average intake of each food and beverage item was calculated based on their repeated reports. Subsequently, we calculated the DII and EDIP indices for each participant using the standardized computational approach. A higher score denotes a greater pro-inflammatory dietary profile, whereas a lower score signifies a more anti-inflammatory dietary profile.

Association between dietary inflammatory and Long COVID

We reported the baseline characteristics of COVID-19 patients grouped by DII/EDIP quartiles (continuous variables: mean ± standard deviation; categorical variables: frequency/percentage). Cox proportional hazards model was used to assess the association between dietary inflammation indices (DII and EDIP) and Long COVID (hazard ratio [HR] and 95% confidence interval [CI]), with a follow-up period from infection to outcome/death/cut-off, whichever occurred first. Two adjusted models were used: Model 1 (adjusted for age at infection and sex) and Model 2 (further adjusted for BMI, ethnicity, education, smoking, energy intake, physical activity, and TDI). Scores were analyzed as continuous variables (higher scores indicate greater inflammatory potential) and quartiles (Q1 as reference). The trend test was conducted using the median-based scoring method, where the median value of each quartile group was treated as a continuous variable in the regression model to calculate the P value for trend. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using the Schoenfeld test.

We used restricted cubic spline (5 nodes: 5th, 35th, 50th, 65th, and 95th centiles) to analyze the nonlinear relationship between dietary inflammatory indices and Long COVID [39, 40]. Guided by prior research, the indices were categorized into high and low groups based on their median values, and Cox regression models were applied separately to estimate the hazard ratio associated with each one-unit increment in DII or EDIP [41]. Furthermore, we utilized restricted cubic splines with three knots to model the associations between dietary inflammatory indices and long COVID, with analyses stratified by sex and age groups (< 65/≥65 years). All analyses were based on fully adjusted models, with nonlinearity validated via likelihood ratio tests.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted four sensitivity analyses to validate the robustness of the primary results: (1) including only participants who completed ≥ 2 dietary assessments to enhance the stability of DII/EDIP scores; (2) extending the washout period from 1 year to 2 years to more strictly exclude pre-existing conditions prior to COVID-19; (3) adjusting the Long COVID definition window from 30 days post-infection to 60 days (T0 + 60d to April 30, 2022), aligning with current guideline standards [42]; (4) excluding individuals who died during the follow-up period without experiencing the outcome event, thereby mitigating potential overestimation of outcome risks due to death as a competing event.

All statistical analyses and data visualization in this study were performed in RStudio (version 4.2.0). All hypothesis tests were performed using two-sided tests, with a P-value < 0.05 set as the threshold for statistical significance.

Data resource and availability

In this study, the UK Biobank dataset used is not publicly accessible but can be obtained through application (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/). All participants provided informed written consent to take part in the study. Ethics approval for the UK Biobank was granted by the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee in 2006 and was updated regularly after that (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/learn-more-about-uk-biobank/about-us/ethics). This study was conducted after approval by the UK Biobank under application reference 657,275.

Participants in higher quartiles of both DII and EDIP generally exhibited less favorable baseline profiles, including younger age at SARS-CoV-2 infection, a higher proportion of women, lower educational attainment, a higher prevalence of overweight/obesity, a greater proportion reporting low physical activity, and greater socioeconomic deprivation. Nevertheless, differences between the two indices were observed for smoking status and energy intake. Higher DII quartiles showed a higher proportion of current smoking and a marked decrease in total energy intake, whereas higher EDIP quartiles were characterized by a higher proportion of never smokers and less pronounced variation in total energy intake across groups.

Association between inflammatory diet and Long COVID

In the continuous variable analysis, DII was associated with an increased risk of Long COVID after adjusting for sex and age at infection (Model 1) (HR = 1.02, 95% CI: [1.01; 1.04], p = 0.006). However, after further adjusting for BMI, ethnicity, smoking status, total energy intake, educational attainment, physical activity, and the TDI (Model 2), this association was attenuated (HR = 1.01, 95% CI: [0.99; 1.03], p = 0.344). Analysis by DII quartiles revealed a significant positive trend for Long COVID risk in Model 1 (p for trend = 0.005). Compared with the lowest DII quartile, the highest quartile was associated with a higher risk of Long COVID in Model 1 (HR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.02–1.16, p = 0.007), whereas this association disappeared at Model 2 (Table 2).

In contrast to DII, EDIP remained significantly associated with Long COVID risk after full adjustment. Specifically, each one-unit increase in EDIP was associated with an 8% higher risk in Model 2 (HR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.04–1.13, p < 0.001). Similarly, a significant positive trend was observed across EDIP quartiles (p for trend < 0.001), with the highest quartile showing an increased risk compared with the lowest quartile in the model 2 (HR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.08–1.22, p < 0.001, Table 2).

In Fig. 2, restricted cubic spline models were used to flexibly model and visualize the associations between dietary inflammatory indices and the risk of Long COVID, with the median value of each index set as the reference point. The RCS analyses suggested significant non-linear associations for both DII (p for non-linearity = 0.002) and EDIP (p for non-linearity < 0.001). The DII curve showed a U-shaped pattern, with risk decreasing below the median reference value of 0.40 (HR = 0.96, 95% CI: [0.93, 1.00], p = 0.024) and increasing above this value (HR = 1.06, 95% CI: [1.02, 1.11], p = 0.007). In contrast, EDIP showed a J-shaped pattern, with risk remaining relatively stable below the median reference value of -0.07 and increasing at higher EDIP levels. Each 1-unit increase in EDIP above the median was associated with a higher risk of Long COVID (HR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.15–1.47, p < 0.001; Table 3).

Stratified analysis revealed that these nonlinear relationships differed significantly by age at infection and sex (Supplementary Fig. 1–2). Among females and individuals under 65 years of age, both the DII and EDIP maintained their original nonlinear characteristics. In contrast, among males and those aged 65 years and above, this nonlinear pattern was either not statistically significant or only marginally significant.

Association between inflammatory diet and individual sequelae

We found that DII and EDIP were significantly associated with the risk of multiple predefined systemic diseases. The DII was associated with an increased risk of coagulation, gastrointestinal, kidney, and mental health system diseases in model 1, with HR ranging from 1.05 to 1.11. After further adjustment, DII remained significantly associated with gastrointestinal, kidney and mental health system diseases, with HR ranging from 1.04 to 1.10. (Supplementary Table 4). Compared with the Q1, the higher inflammation groups (Q3 and Q4) were associated with increased risks of gastrointestinal, kidney, and mental health system diseases, with HR ranges of 1.16–1.49 (p for trend < 0.05).

Likewise, EDIP were associated with increased risks of cardiovascular, kidney, mental health, metabolic, and neurologic diseases in Model 1, and in the fully adjusted Model 2, the association remains significant, but mental health sequelae, with HR ranges of 1.12–1.28 (Supplementary Table 4). Compared with the Q1 of EDIP, the Q4 was associated with increased risks of cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, kidney, mental health, metabolic and neurologic system diseases, with HR ranges of 1.17–1.40 (Model 2, p for trend < 0.05; Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 6).

Sensitivity analysis

We obtained essentially similar association results after performing multiple sensitivity analyses, including: (1) restricting the analyses to participants who had completed at least 2 dietary assessments (n = 44,304); (2) excluding patients with any predefined outcome occurring within 2 years prior to infection with a new crown (n = 63,377); (3) excluding patients with any predefined outcome occurring within 60 days after infection with a new crown (n = 69,044 ); and (4) excluding individuals who died during the follow-up period without experiencing the outcome event (n = 68,959) (Supplementary Tables 7–18).

Discussion

In this study, we utilized the data from the UK Biobank cohort to conduct a follow-up study to investigate the relationship between dietary inflammatory potential, assessed by DII and EDIP, and the risk of Long COVID. Our findings indicate that higher dietary inflammatory scores are associated with an elevated risk of Long COVID. Specifically, participants in the highest quartile (most pro-inflammatory) of DII/EDIP scores showed a significantly higher risk compared to those in the lowest quartile (most anti-inflammatory). Among the predefined multisystem sequelae, those such as the cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, kidney, and mental health system showed significantly elevated risks. Furthermore, dose–response analyses suggested non-linear associations between dietary inflammatory indices and Long COVID risk.

This investigation revealed that both dietary inflammatory indices exhibit significant nonlinear relationships with Long COVID risk, this evidence suggests that an excessively anti-inflammatory dietary pattern may not confer additional protective benefits, whereas a pro-inflammatory diet significantly elevates the risk of developing Long COVID. This finding is consistent with previous research suggesting that chronic low-grade inflammation may be an important factor in the pathogenesis of Long COVID [43]. Sex and age-stratified analyses revealed that the aforementioned nonlinear association was more pronounced in women and individuals under 65 years of age, whereas this pattern was weaker in men and individuals aged 65 years and older. Possible reasons include gender differences in immune responses [44], with estrogen potentially exerting effects through the regulation of inflammatory pathways [45]; age-related immune senescence (inflammaging), with chronic inflammation in older populations potentially masking diet-specific effects [46].

The results of our multisystem analysis demonstrate a high degree of consistency with existing evidence regarding the association between dietary inflammatory scores and chronic diseases. Specifically, existing review studies indicated that pro-inflammatory diet assessed using nutrition-based dietary inflammation indices are associated with increased risks of cardiovascular disease and poor mental health, while associations with other metabolic-related diseases and neurological disorders are weaker or controversial [47]. Notably, our findings further support the association between DII scores and increased risk of kidney diseases (such as chronic kidney disease), consistent with previous studies [48]. Although direct evidence linking DII to hematological diseases is currently lacking, studies suggest that adhering to low-inflammatory potential dietary patterns (such as the Mediterranean diet) can reduce inflammation levels and coagulation abnormalities in healthy populations [49], indirectly supporting our findings.

Additionally, multiple cohort studies have found that higher EDIP scores based on food groups are associated with increased risks of kidney disease [50], cardiovascular disease [19, 37], type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [51], non-alcoholic fatty liver disease and cirrhosis [52], and inflammatory bowel disease [53]. Although some studies have suggested that pro-inflammatory diet may be associated with sarcopenia [47, 54], our analysis did not find a significant association between DII or EDIP and musculoskeletal diseases. This discrepancy may be attributed to the small number of musculoskeletal disease patients in this study (only 177 cases), resulting in insufficient statistical power, rather than a true lack of association.

In summary, our findings suggest a non-linear association between dietary inflammatory potential and Long COVID risk, with higher inflammatory dietary patterns, particularly those captured by EDIP, being associated with increased risk. This study extends the application of dietary inflammatory indices to Long COVID-related outcomes and is consistent with previous evidence linking pro-inflammatory diets to chronic disease risk. Further research is warranted to confirm these findings and explore their potential implications for dietary prevention strategies.

Notably, while both DII and EDIP assess dietary inflammatory potential, only EDIP exhibited statistically significant associations with both the composite Long COVID outcome and a greater number of organ-system-specific sequelae in the fully adjusted model. This divergence may reflect their different constructions: DII is largely nutrient- and component-based, whereas EDIP is a food-based dietary pattern score that may better capture the combined inflammatory effects of overall dietary patterns. For a heterogeneous, multisystem outcome such as Long COVID-related sequelae, this pattern-based approach may be more biologically relevant. In contrast, only 31 of the original 45 DII parameters were available in the UK Biobank, which may have limited the ability of DII to fully capture dietary inflammatory potential. Consistent with these construct differences, higher DII and EDIP quartiles showed overlapping but not identical baseline profiles, particularly in smoking status and energy intake, suggesting that the two indices may reflect partly distinct dietary–lifestyle patterns.

The precise biological mechanisms linking pro-inflammatory diet with Long COVID pathogenesis remain incompletely characterized. However, existing evidence suggests that oxidative stress and systemic chronic inflammation may play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of Long COVID [55–57]. In the elderly population, this chronic inflammatory state is partly due to the cellular aging process, characterized by the sustained release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This mechanism is believed to be closely related to the onset of various chronic diseases such as T2DM, CVD, and respiratory diseases [56]. At the molecular level, multiple inflammation-related biomarkers and transcription factors, including high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), nuclear factor κB (NF-κB), signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), vascular endothelial growth factor, and prostate-specific antigen, have been shown to be involved in the pathophysiological processes of various chronic diseases [57]. These findings provide theoretical support for understanding how pro-inflammatory diet may influence the risk of developing Long COVID through systemic inflammatory pathways. However, how inflammatory dietary patterns specifically regulate these molecular pathways to alter disease risk remains to be elucidated through more in-depth mechanistic studies. Future research should focus on exploring the precise interactive mechanisms between dietary inflammatory potential, immune regulation, and oxidative stress responses, with the aim of providing more targeted intervention strategies for the prevention and treatment of Long COVID.

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective study to examine the association between dietary inflammatory potential, assessed via both the DII and EDIP, and the risk of Long COVID. The main strengths of this study include: (1) the use of the UK Biobank cohort data, which is a large, population-based UK Biobank cohort with long-term follow-up; (2) our cases were confirmed through detailed medical record reviews; (3) the use of two widely validated scores, the DII and EDIP, to assess dietary inflammatory potential from different dimensions, providing comprehensive and complementary results; (4) After considering the impact of the number of dietary assessments and potential reverse causality, we conducted multiple sensitivity analyses, and the main results remain robust. This study also has certain limitations. First, since the DII index used in this study only covers 31 food parameters, it may not fully reflect an individual’s dietary inflammatory potential, thereby underestimating their inflammatory risk. To address this limitation, we further conducted repeated analyses using the EDIP and observed more significant association results. Second, the dietary data used to derive DII and EDIP were collected between 2009 and 2012, several years before SARS-CoV-2 infections occurred. The analysis rests on the assumption that baseline dietary patterns reflect long-term habitual intake, yet these assessments may not fully capture dietary habits at the time of infection. Substantial dietary changes during follow-up could result in non-differential exposure misclassification, which typically biases effect estimates toward the null. Third, individuals who were actually ill but undiagnosed may have been incorrectly classified as healthy, potentially underestimating the observed associations. Fourth, the primary outcome in this study was defined as a composite phenotype of multi-system post-COVID-19 sequelae rather than clinically diagnosed Long COVID. Although this definition enabled the assessment of a broader post-acute disease burden in the study cohort, it may have captured heterogeneous conditions with distinct underlying mechanisms, thereby limiting the direct extrapolation of our findings to clinically diagnosed Long COVID. Finally, caution should be exercised when generalizing these results, as the study cohort was primarily composed of older adults of British white origin.

Conclusions

The findings of this study suggest that a more pro‑inflammatory diet, as measured by both the DII and EDIP, is associated with an increased risk of Long COVID. Furthermore, we observed that this dietary pattern may exacerbate the risk of multi‑organ sequelae, particularly affecting the cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, kidney, and mental health systems. These findings suggest that optimizing dietary patterns to reduce pro-inflammatory potential may help mitigate the risk of Long COVID.
