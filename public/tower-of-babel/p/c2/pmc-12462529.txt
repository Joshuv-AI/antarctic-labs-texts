Independent Effects of Axial Length and Intraocular Pressure on the Highly Myopic Optic Nerve Head
Authors: Maverick Wenhao Wong, Crystal Chong, Swati Sharma, Lee Ching Phang, Samantha Lor, Quan V. Hoang, Michael Girard, Ching-yu Cheng, Leopold Schmetterer, Jost B. Jonas, Hongli Yang, Ya Xing Wang, Jin Wook Jeoung, Rachel S. Chong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to investigate associations among intraocular pressure (IOP), axial length (AXL), and optic nerve head (ONH) characteristics in highly myopic eyes.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study included highly myopic eyes with a refractive error of ≤–6.0 diopters (D) or an AXL of ≥26 mm, without glaucomatous optic neuropathy. Using IOP profiles, obtained retrospectively over a 5-year period, we assessed maximum, median, and range of IOP readings. We performed cross-sectional measurements of morphometric ONH parameters on fundus photographs and optical coherence tomographic (OCT) images.ResultsThe study included 201 eyes (134 individuals) with a mean AXL of 27.6 ± 1.4 mm and a mean maximum IOP of 16.6 ± 3.7 millimeters of mercury (mm Hg; median = 14.9 ± 2.5 mm Hg, range = 3.14 ± 3.13 mm Hg obtained in 5.25 ± 2.76 measurements). Longer AXL was associated (multivariable analysis) with higher disc ovality (β = 0.05, P < 0.001), longer disc-fovea distance (β = 0.22, P < 0.001), shallower lamina cribrosa depth (β = −20.5, P = 0.02), and thicker ganglion cell layer and retinal nerve fiber layer in the temporal/inferotemporal sectors (all P < 0.05) with adjustments for age and gender. Higher mean maximum IOP readings were linked to deeper lamina cribrosa depth (β = 6.03, P = 0.006) and reduced disc rotation angle (β = −0.31, P = 0.04); higher mean median IOP was associated with smaller Bruch’s membrane opening area (β = −0.06, P = 0.02). AXL was inversely associated with mean median IOP (β = −0.19, P = 0.02).ConclusionsHigher IOP and longer AXL are associated with distinct structural changes of the ONH in highly myopic eyes, particularly involving the lamina cribrosa. Elevated IOP but not AXL was associated with ONH features characteristic of glaucoma.

By 2050, almost half of the global population is projected to be myopic, with up to 10% having high myopia (HM).1 HM, typically defined as a refractive error of <−6.00 diopters (D) or an axial length (AXL) of ≥26 mm, is associated with various structural changes of the eye, particularly with alterations of the size and shape of the optic nerve head (ONH).2 Multiple epidemiological investigations and hospital-based studies have reported high prevalence of glaucomatous or glaucoma-like optic nerve damage in highly myopic eyes, identifying HM as an associated risk factor for the presence and progression of glaucomatous optic neuropathy (GON).3,4 Although studies have shown that myopic eyes are more likely to develop glaucoma, the underlying mechanisms driving this increased susceptibility remain unknown.

Like myopia, glaucoma is a chronic condition that involves progressive remodeling of the ONH, specifically at the lamina cribrosa. These changes may be associated with mechanical insult and impairment of retinal ganglion cell axonal transport.5 Raised intraocular pressure (IOP) plays an essential role in the pathological remodeling of the ONH in glaucoma.6 Apart from the translaminar pressure gradient that creates an anterior-posterior stress across the lamina cribrosa, elevated IOP is thought to cause an in-wall circumferential hoop stress that may stretch the scleral canal open, leading to thinning of the lamina cribrosa.7 These forces may transmit mechanical strain to the lamina cribrosa, contributing to the development of GON.

The association between IOP and myopic optic disc remodeling has not been well established. A retrospective study investigating the association between IOP and GON in myopic eyes found that glaucoma was associated with elevated IOP in myopic eyes with an AXL of ≤27.4 mm.8 Surprisingly, in more highly myopic eyes (defined in that study as an AXL of ≥27.5 mm), the relationship between GON and IOP was less clear. This observation raises the question of whether optic nerve damage observed in highly myopic eyes with glaucoma is purely glaucomatous, that is, secondary to the effects of IOP, or due to a different form of myopia-associated neuropathy.

Previous studies have documented associations between AXL elongation and ONH morphometric changes, including disc ovality, temporal retinal nerve fiber layer thickening, and elongation of the disc-fovea distance.9–11 However, most of these studies did not account for longitudinal IOP exposure, or excluded highly myopic eyes without glaucoma. Our study aims to build upon this literature by modeling the independent effects of chronic IOP profiles and AXL in a non-glaucomatous HM cohort, using detailed morphometric analysis of the ONH and 5-year IOP data. We hypothesized that highly myopic eyes show distinctive features of an ONH remodeling that are independent of the effects of IOP exposure. These findings may aid the classification of IOP- versus myopia-associated optic neuropathy in HM, and provide insight into how HM influences glaucoma susceptibility.

Methods

This retrospective cross-sectional study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human participants. Ethical approval was obtained from the SingHealth Centralized Institutional Review Board (2022/SHS/1736), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Individuals were recruited from clinics at the Singapore National Eye Centre between 2021 and 2023. All subjects underwent standardized ophthalmic examinations, including measurement of best corrected visual acuity, auto-refractometry and keratometry (Canon RK5; Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan), measurement of AXL (IOL Master V3.01; Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany), tonometry by Goldmann applanation tonometry (GAT) and dilated fundus examination by ophthalmologists at a single time point. Using clinical records, we obtained the GAT IOP readings made in the previous 5 years. We calculated the median, maximum, and range of the IOP measurements for each eye, with the IOP range defined as the difference between the maximum and minimum IOP values. Central cornea thickness (CCT; IOL Master V3.01; Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany) was measured at the time of optical coherence tomographic (OCT) acquisition.

Inclusion criteria were a myopic refractive error of a spherical equivalent of ≥−6.0 diopters (D) or an AXL of ≥26.0 mm12; absence of category 3 or worse myopic maculopathy or plus signs, following the Meta-Analysis for Pathologic Myopia (META-PM) Study Group13 classifications; no previous ocular surgery including kerato-refractive procedures, and absence of other ocular or neurological diseases including glaucoma (i.e. glaucoma hemifield test “within normal limits” for 5 years). Participants were excluded if they had any history of IOP-lowering therapy or evidence of glaucomatous optic neuropathy, defined by focal neuroretinal rim thinning upon clinical examination or on OCT images and/or abnormal glaucoma hemifield test on standard automated perimetry at any time point during the 5-year observation period.

Colored Fundus Photography Assessment of Optic Disc Morphology

Fundus photographs were taken using a retinal camera (Triton; TRC.NW8, Topcon Inc.) following pharmacological pupil dilation with tropicamide 1% eye drops (Mydracil; Alcon Laboratories Inc., Fort Worth, TX, USA). We determined the longest and shortest chords going through the optic disc centroid (Fig. 1). The disc ovality index was defined as the longest chord distance divided by shortest chord distance through the optic disc centroid.14,15 The optic disc-fovea distance was defined as the distance between the fovea and optic disc centroid.9,16,17 The optic disc rotation angle (RA) was the deviation of the longest chord through the optic disc centroid, from the vertical meridian (a line perpendicular to the disc-fovea axis; see Fig. 1).15

Optical Coherence Tomographic Image Acquisition and Processing

Cirrus spectral-domain OCT (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Inc.) imaging with 6 mm × 6 mm optic disc cube scans was performed following pupil dilation. Sub-quality images with a signal strength of <7, movement or mirror artifacts, inaccurate segmentation, and with an inconsistent signal intensity across the scan were excluded from the analysis. We utilized Reflectivity 3.2 software (Abyss Processing, Singapore), which leverages on artificial intelligence and specific algorithms to enhance and automatically segment three-dimensional raster volumetric OCT scans of the ONH (Fig. 2). The software specifically labeled the termination of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)/Bruch's membrane complex to determine the area of Bruch's membrane opening (BMO); the OCT-visible part of the lamina cribrosa to determine the global shape index (GSI), curvature, and depth of the lamina cribrosa in relation to the BMO plane. The GSI is a single quantitative metric ranging from −1 to 1 that describes the overall three-dimensional curvature of the anterior lamina cribrosa surface – where −1 indicates a fully concave or “cupped” shape, 0 corresponds to a saddle shape, and 1 indicates a fully convex or “domed” shape.18 Pre-laminar (i.e. the tissue layer anterior to the lamina cribrosa) depth, thickness, volume, and scleral angle were measured in the area within the OCT-visible portion of the peripapillary sclera (the region extending from the inner scleral canal wall to the outermost tissue margin visible within the scan window). This included the scleral flange and adjacent pre-laminar tissue. The scleral angle was defined as the angle between parallel lines to the nasal and temporal anterior borders of the peripapillary sclera, reflecting whether the anterior scleral surface followed a V-shaped (positive angle) or inverted V-shaped (negative angle) configuration.19 Measurements of the following were derived from equal octants around the ONH, as well as the total sum of all measurements: ganglion cell complex thickness (GCCT),20,21 the minimum neuroretinal rim width (MRW), defined as the shortest distance between the BMO border and the inner limiting membrane; retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness; and choroidal thickness, similarly measured in the peripapillary region from ONH cube scans.20,21 The posterior boundaries of the sclera and lamina cribrosa were not detectable in the majority of the scans, and, therefore, could not be reliably segmented.

Correction for ocular magnification was performed applying Littmann's formula (s_actual = p x q x s) as modified by Bennett.22 The “p” represents the magnification factor of the camera, that is, 3.382 for the Cirrus SD-OCT; “q” represents the AXL-adjusted ocular magnification 0.01306 × (AXL = 1.82); and “s” is the measurement derived directly from the scan image and “s_actual” is the actual measurement after applying the Littmann-Bennett correction.

Statistical Analysis

In the statistical analyses, we analyzed potential associations among the IOP parameters, AXL and ONH parameters, first in a univariable model and followed by multivariable regression analyses with generalized estimating equation models. We reported continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation. We fitted univariable and multivariable regression with generalized estimating equation models to evaluate associations between ONH parameters and maximum, median and range of IOP, or AXL. All statistical tests were performed using R software language (R version 3.5.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

The study included 201 HM eyes (134 individuals; 55.9% were female patients and 98.0% were of Chinese descent) without pathologic myopia and without glaucoma. The mean age was 55.9 ± 12.6 years, and mean AXL was 27.6 ± 1.4 mm. A mean of 5.25 ± 2.76 IOP measurements had been taken over 5 years per eye. The mean maximum IOP was 16.6 ± 3.7 mm Hg, mean median IOP was 14.9 ± 2.53 mm Hg, and the IOP range was 3.14 ± 3.13 mm Hg (Table 1). The mean CCT in our cohort was 556.43 ± 38.43 µm.

Univariable Analysis of AXL, Maximum IOP, Median IOP, and IOP Range with ONH Parameters

Univariable analysis showed significant associations among longer AXL and higher optic disc ovality, longer disc-fovea distance, larger BMO area, thicker temporal and inferotemporal GCCT and RNFL thickness, and temporal MRW, and smaller lamina cribrosa depth (all P < 0.05; Supplementary Table S1). Higher maximum IOP was associated with decreased optic disc rotation, deeper lamina cribrosa, and pre-laminar depth (all P < 0.05; Supplementary Table S2). Higher median IOP correlated with shorter disc-fovea distance, smaller BMO area, and deeper lamina cribrosa depth (all P < 0.05; Supplementary Table S3). The IOP range did not show associations with any of the ONH parameters measured (Supplementary Table S4).

Results of Multivariable Analysis

Axial Length

With adjustments made for age, gender, and maximum IOP, longer AXL remained to be significantly associated with increased disc ovality (β = 0.05, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.02 to 0.07, P < 0.001), longer disc-fovea distance (β = 0.22, 95% CI = 0.18 to 0.26, P < 0.001), and reduced lamina cribrosa depth (β = −20.5, 95% CI = −38.0 to −3.10, P = 0.02; Table 2). After adjusting for age, gender, median IOP, and IOP range, longer AXL remained to be significantly associated with increased disc ovality (β = 0.04, 95% CI = 0.02 to 0.07, P < 0.001), longer disc-fovea distance (β = 0.22, 95% CI = 0.18 to 0.26, P < 0.001), and reduced lamina cribrosa depth (β = −19.8, 95% CI = −37.6 to −1.98, P = 0.03; Table 3).

When adjusted for age, gender, and maximum IOP (see Table 2), longer AXL was associated with thickening of the GCC in the temporal (β = 3.74, 95% CI = 0.60 to 6.87, P = 0.02) and inferotemporal (β = 4.53, 95% CI = 0.45 to 8.61, P = 0.03) regions, and thickening of the RNFL in the temporal (β = 4.51, 95% CI = 1.64 to 7.38, P < 0.001) and inferotemporal (β = 5.40, 95% CI = 1.04 to 9.76, P = 0.02) regions. In Table 3, when adjusted for age, gender, median IOP, and IOP range, longer AXL was similarly associated with thickening of the GCC in the temporal (β = 3.71, 95% CI = 0.57 to 6.86, P = 0.02) and inferotemporal (β = 4.49, 95% CI = 0.24 to 8.75, P = 0.04) regions, and thickening of the RNFL in the temporal (β = 4.59, 95% CI = 1.77 to 7.41, P < 0.001) and inferotemporal (β = 5.39, 95% CI = 0.85 to 9.93, P = 0.02) regions.

Maximum IOP

We found that a higher maximum IOP was significantly associated with decreased RA (β = −0.35, 95% CI = −0.64 to −0.06, P = 0.02), increased lamina cribrosa depth (β = 6.03, 95% CI = 1.27 to 10.8, P = 0.01) and increased pre-laminar depth (β = 7.33, 95% CI = 1.64 to 13.0, P = 0.01; see Supplementary Table S2). After adjusting for age, gender, and AXL (see Table 2), increasing maximum IOP was still found to be significantly associated with a decreased RA (β = −0.31, 95% CI = −0.61 to −0.02, P = 0.04), increased lamina cribrosa depth (β = 6.03, 95% CI = 1.74 to 10.3, P = 0.006) and increased pre-laminar depth (β = 7.91, 95% CI = 2.53 to 13.3, P = 0.004).

Median IOP

Higher median IOP was associated with decreased disc-fovea distance (DFD; β = −0.03, 95% CI = −0.05 to −0.001, P = 0.04), decreased BMO area (β = −0.06, 95% CI = −0.12 to −0.01, P = 0.02), and increased lamina cribrosa depth (β = 7.90, 95% CI = 0.88 to 14.9, P = 0.03; see Supplementary Table S3). However, when adjusted for age, gender, AXL, and IOP range (see Table 3), the BMO area remained the only parameter reflecting a consistently significant negative association with median IOP (β = −0.06, 95% CI = −0.11 to −0.003, P = 0.04). Maximum IOP was not adjusted for, due to high collinearity (>0.7) with median and range of IOP.

Age

Using multivariable analysis (see Table 2), after adjusting for AXL, maximum IOP, and gender, older age was significantly associated with thinning of the GCCT in the inferotemporal region (β = −0.53, 95% CI = −0.97 to −0.09, P = 0.02), and MRW in the temporal region (β = −1.09, 95% CI = −1.73 to −0.46, P < 0.001). Similarly, in Table 3, after adjustment for AXL, gender, median IOP, and IOP range, increasing age remained significantly associated with thinning of the GCCT in the inferotemporal region (β = −0.53, 95% CI = −0.96 to −0.10, P = 0.02), and MRW in the temporal region (β = −1.15, 95% CI = −1.77 to −0.52, P < 0.001).

In Table 2, after adjusting for AXL, maximum IOP, and gender, multivariable analysis showed that older age was significantly associated with increased ovality index (OI; β = 0.0028, 95% CI = −0.00010 to 0.0054, P = 0.04). However, this association was not statistically significant in Table 3 after adjusting for AXL, gender, median IOP, and IOP range (β = 0.0022, 95% CI = −0.0006 to 0.0049, P = 0.13).

Gender

Multivariable analysis adjusting for AXL, maximum IOP, and age (see Table 2) demonstrated that male gender was significantly associated with decreased OI (β = −0.13, 95% CI = −0.19 to −0.06, P < 0.001) and reduced DFD (β = −0.13, 95% CI = −0.25 to −0.02, P = 0.02). In Table 3, multivariable analysis adjusting for AXL, median IOP, IOP range, and age, male gender was similarly found to be associated with a deceased OI (β = −0.13, 95% CI = −0.19 to −0.06, P < 0.001) and reduced DFD (β = −0.13, 95% CI = −0.25 to 0.02, P = 0.02).

Regression Analysis of Associations Bween AXL and IOP

Additional regression analysis was performed to examine associations between AXL and maximum, median, and range of IOP. Interestingly, we found that a longer AXL was significantly associated with decreased median IOP (β = −0.19, 95% CI = −0.35 to −0.04, P = 0.02). There was no association between AXL with maximum IOP or IOP range.

Discussion

In this hospital-based study, longer AXL was associated with an increased optic disc ovality, longer DFD, thicker GCC and RNFL in the temporal and inferotemporal regions, and a shallower lamina cribrosa after adjustments for age and gender. In contrast, higher IOP was associated with a deeper lamina cribrosa depth in the multivariable analysis. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report associations between chronic IOP profile and ONH tissue morphology in highly myopic eyes.

One of our most notable findings was that increasing AXL and elevated IOP had opposing effects on lamina cribrosa depth. Whereas IOP elevation is associated with deepening or posterior displacement of the lamina cribrosa23 – a pathognomonic feature of glaucoma, even in normal-tension glaucoma – our study found that longer AXL was associated with a shallower lamina cribrosa. This observation suggests that in the absence of an IOP elevation, axial elongation in HM may not predispose individuals to glaucoma through a deepening of the lamina cribrosa. These findings may be helpful to better understand the mechanisms of ONH remodeling in highly myopic eyes, where the interplay between biomechanical stress and ocular geometry may create a myopia-specific type of optic neuropathy.

Our findings on associations between AXL and IOP on RA and BMO area further highlight the complexity of ONH remodeling in myopic eyes under chronic IOP elevation. Clinical studies on associations between IOP and BMO area are limited – however, primate models of acute IOP elevation showed no change or an increase in BMO area.24 In the cross-sectional analysis of our study, we observed a decrease in BMO area associated with higher median IOP after adjusting for confounders. This discrepancy may be related to different levels of intraorbital cerebrospinal fluid pressure in the primate model of ocular hypertension as compared to our clinical cohort,25 although associations between the cerebrospinal fluid pressure and HM remain poorly explored. Increased RA is usually associated with myopia15; however, our study found no clear association with AXL in our cohort. Interestingly, the inverse relationship between increasing IOP and RA, independent of AXL, suggests distinct remodeling patterns associated with chronic IOP fluctuations that may act independently from those associated with AXL elongation.

The observed correlations of longer AXL with increased OI and greater DFD concur with previous studies,9,10,26–29 reinforcing the notion of ONH tilt and DFD elongation as prominent features of myopia. Furthermore, thickening of GCC and RNFL after correcting for ocular magnification in the inferior and inferotemporal regions in highly myopic eyes align with other studies11,30–34 and may be related to crowding of the retinal ganglion cell layers in these regions due to a decrease in the angle between the temporal superior arterial arcade and the temporal inferior arterial arcade with greater axial elongation.35 However, none of these observations provide a plausible explanation for the heightened risk of glaucoma in HM. Therefore, we sought to analyze myopic ONH appearance in the context of long-term IOP exposure, which was modeled independently using 5-year longitudinal data; and excluded individuals with glaucoma.

Age and gender were also found to be associated with the ONH morphology. Older age was significantly associated with thinning of the GCCT in the inferotemporal region and MRW in the temporal region, consistent with age-related neuroretinal tissue loss.36,37 Although older age was also associated with an increase in OI in one of our multivariable analysis models, this relationship was not replicated after adjusting for confounding factors. Male gender was significantly associated with a smaller OI, in agreement with previous studies reporting gender-related differences in disc ovality.38 In contrast, the observed association between male sex and reduced DFD has not been widely reported in the literature and may represent a novel anatomic finding. Further studies are warranted to confirm this relationship and explore its potential clinical implications for ONH susceptibility to AXL or IOP-related stress.

We also found a significant association between longer AXL and lower median IOP. Similar observations have been described in previous studies39,40 including a report suggesting that Schlemm's canal size is larger in HM,41 which may reflect enhanced aqueous outflow in eyes with longer AXL. Our findings differ from previous reports from population-based studies suggesting positive associations between AXL and IOP.42,43 However, in contrast to our study, these studies were typically cross-sectional and performed using non-contact tonometry without exclusion of pathologic myopia or glaucoma. Further research to explore mechanistic interactions between scleral and trabecular meshwork biomechanics is needed to determine their relationship in HM. Setting normative IOP targets in highly myopic eyes is challenging – but remains a key aspect of managing glaucoma risk in these patients. Our findings raise the hypothesis that longer eyes may be more susceptible to IOP-related stress, and this warrants further investigation in prospective, longitudinal studies.

This study has several notable strengths. First, the study benefits from a large cohort of older patients with healthy eyes and chronic IOP profile data spanning from the last 5 years. This focus makes the results potentially relevant to clinical contexts where age-related ocular changes, such as glaucoma and pathologic myopia, are a significant concern. Second, the study also benefited from rigorous participant selection criteria. By excluding patients who had undergone kerato-refractive surgery, we minimized potential biases arising from altered corneal curvature or thickness, ensuring more accurate measurements of IOP. However, we acknowledge that age and corneal curvature may contribute to marginal inaccuracies in GAT-measured IOP that have not been accounted for in this study.44 Last, we performed multivariable regression analyses with multiple adjustment models for confounding factors in order to reduce bias in our analysis.

Several limitations of our study should be taken into account. First, although CCT measurements were available at the time of OCT imaging in our study, CCT was not measured concurrently with IOP in the preceding 5-year period. CCT-based correction formulae for IOP have been proposed previously, although their utility in Asian populations remains unclear.45 Future studies that evaluate the contributions of IOP to ONH remodeling should nonetheless include careful consideration of corneal biomechanical properties that may influence applanation tonometry readings, particularly in HM eyes.46 Second, the use of fundus photography instead of OCT imaging may have introduced inaccuracies in determining disc ovality and rotation angle. For instance, in HM eyes with longer AXL, measurements based on fundus photographs may overestimate the OI and underestimate the area of BMO and optic disc due to perspective distortion and the non-perpendicular alignment of the BMO to the optical axis.47 Therefore, we acknowledge that although ocular magnification corrections based on AXL were applied, these may not fully account for image distortion resulting from peripheral stretching or tilting effects as compared to OCT imaging of the ONH. Third, we did not assess deeper ONH structures such as the anterior scleral canal opening (ASCO) due to poor visibility of the anterior sclera; or perform optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA). We also determined IOP profiles based on retrospective data collection. Irregularly timed IOP measurements or missing data may have affected the robustness of our findings. Fourth, the cross-sectional nature of our analysis precludes definitive causal inferences. Further prospective studies incorporating detailed biomechanical and vascular assessments are warranted to confirm and expand upon our findings. Finally, although multiple statistical comparisons were performed in this study, we did not apply a Bonferroni correction. This decision was based on the exploratory nature of our analyses and the inter-relatedness of the ONH parameters studied. As outlined by Armstrong et al.,48 such correction is most appropriate when (1) a single global null hypothesis is being tested, (2) type I errors must be strictly avoided, and (3) a large number of unplanned comparisons are conducted. As our analyses were hypothesis-driven and exploratory, applying a Bonferroni correction could increase the risk of type II errors and obscure potentially meaningful associations.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that IOP-related and myopia-induced ONH remodeling appear to be independent processes in HM eyes. Consistent with reported findings in non-HM eyes, HM eyes with higher IOP exhibited lamina cribrosa deepening that is a hallmark feature of glaucoma. This underscores the importance of IOP management in guiding glaucoma treatment strategies for patients with HM, and further validates genetic studies that support the critical role of IOP in glaucoma development in myopia.49 Conversely, our findings did not indicate strong associations between elevated IOP and features of myopic ONH remodeling, suggesting these changes may occur independently of IOP. Our study highlights the complex interplay between IOP and HM, and describes key observations that may enable clearer structural phenotyping of the non-glaucomatous myopic ONH. Establishing a normative IOP range in HM remains a crucial aspect of future studies.
