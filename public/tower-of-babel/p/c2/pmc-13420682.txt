Systemic venous anomalies are often associated with other congenital diseases, and isolated venous malformation is a rare condition.1 The overall prevalence of venous anomalies accounts for 1%, and severity of symptoms correlates with the surrounding structures and size of the lesion. During adolescence, 75% of these malformations may progress.2

The bilateral absence of the superior vena cava (SVC) is associated with other cardiac malformations and is a unique anomaly. SVC obstruction may lead to increased venous blood pressure, resulting in dilation of collateral vessels.3,4 Because of its nonspecific clinical manifestation, this anomaly is usually diagnosed accidentally during interventions that need access to the venous system, such as central venous catheterization, pacemaker implantation, and cannulation in cardiac operations.5,6 As a result, its diagnosis before these interventions and cardiac and hepatic operations is needed to prevent life-threatening complications.7 Herein, a rare case of absent SVC diagnosed during gross hematuria evaluation was reported along with a discussion on therapeutic and follow-up considerations.

Case Presentation

An illustrative pediatric case is presented to contextualize the clinical and diagnostic challenges associated with BASVC. A 9-year-old girl was admitted because of gross hematuria and suprapubic pain to the clinic. She did not have any specific disease, recent trauma, or a family history of urogenital disease and did not consume any noticeable medication or food. Suprapubic pain started 1 month before her admission, and when the patient's mother noticed the color of her urine accidentally, she was admitted to the clinic. After confirming her hematuria with microscopy and considering urinary tract infection improbable, she was referred to a urologist.

Physical examination revealed no abnormality in the eyes, skin, genitalia, or joints and no signs of edema or organomegaly, and her blood pressure was in the normal range. On physical examination, there was no visible neck vein distension, jugular venous engorgement, or abnormal superficial venous collateral veins in the cervical or thoracic regions.

Laboratory evaluations revealed no abnormality in the coagulation profile as well as in the complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel, and liver function tests. Urine re-examination also revealed no evidence of red blood cell casts, dysmorphic red blood cells, and crystalluria and showed normal acidity. Renal (kidneys, ureters, and bladder) ultrasonography was performed, which revealed no urinary structural defects but detected a suspected 42 × 37 mm cystic lesion with turbulent blood flow adjacent to the left renal hilum; as a result, the radiologist suggested computed tomography angiography with arterial and venous contrast phases and referred her with suspicion of abdominal aortic aneurysm to a vascular surgeon. Computed tomography angiography (Figure 1) revealed the drainage of the subclavian and brachiocephalic veins into the azygos and accessory hemiazygos veins and then the drainage of these 2 vessels (azygos and accessory hemiazygos veins) into the hemiazygos vein. A supplementary video demonstrating the computed tomography angiographic reconstruction of the abnormal venous anatomy is provided (Video 1). The hemiazygos vein then joins the left renal vein, and finally it drained into the inferior vena cava, which justifies enlargement of the left renal vein. All available findings were in favor of BASVC. During these investigations, the patient's hematuria gradually resolved. Because of the patient's hematuria and the possibility of increased left renal venous pressure, surgical redirection of the left renal venous drainage was proposed. The case was presented in the national vascular surgeons' group, and therapeutic options were discussed, including changing the venous drainage of the left kidney in a an operation or close follow-up of the patient. Finally, we decided on close follow-up of the patient, as there was no evidence of venous hypertension or kidney parenchymal changes due to venous hypertension.

Embryology, Clinical Presentation, and Diagnostic Considerations

Development of the vascular system initiates in the fourth embryological week, and both SVCs are formed from the supracardinal, cardinal, and anterior cardinal veins from the 7th to the 12th embryological weeks alongside the azygous veins and anastomotic ramus.8,9 Venous blood returning from the arms, head, and upper trunk runs into the left and right anterior cardinal veins. The common cardinal vein originates from where anterior and posterior cardinal veins converge, and it reaches the heart from each side. By the 7th to 8th embryonic week, a precardinal venous anastomosis develops between bilateral anterior cardinal veins, and this anastomosis vein eventually becomes the left brachiocephalic vein. Finally, in the left common cardinal vein, the distal portion degenerates and the proximal portion becomes a coronary sinus. The SVC is composed of the right common cardinal vein and the proximal portion of the anterior cardinal vein.10

Since the first reported case of BASVC in 1981,5 some other cases have been reported worldwide (Table 1). The reasons for visiting medical centers before the diagnosis of this abnormality include a wide range of sign and symptoms, such as syncope attacks, heart murmurs, heart palpitations, dizziness, and chronic cough; some patients were also diagnosed by routine ultrasonography of pregnant mothers' (Table 1). This wide range of presentation makes its diagnosis difficult, which could endanger the patient's life during medical procedures, including pacemaker implantation, and central vein line, Shaldon catheter, and Permcath catheter placement.17,25,26

Herein, we have reported the first case of BASVC that was presented with gross hematuria, although the definite cause of hematuria was not yet discovered in our patient. In our case, both renal and postrenal causes were investigated. Patient evaluation during clinical history taking, clinical examination, laboratory checkup, and other mentioned paraclinical evaluations downplays the possibility of urinary tract infection and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. Further investigations and imaging also ruled out the possibility of Alport syndrome and nutcracker syndrome. The patient did not have a history of consuming cyclophosphamide, aspirin, heparin, penicillin, or other medications.

Increase in venous pressure could result in occult or gross hematuria, which has been reported in many cases of nutcracker syndrome, in which the aorta and superior mesenteric artery compress the left renal vein, leading to pelvic congestion syndrome, hematuria, and abdominal and pelvic pain.27,28 Hematuria in the presented patient may be due to a scenario similar to nutcracker syndrome, while its self-limiting course and lack of pelvic congestion signs argue against this etiology. An important challenge for the presented case is the relationship between hematuria and BASVC. If we encounter a scenario similar to nutcracker syndrome in our patient, she would benefit from the therapeutic strategies used in this syndrome. Nevertheless, a wide range of therapeutic options for nutcracker syndrome, ranging from conservative therapy to surgical and endovascular interventions,29 could be used in the same patient if evidence of renal damage is present.

Although chest and abdominal radiographs were not diagnostic in this patient, BASVC may be suggested by plain radiography because of the absence of the normal right mediastinal SVC contour and the presence of an enlarged azygos shadow. However, these findings are subtle and nonspecific, making cross-sectional imaging essential for definitive diagnosis.30 Thus, we did not request chest and abdominal radiography.

Future Directions

Given the rarity of BASVC, current knowledge is largely derived from isolated case reports and small case series. Future studies should focus on the establishment of multicenter registries to better define the natural history, long-term outcomes, and optimal management strategies for affected patients. Advances in noninvasive imaging may further improve early detection and preprocedural planning. In addition, increased awareness among clinicians and interventionalists is essential to reduce procedure-related complications in patients with unrecognized systemic venous anomalies.

Conclusions

BASVC is a rare congenital anomaly with a challenging diagnosis due to its nonspecific symptoms. Physicians should consider this anomaly during medical and surgical procedures to prevent life-threatening complications. Choosing a suitable diagnostic method and a surgical approach in special medical conditions that require procedures such as liver transplantation, pacemaker implantation, central vein line placement, and other interventions is important in these patients. Finally, we suggest close follow-up of patients for their comorbidities.
