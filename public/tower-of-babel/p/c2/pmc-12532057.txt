Precise Characterization of Individual Microfluidic Droplets Using Laser Diffraction
Authors: Shuzo Masui, Yusuke Kanno, Takasi Nisisako
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The expanding use of microfluidic droplets and particles across disciplines, from biology to materials science, highlights the need for developing precise characterization methods. Conventional particle characterization based on light scattering typically relies on averaged data from multiple particles, which can lead to inaccuracies due to contamination from larger particles. To overcome this issue, we here present a versatile laser diffraction (LD) system for characterizing individual droplets and particles flowing in a poly­(dimethylsiloxane) (PDMS) microfluidic device. Our system, mounted on a commercial inverted microscope, facilitates the simultaneous estimation of both the diameter and the refractive index of microparticles and droplets of size 20–50 μm. The LD system captures the angular distribution of scattered light from individual droplets as they pass through the PDMS microfluidic channels. Validation experiments were performed using liquid paraffin with varying refractive indices, oil-in-water (O/W) and water-in-oil (W/O) droplets, and size-certified polystyrene beads. Results showed high accuracy, with mean diameter estimation errors under 5% and refractive index estimation errors <0.5%. This adaptable characterization system can be combined with various microfluidic systems for droplet and particle generation, mixing, and sorting, offering broad potential for applications in multiple research domains.

Introduction

Over the past two decades, droplet-based microfluidic technologies have been extensively explored across a wide range of fields, from biology to materials science. In these technologies, droplets of uniform size are typically generated one by one in microfluidic channels and then transported to downstream processes, such as biomedical analysis or the fabrication of functional particles. Regardless of the specific application, obtaining accurate information about droplets after their formation, such as size and composition, is essential. Therefore, reliable and precise methods for characterizing droplets in microfluidic flows have become increasingly important for production evaluation and process control in both industrial and academic research.

Multiple techniques, including mechanical, hydrodynamic, electrokinetic, and optical techniques, have been developed for particle characterization. Among these, optical methods are generally preferred because of their noncontact nature, broad measurable size range (from nanometers to millimeters), and high speed and accuracy. These methods typically rely on the temporal and spatial characteristics of light scattering. For example, dynamic light scattering (DLS) estimates the hydrodynamic diameter and size distribution of particles suspended in liquid by analyzing time-resolved scattering intensity obtained from multiple particles to calculate diffusion coefficients. Laser diffraction (LD), in contrast, determines the optical diameter and size distribution of particles by comparing the angular distribution of scattered light with theoretical predictions based on Mie scattering theory, requiring prior knowledge of the refractive indices of both the particles and the dispersion medium. LD is particularly suitable for relatively large particles, ranging from several hundred nanometers to several millimeters. While DLS cannot measure individual particles in isolation due to its reliance on interparticle intereference, LD is theoretically capable of sensing and measuring single particles. However, there have been few reports demonstrating the application of LD for single-particle measurements.

While bright-field optical microscopy can accurately measure the size of individual droplets and particles flowing through microfluidic channels, it cannot determine their internal refractive indexan important indicator of key properties such as solute concentration, the pH of the solution in the gel, and the degree of polymerization in resins. To address this limitation, several studies have demonstrated the potential of opto-microfluidic systems for simultaneous measurement of droplet or particle size and refractive index. Cheong et al. measured the diameter and refractive index of silica, poly­(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA), and polystyrene (PS) particles (1.5–2.0 μm in diameter), as well as oil-in-water (O/W) droplets (1–10 μm), using a digital holographic microscope (DHM). Luo et al. employed a one-dimensional line CCD sensor in a DHM setup to determine the 3D shape and refractive index of water-in-oil (W/O) droplets with widths of 200–400 μm and heights of 40 μm, achieving lateral and vertical resolutions of 0.9 μm and 77 nm, respectively. However, interferometric systems such as DHM typically involve high computational costs and require complex and sensitive optical configurations with multiple objective lenses and optical paths.

Meanwhile, Dannhauser et al. developed a microfluidic LD system using a PMMA-based measuring chamber to estimate the diameter and refractive index of size-certified PS beads (0.9–4.0 μm), achieving diameter estimation errors of <7% and refractive index errors of <0.25%. Despite this progress, to the best of our knowledge, no microfluidic LD system has been reported for single-droplet measurements. Furthermore, LD-based characterization of microfluidic droplets or particles in the larger size range (tens of micrometers) remains largely unexplored. This range presents particular challenges, as increasing particle size leads to more complex Mie scattering patterns, which complicate accurate analysis. Additionally, no existing microfluidic platform has been reported that integrates LD-based single-particle or droplet measurements with simultaneous bright-field microscopic imaging in a standard configuration.

This study aims to design a versatile microfluidic LD system integrated into a commercial inverted microscope for the characterization of O/W and W/O droplets, as well as polymer particles in the tens of micrometers range, using a standard PDMS microfluidic device. Our approach combines scattering microscopy , and microfluidic technology, enabling particle characterization through LD alongside conventional microscopic observation. By simultaneously capturing both microscopic images and the angular light distribution of scattered particles, we validate the feasibility of the system by comparing image-processed microscopic data with the LD-based analysis of scattered light. Unlike conventional methods that require prior knowledge of particle refractive indices, our single-particle measurements allow the estimation of both size and optical properties, such as refractive index, by fitting these parameters as unknowns. To demonstrate the system’s performance, we compare two liquid paraffins with slightly different refractive indices as the dispersed phase for O/W droplets and pure water as the dispersed phase for W/O droplets with the diameters of 20–50 μm.

Materials and Methods

Optical Apparatus

A schematic of an on-chip LD system is shown in Figure . The optical setup was integrated into a commercial inverted microscope (IX73; Evident, Tokyo, Japan; Figure S1). A semiconductor laser (λ = 665 nm, FWHM = 1.6 nm; 10KM–10MW, SENBA, China) was coupled with a single-mode fiber (P1-630Y-FC-1; Thorlabs, NJ, USA) connected to a collimator lens (f = 4.43 mm, beam waist = 0.8 mm; F230FC-B; Thorlabs). The laser wavelength was measured using a spectrometer (FLAME-S-UV-VIS; Ocean Optics, FL, USA; Figure S2. The intensity of the laser beam emitted from the collimator was adjusted to approximately 2.0 mW using a half-wave plate (WPH10E-633; Thorlabs) and a linear polarizer (LPVISE100-A; Thorlabs), with the polarization aligned along the x-axis in Figure .

Schematic of the on-chip laser diffraction (LD) system on an inverted microscope. CL: collimator lens, HWP: half-waveplate, LP: linear polarizer, L1: planoconvex lens, DM: dichroic short-pass mirror, OL: objective lens, LPF: long-pass filter, L2: biconvex lens, BB: beam block, Cam 1 and 2: CMOS cameras, L3: imaging lens.

The laser beam was slightly focused by using a planoconvex lens (f = 100 mm, LA1509-A; Thorlabs) to a diameter of ∼100 μm and reflected by a dichroic short-pass mirror (cutoff wavelength = 650 nm; 69–217; Edmund Optics, NJ, USA) onto a microfluidic device. The scattered light was collected by objective lenses (NA = 0.5, 50×, LMPlanFl for measurement; NA = 0.13, 5×, LMPlanFL for observation; Evident) and redirected by the dichroic mirror. A long-pass filter (cut-on wavelength = 630 nm, FGL630M; Thorlabs) was placed between the mirror and the lens to block the illumination light from entering the measurement path.

The first biconvex lens (f = 50 mm, LB1471-A; Thorlabs) was positioned 100 mm from the back focal plane of the objective to form a 1:1 magnification Fourier plane that represents the angular distribution of the scattered light. A custom-made circular beam block (φ = 0.3 mm, Figure S3), created by using acrylic black pigments onto a circular glass substrate (diameter = 30 mm, thickness = 2 mm), was positioned at the conjugate plane of the Fourier plane to obstruct the directly transmitted light. The second biconvex lens (f = 50 mm, LB1471-A; Thorlabs) was positioned 100 mm behind the beam block.

The angular distribution of the scattered light was captured by a monochrome CMOS camera, Cam 1 (pixel size = 3.45 μm, resolution = 1920 × 1200, a2A1920-160umBAS; Basler AG, Ahrensburg, Germany), installed on the conjugate plane of the Fourier plane, 100 mm behind the second biconvex lens. Additionally, a magnified image of the objective plane was captured by a second CMOS camera, Cam 2, of the same model, with a 0.5× imaging lens (0.5X U-TV0.5XC-3, Evident). Both cameras were synchronized to an external 100 Hz trigger from a function generator (FGX-2220; Texio technology, Kanagawa, Japan) to capture 8-bit images via a custom-written code. For characterizing each droplet, 1000 frames of magnified and scattered light images were recorded at 100 Hz.

Device Design

The microfluidic device was designed to integrate a cross-junction droplet generator, a droplet spacing adjuster, and a measurement channel, as illustrated in Figure . The target droplet diameter was set between 20 and 50 μm. To facilitate individual measurements, the droplet-generation frequency was maintained below the camera frame rate of 100 Hz. Droplet generation was operated in the squeezing mode to enable flexible control over the droplet size by adjusting the flow rate ratio between the dispersed and continuous phases. The squeezing mode typically produces lower droplet generation frequencies than the dripping mode, with droplet sizes larger than the channel cross-section. Thus, the cross-junction channel width and height were set to 20 and 17 μm, respectively, slightly smaller than the minimum target droplet diameter of 20 μm.

Schematic (a) and photograph (b) of the microfluidic device used in the on-chip LD system.

To prevent multiple droplets from scattering simultaneously within the laser spot (diameter ∼ 100 μm), a droplet spacing adjustment section was placed downstream the droplet generator, with an additional continuous phase inflow (Video 1). To minimize unwanted diffraction at the channel edges, the measurement channel was designed with a width of 400 μmover four times the laser spot size (∼100 μm)and a height of 68 μm, sufficient for droplets of up to 50-μm diameter.

Device Fabrication

The PDMS microfluidic devices were fabricated using standard soft lithography techniques with a multilayered SU-8 mold (Figure S1). A negative-tone SU-8 3025 photoresist (Kayaku Advanced Materials, MA, USA) was spin-coated onto silicon wafers at 4000 rpm to a thickness of 17 μm. The substrate was soft-baked at 65 °C for 3 min and then at 95 °C for 15 min. After baking, the SU-8 film was exposed to UV light via a maskless lithography system (DDB-701-DL; NEOARK, Tokyo, Japan) with a dose of 150 mJ/cm2.

A postexposure bake was performed at 65 °C for 1 min and 95 °C for 3 min. Without developing the first layer, a second layer of SU-8 3025 was spin-coated directly onto the first at 2000 rpm, creating a 40-μm thick layer. This second layer was soft-baked at 65 °C for 5 min and 95 °C for 20 min and then exposed to a UV dose of 200 mJ/cm2. The substrate was then postbaked at 65 °C for 2 min and 95 °C for 5 min. The SU-8 layer was developed using 2-methoxy-1-methylethyl acetate (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical, Osaka, Japan) for 10 min and rinsed with isopropyl alcohol (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical). The SU-8 mold was then hard-baked at 180 °C for 20 min.

To facilitate the removal of PDMS from the SU-8 mold, the mold surfaces were treated with vaporized chlorotrimethylsilane (Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan). The PDMS precursor and the curing agent were mixed in a 10:1 weight ratio (Toray Industries, Tokyo, Japan) and degassed in a planetary centrifugal mixer (AR-100; THINKY, Tokyo, Japan) for 1 min. This mixture was poured into the SU-8 mold and degassed again in a vacuum chamber for 15 min to remove air bubbles. Curing was performed at 80 °C for 2.5 h. Following curing, the PDMS chips were peeled from the mold, and inlets and outlets were created using a 1 mm diameter punch tool (Biopsy Punch; KAI Industries, Gifu, Japan).

For producing W/O droplets, PDMS-coated glass slides were prepared by spin-coating a mixed and degassed PDMS (10:1) onto glass slides at 1000 rpm for 120 s, followed by baking at 80 °C for 15 min. The punched PDMS chips were then bonded to either bare glass or PDMS-coated glass substratesdepending on whether O/W or W/O droplets were generatedusing O2 plasma treatment (18 W, 20 Pa, 40 s; PDC-32G; Harrick Plasma, NY, USA). To enhance the bonding, the bonded PDMS chips were heated on a hot plate at 80 °C for 1 min. To generate an O/W emulsion, the microchannels were treated with a hydrophilic coating agent (SPRA-202; Tokyo Ohka Kogyo, Tokyo, Japan) for surface modification. No surface treatment was performed for generating a W/O emulsion.

Materials

A continuous phase comprising a 2 wt % aqueous sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical) solution, filtered using a syringe filter (pore size = 0.45 μm, CS045AS; Toyo Roshi, Tokyo, Japan), was employed for O/W emulsion droplets. Liquid paraffin of varying densities (light: 0.8–0.832 g/cm3, heavy: 0.825–0.85 g/cm3; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical) served as the dispersed phase. For W/O emulsion droplets, the dispersed phase was pure water, while the continuous phase consisted of liquid paraffin containing 5 wt % SPAN 80 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical). Size-certified PS beads (Polybead 25 μm; Polysciences, PA, USA) with a nominal mean diameter (d n) of 24.9 μm and a standard deviation of 0.77 μm (CV = 3.1%) were dispersed in pure water at a concentration of 2.91 × 106 particles/mL.

General Equipment

Glass syringes (50 μL for the dispersed phase, and 500 μL and 2.5 mL for the continuous phase in the droplet generator and interval adjuster, respectively; 1700 Series; Hamilton, NV, USA) were filled with the respective liquids and mounted on syringe pumps (Legato 180; KD Scientific, PA, USA). The syringes were connected to microfluidic chips using polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) tubing (outer diameter: 1.59 mm; inner diameters: 250 μm for the dispersed phase and 500 μm for the continuous phase; 6010-35606, 35603; GL Science, Tokyo, Japan) and disposable plastic needles (outer diameter: 1.08 mm; PN-19G-B; Musashi engineering, Tokyo, Japan). Connectors (P-675 and P-678; GL Science), ferrules (9706, Flom, Tokyo, Japan), and hex fittings (9773; Flom) were used to connect the Luer-Lock syringe fittings to the PTFE tubing and adapt the tubing to the Luer-Lock needles. The refractive indices of the liquids (n D20) were measured at 20 °C using a refractometer (wavelength = 589.3 nm; HI 96800; HANNA Instruments, Chiba, Japan).

Laser Diffraction Method

Scattering Angle and Magnification Calibration

To accurately measure the scattering angle of light from a droplet on the image sensor, angular calibration of the LD system was performed using a diffraction grating with a period (Λ) of 10 μm, a filling factor of 0.5, and a height of 6.5 μm (Figure a). This grating, fabricated by using a previously described soft lithography technique, was bonded to a glass slide via O2 plasma treatment using a process similar to that used for the microfluidic chip. The coordinate system on the CMOS camera (Cam 1) was defined as shown in Figures and b, where x = sin θcos φ and y = sin θsin φ, with θ representing the scattering angle in air and φ the azimuthal angle. The measurement beam was incident perpendicularly to the PDMS grating, creating regularly spaced 2D diffracted spots, as shown in Figure b. A pixel scale of 0.00091 px–1 was determined using the theoretical equation for the diffraction angle of the mth order, sin θ = mλ/Λ, resulting in a measurable angular range of sin θ = 0.05–0.5 between the beam block and the maximum NA of the objective lens.

Angular calibration of the LD system using a PDMS grating. (a) Micrograph of a PDMS grating with a periodicity of Λ = 10 μm. (b) Fourier plane image showing diffraction peaks from the grating, illuminated by a slightly focused laser beam (λ = 665 nm) incident at normal angle.

For calibrating the microscopic magnification of Cam 2, size-certified PS beads with a nominal diameter (d n) of 24.9 μm and a CV of 3.1% were used. The PS beads were diluted 100-fold with deionized water and placed stationary between a cover glass and a glass slide. Using Cam 2 (pixel size = 3.45 μm), the microscope was set to 25× magnification using a 50× objective (f = 3.6 mm) and a 0.5× imaging lens (f = 90 mm). The measured diameter d m was obtained by processing the contour of the magnified image. The magnification factor was calibrated to 0.1356 μm/px, ensuring that the measured diameter d m matched the nominal PS particle size (d n = 24.9 μm). This calibration factor aligns closely with the theoretical magnification of 0.138 μm/px, calculated as 3.45/(50 × 0.5). Consequently, the measured CV of 3.0%, based on d m, showed a strong agreement with the nominal CV of 3.1%, confirming the accuracy of the microscopic measurement using our LD system.

To evaluate the influence of defocusing in the measurement system, both the 2D light scattering pattern and the magnified image of the PDMS diffraction grating were acquired while varying the focal position within a range of ±20 μm (Figure S4). Although the magnified image became noticeably blurred with a focal variation of ±5 μm, which is larger than the depth of focus of 1.3 μm, no significant change was observed in the 2D light scattering pattern even at the focal shift of ±20 μm. These results indicate that defocusing on the order of several tens of micrometers has a negligible impact on our LD system, owing to the Fourier transform characteristics of the lens system.

Laser Diffraction Analysis

This section describes the LD analysis of the 2D light scattering pattern generated by a single droplet. The droplet diameter d and the refractive index of the dispersed phase n d were estimated by comparing the experimental and theoretical scattering patterns, assuming the droplet to be a perfectly spherical, single-emulsion droplet. When the wavelength λ and continuous phase refractive index n c are fixed, the pair of d and n d that reproduces a given scattering pattern is mathematically uniquely determined. Therefore, we adopted two-variable pattern matching as the analysis method (see ESI).

The theoretical scattering pattern of the spherical droplet was computed using the MiePython library. As the size parameter πdn c/λ was sufficiently large (>100), the polarization dependence of scattering intensity was considered negligible. Here, d, n c, and λ represent the droplet diameter, refractive index of the continuous phase, and laser wavelength in a vacuum, respectively. The unpolarized scattered light intensity function in the MiePython library is expressed as I sim (sin θ, d, n d), where θ is the scattering angle and n d is the refractive index of the dispersed phase.

For the experimental data, azimuthally averaged 1D scattering intensity profiles were obtained from the 2D scattering images. First, the frame where the droplet was centered in the measurement beam was identified by detecting the local maxima of the average brightness in the scattering image. The nearest four frames without any droplets in the measurement beam were averaged to create background images for both the microscopic and scattered light images, which were then subtracted from the target images to remove unwanted background noise.

The resulting background-subtracted 2D scattering pattern was azimuthally averaged over 360° along angle φ to obtain the azimuthally averaged scattering intensity profile I exp(sin θ). The experimental intensity profile was measured over the sin θ range of 0.05–0.5 to account for noise from the scattered light off the beam block or dust particles. However, the analysis was conducted within a more restricted range of sin θ (0.1–0.4).

The droplet diameter and refractive index of the dispersed phase were simultaneously estimated by matching the experimental and theoretical scattering intensity profiles. The correlation coefficient r, defined as shown below, was used to compare the experimental and simulated intensities I exp/sim(sin θ): r(d,nd)=∑sinθ(Isim(sinθ)−Isim®)(Iexp(sinθ)−Iexp®)∑sinθ(Isim(sinθ)−Isim®)∑sinθ(Iexp(sinθ)−Iexp®) 1

This pattern-matching approach, based on the correlation coefficient r, is both intuitive and reliable, enabling a direct comparison of raw data without requiring any additional image processing, such as peak detection. To simultaneously estimate d and n d, r(d, n d) was calculated by varying d by Δd = 0.05 μm and n d by Δn d = 0.001. The combination of diameter and refractive index that yielded the highest r was chosen as the best estimate for the droplet diameter d e and dispersed phase refractive index n d,e. To validate the estimated droplet diameter d e, the measured droplet diameter d m was also obtained by processing the magnified images obtained using Cam 2.

Although the estimated droplet diameter d e plotted in Figure b shifted upward by up to 1.0 μm compared to the measured diameter d m, subμm scale fluctuations in the time series data for each droplet were consistent for both d e and d m. This consistency suggests that the LD measurement sensitivity is on the subμm scale, despite certain deviations resulting from errors in the estimation process. The average correlation coefficient r = 0.9909, obtained from pattern matching across 16 droplets measured over 2 s, further confirms this stability (see Video 2).

The estimated refractive indices n d,e for light and heavy paraffins are shown in Figure b, with average values and standard deviations as 1.452 ± 0.0012 and 1.458 ± 0.0019, respectively. Compared to the measured refractive index n d,m, the estimates were systematically lower by −0.25 and −0.34%, respectively. However, the coefficient of variation for the estimated n d,e was 0.08%, indicating high reproducibility and sufficient sensitivity to distinguish the refractive index difference Δn d,m = 0.008 between the paraffins, regardless of the droplet size. These results confirm the feasibility and accuracy of our device and analytical procedure for estimating both the diameter and refractive index of single droplets of O/W emulsion.

However, our device and analytical method have certain limitations in terms of applicable size and sensitivity. The measurable droplet size range depends on the optical configuration, but under our current setup, droplets ranging from approximately 5–75 μm can be analyzed with sufficient resolution (Figure S6). The lower limit is constrained by the ability to resolve diffraction patterns, while the upper limit is determined by excessive fringe density and reduced pattern clarity. For larger droplets or high refractive index contrasts, narrower and denser peaks in the recorded images must be resolved. Therefore, larger droplets and particles can be measured with a low-NA objective lens and by adjusting the measurable scattering angle range. For smaller droplets or low refractive index contrasts, capturing scattering peaks with wider intervals within the 3°–30° range is critical for accurate pattern matching. Regarding sensitivity, the system can detect diameter fluctuations smaller than 1 μm (Figure b). The minimum detectable refractive index shift of the dispersed phase is estimated to be ∼0.004, corresponding to twice the standard deviation of n d,e in Table .

In this study, the refractive index of the continuous phase n c was assumed to remain constant, based on off-chip measurements. However, in cases where n c variessuch as during solute diffusion or interfacial transfer processesit becomes necessary to determine both n d and n c directly on-chip. Direct fitting of all three parameters (d, n d, and n c) to a single scattering pattern results in nonuniqueness. To overcome this, a hybrid approach can be adopted in which the droplet diameter d is measured from microscopic images, and n d and n c are subsequently estimated through two-parameter fitting of the measured light scattering profile. This method could enable on-chip, label-free measurement of both phases’ refractive indices, expanding the system’s versatility in diverse microfluidic applications.

Our results indicate that an 8-bit grayscale CMOS camera is sufficient to capture 2D scattering patterns of droplets and particles in the tens of micrometers range. However, low-intensity contrast at high scattering angles presents challenges; a relay system with reduced optical aberration would be beneficial. Measurement speed is essentially limited by the frame rate of the camera. In addition, measurements above 1 kHz would require a high-speed camera or 1D linear CCD sensor. In our study, image analysis of the scattering pattern took approximately 0.6 s per image. Future improvements in image processing and pattern matching, potentially using prebuilt libraries and high-speed hardware such as field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs), could enable real-time measurements. Additionally, the scattered light from satellite droplets, if present within the laser spot, may introduce errors (Figure S7). Incorporating satellite droplet separation devices, such as deterministic lateral displacement systems, could improve the accuracy of droplet characterization.

As an example of practical application, our system holds particular promise for label-free, droplet-based analysis of proteins and small moleculestargets for which conventional fluorescence labeling is often challenging or potentially disruptive to molecular function. By encapsulating analytes in droplets, signal dilution is reduced and local enrichment is enhanced, thereby improving detection sensitivity. Additionally, droplet-based analysis enables digital quantification and prevents sample crosstalk unlike continuous-flow systems. Simultaneous measurement of droplet refractive index and volume allows estimation of analyte concentration or enrichment based on their combined variation. This compact, label-free approach is especially well suited to high-throughput bioanalytical applications where sample isolation and noninvasive detection are critical.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated a versatile microfluidic LD system capable of estimating the diameter and refractive index of O/W and W/O droplets in PDMS microchannels, along with simultaneous bright-field microscopic imaging. By integrating an optical setup into a commercial inverted microscope, we enabled seamless device operation and droplet characterization within PDMS-based platforms. The intensity profiles acquired over a scattering angle range of 3°–30° showed strong agreement with the theoretically calculated Mie scattering angular distributions, allowing an accurate estimation of the droplet diameter and refractive index. For O/W droplets with the diameters of 25–50 μm, the estimation errors were within 5%, while for W/O droplets, the diameter and refractive index were estimated with errors of 10% and 1%, respectively. These findings confirm the feasibility and precision of our LD system for the simultaneous estimation of the droplet diameter and refractive index in both O/W and W/O emulsions. Because the internal refractive index reflects key physicochemical propertiessuch as solute concentration, pH of gel, or the chemical state of encapsulated materialour system enables more detailed characterization at the single-droplet level. The reliability of this on-chip characterization platform highlights its potential for high-precision, microfluidic-based applications across a broad range of fields.
