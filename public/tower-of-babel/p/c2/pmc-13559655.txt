Bergamottin: a multitarget natural furanocoumarin with promising anticancer potential
Authors: Hang Gao, Zhenzhen Wang, Bochen Zhang, Guangyao Lv, Ximei Sun, Xinfu Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bergamottin, a natural furanocoumarin derived from Citrus species, exhibits broad-spectrum antitumor activity by targeting key oncogenic pathways and overcoming therapeutic resistance. This review synthesizes its physicochemical properties, metabolic behavior, and mechanisms of action across diverse malignancies, including leukemia, breast cancer, lung cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, gliomas, and colorectal cancer. Bergamottin exerts its anticancer effects through a hierarchical regulatory network: at the primary level, it directly inhibits P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes, thereby reversing multidrug resistance and altering drug metabolism; at the secondary level, it suppresses oncogenic signaling cascades including signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B/mammalian target of rapamycin (PI3K/Akt/mTOR), and rat sarcoma virus/rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (Ras/Raf/ERK); ultimately, these actions converge on apoptosis induction, cell cycle arrest, and metastasis inhibition. Preclinical studies demonstrate its ability to enhance chemotherapy efficacy via improved bioavailability and metabolic suppression. Additionally, it induces apoptosis, arrests cell cycle progression, and suppresses metastasis through epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) inhibition and Ras-related C3 botulinum toxin substrate 1 (Rac1)/matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) axis regulation. Despite its promise, challenges like pharmacokinetic variability, genotoxicity of metabolites, and limited clinical data necessitate further investigation. While preclinical evidence supports bergamottin's potential as a multi-target chemosensitizer, its standalone antitumor efficacy remains modest and its safety profile incompletely characterized. Future research must prioritize systematic toxicity profiling and cautious preclinical evaluation before any translational attempt.

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the most formidable threats to global public health, characterized by intricate pathogenesis and remarkable heterogeneity. These biological complexities contribute to the limitations of current therapeutic modalities, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapies, which are frequently challenged by drug resistance, systemic toxicity, and suboptimal efficacy (1). The past few decades have seen phytochemicals gain traction as a valuable resource in anticancer drug development. Their structural complexity, generally low toxicity, and ability to target multiple pathways set them apart from many synthetic agents (2,3). Clinically established plant-derived drugs like taxanes (4) and vincristine (5) clearly demonstrate the potential of natural products in oncology. This success has made the search for new bioactive compounds that can overcome the limitations of current therapies a central goal in cancer research.

Among these, furanocoumarins stand out for their broad anticancer activity. Found naturally in various plants, this class of compounds has shown efficacy against several tumor types, including leukemia, glioma, as well as breast, lung, and liver cancers. These compounds exert their antitumor effects through diverse mechanisms such as induction of apoptosis, autophagy, cell cycle arrest, and inhibition of metastasis and multidrug resistance (MDR) pathways (6). Bergamottin, a naturally occurring furanocoumarin predominantly found in Citrus paradisi (grapefruit) and Citrus bergamia (bergamot), has garnered significant attention for its multifaceted pharmacological properties (7-9). Beyond its direct anticancer effects, bergamottin’s well-documented anti-inflammatory (7) and antiviral (8) activities may indirectly contribute to tumor suppression. Chronic inflammation and viral infections [e.g., hepatitis B virus (HBV)/hepatitis C virus (HCV) in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), human papillomavirus (HPV) in cervical cancer] are established oncogenic drivers. By suppressing nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB)-driven inflammation and interfering with viral replication cycles, bergamottin may address these upstream tumorigenic triggers, broadening its therapeutic relevance beyond direct cytotoxicity. These include inhibiting signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling, inducing apoptosis, arresting cell cycle progression, and suppressing metastasis (10-12). Notably, recent studies have demonstrated its efficacy in leukemia, breast cancer, and lung malignancies, particularly when used synergistically with chemotherapeutic agents (13-15). Such findings underscore bergamottin’s translational potential primarily as a chemosensitizing adjuvant to enhance conventional therapies, given that its standalone antitumor activity in vivo appears modest in current preclinical models. This review systematically examines the chemical properties, metabolic behavior, and antitumor mechanisms of bergamottin, aiming to provide a comprehensive foundation for further preclinical development and to advance innovative strategies in natural product-based anticancer drug design.

Methodology

A systematic literature search was conducted across PubMed, Google Scholar, Web of Science, and Scopus using combinations of the following keywords: “bergamottin”, “furanocoumarin”, “furocoumarin”, “anticancer”, “apoptosis”, “drug resistance”, “P-glycoprotein”, “CYP450”, “chemosensitization”, and “natural product”. Articles published in English were included, with a primary focus on studies from 2000 onwards; seminal pre-2000 articles were retained where scientifically relevant to the evidence synthesis. Inclusion criteria were: (I) peer-reviewed original research articles, reviews, or preclinical studies reporting bergamottin’s anticancer activity in vitro and/or in vivo; (II) studies with clearly defined experimental endpoints, including cell lines, animal models, concentrations, and mechanisms of action. Exclusion criteria included: (I) non-peer-reviewed sources without DOI; (II) studies lacking clear compound identification or experimental design; (III) articles reporting only physicochemical characterization without biological activity. Data extraction focused on cancer type, cell line or animal model, concentration or dose, administration route, treatment duration, key molecular mechanisms, and combination therapy regimens.

Physicochemical properties and metabolism of bergamottin

Bergamottin is a natural furanocoumarin derivative predominantly isolated from the peel and essential oil of Citrus paradisi (grapefruit) and Citrus bergamia (bergamot). Its core structure comprises a fused coumarin backbone and furan ring, augmented by a geranyl side chain at the C-5 position, which confers distinct lipophilicity, membrane permeability, and bioactivity (Figure 1A). The compound is typically extracted through solvent extraction from plant materials followed by column chromatographic purification, yielding high-purity preparations suitable for pharmacological studies. Chemically, bergamottin can be synthesized via prenylation of umbelliferone derivatives followed by cyclization, or obtained through semi-synthetic modification of the geranyl side chain to generate analogues such as 5'-OH-bergamottin and 6',7'-dihydroxy bergamottin for structure-activity relationship (SAR) studies. Its lipophilic nature enables solubility in organic solvents such as methanol, ethanol, and dimethyl sulfoxide, while remaining virtually insoluble in water.

Chemical structure of bergamottin and its derivatives.

The compound exhibits pronounced electron affinity due to the conjugated system of its furan and coumarin moieties, facilitating competitive or irreversible binding to cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes, including CYP1A1, CYP3A4, and CYP2B6, thereby inhibiting their catalytic activity (16,17). For instance, CYP3A4-mediated oxidation of the geranyl side chain generates hydroxylated derivatives, while epoxidation of the furan ring promotes glutathione conjugation, yielding detoxified metabolites (18). The clinical implications of bergamottin’s potent CYP450 inhibition extend beyond its chemosensitizing benefits. While CYP3A4 and CYP1A1 inhibition can elevate intratumoral concentrations of co-administered chemotherapeutics (e.g., docetaxel, paclitaxel, lenvatinib), it simultaneously increases the risk of systemic drug-drug interactions (DDIs), analogous to the well-documented “grapefruit juice effect”. Co-administration with CYP3A4 substrates such as certain statins, calcium channel blockers, or immunosuppressants may lead to elevated plasma levels and adverse effects. Therefore, any translational strategy involving bergamottin must incorporate rigorous pharmacokinetic monitoring and dose adjustment protocols to mitigate DDI-related toxicity.

Bergamottin is predominantly absorbed through passive diffusion in the intestinal tract, exhibiting an oral bioavailability of 54–81%, with peak plasma concentrations attained within 1–3 h post-administration (19). Once absorbed, bergamottin’s lipophilic nature promotes its binding to plasma proteins and subsequent distribution to organs such as the liver, kidneys, and adrenal glands. Hepatic metabolism is driven mainly by CYP enzymes, with CYP2B6 and CYP3A5 playing distinct roles. CYP2B6 primarily mediates terminal hydroxylation of the geranyloxy chain, yielding 5'-OH-bergamottin (Figure 1B) and a mixture of 6'- and 7'-OH-bergamottin (Figure 1C) as major metabolites, along with bergaptol (Figure 1D) as a minor product. CYP3A5 mediates cleavage of the geranyloxy chain to form bergaptol as the predominant metabolite, alongside 5'-OH-bergamottin and 2'-OH-bergamottin (Figure 1E). Additionally, CYP3A5 produces two minor metabolites: 6',7'-dihydroxy bergamottin (Figure 1F) and a mixture of 6'- and 7'-OH-bergamottin. Excretion studies reveal that 46–47% of the administered dose is eliminated renally within 24 h in animal models, with 11–14% excreted via feces. In humans, cumulative excretion over five days reaches 62% in urine and 31% in feces, suggesting enterohepatic recirculation (17,20). Notably, 6',7'-dihydroxy bergamottin is detected in plasma after bergamottin exposure, while bergaptol and 6',7'-dihydroxy bergamottin are major urinary metabolites (21-23).

Comparative analysis with structurally related furanocoumarins reveals both shared and distinct pharmacological profiles. Bergapten (5-methoxypsoralen) and xanthotoxin (8-methoxypsoralen) share the core furanocoumarin scaffold but lack the geranyloxy side chain present in bergamottin. This structural difference markedly influences their activity: bergapten exhibits potent phototoxicity upon ultraviolet A (UVA) activation, making it suitable for photochemotherapy but limiting systemic administration, whereas bergamottin’s geranyl moiety enhances lipophilicity and CYP450-binding affinity, conferring chemosensitizing properties absent in bergapten. SAR analysis indicates that the furan ring at C-4,5 is essential for CYP450 inhibition, while the geranyloxy side chain at C-5 determines membrane permeability and P-glycoprotein (P-gp) interaction. Removal or modification of this side chain, as seen in bergaptol, significantly reduces metabolic stability and anticancer potency, underscoring the importance of the intact geranyl moiety for therapeutic efficacy (16-18).

Applications of bergamottin in cancer therapy

Bergamottin has emerged as a versatile phytochemical with demonstrated therapeutic potential across a spectrum of malignancies, leveraging its multi-target mechanisms to address critical hallmarks of cancer, including proliferation, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance (Table 1). By modulating key signaling pathways, inhibiting drug-metabolizing enzymes, and synergizing with conventional therapies, bergamottin exhibits context-dependent antitumor efficacy while offering opportunities to mitigate toxicity and resistance (Figure 2).

Bergamottin’s anticancer activity operates through a hierarchical mechanistic framework. At the primary level, the compound directly inhibits P-gp and CYP450 enzymes, thereby reversing MDR and increasing intracellular drug accumulation. At the secondary level, it suppresses oncogenic signaling cascades including STAT3, phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B/mammalian target of rapamycin (PI3K/Akt/mTOR), and rat sarcoma virus/rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (Ras/Raf/ERK), which govern cell survival, proliferation, and metabolic reprogramming. These upstream perturbations ultimately converge on terminal effector pathways: induction of apoptosis via Bax/Bcl-2 modulation, cell cycle arrest through p21/p27 upregulation, and metastasis inhibition via epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) suppression and Ras-related C3 botulinum toxin substrate 1 (Rac1)/matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) inactivation. Within the furanocoumarin class, shared anticancer mechanisms include reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated signaling, NF-κB-driven inflammation, autophagy induction, and cell cycle arrest. However, bergamottin uniquely combines P-gp inhibition with CYP450 interference, a dual functionality not consistently observed in bergapten or xanthotoxin. Furthermore, while bergapten primarily exerts phototoxic effects through DNA intercalation upon UVA activation, bergamottin’s anticancer activity is largely independent of light exposure, relying instead on kinase pathway modulation and drug metabolism interference. Distinct effects on cancer metabolism have also been noted: bergamottin enhances glucose consumption in HCC cells, suggesting metabolic reprogramming capabilities that warrant further investigation across the furanocoumarin class.

Leukemia

Bergamottin has recently demonstrated potential applications in the field of leukemia treatment, primarily manifested in enhancing the efficacy of antitumor drugs and promoting apoptosis in leukemia cells. In terms of its mechanism of action, bergamottin is capable of reversing MDR by inhibiting the function of P-gp. P-gp, an ATP-dependent drug efflux pump, is overexpressed in leukemia cells, leading to resistance to various chemotherapeutic drugs. Bergamottin competitively inhibits the binding of P-gp to drugs, reducing drug efflux and thereby increasing the intracellular concentration of chemotherapeutic drugs, which enhances their antitumor effects (13). Furthermore, bergamottin can synergize with certain statin drugs (such as simvastatin) to regulate the NF-κB signaling pathway, inhibit the expression of anti-apoptotic genes, and further promote apoptosis in leukemia cells. This process involves downregulating the levels of anti-apoptotic proteins such as cIAP-1, Bcl-2, Bcl-xL, and survivin, while upregulating the expression of cell cycle inhibitory proteins such as p21 and p27, ultimately leading to cell cycle arrest in the S phase and induction of apoptosis (24,25). In vivo evidence for bergamottin as a standalone or adjuvant agent in leukemia remains to be established. Current data are restricted to in vitro models, including K562/ADM and HL-60 cell lines, and the development of relevant patient-derived xenograft or systemic leukemia mouse models is warranted to validate preclinical efficacy, optimal dosing, and safety margins.

Breast cancer

Bergamottin exhibits its antitumor effects in breast cancer through modulating the activity of CYP enzymes, particularly CYP3A4. CYP3A4 is a major enzyme involved in the metabolism of various chemotherapeutic agents, including docetaxel, a commonly used drug in breast cancer treatment. By inhibiting CYP3A4, bergamottin reduces the metabolic clearance of docetaxel, thereby increasing its systemic exposure and intratumoral concentration. Moreover, the development of nanocarrier-based formulations of bergamottin has further potentiated its therapeutic potential in breast cancer. These formulations, such as poly(lactic-co-glycolic) acid (PLGA) nanoparticles functionalized with galactosamine, enable targeted delivery of bergamottin to hepatocytes, the primary site of CYP3A4 expression. This targeted delivery not only enhances the local concentration of bergamottin at the site of action but also minimizes off-target effects and systemic toxicity. As a result, nanocarrier-encapsulated bergamottin, when administered prior to docetaxel treatment, significantly augments the antitumor efficacy of docetaxel, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for the management of breast cancer (14). In vivo validation using MDA-MB-231 xenograft models demonstrated that the combination of docetaxel and PLGA-encapsulated bergamottin significantly prolonged survival compared to monotherapy (14). However, detailed reports of tumor growth inhibition rates, treatment duration, animal body weight monitoring, and hepatic or renal toxicity indices remain limited in the current literature, precluding comprehensive assessment of bergamottin’s therapeutic window in breast cancer.

Furthermore, bergamottin has demonstrated potential direct antitumor effects in breast cancer cells, independent of its CYP3A4 inhibition activity. Specifically, bergamottin has been shown to inhibit the STAT3 signaling pathway in multiple cancer cell lines, including breast cancer cells. Persistent activation of STAT3 is closely associated with growth, survival, proliferation, metastasis, and angiogenesis of various cancer cells, including those of breast cancer. By inhibiting STAT3 phosphorylation, bergamottin disrupts the downstream signaling cascades that promote tumor progression. This results in the downregulation of STAT3-regulated gene products such as cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), cyclin D1, and survivin, which are known to play crucial roles in breast cancer development and progression (10). Notably, VEGF is a key regulator of tumor angiogenesis and vascular permeability rather than a direct mediator of metastatic dissemination; its downregulation by bergamottin primarily suppresses tumor neovascularization, a distinct antiangiogenic mechanism complementary to Rac1/MMP-9-mediated anti-metastatic effects.

Lung cancer

Bergamottin shows promise as a therapeutic agent for suppressing lung cancer metastasis, with its effects tied to the regulation of epithelial mesenchymal transition and related oncogenic pathways. Mechanistic studies indicate that bergamottin interferes with the PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis, a key driver of EMT and metastasis in lung cancer. Treatment with bergamottin reduced both basal and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) induced phosphorylation of PI3K, Akt, and mTOR, thereby dampening downstream signals that support tumor cell survival and invasiveness. These observations point to a dual role for bergamottin: it influences EMT related molecular markers while also modulating oncogenic kinase pathways, positioning it as a potential adjuvant strategy to counteract metastatic progression in lung cancer . In vivo evidence for bergamottin in lung cancer remains to be established. While in vitro studies in A549 and NCI-H1299 cells demonstrate anti-metastatic effects via EMT and PI3K/Akt/mTOR inhibition, the lack of lung cancer xenograft or orthotopic models precludes assessment of its systemic efficacy, biodistribution, and pulmonary toxicity in a physiologically relevant context.

Liver cancer

In HCC, bergamottin exhibits multifaceted therapeutic potential by interfering with drug resistance mechanisms and modulating metabolic reprogramming. Research reveals that bergamottin enhances the efficacy of lenvatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, by inhibiting the CCL5/CCR5/CYP1A1 axis—a pathway activated in residual HCC cells during combined anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD1) and lenvatinib therapy. Bergamottin, as a competitive CYP1A1 inhibitor, suppresses lenvatinib metabolism in tumor cells, thereby increasing intratumoral drug concentration and overcoming resistance. In vivo studies using HCC-bearing mice confirmed that bergamottin synergizes with lenvatinib and anti-PD1 therapy, significantly reducing tumor volume and improving survival outcomes (26).

Additionally, bergamottin exhibits direct antiproliferative effects on HCC cells. In HepG2 cells, bergamottin induces dose-dependent growth inhibition and enhances glucose consumption, suggesting dual roles in both antitumor activity and metabolic regulation (27). These effects are linked to its ability to disrupt oncogenic signaling pathways, such as STAT3 and PI3K/Akt/mTOR, thereby suppressing proliferation and promoting apoptosis. Collectively, bergamottin emerges as a promising adjuvant in HCC therapy, particularly in overcoming resistance to targeted therapies and modulating tumor metabolism. In HCC-bearing mouse models, bergamottin combined with lenvatinib and anti-PD1 therapy significantly reduced tumor volume and improved survival outcomes (26). Systematic toxicity profiling, including serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT)/aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels, histopathological examination of liver tissue, and body weight trajectories, was not extensively reported and requires further investigation to establish safety margins.

Nervous system cancer

Bergamottin exhibits a direct anti-invasive and anti-migratory effect on U87 and U251 glioma cells. Gliomas are the most common primary tumors of the central nervous system, characterized by their highly invasive nature, which limits the effectiveness of conventional treatments such as surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy. Research has revealed that bergamottin significantly reduces the migration and invasion capabilities of glioma cells by inhibiting the activation of the Rac1 signaling pathway. Rac1, a small G protein, plays a pivotal role in the migration, invasion, and metastasis of tumor cells. Bergamottin accomplishes this by decreasing the active level of Rac1-GTP, which in turn suppresses the expression and secretion of matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), thereby blocking the invasive pathway of tumor cells (29).

Furthermore, bergamottin demonstrates promising antitumor effects in SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma, a malignancy originating from neural crest cells, predominantly affecting children. Studies have shown that bergamottin induces apoptosis in neuroblastoma cells by upregulating the pro-apoptotic protein Bax and downregulating the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, thereby modulating the apoptotic pathway. Additionally, bergamottin elevates the levels of ROS in neuroblastoma cells, leading to a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm), which triggers the apoptotic process. These findings provide a scientific basis for the application of bergamottin in the treatment of neuroblastoma and underscore its promising prospects in the field of neurological tumor therapy (28). In vivo evidence for bergamottin in nervous system malignancies remains to be established. Current findings are limited to U87 and U251 glioma cells and SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells in vitro. The blood-brain barrier penetration of bergamottin, its intracranial distribution, and efficacy in orthotopic glioma or neuroblastoma xenograft models have not been reported and require investigation to support any translational potential in neurological tumor therapy.

Digestive system cancer

Bergamottin exerts potent anti-proliferative effects on colon cancer cells, including HT-29 and RKO, by targeting both cell cycle progression and proliferative signaling pathways. It induces G2/M phase arrest through downregulation of cell cycle regulatory proteins such as Cdc2, cyclin A, and cyclin B1, coupled with increased expression of the negative regulators p21Cip1 and p27Kip1, collectively halting cell cycle advancement. Simultaneously, bergamottin inhibits the Ras/Raf/ERK signaling pathway, a critical driver of cancer cell proliferation and survival, by concentration-dependently blocking phosphorylation of Raf-1, MEK1/2, and ERK1/2 without altering their total protein levels. This dual mechanism disrupts cell cycle dynamics and suppresses oncogenic signaling, synergistically inhibiting colon cancer cell growth and survival (11). In vivo evidence for bergamottin in colorectal cancer remains to be established. Current data are restricted to HT-29 and RKO cell lines in vitro, demonstrating G2/M arrest and Ras/Raf/ERK inhibition. Colorectal cancer xenograft studies are needed to evaluate tumor growth suppression, gastrointestinal tolerability, and systemic exposure before any therapeutic utility can be inferred.

Others

Bergamottin also exhibits unique antitumor effects. For instance, bergamottin effectively reduces the growth of prostate cancer cells by inhibiting both the total and nuclear levels of androgen receptor (AR), thereby reducing AR activation. This leads to a decrease in downstream AR signaling, as evidenced by reduced levels of prostate-specific antigen (PSA), a marker for prostate cancer progression. Additionally, bergamottin is capable of blocking the covalent binding of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons to DNA, thereby reducing the formation of DNA adducts and lowering the risk of genetic mutations and cellular carcinogenesis resulting from this process (30). In vivo evidence for bergamottin in prostate cancer and other solid malignancies remains to be established. Current in vitro data, including AR signaling inhibition in LNCaP and MDAPCa2b cells and DNA adduct reduction in MCF-7 cells, require validation in appropriate xenograft or genetically engineered mouse models to determine antitumor efficacy, pharmacokinetic profiles, and off-target toxicity in vivo.

Melanoma

During the literature search for this review, a study (PMID: 34168462) describing bergamottin-induced DNA damage and inhibition of malignant progression in melanoma via modulation of the miR-145/Cyclin D1 axis was identified. However, this article has subsequently been retracted by the publisher due to concerns regarding data reliability and ethical approval. Consequently, the melanoma-related activity of bergamottin cannot be reliably included in the present evidence synthesis. The retraction of this study underscores the importance of rigorous data validation in preclinical research and highlights that the anticancer spectrum of bergamottin in melanoma remains to be independently confirmed by reproducible studies.

Selectivity and safety considerations

The translational potential of bergamottin is constrained by limited data on its selectivity for cancer cells versus normal tissues. While most in vitro studies employ concentrations ranging from 10 to 100 µM to achieve anticancer effects, systematic evaluation of selectivity indices (SI = IC50 normal cells/IC50 cancer cells; IC50 = half maximal inhibitory concentration) remains scarce. Based on its CYP450 inhibition profile and lipophilic nature, bergamottin may theoretically affect normal hepatocytes and colonic epithelial cells at comparable concentrations; however, direct experimental evidence is lacking. Comprehensive toxicity profiling in normal tissues is therefore urgently needed. Furthermore, the genotoxicity of oxidative metabolites such as 6',7'-dihydroxy bergamottin requires elucidation to establish safe exposure thresholds.

Comparative pharmacological analysis

Comparative evaluation of preclinical studies reveals considerable heterogeneity in experimental design across malignancies. In vitro concentrations typically range from 10–100 µM, with exposure durations of 24–72 hours, whereas in vivo studies in xenograft models have employed doses of 10–50 mg/kg via oral or intraperitoneal administration for 14–28 days. Notably, bergamottin’s pharmacological impact is highly context-dependent: as a CYP3A4 inhibitor, it increases systemic exposure to docetaxel in breast cancer models (14); as a CYP1A1 inhibitor, it enhances lenvatinib bioavailability in HCC (26); and as a P-gp inhibitor, it restores vincristine sensitivity in leukemia cells (13). These pharmacokinetic interactions, while therapeutically advantageous, simultaneously elevate the risk of co-administered drug toxicity, necessitating careful dose optimization in any translational strategy.

Perspectives and strategies for future trials

Bergamottin is a naturally occurring furanocoumarin that has drawn attention for its broad-spectrum anticancer activity. Studies have shown its efficacy against various malignancies, including leukemia, breast cancer, lung cancer, HCC, gliomas, neuroblastoma, and colorectal cancer. Mechanistically, it modulates several key oncogenic pathways, such as STAT3, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, Ras/Raf/ERK, and EMT signaling, while also counteracting drug resistance through inhibition of P-gp and CYP enzymes. Bergamottin has been found to boost the effectiveness of conventional chemotherapeutics like docetaxel and targeted agents such as lenvatinib, largely by improving drug bioavailability, reducing metabolic clearance, and interfering with tumor survival mechanisms. Preclinical evidence shows that it can trigger apoptosis, block cell cycle progression, suppress metastasis, and re-sensitize resistant cancer cells, making it a promising candidate for combination therapy.

Clinical translational considerations. Beyond its pharmacological profile, the translational feasibility of bergamottin depends on practical factors including cost, availability, and formulation development. As a plant-derived compound abundant in grapefruit and bergamot peel, bergamottin is extractable through relatively cost-effective solvent-based methods, offering an economic advantage over fully synthetic analogues. However, no standardized pharmaceutical-grade formulation is currently commercially available, and batch-to-batch variability in plant sources poses quality control challenges. Moreover, bergamottin’s poor aqueous solubility and extensive first-pass metabolism necessitate advanced delivery systems—such as the PLGA nanoparticle approach demonstrated in breast cancer models (14)—to achieve predictable pharmacokinetics and tumor-targeted distribution. These formulation and manufacturing hurdles must be resolved before any clinical application can be contemplated.

Beyond its direct antitumor effects, understanding the SARs of bergamottin derivatives may help develop compounds with stronger potency and lower toxicity. Animal studies have raised concerns about possible hepatotoxicity and phototoxicity, yet recent evidence suggests that bergamottin itself may lack significant phototoxic potential. An in vitro study using the OECD TG 432 assay (31) demonstrated the absence of phototoxicity/photoirritation potential for bergamottin, indicating that phototoxicity risk may be context-dependent or lower than previously assumed for certain furanocoumarins. This discrepancy highlights the need for standardized, validated phototoxicity assays and species-specific toxicity profiling. Hepatotoxicity, whether intrinsic or arising from DDIs via CYP3A4/P-gp inhibition, represents a more consistent and clinically relevant concern. Given these unresolved safety issues, bergamottin cannot currently be regarded as a ready-to-use clinical compound; rather, it should be classified as a preclinical candidate requiring rigorous safety evaluation, including genotoxicity assessment of metabolites such as 6',7'-dihydroxy bergamottin, before any human exposure.

Despite strong preclinical evidence, clinical data on bergamottin as a cancer therapy in humans are still absent. This is not uncommon for natural product-derived compounds at early translational stages. Nonetheless, useful insights can be gained from the clinical use of structurally similar furanocoumarins, as well as from bergamottin’s well-known role in grapefruit juice and its associated food-drug interactions. These observations offer a valuable starting point for designing first-in-human trials. Two main types of dose-limiting toxicity are likely: mechanism-based toxicities common to other furanocoumarins, such as phototoxicity, which is a known class effect and dose-limiting factor in PUVA (psoralen + ultraviolet A) therapy with methoxsalen; and toxicity arising from clinically significant drug interactions, given bergamottin’s strong inhibition of CYP3A4 and P-gp. This inhibition can markedly increase systemic exposure to co-administered drugs, similar to the well-documented interactions between grapefruit juice and medications like certain statins, calcium channel blockers, and immunosuppressants, resulting in higher plasma levels and increased risk of adverse effects.

Given these safety considerations, any translational attempt must prioritize rigorous preclinical toxicity profiling in relevant animal models, including selectivity assessment in normal tissues and genotoxicity evaluation of major metabolites. Only upon establishment of a robust safety margin and comprehensive pharmacokinetic data can cautious exploration of bergamottin’s adjuvant potential be ethically justified. In conclusion, while bergamottin exhibits a mechanistically intriguing preclinical profile as a multi-target chemosensitizer, several fundamental limitations preclude immediate clinical translation. Its standalone antitumor efficacy in vivo appears modest, and its safety profile—particularly hepatotoxicity risk and the potential for clinically significant DDIs via CYP3A4 and P-gp inhibition—remains incompletely characterized. The absence of human pharmacokinetic and toxicity data, combined with batch-to-batch variability in plant-derived sourcing, positions bergamottin at an early proof-of-concept stage rather than a development-ready therapeutic candidate. Future research should therefore prioritize systematic preclinical toxicity profiling, including selectivity assessment in normal tissues, structure-activity optimization to mitigate adverse effects, and advanced formulation development to improve bioavailability and reduce systemic exposure. Only upon establishment of a robust safety margin can cautious exploration of bergamottin’s adjuvant potential in combination regimens be ethically and scientifically justified.
