Single nucleotide switches confer bacteriophage resistance to Pseudomonas protegens
Authors: Jordan Vacheron, Clara M Heiman, Daniel Garrido-Sanz, Martine Caroff, Maryam Darabi, Christoph Keel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhage therapy offers a promising strategy against bacterial pathogens in medicine and agriculture, but the rise of phage-resistant bacteria presents a significant challenge to its sustainability. Here, we used an environmental model bacterium, Pseudomonas protegens CHA0, to investigate phage resistance mechanisms in laboratory conditions through genomic analysis of four phage-resistant variants (C2, C4, C17, C18). Whole-genome sequencing revealed frequent deletions, insertions, and single nucleotide substitutions, particularly in genes encoding enzymes involved in cell surface modifications. The T428P mutation in AlgC, a phosphoglucomutase, and the P229T substitution in YkcC, a glycosyltransferase, each conferred resistance by altering phage receptor accessibility while preserving bacterial fitness. These findings emphasize that subtle mutations in surface–modifying enzymes enable P. protegens to evolve resistance to bacteriophages without compromising their ecological performance.

Introduction

The alarming rise of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the most pressing challenges of modern medicine and environmental microbiology (Larsson and Flach 2022, Darby et al. 2023). Various strategies have been proposed to combat this problem, some of which include destabilizing bacterial membranes to enhance antibiotic uptake (Lin et al. 2015) or inhibiting efflux pumps to reduce antibiotic release and consequently increase antibiotic concentration in bacterial cells (Alav et al. 2022). While these strategies aim to make current antibiotics more efficient by thwarting resistance mechanisms, a biologically driven approach is resurfacing. This approach permits to target specific bacteria without collateral damage to beneficial commensal microorganisms by relying on the specific interaction between bacteria and one of their natural predators, bacteriophages. Bacteriophages, or phages and their derivatives (e.g. tailocins or lysins) currently represent a promising alternative to combat AMR, as evidenced by recent cases, such as the remission of a patient previously infected with multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Köhler et al. 2023). However, relying on phages solely, even in combination with antibiotics, often falls short of ensuring pathogen eradication (Nilsson 2019, Principi et al. 2019). As against antibiotics, bacteria have evolved multiple mechanisms to resist phage attacks, leading to a parallel arms race between bacteria and antibiotics as well as phages.

One prominent strategy employed by bacteria to resist phage infection involves the utilization of the CRISPR-Cas system for adaptive immunity, storing and recalling phage DNA fragments. In addition to this strategy, bacteria can also produce anti-phage proteins that disrupt different stages of the life cycle of their viral predator. Bacteria can also modify their cell surface decorations that serve as phage receptors. For instance, bacteria can alter or mask these receptors, making it challenging for phages to recognize and bind to their target cells (Bernheim and Sorek 2020). The interplay between phages and bacteria, shaped by these diverse resistance mechanisms, highlights the complexity of the microbial warfare that unfolds in diverse environments, including clinical and agricultural settings.

While phage resistance is well documented, its fitness costs are highly context-dependent. In some cases, resistance mutations can impair nutrient uptake, motility, or virulence, suggesting a cost-benefit balance under phage pressure (Bohannan and Lenski 2000, Burmeister et al. 2020, Gurney et al. 2020). For instance, mutations that confer resistance by altering cell-surface lipopolysaccharide (LPS) molecules can incur significant pleiotropic costs because these changes may impair the essential functions of the molecules and thereby reduce bacterial fitness (Brockhurst et al. 2004, Koskella and Brockhurst 2014). Understanding this variability is essential for predicting the persistence of resistance and for designing sustainable phage applications in clinical or agricultural systems (Mangalea and Duerkop 2020).

In this study, we investigated spontaneous phage-resistant variants of Pseudomonas protegens CHA0, a beneficial plant-associated bacterium targeted by the virulent phage ΦGP100. Previous work demonstrated that ΦGP100 reduces CHA0 root colonization and biocontrol activity in the rhizosphere of cucumber plants, while natural phage-resistant variants of CHA0 that spontaneously emerged following phage exposure on agar plates retained their protective effect against pathogens and were no longer susceptible to ΦGP100 (Keel et al. 2002). We sequenced and characterized four of these phage-resistant variants isolated in 2002 to identify the mutations underlying resistance and to evaluate their potential fitness costs. We found that phage resistance arises from specific single nucleotide substitutions in genes involved in LPS biosynthesis, namely algC and ykcC. We then assessed the structural consequences of these mutations, their impact on phage susceptibility, and their ecological relevance across multiple fitness assays. Our results provide a mechanistic example of how bacteria can resist phages through limited surface modifications without detectable trade-offs, offering insights into the evolutionary stability of resistance in beneficial microbes.

Materials and methods

Genome sequencing and variant calling analysis

Four phage ΦGP100-resistant bacterial variants of P. protegens CHA0 (C2, C4, C17, and C18) were obtained within the clear lytic halo, after two to three days of exposure of a bacterial lawn of CHA0 to the phage (Keel et al. 2002). The four CHA0 variants were cultured overnight in nutrient yeast broth (NYB) and their genomic DNA was extracted using the MagAttract high-molecular-weight (HMW) DNA kit (Qiagen). DNA samples were then sheared in Covaris g-TUBEs to obtain fragments with an average length of 10 kb. The PacBio SMRTbell template preparation kit v1.0 was used to prepare the libraries. The resulting libraries were size selected on a BluePippin system (Sage Science, Inc.) for molecules larger than 7 kb. The libraries were multiplexed and sequenced using one single-molecule real-time (SMRT) cell and a Sequel system (movie length, 600 min). Genome assembly was performed using the RS_HGAP_Assembly.4 protocol in SMRT Link v6.0. Variant calling analysis was performed using Snippy (version 4.3.6. Seeman) to identify genomic differences that could explain the emergence of the phage resistance in the genomes of the four ΦGP100-resistant variants of CHA0 (C2, C4, C17, and C18). The complete genome of P. protegens CHA0 (accession number LS999205, Smits et al. 2019) was used as reference for these analyses. Graph representations were generated using the R package circlize (Gu et al. 2014) in RStudio (R version 4.4.0).

Deletion mutants and site-directed mutagenesis

Target gene deletions were performed following the procedures previously outlined in Vacheron et al. (2019). Site-directed mutagenesis was realized as previously described in Kupferschmied et al. (2014). Briefly, the entire genes of interest (either ykcC or algC) were cloned into the suicide plasmid pEMG, resulting in plasmids designated as pME11180 and pME11181, respectively (Tables S1 and S2, Supporting Information). PCR amplification was then performed, using these plasmids as templates and sets of primers designed to introduce specific mutations to subsequently replace the desired amino acid residues. Following PCR amplification, the plasmid templates used were digested with DnpI for 1 h at 37°C. PCR products were purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Germany). The purified PCR products were then electroporated into E. coli DH5α λpir cells. Selection of E. coli that had successfully recircularized the vectors was performed on lysogeny broth (LB) agar supplemented with kanamycin (25 µg mL−1). Once these vectors were obtained (Table S1, Supporting Information), they were electroporated into CHA0 for double recombination as described previously by Kupferschmied et al. (2014). All constructed plasmids and mutant strains were controlled by DNA sequencing (Microsynth AG).

Validation of the phage resistance phenotypes

The sensitivity of the CHA0 wild type and the different variants and mutants to the phage ΦGP100 was tested using the double-layer agar method. Briefly, 50 µL of overnight bacterial cultures were mixed with 4 mL of LB soft agar (7.5% of agar) and poured onto a nutrient agar plate. Serial dilutions of the phage suspension were spotted (4 µL) onto the bacterial lawn. All the plates were incubated overnight at 25°C.

Analysis of amino-acid conservation and protein structure visualization

To determine if the amino acid substitutions observed in the natural phage-resistant variants of CHA0 affected the conserved regions of the proteins through bacterial evolution, we aligned the amino acid sequences of AlgC and YkcC orthologs detected in Pseudomonas and more distantly related bacterial genomes. Amino acid sequences were obtained by Blastp and aligned using ClustalO (Sievers and Higgins 2014). Amino-acid conservation was characterized for each residue position by calculating the Shenkin divergence score (Shenkin et al. 1991) and visualized using JalView (Troshin et al. 2018). The structures of AlgC and YkcC were predicted using AlphaFold 3 (Abramson et al. 2024) and visualized with ChimeraX v1.8 (Meng et al. 2023).

Protein purification

The wild-type and variant proteins of AlgC were produced and purified using standard techniques. Briefly, the wild-type and mutant coding sequences for algC were cloned into a pET-based expression vector under control of a T7 promoter (Table S1, Supporting Information). A His-tag was added at the N-terminus to enable pulldown after overexpression in Escherichia coli. The resulting expression vectors were transformed into chemically competent BL21(DE3) cells and clones were selected on LB plates containing 50 µg mL−1 of kanamycin. Colonies were then inoculated into 10 mL of TB (terrific broth) medium and grown to an OD600nm of 0.5 at 37°C. The cultures were then cooled down to 18°C and expression was induced by the addition of 0.4 mM of IPTG and incubated overnight (16 h). To collect samples for “total protein” contents of the bacteria, 60 µL of each culture was spun down, and the pellet was resuspended in 50 µL of 1× SDS loading dye (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 5% beta-mercaptoethanol, 2% SDS, 0.1% bromophenol blue, 10% glycerol). The suspension was then heated to 95°C for 10 min and centrifuged at 14 000 rpm for 10 min to pellet cell debris. Eight µL of the resulting supernatants were loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels. The remainder of the overnight culture (10 mL) was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatants were discarded, and the pellets were resuspended in 2 mL of lysis buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 25 mM imidazole). The cell suspensions were then sonicated to lyse the cells, and the lysate was cleared by centrifugation at 15 000 rpm at 4°C. The supernatant was incubated with magnetic His-affinity resin (HisMag Sepharose, Cytiva, USA) for 1 h at 4°C on a rotating wheel. Then the beads were collected on a magnetic rack and washed twice with 1 mL of lysis buffer. Finally, the bound material was eluted by incubation with 30 µL of elution buffer (lysis buffer supplemented with 500 mM imidazole). The supernatant was mixed with an equal volume of 2× SDS loading dye (100 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 10% beta-mercaptoethanol, 4% SDS, 0.2% bromophenol blue, 20% glycerol), heated to 95°C for 5 min, and 8 µL of this solution was loaded onto an SDS-PAGE gel. After the run, the gel was fixed using a fixing solution (50% ethanol, 10% acetic acid) and then stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (50% methanol, 10% acetic acid, 2 mg mL1 Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-350).

Bacterial growth monitoring

To test differences in the growth of the variants and mutants with the wild type CHA0, bacterial suspensions obtained from an overnight NYB culture were restarted (1:100 v/v) in fresh medium until they reached an OD600nm of 0.5. Then 5 µL of a bacterial suspension from an OD600nm of 1 were inoculated in each well of the 96-well plate containing NYB rich medium (i.e. final OD600nm of 0.05). The bacterial growth of the different strains was monitored every 10 min for 24 h using a Synergy H1 plate reader (BioTek, USA). Three independent replicates were performed. Growth parameters such as the carrying capacity, the growth rate, the generation time, and the area under the curve were calculated using the R package GrowthCurver (Sprouffske and Wagner 2016). Statistical differences were determined by ANOVA coupled with Tukey’s HSD test, after assessing the dataset for normality and homoscedasticity.

Phage adsorption assay

The adsorption rate of the phage ΦGP100 was determined as previously described in Vacheron et al. (2023). Briefly, a bacterial suspension of 400 µL containing 2.5 × 108 bacterial cells of each bacterial strain at exponential growth stage (OD600nm = 1) was mixed with 100 µL containing 2.5 × 107 PFU, i.e. at an MOI of 0.1, and incubated at room temperature for 5 min without shaking. A total of 100 µL of this suspension was collected in a new tube containing 5 µL of chloroform and then proceeded to serial dilution before plating onto a lawn of CHA0. The plates were incubated overnight at room temperature before counting plaque. This experiment was done three times.

Lipopolysaccharide extraction and visualization on SDS-PAGE

Biomass was collected from 1 L of bacterial culture and lyophilized prior to LPS extraction. The dried material was then used for LPS isolation. LPSs were extracted by a patented protocol of the company LPS-BioSciences, using isobutyric acid/1 M ammonium hydroxide method. To minimize the presence of capsular polysaccharides and prevent their interference with LPS migration, the samples underwent several washing steps prior to processing. The effectiveness of this step was verified using Alcian Blue staining, a cationic dye that binds to acidic polysaccharides typically present in the bacterial capsule. Extracted LPS was then separated by SDS–PAGE (separating and stacking gels containing 15% and 4% acrylamide, respectively). Electrophoresis was carried out in Tris-glycine-SDS buffer at a constant current of 20 mA for stacking gels and 25 mA for separating gels. The gel was then fixed and stained with silver nitrate.

Competition assays and spatial distribution

Pairwise competition assays were performed as previously described in Vacheron et al. (2019). In brief, adjusted bacterial cultures of the tagged strains were mixed at a 1:1 ratio, and 100 µL of the mixture was spotted onto a disk placed on a nutrient agar plate. The plates were incubated for 24 h at 25°C prior to colony forming unit (CFU) enumeration. Four disks were placed on each plate, and each condition was tested in triplicate. To assess the spatial distribution of the competitors, the plates were incubated for an additional week at 25°C, after which images were captured using a Dino-Lite AM4115T-GRFBY digital microscope (Dino-Lite, France) equipped with 465/580 nm LEDs and 510/610 nm emission filters to detect the mScarlet-I and GFP-tagged strains. The images were then analyzed using a custom R script to quantify the surface area occupied by each tagged strain.

In planta fitness

Each tagged bacterial strain was inoculated at a concentration of 2 × 104 CFU/g into four microcosms containing a soil matrix, a soil extract, a standardized complex natural wheat rhizosphere community (RhizCom), and a pregerminated 2-day-old wheat seedling (Triticum aestivum cv. Arina), as described in Garrido-Sanz and Keel (2025). The inoculated plantlets were incubated for 7 days in a growth chamber with a 22/18°C day/night cycle, a 16 h/8 h light/dark photoperiod (light intensity: 160 µE m−2 s−1), and 70% relative humidity. The roots, together with their associated soil, were excised at the point of emergence from the seed. Rhizosphere samples from four individual plants were pooled, and the total weight of each composite sample was measured. To each pooled sample, 25 mL of minimal medium (MM) was added, and the suspension was vortexed for 20 min. The samples were then centrifuged at 300 × g for 1 min to pellet soil debris. The supernatant (rhizosphere wash) was collected and used for CFU counts. The RhizCom was enumerated by plating on R2A agar, and the tagged strains were enumerated by plating on NA supplemented with gentamicin (10 µM). This experiment was performed three times independently.

Statistical analysis

For the bacterial growth monitoring and microcosm experiments, normality and homoscedasticity were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk and Bartlett tests, respectively. If both conditions were satisfied (P ≥ 0.05), one-way ANOVA followed by a Tukey HSD test was performed. If either condition was not satisfied (P < 0.05), a Kruskal–Wallis test followed by a Dunn test was used. The significance threshold was set at P < 0.05. In the competition assays, competitive indices were compared using t-tests, and each index was additionally compared to the theoretical value of 1 (representing competitive equilibrium between strains).

Results and discussion

Identification of mutations in genes involved in cell surface decorations in phage-resistant variants of Pseudomonas protegens CHA0

The four bacterial variants that are resistant to the phage ΦGP100 were originally isolated in 2002 during double-agar experiments in which small colonies emerged within the lysis zone of the phage (Keel et al. 2002). This study confirmed their resistance to the phage by conducting double-layer agar experiments using each of the isolated variants in the second layer (Fig. 1A).

(B) Overview of the distribution of mutations occurring within the coding sequences for the different phage-resistant variants. The genomic locations of the different mutations are represented by squares, circles and triangles, substitution, insertion, and deletion mutations, respectively.

To identify the genetic basis of phage resistance in the four different CHA0 variants (C2, C4, C17, and C18), long-read sequencing (PacBio) was used to detect mutations. The genome sequences of the phage-resistant variants were then compared with the complete genome sequence of the CHA0 wild type (LS999205.1). Genomic analysis of the phage-resistant bacterial variants revealed a complex landscape of mutations, with both shared patterns and unique differences among the strains (Fig. 1B; Tables S3–S6, Supporting Information). Deletions in coding sequences emerged as the predominant type of mutation, occurring in all variants and ranging from 10 to 14 events per genome. In addition to deletions, insertions and substitutions were also identified, albeit less frequently. Furthermore, between 14 and 21 mutations were detected in intergenic regions for each variant, with several of these mutations being common among variants. The reference genome used in this study was sequenced in 2019 from a CHA0 strain that had been maintained and propagated over time, in a manner similar to that described for other bacterial models such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 (Klockgether et al. 2010, Chandler et al. 2019). In contrast, the four phage-resistant variants were isolated in 2002 and stored at −80°C without further culturing. It is therefore possible that the CHA0 reference strain accumulated mutations over the subsequent 17 years, resulting in discrepancies that are unrelated to the phage resistance phenotype.

While we cannot rule out the possibility that additional mutations, including those in intergenic regions or regulatory elements, may contribute to phage resistance, our focus was placed on mutations affecting genes involved in cell surface decorations. This decision was based on our prior Tn-Seq data demonstrating the importance of surface decorations in phage sensitivity (Vacheron et al. 2023). Among them, two single nucleotide substitutions were identified as potential candidates: one in algC, observed in the genomes of variants C17 and C18, and one in ykcC, which encodes a glycosyltransferase in the gene cluster for the long O-polysaccharide (O-PS), observed in the genomes of variants C2 and C4 (Tables S3–S6, Supporting Information). These were selected as the primary targets to confirm that the observed single nucleotide changes in the phage-resistant variants specifically confer resistance to the phage ΦGP100 and to understand their impact on the affected proteins.

Point mutations in surface glycan biosynthesis genes confer phage resistance

AlgC is a bisubstrate mutase that transfers a phosphate group from position 6 to position 1 on glucose and mannose (Coyne et al. 1994, Olvera et al. 1999, Feng et al. 2023). This enzyme plays a crucial role in carbohydrate metabolism by catalyzing the interconversion of glucose-1P and glucose-6-P or mannose-1P and mannose-6P, which are important intermediates in the biosynthesis of glycoconjugates, such as LPS in bacteria (Coyne et al. 1994). The spontaneous mutants C17 and C18, which are resistant to the phage ΦGP100 (Fig. 1A), were found to harbor a single nucleotide substitution (C1279G) in algC, resulting in a T428P amino acid change (Fig. 2A). To test the functional impact of this mutation, we introduced the same T428P substitution into the wild-type CHA0 genome via site-directed mutagenesis and we also generated a full gene deletion. Both the point mutant (AlgC*) and the deletion mutant (ΔalgC) displayed complete resistance to phage ΦGP100 (Fig. 2B), confirming that disruption of algC alone is sufficient to confer resistance. The T428 residue is located near the active site of AlgC (Fig. 2A and 2C). Although they are distant in the linear amino acid sequence, the predicted protein folding (AlphaFold prediction) brings these predicted active sites into proximity to form a functional pocket (Fig. 2C). This specific folding likely contributes to the catalytic activity of AlgC by positioning key residues, such as T428, in close interaction with the active site. Sequence alignment reveals that the T428 residue in AlgC is conserved across species within the Pseudomonas genus and Neisseria gonorrhoeae, while it can be different in other bacterial species (Fig. 2D). Although there are variations in the residues observed at this position in other species, none of them carry a proline at this position, as seen in the C17 and C18 mutants. On the one hand, this variation in orthologs across different bacterial lineages suggests a degree of functional flexibility in the active site of AlgC, which could have implications for its role in glycoconjugate biosynthesis, phage resistance, and other cellular processes. On the other hand, replacing threonine with a proline could significantly impact the functionality of the enzyme, given that prolines are known for their distinctive properties that can affect protein structure (Bajaj et al. 2007, Melnikov et al. 2016). Indeed, unlike most amino acids, proline possesses a cyclic structure, forming a rigid ring, which restricts its flexibility. This characteristic sets proline apart in the context of protein structures, as it introduces kinks or bends in the polypeptide chain (Bajaj et al. 2007, Melnikov et al. 2016). To determine if the mutation affects AlgC folding and/or protein stability, we His-tagged both the wild-type and variant proteins and purified them following heterologous expression in E. coli (Supplementary Fig. S1). The bands corresponding to wild-type and variant AlgC appeared identical in size and intensity (Supplementary Fig. S1), indicating that the variant protein is produced at comparable levels. These findings suggest that the mutation does not alter the physicochemical properties of AlgC, such as solubility, and confirm that the variant remains stable and soluble.

A second mutation in cell surface decorations was identified in variants C2 and C4, consisting of a P229T substitution in ykcC (Fig. 3A). This gene, which encodes a glycosyltransferase, is located within the previously described OBC3 gene cluster involved in the formation of long O-PS in CHA0 (Kupferschmied et al. 2016, Vacheron et al. 2023). Functional validation through site-directed mutagenesis and gene deletion confirmed that both the P229T mutation and the full loss of ykcC both confer phage resistance in CHA0 (Fig. 3B). Structural protein predictions indicated that P229 is located away from the predicted catalytic site, but it still may contribute to protein folding or stability (Fig. 3C). Unfortunately, the purification of the His-tagged YkcC protein was unsuccessful, potentially due its low solubility. A comparative analysis of ykcC homologues revealed that the proline at position 229 is highly conserved and is never replaced by threonine in any of the examined species (Fig. 3D). This suggests that it may play a key role in maintaining the structure and function of this enzyme.

These two cases demonstrate that distinct point mutations targeting surface glycan biosynthesis pathways are sufficient to prevent phage infection, and that their precise location (whether proximal or distal to active sites) can have substantial phenotypic consequences. The lack of evolutionary fixation of the mutations across species in both cases supports the idea that they emerge rarely under specific selection pressures, such as phage exposure.

Phage resistance is associated with specific LPS alterations

Phage adsorption assays revealed that all resistant variants and mutants exhibited a drastic reduction in ΦGP100 binding, comparable to the Δobc3 mutant lacking the long O-antigen (Supplementary Fig. S2). This suggests that resistance is linked to alterations in cell surface decorations, leading us to examine LPS profiles in more detail.

To assess whether mutations in algC and ykcC affect LPS structure, we analyzed the LPS extracts of the four phage-resistant variants (C2, C4, C17, C18) using SDS-PAGE followed by silver staining (Fig. 4). As references, we included the wild-type CHA0 strain as well as known LPS-deficient mutants: ΔwbpL (lacking both short and long O-PS), Δwzx (no short O-PS), and Δobc3 (no long O-PS).

M: LPS-BioScience Marker. This image has been cropped to highlight the LPS profile of the bacterial strains relevant to this study. The full uncropped gel image is provided in Supplementary Fig. S3.

Variants C2 and C4 (harboring the ykcC mutation) displayed an LPS profile similar to that of CHA0, with visible bands corresponding to short and long O-polysaccharides. However, some bands were missing at the very top of the long O-PS. These results demonstrate that a subtle or localized changes to the glycan structure are sufficient to impair phage adsorption without visibly affecting the O-PS architecture.

In contrast, variants C17 and C18 (carrying the algC T428P mutation) showed slightly stronger staining in the short O-PS region, alongside a decrease in the long O-antigen signal. This shift points to a remodeling of the LPS structure, possibly reflecting an altered efficiency or specificity of sugar transfer during O-PS extension. Variants C17 and C18 displayed an LPS profile similar to that of the Δobc3 mutant, which lacks the long O-PS. This suggests that the T428P mutation in algC results in a significant reduction in long O-PS, while retaining the core and short O-PS.

Altogether, the LPS analysis reveals that phage-resistant variants exhibit distinct disruptions in the long O-antigen region. Variants C2 and C4 (ykcC mutants) maintain a long O-antigen ladder, albeit with fainter high-molecular-weight bands, suggesting subtle modifications to the chain length or structure. By contrast, variants C17 and C18 (algC mutants) lack detectable long O-antigen bands and resemble the Δobc3 mutant, indicating a more severe defect in long O-PS production. These findings demonstrate that both minor and more extensive alterations to surface glycans can prevent phage adsorption, emphasizing the sensitivity of phage-host interactions to the fine architecture of the bacterial envelope.

Phage resistance mutations do not impair competition and colonization abilities

While these LPS alterations clearly play a role in phage resistance, we next assessed whether such mutations also influence bacterial fitness and ecological performance when phage pressure is absent.

In liquid culture, all phage-resistant variants showed growth comparable to that of CHA0, with no significant differences observed (Fig. 5A). The only exception was the ΔalgC full gene deletion mutant, which exhibited impaired growth. This is consistent with the pleiotropic role of AlgC in central sugar metabolism, which extends beyond LPS biosynthesis. In direct competition assays (Fig. 5B), in which CHA0 was co-inoculated with each mutant or variant onto agar plates, most strains outcompeted the wild type. Variants C17 and C18 and C2 showed a significant advantage over CHA0, whereas C4 behaved similarly to the wild type. The ΔalgC mutant was strongly outcompeted, reinforcing the idea that full loss of AlgC affects core metabolic functions. These fitness trends were further explored using fluorescently tagged strains in colony expansion assays over an extended period of time (Fig. 5C). Spatial overlap between the tested strains (red) and CHA0 (green) generated a yellow central zone, reflecting their shared distribution. Overall, no major segregation differences were observed, except again for the ΔalgC mutant, which exhibited reduced spatial expansion. By contrast, the ΔykcC mutant was well distributed throughout the colony. Finally, we evaluated the fitness of the variants and mutants in a plant-soil microcosm system. This involved inoculating individual strains within a standardized, complex, natural wheat rhizosphere community (RhizCom) (Garrido-Sanz and Keel 2025) and quantifying their root colonization abilities (Fig. 5D). All phage-resistant variants colonized the roots as efficiently as the wild type CHA0 in presence of the RhizCom, except for the ΔalgC mutant, which exhibited a consistent fitness defect. The overall abundance of the RhizCom remained stable across treatments.

Taken together, these results indicate that the mutations conferring phage resistance do not lead to major fitness trade-offs under the tested conditions. Variants carrying point mutations in algC or ykcC retain their ability to grow, compete, expand, and colonize effectively. Although trade-offs may arise in the presence of phage or in more complex environments, no substantial cost was detected here, which highlights the potential for the stable maintenance of resistance in the absence of selective pressure. It is important to emphasize, however, that this resistance emerged and was characterized under in vitro laboratory conditions, where nutrients are abundant and selective pressures are minimal. This could potentially favor the selection of low-cost resistance mutations that may not arise or persist under more restrictive natural conditions.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrate that specific point mutations in algC and ykcC in P. protegens CHA0 confer robust resistance to phage ΦGP100 under laboratory conditions without any detectable fitness costs. These mutations subtly remodel LPS architecture, enabling evasion of phage adsorption while preserving growth, competitiveness, and root colonization.

It is important to note that this resistance emerged and was studied in vitro, under nutrient-rich and low-stress conditions that may favor the emergence of low-cost resistance. Whether similar outcomes would occur in more complex or resource-limited natural environments remains to be tested.

Overall, our findings illustrate that phage resistance does not necessarily impose a burden on bacterial fitness and can represent an advantageous adaptation under certain circumstances with no evident trade-off.
