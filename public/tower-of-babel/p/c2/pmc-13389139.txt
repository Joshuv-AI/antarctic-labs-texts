Cardiovascular safety of romosozumab versus denosumab for osteoporosis treatment: a multinational real-world data analysis
Authors: Balqis Istiqomah Gusbela, Min Huei Hsu, Chun Feng Huang, Meng Huang Wu, Daniel C. A. Nugroho, Muhammad Solihuddin Muhtar, Christine Y. Lu, Ming Hung Teng, Jason C. Hsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Romosozumab and denosumab are two highly effective, widely used osteoporosis treatments. There has been no study directly comparing the effectiveness of romosozumab and denosumab in a clinical setting using large-scale, real-world data.Objectives:To evaluate the association between romosozumab and cardiovascular outcomes compared to denosumab in patients with osteoporosis.Design:Retrospective propensity score-matched cohort study.Methods:Using the TriNetX global health research network, we identified 59,058 patients aged 60 years or older with osteoporosis who initiated romosozumab (n = 5200) or denosumab (n = 53,858) between January 2019 and December 2024. After 1:1 propensity score matching, 2 balanced cohorts of 5192 patients each were obtained. Cardiovascular outcomes, including three-point major adverse cardiac events (3P-MACE ), 4P-MACE, 5P-MACE, individual MACE components, and other cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), were assessed across three follow-up periods: short-term (1 year), mid-term (3 years), and long-term (5 years). Primary analyses were conducted among patients without a prior cardiovascular history, and sensitivity analyses were performed among those with a prior cardiovascular history.Results:Among patients without a prior cardiovascular history, romosozumab was associated with significantly lower risks of 3P-MACE at 3-year (hazard ratio (HR) 0.645, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.438–0.950, p = 0.023) and 5-year follow-up (HR 0.755, 95% CI 0.512–0.873, p = 0.028), and lower mortality at 3-year (HR 0.554, 95% CI 0.316–0.972, p = 0.038) and 5-year follow-up (HR 0.533, 95% CI 0.312–0.913, p = 0.028). No significant differences were observed at 1-year follow-up. In contrast, among patients with a prior cardiovascular history, romosozumab was associated with significantly higher risks of 4P-MACE, 5P-MACE, stroke, mortality, and chronic ischemic heart disease across all three follow-up time horizons.Conclusion:Romosozumab was associated with lower risks of 3P-MACE and mortality compared to denosumab among patients without a prior cardiovascular history at mid-term and long-term follow-up. In contrast, romosozumab was associated with consistently higher cardiovascular risks in patients with preexisting CVD. These findings highlight the importance of careful cardiovascular risk assessment prior to initiating romosozumab, particularly in patients with preexisting CVD. The 3P-MACE finding in patients without prior cardiovascular history is the primary confirmatory result; all other outcomes should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a prevalent metabolic disorder that exacerbates the risk of fractures, weakens bone strength, and is a significant public health concern, particularly among postmenopausal women and the elderly.1,2 Pharmacological treatments aimed at reducing fracture risks in osteoporosis patients have significantly expanded over the last 3 decades. 3 Several pharmacological treatments are available for preventing and managing osteoporosis. These include bisphosphonates, selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs), parathyroid hormone (PTH) analogues, receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) inhibitors, and sclerostin inhibitors.4 –6 The choice of anti-osteoporotic treatment depends on the type of osteoporosis, fracture risk, age, comorbidities, and drug safety, ensuring that treatment is tailored to individual patient requirements.4,6

Denosumab and romosozumab have recently become two highly effective and widely used osteoporosis treatments. 7 The fully human monoclonal antibody, denosumab, is a potent antiresorptive drug for treating osteoporosis. 8 Clinical trial data spanning approximately 15 years have demonstrated its safety and efficacy in reducing fracture risks. 8 Romosozumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that targets the sclerostin protein, which inhibits bone formation.9,10 Romosozumab is unique in its dual mechanism of action of increasing bone formation while decreasing bone resorption.10,11 Romosozumab was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2019. 12 It is currently prescribed in the US, Europe, Australia, and a few Asian countries, such as Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan.13 –15

Romosozumab’s use approval was temporarily withheld due to cardiovascular disease (CVD) safety concerns and restrictions. The USFDA prescribing information includes a boxed warning stating that romosozumab should not be initiated in patients who have had a myocardial infarction or stroke within the previous year. Similarly, the European Medicines Agency Summary of Product Characteristics advises against use in patients with a history of myocardial infarction or stroke, although this is not presented as a boxed warning.13,16 The Active-Controlled Fracture Study in Postmenopausal Women with Osteoporosis at High Risk (ARCH) clinical trial found that patients receiving romosozumab had higher incidences of serious adverse CVD events than those treated with alendronate.17 –19 Another study discovered an increased risk of four-point major adverse cardiac events (MACE), which includes myocardial infarction and stroke, in elderly men and postmenopausal women with osteoporosis. 20

Real-world evidence evaluating cardiovascular outcomes associated with romosozumab remains limited and is needed to inform clinical practice.16,21 Accordingly, a better interpretation of CVD events is mandated to determine the risks and benefits of prescribing anti-osteoporosis medications. 22 Nevertheless, this point has thus far been overlooked, and no study has directly compared the efficacy of romosozumab and denosumab in clinical settings using large-scale, real-world data.7,20 Therefore, we utilized TriNetX big data to analyze and compare the CVD safety of romosozumab and denosumab.

Methods

Data source

Data utilized in this study were collected in March 2026 from the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network (https://trinetx.com/), a federated research platform that aggregates de-identified electronic health records (EHRs) from healthcare organizations worldwide, including academic medical centers, community hospitals, and specialty practices and which provides access to electronic medical records (diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory values, and genomic information) of approximately 191,052,147 patients from 174 healthcare organizations.

Study design and study populations

The study populations included all patients aged 60 years or older who began treatment with romosozumab or denosumab for osteoporosis management between January 2019 and December 2024. We identified the diagnosis through International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD-10). Osteoporosis was identified using ICD-10 codes M80 (osteoporosis with current pathological fracture) and M81 (osteoporosis without current pathological fracture). Exclusion criteria were broadly defined as patients with prior CVD, as indicated by ICD-10 codes I00–I99 (diseases of the circulatory system). Malignant neoplasms were identified using ICD-10 codes C00–C97 (malignant neoplasms). Patients with documented exposure to both romosozumab and denosumab before the index date were excluded. The primary analytical cohort comprised patients without prior CVD (no ICD-10 codes I00–I99 before the index date). A separate, pre-specified sensitivity cohort was independently constructed from patients with documented prior CVD (at least one ICD-10 code I00–I99 before the index date), using the same TriNetX database, drug exposure criteria, and propensity score matching (PSM) procedures. This sensitivity cohort was independently analyzed and was not part of the primary analysis.

Denosumab was chosen as a comparator because of its similar clinical indications for treating osteoporosis and its significant efficiency in reducing fracture risks. 4 Evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses20,23 indicates that denosumab does not significantly alter cardiovascular risks compared to a placebo or other antiresorptives, although some observational studies suggested potential differences (Figure 1).

Drug exposure and follow-up

Patients entered the cohort on the date of their first administration of romosozumab (RXNORM: 2123126) or denosumab (RXNORM: 993449). To reduce immortal-time bias and ensure therapy persistence, we required patients to remain on treatment for at least 365 days. The index date was therefore defined as the first day after the initial dose. To attribute observed outcomes to a single index agent and minimize confounding by indication and potential carryover effects arising from sequential therapy, patients with documented exposure to both romosozumab and denosumab at any point during the study period were excluded from the analysis. Treatment exposure was thus defined as a fixed, mutually exclusive classification established at cohort entry and was not treated as a time-varying covariate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the TriNetX platform. Baseline characteristics between cohorts were compared using a two-sided t-test, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05 and a standardized mean difference (SMD) of at least 0.1. PSM was conducted using TriNetX’s built-in algorithm, with a 1:1 greedy nearest-neighbor matching method and a caliper distance of 0.1 pooled standard deviations (SDs) of the propensity score logit.

A survival analysis was performed to assess outcome probabilities across three pre-specified follow-up time horizons: short-term (1 year), mid-term (3 years), and long-term (5 years), with calculations performed at daily intervals. Each time horizon was conducted as a separate, independent time-to-event analysis. Only patients with documented observation times in the TriNetX database meeting or exceeding the respective follow-up durations from the index date were included in each analysis (1, 3, and 5 years, respectively), using the TriNetX “Require a Specified Follow-Up Period” feature. Consequently, denominators differed across time horizons and should not be interpreted as a sequential at-risk count within a single Kaplan–Meier model.

Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), log-rank tests, and Kaplan–Meier cumulative incidence curves were generated using Python. All p values were derived from the same Cox proportional hazards model as the corresponding HRs and CIs to ensure internal consistency. Given the exploratory nature of secondary and other cardiovascular outcomes, no formal correction for multiple testing was applied, and findings are presented with an explicit outcome hierarchy across three follow-up time horizons to aid transparent interpretation. The reporting of this study conformed to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement.

Covariates and outcomes

Covariates used for PSM included demographics, comorbidities, concomitant medications, prior fracture history, and laboratory values. A full list of covariates is provided in Supplemental eTable 3. The primary outcome was 3P-MACE (myocardial infarction, stroke, and mortality), with 4P-MACE (adding heart failure) and 5P-MACE (adding angina) as secondary composite outcomes. Individual MACE components and other CVDs, including hypertension, cardiomyopathy, peripheral arterial disease, aortic stenosis, and chronic ischemic heart disease, were examined as exploratory outcomes. All outcomes were identified using ICD-10 diagnostic codes provided in Supplemental eTable 4. Encounters for immunization (ICD-10: Z23), skin and subcutaneous disorders (ICD-10: L00–L08), and contact dermatitis (ICD-10: L23–L25) were included as negative control outcomes to assess the adequacy of confounding control, as these conditions have no plausible biological or pharmacological relationship with either romosozumab or denosumab and should not systematically differ between the two treatment groups. Primary analyses were conducted among patients without a prior cardiovascular history, and sensitivity analyses were conducted among patients with a prior cardiovascular history, both stratified by short-term (1-year), mid-term (3-year), and long-term (5-year) follow-up time periods. For each outcome, patients with a pre-existing diagnosis of that specific condition at baseline were excluded from the denominator, resulting in outcome-specific at-risk populations that may differ from the total matched cohort size of 5192 per group.

Before PSM, the romosozumab group comprised 5200 patients, compared to 53,858 in the denosumab group. Romosozumab users were slightly younger (mean age 72.4 vs 73.7 years) and had a higher proportion of females (97.7% vs 93.5%). The romosozumab group had a higher prevalence of prior fractures across all skeletal sites, greater use of bisphosphonates (29.1% vs 20.4%), SERMs (5.7% vs 2.8%), and PTH analogues (10.4% vs 4.0%), and higher rates of outpatient, inpatient, and emergency service utilization. Denosumab users had higher rates of type 2 diabetes mellitus (15.3% vs 11.8%) and chronic kidney disease (11.2% vs 8.8%). Laboratory values showed higher glucose (108.3 vs 101.8 mg/dL), urea nitrogen (18.1 vs 16.9 mg/dL), and intact PTH (65.8 vs 50.6 pg/mL) in the denosumab group, while romosozumab users had higher high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (68.3 vs 63.4 mg/dL) and slightly lower glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c; 5.8% vs 6.0%). Several variables showed statistically significant differences before matching, with SMDs exceeding 0.1. After 1:1 PSM, both groups comprised 5192 patients each. The majority of baseline characteristics were well balanced, with SMDs of <0.1 across demographic, clinical, medication, and laboratory variables, confirming adequate comparability between the two groups for an outcome analysis (Table 1).

Table 2 presents cardiovascular outcomes among patients without a prior cardiovascular history across three follow-up time horizons, including the number of patients at risk at each time point. At the 1-year follow-up, no significant differences were observed between the two groups across all outcomes. At the 3-year follow-up, the romosozumab group had fewer 3P-MACEs than the denosumab group (38 of 5432 vs 82 of 5279). All-cause mortality was also lower in the romosozumab group (321 of 5588 vs 425 of 5470). At the 5-year follow-up, this pattern persisted for 3P-MACE (39 of 5588 vs 80 of 5435) and for all-cause mortality (329 of 5588 vs 509 of 5435; Table 2).

Supplemental eTable 1 presents cardiovascular outcomes among patients with a prior cardiovascular history across the three follow-up periods. At the 1-year follow-up, the romosozumab group had higher rates of stroke (55 of 1182 vs 37 of 1175), 4P-MACE (129 of 705 vs 103 of 714), and 5P-MACE (124 of 689 vs 100 of 695). At the 3-year follow-up, this pattern extended to mortality (84 of 89 vs 72 of 83) and chronic ischemic heart disease (162 of 691 vs 164 of 682). At the 5-year follow-up, higher rates persisted for stroke (102 of 1053 vs 117 of 1056), mortality (84 of 84 vs 75 of 77), 4P-MACE (255 of 719 vs 286 of 681), 5P-MACE (255 of 703 vs 288 of 672), and chronic ischemic heart disease (178 of 691 vs 208 of 682; Supplemental eTable 1).

To validate the adequacy of confounding control, three negative control outcomes were examined in the matched cohorts. Immunization encounters (HR 0.992, 95% CI 0.826–1.208, p = 0.512), skin and subcutaneous disorders (HR 0.942, 95% CI 0.758–1.169, p = 0.883), and contact dermatitis (HR 1.132, 95% CI 0.787–1.629, p = 0.083) all showed no significant differences between the romosozumab and denosumab groups. These null findings for the three negative control outcomes provide evidence against gross systematic confounding driven by general healthcare utilization patterns; however, they cannot exclude residual confounding by cardiovascular-specific risk factors that differed between the treatment groups in ways not fully captured by the propensity score model, such as unmeasured differences in cardiovascular surveillance intensity or physician prescription selectivity related to the FDA boxed warning (Supplemental eTable 5).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the cardiovascular risks of romosozumab versus denosumab for osteoporosis treatment in real-world clinical practice. We used the TriNetX global health research network, which provides access to anonymized EHRs from more than 190 million patients across multiple healthcare institutions worldwide. We conducted comprehensive 1:1 PSM on all relevant cardiovascular risk factors, creating 2 balanced cohorts of 5192 patients without a prior cardiovascular history who were prescribed romosozumab or denosumab.

Outcomes were stratified into three clinically meaningful follow-up periods: short-term (1-year), mid-term (3-year), and long-term (5-year) for both the primary and sensitivity analyses among patients with a prior cardiovascular history. This stratification enabled a comprehensive assessment of cardiovascular risks across different treatment exposure windows and allowed direct comparisons of short-term, mid-term, and long-term cardiovascular associations between the two treatment groups.

Among patients without a prior cardiovascular history, romosozumab was associated with significantly lower risks of 3P-MACE and mortality at both the 3-year and 5-year follow-up periods, whereas no significant differences were observed at the 1-year follow-up or for other cardiovascular outcomes across all time periods. In contrast, the sensitivity analysis among patients with a prior cardiovascular history revealed consistently higher cardiovascular risks with romosozumab across all three follow-up time horizons, with significantly higher risks of 4P-MACE, 5P-MACE, and stroke emerging as early as the 1-year follow-up, and additional significant associations for chronic ischemic heart disease and mortality persisting through the 3-year and 5-year follow-up periods. These findings underscore the importance of careful cardiovascular risk assessment before initiating romosozumab in patients with preexisting CVD.

To the best of our knowledge, this research, using the largest real-world dataset, is the first to compare the drug safety of romosozumab and denosumab in a clinical context. Denosumab and romosozumab are both monoclonal antibodies used to treat osteoporosis, but they have different mechanisms of action and clinical applications.6 –8 Denosumab is administered by a subcutaneous injection every 6 months and inhibits RANKL, a key regulator of osteoclast activity, effectively reducing bone resorption.7,20 Romosozumab inhibits sclerostin, leading to increased bone formation and decreased bone resorption. It is administered by a subcutaneous injection once monthly for 12 months.11,17

The USFDA previously approved romosozumab in 2019. 24 However, a black box warning was noted because of an increased CVD risk.13,16,24 Current guidelines advise against the use of romosozumab in individuals with a history of myocardial infarction or ischemic stroke.22,25 In addition, a cautious approach is recommended for patients with a high overall risk of CVD.22,25 Recent studies highlighted concerns regarding the CVD safety of romosozumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits sclerostin to treat osteoporosis. 26 The ARCH study compared romosozumab and alendronate and found that the romosozumab group experienced a higher rate of major CVD adverse events, such as myocardial infarction and stroke.18,19 A study using the Japanese Adverse Drug Event Report database found increased cardiac and cerebrovascular events in romosozumab users, particularly those with preexisting conditions. The risks of cardiac events were higher in patients with cardiac disease (odds ratio (OR): 5.9, 95% CI 3.5–9.9; p < 0.01) and hypertension (OR: 1.6, 95% CI 1.0–2.7; p = 0.047). Similarly, cerebrovascular event risks increased with cerebrovascular disease (OR: 2.7, 95% CI 1.2–6.2; p = 0.02) and hypertension (OR: 2.6, 95% CI 1.7–3.9; p < 0.01). 27 Evidence from a meta-analysis of RCTs suggested a potential increase in CVD risk associated with romosozumab. Genetic studies further supported this finding, indicating that variants of the SOST gene, which mimic the effects of sclerostin inhibition, may contribute to an elevated risk of CVD events. 28 Similarly, a meta-analysis of six clinical trials demonstrated a statistically significant 39% increase in the risk of 4P-MACE, encompassing death, myocardial infarction, stroke, and heart failure, associated with romosozumab. 20

These findings from the existing literature are supported by our sensitivity analysis among patients with a prior cardiovascular history, which demonstrated consistently higher cardiovascular risks associated with romosozumab compared to denosumab across all three follow-up periods. At the 1-year follow-up, romosozumab was associated with significantly higher risks of 4P-MACE (HR 1.407, 95% CI 1.085–1.823, p = 0.036), 5P-MACE (HR 1.387, 95% CI 1.066–1.806, p = 0.028), and stroke (HR 1.645, 95% CI 1.084–2.496, p = 0.029) compared to denosumab. At the 3-year follow-up, these associations persisted, and additional significant risks were observed for mortality (HR 1.458, 95% CI 1.062–2.001, p = 0.043) and chronic ischemic heart disease (HR 1.297, 95% CI 1.042–1.613, p = 0.013). At the 5-year follow-up, significantly higher risks remained for 4P-MACE (HR 1.294, 95% CI 1.089–1.537, p = 0.037), 5P-MACE (HR 1.319, 95% CI 1.111–1.567, p = 0.033), stroke (HR 1.381, 95% CI 1.054–1.809, p = 0.027), all-cause mortality (HR 1.646, 95% CI 1.200–2.257, p = 0.012), and chronic ischemic heart disease (HR 1.253, 95% CI 1.023–1.535, p = 0.015). The progressive and sustained elevation of cardiovascular risks from as early as 1 year and extending through the 5-year follow-up suggests that patients with preexisting CVD are at significantly higher risk when treated with romosozumab, and that this drug should be used with caution or avoided in this population, in line with current guideline recommendations and regulatory warnings.

In contrast to the findings observed in patients with a prior cardiovascular history, our primary analysis among patients without prior CVD demonstrated significantly lower risks of 3P-MACE at both the 3-year (HR 0.645, 95% CI 0.438–0.950, p = 0.023) and 5-year follow-up (HR 0.755, 95% CI 0.512–0.873, p = 0.028), as well as lower all-cause mortality at 3-year (HR 0.554, 95% CI 0.316–0.972, p = 0.038) and 5-year follow-up (HR 0.533, 95% CI 0.312–0.913, p = 0.028).

Importantly, the significant reduction in 3P-MACE associated with romosozumab appears to be primarily driven by differences in all-cause mortality rather than reductions in non-fatal cardiovascular events. Neither myocardial infarction nor stroke showed statistically significant differences between the two groups at any follow-up period (Table 2). This pattern warrants cautious interpretation in this elderly, predominantly female osteoporosis population, where all-cause mortality may reflect a broad spectrum of health conditions beyond CVD, including frailty, falls, post-fracture complications, and differences in overall healthcare engagement rather than a direct cardiovascular protective effect of romosozumab.

These findings are consistent with and supported by previous research. A cohort study involving 21,600 patients (mean age: 69 years, 94% female) found that romosozumab was associated with reduced incidences of composite serious CVD events (HR 0.73, CI: 0.58–0.92) and mortality (HR 0.78, CI: 0.58–1.05) during a 1-year follow-up compared to PTH analogues. 16

In validation of our results, another cohort study (49,104 patients, mean age: 80 years, 88% women) comparing the two medications found no significant difference in the risk of MACE between romosozumab and teriparatide. 13 Similarly, a cohort study using Japan’s national claims database found no statistically significant difference in MACE risk between romosozumab and teriparatide (new-user, active comparator design), with E-value analyses indicating that an unmeasured confounder would need a risk ratio of 1.97–3.33 to fully explain away the null result. 29 Notably, the authors attributed the relatively favorable cardiovascular profile of romosozumab in real-world settings partly to confounding by indication driven by the FDA black-box warning, which may have led to preferential prescribing of romosozumab to patients at low risk of cardiovascular events. Further evidence from a systematic review and network meta-analysis revealed no significant differences in CVD mortality or MACE among romosozumab, denosumab, teriparatide, and alendronate compared to a placebo. 4 Additional support from a meta-analysis indicated that romosozumab treatment did not increase the overall risks of adverse events, serious adverse events, or CVD events compared to a control group within 12 or 24 months, including in an Asian population receiving sequential anti-resorptive therapy. 10

An important mechanistic consideration for the higher mortality observed in the denosumab group at the 3- and 5-year follow-up is the risk associated with denosumab discontinuation. Recent evidence from a large propensity score-matched cohort study using TriNetX data demonstrated that patients who withdrew from denosumab without sequential antiresorptive therapy experienced significantly higher risks of vertebral fractures (HR 1.479, 95% CI 1.222–1.789) and all-cause mortality (HR 1.588, 95% CI 1.475–1.710) compared to those who transitioned to zoledronate. 30 Rebound bone resorption following denosumab discontinuation, resulting in multiple vertebral fractures, prolonged immobilization, and associated cardiopulmonary complications, may contribute to elevated long-term mortality in denosumab users who discontinue its use without appropriate sequential therapy. However, as TriNetX does not capture post-index treatment switching or sequential therapy decisions, we could not determine what proportion of denosumab users in our cohort discontinued its use without transition therapy, and this explanation must be considered speculative. This represents an important limitation of our fixed-exposure design and underscores the need for studies that incorporate time-varying treatment information.

Romosozumab and denosumab are both effective treatments for osteoporosis. Both drugs significantly increase bone mineral density and reduce fracture risks.4,7,31,32 In a long-term study, denosumab did not show a significant increase in CVD events. 32 In contrast, romosozumab was associated with an increased risk of MACE, particularly in patients with preexisting CVD conditions.4,26,31,33 Evaluating the CVD risks of these two drugs is essential for optimizing treatment decisions, particularly for high-risk patients.24,26,33 This comparison will enable physicians to prescribe the most appropriate medication based on individual CVD risk profiles.20,22,24 Comparative safety data from real-world evidence can inform regulatory guidelines and clinical practice, ensuring that treatments are effective and safe for patients.4,33 Furthermore, because romosozumab is approved for a maximum of 12 months of therapy, while denosumab is often continued long-term, cardiovascular outcomes observed at 3- and 5-year follow-up in our study may partly reflect the influence of post-romosozumab sequential antiresorptive therapy, the nature and timing of which are not captured in the TriNetX prescription database, rather than romosozumab exposure alone. Future studies incorporating time-varying treatment classification and post-index sequential therapy patterns are needed to disentangle the long-term cardiovascular contributions of each agent. Moreover, subsequent investigations involving Asian and other underrepresented cohorts are essential to validate the generalizability of these findings, given that the dataset predominantly reflects populations in the US and Europe.

The contrasting findings between the primary analysis (lower 3P-MACE and mortality with romosozumab in patients without prior CVD) and the sensitivity analysis (higher MACE and mortality in patients with prior CVD) warrant a mechanistic explanation. Romosozumab inhibits sclerostin, thereby activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. While this promotes osteoblast differentiation and bone formation, activation of the Wnt pathway has been implicated in the transdifferentiation of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) toward an osteoblastic phenotype, contributing to arterial calcification and endothelial dysfunction. 34 Critically, recent experimental evidence demonstrated that the vascular role of sclerostin is context-dependent and regulated by local tissue oxygen tension through the hypoxia-inducible factor-1α pathway. 35 In ischemic or hypoxic vascular environments characteristic of patients with established CVD, atherosclerosis, or chronic ischemic heart disease, sclerostin may serve as a compensatory inhibitor of vascular calcification by restraining Wnt/β-catenin-driven VSMC osteogenic transdifferentiation. Pharmacological inhibition of sclerostin by romosozumab in this already-hypoxic, pro-calcific environment could therefore disinhibit osteogenic signaling in VSMCs, including activation of bone morphogenetic protein 2 and runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) pathways, accelerating arterial mineralization or destabilizing vulnerable atherosclerotic plaque, and thus contributing to the higher cardiovascular risk observed in patients with preexisting CVD. 35 Conversely, in patients without prior CVD and a minimal atherosclerotic or ischemic vascular burden, these pro-calcific effects may be insufficient to precipitate acute cardiovascular events within the study follow-up window, while indirect benefits from improved bone health and reduced fracture-related morbidity may emerge over longer follow-up periods, contributing to the lower observed mortality. This mechanistic model is further supported by genetic data demonstrating that SOST variants mimicking sclerostin inhibition are associated with elevated cardiometabolic risks through Wnt pathway activation in the vascular wall. 28 Furthermore, confounding by indication likely operates differently across the two subgroups: real-world evidence indicates that the FDA black-box warning has led to the preferential prescription of romosozumab to patients perceived as being cardiovascular low-risk,29,36 which may have independently contributed to the more-favorable outcomes observed in the primary cohort. These explanations are mechanistically plausible but remain speculative in the context of this observational study, and all findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the TriNetX platform records deaths without cause, preventing the separation of cardiovascular-specific mortality from all-cause mortality. While the TriNetX platform does provide a competing risks analysis function based on the Fine-Gray sub-distribution hazard model, this function requires the specification of a competing event, in our case, the cause of death, which was not available in the TriNetX database. Accordingly, deaths from non-cardiovascular causes could not be formally treated as competing events in the analysis of non-fatal cardiovascular outcomes such as myocardial infarction and stroke. This limitation may have resulted in a modest overestimation of the absolute risk of non-fatal cardiovascular events, particularly over longer follow-up periods in which non-cardiovascular mortality accumulates. Future studies using data sources that capture cause-specific mortality (e.g., national vital statistics linkages) would enable a competing risks analysis that more precisely isolates cardiovascular event risks.

Second, exposure to romosozumab or denosumab was defined as fixed at the index date, and treatment switching, discontinuation, or sequential antiresorptive therapy after the initial romosozumab drug was not accounted for in the analysis. In addition, while TriNetX records prescriptions, it does not capture whether patients took the medication or switched to a different drug. Treatment classification was based on prescription records, without detailed measures of adherence or persistence, such as the medication possession ratio or the exact number of doses administered. Although persistence for at least 365 days was required to reduce misclassification, heterogeneity in cumulative drug exposure likely remains. Cardiovascular outcomes observed during extended follow-up periods may therefore partly reflect the influence of subsequent treatments rather than romosozumab alone. This is particularly relevant given that romosozumab is approved for a maximum of 12 months of therapy, after which patients are typically transitioned to antiresorptive agents; the cardiovascular effects of these sequential therapies may be conflated with those of the index drug in the 3- and 5-year analyses. 30

Third, despite PSM on a comprehensive set of observed covariates, residual confounding by indication cannot be fully excluded. The known cardiovascular safety warning associated with romosozumab may have led to more careful patient selection and closer clinical monitoring in real-world practice, potentially introducing surveillance bias or healthy user bias. Real-world evidence supports the clinical significance of this bias: studies have explicitly noted that the FDA black-box warning has led to preferential pre-selection of cardiovascular low-risk patients for romosozumab, which may have independently lowered the observed MACE rate in romosozumab-treated cohorts regardless of the drug’s intrinsic cardiovascular effects. 36 Accordingly, the apparently favorable cardiovascular profile of romosozumab observed in the primary analysis (patients without prior CVD) should be interpreted with caution, as it may partly reflect the characteristics of the patients selected to receive romosozumab rather than a direct drug effect.

Fourth, PSM was performed within the TriNetX federated research platform, which limited the availability of detailed balance diagnostics beyond SMDs. Visual diagnostics, such as Love plots and variance ratio tests, are not feasible on this platform. More-sophisticated analytical approaches, such as inverse probability of treatment weighting or marginal structural models, would be methodologically preferable but were not feasible within the constraints of the TriNetX federated platform. In addition, while the TriNetX Advanced Analytics framework supports competing risks analysis and follow-up period specification, 37 it does not support time-varying covariate models or marginal structural models within its federated structure, precluding formal adjustment for time-varying confounding from treatment switching after the index date.

Fifth, the data were predominantly based on US and European populations, which may limit applicability to Asian or other ethnic groups. Furthermore, the TriNetX database provides only fracture diagnoses, without additional details on the circumstances or conditions of their occurrence, such as trauma mechanisms or fracture sites.

Sixth, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed due to the study’s retrospective, observational design. The final sample size was determined by the availability of eligible matched patients in the TriNetX database, which may have limited the statistical power to detect smaller effect sizes, particularly for less-common outcomes.

Finally, all findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects, and results from secondary and exploratory outcomes should be considered hypothesis-generating, given the large number of outcomes examined without formal correction for multiple comparisons.

Conclusion

In this large, real-world propensity score-matched cohort study, romosozumab was associated with significantly lower risks of 3P-MACE and all-cause mortality than denosumab at mid- and long-term follow-up among patients without a prior cardiovascular history. In contrast, among patients with a prior cardiovascular history, romosozumab was associated with consistently higher risks of composite MACE, stroke, mortality, and chronic ischemic heart disease across all follow-up time horizons. These contrasting findings highlight the critical importance of cardiovascular risk assessments prior to initiating romosozumab, particularly in patients with preexisting CVD. All findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects, and future prospective studies are needed to confirm these observations. The 3P-MACE finding in patients without prior cardiovascular history is the primary confirmatory result; all other outcomes should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.
