Data on the cytotoxicity of surfactants used to increase the dispersion of micro- and nanoplastics
Authors: Maaike Vercauteren, Miao Peng, John Seghers, Ilias Semmouri, Håkan Emteborg, Colin R Janssen, Jana Asselman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGathering reliable micro- and nanoplastic toxicity data is challenging but of utmost importance for risk assessment. The heterogeneity of plastics is one of these challenges. The production of micro- and nanoplastics, with small sizes, different polymer types or surface charges, often requires the use of surfactants to reduce the surface tension and increase the solubility of plastics. The toxicity of these surfactants may result in potential false positives, hampering conclusions on the toxicity of micro-and nanoplastics itself. This dataset therefore describes the cytotoxic effects of five surfactants, including four synthetic and one bio-surfactant, using two human cell lines (mimicking ingestion and inhalation exposure pathways). The data aids in the selection of proper surfactants and urges the need for adequate solvent controls which could account for potential unknown effects of unreported surfactants in commercially available plastics.Keywords: Surfactant, Microplastic, Nanoplastic, Toxicology, Biosurfactants, Reporting standards

Background

Research on microplastics is increasing steadily, yet many questions about the toxicological effects of exposure to micro- and nanoplastics remain. A key challenge is their heterogeneity, with different sizes, shape, polymers, surface characteristics and additives [[2], [3], [4]]. Currently, we are far from capturing the heterogeneity of micro- and nanoplastics in human health and, by extension, in environmental effect studies, even though this heterogeneity is expected to affect the bioavailability, cellular uptake, cellular effects and subsequently whole organism effects [2,4].

To address this, future research must incorporate multifactorial exposure scenarios, including particles with diverse characteristics. In contrast to the commonly used polystyrene with inherent polarity [1], production of small scale particles of other polymer types might require the addition of surfactants [5], to reduce the surface tension, enhancing particle dispersion and stability in aqueous solutions [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. However, some surfactants are inherently toxic [5,8] which has been described both in cell lines [7] and environmental model organisms such as the waterflea Daphnia magna [9]. Biosurfactants, surfactants of natural origin, are being explored for their potentially lower toxicity [5]. Similar to DPPC, other biosurfactants often synthesized by plants, animals and microbes are commercially available and already deployed in industrial and pharmaceutical applications [11]. Examples of biosurfactants include glycolipids, lipopeptides, phospholipids, surface-active antibiotics, fatty acids/neutral lipids [12]. Extracellular polymeric substances produced by microalgae have already been proven to be as efficient as Tween80 to stabilize polyethylene nanoplastic suspensions [5].

Understanding and managing the role of surfactants in micro- and nanoplastic toxicity is essential for generating reliable data on their health and environmental effects.

Data Description

This article describes the dataset that gathers information on the cytotoxic effects of five surfactants (including one biosurfactant) using two human cell lines, representing the inhalation (BEAS-2B, lung cell line) and ingestion (Caco-2, intestinal cell line) microplastic exposure pathways [10] (Fig. 1). The data is based on three cytotoxicity assays.

Graphs representing the effects of Triton X-100 (A), Tween 80 (B), sodium cholic acid (C), Sodium hexametaphosphate (D) and DPPC (E) on the cell viability measured with MTT, NR and SRB cytotoxicity assays relative to the respective negative control. Toxicity data was gathered in BEAS-2B and Caco-2 cell lines and data of six technical replicates is represented using a box plot. The statistical significance is provided in a separate file in the data repository (LOEC.xlsx). Due to technical errors no data is available in some of the treatments and concentrations.

Both cell lines showed responses on the exposure to the surfactants. Negative effects on cellular viability in a dose-dependent manner were observed with Triton X-100, Tween80, sodium cholic acid and sodiumhexametaphosphate, all synthetic surfactants. Dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC), the tested natural surfactant, did not show such dose-dependent negative effects. The lowest cell viability effect concentrations were found for Triton X-100 (Fig. 1A) and Tween 80 (Fig. 1B), with clear negative effects at 8 and 200 mg/L, respectively. In comparison, sodium cholic acid (Fig. 1C) and sodiumhexametaphosphate (Fig. 1D) did induce complete cellular mortality for both cell lines at higher tested concentrations with pronounced negative effects at 1000 and 5000 mg/L, respectively. For both BEAS-2B and Caco2, DPPC did not induce negative effects on cell viability in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1E).

Measured cell viability showed quite some variability. A summary of all three assays performed for both cell lines, the derived lowest effect concentrations (LOEC) values and the statistical significance is provided in the data repository (LOEC.xlsx). The observed variability, even upon DPPC exposure, shows that adding a chemical, either from synthetic or natural origin, will affect cellular functions and could lead to misinterpretation of observed effect of micro-and nanoplastics exposure. This highlights the importance of carefully considering the potential effects of surfactants on toxicity outcomes in any experiment and for each measured endpoint, even when natural surfactants are used. Introducing a dedicated surfactant control, analogous to the organic solvent control recommended in OECD guideline 249 [13], would provide a clearer understanding of the toxicological contributions of surfactants themselves and enable a distinction between surfactant-driven effects and those arising from micro- or nanoplastics.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Tested surfactants

Five surfactants were selected to be tested: Triton X-100 (CAS 9036–19–5, Sigma Aldrich, product nr. X100), Tween 80 (CAS 9005–65–6, Sigma Aldrich, product nr. 8.22187), Sodium cholic acid (CAS 361–09–1, Sigma Aldrich, product nr. TA9H97BAEB88), sodium hexametaphosphate (CAS 10,124–56–8, Rectapur, product nr. VWRC301475H) and DPPC (CAS 63–89–8, Sigma Aldrich, product nr. 850355P). The first four are synthetic surfactants and the latter is a biosurfactant, the most abundant lipid in the pulmonary surfactant in human lung tissue [14,15].

For all surfactants, a stock solution of 100 g/L was prepared in distilled water, except for DPPC, which has a maximum solubility of 30 g/L. The stock solutions were filter sterilized (0.22 µm) and diluted in serum free cell culture medium to obtain a concentration range between 0.008 mg/L and 5000 mg/L, which spans a broad range of concentrations with relevant concentrations assumed to be on the lower end of our dilution series.

Cell culture

Two cell lines, noncancerous human bronchial epithelium (BEAS-2B) and human colorectal adenocarcinoma (Caco-2) cell lines, were used. The BEAS-2B cell line was available at the Laboratory of Environmental Toxicology and Aquatic Ecology (GhEnToxLab, Ghent University) and deposited under Biobank number BR-42 [16]. The cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with phenol red, 10 % heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin [16]. The Caco-2 cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA) and deposited under Biobank number BB190156. The cells were grown in supplemented DMEM medium, as described above, with addition of 2 % nonessential amino acids (NEAA) [17]. All culture media and supplements were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA. Both cell lines were seeded and incubated at 37 °C, 5 % CO2 and a relative humidity of > 95 %. Confluent cultures were sub-cultured using 0.5 % Trypsin - Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and split once a week. During the experiments, the passage number of all cultured cells remained between 13 and 31 for Caco-2 cells and 32 to 34 for BEAS-2B cells. The experiments were conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines.

Exposure experiments

Before exposure, cells were detached using 0.5 % Trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and re-suspended in serum-free cell culture medium. The cell suspension was seeded in 96-well plates at 20,000 cells per well (200 µL). After 24 h, the adhered cells were exposed to the surfactants for 48 h. Six technical replicates were used per tested concentration (i.e. 0.008; 0.32; 1.6; 8; 40; 200; 1000 and 5000 mg/L) and per surfactant. Untreated cells that were cultured using serum-free cell culture medium were included as negative control.

Cellular toxicity assays

After a 48 h exposure period, three biochemical assays were performed to evaluate the effects on mitochondrial activity (MTT-assay), protein content (Sulforhodamine B (SRB)-assay) and cytotoxicity (Neutral Red (NR)-assay), all indications of cell viability.

MTT-assay. Half of the cell culture medium (100 µL) was removed after which 20 µL filter-sterilized (0.22 µm) 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT, Invitrogen Thermofisher Scientific) (final concentration 5 mg/mL, dissolved in Dulbecco’s modified phosphate buffered saline (PBS) was added to each well. After incubation (2 h, dark, 37 °C), all media were carefully removed and 200 µL pure dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma Aldrich) was added to each well as a solvent and homogenized. The absorbance of the solution in each well was measured with a spectrometer (Thermo Labsystems, Multiskan Ascent) at 570 nm.

NR-assay. A Neutral Red stock solution (NR, 250 µL, Sigma Aldrich) was prepared and filtered (0.22 µm) in serum-free cell culture medium with a final concentration of 3.3 mg/mL. The cell culture medium with surfactants was removed and 200 µl of the NR-solution was added in each well. After incubation (3 h, 37 °C) the solution was removed and 150 µL desorption solution (1 % glacial acetic acid (Sigma Aldrich), 50 % ethanol (Chem-Lab), 49 % distilled water) was added to each well and homogenized. The absorbance of the solution in each well was measured at 540 nm with the spectrometer.

SRB-assay. Cells were fixed with 50 µL 50 % trichloroacetic acid (TCA) at 4 °C for 1 h. The plate was mildly rinsed with tap water five times and air dried. The cells were stained with 50 µL Sulforhodamine B sodium salt (SRB, Biotium) solution (0.4 % in 1 % glacial acetic acid; 21 °C, 30 min). The stained cells were subsequently rinsed (5 times) with 1 % glacial acetic acid and air dried. Tris(hydroxymethyl)-aminomethane buffer (200 µL per well, 10 mM, Sigma Aldrich) was added and the solution was resuspended. Finally, the absorbance of the solution in each well was measured at 490 nm.

Data analysis

The raw absorbance data of the cellular assays was corrected for the blank measurements (cell culture medium) and transformed to an absorbance relative to the untreated cells (absorbance of the treated cells / absorbance of untreated cells). Normality of the data and homogeneity of the variance were checked and statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by a Tukey post hoc test or pairwise Wilcox test to study significant differences (p < 0.05) with the untreated cells. RStudio software (version 4.2.2) was used for data analysis. Ggplot2 package was used for creating the graphs.

Limitations

Variability on cell viability was observed for all tested surfactants and endpoints in concentrations below the NOEC values, that often remained within a 20 % range in comparison with the control group. This range is wider compared to the 10 % biological background variation mentioned in the OECD guideline 249 [13]. As an explanation for higher observed biological variation, we pose that the 48 h exposure scenario as used in this study could, however, have introduced more biological variation, in comparison with the 24 h exposure. This highlights the importance of including a proper surfactant control to test the toxicity of the media itself in any experiment and for each measured endpoint, even with the use of natural surfactants.

In preparation of dispersed micro- and nanoplastics, other surfactants can as well be used such as sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS), sodium poly(styrene sulfonate) (PSS). The surfactants used in this study are therefore not exhaustive and if another surfactant is being used or if the exact composition of the aqueous solution in which the micro- or nanoplastics are provided is unknown, inclusion of proper surfactant/solvent controls remains of detrimental importance.
