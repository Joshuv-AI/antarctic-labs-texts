Oral probiotic delivery-based therapeutic strategies for inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Sha Li, Hongdan Deng, Shu Fu, Juan Li, Xinyu Luo, Zhuoping Chen, Pei Wang, Xi Feng, Yi Chen, Chaomei Fu, Zhejie Chen, Shengpeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOver the past decade, accumulating evidence supporting the efficacy of probiotics in gut microbiota modulation has spurred interest in their therapeutic potential for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), a chronic intestinal disorder for which safer and more sustainable alternatives to conventional immunosuppressive and biologic treatments are urgently needed. Probiotics represent a promising therapeutic strategy due to their favorable biosafety profile and compatibility with daily health practices. Mechanistically, probiotics mitigate intestinal inflammation by suppressing pathogenic bacteria, modulating gut immunity and regulating microbial metabolites. However, their application is limited by low viability and poor intestinal colonization following oral administration. To address these challenges, advanced delivery strategies, including physical encapsulation, biohybrid nanocoating and chemical modifications, have been developed to enhance probiotic survival and targeted delivery. Despite these advances, current probiotic delivery systems face limitations, such as the impairment of microbial activity by surface modifications, deficiency in formulating intestinal digestion resistance strategies, lack of strategies for active colonization, and incomplete understanding of therapeutic mechanisms. By addressing these barriers, targeted and sustained research into innovative probiotic delivery systems may yield novel therapeutic approaches for IBD. This review indicates that relying solely on physical encapsulation or simple coating can no longer meet therapeutic demands. Future efforts must shift toward a functional synergy direction, developing multifunctional systems that protect probiotic viability without inhibiting their metabolic and physiological functions while integrating active colonization and targeted release. Furthermore, the review emphasizes the current incompleteness in understanding the therapeutic mechanisms of probiotics and calls for embedding mechanistic studies into the rational design of delivery systems. These insights provide a pathway for the field to move from determining whether live probiotics can be delivered to exploring how to achieve efficient, safe and functionally intact oral probiotic therapy.Keywords: Probiotic delivery, Inflammatory bowel disease, Gut microbiota, Engineered bacteria, Microbial therapeutics

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), comprising Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), represents a group of chronic, nonspecific intestinal inflammatory disorders [1]. With complex pathogenesis, heterogeneous clinical presentations and persistent disease course, IBD has emerged as a significant global health challenge. Epidemiological data demonstrate a substantially higher prevalence in Western regions, especially Northern Europe [2], the United Kingdom and North America [3]. Current projections indicate IBD prevalence will surpass 0.3% in North America and Europe by 2030 [4,5]. The significant upward trends indicate the growing pressure of IBD on global public health resources, highlighting that the disease has gradually evolved into an unavoidable worldwide health burden [5]. While etiological understanding has progressed, IBD pathogenesis remains incompletely characterized. Current evidence implicates multifactorial interactions involving environmental triggers (dietary factors [6], smoking [7], and infections [8]), genetic predisposition, gut microbiota dysbiosis and immune dysregulation. These elements collectively trigger aberrant mucosal immune activation and chronic intestinal inflammation. UC typically manifests as continuous mucosal inflammation restricted to the colon, whereas CD presents as transmural inflammation that may affect any gastrointestinal segment [9]. Clinically, patients with IBD present with growth impairment, anemia, abdominal pain, hematochezia and arthritis. Additionally, they face a significantly increased risk of venous thromboembolism and colorectal cancer [10], which severely impairs their physical health, while also greatly diminishes their quality of life and occupational performance. The condition imposes considerable economic burdens through treatment costs and hospitalization expenses, while chronic disease management creates significant psychosocial strain for patients and families [11].

Due to the characteristics of IBD being incurable and prone to relapse, long-term use of existing drugs is inevitable. Conventional therapeutic agents for IBD include aminosalicylates, corticosteroids, antibiotics and immunosuppressants [12]. However, these medication regimens exhibit prominent drawbacks, such as high costs, unacceptable side effects and unsustainable therapeutic effects [13]. For instance, aminosalicylates show limited efficacy in moderate-to-severe patients [14]. Long-term administration of corticosteroids may lead to osteoporosis, metabolic disorders and other side effects; immunosuppressants may increase the risk of infections and lymphoma [15]; and biological agents are challenged by drug resistance, high economic costs and non-response in certain patients [16]. Moreover, IBD necessitates lifelong pharmacological intervention, yet the long-term safety of conventional medications continues to be debated [17]. Consequently, it is imperative to explore and develop novel therapeutic formulations with improved safety profiles and long-term tolerability. As research in the gut microbiota field advances steadily, accumulating evidence has demonstrated a tight link between the gut microbiota and the development and progression of IBD. As the largest microbial reservoir in the human body, changes in the composition and function of the gut microbiota directly affect intestinal health [18]. IBD patients exhibit significant gut microbiota dysbiosis, featuring reduced probiotic abundance and expanded pathogenic species. This microbial imbalance results in intestinal inflammation, compromises mucosal barrier function and immune homeostasis, thereby facilitating the development of microbiota-targeted therapies [19].

Studies have shown that probiotics possess superior advantages in the alleviation or treatment of IBD, with multiple mechanisms that have been extensively investigated both in vitro and in vivo [20]. Probiotics have a long history of application in the therapeutic management of IBD. Since Metchnikoff first proposed the health benefits of lactic acid bacteria in the early 1900s, probiotic research has gradually gained attention. The US FDA classifies some probiotics as “Live Biotherapeutic Products,” while the Chinese Pharmacopoeia defines them as “microecological live bacterial products,” emphasizing their live bacterial characteristics. After the 1990s, along with the continuous progress of gut microbiota research technologies, the potential value of probiotics in the management of IBD has been gradually uncovered. At present, probiotics mainly refer to live bacteria that are beneficial to the host, such as lactic acid bacteria and bifidobacteria. Their mechanisms of action include competitive inhibition of pathogenic bacteria, modulation of immune responses and enhancement of intestinal barrier function [21]. Furthermore, probiotics can coordinate immune responses by promoting the synthesis of anti-inflammatory factors while suppressing the production of inflammatory mediators. Their effectiveness in regulating intestinal barrier integrity has also been observed, leading to tangible alleviation of colitis [22]. Consequently, oral probiotic therapy has gained widespread application in the treatment of IBD due to its inherent convenience, non-invasiveness and well-established safety profile. This review systematically elucidates innovative strategies for probiotic-based therapy in IBD, providing theoretical support for developing novel microbiome-targeted treatments. By analyzing the design principles and mechanisms of probiotic delivery systems, we aim to provide new insights into the optimization of IBD treatment.

Therapeutic potential of probiotics in IBD

Currently, therapeutic approaches for IBD are subject to substantial limitations. While corticosteroids are able to effectively suppress gut inflammation, long-term administration is accompanied by side effects including osteoporosis and metabolic complications, including glucose metabolism [23]. Biological agents, while being highly effective, are costly and some patients may become resistant to these drugs [13,15]. Given these challenges, there has been a growing interest in the use of probiotics as a new treatment strategy for IBD [24]. Probiotics, derived from human commensal flora and symbionts, have good biosafety. They also have relatively low requirements for storage and transportation, enabling long-term usage at home. This could help to increase patient adherence and facilitate everyday health maintenance [25]. Thanks to genetic engineering technology, probiotics can now be genetically modified to produce therapeutic proteins or enzymes, thus further enhancing their functional properties. Moreover, probiotics can restore the microecological balance of the gut by altering the intestinal microbiota. They can also strengthen the intestinal mucosal barrier and modulate immune responses in multiple ways, and are also low in cost [26]. In summary, probiotics have multiple advantages for the treatment of IBD, which include good biosafety, convenience of use, low cost and genetic manipulability. These characteristics make them an attractive new therapy for IBD (Fig. 1). Distinctive safety advantages

Probiotics have significant biosafety benefits in the treatment of IBD, and this has been backed up by scientific research and robust regulatory procedures around the world. Probiotics in the United States are primarily regulated as foods or dietary supplements and can be marketed as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS), approved novel food additives or notified New Dietary Ingredients (NDIs) [27]. Lactobacillus plantarum (L. plantarum) GUANKE was approved following rigorous toxicological and genetic assessments of safety [28]. The European Union has adopted the Qualified Presumption of Safety (QPS) system for safety evaluation prior to market entry. Strains such as Bacillus subtilis (BS) have been granted QPS because of the safety studies conducted [29]. China has a more rigorous regulatory framework, in which probiotics must be thoroughly evaluated for safety and function. For example, Lactobacillus mali APS1 must undergo genotoxicity tests and the registration of health food requires clinical trials in humans [30].

Probiotics have also been shown to be safe via preclinical and clinical assessments.. For instance, genotoxicity tests demonstrated that Lactobacillus curvatus WiKim did not induce chromosomal aberrations [31]. Subchronic toxicity tests showed that Bifidobacterium longum BLI-02 had no toxic effects in rats at even the highest dose (10 g/kg body weight) [32]. Human tolerance tests also contributed. For example, Butyricicoccus pullicaecorum did not produce an increased incidence of adverse events compared to placebo groups. Probiotics have been shown to have fewer side effects than many drugs. For example, antibiotics can alter the microbiome. It can also lead to Clostridium difficile (C. difficile) infection [29]. However, probiotics such as Lactobacillus spp. may restore balance and decrease inflammation. Chemotherapy results in gastrointestinal complications in 30%–50% of patients. But probiotics such as Lactobacillus acidophilus (L. acidophilus) TYCA06 can enhance tolerance of the treatment [33]. Similarly, psychiatric drugs such as aripiprazole can cause side effects including weight gain and somnolence [34]. In contrast, Lactobacillus fermentum (L. fermentum) HM3 carries no such adverse risks and may even improve gut enzymatic activity [35].

Current evidence highlights the superior biosafety of probiotics in terms of genotoxicity, acute/chronic toxicity and clinical tolerance, outperforming conventional medications. Although rare cases of mild discomfort have been reported, such as Lactobacillus pentosus IBRC = 11143 is still under review [36], the overall risk profile of probiotics is substantially lower than that of chemical drugs [37]. Further standardization of strain-specific evaluations will strengthen the role of probiotics as a safe therapeutic option for IBD management.

Facilitating daily health management of IBD

As a chronic inflammatory disorder, IBD necessitates long-term management strategies that emphasize patient adherence, enhancement of quality of life and cost-effectiveness [38]. Probiotics represent a potentially effective therapeutic modality, as they can be easily incorporated into daily regimens while contributing to disease management and overall well-being. Generally, IBD patients exhibit distinct therapeutic needs during acute exacerbations compared to chronic maintenance phases. Acute flare-ups are commonly treated with corticosteroids and immunosuppressants to mitigate inflammation, yet these drugs cause notable adverse effects and may induce severe complications with long-term use [39]. Conversely, probiotics represent a promising maintenance therapy, offering unique clinical and economic merits. They achieve superior cost-effectiveness relative to monoclonal antibody treatments, which cost over $20,000 per patient annually [40]. Their oral administration route eliminates risks associated with invasive delivery systems while maintaining exceptional safety profiles during extended therapeutic use.

Guidelines for the management of IBD call for a multidisciplinary team of gastroenterologists, nutritionists and psychologists to improve the effectiveness of treatment [41]. But traditional drugs have drawbacks. For instance, TNF-α inhibitors such as infliximab, have complicated dosing and severe side effects [42]. With their proven safety, probiotics like L. plantarum GUANKE act as effective supplementary treatments that simplify therapy management. Probiotic formulations have grown to include Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and even new multi-strain combinations [43]. This diversity helps to create therapies tailored to the needs of each patient [44]. And probiotics have unique contributions to quality of life. Approximately 24% of IBD patients have extra-intestinal symptoms, such as arthritis or skin lesions. Meanwhile, anemia occurs in anywhere from 8.8% to 74% of patients [45]. Specific probiotic strains, such as Bifidobacterium longum (B. longum) BLI-02, may indirectly alleviate systemic symptoms by modulating the gut microbiota. Their ability to modulate the gut-brain axis provides a non-drug treatment option for the 30%–40% of IBD patients dealing with both anxiety and depression [46]. In addition to following guideline-recommended lifestyle changes, probiotics can boost intestinal barrier function and help maintain a balanced gut microbiome. This provides a scientifically grounded supplement to dietary adjustments and exercise routines. Unlike nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, which may exacerbate inflammation [47], probiotics exhibit excellent safety profiles for chronic use [48]. From an economic perspective, probiotic supplementation could reduce unnecessary hospitalizations by 20% [41]. This reduction would significantly alleviate healthcare burdens. As better formulations continue to be developed, probiotics are likely to find even more clinical applications in the future.

Probiotics offer comprehensive solution for IBD. They align with current treatment guidelines and have the potential to address currently unmet needs for the treatment of chronic disease, making them a promising option. Advances in probiotic development are expected to improve their use in individualized treatment for IBD. Research into the mechanisms of action and formulation of probiotics is ongoing, and promise to make their use in the treatment of IBD even more exciting.

Enabling potential of probiotics

In the early 20th century, bacteria were employed to treat tumors and intestinal infections. The advent of recombinant DNA technology subsequently made genetically engineered bacteria a major focus of research. By the late 20th century, genetically modified bacteria began were being used to treat gastrointestinal inflammation and tumors. More recently, tools like CRISPR-Cas have increased the accuracy and effectiveness of bacterial genetic modifications [49].

Genetically modified probiotics regulate immune responses in the gut by producing particular therapeutic substances. For example, modified Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) that produces all-trans retinoic acid significantly reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines, like IL-1β and TNF-α, while increasing anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 levels [50]. Similarly, IL-10-expressing Lactococcus lactis (L. lactis) has demonstrated safety and improved inflammatory markers in CD patients [51]. The engineered L. lactis strain LL-Thy12, which delivers IL-10, reduces disease activity indices with minimal adverse events [52]. Additionally, LL-IL-27, developed by Hansen et al., synthesizes IL-27 in situ, exerting protective effects against colitis and reducing intestinal damage [53]. Probiotics reduce oxidative stress and enhance barrier function by promoting antioxidant and metabolic enzymes like catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) [54]. Moreover, Bifidobacterium strains that produce manganese SOD help improve dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced IBD [55]. Genetically engineered probiotics also show increased therapeutic potential by producing a variety of bioactive compounds. Specifically, probiotics that express bile salt hydrolase (BSH) or enzymes induced by bile acids (BAs) influence the BA metabolism, leading to improved enhanced intestinal barrier function and the reestablishment of immune balance [56]. Modified Lactobacillus strains expressing recombinant murine heme oxygenase-1 reduce the incidence of acute colitis. Likewise, elastin-producing Lactobacillus significantly alleviates symptoms of IBD [55]. Furthermore, probiotics that express antimicrobial proteins mitigate intestinal disturbances caused by chemotherapy [57].

Genetically engineered probiotics offer a targeted treatment approach for IBD by modulating the “microbiota-immune-metabolism” regulatory network. Advances in synthetic biology is driving the development of third-generation “smart probiotics”, which have the potential to deliver environment-responsive, and personalized therapies, bringing new promise to individuals with IBD.

Mechanisms of probiotics intervention in IBD

Antagonizes pathogenic bacteria

It is well documented that the gut microbiota plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of IBD. Therefore, the restoration of an optimal balance of gut microbiota is an important therapeutic objective. Probiotic preparations have been proven to effectively restore this balance in clinical trials, primarily by limiting the invasion of pathogenic bacteria [58]. Thus, the supplementation of probiotics to increase the number of beneficial bacteria and restore a healthy balance of gut microbiota are well accepted as effective approaches for preventing IBD [59]. Probiotics have a broad range of mechanisms to suppress the growth of pathogenic bacteria, including competing for nutrients, preventing pathogenic bacteria from colonizing the gut and producing antimicrobial metabolites (Fig. 2A) [60,61]. These three pathways are complementary to each other, and together, constitute a protective network of probiotics against pathogenic bacteria. Nutrient competition is a primary mechanism, colonization competition is an ecological mechanism and metabolite secretion is a chemical mechanism.

The nutrients in the intestinal environment, such as sugars, amino acids, vitamins and metal ions, are scarce. Competition for nutrients between probiotics and pathogens occurs in IBD during their metabolism. Competition for the same ecological niche is considered to be one of the main mechanisms of effective probiotics. For instance, the nutritional demands of non-toxigenic C. difficile and toxigenic C. difficile may be similar, and this can be used to treat C. difficile infections [62]. Similarly, the pathogen requires oxygen to grow, and probiotics may be able to increase their colonization by competing with Salmonella for oxygen [63]. Crucially, gut microbes also compete for metal ions, such as transition metals (iron, zinc and manganese), among nutrients. These metals play an important role in many biological processes such as catalysis, protein structural stabilization, signal-transduction, etc. It has been shown that one of the factors that affect the abundance of Lactobacillus in the gut microbiota is the dietary intake of essential metals such as zinc, manganese and iron [64]. For example, EcN has been shown to decrease the attachment of Salmonella Typhimurium (ST) in the gut through iron competition [65]. This is presumably due to several iron acquisition mechanisms of EcN, which can produce siderophores such as Salmochelin to compete for iron [66]. Understanding these types of nutritional competition approaches and mechanisms is a starting point for developing probiotics engineered for the disease state of IBD.

Adherent probiotic strains can block pathogens from adhering to host cells, thereby reducing pathogen load [67]. Interestingly, bacterial adherence to mucosal surfaces is a two-step process: the initial stage of binding is due to non-specific, physical interactions (such as hydrophobic interactions) that are followed by a second stage of specific adhesion involving cell wall components [68]. Probiotics competitive block pathogens by taking advantage of this process. For instance, some Lactobacillus strains have been shown to inhibit the adherence of Candida to mucosal surfaces by competing for binding sites and displacing existing adherent yeast cells; probiotic blocking ability is associated with the cell surface hydrophobicity of Lactobacillus, which obviously reflects the competition for binding sites during the first stage of non-specific binding [69]. Moreover, probiotics can block the binding of pathogenic bacteria to receptors on the enterocytes through steric hindrance. For example, the low toxicity of Heyndrickxia coagulans is mainly due to its ability to inhibit the adhesion of pathogens, such as Listeria monocytogenes, Bacillus cereus, Staphylococcus aureus and E. coli O157:H7, via non-specific steric hindrance, another type of competitive for receptors [70]. In fact, the ability of probiotics to sterically compete with pathogens for binding sites is strain-dependent, with the strain distribution pattern and the interaction between the adhesins of the strain and competitive stereospecific receptors [71]. For example, it has been shown that Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG) can play an effective role in competing with E. coli and Salmonella spp. (having low hydrophobicity but high-affinity adhesin-receptor interactions) for binding sites on Caco-2 cells. This may involve steric hindrance of the attachment of these gastrointestinal pathogens by LGG [71]. In addition, the differences in effectiveness of the inhibition correlate with the presence of adhesin receptors and hydrophobic proteins on the surface of Caco-2 cells, which highlights the strain-dependent nature of this effect.

Another mechanism of antimicrobial activity involves the production of antimicrobial metabolites, such as organic acids, hydrogen peroxide and bacteriocins, to prevent growth or kill pathogenic bacteria in the gut. Probiotics produce organic acids, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and lactic acid that decrease the pH of the digestive tract. This reduces acid-sensitive Gram-negative bacteria, such as Salmonella [72]. These organic acids accumulate in their undissociated form inside the bacteria, dissociate in the cytoplasm and cause a pH drop or accumulation of ionized form of organic acids, leading to the death of pathogens. Moreover, some probiotics may also secrete bacteriocins, such as nisin, which have an anti-microbial effect. These are usually peptides and are analogous to genomically encoded immune proteins. Bacteriocins work by aggregating multiple protein toxins, and have a wide range of modes of action, mainly including increasing cell membrane permeability or cell wall degradation, leading to cell death [73].

These studies comprehensively explain the mechanisms of probiotic antimicrobial activity by producing metabolites, competition and signal blocking. The findings offer a theoretical basis and practical approach to developing new probiotic-based interventions for IBD.

Regulates the intestinal immune system and reduces inflammation

The balance of the intestinal immune system is maintained by the interaction of the host and microbiota. Probiotics, as key regulators of microorganisms, modulate intestinal immune responses in several ways [74]. Recent research has shown that probiotics not only directly affect immune cell activities but also affect the production of inflammatory cytokines as well as the functions of key signaling pathways, contributing to the immune tolerance in the intestine (Fig. 2B).

On the cellular level, probiotics have a wide range of immunomodulatory effects. Macrophages, being the major effector cells of the immune system in the intestine, undergo polarization processes that are crucial for regulating inflammation [75]. Research illustrates that probiotics, such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, markedly inhibit the pro-inflammatory activities of M1 macrophages, by downregulating TNF-α, IL-6 and iNOS production [76]. They also promote the switch to anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages [77], thereby downregulating the inflammation. EcN can be modified by bioorthogonal chemistry and enterically coated for intestinal delivery. This approach promotes the conversion of M1 to M2 macrophages and alleviates inflammation [78]. B. longum and its metabolites, can suppress the formation of the NLRP3 inflammasome by downregulating GBP5 expression, alleviating inflammation in UC [79]. Moreover, extracellular polysaccharides (EPS) from probiotic strains have been shown to directly activate macrophages, enhancing their anti-inflammatory function [80]. For example, the EPS of BS promotes the development of M2 macrophages (in a TLR4-dependent manner) and inhibits T cell activity, thereby preventing acute colitis [81]. These EPS components not only increase the anti-inflammatory activity of macrophages but also inhibit inflammatory responses via TGF-β- and PD-L1-dependent pathways [82]. As for T cell regulation, probiotics demonstrate highly specific immunomodulatory actions by keeping a balance between Th1, Th2, Th17 and regulatory T cells (Treg). For instance, the Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species play a prominent role in increasing Treg proliferation and activity, which curtails excessive immune responses through the production of IL-10 and TGF-β [83,84]. And some specific strains, like Akkermansia muciniphila (AKK), can selectively suppress pro-inflammatory Th17 cells [85].

In the humoral response, probiotics like Lactobacillus casei (L. casei) stimulate the secretion of secretory IgA by intestinal B cells, improving mucosal immunity against pathogens [86]. Dendritic cells, as key players in the crosstalk between innate and adaptive immunity, are markedly affected by probiotics. Bifidobacterium infantis (B. infantis) stimulates dendritic cells to secrete IL-10, inducing immune tolerance [87]. Certain probiotics, such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus (L. rhamnosus) enhance the activity of natural killer cells, resulting in enhanced antitumor and antimicrobial responses [88].

Probiotic regulation of cytokines at the molecular level is highly sophisticated. L. plantarum efficiently inhibits E. coli-related inflammation by down-regulating the pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β and TNF-α) [89]. In fact, probiotic metabolites such as SCFAs suppress nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) activity via G protein-coupled receptors (GPR43/109A), and this may be a new mechanism of probiotic anti-inflammatory action [90,91]. Probiotic strains have multi-target regulatory effects on signaling pathways. The Toll-like receptor (TLR) pathway is a prime target [92], with L. plantarum modulating NF-κB through TLR2/4 [93]. Probiotics also regulate gene expression of the JAK/STAT pathway in IBD to exert anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects [94,95].

There is strong evidence for probiotic immunomodulation in clinical and laboratory studies. A multi-strain probiotic (MCP®BCMC®) containing six strains of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium has been demonstrated in clinical trials to restore and balance mucosal immunity and maintain the integrity of the intestinal barrier [96]. In bench research, polyphosphates produced by lactic acid bacteria trigger production of heat shock protein 27 (HSP27) in intestinal epithelial cells, which impacts expression of inflammatory cytokines via p38 phosphorylation, as demonstrated in colitis [97]. Interestingly, certain probiotics such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus paracasei (L. paracasei) directly regulate Th17/Treg balance via TLRs, which can be a new approach for IBD treatment [98].

Overall, probiotics modulate the intestinal immunity through multiple mechanisms. They are involved in immune cell modulation, cytokine balance, signal transduction and gene regulation, exhibiting a wide range of immunoregulatory effects. These studies not only offer new insights into the interactions between the host and microbes but also lay a foundation for the theoretical basis of developing probiotic-induced immunomodulation. Future studies should focus on illuminating the mechanisms of different strains and their application in precision medicine to potentially open new avenues for the prevention and treatment of intestinal diseases.

Regulates intestinal metabolites

Probiotics demonstrate significant therapeutic effects for IBD through the modulation of key intestinal metabolites, mainly by the specific regulation of SCFAs, BAs and tryptophan metabolism (Fig. 2C) [51,56,99].

Regulation of SCFAs is a key action of probiotics. Some probiotic species, like Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, break down dietary fibers and generate SCFAs (acetate, propionate and butyrate) [96]. These metabolites have many functions. For example, butyrate has an anti-inflammatory effect by inhibiting histone deacetylases and NF-κB pathways, which significantly reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6) [100]. In terms of the role of intestinal barrier function, SCFAs increase the secretion of mucin (MUC) and the expression of tight junction proteins, which enhances the epithelial barrier [101]. In terms of the immune functions, SCFAs stimulate the GPR43/109A receptors to induce Treg differentiation and maintain the immune balance in the gut [102]. In addition, SCFAs also promote the repair of the intestinal barrier and prevent inflammation and oxidative stress by suppressing the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and regulating autophagy [103]. In particular, butyrate is a source of ∼70% of energy for colon epithelial cells through β-oxidation [104]. While probiotics adapt to produce either acetate/formate or acetate/lactate under low or high carbohydrate conditions. The immune-modulatory and barrier-protective roles of SCFAs are mediated through key nodes. By activating GPR109A receptors, butyrate not only facilitates Treg differentiation, but also increases translocation of Nrf2 to the nucleus via activation of the ERK1/2 pathway to promote transcriptional activity of claudin-4 and occludin [105,106]. This “metabolism-immunity-barrier” triple feedback loop suggests that SCFAs are key players in translating microecological changes in the gut to the functional reprogramming of host cells. Additionally, butyrate increases the secretion of IL-10 in M2 macrophages via inhibition of HDAC3 and induction of H3K9 acetylation at the promoter of IL-10 gene, creating a positive feedback loop [107,108].

Regulation of BA metabolism is another important function of probiotics. Certain probiotic species, such as Akkermansia, produce BSH that convert primary BAs into secondary BAs, such as deoxycholic acid [109,110]. This bioconversion has two-fold therapeutic benefits. First, secondary BAs activate nuclear receptor FXR and membrane receptor TGR5 [111], thereby inhibiting epithelial apoptosis and enhancing antimicrobial peptide expression [112,113]. Second, the altered BAs profile selectively inhibits pathogenic colonization by microorganisms such as C. difficile, thereby preserving microbial equilibrium [114,115]. Some Clostridium strains with BSH/thioesterase activity can conjugate BAs with amino acids (an acidic environment) to generate novel antimicrobial products [116].

The alteration of tryptophan metabolism is an example of the complexity of probiotic mechanisms [117,118]. Microbiota transform tryptophan into a range of bioactive products, such as indole derivatives (indole-3-propionic acid, IPA and indole-3-aldehyde, IAM) and neurotransmitter serotonin precursors [119]. These metabolites have various effects. IPA enhances the gut barrier [120], and also enhances anti-tumor immunity by epigenetically regulating CD8+ T cells (enhancing H3K27 acetylation in the super-enhancer region of Tcf7 gene) [121]. IAM and indolelactic acid (ILA) contribute to gut homeostasis by activating aryl hydrocarbon receptors (AhR), and some probiotics, such as L. rhamnosus, help tryptophan metabolize to IPA through metabolic synergy or inhibit colorectal cancer via increased tryptophan levels [122]. There is a crosstalk between the metabolisms of SCFAs, BAs and tryptophan. Secondary BAs suppress NF-κB activity via the FXR receptors, and work together with HDAC inhibitory action of butyrate to downregulate pro-inflammatory factors' production and tryptophan metabolite IAM activates AhR pathway to increase IL-22 secretion, and IL-22 subsequently enhances goblet cell differentiation and mucus secretion, complementing the effect of butyrate-induced MUC2 expression [119,120]. This bidirectional interaction between the metabolic pathways forms the basis of the biochemical basis of probiotic multi-faceted actions.

Besides the key mechanisms mentioned above, probiotics have other therapeutic effects through the following mechanisms. This includes the synthesis of bacteriocins (such as nisin) to hinder the growth of pathogenic bacteria [123], polyamines (including spermidine) to accelerate repair of the gut epithelium [124] and activation of endogenous antioxidant enzymes through the production of glutathione and SOD to limit oxidative stress [125]. Moreover, probiotic strains such as L. fermentum CECT5716 and L. rhamnosus enhance nutrition by producing essential B vitamins (thiamine, riboflavin, folate and cobalamin) [126], which are often deficient in IBD patients. In the aspect of energy, gut microbiota plays a role in energy metabolism of the host, such as tricarboxylic acid cycle. SCFAs and B vitamins produced by probiotics are key metabolites and coenzymes in ATP production [127], which provide energy to the intestinal epithelium, and promote energy balance of the host.

It is important to note that the above metabolic pathways are not independent but comprise a complex interplay that together mediate probiotic benefits. For instance, SCFAs (such as butyrate) act as energy substrates, histone deacetylase inhibitors, and also function as ligands for GPR41/43 to upregulate tight junction proteins (such as ZO-1 and occludin) in the intestinal epithelium and promote Treg cell differentiation, thus directly connecting metabolism with barrier repair and immune tolerance [90,91,128]. At the same time, probiotic regulation of BAs has multiple impacts: secondary BAs (such as lithocholic acid) on one hand are ligands for FXR/TGR5, which regulate host metabolism and inflammation, and on the other hand indirectly affect the synthesis of SCFAs and tryptophan metabolites by influencing the composition of the gut microbiota [111]. More complexly, tryptophan metabolites (such as indole) have been reported to indirectly activate the pregnane X receptor to regulate the expression of tight junction proteins and inhibit inflammation, thus forming another link between metabolism, barrier and immunity [119]. For instance, the probiotic EcN, in addition to competing with pathogens for iron, also has metabolic effects that influence the levels of SCFAs and indole derivatives in the gut, which in turn modulate the macrophage polarization to M2, as well as ZO-1 expression, to repair the barrier, which highlights the integrated, multi-target, multi-pathway nature of single probiotics. Similarly, LGG has a multifunctional effect by regulating Treg/Th17 balance, barrier repair and MUC secretion via its pili and metabolites (such as IPA) [83,84].

In conclusion, probiotics have multifaceted therapeutic effects through the accurate regulation of SCFAs, BAs and tryptophan metabolism. This helps resolve gut inflammation, restore barrier function, restore immune dysfunction and improve nutritional status. These findings help to deepen our knowledge of the host-microbe interface and offer a theoretical basis for personalized probiotic interventions. Future research will expand the use of probiotics in gut health and the treatment of intestinal diseases.

Promotes barrier restoration

Disruption of the intestinal barrier is a prominent feature of IBD. The barrier is restored by probiotics via several mechanisms. On a molecular level, probiotics upregulate tight junction proteins (ZO-1 and occludin), and downregulate claudin-1 and claudin-2 to enhance intestinal permeability [129]. SCFAs, particularly butyrate, help strengthen the barrier by activating the PPARγ and GPR109A receptors that promote the function of mitochondria in the epithelium [130]. Some probiotic strains, such as AKK and L. rhamnosus MP108, improve the mucus layer by enhancing the number of goblet cells and MUC-2 secretion [131]. Lactobacillus johnsonii (L. johnsonii) has an anti-colitis effect mostly via its secreted extracellular vesicles (LJ-EVs), which directly bind to the intestinal epithelial cells and increase the expression of tight junction proteins, contributing to the integrity of the intestinal barrier [132]. Moreover, probiotics influence the gut microbiota-BA axis, suppress inflammation by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome and increase antioxidant activity via the Nrf2 pathway [133]. They also enhance epithelial through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and IL-22-induced repair [134,135]. It is worth noting that there is considerable crosstalk between the above mechanisms: for instance, SCFAs not only directly reinforce tight junctions, but also activate GPR43 to induce Treg cell differentiation. The IL-10 produced by Treg cells promotes epithelial repair [91,119]. Activation of the Nrf2 pathway not only has antioxidant effects but also increases the expression of tight junction proteins [133]. This multi-targeted, multi-faceted mode of action gives probiotics great potential to repair the intestinal barrier. The above studies show that probiotics can restore intestinal barrier function by regulating tight junctions, microbial metabolite production, mucus layer strengthening, anti-inflammatory/antioxidant effects and epithelial repair (Fig. 2D). Future studies should examine strain-specific mechanisms to enhance therapeutic strategies for IBD.

Regulates extra-intestinal symptoms of IBD via the “gut-X axis”

IBD patients commonly suffer from extra-intestinal symptoms, including anxiety and depression, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), also known as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), resulting in a reduced quality of life. Epidemiological research has shown that the prevalence of neuropsychiatric disorders in patients with IBD is as high as 35%, with generalized anxiety disorder and major depressive disorder being the most prevalent. Recent research suggests probiotics might help to reduce these extra-intestinal symptoms via cross-talk along the gut-X axis (such as the gut-brain axis and gut-liver axis) (Fig. 2E). This is achieved by the influence of gut microbiota and their metabolites on both the local intestinal milieu and the activity of remote organs through neural, endocrine, immune and metabolic pathways [136].

Gut-brain axis

The gut-brain axis represents a bidirectional pathway that connects the gastrointestinal tract (GIT) and the central nervous system (CNS), in which the gut microbiota plays a crucial role [137]. Recent research highlights the significant influence of gut dysbiosis on neuropsychiatric disorders, especially anxiety and depression, which are often associated with IBD [138]. In this regard, probiotics, as microbial therapies, have emerged as potential therapeutic agents due to their ability to re-establish gut homeostasis and reduce neuroinflammation, thus holding promise for the treatment of IBD-associated psychological comorbidities [139].

Gut microbiota dysbiosis is now implicated in IBD-related anxiety and depression. In particular, this includes an abundance of Enterobacteriaceae and increased concentrations of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) which induce systemic and hippocampal inflammation [140]. LPS, a component of Gram-negative bacterial membranes, triggers the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α, which then compromise the integrity of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and activate microglia, leading to neuroinflammation [141]. Moreover, transplantation of the gut microbiota from IBD patients with depression into germ-free mice results in depression-like behavior and colitis, showing the causal role of dysbiosis in these conditions. In contrast, FMT from healthy mice or administration of anti-inflammatory drugs (e.g., sulfasalazine) reverses these effects, suggesting that microbiota manipulation may be a viable therapeutic strategy [140]. Probiotics, especially certain strains of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, have beneficial effects via several mechanisms. First, in terms of anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects, probiotics like L. plantarum NK151 and B. longum NK173 inhibit NF-κB activation in macrophages, leading to decreased IL-6 and TNF-α secretion [142]. These strains also increase Treg, offsetting the Th17 response responsible for inflammation in IBD and depression [143]. Second, with regard to microbial metabolite production, SCFAs, such as butyrate, play a key role in probiotic action. Butyrate strengthens the intestinal barrier, mitigates LPS translocation and enters the brain via the BBB to exert neuroprotective effects, including improving mitochondrial metabolism and inhibiting microglial activation [144]. Consistent with this, in clinical trials butyrate levels are associated with lower levels of anxiety and depression, and reduced amygdala activation to negative stimuli [145]. Third, probiotics regulate neurotransmitters by modulating synthesis of serotonin, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and dopamine. For example, B. longum NCC3001 boosts plasma tryptophan (a precursor to 5-HT), and Lactobacillus helveticus R0052 boosts GABA, thus reducing anxiety-like symptoms [146]. These actions are mediated by the vagus nerve and direct interactions with Enterochromaffin cells [147]. Lastly, in terms of barrier repair, probiotics enhance tight junction proteins (occludin, ZO-1) to restore “leaky gut” and subsequent inflammation. In particular, L. rhamnosus and Bifidobacterium breve decrease intestinal permeability, which decreases neuroinflammation [148].

Moreover, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) demonstrate the effectiveness of probiotics in IBD patients with psychological symptoms. For instance, engineered probiotics such as EcN have been shown to alleviate anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors in murine models of IBD, an effect associated with the restoration of gut barrier integrity and the modulation of neuroprotective metabolites (such as indoleacetic acid and GABA) via the gut-brain axis [149]. Likewise, synbiotics (probiotics mixed with prebiotics) increase stress resilience via ileal Treg/Th17 ratios and hippocampal chemokine expression [150]. Overall, these observations are consistent with the “cytokine theory of depression” which suggests probiotics relieve depression by suppressing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6 and TNF-α) and increasing anti-inflammatory IL-10 levels [151]. These findings notwithstanding, challenges lie ahead. The specificity of probiotic strains requires careful consideration in therapeutic development, as shown by the enhanced antidepressant effect of combining B. Bifidum novaBBF7 and L. paracasei TJB8 [144]. The complexity of IBD and depression phenotypes requires tailored strategies, possibly involving multi-omics analysis to discover biomarkers such as Prevotella abundance and BA metabolites [152]. In the future, studies should focus on mechanistic research in germ-free animal models and large-scale human studies to support translation [147].

Overall, the gut-brain axis is a key link in the management of IBD-associated anxiety and depression. Probiotics present a complex interaction with this axis, harnessing anti-inflammatory, metabolic and neuroactive pathways to promote balance. Probiotics, through their ability to reverse microbial imbalance and subsequent inflammatory pathways, provide a new, non-invasive approach to treat gut and brain symptoms. To advance this field, collaboration between disciplines is needed to help dissect the intricate relationship between microbial communities, immune responses and neural networks, leading to the development of individualised microbiome therapies [137].

Gut-liver axis

The gut-liver axis is a two-way communication link between intestinal homeostasis and hepatic metabolism and immunity, which is critical for the development of IBD-related hepatic fatty disease [153]. Gut microbiota dysbiosis, a characteristic of IBD, results in compromised intestinal barrier function, facilitating the translocation of microbial mediators, including LPS and pathogen-associated molecular patterns to the liver through the portal vein [96]. In consequence, microbial products trigger hepatic innate immunity, especially via TLR4 and NF-κB pathways, resulting in inflammation, steatosis and fibrosis, the hallmarks of NAFLD [154]. Probiotics, particularly species of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, have shown potential in this regard by altering the gut microbiota, enhancing the intestinal barrier and reducing hepatic inflammation through various mechanisms [155].

IBD is associated with depletion of protective bacteria (Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Bifidobacterium spp.) and increase in pro-inflammatory bacteria (Enterobacteriaceae and Proteobacteria), which further contribute to increased gut permeability and systemic endotoxemia [155]. As a result, increased portal LPS concentrations activate Kupffer cells (hepatic macrophages) leading to M1 polarization and the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β [156]. This inflammatory response not only sustains hepatic insulin resistance but also suppresses peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α, a key factor in fatty acid metabolism, leading to increased lipid deposition [157]. Interestingly, probiotics including Bifidobacterium breve CNCM I-4035 and L. paracasei CNCM I-4034 have been demonstrated to restore gut microbiota composition, decrease LPS translocation, and inhibit hepatic NF-κB activation, leading to reduced hepatic steatosis and inflammation in animal models [158]. One of the key mechanisms of probiotic-mediated improvement of NAFLD during IBD is regulation of macrophage polarization. M1 macrophages in the liver contribute to inflammation through induction of Nos2 and Cd86, while M2 macrophages facilitate tissue repair by expressing Cd163 and Arg1 [159]. Probiotics promote the dominance of M2 macrophages, which is shown by the decrease in hepatic leukocyte infiltration and myeloperoxidase activity in Zucker-Lepr fa/fa rats that received L. rhamnosus CNCM I-4036 [160]. This shift in the immune response is achieved through increased expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines and decreased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines like monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 and RANTES, which have been shown to drive fibrogenesis. Moreover, probiotics increase the hepatic ratio of phospho-Akt/Akt, an important signaling pathway of insulin sensitivity, thereby ameliorating the metabolic aberration in NAFLD [161].

In terms of metabolism, probiotics modulate host metabolism by producing SCFAs such as acetate, propionate and butyrate, which act as substrates for colonocyte energy production as well as ligands for G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) such as GPR41 and GPR43 [162]. In particular, SCFAs suppress hepatic de novo lipogenesis by suppressing sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c) and simultaneously activating PPARα-dependent fatty acid β-oxidation [163]. Furthermore, probiotics alter BA metabolism by promoting BSH activity that converts conjugated primary BAs to secondary BAs, including hyodeoxycholic acid (HDCA). HDCA, in turn, inhibits intestinal foresaid X receptor (FXR), decreasing hepatic lipogenesis and inducing cytochrome P450 7B1 (CYP7B1), an enzyme leading to alternative BA synthesis [164]. This combined effect, which involves both FXR inhibition and CYP7B1 induction, makes HDCA a promising therapeutic metabolite for NAFLD [165]. Although probiotics hold promise for NAFLD, clinical trials have produced mixed results. For example, a RCT of multi-strain probiotics (Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium spp.) did not improve hepatic steatosis or fibrosis, but did help maintain intestinal barrier integrity, as measured by maintenance of ZO-1 expression in the small intestine [166]. This suggests there is a role for strain optimization, longer treatment times and individualized microbiota analysis [96]. New approaches to overcoming these limitations include engineered probiotics, such as Pediococcus pentosaceus KID7, to target cholesterol levels, and synbiotics for improved microbial survival, which contain probiotics and prebiotics [167]. Furthermore, FMT from healthy donors has been shown to alleviate depressive-like symptoms and colitis in mice, suggesting the potential role of FMT in modifying the gut-liver axis in IBD-NAFLD [168].

In summary, the gut-liver axis plays a pivotal role in the development of IBD-related fatty liver disease. Probiotics are a promising therapeutic strategy that addresses multiple factors by normalizing gut microbiota, improving barrier integrity and regulating liver immunity and metabolism. This is achieved via macrophage polarization, SCFA and BA metabolism, but more research is needed to translate this into clinical practice. Research should also address strain-specific impacts, host-microbe metabolic crosstalk and combination therapies to fully exploit probiotics in the treatment of this multifaceted comorbidity [169].

Synergistic multi-mechanism action of probiotics

Probiotics have numerous mechanisms of action to treat IBD. These mechanisms work in a concerted manner. Probiotics compete with pathogens for nutrients and adhesion sites, and produce antimicrobial organic acids and bacteriocins. This sets the stage for immune and barrier repair [61]. They regulate the immune system of the gut: they drive macrophages towards an anti-inflammatory M2 profile, block activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, induce the differentiation of regulatory T cells, and maintain a ratio of Th17/Treg cells. Key pathways involved include TLR, NF-κB and JAK/STAT [83,95]. In addition, probiotics regulate SCFA, BA and tryptophan metabolism [128]. Secondary BAs act via FXR/TGR5 to modulate metabolism and inflammation, while tryptophan metabolites such as IPA and IAM gut homeostasis and repair the intestinal epithelium via the AhR pathway [119]. Probiotics restore the gut barrier by increasing junction proteins (ZO-1 and occludin), goblet cells and MUC-2, triggering the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway and epithelial regeneration via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [78].

These four key mechanisms work in a multi-faceted, multi-targeted, multi-pathway fashion. Suppression of pathogens enables immune and barrier functions. Metabolites function as signaling molecules that link the immune and barrier. Cytokines produced by immune cells also contribute to barrier integrity and, in turn, the barrier protects metabolism and prevents pathogen entry [91,119]. This multi-faceted action is the basis for probiotic treatment of intestinal inflammatory disorders.

Strategies for constructing oral probiotic delivery systems

The oral administration of probiotic supplements demonstrates a range of important physiological functionalities, including lowering high cholesterol, producing essential vitamins and natural antibacterial substances in the body, and helping to regulate the immune system [170,171]. These microbes are not only used to maintain general health and prevent disease. They are also widely applied in treatments for both gut-related and systemic conditions, including diarrheal diseases, IBD, autoimmune diseases, cancer, etc. [172,173]. However, oral delivery-based probiotic therapy faces plenty of challenges, mainly due to the sensitivity of probiotics to harsh conditions of the digestive tract and often fail to settle in the gut effectively, which leads to significant bacterial inactivation and compromised therapeutic efficacy [174].

Successful oral administration of probiotics is to keep the bacterial cells alive, ensure adequate colonization of the gut and sustain the therapeutic effect. Once probiotics go into the GIT, they face survival problems because of the harsh chemical environment [175]. The stomach maintains a very low pH level. In the gut, BAs show surface-active characteristics, and there are also many hydrolytic enzymes present. Any of these conditions can harm the internal structure of probiotic cells. When this happens, the cells cannot carry out their regular functions normally, and their survival capacity will be greatly weakened [17]. There are a number of ways scientists attempt to overcome these problems. One is to protect the cells by microencapsulation with physical materials that can resist digestion. Alternatively, chemical or genetic engineering can be used to increase acid resistance or protect the sites on the cells where enzymes normally bind [176]. Probiotics are also under threat from physical shear. This is caused by the motility of the digestive tract, and can break weak bacterial cells or carriers. This makes probiotics leak out. So we need to create more flexible and mechanically stable cells and carriers [177]. Probiotics need to adhere to the gut and colonize the gut wall. However, it is a major challenge for oral delivery [178]. The intestinal mucus layer, which is made mostly of MUC, acts like a dense physical barrier. It prevents probiotics from interacting with the mucous membrane of the gut so that they cannot colonize the gut and are rapidly eliminated due to peristalsis [179,180]. So, researchers create probiotics that secrete adhesins that bind to MUC. They also make them produce mucinases, which allow the bacteria to penetrate through the mucus and get in contact with the epithelial cells. Both can enhance their ability to colonize the gut [181]. Also, the immune system in the gut may consider probiotics as foreign bodies when they reach the gut. This triggers cells, such as macrophages, to ingest and digest the probiotics, and therefore makes permanent colonization difficult [182]. The above strategies can generally be divided into three categories according to the modifications made to probiotics physical encapsulation, biological and chemical modification [183] (Table 1). The core purpose of all these strategies is to protect probiotics while they travel through the digestive tract, boost their ability to colonize the gut, and ultimately build a precise, efficient and stable oral probiotic delivery system.

Physical engineering of probiotics

Hydrogel encapsulation

Hydrogels are highly porous, three-dimensional (3D) polymer networks [213]. These hydrogels are typically formed by covalent crosslinking or supramolecular assembly via non-covalent interactions such as electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking [214]. This allows the development of multifunctional composite hydrogels with properties. In particular, these hydrogels are extremely biocompatible and can modulate their viscoelastic properties. They also exhibit responsiveness to stimuli for programmed degradation, and in some instances even self-healing behavior. These characteristics have led to the use of hydrogels as a platform for many applications. They serve as biomimetic scaffolds for 3D cell culture [215], bioactive scaffolds for regenerative medicine [216] and carriers for drug and cell-based therapies [217]. As a novel strategy in microbial therapy, hydrogels are being used as protective encapsulation matrices for probiotics to overcome key problems of oral probiotic therapy.

A variety of polysaccharide-based hydrogels have been developed for encapsulation of probiotics, because they are an excellent physical barrier against gastric fluid and BAs. A good example is alginate, a naturally occurring polysaccharide [187]. However, most of the single component-based hydrogel systems have inherent structural limitations such as weak mechanical strength and high fluid permeability as a result of large pore sizes [218]. As such, alginate is frequently used in conjunction with other materials. Kim et al. have developed a bentonite/alginate nanocomposite hydrogel that can protect LGG against the penetration of gastric fluid and provide on-demand release in the intestinal tract (Fig. 3A) [219]. The impermeable and cushioning bentonite is intercalated and forms a hydrogen-bonded barrier. This decreases the size of the hydrogel pores and thus improves the survival of LGG over alginate alone. In recent developments, pure polysaccharide-based matrices have been extended towards protein-polysaccharide composite matrices. Here, enzymatic crosslinking is crucial to maintain the structure. For example, the multifunctional transglutaminase (TGase) enzyme. It catalyzes the acyl transfer between glutamine and lysine residues to form isopeptide bonds through covalent crosslinking [220], which enhances the density and mechanical properties of the protein network. This makes TGase particularly promising for improving gastric resistance in probiotic delivery. Sodium caseinate (NaCas), which is rich in glutamine and lysine residues, serves as an ideal substrate for TGase‑mediated crosslinking [221]. Kim et al. innovatively developed dual-crosslinked ι-carrageenan (ιC)/NaCas synbiotic microgels by using sequential ionic and enzymatic crosslinking (Fig. 3B) [195]. In vitro gastrointestinal studies showed improved probiotic viability under simulated gastric fluid (SGF) and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) compared to free cells. Extended stability assessments revealed above 6 log CFU/g after 14 d of storage at 4 °C, attributed to the dense TGase-modified NaCas network that restricts oxygen permeability.

Emerging research is advancing “smart” hydrogel systems that can precisely modulate the gut microbiota in a targeted way and improve treatment outcomes for both gastrointestinal and systemic diseases. By taking advantage of the excessive H2S production by gut microbes such as ST in inflamed intestinal tissues, an H2S-responsive hydrogel was fabricated (Fig. 3C) [194]. Based on thiolated hyaluronic acid (HA-SH), this system degrades through redox-induced disulfide bond cleavage in response to H2S at the target site, allowing intestinal-targeted delivery of L. rhamnosus. Huang et al. engineered a ROS-responsive hydrogel system utilizing methacrylated HA (MeHA) for encapsulating and site-specifically delivering Lactobacillus reuteri (L. reuteri) (HA-LR) (Fig. 3D) [193]. The hydrogel network was formed through thiol-ene click chemistry between MeHA and thiol-functionalized thioketal linkers under physiological conditions. Oral administration experiments showed remarkable protection efficacy. After sequential exposure to SGF and SIF, the HA‑LR formulation maintained less than a one log reduction in colony‑forming units, whereas bare bacteria lost all viability. Once the anionic hydrogel reached the inflamed colon, it preferentially adhered to the positively charged mucosal surface. The excessive generation of ROS at the site of inflammation led to the cleavage of the thioketal and controlled release of probiotics. Notably, the thioketal moiety had dual functions. It was a cleavable component of the structure and also a scavenger of ROS which helps to ameliorate the oxidative stress associated with IBD.

The evolution of oral probiotic hydrogel delivery systems from single-polymer-based hydrogels to polysaccharide-protein composite hydrogels and to smart hydrogels are occurring in the carrier materials. Through material modification, hydrogels can now simultaneously provide probiotic protection and regulate probiotic bioactivity. This enhances the efficiency and accuracy of delivery and treatment in the GIT [222]. But insufficient mechanical strength and probiotic release in the stomach still hinder their practical applications and industrialization .

Film-inspired encapsulation

Microorganisms are often embedded in a concentrated extracellular polymeric matrix, which is mainly comprised of polysaccharides, proteins and extracellular DNA. This dense structure of microorganisms is referred to as a biofilm. Biofilms are extremely adept at allowing microorganisms to survive under a wide range of extreme conditions, and physical or chemical assaults. They can be disrupted by hostile environments, removed by mechanical forces, attacked by antibiotics, or destroyed by host immune system [223]. Taking cues from the resilience of natural biofilms, researchers have begun to explore biofilm-based probiotic delivery systems as a way to deliver probiotics [224]. Wang et al. have created a self-assembled biofilm matrix containing 93.6 wt% water and 8.9 × 109 CFU/g bacteria by growing BS on minimal salts glycerol glutamate (MSgg) agar plates (Fig. 4A) [187]. This biofilm-coated BS (BCBS) exhibits remarkable properties. It does not dissolve in SGF (pH 1.2), enhancing survival through stomach acid. Moreover, the biofilm-derived non-wetting surface properties endowed BCBS with antibiotic resistance against ampicillin (10 mg/ml). Compared to free BS, a significantly higher number of BCBS was quantified adhering to intestinal segments including the small intestine, large intestine and cecum, with retention lasting up to 120 h. The sustained mucoadhesion was mediated by continuous TasA protein secretion from the biofilm matrix. These findings highlight biofilm self-coating as a robust platform for next-generation probiotic delivery systems.

However, not all therapeutic probiotic strains can form biofilms naturally, encapsulation into preformed biofilms improves gastrointestinal colonization and retention is necessary. Biofilm matrix materials mainly derive from polymeric macromolecules of natural or synthetic origin. Savitskaya et al. developed an oral probiotic delivery system (PMPB) for LGG encapsulated in a polysaccharide matrix biofilm [189]. The biofilm matrix was produced by coculturing two microbial producers: Komagataeibacter xylinus (bacterial cellulose) and Aureobasidium pullulans (pullulan). Then, bacterial cellulose/pullulan granules were placed into a PYGm medium containing LGG, to facilitate biofilm formation during 48 h incubation at 37 °C (Fig. 4B). The developed matrix showed excellent protection for LGG under simulated gastrointestinal stress such as low pH (2.0) and 0.3% bile salt. In colon fluid simulations using fecalase, the system showed sustained release. The PMPB granules gradually released 11.4 log CFU/g of viable probiotics over a 12 h period. The in vivo results in rats with LGG cells coated with fluorescent-tagging showed effective delivery to the colon, and the cells could be found in the feces for up to 72 h. Another effective strategy in biofilm‑based delivery systems involves the use of hollow semi‑permeable dextranomer microspheres (DM). This approach is especially useful for the oral administration of L. reuteri [226]. Bacterial adhesion to DM can be improved by supplementing with carbohydrates. Navarro et al. developed a biofilm-based delivery approach by immobilizing L. reuteri on biocompatible DM substrates (Fig. 4C) [188]. When wild‑type L. reuteri was combined with DM‑sucrose or DM‑maltose conjugates, acid resistance at pH 2 improved significantly, with survival rates rising by nearly one log compared to planktonic cells. Moreover, biofilm‑encapsulated L. reuteri with these carbohydrate additives showed better adhesion to intestinal epithelial cells.

Recently, a montmorillonite-based composite delivery platform fabricated via microfluidics has been proposed and utilized in the treatment of UC (Fig. 4D) [225]. Montmorillonite has excellent cation‑exchange capacity, so under acidic conditions it can interact with negatively charged L. acidophilus to form a montmorillonite-L. acidophilus biofilm (MLB) [227]. Subsequently, by integrating microfluidic electrospray technology, a calcium alginate composite microcarrier loaded with both MLB and IPA was successfully constructed. This biofilm‑involved system enhanced the gastrointestinal tolerance and intestinal colonization of L. acidophilus. It also boosted therapeutic effects against UC through the synergistic action of IPA. By realizing the efficient synergistic co-delivery of probiotics and prebiotics, this strategy provides a promising approach to enhance the intestinal retention and bioactivity of probiotics via biofilm-based oral delivery.

Probiotic biofilms, recognized as the fourth-generation of probiotics, are an innovative probiotic encapsulation strategy. They protect probiotics via the physical barrier formed by EPS, enhance their host colonization ability, and enable probiotics to evade host immune defense [228]. The main types of probiotic biofilms include self-encapsulated biofilms, biofilm-component-based biofilms and carrier-mediated biofilms [183]. Traditional biofilm-based delivery systems only focus on improving probiotic survival, while future designs will move toward multifunctional synergy, such as antibacterial activity, anti-inflammatory effects and nutrient co-delivery, to expand application scenarios. However, current research on biofilm-mediated probiotic delivery remains at the laboratory stage. Practical application is hindered by unresolved challenges, including complex preparation, high costs and prominent microbial safety issues.

Microsphere encapsulation

Microencapsulation is now recognized as a promising strategy for supporting the survival of probiotics in the hostile gut environment. This involves encapsulating these “good” bacteria within a protective polymer, which physically protects and facilitates controlled release [229]. Biopolymers derived from polysaccharides such as sodium alginate (SA), chitosan (CS), pectin and their derivatives are now preferred materials due to their biocompatibility and other desirable properties [230]. SA microcapsules are also more acid-resistant than other biopolymers, which is essential to protect probiotics during passage through the stomach (pH 1.5–3.5). This makes SA the material of choice for probiotic encapsulation. However, the porous nature of SA can lead to premature release of probiotics. It has been shown that adding inorganic nanoparticles can improve the viability of probiotics by blocking the pores in probiotic-loaded microgels [231]. Mohamadzadeh mixed L. reuteri and MgO nanoparticles in a synbiotic suspension (Fig. 5A) [200]. This was mixed with 2% SA and extruded into 0.1 M CaCl2 solution. The calcium ions from CaCl2 solution crosslinked the carboxylate groups of alginates to form a hydrogel to encapsulate the probiotic. The SA-based bionanocomposite structure provides a twin protective effect. The MgO nanoparticles increase the strength and resistance to degradation in the stomach. Meanwhile, the pH-sensitive nature of the alginate pores allows controlled release in the gut and high viability by protecting probiotics from environmental stresses during processing and digestion. In the area of microsphere encapsulation, recent breakthroughs have highlighted the synergistic potential of co‑encapsulating probiotics with plant‑derived bioactive compounds, especially those from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) [232]. Luo et al. developed a microencapsulation system using zein and isomalt oligosaccharides for L. casei BYglAB01, Lactobacillus bulgaricus (L. bulgaricus) and Polygonatum sibiricum saponins (PSS) (Fig. 5B) [201]. This system features a protective zein shell and a hydrophilic core that allows probiotic-metabolite interactions. The PSS inside the microsphere matrix acts as a prebiotic. The L. casei and L. bulgaricus were co-culture at a 2:1 ratio within this confined microenvironment, they showed stronger α‑glucosidase inhibitory activity than free strains. The zein shell preserved probiotic viability, keeping it above 7 log CFU/ml under gastric acidic conditions at pH 1.2. It also enabled sequential release, with sustained PSS delivery occurring in both the stomach and the intestine. This work demonstrates that microsphere engineering can effectively integrate phytochemical-probiotic synergism to improve therapeutic efficacy via functional integration.

Next-generation probiotic microencapsulation systems are expected to enable robust protection in the upper GIT and site-specific release in colonic lesions via intelligent modulation of microsphere cross-linking density, with integrated active chemotaxis and gut microbiota regulation capabilities. A recent study innovatively encapsulated EcN into pH-responsive DNA microcapsules. These microcapsules were made by crosslinking hyaluronic acid methacrylate (HAMA) with acrylate-modified A/C-rich oligonucleotides. Manganese carbonyl (MnCO) was also loaded into them, giving the microcapsules inflammatory-targeted micromotor properties for treating DSS‑induced IBD [233]. When these microcapsules pass sequentially through the stomach (pH ∼2) and duodenum (pH ∼5), A-rich and C-rich sequences form stable A-motif and i-motif structures, respectively (Fig. 5C). This significantly increases cross-linking density, induces microcapsule shrinkage, and forms a dense physical barrier between EcN and gastrointestinal fluids. Only upon reaching the colon (pH > 7) do their cross-linking density and particle size recover. Excessive nitric oxide (NO) in the inflamed colon induces microcapsule disintegration, releasing EcN and MnCO. MnCO reacts with elevated ROS in inflamed tissues to generate carbon monoxide (CO), which CO not only drives microcapsule chemotaxis to the inflammatory site but also promotes M2 macrophage polarization. It also exerts immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects, synergizing with EcN to remodel intestinal flora and restore intestinal barrier function [234]. This study proposes a novel micromotor probiotic microcapsule strategy, enabling intelligent delivery with high cross-linking protection in the small intestine and low cross-linking release in the colon. It provides a new approach for probiotic chemotaxis to the inflamed colon and responsive release in the lesion microenvironment.

Microspheres are uniform monolithic particles, usually between 0.1 and 1000 μm in diameter. They have become an important type of carrier for building controlled drug release systems [235]. They have been widely employed in oral delivery systems for probiotics. Researchers didn't satisfy at protective encapsulation using single matrices. They moved on to composite material coatings to intelligent carriers that achieve precisely controlled release. The core advantages of microspheres include customizable intestinal targeting, tunable layer number and wall thickness, as well as versatile dosage forms and application adaptability. However, conventional preparation methods like extrusion and spray drying are not ideal for production. These processes are usually inefficient and prone to damage the bacteria [236]. In conclusion, microsphere encapsulation is a reliable method for industrializing and practicing oral probiotics. With better design of the microcarriers or preparation methods, we may expect more efficient and stable probiotics in the future.

Layer by layer encapsulation

Layer by layer (LbL) was initially suggested by Gero Decher in 1991 [237]. It involves alternate electrostatic assembly of polyanions and polycations to form well-defined multilayers [238]. The technique was first developed to enhance the drug delivery of pharmaceuticals. It has since been adapted for the encapsulation of individual probiotic cells. This is because of its simplicity, controllability of thickness and semi-permeability [204]. LbL provides a higher material efficiency with high coating efficiency and minimal matrix wastage compared to traditional microencapsulation in beads. Biodegradable polyelectrolytes are required for the ideal encapsulation system to maintain high cellular viability, withstand harsh gastrointestinal conditions (pH 1.5–3, bile salts, enzymes) and extreme storage temperatures (45–75 °C). Maram et al. adopted a food-grade degradable black seed protein (BSP) derived from Nigella sativa defatted seed cakes (NsDSC) to coat L. rhamnosus via LbL (Fig. 6A) [205]. They used TGase to cross-link the BSP layer by forming isopeptide bonds between glutamine and lysine residues. This approach using BSP and alginate was highly protective. This significantly enhanced the resistance of L. rhamnosus to simulated gastrointestinal and thermal stressors, demonstrating the LbL technology that can be used to improve probiotic stability.

Du et al. successfully developed a double-layer SNUP encapsulated Liiliilactobacillus salivarius (LS@CCMC) (Fig. 6B) [202]. This approach forms a robust multilayered encapsulation using an exchange of CS and carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC). The coating is achieved by 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide/N-hydroxysuccinimide (EDC/NHS)-mediated covalent bonding and electrostatic forces. CS offers positively charged surfaces and inherent biocompatibility, with CMC providing increased stability and adding to the negative charge. The combination of this LbL coating allows functionalized delivery of bacteria. By doing so, the bacterial cells can colonize the small intestine well and effectively alleviate the symptoms of a mouse model of Parkinson's disease via the sustained release of GABA. Recently, Huo et al. fabricated multilayer nanocoating on EcN through salting-out with 0.1 M K⁺ phosphate buffer (pH 5.5) [203]. This technique allowed the transition of the silk fibroin structure from random coil to β-sheet, and permitted self-assembly by LbL deposition (Fig. 6C). In vivo results demonstrated that oral administration of EcN with nanocoating resulted in greater colonization in the intestines of mice than uncoated EcN. As EcN grew in the intestine, the coating was naturally diluted to release EcN for local colonization. The pharmaceutical effect of the natural silk fibroin nanocoating synergistically improved the therapeutic effect in a mouse model of intestinal mucositis.

In another recent study, Falco et al. developed a linearly tunable viscoelastic coating for bacteria (Fig. 6D) [204]. They applied a LbL electrostatic self-assembly process with positively charged CS and a negatively charged sulfated β-glucan (sbG4) at pH 5.6. This design has adaptive properties. By changing the time of rinsing, they were able to control the stiffness and swelling behavior of the coating, which is a significant breakthrough in dynamic coating design. They used quartz crystal microbalance with dissipation (QCM-D) to study the assembly process. This revealed a new assembly mechanism. During CS deposition there were large frequency shifts (ΔF), which reflect soft matrixes, and small dissipation shifts (ΔD). SbG4 adsorption showed very different ΔD curves. The sbG4 layers expanded and the CS layers shrunk during rinsing, which resulted in ΔF/ΔD increases and decreases, respectively. This resulted in the formation of the zigzag patterns in the ΔF/ΔD traces, which are a kinetic signature of LbL assembly. The multilayer coating also exhibits hierarchical mechanical properties. The initial four layers are controlled by swelling and compression. Increasing the number of layers stiffens the film through interpenetration. This results in pH- and bile salt-responsive coating. It remains stable at pH 1.2 but deforms at pH 6.8 due to bile salt-sbG4 interactions. This spatiotemporal responsiveness to multiple stimuli is a paradigm for the next generation of gastrointestinal drug delivery matrices that can deliver drugs at targeted locations.

LbL has proven to be a promising method for the surface encapsulation of probiotics. As compared with single wall encapsulation, LbL can construct multi-layer shells through the sequential deposition of different functional polymers [239]. This method not only avoids the limitations of single wall materials, including low mechanical stability, acid sensitivity and burst release, but also allows great flexibility in tuning the barrier and release properties without increasing the particle size [240]. Despite disadvantages such as complex preparation process and poor biocompatibility of some materials, the strength of LbL is most evident in the integration of functional properties.

Emulsification

Emulsions offer a gentle protection. Emulsifiers and phase volumes can be varied to suit different delivery systems, with damage to the structure and probiotic activity reduced [241]. Huang et al. used SA as the encapsulant for Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. Lactis F1–7 via using emulsification internal gelation technique [196]. They added the bacterial cells to human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs) and alginate-CaCO3, then emulsified with soybean oil containing Tween 80 to make a water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion. The alginate ionic network protected the bacteria from gastric acid and the oil phase protected against oxidation and enzyme degradation. The optimized formulation resulted in increased bacterial survival during freeze-drying, increased stability during storage (28 d at 4 °C) and higher survival in simulated gastrointestinal environments. Water-in-oil-in-water (W/O/W) emulsions have demonstrated great potential for encapsulating hydrophilic bioactive compounds. Proteins are good natural emulsifiers due to their amphiphilic properties [242]. They lower the water-oil interfacial tension and stabilize the emulsion [243]. In a recent study, Zhang et al. developed a W/O/W double emulsions system with high-intensity ultrasound-treated pea protein isolate (HIU-PPI) and pectin to encapsulate L. plantarum encapsulation [197]. This system had an encapsulation efficiency of 52.65% at an optimum HIU-PPI concentration of 4%. The optimal HIU-PPI concentration was 4%, which led to an encapsulation efficiency of 52.65%. Studies on the mechanisms of the action of HIU-PPI showed that it enhanced probiotic viability in the different stages of digestion. Zhao et al. used multi-layer emulsification to produce a core-shell microcapsule for the encapsulation of L. plantarum KLDS1.0318. The inner aqueous phase was a suspension of the probiotic in SA, cryoprotectants and CaCO3. This was mixed with an oil phase, which was then mixed with an outer aqueous phase of SA or Shellac (SC, FDA-approved E904) [198]. The inner phase created a protective network through Ca2+-SA crosslinking and the intermediate oil layer created a hydrophobic barrier to water and oxygen. The pH-sensitive outer shell protected the probiotic from acidity and released it in the gut. This layered W/O/W emulsification process and freeze-drying yielded stable microcapsules.

Overall, emulsification is a flexible and mild strategy for the encapsulation of probiotics, which provides effective protection for probiotics against the gastrointestinal environment. Emulsification is easy to operate and low-cost, showing good application prospects in food and pharmaceutical fields. However, some problems still exist, such as the difficulty in precisely controlling the release behavior. In addition, the product is hard to preserve due to the unstable droplet system, and usually requires further solidification to ensure high viability and realize industrial application.

Natural cell membrane coating

Natural cell membranes play essential roles in cellular life, mediating cell-to-cell communication, competition, survival and stress responses [244]. Membrane-coated probiotics inherit the biological functions of cell membranes, including membrane receptors like TLRs, interferon receptors and tumor necrosis factor receptors. These receptors neutralize inflammatory factors through receptor-ligand interactions [245]. Lin et al. coated EcN with yeast membrane (YM) to enhance its accumulation in Peyer’s patches (PPs) after oral administration (Fig. 7A) [184]. β-glucan (GN) on YMs showed superior stability in SGF and SIF, maintaining structural integrity for over 4 h, which is important for M cell uptake. Oral administration revealed YM-coated EcN accumulated in PPs, and may further facilitated Dectin-1 receptor-mediated phagocytosis of M cells distributed in the intestinal epithelium. In vitro co-culture studies showed YM coating significantly increased M cell internalization of EcN, confirming enhanced probiotic uptake. This was further validated in mice, where YM-EcN accumulated in PPs and mesenteric lymph nodes (MLNs), leading to elevated levels of IgA in intestinal lavage fluid and indicating mucosal immune priming. The induced immunity modulated the microbiota to maintain homeostasis during pathogenic challenges. In infection and intestinal manipulation (IM) models, YM-EcN administration alleviated intestinal barrier dysfunction, reduced systemic inflammation markers (TNF-α and IL-6) and demonstrated comprehensive barrier protection.

AKK membrane (AKM) vesicles have been demonstrated to modulate mucosal adaptive immunity and restore the gut physicochemical barrier. They also help to re-balance the altered gut microbiota. This is thought to be due to proteins on the surface of AKM [246]. Su et al. developed a probiotic encapsulation platform via sequential electrostatic self-assembly, integrating EcN with AKM and astaxanthin (AST) (Fig. 7B) [186]. This process began with the resuspension of EcN in calcium phosphate buffer and vortex-mediated co-precipitation with hypertonic CaCl2. This rendered EcN positively charged via calcium ion coordination, while phospholipid vesicles derived from AKM and AST remained negatively charged. The cationic potential of the EcN and the anionic potential of the vesicles enabled spontaneous membrane fusion and allowed the phospholipids to self-organize to form a coating. AKM coating greatly enhanced the mucosal adhesion, colonization and growth of EcN. This strategy significantly improved probiotic survival in vitro SGF and SIF (pH 1.2 and pH 6.8, respectively) and in vivo. Cao et al. developed a novel approach to create stealth bacteria by cell membrane camouflage (Fig. 7C) [185]. This method generates cell membrane-coated bacteria (CMCB) via co-extrusion of erythrocyte membranes with EcN. Erythrocyte membranes were selected due to their low immunogenicity and inherent anti-phagocytic properties, which minimize the clearance of CMCB. Assaying the circulation of bacteria time in mice, erythrocyte-coated EcN was compared to uncoated EcN. The coated bacteria remained in the bloodstream for longer and were less susceptible to monocyte phagocytosis. They also induced less inflammatory reactions and had improved biocompatibility. CMCB exhibited enhanced tumor retention in a 4T1 tumor-bearing mouse model. They accumulated quickly, within 1 h of injection, with five times more bacteria than uncoated EcN. Indeed, CMCB showed a 3240-fold higher retention in tumor than in healthy tissues 12 days later, suggesting strong targeting specificity and long-term retention of the bacteria. Finally, yeasts with high surface glycoproteins can also be effectively taken up by M cells, which bear Dectin-1 receptors [247].

Cell membrane encapsulation brings unique advantages to probiotic delivery. It helps reverse dysbiosis and re‑establish immune tolerance, which directly addresses the underlying causes of immune disorders in IBD [248]. Notably, probiotics can mimic host cells or commensal bacteria to achieve immune evasion. Meanwhile, fimbriae and polysaccharide ligands retained on the membrane surface can directly bind to receptors on intestinal epithelial cells, thereby improving colonization efficiency. Nevertheless, this strategy also has several limitations, including complex preparation processes, unstable membrane sources, poor storage stability and relatively high cost, which restrict its practical application.

Electrostatic interaction mediated encapsulation

Bacteria can be classified into two types, Gram-positive and Gram-negative, based on the structure and composition of their cell wall. Cell walls of Gram-positive bacteria are negatively charged because they contain peptidoglycan and phosphodiester-linked teichoic acids, while cell walls of Gram-negative bacteria are negatively charged because they have phospholipids and LPS coating their outer membranes [249]. The surface of probiotics is negatively charged, allowing for their rational encapsulation. It simplifies the development of probiotic delivery systems that are driven by electrostatic interactions and provide tunable interface interactions. Zhang et al. designed a multifunctional colloidal system (NTc) through room-temperature co-complexation of ethylenediamine-modified poly-β-cyclodextrin (NPCD) and tannic acid (TA) (Fig. 8A) [190]. The structure is based on the bifunctional structure of NPCD. The NPCD has positively charged surfaces (+20 mV) that aid in targeting bacteria and protect them from gastric acid. It has cyclodextrin cavities that offer host-guest interactions. The negatively charged EcN destabilizes the equilibrium of metastable NTc through electrostatic interactions. Then host-guest interactions promote the formation of compact shells surrounding the bacteria by mimicking extracellular matrices. In vitro experiments confirmed NTc-encapsulated EcN provides multiple protections. It includes increased survival under gastric acid conditions (pH 1–2.5), tolerance to bile salts (>63% viable after deoxycholate treatment) and sterilization conditions. The encapsulated form of the bacteria remained intact after exposure to 30% ethanol, 8 mM H2O2, and 254 nm UV light. Mucoadhesion was confirmed by isothermal titration calorimetry binding assays, which show that MUC has greater mucoadhesion than NTc. The binding constant (K) of MUC was 10 times greater than EUDRAGIT L100 and mucin. In a mice model of ST-induced colitis, NTc-encapsulated EcN delivered a 12-fold greater EcN to pathogen ratio (76:1) compared to free EcN. Meanwhile, treatment improved the severity of the disease. The animals experienced less weight loss, increased colon length and decreased inflammation. Zhu et al. used nanozyme engineering to create a multi-functional probiotic coating system through supramolecular self-assembly. They first created epigallocatechin gallate-CS conjugates (EC) by forming Schiff bases between oxidized EGCG (QEGCG) and the amino groups on CS. Then, they complexed the conjugates with gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) to form ECA complexes (Fig. 8B) [191]. This approach took advantage of the CAT-like effect of the gold nanoparticles and the free radical scavenging effect of the EC conjugates. This led to a much more efficient H2O2 decomposition than either component could provide. These ECA complexes could then be attached to EcN via phenolic-electrostatic interactions to form a protective barrier, ECA@EcN. This system was particularly effective due to its pH-responsive protection. This ECA@EcN survived gastric conditions (pH 1.5, 0.32% pepsin) for >2 h with a survival rate of over 1400 CFU/ml. This was in contrast to native EcN, which was completely killed. Studies of the mechanism found this gastric protection resulted from two forms of interfacial protection. Hydrogen bonding stabilized the coating structure and electrostatic repulsion prevented acid ingress.

Based on the electrostatic interaction between probiotics and oppositely charged materials, one-step or two-step physical encapsulation strategies can rapidly form protective layers in the aqueous phase at room temperature, thereby achieving efficient protection of probiotics through simple and mild processes. However, the stability of electrostatic complexes is susceptible to pH and ionic strength, which may weaken the protective effect in vivo. Meanwhile, the encapsulation efficacy is related to the surface charge characteristics of the strains, making it difficult for a single formulation to be universally applicable to multiple probiotic strains [250]. Furthermore, the long-term biosafety of some polyelectrolytes has not been fully elucidated, and the accurate control of coating thickness and uniformity still faces great challenges.

Biological modification approach

Genetic engineering approach

Advances in synthetic biology and CRISPR-based technologies have transformed the concept of “genome reprogramming” from a vision to a reality. This has led to new approaches to designing smart probiotic delivery systems [183]. Genetic engineering enables strain engineering with a high degree of customization and great potential for application via manipulation of microbial genomes [251]. Culpepper et al. developed a new Saccharomyces boulardii strain (Eng Sb) genetically engineered to stably express a fibronectin-binding protein on its surface and to secrete anti-TNF-α nanobodies [210]. In acute DSS‑induced colitis models, the engineered Eng Sb strain selectively adhered to ulcerated regions in the colon through specific binding to fibronectin. This resulted in a 100-fold higher colon tissue colonization than the wild-type S. boulardii strain. As mucosal repair occurred, Eng Sb lost its fibronectin binding sites. This resulted in a self-limiting end of colonization. This two-mechanism strategy ensures the targeted delivery of anti-inflammatory nanobodies to the colon, and at the same time, completely prevents the risk of long-term colonization. It thus sets the stage for a paradigm-shifting oral biotherapy for IBD and colitis-associated colorectal cancer.

Genetic engineering enables probiotics to synthesize and deliver therapeutic metabolites directly within the gut. Palmitoylethanolamide (PEA), an endogenous N-acylethanolamide synthesized on demand by N-acylphosphatidylethanolamine-preferring phospholipase D (NAPE-PLD) [252], exhibits potent anti-inflammatory activity, ameliorating intestinal inflammation via intraperitoneal or oral administration [253]. However, the primary reason for its limited in vivo therapeutic efficacy is the low yield, which makes it difficult to achieve an effective therapeutic dose. To address systemic delivery limitations, Esposito et al. developed an in situ therapeutic platform by genetically modifying L. paracasei subsp. paracasei F19 (pNAPE-LP) to express human NAPE-PLD [209]. HPLC-MS analysis showed time-dependent release of PEA in bacterial supernatants at 1, 3, 6 and 12 h, demonstrating that pNAPE-LP sustains PEA production in the GI tract when supplemented with ultra-low-dose exogenous palmitate. In a DSS-induced colitis model, 4 days of pNAPE-LP administration promoted sustained bacterial colonization, with peak PEA concentrations localized to the distal colon. The antibacterial activity of EcN is mainly attributed to its production of microcins MccM and MccH47, collectively referred to as “siderophore Mcc” [254]. These siderophore Mccs work by latching onto the catechol siderophore receptors on target bacteria, then crossing their cell membranes and disrupting the F0 proton channel in the inner membrane, which ultimately kills the bacterial cells [255]. However, the therapeutic effect of oral EcN on IBD is dually limited by the stress of the GIT and the limited secretion of siderophore Mccs. Jiang et al. developed a nanocomposite coating through electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions, utilizing the physical barrier effect of oxidized starch (OS) and the protonophilic effect of polyethyleneimine (PEI) [66]. This coating significantly increased the survival rate of EcN in the GIT, with a 40-fold increase in the stomach and a 74-fold increase in the small intestine. Subsequently, they engineered EcN to synthesize MccM in situ. As a bacteriostatic agent, MccM enables the dynamic regulation of intestinal microecology. By combining this genetically modified EcN with the nanocomposite coating, a “super probiotic” (EcN::mcmA@P/O) was constructed and applied for the treatment of UC. Overall, this synergistic probiotic therapy, which integrates chemical engineering and genetic engineering strategies, has exhibited promising outcomes in the management of UC.

Another exciting possibility for genetic engineering is in systemic disease, where delivery outside of the gut is required. Glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) has potential as a treatment for diabetes mellitus [256]. But its therapeutic potential is hampered by its susceptibility to enzymatic degradation and lack of specificity [257]. In an attempt to overcome these limitations, Wang et al. created a system by incorporating plasmids encoding GLP-1 into EcN and then coating the bacteria with a protective dilauroyl phosphatidylcholine (DLPC) lipid membrane through self-assembly [208]. This approach not only protects the probiotic from degradation in the gut but also leverages the chemotactic behavior of probiotic to deliver the drug to the pancreas. The genetically modified EcN serves as a local “drug factory” and produces GLP-1 in the pancreas. This circumvents degradation of the drug in the body, ensures sustained drug concentrations at the target site, and overcomes the challenges of GLP- delivery due to poor drug stability and bioavailability.

The use of genetically engineered probiotics to develop a novel and promising oral probiotic delivery system is an emerging and innovative approach, with unique engineering advantages over conventional physical encapsulation. Probiotics can be engineered via gene editing to overcome the main challenge of low survival and efficacy of free probiotics in oral delivery [258]. As the rapid development of gene editing technologies and the progress in understanding the intestinal microecology, the targeted design and engineering modification of probiotics will further overcome current limitations, and drive the improvement of more effective, stable and safe oral probiotic delivery systems.

Others

Leveraging the intrinsic biological traits of probiotics or distinctive properties of other living materials to enable oral delivery of probiotics has emerged as a new, efficient and promising approach. Centurion et al. have developed an in situ approach in which probiotics exploit their own nutrient storage compartments to drive the formation of protective PDA nano-shells [207]. The major breakthrough is that they use the manganese storage of lactic acid bacteria to trigger radical-mediated dopamine polymerization in situ. This cell-guided approach enables fast and uniform PDA shell coating via Mn2+-mediated oxidative crosslinking, which is distinct from multi-step encapsulation strategies. The efficiency of these microbially derived nano-coatings show superior acid resistance with 3 log CFU of viable cells in SGF (pH 2.5). Mechanistic analyses show that microbial-derived Mn2+ speeds up the rate of phenolic oxidation coupled with an abiotic system, allowing for localized free-radical formation and sustaining probiotic metabolism. Apart from taking advantage of the microbes' own components, another innovative strategy leverages mammalian cells as encapsulation agents. Undoubtedly, receptors such as scavenger receptors, TLRs and cytokine/ chemokine receptors and external stimulus factors (such as CD163, TNF-α and IL-1) can bind to the macrophage by receptor-ligand binding to induce downstream signaling pathways [259]. By harnessing this property, Wang et al. proposed an attractive intracellular gelation approach to fabricate gelated peritoneal macrophages (GPMs) as a bifunctional platform for probiotic bioprotection and inflammation regulation [211]. This bioinspired strategy exploited enhanced cryo-induced cellular uptake of polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEG-DA) and photoinitiator I2959 through freezing-thawing process (10 min at −80 °C). Further UV-induced spatially controlled polymerization to form cytocompatible hydrogel networks inside macrophages without compromising the original membrane structure. Most GPMs maintained cell morphology after 2 h pre-incubation in SGF (pH 1.2) containing 1.5 × 106 U/g acid-resistant gastric mimic enzyme, demonstrating stability in the low-pH, enzyme-rich gastrointestinal environment. Subsequently, owing to GPM-hitchhiking delivery to inflamed colitis tissue, orally administered GPM-EcN significantly enhanced intestinal accumulation of the surface-anchored EcN, with a nearly 4.8-fold increase compared with free EcN.

The spiral structure of Spirulina platensis (SP) endows it with the ability of autonomous movement in the intestinal tract, making it a natural microrobot [260]. Researchers encapsulated L. plantarum armed with AuCe nanozymes (LP@AuCe) onto SP to construct a composite system (SP@LP@AuCe) [261]. In this study, the spiral structure of SP improved the stability and bioavailability of SP@LP@AuCe in the intestinal tract, and promoted its long-term retention at inflammatory sites [262]. Chlorophyll, which is abundant in SP, possesses strong fluorescence imaging capability, enabling non-invasive and real-time monitoring after oral administration. Results demonstrated that the microrobot loaded with LP@AuCe retained the viability of L. plantarum, reduced intestinal ROS levels, inhibited the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and TNF-α), promoted the reprogramming of macrophages to the anti-inflammatory phenotype. Meanwhile, the microrobot repaired the damaged intestinal barrier and restored the intestinal microbiota. This work provides a basis for the development of next-generation microrobots for gastrointestinal cargo delivery and colitis treatment.

Chemical modification approach

EDC/NHS chemistry

EDC/NHS chemistry offers unique advantages in oral probiotic delivery systems as an efficient and flexible carboxyl activation crosslinking strategy. This approach starts by activating surface carboxyl groups so they form stable covalent bonds with amino or nucleophilic moieties. It then builds functionalized delivery vectors that boost probiotic viability during processing, storage, and gastrointestinal transit, while also improving targeted delivery efficiency. Chen et al. took advantage of the carboxyl groups found in Gram‑negative peptidoglycan to create EcN‑Apt conjugates using EDC/NHS‑mediated amide bonding (Fig. 9A) [17]. This strategy preserved bacterial viability while achieving precise aptamer anchoring through peptidoglycan-accessible carboxyls. The EcN-Apt was further encapsulated in dynamically crosslinked hydrogel (EcN-Apt@HG) composed of aldehyde-modified chondroitin sulfate (CS-CHO) and amine-terminated PAMAM dendrimers. Hydrogel shielding and aptamer-guided localization synergized to improve bacterial survival and site-specific accumulation, outperforming non-targeted EcN@HG controls in disease modulation.

In another study, EcN was functionalized via sequential chemical modifications: cis-aconitic anhydride (CA) conjugates doxorubicin (Dox) to form CA-Dox, which links to 3-mercaptopropanohydrazide (Hyd-SH) via acid-labile hydrazone bonds. Gold nanorods (AuNRs) are complexed with Hyd-SH—CA-Dox, and EDC/NHS mediates covalent immobilization onto EcN surfaces using amine-reactive CA linkers (Fig. 9B). This strategy leverages bacterial surface amines for tumor-targeted integration of chemotherapy and photothermal therapy [263]. Yao et al. engineered a stimuli-responsive probiotic-nanosystem by chemically conjugating MMP-2-responsive peptide (DC7: d-Asp-Pro-Leu-Gly-Val-Arg-Cys)-modified liposomes to Clostridium butyricum (CB) via EDC/NHS-mediated coupling [264]. The covalent linkage occurred between the DC7 sulfhydryl terminus and maleimide-functionalized liposomal surfaces. Tumor-overexpressed MMP-2 cleaves DC7 to release drug-loaded liposomes, enhancing local drug delivery in tumor tissues. CB effectively suppresses drug degradation by EcN, inhibits EcN viability, and potentiates drug efficacy. Compared to free drug-loaded liposomes, this system shows superior tumor targeting and colonization in pancreatic tumors.

EDC/NHS chemistry represents a facile, precise and controllable crosslinking strategy with considerable potential for the surface engineering and functionalization of probiotics in oral delivery. Bacterial cell wall composition is the most critical factor dictating EDC/NHS conjugation efficiency. Compared with Gram-negative bacteria, Gram-positive bacteria have a much thicker peptidoglycan layer on their cell wall, which can reach 30–100 nanometers or even larger, whereas the peptidoglycan layer of Gram-negative bacteria is only a few nanometers [265]. This indicates that Gram-positive bacteria possess more natural reactive sites for EDC/NHS chemical modification. Synthetic adhesins were covalently conjugated to the surfaces of live bacteria via NHS ester chemistry [181], leveraging bacterial primary amines to form stable amide bonds (Fig. 9C). The viability of each species, including the Gram-positive L. casei (LC), Bacillus coagulans (BC) and the Gram-negative EcN, remained unchanged following biotinylation. LC exhibited a higher degree of biotinylation. These differences may be attributed to variations in primary amine density, surface charge and total surface area across the three bacterial species. EDC/NHS modification has a strong reaction-condition dependent influence on probiotic survival. For example, the pH value for EDC-activated carboxylation can cause acid stress in some bacteria. Also, the conjugate-to-bacterium mass ratio plays an important role. For conjugations of L. plantarum with Nisin/Tremella fuciformis polysaccharide (NTN) nanoparticles, a mass ratio of NTN-to-LP of 2–1 was optimal. This ratio gave a high bacterial survival rate of 85.6% ± 2.11% and a satisfactory grafting efficiency of 68.8% ± 1.96% [266]. The stress resistant spore form of BS is another approach. Spores have natural survival capacity against high temperatures, UV, organic solvents and dryness, and are thus able to withstand a wide variation of EDC/NHS reaction conditions [267]. In general, Gram-negative bacteria are more suitable for conjugation. However, direct EDC/NHS-based surface modification of probiotics may compromise their biological activity. By optimizing reaction parameters, such as the conjugate-to-bacterium mass ratio, reaction time, and solvent pH, the adverse effects of chemical modification on probiotic viability can be partially mitigated.

Bioorthogonal chemistry

Bioorthogonal chemistry allows researchers to covalently attach biomolecules such as nucleic acids and carbohydrates within living organisms specifically [268]. These are not interfered with by biological molecules and reactions already present in the body. A common bioorthogonal reaction is azide-cyclooctyne click chemistry. This reaction is a cycloaddition of an azide (N3) and dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO). I This reaction is highly efficient and selective. The efficiency and biocompatibility of bioorthogonal conjugation in biological systems makes it an attractive strategy to selectively functionalize probiotics [269]. This strategy was used by Peng and coworkers to engineer EcN for the treatment of IBD via CD44-mediated immunomodulation [78]. They initially modified EcN with N3 ligands by conjugating EcN with an azido-PEG4-NHS ester. They then used copper-free click chemistry to attach DBCO-conjugated hyaluronic acid to the N3-modified EcN to form EcN—HA conjugates. They then coated them with a pH-sensitive Eudragit L100–55 polymer, via calcium-induced electrostatic attraction, to enhance their survival in the stomach, creating EcN—HA@L. This polymer is pH-sensitive and dissolves in the intestine to protect probiotics from stomach acid. EcN—HA@L efficiently grew in the gut and colonized the colon, with the highest accumulation in the inflamed colon. The modified probiotics promoted the polarization of macrophages from M1 to M2 via the sustained release of HA, exerting therapeutic effects. This dual-modification approach overcomes both the therapeutic efficacy and probiotic viability issues often encountered in oral delivery systems through targeted delivery and protective coating, respectively.

D‑amino acids (DAAs) are key building blocks of bacterial peptidoglycans (PGNs). During PGN biosynthesis, DAAs can be specifically incorporated into bacterial surfaces through their biosynthetic machinery. Based on this metabolic amino acid engineering, Song et al. incorporated azido‑modified D‑alanine (N3‑DAA) into the peptidoglycan of gut microbiota to achieve azido modification [269]. Then, the DBCO‑decorated probiotics were orally administered to mice. They undergo bioorthogonal reactions with the gut microbiota in situ to increase their retention time. Imaging analysis of bacterial colonization demonstrated that the bioorthogonal process led to a significant increase in bacterial clustering and adherence, and persisted for up to 48 h. This bioengineered interface forms covalent bonds between the probiotics and the resident microbiota, essentially gluing the therapeutic bacteria in place while maintaining their metabolism. This platform has also been used for single-cell functionalization using strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC) in a LbL approach. This involves a prebiotic “armor” that accomplishes improved functionalization. Ji et al. expressed N3 groups on L. plantarum (Lp-N3) by allowing the bacteria to assimilate N3-DAA via their endogenous biosynthetic pathways [74]. They then coated the bacteria with GN. GN is a prebiotic that is selectively fermented by gut bacteria into SCFAs to treat UC [270]. The prebiotic was modified with DBCO (dGN) and N3 (aGN), which were then assembled by copper-free click chemistry to form a prebiotic multilayer coating (Lp@CGN). Lp@CGN was pH sensitive in DSS-induced colitis models. The coating remained intact in the stomach and degraded in the colon where GN was metabolized into anti-inflammatory SCFAs by the resident microbiota. This ensured timely release of L. plantarum, for them to perform their functions without the restraint of CGN coating. Together, these studies demonstrate how metabolic engineering and bioorthogonal chemistry can be combined for microbiome therapies. They improve probiotic survival and allow site-specific delivery via chemically functionalized microbial surfaces.

Compared with EDC/NHS chemistry, bioorthogonal chemistry offers higher selectivity and better biocompatibility for modifying probiotic surfaces. It avoids interference from endogenous functional groups and does less damage to bacterial viability. However, bioorthogonal chemistry generally requires pre‑modifying the bacteria or the ligands to introduce the necessary reactive groups. This makes the process more complex and limits its broader applicability. The high cost of reagents and the overall complexity of the procedure remain major hurdles for clinical translation and large‑scale use.

Others

The surface chemistry of probiotics plays a crucial role in chemical modifications through the variety and quantity of intrinsic functional groups on probiotic surfaces. Chemical modification of a prebiotic surface via thiolation offers a flexible approach to increase the functionality of probiotics. This approach enables the researchers to tune the number of thiol groups on each bacterial cell by varying the ratio of precursors. This enables them to fine-tune interactions between bacteria and mucus without influencing the metabolic activity of the bacteria. Luo and co-workers were the first to apply this technique, which involves the transformation of primary amino groups on EcN into active thiol groups with a cytocompatible one-step imidoester reaction [212]. Critically, the thiolated EcN retained their tryptophan metabolism, demonstrating the superb biocompatibility of the approach. It also exhibited enhanced retention in the mouse jejunum. This was achieved by the spontaneous thiol-disulfide exchange with the cysteine-rich mucins, and enhanced fluorescence signals detected with In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS) clearly indicated the improved retention in the intestine. Surprisingly, thiolation is not limited to one strain, but can be applied to a wide range of species, such as Gram-negative AKK and ST, Gram-positive Enterococcus faecalis (EF), Bacillus megaterium (BMB) and BC. This kind of versatility makes thiolation a game-changing approach for the modification of microbial therapeutics, particularly to treat mucus-enriched inflammation. The modified bacteria retain their viability and tryptophan metabolism, while improving their mucosal adhesion, so they may have the potential to mitigate inflammation associated with jejunal mucositis.

Researchers engineered a multi‑layered probiotic encapsulation system called LP@PDA@GC using sequential oxidative polymerization and bioadhesive assembly [206]. They first suspended probiotics in Tris‑HCl buffer at pH 8.5 and applied a dopamine coating via 30 min of oxidative polymerization, which relied on covalent and hydrogen bonding between PDA and bacterial surface groups like amines. Then, they incorporated a gelatin‑-sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (GC) layer via Schiff base reactions with the amino groups on the PDA. This process ultimately generated a four‑layer protective architecture. Metabolic profiling confirmed biocompatibility of the coating material. Encapsulated L. plantarum maintained production of butyric acid, while exhibiting higher output post-SGF/SIF exposure, demonstrating enhanced gastric survival and sustained metabolic activity. Spatiotemporal control was achieved via the photothermal response: NIR irradiation (808 nm, 1 W/cm2, 5 min) generated localized heating that activated endogenous cellulose-degrading enzymes to digest the GC layer. This exposed the PDA subcoating, which adhered to intestinal epithelia via catechol-mediated interactions, achieving at least 2-fold greater colonic retention versus unmodified L. plantarum. Thus, the NIR-triggered phase transition enables programmable adhesion modulation, where exposure duration correlates with mucosal attachment density. In another study, Chen et al. immobilized two dietary-derived components, zinc and indole-3-methanol (I3C), onto a ZI platform. Using C18-PEG-PBA as a linker, the phenylboronic acid groups were condensed with the diol groups of sugars on the surface of EcN to conjugate ZI onto the EcN surface, while the C18 moieties were embedded into the pores of ZI, thus constructing a chemically modified EcN platform (ZI@EcN) [271]. Leveraging the colon-targeting property of EcN, zinc and I3C were selectively delivered and sustainably released, synergistically restoring the intestinal epithelial barrier and reshaping the gut microbiome in IBD. ZI@EcN exhibits promising potential for restoring the intestinal microenvironment and provides an effective therapeutic strategy for colitis.

Probiotics used for oral delivery in the treatment of IBD

Probiotics exhibit substantial therapeutic potential in the management of IBD. Different probiotic distinct functions in the gut, and their roles in the development of IBD vary considerably. Since IBD primarily affects the GIT, oral delivering engineered probiotics and their derivatives offers clear advantages including high biological safety and better patient compliance [54]. Through genetic engineering and functional modification, probiotics can gain enhanced resistance to harsh gastrointestinal conditions and improved ability to colonize target sites. More benefits include better regulation of gut microbiota balance, reinforcement of the intestinal barrier, and modulation of the immune microenvironment [248]. In this section, we focus on orally deliverable engineered probiotics for IBD treatment and summarize how they work therapeutically in disease management (Table 2).

Summary of orally deliverable engineered probiotics for IBD treatment and their therapeutic mechanisms.

EcN

EcN was first isolated in 1917 by Professor Alfred Nissle from a fecal sample of a German soldier. It has since been acknowledged as a clinical probiotic for the treatment of IBD, especially UC. EcN is a nonpathogenic, Gram-negative bacterium. It synthesizes a K5 capsular polysaccharide that enhances colonization of the gut by increasing bacterial adhesion and persistence in the gut [289]. The K5 capsule also helps regulate immunity. It stimulates TLR5 and the release of chemokines when interacting with host epithelium [290]. Flagella proteins assist EcN to adhere longer to the mucosal layer at the sites of IBD lesions [291]. These results demonstrate the adhesive and immunoregulatory capabilities of EcN, given that it is able to regulate in vivo immune factor release and boost host immunity [292].

EcN has inherent immunoregulatory activity and has been shown to be safe as a delivery vehicle. Exploiting these characteristics, EcNs have been designed to express therapeutic molecules that could enhance treatment of IBD. Li et al. engineered EcN to express IL-2 for IBD therapy. They coated the engineered strain with an enteric coating of Eudragit L100–55 to improve its efficacy, and called it EcN-IL2@L100 [272]. This formulation effectively modulated the innate immune response, including suppression of Th17 cell and neutrophil expansion. Both EcN-IL2@L100 and EcN@L100 reduced the frequencies of IL-17A+CD3+ cells and neutrophils when compared to untreated IBD mice. The regulation was greater in the EcN-IL2@L100 group. In contrast to EcN@L100, EcN-IL2@L100 reversed the loss of Foxp3⁺ Tregs in the colon. In another study, researchers developed an EcN strain engineered to secrete Sj16 (EcN-Sj16) for IBD intervention [273]. That is, EcN-Sj16 dramatically enhanced the percentage of CD3e+CD4+CD25+Foxp3+ Treg cells, whereas significantly decreasing that of CD3e+CD4+IL-17A+ Th17 cells, in comparison with unmodified EcN strain. Macrophages are key cells of the innate immune system. They can be polarized into two different classes: the pro-inflammatory M1, and the anti-inflammatory M2 [293]. Cheng et al. recently described the in situ formation of biocatalytic ferrihydrite nanoparticles (Fh NPs) on EcN by a one-step mineralization process [274]. Then they protected the nanoparticles with a coating of fucoidan. The nanoparticles were coated to enhance colon-specific delivery and the nanoconjugate was termed EcN-Fh@F. EcN-Fh@F treatment resulted in a significant decrease in the CD11c expression of macrophages with a simultaneous increase in the expression of CD206. This suggests that EcN-Fh@F induces a transition from M1 to M2 macrophages, which helps restore the immune balance in the intestine.

SCFAs are produced by the gut microbiota through the fermentation of dietary carbohydrates. They play essential roles in regulating host immunity, inflammatory responses and energy metabolism [294]. Ma et al. developed a targeted gut delivery system by engineering EcN to carry a CO/H2S-releasing copolymer (POSR) [149]. When this system is given orally and combined with ultrasound irradiation, it facilitates the localized release of CO and H2S within the inflamed intestine of IBD mice, resulting in a marked increase in SCFA production and subsequent alleviation of IBD symptoms. Elafin is an endogenous inhibitor of neutrophil elastase (NE) and proteinase 3. In the colonic mucosa of IBD patients, elafin expression is significantly downregulated, and this reduction correlates with higher disease activity indices [149]. Teng et al. engineered EcN to secrete elafin (EcN-Elafin). By delivering this protease inhibitor directly to the colonic mucosa in colitis mice, the engineered strain aims to restore elafin levels at the site of inflammation [276]. Compared with the DSS group, administration of EcN-Elafin resulted in a significant increase in luminal concentrations of butyrate and valerate. Given that gut dysbiosis-induced disruptions in tryptophan metabolism compromise intestinal barrier integrity, the restoration of tryptophan homeostasis emerges as a promising therapeutic approach for IBD. Liu et al. constructed a polynorepinephrine-coated probiotic (NE-EcN-pA) expressing α-aminoadipate aminotransferase (Aadat) [277], which continuously produces Aadat in the intestine to regulate endogenous tryptophan metabolism. Notably, NE-EcN-pA alleviated DSS-induced UC by increasing fecal SCFAs, whereas interventions employing single-component (EcN, NE-EcN or EcN-pA) did not yield significant effects. Li et al. constructed an engineered probiotic (EcN-TRP) with enhanced tryptophan biosynthetic capacity, further processing it via a two-step strategy integrating high-voltage electrospraying and liquid interface self-assembly to generate pH-responsive dual-layered EcN-TRP@A/G microcapsules [295]. Orally administered EcN-TRP@A/G induced sustained elevation of colonic tryptophan and its indole metabolites in IBD mouse models, alleviating disease symptoms by modulating tryptophan metabolism. This formulation brought about a clear increase in colonic tryptophan levels. In DSS-treated mice, it also restored the concentrations of IPA and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) in DSS-treated mice. Notably, neither unencapsulated EcN-TRP, empty EcN@A/G microcapsules, nor free tryptophan supplementation significantly modulated these metabolites. These observations highlighting the necessity of combining genetic engineering and microencapsulation to restore indole metabolism in IBD.

Gut probiotic EcN engineered for the gut promotes microbiota development and pathogen growth. Han et al. developed LbL-coated probiotics (PCLip@EcN) using curcumin-loaded liposomes coated with polymers [275]. This formulation significantly enhanced microbial diversity and richness (Shannon, Ace, Chao and inverse-Simpson indices) in mice with DSS-induced colitis. As well as increasing diversity, PCLip@EcN also increased the abundance of beneficial species and subsequently alleviated colitis. At the family and genus level, PCLip@EcN increased the percentage of Lactobacillus, a beneficial genus known to alleviate IBD symptoms in mice and humans. Ruminococcaceae, which is key to the breakdown of dietary fiber and the production of gut-protective SCFAs, also contributes to the maintenance of intestinal homeostasis and is often considered a beneficial member of the microbiota associated with numerous health benefits [296]. In the gut, EcN and coated EcN, particularly PCLip@EcN, significantly raised the level of Ruminococcaceae. In a separate study, Hu et al. built a nanostructured pBDT-TA composite from a self-polymerizable aromatic dithiol (BDT) and TA, and then applied an SA outer layer over EcN through LbL assembly to obtain the EcN@SA-pBDT-TA platform [129]. The platform preferentially boosted beneficial bacteria while suppressing pathogenic ones, helping to restore microbial homeostasis and ease IBD symptoms. Fecal sequencing revealed that DSS treatment reduced the richness of microorganisms. However, treatment with EcN@SA-pBDT-TA largely reversed this reduction and restored both the number of OTUs and the Shannon index. At the phylum and family levels, EcN@SA-pBDT-TA expanded Bacteroidota while shrinking Proteobacteria. At the genus level, the proinflammatory pathogen Escherichia-Shigella, which had bloomed in DSS-treated mice, was sharply reduced by EcN@SA-pBDT-TA, together with other proinflammatory taxa including Clostridia_UCG-014 [54]. At the same time, native commensals such as Akkermansia and Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group became more abundant [297].

Furthermore, engineered EcN has demonstrated considerable effectiveness in the restoration of intestinal mucosal barrier integrity in IBD. Yang et al. developed a colonic-targeted mucoadhesive hydrogel microsphere system (EcN+IPA@DT) designed as a multifunctional delivery platform for the co-delivering of EcN and IPA [298]. Specifically focusing on barrier restoration, treatment with EcN+IPA@DT in DSS-colitis mice resulted in the normalization of occludin-1 and ZO-1. Notably, serum levels of diamine oxidase, endotoxin and d-lactic acid, which serve as key biomarkers of intestinal permeability, were markedly reduced by EcN+IPA@DT treatment, thus offering direct evidence for effective restoration of intestinal barrier function. Kang et al. also generated a probiotic strain that produces propionate (EcNP3) to maintain high levels of propionate in mice with DSS-induced colitis [107]. Upon oral administration in mice, EcNP3 had various beneficial effects on the colon. Crypts were more structured and the extent of crypt loss was lowered. As well, goblet cells were found in higher numbers. Such therapeutic effects were greater than those achieved with the parental EcN strain. EcNP3 also enhanced the integrity of the epithelium. EcNP3, as well as unmodified EcN, enhanced the integrity of the intestinal epithelium, as indicated by the increased expression of tight junction proteins, including Occludin, ZO-1, Claudin-5 and Claudin-2.

EcN has emerged as a central living biotherapeutic product for targeted therapeutic intervention in gut diseases from being a probiotic. A multifunctional EcN system has been engineered. This system has broken the clinical barriers of traditional drugs, including poor targeting. It enables multiple-dimensional therapy such as immune microenvironment reconstruction, metabolic network regulation, restoration of gut microbiota balance and re-establishment of the intestinal mucosal barrier.

Lactobacillus

L. rhamnosus

L. rhamnosus is a probiotic Gram positive, facultatively anaerobic bacteria of the genus Lactobacillus. It can effectively alleviate the symptoms of IBD by several mechanisms, including fortifying the intestinal barrier [299], modulating the composition of the gut microbiota [300], downregulating immune-inflammatory responses [301], and reducing intestinal oxidative stress [302]. One possible reason for the therapeutic efficacy of L. rhamnosus is that it can persist in the GIT for a longer time. This is in part due to the ability of the bacterium to adhere to the intestinal mucosa [303]. Studies suggest that surface polysaccharides and fimbriae of L. rhamnosus are crucial in improving the adhesion to intestinal epithelial cells [304]. This in turn leads to increased mucosal colonization and metabolic activity. Gao et al. created metal-phenolic networks (MPNs) with three of the most abundant tea polyphenols, gallic acid (GA), epigallocatechin (EGC) and epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), bound with iron ions for IBD treatment [305]. The hydroxyl groups of the MPNs coatings improved L. rhamnosus adhesion and capacity increased with increasing size of the polyphenols. MPNs-coated probiotics demonstrated several beneficial effects in DSS-induced colitis. They restored the intestinal epithelium: crypt structure and goblet cell number returned to normal. They also had an immune-regulatory effect by decreasing pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, TNF-α and MPO and increasing anti-inflammatory IL-10. Moreover, they preserved ecological balance of the gut microbiota, as evident from increased Chao1 and Shannon indexes. Of them, EGC@L and EGCG@L were particularly effective. Their enhanced adhesion prolonged probiotic-epithelial interactions and thus enhanced their therapeutic effects for IBD.

The efficacy of bioengineered L. rhamnosus in IBD is linked to the repair of the intestinal mucosal barrier. The acid and iturin A (IA) treated Ganoderma lucidum spore shells (IH-bGLS) have been used as a carrier to improve the gastrointestinal targeting of L. rhamnosus SHA113 [278]. The carrier system enhanced L. rhamnosus attachment to the digestive tract mucosa, most notably the colon tissues. Mechanistic investigations in UC models of DSS-induced colitis demonstrated this preparation reduced gut inflammation by regulating cytokines, decreasing IL-1β and IL-6 and increasing IL-4 and IL-10. It also restored tight junction proteins such as ZO-1 and occludin, which play an important role in maintaining the epithelial barrier. In another study, a genetically engineered strain of L. rhamnosus was developed, with a multifunctional prodrug shell that contained a lipid prodrug (LPC-Bal) was designed to reverse DSS-induced UC [306]. In this strategy, Bal was conjugated to 1-palmitoyl-sn‑glycero-3-phosphocholine (LPC-Bal) and subsequently modified onto L. rhamnosussurfaces via interfacial supramolecular self-assembly to construct an “engineered probiotic”. In vivo studies show LbL exhibits enhanced intestinal colonization compared to the unmodified L. rhamnosus. LbL administration significantly lowers IL‑6, IL‑1β, TNF‑α and MPO. It also upregulates ZO‑1 and occludin while promoting goblet cell regeneration within colonic tissue. Mechanistically, the recruitment of PI3K facilitates the phosphorylated and activation of EGFR, which subsequently triggers the downstream Akt pathway [307]. Zai et al. developed a TA-casein phosphopeptide (CPP) nanocomposite probiotic coating utilizing LbL [239]. This coating significantly enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of L. rhamnosusby activating the EGFR/AKT signaling pathway, as evidenced by elevated levels of phosphorylated EGFR and AKT. This mechanism protected the epithelial barrier function of colonic epithelium in a DSS-induced colitis mouse model. Another study reported the development of acid-resistant, colon-targeted double-layer microgels (LGG Gels) were developed to encapsulate L. rhamnosus [308]. In DSS-induced UC models, LGG Gels significantly alleviated the symptoms in mice and modulated the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, a key parameter positively correlated with anti-inflammatory effects. This intervention increased probiotic accumulation at colonic lesions while reducing pathogenic bacteria such as Verrucomicrobia. Additionally, LGG Gels elevated Chao1, Shannon and Simpson indices, indicative of improved gut microbiota species richness and diversity.

L. plantarum

L. plantarum is a Gram-positive anaerobe or facultative anaerobe belonging to the Lactobacillus genus, one of the most well-characterized probiotic lineages [135]. L. plantarum has a number of unique biochemical characteristics. This strain is acid-tolerant and non-sporogenic. It has a non-respiratory metabolism with a heterofermentative pathway [309]. L. plantarum relevance extends beyond the food industry, and it also holds considerable promise in biomedicine. It can treat a variety of diseases with relatively few side effects, and this makes it a safe therapeutic agent for use in humans [310]. Specifically, L. plantarum has been used to treat heart diseases such as diabetes, obesity and hypertension, and as an adjunct therapy in cancer treatment [311].

The extensive therapeutic potential and favorable safety profile of L. plantarum have drawn growing research interest in its application to IBD therapy. Encapsulation strategies are being explored as a means to enhance its delivery and therapeutic efficacy. Wang et al. developed a single-strain L. plantarum encapsulation strategy employing a dual-layer system comprising a TA-calcium network and a polysaccharide coating (gellan gum-tamarind gum), designated as L.P-C/T-G/T [280]. This formulation markedly alleviated DSS-induced colitis symptoms. Mechanistic investigations showed that L.P-C/T-G/T reduced inflammation by lowering IL-1β, TNF-α and iNOS levels while upregulating IL-10 expression. The treatment also enhanced SOD and CAT activities and significantly decreased malondialdehyde (MDA) and MPO levels, a sign of reduced oxidative stress. Free L. plantarum administration did not produce these improvements. Thus, this dual-layer encapsulation strategy holds potential for targeted probiotic delivery in IBD intervention. Building on the concept of encapsulation for improved IBD treatment, recent investigations have explored combining L. plantarum with bioactive compounds. Ji et al. devised a single-cell LbL strategy to coat GN onto L. plantarum (Lp@CGN) [74]. Lp@CGN markedly reduced proinflammatory cytokine levels (TNF-α and IL-6), and inhibited MPO activity, while increasing anti-inflammatory marker expression (TGF-β and IL-10). GN is selectively metabolized by colonic microbiota into SCFAs to mitigate UC [270]. Oral administration of Lp@CGN restored acetic, propionic, butyric, valeric and caproic acid levels to normal in DSS-induced UC mice. In another study, L. plantarum p - 8 was encapsulated using gelatin and gum Arabic [281]. Probiotic microcapsules attenuated DSS-induced colonic mucosal damage by improving histopathological features, upregulating MUC-2, ZO-1 and occludin, and regulating inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-2). Mechanistically, they inhibited NLRP3 inflammasome activation, evidenced by reduced protein levels of NLRP3, ASC and caspase-1 in colonic tissues.

Others

Lactobacillus encapsulation strategies have been extensively explored to enhance the survival, delivery and functional efficacy of probiotics within the GIT. For instance, L. acidophilus was encapsulated in W1/O/W2 double emulsions [286], composed of polyglycerol polyricinoleate (PGPR), soy protein isolate (SPI), SA and fish oil aimed at treating DSS-induced UC. The emulsions co-loaded with fish oil and probiotics demonstrated a significant reduction in inflammation by upregulating the expression of tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Occludin and Claudin-1), suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-1β) and promoting SCFAs production (acetic, propionic, isobutyric, butyric and valeric acids). These emulsions raised the Shannon and Chao diversity indices as well. They modulated the gut microbiota by promoting beneficial bacteria and suppressing pathogens, which helped restore microbial balance. Beyond conventional encapsulation, co-encapsulation has been developed to pair Lactobacillus with synergistically acting compounds. In another study, LbL coacervation of Fu brick tea polysaccharides (FBTP) and CS-whey protein isolate was used to co-encapsulate GA with Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens JKSP109 (LK). The FBTP and GA created a nutrient-rich environment for probiotics. This environment enhanced the production of beneficial metabolites, increased the bioavailability of the GA, and these effects combined to exert anti-inflammatory effects [312]. This strategy ameliorated acute colitis in mice induced by DSS by decreasing the levels of IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β and increasing IL-10 expression in the colon. It also decreased severe tissue damage with a reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, restored crypt architecture and maintained the integrity of the colonic mucosa. Yao et al. designed a LbL delivery system to encapsulate single Li01 cells with CS and alginate (LbL-Li01) [287]. LbL Li01 successfully reduced colonic inflammation in the DSS-induced acute colitis mice model, with lower levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α) and higher levels of anti-inflammatory IL-10 in the plasma. LbL-Li01 treatment resulted in increased Muribaculaceae compared to Li01 treatment, suggesting LbL delivery increases the therapeutic potential of Li01via the gut microbiota. Therefore, this system augments function of Li01 and enhances its ability to alleviate DSS-induced colitis in mice.

Another strategy involves using bacterial polymers to promote biofilm formation. One such polymer is poly-β-hydroxybutyrate (PHB), which is composed of β-hydroxybutyrate (β-HB). PHB is an important energy storage compound for bacteria. It is synthesised when carbon is plentiful and nitrogen or phosphate is scarce. In these circumstances, PHB enables bacteria to conserve energy during times of nutrient scarcity and stress [313]. Ma et al. found that pre-incubation with PHB enhanced biofilm formation of L. johnsonii on PHB surfaces (PLB) [288]. The biofilm-coated L. johnsonii maintained sustained viability up to 3 h. In contrast, cells not treated with PHB showed membrane damage within 1 h. The enhanced survival is due to protection from BA penetration of the bacterial cells and maintained cell integrity. Moreover, PLB also improved the symptoms of colitis, with lymphocyte infiltration and mucosal damage significantly reduced. PLB also induced differentiation of goblet cells and increased the sulfomucin expression. Animals administered PLB showed significantly reduced levels of the pro inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α in their colon.

As the core beneficial flora in the human intestinal microecosystem, Lactobacillus, relies on its natural advantages in intestinal colonization, diverse probiotic functions, and excellent biosafety. It has thereby evolved from a traditional food-grade probiotic into a core living biotherapeutic carrier for precise IBD intervention. With engineering modifications centered on advanced strategies such as LbL assembly, targeted encapsulation and surface functional modification, Lactobacillus has achieved a leapfrog upgrade from a passive probiotic effect to active regulation. This approach not only enhances the survival efficiency and colonization stability of Lactobacillus in the inflammatory microenvironment of IBD but also enables integrated symptom intervention through multi-mechanism synergy. These outcomes highlight the precise therapeutic potential of engineered probiotics.

Bifidobacterium Bifidum

Bifidobacterium Bifidum (B. bifidum) is a Gram-positive, anaerobic probiotic actinobacterium that predominantly colonizes the infant gut as well as the uterine environment during pregnancy [314]. A range of evidence indicates that it alleviates IBD symptoms through multiple mechanisms. It attenuates colonic inflammation, preserves intestinal barrier integrity, modulates the gut microbiota, mitigates oxidative stress and regulates metabolic pathways [315]. The therapeutic potential of natural B. bifidum strains may be limited, which has driven the creation of genetically engineered variants. One such variant, BGN4-SK, was modified to secrete SOD and CAT, while another, BGN4-pBESIL10, was engineered to produce IL-10 [125]. In LPS-stimulated HT-29 cells, both engineered strains significantly reduced the levels of TNF-α and IL-8. When cultures were treated with BGN4-SK or BGN4-pBESIL10, the enzymatic activities of SOD, glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) and CAT were higher than in cultures treated with DSS alone. Treatment with BGN4-SK also increased the mRNA expression of ZO-1 and claudin compared to the DSS-treated groups. In contrast, unmodified B. bifidum did not show any significant therapeutic effect against IBD in the DSS-treated model. Beyond genetic engineering, surface modification has also been pursued to enhance B. bifidum function. For instance, the strong adhesion offered by PDA coatings has been harnessed through a cell-mediated biointerfacial phenolic assembly to encapsulate the bacterium [279]. CeO2 NPs, known for their efficient ROS-scavenging activity and positive surface charge, were adsorbed onto the PDA layers (B.B.@PDA@CeOx) through electrostatic interactions. This modification endowed the probiotics with significantly improved ROS-scavenging capacity. In a DSS-induced IBD model, treatment with B.B.@PDA@CeO2 proved highly effective. It reversed crypt loss, restored goblet cell populations, and reduced immune cell infiltration within colonic tissues. The treatment also lowered MPO activity and decreased the levels of pro-inflammatory factors including IL-6, CRP and TNF-α. Beyond these direct effects, B.B.@PDA@CeO2 reshaped the gut microbiota. It increased the relative abundance of Muribaculaceae and Prevotellaceae_UCG-001, while suppressing Desulfovibrionaceae.

Research on oral delivery of B. bifidum is scarce, which is associated with intrinsic factors including the strict physiological requirements of B. bifidum strains and their inherently weak viability upon oral administration. As an anaerobe, B. bifidum is extremely sensitive to oxygen and prone to inactivation during in vitro cultivation, storage and gastrointestinal transit [316]. Furthermore, it is less resistant to acid and bile salt than model strains such as lactic acid bacteria and EcN. The future development of oral delivery of B. bifidum can adopt dual strategy of strain engineering and external protection. At the strain modification level, genetic engineering can be applied to improve the tolerance to external environment. At the protection level, microencapsulation combined with biodegradable and pH-responsive materials can be used to develop an encapsulation system with anaerobic protection, acid resistance and intestinal targeting. This system can increase the survival of B. bifidum when orally administered.

AKK

AKK is a Gram-negative, anaerobic bacterium capable of degrading MUC and colonizing the intestinal mucosa of humans and rodents [317]. Upon MUC degradation, AKK produces SCFAs, specifically acetate and propionate, which serve as metabolic substrates for other gut microbes and the host organism [318]. These SCFAs are linked to the regulation of body weight gain, exerting anorexigenic, anti-inflammatory and metabolic effects [319]. Several studies have pointed to the therapeutic potential of the outer membrane components of AKK for managing intestinal inflammation in IBD [320]. Nonetheless, to fully harness the full therapeutic benefits of AKK within the harsh gastrointestinal environment and to effectively target inflamed regions, advanced delivery systems are needed. Zhang et al. developed a mucoadhesive probiotic nanotherapy (AM@HMPB@E) that combines Akkermansia-mimicking bacteria with entinostat-loaded hollow mesoporous Prussian blue (HMPB) for targeted treatment of UC [282]. This system helps repairs the intestinal barrier and eliminates reactive oxygen/nitrogen species (RONS). Treatment with AM@HMPB@E corrected many typical signs of colitis, such as epithelial cell damage, crypt abnormalities, goblet cell loss, inflammatory cell infiltration and lymphocyte enlargement within the mucosal and submucosal layers. The system also preserves colonic mucosal barrier integrity. This effect is achieved through modulation of the Acetyl-Histone H3/VDR signaling pathway.

In another study, Zhang et al. constructed a metal-phenol matrix network on AKK surfaces via noncovalent coordination of natural proanthocyanidins (PAs) with Ca2+ [283]. Onto this network, they sequentially coated two components naturally found in intestinal mucus, namely MUC and phosphatidylcholine (PC). The result was a self-sufficient probiotic composite which they named AKK@PAs-MUC-PC, or AKK@PMP for short. This composite has both anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. To offer additional protection and to achieve targeted release in the intestine, the researchers then encapsulated AKK@PMP within gelatin porous microgels, producing AKK@GPMGs. These microgels exhibited a pH-responsive pore-sealing behavior. Under highly acidic conditions, the surface pores closed up. Once in the intestine, the microgels broke open through enzyme-triggered cracking, releasing their contents. When compared with free AKK, AKK@GPMGs reduced colonic IL-6 levels by 3.77 times and TNF-α levels by 5.09 times. AKK@GPMGs also supported intestinal barrier repair by increasing the expression of ZO-1 and occludin. Microbiome analysis revealed several changes, including increased α-diversity as measured by the Shannon and Simpson indices. The treatment replenished beneficial bacterial families such as Lactobacillaceae, Muribaculaceae and Akkermansiaceae, while reducing detrimental taxa including Enterobacteriaceae, Desulfovibrionaceae and Porphyromonadaceae. This material-based approach opens up possibilities for personalized probiotic therapies and precision medicine, supporting the development of bioactive agents that harness the unique properties of probiotics.

AKK is a core candidate strain for probiotic therapy of IBD. Novel oral delivery systems have now solved the problems of bacterial survival and targeting, which previously limited its therapeutic value. With continued optimization, this strategy could become a new and efficient treatment for IBD, and it shows considerable promise for clinical translation.

Bacillus subtilis

Bacillus species have drawn increasing interest as promising probiotics, with Bacillus subtilis (BS) standing out for its ability to maintain gut microbiota balance in the GIT [321]. BS is a non-pathogenic and spore-forming bacterium that can withstand extreme pH and oxygen-deprived environments. This enables dormant but not dead cells to reach the lower intestine and release active compounds with beneficial effects [322]. BS exerts therapeutic effects on various IBD models, such as LPS-induced acute intestinal damage [323], 2,4,6-Trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid-induced colitis [324] and azoxymethane or DSS-induced colitis [325]. The effects are based on the reduction of IBD symptoms in multiple ways. BS controls inflammatory and antioxidant molecules, immune responses, and restores the intestinal barrier and gut flora. A specific strain, BS RZ001, restores the intestinal barrier in DSS-induced IBD mice through upregulation of MUC2. This is achieved by blocking the Notch signaling pathway and increasing the expression of Atoh1 [326].

Building on the inherent advantages of BS, novel delivery platforms are under construction to boost its therapeutic potential. Yang et al. developed cyanobacterial-probiotic symbionts (ASp@BS) by one-step biomimetic mineralization of polydiallyldimethylammonium chloride (PDADMAC) for the treatment of IBD [285]. This symbiont system involves BS using up local oxygen and forming an anaerobic environment. As a consequence, [NiFe] hydrogenase in cyanobacteria is activated. This stimulates H2 generation and its subsequent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Thus, ASp@BS effectively treats DSS colitis. It decreases the concentration of pro inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IFN-γ and TNF-α) in the colon and increases anti-inflammatory cytokines (TGF-β and IL-10). 16S rRNA gene sequencing confirms role of ASp@BS in restoring gut health. AS, BS as well as ASp@BS treatment enriches beneficial bacteria, including AKK and Bifidobacterium. It also decreases the abundance of pro-inflammatory families such as Enterobacteriaceae and Romboutsia ilealis, which are found in inflamed intestines and drive IBD disease [327].

In another study, Li et al. constructed a colon-targeted drug delivery system by co-encapsulating BS and rhein (Rh) in yeast cell wall microparticles (YPs) via electrostatic self-deposition and solvent hydration/lyophilization [284], termed Rh-YBS. The YPs are designed to target M cells because of their stability in the stomach and their content of GN, providing nutrients for BS proliferation and colonization. Rh regulates colonic microbiota by suppressing pathogenic bacteria, facilitating BS colonization. Consistently, Rh-YPs, YBS and Rh-YBS reduced colonic IL-6, TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-1β and MPO levels in the colon, suppressed pathogenic bacteria, encouraged beneficial probiotics and restored SCFAs reduced by DSS. Notably, Rh-YBS treatment uniquely promoted mucosal repair through a distinct neuroimmune mechanism. To confirm the delivery mechanism, laminarin (Lam), an M cell blocker, significantly reduced CGRP upregulation in the Lam + Rh-YBS group, indicating that M cell-mediated submucosal delivery underlies Rh-YBS bioactivity. Mechanistically, CGRP release stimulates goblet cells to secrete mucus and restore the expression of Claudin-1, Occludin and ZO-1, thereby promoting mucosal repair.

Challenges in oral delivery system for probiotics in IBD

As a microbial community possessing distinct physiological functions and intricate microbiological traits, probiotics show great promise for applications in intestinal health. Whether directly incorporated as active ingredients for regulating gut microbiota in functional foods or developed as targeted therapeutics for intestinal disorders, probiotics hold significant promise [328]. However, due to their microbial nature and complex interactions within the gut environment, probiotics are highly susceptible to environmental factors in vitro or prone to interact with various gut microbes, immune cells and bioactive compounds produced by the host in vivo [329]. The three factors interact to dynamically control the efficiency of mass transport, the balance of microenvironments and the timing of cell activation in microcapsules, which together regulate the survival, proliferation and function of cells in the oral delivery systems. Therefore, in the development of probiotic products for gut health, it is critical to thoroughly investigate the interaction of probiotic formulations and external and internal environments and to conduct in-depth studies. This review offers a detailed overview and analysis of the challenges in the use of probiotics for gut health and propose several strategies for the beneficial use of probiotics.

Surface modifications affect the biological activity of probiotics

Surface modifications of probiotics can be used to improve their viability and function in the GIT. While different encapsulation and modification strategies can enhance gastrointestinal tolerance, over protection may compromise cell proliferation or functional expression [200]. Embedding layer thickness, degradation and release threshold are critical parameters for balancing the gastrointestinal tolerance and biological activity. An embedding layer with intermediate thickness can establish a physical and chemical protective barrier to improve the gastrointestinal tolerance of probiotics [202]. But a thick embedding layer significantly retards the transport of nutrients, oxygen and wastes, resulting in a hypoxic, acidic and nutrient poor microenvironment that inhibits probiotic growth and function [204]. A suitable degradation time, similar to the gastrointestinal transit time, guarantees structural stability of the carrier during the delivery process and timely degradation after reaching the target intestinal site. A suitable threshold allows the carrier to responsively collapse in response to the signals from the target microenvironment (such as pH, enzymes and redox potential), which leads to targeted and on demand release [204]. On the other hand, a too high threshold will maintain the probiotics in a constrained state for a longer time, and elevate the barrier for their metabolic and signal transduction processes. And it may affect the responsiveness of engineered environment responsive promoters to intestinal microenvironmental signals (such as ROS and TNF-α) [330]. Moreover, the ability of probiotics to colonize the intestine is a key determinant to exert beneficial effects. Probiotics need to firmly adhere to the intestinal epithelium and establish biofilms for effective interaction with intestinal microbiota, pathogen exclusion and immune regulation [331]. Surface modification might change the surface charge, hydrophobicity and mucus affinity of probiotics, thus affecting the ability to adhere and colonize in the intestinal tract, which may be quickly cleared away by peristalsis [332]. As such, the development of porous and loose coating structures, rapidly responsive coating materials and synergistic regulation systems that pair coating matrices with responsive promoters to synergistically regulate probiotic activities are critical scientific questions that need to be addressed in future studies.

In order to determine the potential impact of surface modification on the probiotic activity of probiotics, it is critical to develop strategies for evaluating their viability. This includes comprehensive physicochemical characterization of probiotics before and after modification, including cell sizes and shapes, cell wall composition and enzyme activities. Next, in vitro experiments should mimic the physiological environment in the intestines by considering factors such as pH, temperature and composition of digestion fluid to evaluate the colonization and growth of probiotics in a simulated gut environment [333]. Finally, animal studies should be conducted to assess the survival and colonization rates and effect on gut microbiota and host immunity of the modified probiotics [334]. Further, mild processing parameters can be designed to reduce the viability loss of probiotics caused by the modification procedure. Furthermore, antioxidant, anti-freezing and anti-dehydration functions should be incorporated into the modification system. Specifically, the addition of EGCG, mannitol and peptides with cryoprotective properties into the coating layer can substantially enhance the oxygen tolerance and storage stability of anaerobic probiotics, thereby alleviating protein denaturation during freeze-drying and freeze-thaw cycles [335]. For chemical modification, non-covalent interactions are preferred. Alternatively, mild surface covalent modifications like EDC/NHS can be adopted to minimize the interference with the cell membrane structure and metabolic activity of probiotics. By continuously experimenting and improving, surface modification techniques can be developed that simultaneously enhance intestinal survivability while preserving probiotic bioactivity, offering more dependable options for using probiotics in managing intestinal health.

Deficiencies in formulating intestinal environment resistance strategies

When probiotics enter the human intestinal tract, they come into contact with different digestive fluids that greatly affect their viability and effectiveness [336]. Considerable progress has been made in improving probiotic resistance to stomach acid. However, strategies to cope with other digestive fluids, particularly BAs and intestinal juice, remain relatively underdeveloped [337]. The digestive enzymes in your intestines break down bacterial cell walls. This is especially true for Gram-positive probiotics, since their cell walls are rich in peptidoglycan and easily get damaged by these enzymes, which harms their structure and lowers their activity.

To overcome this challenge, it is crucial to assess changes to probiotics that could enhance their resistance to intestinal digestive fluids [338]. The first attempts should be made using in vitro models mimicking the small intestine environment with synthetic BA solutions and intestinal fluid mimics [339]. This would allow controlled evaluation of probiotic survival, membrane integrity indices and metabolic changes. Parallelly, mechanistic research should use molecular biology tools, such as gene expression analysis and proteomics, to investigate the impact of BAs and enzymes on cell structure and function [340]. Based on this knowledge, specific resistance mechanisms should be designed. Genomic engineering could alter probiotic membranes to increase resistance to BAs and enzymatic degradation [341]. Alternatively, new protective materials can be developed to act as protective shields around probiotics, reducing their exposure to adverse fluids. The latter approach could harness microbial interactions, where probiotics are co-cultured with protective strains that are inherently resistant to intestinal fluids and rely on species interaction to boost their viability and survival [342]. These thorough assessments and targeted strategies can increase the modified probiotics' survival in the intricate gut environment, allowing them to perform at their best to improve human gut health.

Lack of strategies for active colonization

In order to exert a beneficial effect, probiotics need to successfully colonize the GIT to ensure adequate and continuous interaction with the host. The process of colonization mainly involves two stages. The first is the initial adhesion phase, in which probiotic bacteria adhere to host intestinal epithelial cells via surface proteins. The second is the colonization phase, when colonized probiotics create a “biological barrier” that inhibits the growth of pathogenic microorganisms and maintains the integrity of the intestinal mucosal layer. Effective colonization not only reduces the frequency of administration, but also enhances the probiotics' capacity to modulate the gut environment, which is important for immune development and gut barrier creation. Previous studies have used encapsulation strategies to enhance the colonization potential of probiotics through the improvement of their gastric survival. These mostly depend on the use of functional surface coatings, such as biofilm- and mussel-inspired MPNs [312], which enhance adhesion in the gut. Catechol-containing coating materials, such as polydopamine, can interact with the mucus layer and mucosal surface of the intestine, thus greatly improving the adhesion and retention of carriers or probiotics in the colon [343]. Their applications are limited by non-specific colonization. Improving active colonization is important for targeted delivery and retention at specific areas of the intestine or at lesion sites. Immobilization of synthetic adhesins on probiotics can enhance the colonization on abiotic surfaces, monolayers of mammalian cells and mouse GI tract [181]. Exploring disease-specific characteristics of IBD, such as up-regulation of certain inflammatory cytokines, probiotics can be modified with antibody or aptamers for selective colonization at the sites of inflammation [17]. For example, chemotactic micromotors as probiotic microcapsules are of great interest. The micromotors are designed to drive probiotics towards the inflamed region for targeted release, allowing for site-specific delivery and colonization [233].

Unclear therapeutic mechanisms of probiotics in IBD

In biomedical research systems, in-depth elucidation and empirical validation of drug action pathways have emerged as a critical step in driving the development of innovative therapeutics. Whether in the preclinical stage for functional validation of potential targets, or in post-approval risk identification and intervention based on real-world data, mechanistic exploration consistently serves as the core bridge connecting basic research with clinical practice. Currently, although probiotic-based preparations have been widely used as functional foods or adjunctive interventions in health maintenance, research into their mechanisms of action largely remains at the phenotypic level, such as remodeling of gut microbiota structure, modulation of metabolites like SCFAs, moderate activation of mucosal immune responses, and suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokine expression levels [344]. In contrast, there remains a lack of systematic integration regarding the deeper molecular logic underlying probiotic-host interactions, including the recognition specificity of specific strains toward intestinal epithelial cell surface receptors such as TLRs and NOD-like receptors, the trans-epithelial transport kinetics of key metabolites (e.g., tryptophan derivatives and secondary BAs), and the multilevel, multidirectional signal integration mechanisms within the microbiota-gut-central neural network. Notably, IBD is not only characterized by persistent immune activation in the gut lamina propria, epithelial barrier dysfunction and tissue damage, but is also frequently accompanied by neuropsychiatric symptoms such as anxiety and depression, substantiating the existence of bidirectional regulation along the gut-brain axis [345]. Additionally, IBD is frequently accompanied by hepatic metabolic abnormalities, indicating its nature as a multisystemic disorder [346]. That means, there is an urgent need to move beyond traditional single-organ, single-pathway research paradigms and shift focus toward probiotic-driven inter-organ crosstalk mechanisms. We advocate for the synergistic incorporation of the gut-liver axis and the gut-brain axis as core research dimensions, integrating metagenomics, host metabolomics and spatial transcriptomics to establish an integrated analytical framework of “microbiota-metabolite-host response,” thereby systematically decoding the intervention of probiotics in IBD. This strategy holds promise for expanding therapeutic avenues for IBD, reducing disease-associated comorbidity burdens, and providing new insights for the rational design of microbiota-based therapies.

Challenges in the synergistic regulation of structural integrity and permeability of physical barriers

The intestinal physical barrier consists of intestinal epithelial cells and the overlying highly viscous mucus layer, which serves as a critical line of defense preventing microbial contact with the epithelium [347]. Studies have demonstrated that the presence of mucus reduces the translocation of probiotics across the epithelial barrier by 7-fold compared with its absence [348]. Under the pathological condition of IBD, downregulation of MUC2 expression leads to thinning or even depletion of the mucus layer, resulting in persistent infiltration of luminal antigens and subsequent chronic inflammation. This suggests that, relative to healthy intestines, probiotics may more readily penetrate the compromised barrier and achieve effective colonization to facilitate repair. But as the barrier is repaired, penetration and colonization abilities of probiotics are diminished. Therefore, the maintenance of the barrier integrity along with sufficient probiotic permeability represents a major challenge in probiotic surface engineering. The simplest strategy is to confer probiotics with an active mucus-penetrating ability. For example, conjugation with 2 kDa PEG makes the bacterial surface more hydrophilic and neutrally charged, thus increasing motility and mucus penetration [349]. In animal models of IBD, PEGylated bacteria also increase IgA levels and prevent pathogen invasion, as well as mitigate the effects of inflammation. Physical penetration alone may not be enough to colonize and sustain the site, especially in the lower GIT due to the strong peristalsis and luminal flow. So, improving chemical adhesion to the mucus layer is preferred. Thiolation on the bacterial surface has been used to allow chemical anchoring of bacteria to the mucus layer. These bacteria exhibit thiol groups on their surface, and are capable of naturally attaching to the mucus layer through thiol-disulfide exchange reactions. This forms robust covalent bonds, thus locking them to the GIT wall and enhancing adhesion by 170-fold [212]. This suggests that recovery of the mucus layer in IBD would enhance the attachment of surface-thiolated bacteria. Motility has been identified as a critical factor for bacteria to cross the mucus barrier. Flagella serve as both motility organelles and adhesins, facilitating bacterial interaction with and colonization of the mucus layer [350]. In the presence of mucus, motile pathogenic Salmonella efficiently penetrates the mucus and epithelial layers, whereas a flagellin-knockout non-motile mutant exhibits markedly reduced penetration capacity. These findings suggest that selecting inherently motile probiotic strains or engineering motility into probiotics may offer a potential strategy to overcome the reduced colonization following barrier restoration.

Challenges in the clinical translation of engineered probiotics as live biotherapeutic agents

As an emerging class of live biotherapeutic interventions, engineered probiotics still face several barriers before routine clinical use. Strain selection is fundamental because candidate chassis differ in safety profiles, genetic tractability and endogenous biological features [351]. A suitable strain should have a clear record of safe use while retaining sufficient fitness in the acidic, bile-rich and otherwise stressful gastrointestinal environment. Colonization and host-microbe interactions add another layer of uncertainty. Inter-individual differences in genetic background, diet and baseline microbiota can influence both the engraftment of engineered strains and their therapeutic output, which makes efficacy observed in animal models difficult to extrapolate to humans in a reproducible way [352]. Manufacturing and formulation also remain limiting steps. Unlike conventional small-molecule drugs, live biotherapeutic products require stringent process control: batch-to-batch consistency during large-scale fermentation, preservation of viability during lyophilization, and adequate survival throughout the product shelf life are all practical bottlenecks for industrial development [353]. Biosafety must likewise be considered, especially the possibility of horizontal gene transfer. Once administered, engineered probiotics enter a complex intestinal ecosystem in which introduced genetic elements could, in principle, be transferred to resident commensals or opportunistic pathogens [354]. Finally, regulatory uncertainty continues to slow clinical translation. Although the U.S. FDA defines live biotherapeutic products as biological products containing live organisms that are intended to prevent or treat disease, assessment criteria and regulatory pathways for these emerging modalities are still evolving [355]. Continued progress in synthetic biology, safer biocontainment designs and a more coherent regulatory framework will be needed to move engineered probiotics from experimental platforms toward precise, clinically deployable therapeutics.

Conclusion and perspective

IBD is a relapsing inflammatory disease of the GIT which has a lifelong propensity for recurrence [356]. Pharmacotherapy and nutritional support are the conventional approaches for IBD treatment, which not only alleviate symptoms, but also progressively repair the damaged intestinal epithelium and tissues [357]. But these treatments may not be specific and may have side effects. Further, they do not address the problem of dysbiosis. Probiotics have emerged as an alternative treatment for IBD due to their superior safety, targeted modulation of the gut microbiota, strong anti-inflammatory and barrier repair properties [358]. This systematically evaluates the therapeutic benefits of probiotics for intestinal disorders and elucidated their mechanisms of action in IBD management [359]. Additionally, we evaluated the applications of probiotic delivery systems in IBD therapy and reviewed design methodologies for innovative probiotic delivery platforms. Physical encapsulation is currently the most industrially promising strategy for oral probiotic delivery. Benefiting from its mild preparation process, readily available raw materials and strong universality, this strategy exhibits high regulatory compatibility and controllable large-scale production costs. In contrast, chemical modification achieves a favorable balance between targeting efficiency and operational mildness, thus emerging as a crucial development direction for future applications. However, linker toxicity remains its major limitation [360]. Bioengineering exhibits superior targeting ability and functional specificity. It can endow probiotics with high stress tolerance, precise targeted colonization and the ability to secrete functional factors. However, this strategy is restricted by technical complexity. Therefore, bioengineering is currently more suitable for cutting-edge basic research and difficult to achieve industrialization in the short term.

While recent research has shown that probiotics have a therapeutic effect on IBD, the development of their precision therapy is limited. A key step to surmount these challenges is to create new probiotic strains that can survive under extreme conditions [361]. In addition, probiotic effectiveness is greatly affected by the high inter-individual variability of the gut microbiota and gut environment, affected by diet and physiology. This type of diversity highlights the potential for personalized probiotic therapy. Probiotics could thus be tailored to individual characteristics such as disease-specific biomarkers. But we still have limited knowledge of probiotic-host interactions. We also need more detailed studies of the therapeutic effects of engineered strains on IBD. For the delivery aspect, conventional single material-based systems inherently have limited functional capacity and performance issues, including poor physiological stability, poor encapsulation and precise gastrointestinal targeting. So, probiotic encapsulation must incorporate additional materials to increase functionality. Such systems need to protect probiotics from premature release and to enable site-specific release in the colon. However, over-protecting probiotics could prevent their proliferation while protecting the probiotics from the environment. As a result, this has driven the development of new biomaterials, including microbial spores, probiotic metabolites [186]. In addition to the materials used for encapsulation, the processes have also evolved. The use of 3D printing and microfluidics is a new trend with benefits in processing mildness, speed and cost-efficiency [362]. With solutions to these issues, probiotic delivery systems are expected to be clinically applied for IBD patients in future decades, improving human health and well-being.
