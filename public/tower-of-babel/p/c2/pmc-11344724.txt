Savolitinib conferred sensitivity in a patient with D1228H mutation-induced capmatinib-resistant MET exon 14 skipping mutated lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Xiuzhen Li, Yuefei Lu, Jie Zhao, Yinghui Yu, Heshen Tian, Hao Zhu, Wen Li, Yang Xia, Laijuan Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Traditionally, the D1228 E/G/H/N mutation has been thought to cause Type I MET-TKI resistance. We reported a 75-year-old female with non-small cell lung cancer harboring MET exon 14 skipping mutation, who developed acquired MET D1228H mutation induced by capmatinib treatment. Interestingly, the patient demonstrated marked response to second-line savolitinib treatment with the duration of response of 19 months and several additional metastatic lesions appeared. Pathological assessment of rebiopsy sample showed adenocarcinoma and targeted next-generation sequencing revealed the loss of MET D1228H mutation and presence of MET p.Y1230N mutation. In response, the treatment regimen was amended to include a daily administration of 60 mg of cabozantinib, which resulted in moderate size reduction of the tumours. The switch of resistance mutations indicated that different type Ib MET inhibitors may exhibit distinct mechanisms of resistance. We call for futher studies on resistance based on patient-derived pre-clinical models including patient-derived tumor-like cell clusters, patient-derived organoids, and patient-derived xenografts.

Introduction

Met mutation is one of the most significant driver genes in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), contributing to primary pathogenesis and acquired resistance for tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) (Bubendorf et al. 2017). Currently, targeted therapies for MET mutations become a hot topic, and MET-TKIs have achieved promising responses in clinical work. Based on binding mechanisms, MET-TKIs are divided into three classes (typeI, typeII and type III)(Han et al. 2024). Among them, capmatinib and savolitinib, both type Ib MET-TKIs, have reported objective response rates (ORR) of 41–68% and 42.9–49.2%, respectively in the backline (Wolf et al. 2020; Lu et al. 2021). Interestingly, we reported a case where savolitinib demonstrated sensitivity in a 75-year-old female with non-small cell lung cancer who acquired MET D1228H mutation induced by capmatinib treatment for a MET exon 14 skipping mutation.

Case presentation

A 75-year-old female never-smoker with the chief complaint of a productive cough was hospitalized for pulmonary lesions and pleural effusion. Enhanced computed tomography (CT) detected a lesion in the lingular segment of the left lung as well as a suspicious metastasis at mediastinal lymph node station 4 L, pleural effusion, and pericardial effusion. Integrated analysis of the clinical and percutaneous lung biopsy pathological results indicated a stage IVA (cT1cN2M1), poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma. Mutation profiling using next-generation sequencing for 425 cancer-related genes revealed an MET exon 14c.2888-37_2890-del14 skipping mutation in both the patient’s tissue and plasma, as described previously (Wang et al. 2020). The patient was prescribed a daily dose of 200 mg of capmatinib, showed dramatic regression of both the primary nodule and mediastinal lymph nodes at the 3-month response evaluation. However, the primary lesion progressed after 12 months of treatment with capmatinib. A re-biopsy showed an adenocarcinoma and DNA based next generation sequencing identified the sustained MET exon 14c.2888-37_2890-del14 skipping mutation. Notably, a MET exon 19 p.D1228H c.3682G > C missense mutation was also detected, known as a resistant mutation site. Since type II MET inhibitors were not available in China at the moment and the patient refused chemotherapy, second-line treatment with 200 mg of savolitinib daily was selected. Surprisingly, positron emission tomography (PET)-CT showed that the tumor had shrunk dramatically after only 1 month of treatment and the response was sustained at the 3-month and 6-month evaluations. The duration of response sustained for 19 months, but several additional metastatic lesions appeared in the patient’s right lung. Pathological assessment of rebiopsy sample showed adenocarcinoma and targeted next-generation sequencing found absence of D1228H mutation and presence of MET exon 19 p.Y1230N c.3688T > A missense mutation. The therapeutic strategy was adjusted to a daily dose of 60 mg cabozantinib, which elicited moderate size reduction of tumours. Alterations in the patient’s disease status are depicted in in Fig. 1. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first report to indicating that savolitinib was effective in treating an acquired MET D1228H mutation induced by capmatinib treatment of a MET exon 14 skipping mutation. Generally, MET- TKIs are divided into three classes according to their regions of interaction with MET. Type I inhibitors represent adenosine triphosphate (ATP) competitors that bind to the ATP-binding pocket in the active state of MET (DFG-in). According to their interaction with G1163 residue, Type I MET inhibitors are further subdivided into Types Ia (crizotinib and ensartinib) and Ib (capmatinib, savolitinib, and tepotinib). Type II TKIs include cabozantinib, merestinib, and glesatinib, which compete with ATP and bind to the inactive conformation (DFG-out). Type III inhibitors are negative allosteric inhibitors (Han et al. 2024).

Despite the known efficacy of MET-TKIs in MET exon 14 skipping mutant lung cancer, the acquired resistance mechanisms and strategies for overcoming secondary resistance have been poorly studied. Located within the activation rings of the MET kinase domain, D1228 plays an important role in stabilizing the activating ring conformation, resulting in MET constitutive phosphorylation and promoting cell transformation. Traditionally, the D1228 E/G/H/N mutation is thought to cause Type I MET-TKI resistance (Piper-Vallillo et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2022), whereas D1228A/Y causes resistance to both Type I and II MET inhibitors(Fujino et al. 2019; Guo et al. 2020). Crizotinib treatment can be disrupted by four acquired MET mutations (Y1230H, D1228H, D1228N, and D1231Y), preventing proper contact with the amino acid, whereas these mutations do not interfere with the action of the Type II blocker cabozantinib (Kang et al. 2018). By contrast, our case demonstrates that the tumor remains sensitive to savolitinib upon the acquisition of a D1228H mutation. The observation could be explained by the discrepancies between in vitro kinase activity and clinical outcomes. But the loss of MET D1228H mutation and presentation of p.Y1230N raise another interesting hypothesis that different type Ib MET inhibitors might demonstrate differentiated resistant mechanisms. On the other hand, the principal resistance sites for type Ia and Ib MET-TKIs are traditionally thought to be identical (Fujino et al. 2019). Resistance sites including Y1230, D1228, G1090, V1092, G1163, and D1010H are commonly involved. Capmatinib (type Ib) demonstrated 10% objective response and 80% disease control rate in patients who were previously treated with crizotinib (type Ia) (Dagogo-Jack et al. 2021). We presented a case who exhibited sensitivity to savolitinib (type Ib) after ensartinib (type Ia) resistance (Xia et al. 2023). We urgently call for additional studies regarding the MET inhibitor resistant mechanism using patient-derived tumor-like cell clusters, patient-derived organoids and patient-derived xenografts (Tang et al. 2022; Yin et al. 2020; Yoshida 2020).

Conclusion

Here, we reported a case who remained sensitive to savolitinib after capmatinib induced a MET D1228H mutation. After savolitinib resistance, the rebiopsy testing found absence of MET D1228H mutation and presence of MET p.Y1230N mutation. We speculate that the discrepancies between in vitro kinase activity and clinical outcomes may exist, and concomitantly, different type Ib MET inhibitors might demonstrate differentiated resistant mechanisms. Further studies are required.
