Introduction

Retronychia is defined as a condition where the proximal nail plate grows into the proximal nail fold with the formation of multiple layers of nail plates [1,2]. It tends to happen in adolescents with female predominance [2,3]. Retronychia relates to frequent microtrauma that affects the toenails, especially the big toe and it is less common on the fingernails [3,4]. It typically occurs unilaterally but bilateral cases may be encountered [5,6]. The nail seems like the back of a shrimp, a horseshoe crab, or even an oyster shell [4]. The majority of retronychia cases are noted at the age of 25 [5]. Correct Diagnosis usually takes (1–48) months after symptoms appear [2]. Retrochinya is suspected when there is chronic proximal paronychia not responding to treatment and multiple layers of nail plates [3]. Other symptoms are noticed like pain, xanthonychia, oozing, impaired walking, onycholysis, and granulation tissue between proximal and lateral folds [3]. Clinical diagnosis is the basis of retronychia and ultrasound can be utilized as a non-invasive technique to verify the diagnosis [3,6]. In this paper, we report three cases of retronychia caused by trauma in which conservative treatment failed and was treated surgically. This retrospective case series has been reported in line with the PROCESS guidelines [7].

Cases presentation

Case 1

A 14-year-old female presented with nail growth stopped and painful swelling in her left hallux that did not respond to topical steroids and systemic antibiotics and in the right toe there is an onychomadesis. The patient had been traumatized at the same toenail months ago. On examination, the left great toenail showed yellowish discoloration, hematoma under the nail, and marked swelling with erythema of the periungual skin (Fig. 1a). Depending on clinical features, retronychia was diagnosed, and nail avulsion was performed without any complication. Post-avulsion revealed an underlying yellowish nail that was separated from the underlying nail bed. Three translucent reddish/bluish papules were observed. One month later, the patient exhibited normal growth of a healthy-looking nail (Fig. 1b). She had no recurrence at a 6-month follow-up.

A: The figure shows the great toenail exhibited yellowish discoloration, hematoma under the nail, and marked swelling with erythema of the periungual skin.

b: After one month the patient exhibited normal growth of a health looking nail.

Case 2

An 18-year-old man came to our department with painful swelling and stopped growth in his right great toenail that did not improve with antibiotics. The patient had previously trauma the same toenail. Upon examination, the toenail displayed yellow discoloration, infection, granulation tissue, and significant swelling with redness of the surrounding skin (Fig. 2b). Retronychia was diagnosed clinically, and nail avulsion was performed without any complication (Fig. 2a). After the procedure, a yellowish nail separated from the nail bed was revealed. At a follow-up appointment 7 months later, there was no recurrence of symptoms.

a: The toenail displayed yellow discoloration, infection, granulation tissue, and significant swelling with redness of the surrounding skin.

b: Nail avulsion.

Case 3

A 30-years-old woman presented with Inflammation and severe pain in her right toenail. Treatment with systemic and topical antibiotics had failed. While taking the clinical history, she mentioned a previous severe trauma to her foot a month ago and she was wearing uncomfortable tight shoes. During the clinical examination, it was found swelling, redness in her toe and discoloration of nail to yellow (Fig. 3a). Retronychia was diagnosed clinically and she underwent onychectomy without any complication (nail avulsion) (Fig. 3b), and the outcome was successful with a 6-month follow-up without recurrence (Fig. 3c).

a: The examination showed swelling, redness in her toe and discoloration of nail to yellow.

b: Onychectomy (nail avulsion) during surgery.

c: The nail after 6-month follow-up without recurrence

Discussion

Retronychia is a chronic nail abnormity based on unresolved chronic proximal paronychia caused by disintegrated nail growth and several generations of nail plate localization underneath the damaged nail [3]. It also comes with many variable symptoms such as pain, xanthonychia, oozing, impaired walking, onycholysis, and granulation tissue between proximal and lateral folds [3]. The most common reason for retronychia is trauma which also happens after repetitive micro-trauma (running), some strenuous sports (hiking, mountaineering, and jogging), spontaneous trauma (wearing tight shoes), or acute trauma [3,8,9]. The presence of trauma was reported in all cases in this paper. In some cases, it can be due to anatomical factors such as congenital malformation or systematic conditions like compartment syndrome, arthritis, thrombophlebitis, or postpartum changes in the body [3,9]. Depending on the presence of familial cases and rates of concordance among twins, genetic factors contribute to retronychia [8]. In our paper, the case (1) has a familial history with retronychia. Where retronychia is seen combination of the stop of the nail plate creation, the change in adhesion between the nail plate to the nail bed, and the absence of the link between the matrix and the nail plate [8]. It has two clinical stages: the early stage is often underdiagnosis and appears by discoloration of the nail plate to yellow, discontinued nail growth, mild paronychia, and secretions from the proximal nail fold then retronychia advance to its late stage, all of these symptoms become worsen including severe paronychia and proximal nail plate is visibly noticeable. Other signs that may be notable in the late stage like subungual hyperkeratosis, distal onycholysis, and Beau's lines [8,10]. In our paper, the case (1&2) came with a clear clinical appearance indicating the presence of retronychia, which can be classified as late stage of retronychia. In cases of long-term retronychia, the development of a distal bulge is a key factor in the ongoing maintenance, stubborn progression, and potential relapses of the condition [4]. This bulge forms as a result of continuous pressure on the nail, pushing it back toward the nail matrix [4]. This repetitive action causes the new nail to disconnect from the matrix, leading to an increase in the number of nail layers and resulting in a thicker, yellowed nail plate [4]. In some cases, the nail may also take on a dirty greenish hue [4]. The main diagnosis of retronychia is clinical [3,8]. All cases in this paper were clinically diagnosed. Early diagnosis prevents recurrence and makes management getting easier [3,9]. The diagnosis depends on the presence of chronic inflammation of the proximal nail fold, xanthonychia, thickening of the proximal portion of the nail plate, granulation tissue, and chronic aches [3,11]. Most of these clinical signs were found in our cases (chronic inflammation, xantonychia…). Retronychia is similar to many diseases that cause chronic paronychia such as bacterial onychitis, onychomycosis, rheumatoid arthritis, subungual tumors, myxoid cyst, inflammatory diseases, Lichen planus, psoriasis which make the diagnosis more difficult, especially in the early stages [3,8,12]. Ultrasound is a non-invasive method that can be useful in recognizing differential diagnoses, exclusion tumors and complications that may associated with it, and determining the time of injury [3,11]. It shows reducing the distance between the origin of the nail plate and the base of the distal phalanx, reduced echogenicity, and increased thickness [3,6,12]. Doppler ultrasound is another useful technique that can detect anatomical changes and appears more blood afflux in the dermis of the posterior nail fold and proximal nail bed [1,8]. Histological examination may not be necessary for the diagnosis, but it plays rule to exclude any potential neoplasia in the proximal nail fold [11]. Retronychia treatment varies between surgical treatment and conservative treatment based on the severity of the case [10]. Surgical treatment was performed in our three cases. Conservative treatment can be useful in the early stages such as topical steroids applied on the proximal nail plate, taping, and orthosis [3,8]. This way of therapy could be helpful by degrading the hyperkeratosis with the application of 50 % urea occlusive dressing at night and debridement of hyperkeratosis with a scalpel [3]. The recurrence is common in conservative Treatment [8]. If there is a superficial infection, systemic or topical antibiotics could be useful, but they are ineffective in resolving retronychia [5]. Surgery is advised for advanced cases of chronic paronychia with granulation tissue. The surgical procedure involves removing the overlapping nail plates and excising the granulation tissue [8]. Two surgical approaches were described, including complete nail plate avulsion and partial avulsion [2]. We managed the three cases in this paper with complete nail plate avulsion. However, complications are possible after surgery, such as post-surgical permanent nail dystrophy with an incidence rate of 13–33 %, including retraction of the nail bed with pincer nail, micronychia and flawed nail regrowth with hypertrophy of periungual soft tissues [7]. The management of retronychia should address associated risk factors such as proper footwear, micro-trauma, and correction of static foot disorders, especially for patients who have previously retronychia as they are at higher risk for recurrence [8,10].

Conclusion

This paper highlights the importance of considering retronychia as a potential differential diagnosis in cases of chronic paronychia, and stresses the significance of early detection to prevent complications. Investigating the patient's medical history is crucial in identifying underlying factors to preventing recurrence.
