Brachial Plexus Schwannoma With Neural Foraminal Extension
Authors: Metasebia W. Abebe, Biniam G. Gebru, Abraham T. Gebremedihen, Samuel E. Alem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Summary:Schwannomas are benign nerve sheath tumors arising from the proliferation of active peripheral Schwann cells. Although schwannomas represent the most common benign peripheral nerve sheath tumors, brachial plexus schwannomas are rarely documented in the literature, presenting a unique challenge due to their complex anatomical location and potential for significant complications. In this report, we present a 36-year-old man presenting with progressive swelling over the right side of his neck, gradually enlarging during the past 10 years. On clinical examination, a firm, nonpulsatile, mobile mass measuring 4 × 3 cm with smooth, round borders was observed on the right side of the neck, with no motor or sensory abnormalities in the right upper limb. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a fusiform mass along the right C6 nerve root extending to the upper trunk, exhibiting homogeneous postcontrast enhancement. Fine needle aspiration cytology indicated a spindle cell mesenchymal neoplasm, consistent with schwannoma. Given the clinical findings, imaging, and the patient’s discomfort, surgical intervention was decided. A firm, glistening yellowish tumor extending intraforaminally was dissected free from its capsule, and the intraforaminal segment was meticulously dissected and removed in piecemeal fashion to achieve gross total resection while preserving the integrity of the C6 nerve root. Histological analysis confirmed the diagnosis of schwannoma. During a 12-month follow-up period, the patient developed a hypertrophic scar at the incision site, which was managed with intralesional steroid injections. No sensory or motor deficits were noted, and follow-up magnetic resonance imaging scans at 3, 6, and 12 months showed no evidence of recurrence.

Most brachial plexus tumors are benign, commonly presenting either as schwannomas or neurofibromas; primary malignant tumors in this region are rare.1 Schwannomas, also known as neurilemmomas, are slow-growing benign tumors of the nerve sheath that arise from Schwann cells. These cells are found on spinal nerves, peripheral nerves, cranial nerves (except for the optic and olfactory nerves), and the autonomic nervous system.2–4 Although schwannomas are commonly found in the head and neck region, particularly originating from the cranial nerves, they spare the brachial plexus in most cases. Brachial plexus schwannomas account for approximately 5% of all schwannomas.3,4 These tumors typically present as a palpable mass, with or without associated sensory or motor deficits.3,5 Schwannomas are often encapsulated and present as masses compressing the nerve, making it theoretically possible to excise them while minimizing the risk of direct nerve injury.2 Diagnosis of schwannomas is based on clinical evaluation, imaging, and histopathologic studies. Although conservative observation of small asymptomatic schwannomas is possible, larger schwannomas with neurological symptoms can safely be managed with surgical excision. The use of intraoperative nerve monitoring is recommended to minimize postoperative neurological deficits during enucleation of brachial plexus schwannomas.1,3 This is definitely an invaluable tool for identifying and preserving nerves but often unavailable in resource-limited settings. Therefore, precise anatomical knowledge, meticulous microsurgical dissection, and nerve preservation can serve as a reasonable alternative.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 36-year-old man presented with right-sided, painless lateral neck swelling that had gradually increased in size over the last decade. On clinical examination, he had a 4 × 3 cm firm mass in the right supraclavicular area with a smooth surface, round borders, and no fixation to underlying or overlying structures. It was a nontender, nonpulsatile mass with no associated sensory/motor deficit of the right upper limb. He had no hyperpigmented macules, freckles, or any other stigmata of neurofibromatosis. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a T1 hypointense and T2 hyperintense fusiform mass following the right intraforaminal C6 nerve root extending to the upper trunk of the brachial plexus. The mass showed homogeneous enhancement on T1 postcontrast sequence and was seen to widen the C5/C6 neural foramina with moderate scalloping of the lateral aspects of the vertebral bodies at C5 and C6. There was no compression of the cervical spinal cord. The right vertebral artery was displaced ventrally but was not encased or stenosed (Fig. 1). Fine needle aspiration cytology suggested a spindle cell mesenchymal neoplasm, favoring a diagnosis of cellular schwannoma. Although conservative management was offered as an option, given the slow-growing nature of the mass and the absence of any neurological or functional deficits, the patient insisted on surgical removal due to the psychological distress related to the presence of the mass. A supraclavicular horizontal incision was made over the swelling, and an encapsulated mass was identified under the platysma. Extracapsular dissection of the tumor was performed through the peritumoral fascial plane. The tumor capsule was then opened, and the mass was debulked to facilitate further peritumoral dissection. The centrally debulked extraforaminal segment was then carefully dissected from the upper trunk of the brachial plexus in a fascicle-sparing manner (Fig. 2). The intraforaminal segment of the mass was accessed through the widened C5/C6 foramen and removed piecemeal using standard microsurgical techniques while preserving C6 nerve root. Histopathologic examination confirmed the diagnosis of schwannoma with hypercellular (Antoni A) and hypocellular (Antoni B) areas consisting of sheets and fascicles composed of monomorphic, bland spindle cells with hyalinized vessels in an edematous and fibrotic stroma with no mitosis, necrosis, or atypia (Fig. 3). On subsequent 12-month postoperative follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with no sensory or motor deficits except for a hypertrophic scar over the incision site, which was treated with intralesional steroid injections. Follow-up radiological (MRI) evaluations done at 3, 6, and 12 months showed no evidence of residual or recurrent tumor. DISCUSSION

Schwannomas, particularly those involving the brachial plexus, represent a rare subset of nerve sheath tumors that pose significant diagnostic and surgical challenges due to their anatomical intricacy. The brachial plexus, with its deep location and complex network of nerve roots and trunks, makes surgical intervention particularly delicate.1–4,6

Clinical presentation in brachial plexus tumors will depend on the pathology, location, and extent of the tumor, as well as the degree of compression of nerves.2,5,7 In this case, the tumor presented as a slowly enlarging mass during a 10-year period, with no associated neurological deficits, with literature mentioning durations from 6 months to 10 years.8,9 This absence of functional impairment is perhaps related to the slow-growing nature of schwannomas, which allows for compensatory mechanisms to develop over time. Furthermore, these tumors are often encapsulated and tend to compress most fascicles rather than infiltrate/destroy them.9 MRI findings were critical in confirming the tumor’s fusiform shape and origin from the C6 nerve root, as well as identifying features such as homogeneous postcontrast enhancement and foraminal extension, which are characteristic of schwannomas.5,10 Additionally, cytological analysis provided further evidence, highlighting the importance of combining clinical, imaging, and histopathologic approaches for accurate diagnosis.

Despite the absence of symptoms, the mass had a significant psychological impact on the quality of life of the patient. Additionally, the operating surgeons believed that, given the patient’s young age and the tumor’s intraforaminal extension, there was a high likelihood of future spinal cord compression, due to the tumor’s slow but relentless growth, and the rare possibility of malignant transformation. On subsequent 12-month postoperative follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with no sensory or motor deficits, except for a hypertrophic scar over the incision site, which was treated with intralesional steroid injections. Follow-up clinical and radiological (MRI) evaluations done at 3, 6, and 12 months showed no evidence of residual or recurrent tumor.

The surgical management in this case successfully achieved gross total resection while preserving nerve integrity. This underscores the surgical principle of enucleation in schwannomas, where anatomical knowledge and careful dissection of the tumor capsule from the nerve fibers allow for safe removal. Intraforaminal tumor extension necessitated piecemeal resection, emphasizing the importance of meticulous microsurgical technique in minimizing iatrogenic nerve injury.2,3,5 Postoperative outcome in this patient was favorable, with no evidence of neurological deficits or recurrence during the 12-month follow-up period.

This case contributes to the existing literature by demonstrating the feasibility of gross total resection of brachial plexus schwannomas in young, asymptomatic patients, while preserving neurological function with the right anatomical knowledge and meticulous surgical techniques in setups where advanced intraoperative electric stimulation and mapping are not available.
