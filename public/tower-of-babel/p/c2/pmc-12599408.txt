Mesenchymal Stem Cells With Adjuvant Dexamethasone in Patients With Alzheimer’s Disease: A Phase IIa Trial
Authors: Na Kyung Lee, Hyemin Jang, Yejoo Choi, Song Hwangbo, Seunghoon Lee, Jung Il Lee, Young Ju Kim, Juhee Chin, Jong Wook Chang, Sang Won Seo, Hyo Jin Son, Soo Jin Choi, Duk L Na, Hee Jin Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and PurposeThis phase IIa trial assessed the safety and efficacy of mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) therapy for Alzheimer’s disease (AD). An open-label extension (OLE) further explored the adjunctive role of dexamethasone.MethodsMSCs (n=24) or a saline placebo (n=12) were administered intraventricularly three times at four-week intervals. In the OLE, MSCs and dexamethasone (15 mg, n=5) were administered to patients who received saline in phase IIa. Clinical parameters and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) markers were evaluated.ResultsMSC therapy exhibited no significant clinical benefits, but was associated with reductions in CSF AD biomarkers (amyloid-beta, phosphorylated-tau, and total-tau) compared to placebo. The MSC group experienced more adverse events (fever, headache, nausea, and vomiting), while co-administration of dexamethasone appeared to attenuate immune-related reactions and limit increases in CSF white blood cell and interleukin-6.ConclusionsThese findings suggest exploratory biological effects of MSCs on AD biomarkers, with dexamethasone potentially mitigating MSC-induced immune responses.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT02054208, NCT03172117, and NCT04954534Keywords: Mesenchymal Stem Cell, Dexamethasone, Cerebrospinal Fluid, Inflammation, Cytokine

INTRODUCTION

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are a promising therapeutic option for neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD),1,2,3 Despite recently approved anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies, the complex and multifactorial nature of AD pathogenesis4,5,6 underscores the need for comprehensive therapeutic approaches. MSCs have the potential to address multiple aspects of AD pathology,7,8,9 including reducing amyloid beta (Aβ)7,10,11 and tau burden,12 as well as enhancing neuroprotective effects13 and proteasomal activity.14 Notably, previous phase I clinical trials confirmed the safety of MSCs in patients with AD,15,16,17 further supporting their potential clinical applications.

In a recent phase I clinical trial, most participants with AD experienced fever within 24 hours of intraventricular injection of human umbilical cord blood-derived MSCs.15 We proposed that this transient fever was caused by inflammatory or immune reactions against the injected MSCs, as we observed increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin (IL)-1β, and IL-6 in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of the MSC injected group.18 MSCs are widely believed to be immune-privileged because of their lack of expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) II, such as human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-DR, and low levels of MHC class I.19,20,21,22,23 However, evidence from the above clinical trials and recent studies has raised doubts regarding the hypoimmunogenic properties of MSCs.18,24,25,26,27,28,29 Thus, the immune response might explain the low engraftment of MSCs in the recipient,30 as a previous study demonstrated limited persistence of donor MSCs in the recipient tissue after intravenous MSC transplantation.31 Additionally, it has been observed that the survival time of MSCs is relatively short following intrathecal injection.32 Given this, it is reasonable to expect that use of immunosuppressants could minimize adverse events and maximize the therapeutic benefits of MSC therapy.

Herein, we present the results of a phase IIa randomized controlled trial which aimed to investigate the safety and exploratory efficacy of MSC injections into the lateral ventricle of patients with AD. We assessed changes in cognitive scores, CSF AD biomarkers, and amyloid uptake on positron emission tomography (PET). Furthermore, in an additional open-label extension (OLE) study (Fig. 1), we administered both MSCs and dexamethasone to patients who were allocated to the placebo group in the phase IIa trial. We investigated whether intraventricular MSC injections induce an immune response in patients with AD, and whether treatment with dexamethasone, known for its anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive properties, could mitigate this response. We further analyzed the changes in inflammatory markers in the CSF of the MSC, placebo, and MSC + dexamethasone groups.

Study flow diagram. A total of 40 participants who met the probable criteria for AD were selected for inclusion in this study. Following the exclusion of four participants, the final analysis was conducted on the remaining 36 participants. The participants were randomly assigned to two groups. The participants were randomly assigned to receive either MSC injections or placebo injections. Twenty-four participants were allocated to the MSC group, and 12 were allocated to the placebo group. Of the 12 participants who received placebo injections, an OLE study was subsequently performed. Of the 12, seven were excluded, and five were included in the final analyses.

METHODS

Study design and participants

This phase I/IIa clinical trial, named the “Safety and Exploratory Efficacy Study of NEUROSTEM® Versus Placebo in Patients with Alzheimer’s Disease,” was registered at Clinical Trials.gov, NCT02054208. Details of the Phase IIA study plan and statistical analysis plan are described in the Supplementary Methods in Supplementary Data 1.

After confirming the safety of MSCs therapy in a phase I (n=9) trial,15 we recruited participants (n=36) for the phase IIa trial from December 2017 to May 2019 at the Samsung Medical Center, Seoul, South Korea. Due to its exploratory nature, this combined phase I/IIa trial did not calculate sample size based on statistical considerations. Instead, it was determined based on practical considerations such as resource availability and recruitment feasibility. Participants received either intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection of 3.0×107 MSCs/2 mL (n=24) or normal saline 2 mL (n=12), via the Ommaya reservoir three times at 4-week intervals. MSC preparation is described in the Supplementary Methods in Supplementary Data 1. The cell concentration was determined as the maximum tolerable dose without dose-limiting toxicity in a phase I trial. Randomization was performed using a Web-based system (www.cubecdms.com). Participants were randomly allocated to receive MSCs or placebo in a 2:1 ratio using a central block randomization with block sizes of three and six. Participants, physicians, and those assessing outcomes were blinded to the intervention.

We further conducted an OLE study from July 2021 to January 2022 to administer MSCs to participants previously allocated to the placebo group (NCT04954534) (Fig. 1). Of the 12 participants in the placebo group, five agreed to participate in the OLE study. To mitigate adverse events, participants received an intravenous injection of 15 mg dexamethasone (5 mg, 30 minutes before MSC injection and 10 mg, 4 hours after MSC injection). Two participants declined to have their samples used further, leaving three available in the MSC+Dexa group. The OLE study examined inflammation-related outcomes, specifically CSF white blood cell (WBC). All participants (n=36) were monitored for 36 months as a follow-up for safety (ClinicalTrials. gov, NCT03172117).

The inclusion criteria for AD participants were adapted from prior reports.15 The study’s eligible participants were between 50 and 85 years of age, met the National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA) criteria for probable AD,33 and had Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores ranging from 18 to 26. Eligible participants were screened with 18F-florbetaben PET, 18F-fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose (FDG) PET, and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Enrollment was confirmed for participants who met the following criteria: 1) amyloid positivity on 18F-Florbetaben PET and 2) evidence of neurodegeneration, either as structural atrophy on MRI or hypometabolism on FDG PET.

Written informed consent was obtained from all patients and legally authorized representatives in cases of cognitive impairment. This study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Medical Center. The clinical studies were registered at ClinicalTrials.gov under the approval numbers NCT02054208, NCT03172117, and NCT04954534.

Safety, clinical, and biomarker assessments

Adverse events were assessed at each study visit using the National Cancer Institute- Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE).34 Additionally, safety monitoring was conducted at 18, 24, and 36 months, with external oversight provided by C&R Research Inc. To evaluate potential immune responses between MSC-treated participants and placebo recipients, mixed lymphocyte reaction (MLR) testing was conducted at baseline and again 12 weeks following the initial injection. Proliferation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) was used as an indicator of the alloreactive immune response. In order to assess proliferation, PBMCs were isolated and cultured under three conditions: 1) stimulation with phytohaemagglutinin (PHA) as a positive control, 2) co-culture with unrelated donor PBMCs, and 3) co-culture with the MSCs utilized in the study. Immunological safety was determined if the proliferation rate of the subject’s PBMCs co-cultured with the same lot of cells administered was less than or equal to 50% of the highest proliferative response observed with PHA stimulation or co-culture using unrelated donor PBMCs.

The primary clinical endpoint was defined as the change in the Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale–Cognitive Subscale (ADAS-Cog 11) score over a 24-week period.35 Secondary outcomes encompassed the Clinical Dementia Rating Scale Sum of Boxes Scores (CDR-SOB),36 MMSE, the Seoul Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (S-IADL),37 and the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI).38 All assessments were conducted at the baseline and subsequently at 4, 8, 12, and 24 weeks following the initial injection. CSF was collected at several time points: immediately prior to and one day following each injection. The time points included baseline, baseline +1 day (after the first injection), 4 weeks and 4 weeks +1 day (after the second injection), 8 weeks and 8 weeks +1 day (after the third injection), as well as 12 weeks and 24 weeks. The analyses encompassed CSF WBC counts; AD biomarkers (Aβ42, to tal tau [t-tau], and phosphorylated tau at threonine 181 [p-tau181]), measured with INNOTEST enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Fujirebio Europe N.V.)39; and MSC-related proteins (decorin, sICAM, GDF-15, progranulin, and galectin-3), assessed by ELISA. Furthermore, florbetaben PET was performed at the baseline and at week 24, with tracer uptake quantified using global standardized uptake value ratios (SUVR).40 In the OLE study, CSF was collected one day after the first MSC+Dexa injection and seven days after the second and third injections.

Human cytokine array and ELISA

CSF cytokines were analyzed by array and ELISA in six participants: three who participated in both the placebo and MSC+Dexa groups (52-year-old woman, 59-year-old woman, 65-year-old man), and three additional age- and sex-matched participants who received MSC alone (57-year-old woman, 61-year-old woman, 60-year-old man). Among the placebo group, three participants in the MSC+Dexa group were selected for CSF analyses.

The cytokine profiles of the CSF were analyzed using a cytokine array that detected 36 human cytokines (R&D Systems, ary005b). We calculated the relative changes in the levels of cytokine (CCL2, CXCL10, IL-1ra, IL-6, IL-8, IL-18, MIF, and Serpin E1) from baseline.

ELISA was performed using R&D Systems human interleukin-6 IL-6 (DY206), each sample tested in duplicate wells. The ELISA samples were measured three times and averaged.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0; GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Demographic factors and the prevalence of adverse events following the intervention across the three groups were compared using a Welch’s t-test (unpaired, two-tailed) or Fisher’s exact test with Bonferroni correction.

Efficacy analyses were conducted using two-sample t-tests from full analysis set to compare changes in ADAS-Cog 11, CDR-SOB, MMSE, S-IADL, and NPI scores from baseline to each time point between the MSC and placebo groups. Changes in CSF WBC counts, AD biomarkers, and MSC-related markers from baseline to each time point in the MSC and placebo groups were compared using two-sample t-tests. Changes in the Florbetaben PET SUVR from baseline to the 24-week mark were investigated within each group using paired t-tests, and subsequently compared between the two groups using two-sample t-tests.

The human cytokine array and ELISA results were analyzed for statistical differences between the MSC and MSC+Dexa groups using a Welch’s t-test (unpaired, two-tailed). A p-value of ≤0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Changes in clinical outcome and AD biomarkers

In the phase IIa trial, MSC did not show clinical benefit. There were no significant differences in ADAS-Cog 11, CDR-SOB, MMSE, S-IADL, or NPI scores between the MSC and placebo groups (Supplementary Table 1).

The florbetaben PET SUVR changes from baseline to 24 weeks showed a slight increase (0.05±0.10) in the placebo group and a slight decrease (−0.01±0.09) in the MSC group. However. these differences did not reach statistical significance between the two groups (p=0.055).

For CSF AD biomarkers,41 Aβ42 and p-tau levels from baseline to baseline+1d, 4 week+1d, and 8 week+1d were significantly decreased in the MSC group compared to the placebo group (all p≤0.05). Changes in t-tau levels from baseline to baseline+1d and 4 weeks+1d also showed a significant decrease in the MSC group (all p≤0.05) (Fig. 2). For MSC-related markers in CSF, MSC group showed notable increases in Decorin (at baseline+1d), sICAM-1 (at all visits except at 24 weeks), GDF-15 (at all visits except for 12 and 24 weeks), Progranulin (at all visits), and Galectin-3 (at baseline+1d, 4 weeks+1d, and 8 weeks+1d), compared to the placebo group (all p≤0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Adverse events

In the phase IIa trial, subjects in the MSC group exhibited a considerably elevated incidence of fever (p<0.001), headache (p<0.001), nausea (p=0.005), and vomiting (p=0.001) in comparison to those administered a placebo. In contrast, the OLE study demonstrated that the addition of dexamethasone resulted in a reduced incidence of fever (p=0.020) and vomiting (p=0.003) in the MSC+Dexa group compared to the MSC group. The overall rate of adverse events did not differ significantly between the MSC+Dexa and placebo groups (Table 1). CSF cultures obtained after fever episodes were negative for bacterial growth. The elevated body temperature and increased CSF WBC counts resolved without the administration of antimicrobial agents. MLR testing demonstrated that all participants remained immunologically stable.

In the phase IIa trial, serious adverse drug reactions (SADR) occurred in one participant (a 65-year-old female) in the MSC treatment group, who required additional admission for markedly elevated CSF WBC counts (>1,000/uL) one day after the second injection. The participant exhibited no atypical MRI findings, and the CSF WBC count diminished to 12/µL within two days without further therapeutic intervention. The patient subsequently received the third injection. Subsequent Gram staining of the CSF showed negative results. No SADR was observed in the placebo group. In the OLE study, SADR occurred in one participant (a 65-year-old female) who required additional admission for fever. CSF WBC in CSF was elevated over two weeks. CSF Gram staining revealed gram-positive cocci. The patient received intravenous antibiotics for two weeks and discontinued participation in the study.

Dexamethasone mitigated increment of CSF WBC counts in CSF induced by MSCs

In comparison with the baseline, CSF WBC counts measured one day after injection were significantly elevated in the MSC group (p<0.001). However, no significant changes were observed in either the placebo or MSC+Dexa groups (Fig. 3). Concurrently, the MSC+Dexa group exhibited a marked decrease in CSF WBC counts relative to the MSC group (p=0.015). Differential cell analysis indicated a predominance of neutrophils at post–day 1 in both MSC-treated cohorts: The MSC group exhibited a neutrophil percentage of 73.2%±11.6%, a lymphocyte percentage of 9.1%±7.1%, and an eosinophil percentage of 0%. The MSC+Dexa group demonstrated a neutrophil percentage of 60.8%±37.4%, a lymphocyte percentage of 14.2%±12.8%, and an eosinophil percentage of 0%.

Human cytokine arrays were performed for three participants in each group. Eight (CCL2, CXCL10, IL-1ra, IL-6, IL-8, IL-18, MIF, and SerpinE1) of the thirty-six cytokines tested were detected in the CSF one day after MSC or MSC+Dexa injection, with no change detected after saline injection (Supplementary Fig. 2). The relative changes in cytokines at day 1 compared to baseline showed that the increase in expression of IL-6 (p=0.017) and IL-8 (p=0.004) was smaller in the MSC+Dexa group than in the MSC group (Fig. 4A). Validation of human cytokine array results via ELISA

The results of the cytokine array were validated by quantifying the expression of IL-6, which showed the most robust difference in cytokine levels between the two groups, using ELISA placebo (n=3), MSC (n=3), and MSC+Dexa (n=3) groups (Fig. 4B). Both the MSC (P=0.003) and MSC+Dexa (p=0.036) groups showed a significant increase in IL-6 levels 1 day after injection. We further confirmed that the increment in IL-6 level was significantly reduced in the MSC+Dexa group compared to that in the MSC group, validating the cytokine array results (p=0.002).

DISCUSSION

In this phase IIa trial, MSC injections into the lateral ventricle of AD patients showed no clinical benefit. However, MSCs lowered Aβ42, p-tau, and t-tau in the CSF compared with placebo. Notably, participants receiving MSC injections experienced more adverse events, including fever, headache, nausea, and vomiting, than those in the placebo group. One participant in the MSC group experienced a serious adverse drug reaction after the second injection; WBC counts normalized within two days, and the participant subsequently received the third injection. In the OLE study, the addition of dexamethasone to MSC injections appeared to attenuate these common immune-related adverse events. However, one participant in the MSC+Dexa group experienced a serious bacterial infection requiring intravenous antibiotics. We considered this infection to be procedure-related rather than dexamethasone-related. Furthermore, CSF analyses further revealed smaller increases in CSF WBC and IL-6 levels with dexamethasone, suggesting that it mitigates the immune response induced by MSC.

This trial failed to show clinical benefits of MSCs in AD, despite positive results in previous animal studies.7,42 This discrepancy might be due to the small sample size, short-term follow-up, or insufficient MSC dosing in humans. Nevertheless, exploratory signals suggestive of effects of MSCs on AD pathology rather than definitive therapeutic benefits were observed. Although the change in florbetaben PET SUVR at 24 weeks was not significantly different, the placebo group exhibited an increase while the MSC group showed a slight decrease compared with baseline. In addition, CSF p-tau 181, and t-tau levels significantly decreased in the MSC group compared to the placebo group. Increased paracrine factors (decorin, sICAM, GDF-15, progranulin, and galectin-3)43,44,45,46 may be a plausible mechanism for this effect. These findings suggest that MSCs may have the ability to modulate amyloid and tau pathology. However, further studies with larger cohorts are needed. Considering that low CSF Aβ42 levels reflect AD pathology,47,48,49 it was interesting to note that a decrease in CSF Aβ42 levels was detected following MSC injection. Decreased AD biomarkers in the CSF were observed one day after each injection but returned to baseline levels after 4 weeks. These changes may be due to an acute reaction within the CSF, which could also explain the decrease not only in p-tau and t-tau, but also in Aβ42.

The MSC group experienced more adverse events than the placebo group, which were ameliorated when dexamethasone was added in the OLE study. In the MSC group, the most common adverse events were headache (91.7%), fever (>37.5°C; 87.5%), and nausea (87.5%), which, along with increased CSF WBC in the CSF, indicated an immune response in the central nervous system. We further found that the MSC+Dexa group in the OLE study had fewer adverse events compared to the MSC group: headache (60%), nausea (40%), and fever (20%). In addition, the MSC+Dexa group demonstrated a lower increase in CSF WBC count than the MSC group. Therefore, the Phase IIa and OLE studies presented herein support the adjuvant use of immunosuppressants in MSC therapy. Taken together, these data suggest that co-administering dexamethasone may reduce the occurrence of common, immune-related adverse events. However, the occurrence of a serious infection underscores the importance of carefully monitoring procedure-related complications.

We further explored the changes in CSF cytokine levels. In both the cytokine array and ELISA analyses, the MSC+Dexa group showed a lower increase in pro-inflammatory IL-6 levels than the MSC group. Despite the absence of ELISA testing, the results of the cytokine array for IL-8, another pro-inflammatory cytokine, were validated through the assessment of CSF WBC counts. This analysis revealed a decrease in neutrophil levels in the MSC+Dexa group one day after injection. It has been established that IL-8 exerts a chemotactic effect on neutrophils.50 Together with the changes in CSF WBCs and IL-6, we concluded that dexamethasone should be considered as an adjuvant treatment to reduce immune reactions in MSC therapy.

In this study, we identified and validated alterations in immune-related CSF cytokines. The findings of this study further shed light on changes in the CSF environment in response to MSC with and without dexamethasone administration. However, this study had several limitations. First, we enrolled a relatively small number of participants from a single-center in Korea, and only 5 of 12 patients in the placebo group participated in the OLE study, which limit the generalizability of findings. Second, CSF cytokine analyses were conducted only at the baseline and one day following the first injection. In the OLE study, CSF samples were collected 7 days after the second and third injections, as participants were not hospitalized due to fewer adverse events following the first injection. Thus, the simultaneous comparison of CSF from all three MSC injections was not feasible due to the differing collection time points. Third, the small number of samples available for cytokine analysis (n=3 per group) limited the statistical power and generalizability of these findings.

Despite the inability to demonstrate substantial clinical benefit of intraventricular injections of MSCs in AD, the observed changes in amyloid PET SUVR, CSF p-tau, and t-tau levels suggest the presence of biological activity. The OLE study further indicated that dexamethasone may attenuate immune-related adverse events and modulate inflammatory responses. It is imperative to interpret these results with caution, as they are exploratory in nature. Future studies with larger sample sizes and earlier intervention, such as in amyloid positive cognitively normal individuals, will be essential to ascertain their clinical relevance.
