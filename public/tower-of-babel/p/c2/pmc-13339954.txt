ΔVExPLUs: prognostic value of integrating serial changes in VExUS score and lung ultrasound in acute decompensated heart failure
Authors: Gustavo Paes Silvano, Marina Petersen Saadi, Henrique Cabral Scherer, Guilherme Pinheiro Machado, Renato Ferraz Almeida, Kaleb Pinto Spannenberger, Fernando Luis Scolari, Andreia Biolo, Luis Eduardo Paim Rohde, Hatem Soliman Aboumarie, Guilherme Heiden Telo, Anderson Donelli da Silveira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo investigate whether early combined changes in venous excess ultrasound (VExUS) and lung ultrasound (LUS) were associated with outcomes in acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF).Methods and resultsWe prospectively enrolled 104 patients with ADHF [mean age 64.3 ± 13.5 years, 69.2% male, median left ventricle ejection fraction 24.5% (18.0, 32.0)]. VExUS score and LUS were performed within 24 h of admission and after 72 h. Changes in congestion were integrated into a single metric (ΔVExPLUs = [2 × ΔVExUS] + ΔLUS), with ΔVExUS multiplied by two to balance its narrower scoring range. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included 30-day all-cause mortality and a composite of 30-day all-cause mortality, heart transplantation and left ventricular assist device implantation. Patients with greater ΔVExPLUs improvement had lower rates of in-hospital mortality (3% vs. 46%; P < 0.001), 30-day mortality (12% vs. 50%; P = 0.007), and the composite outcome (19% vs. 64%; P = 0.002). In multivariable analysis, ΔVExPLUs ≥ 1 was independently associated with in-hospital mortality [odds ratio (OR): 0.65; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.51, 0.83; P < 0.001], 30-day mortality (OR: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.65, 0.94; P = 0.009), and the composite outcome (OR: 0.76; 95% CI: 0.64, 0.92; P = 0.004). For in-hospital mortality, ΔVExPLUs achieved the highest area under the curve (AUC) (0.76), with a sensitivity of 95.6% and a negative predictive value of 97.0%, compared with ΔVExUS (AUC: 0.70) and ΔLUS (AUC: 0.65).ConclusionSerial integration of systemic and pulmonary ultrasound parameters through ΔVExPLUs is independently associated with short-term outcomes in ADHF and may improve early risk stratification.

Introduction

Acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) is a leading cause of hospitalization and morbidity worldwide,1 with volume overload being a central pathophysiological feature.2 Precise congestion assessment is essential for guiding decongestive therapy and improving clinical outcomes,3 with several congestion scores proposed to aid in this task. However, these tools often rely on subjective or static measures that may not capture dynamic changes during hospitalization.4

Clinical assessment of congestion traditionally relies on symptoms and physical examination findings, such as peripheral oedema, pulmonary rales, jugular venous distension, hepatomegaly, and body weight variation.5 Although widely used, these parameters have important limitations. Clinical signs of congestion often present late in the disease course,6 have low sensitivity,7 and may be absent in patients with ongoing haemodynamic congestion.8 As a result, reliance solely on clinical evaluation may lead to underestimation of residual congestion and suboptimal risk stratification.

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) complements conventional assessment tools by offering a real-time, objective evaluation of both pulmonary and systemic congestion, and has emerged as a valuable modality for bedside management.9 Lung ultrasound (LUS) enables direct visualization of pulmonary interstitial oedema through B-line quantification, offering a non-invasive and sensitive measure of pulmonary congestion that enhances diagnostic accuracy when combined with clinical examination.10 In parallel, venous excess ultrasound (VExUS) score has been proposed to assess systemic venous congestion through Doppler evaluation of hepatic (HV), portal (PV), and intrarenal veins, along with standard inferior vena cava (IVC) assessment.11 While both methods have demonstrated prognostic value in heart failure (HF) populations,12 they provide complementary information: LUS reflects left-sided congestion, whereas VExUS score captures right-sided venous congestion.

Despite growing interest in integrated ultrasound approaches for congestion assessment in HF, data regarding the prognostic relevance of serial changes combining systemic and pulmonary congestion parameters remain limited. In a previous study from our group, early changes in VExUS score (ΔVExUS) within 72 h were strongly associated with short-term outcomes in ADHF. We also found that LUS variation was prognostically significant and often evolved independently from VExUS score.13 Based on these findings, we hypothesized that combining both measures could improve prognostic accuracy. This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of integrating serial changes in VExUS score and LUS in patients hospitalized with ADHF.

Material and methods

Study population

Between October 2022 and January 2024, we prospectively enrolled consecutive adult patients with primary diagnosis of ADHF in a cohort study conducted at the cardiovascular intensive care unit (CICU) of a tertiary public hospital. The diagnosis of ADHF was established based on clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings, in accordance with contemporary HF guidelines.14

Eligible patients were individuals aged ≥18 years with documented left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) <50%, who underwent POCUS evaluation within 24 h of CICU admission. Patients were excluded if suboptimal acoustic windows precluded adequate image acquisition.

The investigation conforms with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (Br Med J 1964; ii: 177) and the study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee (opinion number 6.468.353).

Data collection

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were prospectively recorded at CICU admission. Vital signs, body weight variation, urine output, laboratory measurements, POCUS findings, and clinical congestion assessment were systematically recorded within first 24 h of CICU admission and at 72-h reassessment. The clinical congestion was assessed using the EVEREST score, which includes orthopnoea, jugular venous distension, peripheral oedema, and pulmonary rales and ranges from 0 to 18, with values ≤2 suggested as a predischarge target.4

POCUS evaluation

All examinations were performed at bedside using a portable Mindray ME Series ultrasound system equipped with a 2.5-MHz phased-array sector transducer, while patients were in the supine position under simultaneous electrocardiographic monitoring. The study protocol included LUS and a modified version of VExUS score,13,15 which excluded intrarenal Doppler assessment. Additional concurrent POCUS evaluations were performed, including estimation of left-sided filling pressures using septal E/e′ ratio and measurement of left ventricular outflow tract velocity-time integral (LVOT VTI), as recommended by current guidelines.16

LUS involved scanning four pulmonary fields in each hemithorax: upper anterior, lower anterior, upper lateral, and lower lateral. The anterior zones were located between the parasternal margin and the anterior axillary line, while the lateral zones extended from the anterior to the posterior axillary line. The upper and lower zones within each region were separated by the third intercostal space or the nipple line. LUS was quantified using a standardized eight-zone B-line assessment protocol, with a zone considered positive if three or more B-lines were present. An eight-zone scanning protocol was selected because it represents a widely validated and pragmatic approach for HF assessment, balancing feasibility, rapid bedside applicability, and diagnostic accuracy for serial evaluations in critically ill patients.17

VExUS score included measurement of IVC diameter and collapsibility, along with Doppler waveform assessment of HV and PV. Initially, the IVC diameter was measured through a longitudinal subcostal view, just proximal to the entrance of HV. The maximal diameter during the respiratory cycle was recorded. If IVC was plethoric (≥2 cm), further assessment with HV and PV Doppler was performed using pulsed-wave Doppler. HV flow was classified as grade 1 [normal: systolic (S) wave > diastolic (D) wave], grade 2 (mild abnormality: S < D), or grade 3 (severe abnormality: reversed S wave). PV flow was classified as grade 1 (normal: continuous flow with pulsatile fraction < 30%), grade 2 (mild abnormality: pulsatile fraction 30–49%), or grade 3 (severe abnormality: pulsatile fraction ≥ 50%). VExUS score was then assigned as follows: VExUS grade 0 (no congestion) for IVC < 2 cm; VExUS grade 1 (mild congestion) for IVC ≥ 2 cm with normal Doppler in both HV and PV or only mild abnormalities; VExUS grade 2 (moderate congestion) for IVC ≥ 2 cm with at least one severe Doppler abnormality; and VExUS grade 3 (severe congestion) for IVC ≥ 2 cm with severe abnormalities in both HV and PV.11

The omission of intrarenal Doppler from VExUS score has been validated in a prospective cohort of patients with cardiorenal syndrome (CRS), including those with decompensated HF, where resolution of acute kidney injury (AKI) was significantly associated with VExUS score improvement, and score changes were linked to fluid balance.18 In another study, VExUS score without intrarenal Doppler assessment demonstrated comparable performance to conventional VExUS score for identifying right atrial pressure (RAP) > 12 mmHg,15 with a simplified image acquisition process that may further improve bedside applicability.

All POCUS examinations were performed by two cardiology fellows (M.P.S. and G.P.S.) who completed 4 h of theoretical and practical training, followed by daily hands-on POCUS assessments during their one-month rotation in CICU. Each fellow conducted over 50 supervised examinations during the training period.

Interobserver agreement for VExUS score and LUS were evaluated as previously described by our group.19 Briefly, independent assessments by three trained raters demonstrated excellent reproducibility for identification of lung positive zones [intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC): 0.903; P < 0.001] and the overall VExUS score (ICC: 0.957; P < 0.001).

ΔVExPLUs

ΔVExPLUs (venous excess and pulmonary lung ultrasound) represents the strategy to combine temporal changes in two complementary assessments of congestion in ADHF: VExUS score and LUS. To account for their different scoring ranges, with changes in VExUS (ΔVExUS) ranging from −3 to +3 and changes in LUS (ΔLUS) from −8 to +8, ΔVExUS was multiplied by two and then added to ΔLUS, resulting in a unified variable: ΔVExPLUs = (2 × ΔVExUS) + ΔLUS.

This approach was not intended as a new score, but rather as a strategy to merge complementary ultrasound measures of systemic and pulmonary congestion into a single variable that captures their early temporal changes, providing a balanced and integrated representation of overall congestion by incorporating both right- and left-sided components.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included 30-day all-cause mortality and a composite of 30-day all-cause mortality, heart transplantation (HTx) and left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implantation. All outcomes were collected via medical record review and telephone follow-up conducted 30 days after discharge.

Statistical analysis

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk test and presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (25th–75th percentiles), with comparisons performed using Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as absolute frequencies and percentages and compared using chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Changes in continuous variables across predefined time points were analysed using paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For paired categorical variables, McNemar–Bowker test was used to assess changes between time points.

Statistical analyses focused on the variation (Δ) of POCUS and clinical parameters between CICU admission and 72-h reassessment, reflecting the study’s aim of evaluating the serial use of VExUS score and LUS. As the analysis sought to assess prognostic associations with fixed short-term outcomes rather than develop a formal prediction model, logistic regression was selected as the primary analytical approach, with results expressed as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multivariable logistic regression models were built to identify independent associations between congestion parameters and outcomes, including variables with biological plausibility and known association with HF prognosis.

The discriminative ability of ΔVExPLUs was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, with calculation of area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value (NPV). Comparisons with changes in other individual congestion parameters were performed using DeLong’s test.

Because this was an exploratory prospective cohort evaluating a novel integrated ultrasound strategy, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. Consecutive eligible patients were enrolled during the predefined study period.

All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA), MedCalc Statistical Software version 14.8.1 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium) and R version 4.3.3 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Study population

The study included 104 patients with ADHF and LVEF < 50% (Figure 1). Mean age was 64.3 ± 13.5 years, 69.2% were male, and 69.2% were classified as haemodynamic profile B. The overall median CICU length of stay was 6.5 days (4.0; 15.5), and median hospital length of stay was 22.0 days (12.2; 39.7). Most patients had prior HF diagnosis (84.6%) and ischaemic heart disease was leading aetiology (43.3%). Common comorbidities included hypertension (67.3%), diabetes (51.0%), and chronic kidney disease (40.4%). At CICU admission, EVEREST score had a median of 8.0 (5.0, 13.0) and B-type natriuretic peptide measured 2488.4 pg/mL (1317.9, 3302.6). Overall median LVEF was 24.5% (18.0, 32.0), and 63.5% of patients exhibited signs of right ventricular (RV) dysfunction, as defined by tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) ≤ 17 mm, S’ TDI (peak systolic velocity of the tricuspid annulus by tissue Doppler imaging) ≤ 9.5 cm/s or fractional area change ≤ 35%. Severe mitral and tricuspid regurgitation were observed in 11.5% and 19.2% of patients, respectively (Table 1).

Changes in congestion markers during CICU stay

Between CICU admission and 72-h reassessment, the study population demonstrated evidence of decongestion (Table 3 and Supplementary data online, Table S1). Median weight change was −3.4 kg (−5.4, −0.6) and median EVEREST score decreased by −3.0 (−7.0, −1.0). POCUS findings reflected this improvement, with reduction in proportion of patients with VExUS grade 3 from 27,9% to 7.7% (P < 0.001), and decline in median LUS-positive zones from 6.0 (4.0, 8.0) to 4.0 (2.0, 7.0) (P < 0.001). The septal E/e′ ratio showed only slight reduction, from 17.5 (14.7, 21.9) to 17.1 (13.1, 20.2) (P < 0.001), whereas mean LVOT VTI increased from 10.9 ± 3.1 cm to 11.6 ± 3.6 cm (P = 0.033). The distribution of changes in VExUS, LUS, EVEREST, and the combined ΔVExPLUs between admission and 72-h reassessment is illustrated in Supplementary data online, Figure S1.

To categorize patients according to degree of improvement in congestion, the continuous variable ΔVExPLUs was divided into tertiles based on its distribution (Table 3). The resulting categories were: tertile 1 (ΔVExPLUs ≤ −5.0), representing greatest improvement in congestion; tertile 2 (−5.0 < ΔVExPLUs ≤ −1.0), indicating intermediate improvement; and tertile 3 (ΔVExPLUs > −1.0), reflecting unchanging or worsening. Compared to tertile 3, patients in tertile 1 had greater weight loss (−4.5 kg [−6.5, −2.4] vs. −2.2 kg [−4.6, 0.5]; P = 0.009) and larger reductions in EVEREST score (−5.0 [−9.5, −3.0] vs. −1.0 [−4.0 to 2.0]; P = 0.002). Beyond expected greater reductions in VExUS score, IVC diameter and number of LUS-positive zones in tertile 1, modest reductions in septal E/e′ ratio were also observed across tertiles, with greater decline in tertile 1 compared to tertile 3 (−2.7 [−7.3 to 0.8] vs. 0.2 [−1.0, 2.1]; P = 0.009). There were no significant differences in creatinine or lactate changes among groups. Echocardiographic parameters at baseline were mostly similar across tertiles, except for higher prevalence of severe tricuspid regurgitation in tertile 2 (P = 0.017) and lower tricuspid S′ velocity in tertile 2 compared to tertile 1 (P = 0.020).

In-hospital mortality

In-hospital mortality occurred in 27 patients (26% of the cohort) and was significantly lower among those in tertile 1 (greatest improvement in ΔVExPLUs), with a rate of 3%, compared to tertile 3 (unchanging or worsening congestion), with a rate of 46% (P < 0.001) (Figure 2 and Supplementary data online, Figure S2).

Univariable analysis showed that ΔVExPLUs ≥ 1 (representing improvement of at least one point between admission and 72-h reassessment) was associated with 28% reduction in odds of in-hospital mortality (OR: 0.72; 95% CI: 0.59, 0.87; P < 0.001) (see Supplementary data online, Figure S3). After adjustment for age, baseline serum creatinine, vasopressor use, serum sodium, and left ventricular ejection fraction, ΔVExPLUs remained independently associated within-hospital mortality (adjusted OR: 0.65; 95% CI: 0.51, 0.83; P < 0.001). Similar associations were observed forΔVExUS (adjusted OR: 0.30, 95% CI: 0.14, 0.68; P = 0.004) and ΔLUS (adjusted OR: 0.70; 95% CI: 0.54, 0.92; P = 0.010) (see Supplementary data online, Table S3).

30-day outcomes

A total of 3 (2.9%) patients were lost to follow-up. Among 32 patients who died within 30 days (30.8% of the cohort) and 42 who experienced the composite outcome of 30-day mortality or HTx/LVAD implantation (40.4%), event rates were significantly lower in those with greatest improvement in ΔVExPLUs compared to those with unchanging or worsening congestion (12% vs. 50% for 30-day mortality; 19% vs. 64% for the composite outcome; P = 0.007 and P = 0.002, respectively; Figure 2 and Supplementary data online, Figure S2). Causes of death during in-hospital and 30-day follow-up are described in Supplementary data online, Table S2, whereas Supplementary data online, Figure S4 and Figure S5 illustrate Kaplan–Meier survival curves stratified by tertiles for 30-day mortality and the composite outcome.

Consistent with these findings, greater improvement in ΔVExPLUs was associated with better 30-day outcomes in logistic regression analyses. For 30-day mortality, each one-point decrease in ΔVExPLUs corresponded to 16% reduction in odds of death in univariable analysis (OR: 0.84; 95% CI: 0.73, 0.97; P = 0.016). This association remained significant after multivariable adjustment (adjusted OR: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.65, 0.94; P = 0.009), including age, baseline creatinine, vasopressor use, serum sodium, and LVEF. Similarly, ΔVExPLUs was independently associated with lower risk of the composite outcome of 30-day mortality or HTx/LVAD implantation (adjusted OR: 0.76; 95% CI: 0.64, 0.92; P = 0.004; see Supplementary data online, Table S3).

Prognostic performance of ΔVExPLUs compared to other parameters

Across all outcomes, ΔVExPLUs showed the highest observed AUC values among the evaluated parameters (Figure 3 and Table 4). For in-hospital mortality, ΔVExPLUs achieved an AUC of 0.76 (95% CI: 0.66, 0.84), and high NPV (97.0%), while ΔVExUS (AUC: 0.70; 95% CI 0.60, 0.79) and ΔLUS (AUC: 0.65; 95% CI 0.54, 0.74) showed lower valuer. For 30-day mortality, ΔVExPLUs maintained a numerically higher AUC (0.68; 95% CI: 0.57, 0.77), with better balance between sensitivity (64.3%) and specificity (68.2%) compared to other markers. Similarly, for the composite outcome of 30-day mortality or HTx/LVAD implantation, ΔVExPLUs demonstrated the highest observed AUC (0.70; 95% CI: 0.59, 0.79), with favourable overall accuracy (69.1%) and highest NPV (74.5%). In contrast, ΔEVEREST score demonstrated the lowest AUCs across all outcomes (ranging from 0.51 to 0.55).

Discussion

ΔVExPLUs: integrated dynamic assessment of congestion: main findings

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the prognostic relevance of integrating VExUS score and LUS assessment in ADHF, applied serially over the early course of hospitalization. To operationalize this combined assessment, we developed ΔVExPLUs, defined as a weighted combination of serial changes in VExUS score and LUS [(2 × ΔVExUS) + ΔLUS]. In adjusted analysis for known clinical risk factors, a one-grade decrease in ΔVExPLUs during the first 72 h of CICU stay was independently associated with low in-hospital mortality (adjusted OR: 0.65; 95% CI: 0.51, 0.83; P < 0.001), 30-day mortality (adjusted OR: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.65, 0.94; P = 0.009), and a composite outcome of 30-day mortality or HTx/LVAD implantation (adjusted OR: 0.76; 95% CI: 0.64, 0.92; P = 0.004). The AUC of ΔVExPLUs was numerically higher than that of its individual components and ΔEVEREST score. For in-hospital mortality, ΔVExPLUs achieved an AUC of 0.76, with a sensitivity of 95.6% and a negative predictive value of 97.0%.

ΔVExPLUs and markers of decongestion

To explore the relationship between ΔVExPLUs and markers of decongestion, we categorized patients into tertiles based on its distribution. Patients with greatest improvement in ΔVExPLUs exhibited greater weight loss, larger reductions in EVEREST score, and more pronounced decreases in septal E/e′ ratio, without significant differences in creatinine change across groups. Given that body weight change is a widely used surrogate of clinical decongestion20 and EVEREST score has been proposed as a parameter to guide therapy,4 these parallel findings reinforce the construct validity of ΔVExPLUs. Moreover, the observed association with E/e′ seems to be primarily driven by the LUS component, which reflects left-sided filling pressures,21 although prior studies have also reported a relationship between VExUS score and E/e′.22 This relationship may arise from ventricular interdependence, whereby systemic venous congestion and elevated RAP restrict left ventricular filling, or from a broader reflection of overall congestion severity.23 In contrast, no association was found between ΔVExPLUs improvement and change in creatinine, which may be attributable to multifactorial mechanisms underlying renal dysfunction in ADHF and limited sensitivity of creatinine as a marker of decongestion, since mild increases during therapy are expected and not necessarily indicative of adverse prognosis.24

ΔVExPLUs and right-sided parameters

Extending this analysis to right-sided parameters, we observed that ΔVExPLUs also captured distinct congestion profiles related to RV function. Although VExUS grade 3 has been independently associated with adverse RV remodelling and severe tricuspid regurgitation,22 in our cohort the highest prevalence of severe tricuspid regurgitation and reduced tricuspid S′ velocity occurred in the intermediate improvement group rather than in those with least improvement. A plausible explanation is that this intermediate group may have included a more homogeneous subset of patients with congestion primarily driven by RV dysfunction. In such cases, although decongestive therapy could partially improve systemic venous congestion, the limited contractile reserve of the RV likely restricted the extent of improvement achievable.25 Conversely, the group with minimal improvement may have encompassed a more heterogeneous population, including patients whose limited response was related not only to RV impairment but also to non-haemodynamic factors such as hypotension, low output states, or renal dysfunction limiting diuretic use.26

ΔVExUS and ΔLUS isolated findings

Taken together, these findings illustrate that congestion patterns are heterogeneous and evolve over time, which further justifies our decision to focus on serial rather than isolated measurements. Our decision was made to reflect the dynamic nature of congestion during ADHF hospitalization, as single-timepoint assessments may fail to capture important variations in haemodynamic status and treatment response. Although evidence on the prognostic value of serial VExUS score assessment for hard clinical outcomes remains limited, prior studies have shown that early changes were linked to lower in-hospital mortality,13 predicted major adverse kidney events,27 and correlated with AKI resolution in CRS.18 In our study, ΔVExUS demonstrated moderate discriminative ability for in-hospital mortality (AUC 0.70), in line with previously reported values for VExUS score, while adding the advantage of capturing dynamic changes over time,28–30 except for one study in ADHF patients that incorporated multi-organ ultrasound evaluation and reported substantially higher AUC value (0.88) for VExUS grade 3 regarding in-hospital mortality.31 This discrepancy may reflect methodological differences, as that study focused on the most severe grade of VExUS. In contrast, our analysis captured the full range of VExUS score variation over time, emphasizing dynamic changes rather than extreme congestion, which may have diluted the discriminatory power but provided a more comprehensive picture of evolving haemodynamic status.

In relation to LUS, substantial evidence in patients with ADHF indicates that improvements in B-line count are associated with lower risk of adverse outcomes32,33 and that residual congestion on LUS at time of discharge identifies those at high risk for decompensation34–36. In the present study, ΔLUS demonstrated a moderate discriminative ability for in-hospital mortality (AUC 0.65), which was comparable to findings from a multicentre study including patients with both reduced and preserved LVEF, where the per cent change in B-lines between discharge and admission yielded an AUC of 0.67 regarding cardiovascular death and rehospitalization within 6 months.37 By contrast, higher discrimination (AUC 0.81) has been reported to predict 30-day death or ADHF readmission when ΔLUS was combined with RV function parameters such as TAPSE,38 suggesting that integrative approaches may enhance prognostic accuracy.

ΔVExPLUs prognostic performance and the value of a combined ultrasound approach

In line with this concept, in our cohort ΔVExPLUs demonstrated the highest observed AUCs for in-hospital and 30-day outcomes compared to changes in individual components and EVEREST score. This more favourable prognostic performance of ΔVExPLUs may be explained by its capacity to integrate complementary haemodynamic domains that can evolve independently during decongestive therapy, where improvement in one does not necessarily parallel improvement in other. Importantly, the concept of integrating systemic and pulmonary ultrasound parameters is not new. The combination of LUS and cardiac ultrasound has demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy for identifying ADHF in patients presenting with acute dyspnoea.39 In a complementary way, the CaTUS approach, which integrates LUS with E/e′ ratio and IVC assessment, has been associated with greater decongestion and shorter hospitalizations without an increase in adverse events.33 Recently, Scherer et al.40 proposed the dynamic ultrasound congestion score (DUCS), integrating modified VExUS and LUS into a structured congestion score for patients with ADHF, demonstrating prognostic associations with in-hospital and short-term outcomes. In contrast to DUCS, which was designed as a composite congestion score incorporating categorical weighting, our study focused specifically on the prognostic relevance of serial temporal changes in congestion parameters through a simplified continuous integration strategy (ΔVExPLUs). This approach was intended to emphasize the dynamic evolution of congestion during hospitalization rather than static congestion burden alone.

Although ΔVExPLUs showed the highest AUCs, its overall discriminative ability remained moderate. Its main prognostic value appears to be related to its high sensitivity and NPV, which may be particularly useful for identifying low-risk patients. This suggests that while integrating complementary ultrasound domains can enhance risk stratification, these tools should not replace clinical evaluation, but rather be used in conjunction with it. However, ΔEVEREST score demonstrated the lowest AUCs, reinforcing that clinical assessment alone also may be insufficient and that combining it with objective imaging measures could offer incremental prognostic value in ADHF.

Clinical implications and future directions

While previous studies evaluating LUS or VExUS score in isolation have demonstrated prognostic value, our results suggest that a dual-domain approach incorporating serial assessment, as captured by ΔVExPLUs, can enhance early risk identification, particularly during the initial phase of hospitalization, when congestion is most dynamic and potentially modifiable through timely therapeutic interventions. In clinical practice, this strategy could complement established risk assessment tools, informing decisions on monitoring intensity, discharge planning, and individualized decongestive therapy. Future research should aim to validate these findings in larger, multicentre cohorts and across diverse HF phenotypes and care settings. In addition, it will be important to determine whether management strategies guided by the serial integration of VExUS score and LUS can influence therapeutic decision-making and ultimately translate into improved patient outcomes through randomized controlled trials.

Limitations

Some methodological aspects of our study deserve consideration. Because it was conducted at a single tertiary care centre with a dedicated CICU, the study population likely represented a higher-acuity subset of ADHF patients with greater haemodynamic instability and congestion burden, which may have introduced spectrum bias and limited the generalizability of the findings to lower-risk patients managed in general wards or outpatient settings. In addition, the initial POCUS assessment was performed within 24 h of CICU admission, during which therapeutic interventions, such as diuretics, vasodilators, or ventilatory support could already have modified congestion findings and introduced variability in baseline measurements. The relatively small sample size and absence of a formal sample size calculation may also have reduced statistical power, particularly for subgroup analyses, and may have contributed to the lack of statistical significance in most DeLong’s test comparisons between AUCs. Although POCUS assessments were performed by trained operators using standardized protocols, the reproducibility of this approach in centres with less ultrasound expertise remains uncertain. Moreover, the study population comprised both patients with acute decompensation of chronic HF and those with de novo HF, a heterogeneity that may have influenced lung ultrasound findings, given that advanced HF patients can present with elevated filling pressures in the absence of detectable interstitial pulmonary congestion on LUS.41 In addition, the analysis was also restricted to early changes within the first 72 h of admission, and therefore the prognostic value of combining VExUS score with LUS beyond this timeframe remains unknown. Finally, the observational design precludes causal inference and is subject to potential residual confounding despite multivariable adjustment. Nevertheless, our findings expand the current evidence supporting integrated ultrasound assessment in ADHF by emphasizing the prognostic relevance of serial changes in systemic and pulmonary congestion during hospitalization.

Conclusions

The combined temporal changes in VExUS score and LUS, as captured by ΔVExPLUs, were independently associated with both in-hospital and 30-day outcomes in patients with ADHF. Across the evaluated outcomes, ΔVExPLUs demonstrated favourable prognostic performance when compared with changes in the individual components and EVEREST score. These findings emphasize the complementary nature of VExUS score and LUS in capturing distinct yet interrelated aspects of congestion during hospitalization. Therefore, serial integration of systemic and pulmonary ultrasound parameters has the potential to improve risk stratification during ADHF hospitalization.
