Nutritional status and outcomes throughout the intestinal transplant journey: Experience from the Cambridge Intestinal Transplant Unit
Authors: Pinal S. Patel, Emma McKenzie, Rebecca Maddison, Htar H. Hlaing, Irum Amin, Neil Russell, Andrew J. Butler, Jeremy M. Woodward, Dunecan CO Massey, Lisa M. Sharkey, Charlotte S. Rutter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGuidance on the optimum nutritional management of patients receiving intestinal transplants (ITx) is limited. We aimed to i) describe the nutritional management of adult ITx patients in the immediate post-ITx period and ii) examine nutritional status using BMI across the ITx journey.MethodsA retrospective analysis included all adult-recipients of an intestine-containing graft at Addenbrooke’s Hospital, Cambridge between 2007 and 2024. Data collected included nutritional routes immediately post-ITx (parenteral nutrition-PN, enteral nutrition-EN and oral feeding-OF) and BMI at ITx and 1 yr post-ITx. Associations between baseline BMI (at-ITx) and 1 yr post-transplant (1yr-ITx) were examined using logistic-regression for the risk of being overweight-obese at 1yr-Tx (BMI:<25 vs. ≥25 kg/m2).Results166 patients (median age 45 yrs (IQR: 34–53), and 49% female) were included in the study. Duration of PN, EN, and time to OF post-ITx was 24 days (IQR: 14–35); 38days (IQR: 15–82), 13 days (IQR: 9–24), respectively. Median weight loss 3 months post-ITx was 9% of Tx-weight, reducing to 2% at 6 months. In logistic-regression, baseline-BMI (<25 kg/m2 vs. ≥25 kg/m2) predicted 1yr-BMI (<25 kg/m2 vs. ≥25 kg/m2) [OR:4.9, 95%CI:1.88-12.77], in adjusted analyses. 24% patients with a healthy baseline-BMI became overweight or obese at 1yr-Tx (P < 0.0001).ConclusionOur study shows that i) weight loss occurs within the first 3 months post-ITx, though mostly re-gained within 6months; ii) post-MVTx oral diet is initiated by 2 weeks; and iii) baseline BMI is the only significant predictor of 1 yr post-ITx BMI. However, 1 in 4 ITx patients with a healthy baseline BMI were observed to become overweight or obese at 1 yr. Further research is required to elucidate what mechanisms lead to the development of obesity after transplant.

Introduction

Intestine-containing transplants (ITx) are one of the most recent solid-organ transplant surgeries. The indications for ITx include irreversible intestinal failure (IF) with complications of parenteral nutrition; the need for extensive abdominal evisceration; the need for multi-organ transplantation; and acute widespread mesenteric ischaemia [1]. This is especially noteworthy given that the incidence of chronic intestinal failure (CIF) is rising, and national reports suggest an annual increase of 20% in home parenteral support registrations [2].

The 5 yr survival of ITx is relatively on par with the 5 yr survival of patients with CIF requiring life-long parenteral nutrition. In the USA, from 2016 to 2018, adult recipients of ITx without a liver had an overall survival rate of 89.8% at 1 yr and 62.5% at 5 yrs [3]. Whilst in the UK, from 2015 to 2025, adult recipients of ITx without a liver and a non-malignant indication, had an overall unadjusted survival rate of 88.6% at 1 yr and 79.3% at 5 yrs [4]. A UK IF referral centre reported a 5 yr survival of 69.3% in n = 840 non-malignant CIF patients between 1978 and 2018 [5]. Similar rates were also observed in a multicentre study in Europe and USA of n = 472 CIF patients of a 5 yr survival of 64% [6].

Given that ITx are one of the more recent solid-organ transplants there is limited data on nutritional outcomes post-ITx. A recent review by Dowhan and colleagues [7], discusses the clinical protocols used to manage ITx recipients pre- and post-transplant. They report on previous USA based studies in the immediate post-ITx period, showing that patients tend to start oral or enteral feeding within 14days of ITx and are usually on PN for more than 28 days [8], [9].

One of the indicators of successful ITx is nutritional autonomy. Rovera and colleagues (2003), in the USA have shown that in the immediate post-transplant period nutritional autonomy was achieved sooner with intestine-only grafts compared to those with stomach-containing grafts [8]. The authors also showed that 55% of patients achieved full nutritional autonomy at 1 yr post ITx [8]. Another USA based study reported nutritional autonomy rates of 63% at 1 yr and 78% at 5 yrs [9]. Similar rates have also been shown at a single UK centre of adult ITx recipients between 2006 and 2016, which showed that by the end of the study period (January, 2017), 74% of patients achieved nutritional autonomy [10]. Differences in the reported rates of nutritional autonomy may be due to time (i.e. improvements in transplant management), differences in clinical care across institutions, and/or nutritional management.

Several conference proceedings have also reported on nutritional status post-transplant. In a UK single centre, between 1991 and 2013, body weight reduced by 25% at 1 yr post-Tx [11]. Data from the same UK centre, between 2007 and 2013, showed that over a median of 26 month follow-up post ITx, a maximum mean weight loss of 16.6% was observed from pre-transplant weight [12].

We aimed to describe the nutritional feeding routes of adult ITx recipients in the immediate post-transplant period and nutritional status changes from ITx to 5 yrs follow-up post ITx.

Material and methods

Setting and sample

The Cambridge Intestinal Rehabilitation and Transplant unit in Cambridge, UK, is one of two adult ITx centres in the UK conducting intestine-containing transplants nationally and internationally in Europe. All ITx candidates in the UK are discussed at a national forum to ensure all aspects of transplant and non-transplant options are considered, including nutritional assessment. We conducted a retrospective analysis of all consecutive recipients of an intestine-containing graft from 2007 to 31st December 2024.

Data collection

Data was collected from electronic patient records and internal databases. Patient characteristics included: age at transplant, sex, indication for ITx, graft types (small bowel-SB, liver small bowel- LSB, multi-visceral-MVT, modified multi-visceral-MMVT) and length of stay.

Nutrition at assessment included: weight, BMI, length of time on PN prior to assessment, recommendations made to the local nutrition team managing the patient.

Nutrition post-transplant included: lowest weight at 3 months, weight and BMI at 6 months, nutritional feeding routes post ITx (parenteral nutrition-PN, enteral nutrition-EN, oral feeding), if PN was required again post-ITx and reasons for resuming PN.

Weight, BMI and additional anthropometrics were collected yearly from year 1 to year 5 post ITx, if available.

Additional anthropometrics included: mid upper arm circumference (MUAC), tricep skinfolds (TSF), and mid upper arm muscle circumference (MAMC); derived from the USA National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data [13], [14]. In addition, hand grip strength (HGS, kg) was collected and corresponding percentage of the referent patients age group was calculated. The reference centiles used for HGS were based on 12 UK studies [14], [15], [16].

Statistical analysis

For analyses related to baseline characteristics, nutritional assessment pre-transplant, and feeding routes in the immediate post-transplant period was based in the whole cohort (n = 166). For analyses examining nutritional status from transplant to 5 yr follow-up, patients who died within 6 months of the ITx (n = 20) were excluded from the cohort. Data are described using means (SD) for parametric data, medians (IQR) for non-parametric data, and frequencies (%) for categorical variables.

Associations between BMI at-transplant and 1 yr post-transplant (1yr-ITx) were examined using Chi-squared test. A pearson correlation was also conducted to describe the relationship between BMI at ITx and at 1 yr follow-up. Key baseline characteristics were also examined by BMI categories at transplant with differences tested using Kruskal-Wallis for non-parametric variables and Chi-squared test for categorical variables. We also examined BMI at assessment with BMI at 1 yr post ITx. Differences between these two time points was conducted using the Wilcoxon sign-rank test.

The outcome of becoming overweight or obese post ITx was chosen as this was a clinical observation that some patients were increasing BMI at 1 yr post ITx. In addition, a healthy BMI post Tx would be a successful outcome to evaluate. Logistic regression was used to assess the association between weight status at Tx (BMI: <25 vs. ≥ 25kg/m2) and the odds of being overweight or obese at 1yr-Tx in crude and adjusted analyses. The adjusted model(s) included the following confounders: age, sex, graft type and indication for transplant. The confounders were chosen based on an exploratory basis given the limited literature on factors associated with ITx and BMI.

All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA-IC software version 15 [TexasCorp, USA]. Analyses were considered statistically significant with a P-value < 0.05.

This work was registered as a service evaluation with the Cambridge University Hospitals patient outcomes team (ID: 7031).

Results

Baseline characteristics of the cohort (Table 1)

The patient cohort consisted of 166 ITx patients, with median age 45 yrs (IQR:34–53) of whom 49% were female. The underlying pathology of patients requiring ITx was mainly short bowel syndrome (54%) and non-malignant tumour (12%). The graft types included: small bowel (35%), small bowel-liver (21%), multi-visceral (32%) and modified multi-visceral (12%).

Nutrition status and anthropometrics at assessment (Table 1)

On assessment, general markers of nutritional status showed that patients were within a healthy BMI (median BMI was 21.6 (19.7–24)). Detailed anthropometrics showed that 46.7–63.5% of patients had a MUAC, TSF and MMAC of < 25th percentile. The median hand grip strength was 68.2% of the normal range. 85% of ITx patients were on PN pre-ITx. Time on PN at assessment ranged from 1month to 28 yrs. For patients at assessment, 84% did not require any recommendation to adjust their nutritional support,

Nutritional feeding routes post-transplant (Table 1, Table 2)

There was a median 263 days (IQR: 120–505) from nutritional assessment to transplant.

Duration of PN post-ITx was 24 days (IQR: 14–35). Duration of EN post-ITx was 38 days (IQR: 15-82), and time to oral feeding was 13 days (IQR: 9–24). Time to EN initiation in those who could not eat was 8 days (IQR: 5–13) in n = 45 patients. Stomach-containing ITx took longer to resume oral feeding (Fig. 1). Those who had routinely placed jejunostomy-feeding tubes during ITx were on PN for a shorter duration post-ITx than those who did not [18 days vs. 26 days, P < 0.01] (Table 2). The median maximum weight loss at 3 months post-ITx was 9.4% of Tx-weight, which reduced to 1.8% at 6months post-ITx. BMI trajectory post-transplant

Baseline median BMI was 22.3 kg/m2 which increased to 23 kg/m2 at 1 yr-Tx and plateaued in the next 4 yrs (Fig. 2, Table 3). There was a borderline statistically significant increase in BMI at assessment [BMI: 21.6 kg/m2] to 5 yr post-ITx [23.5 kg/m2], P = 0.056. 24% patients who had a healthy baseline BMI became overweight or obese at 1yr-Tx (P < 0.0001), (Table 4). BMI at transplant categories were as follows: underweight (9%), healthy (68%), overweight (15%), and obese (8%). Only age at transplant and hand grip strength at assessment were different by BMI categories at 1 yr-Tx. Those with a BMI > 25 kg/m2 were more likely to be older and have a higher hand grip strength (Supplementary table 1). Discussion

Summary of findings

To our knowledge this is the largest known cohort evaluating nutritional status and outcomes of adult patients receiving intestinal transplants. Our main findings are that on assessment, pre-ITx patients have low muscle mass stores but functional capacity is above average, and patients have been nutritionally optimised within the remits of their clinical condition. In the immediate post-transplant period, we observe that patients are on PN for approximately 3 weeks and resume oral intake by 2 weeks. As would be expected, patients with stomach containing grafts take longer to establish oral or enteral intake. Across the whole ITx journey, BMI at transplant is predictive of BMI at 1 yr follow-up. However, there is a small proportion of patients with a healthy BMI at transplant who become overweight or obese at 1 yr follow-up which corroborates clinical observation.

Post-transplant feeding

We have observed that the median time on PN post ITx is 24 days and patients start eating solid food by day 13, which is within the range that is reported by other centres [8], [9]. There was a statistically significant observation that patients who have jejunostomy feeding tubes placed during ITx require PN for a lower duration of time in their ITx post-operative period. This may support resuming gut function sooner and reducing IV access than if a tube was not placed. This is an important factor given that some patients are transplanted due to lack of venous access. Stomach containing grafts were found to require parenteral nutrition for longer than non-stomach containing grafts, suggesting that if feeding tubes were going to be inserted at transplant then this would be the cohort that may benefit from weaning off parenteral nutrition sooner.

As mentioned previously, the median weight loss that occurs in the immediate post ITx period has reduced over time in our centre (from 25% to 9%) and this likely reflects improvements in clinical management including nutritional care over time, but also different time points used across analyses. The immediate ITx period involves a highly catabolic state and even with sufficient artificial nutrition support, patients can experience weight loss, in the form of both fat and muscle mass. However, by 6 months post ITx, we observed that weight is mostly regained. Studies in other related Tx cohorts, for example, have shown that approximately 50% of post liver transplant patients lose 10% of their body weight by 1 month post Tx [17].

With regards to nutritional autonomy, of all surviving ITx patients at the end of the study period, we found that only 2 patients required parenteral nutrition at 1 yr post-Tx.

Nutritional status throughout the transplant journey

Most intestinal rehabilitation centres managing transplant candidates who proceed to ITx are managing patients appropriately and 84% do not require adjustments to their artificial nutrition. This is reflected in part by the fact that the median BMI on assessment pre-ITx was 21.6 kg/m2 and BMI at ITx was 22.3 kg/m2. Whilst we show that body composition is not optimal at assessment (with 84% of patients having a mid-arm muscle circumference less than the 50th percentile for the reference range), functional nutritional status measured by hand grip strength was modestly good. This may reflect that patients are nutritionally optimised within the remits of their clinical condition.

In regression analyses we have shown that the most significant factor predicting being overweight or obese 1 yr after ITx is being overweight or obese at the time of transplant. No other factors such as indication for transplant or graft type were shown to have an influence on BMI at 1 yr post ITx. There is, however, a small group (24%) of patients with a healthy BMI at baseline who became overweight or obese 1 yr post ITx. Unmeasured factors that may be plausible explanations for this weight gain could be changes in the gut hormone profile or gut microbiome from the donor or obesity prior to illness (e.g., patients who had previous bariatric surgery).

Strengths and limitations

This is the largest UK-based adult cohort examining nutritional management in the immediate post-operative period and nutritional status across the ITx journey over a 5 yr follow-up. Our study informs current clinical practice in the field of ITx which could be used collaboratively to increase statistical power in future studies. Limitations also warrant discussion. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, there was missing information in anthropometric data particularly for the beginning of the study period and during the COVID pandemic. There was also variation in time between each follow-up period as clinic appointments were not precisely at yearly follow-ups but within 3 months of the 1 yr mark. We believe that this variation is unlikely to affect the findings of our study.

Implications for clinical practice

The observations from our study are informative for transplant candidates and as such can provide the candidate with information regarding feeding routes in the immediate post-ITx period. This is noteworthy as the majority of patients proceeding to ITx are on parenteral nutrition with the expectation of nutritional autonomy post ITx. The implications of being overweight or obesity post-transplant are significant given that transplant patients are at a heightened metabolic risk from immunosuppression [18], which if combined with obesity could increase this risk further. In addition to metabolic risk, other organs may also be affected. For example, immunosuppression is known to be nephrotoxic [19] which when compounded with metabolic disease may lead to further renal decline. Increased mortality has also been observed in transplant patients who become obese post-transplant. In the liver transplant cohort, obesity post-transplant has been associated with a 2-fold increase in mortality [20]. In theory, obesity may increase morbidity and mortality in the ITx cohort. Whilst the finding in our study that 1 in 4 ITx patients with a baseline healthy BMI progress to an overweight or obese BMI post ITx was an observation, it merits monitoring in the long term within and between centres. It is useful for the ITx patient to have an awareness that once they are stable post ITx and nutritionally optimised, the focus should be on maintaining a healthy diet and lifestyle. Future research should explore metabolic risk factors such as lipid profile, glucose control and hypertension, pre- and post- transplant.

Conclusions

Our study shows that i) weight loss occurs within the first 3 months post-ITx, though this is mostly re-gained within 6 months; ii) the trajectory of nutritional routes post-ITx is of an approximate 3 week period on PN with oral diet being initiated by day 13; and iii) baseline BMI is the only significant predictor of 1 yr post-ITx BMI. However, 1 in 4 ITx patients with a healthy baseline BMI were observed to become overweight or obese at 1 yr. Further research is required to elucidate what mechanisms lead to development of overweight or obese after transplant.
