Comparative effects of e-cigarette smoking on periodontal status, salivary pH, and cotinine levels
Authors: Nurul Wahida Mohd Hasan, Badiah Baharin, Nurulhuda Mohd, Mariati Abdul Rahman, Nooraryana Hassan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe nicotine in e-cigarette liquid can negatively impact periodontal tissues by altering the salivary pH and elevating cotinine levels. Thus, the study aimed to determine the periodontal parameters, salivary pH, and cotinine levels among cigarette, e-cigarette, and never-smokers.MethodsA total of 144 participants were recruited (48 cigarette smokers, 48 e-cigarette smokers, and 48 never-smokers). Clinical periodontal parameters, including plaque index (PI), gingival index (GI), periodontal probing pocket depth (PPD), and clinical attachment loss (CAL) were recorded, excluding third molars. The level of unstimulated whole salivary pH was measured using a portable pH meter and the levels of salivary cotinine were measured using Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA).ResultsData were analysed statistically using analysis of variance. Mean scores of PPD, percentage of pocket depth ≥ 4 mm, and CAL (p < 0.05) were significantly higher among cigarette smokers than those in e-cigarette and never-smokers, while GI (p < 0.05) were significantly higher among e-cigarette smokers. The unstimulated salivary pH was more acidic among cigarette smokers (p < 0.05) and e-cigarette smokers (p < 0.05) than in never-smokers. The cotinine levels were higher among cigarette smokers (p < 0.05) and e-cigarette smokers (p < 0.05) than in never-smokers.ConclusionsClinical periodontal parameters were poorer in cigarette smokers than in e-cigarette smokers and never-smokers. Meanwhile, cigarette and e-cigarette smokers have more acidic salivary pH and higher cotinine levels than in never-smokers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-04650-7.Keywords: Periodontal diseases, Smoking, Vaping, Cigarette smoking, Cotinine, Saliva

Background

The emergence of electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes) represents a trending shift in smoking habits, yet the conclusive evidence concerning their effects on periodontal health remains scant when compared with tobacco smoking. These electronic nicotine delivery systems function by heating an e-liquid that contains diverse nicotine concentrations and flavourings, producing an aerosol simulating the experience of tobacco smoke [1]. E-cigarette smoking is associated with pernicious effects on general health, such as e-cigarette product use-associated lung injury (EVALI) [2] and to oral health [3]. Moreover, it has been promoted as a smoking cessation aid and perceived as safer than cigarette smoking [4] though the actual effect is unknown. E-liquids from e-cigarettes have been reported to induce oxidative str ess, it was revealed by the laboratory study that the e-liquids used in e-cigarettes resulted in higher production of inflammatory substances in epithelial cells and the fibroblast [5]. Though previous clinical studies reported a notable rise in probing depth among cigarette smokers compared to e-cigarette smokers and non-smokers [6–8] caution is warranted in interpreting these findings. The probable explanations revolved around the extended exposure to harmful substances, typically seen in older cigarette smokers (average age of 42 years), as opposed to e-cigarette smokers (average age of 28 years) [6–8].

Elevated cotinine levels and decreased salivary pH have been reported by numerous studies among cigarette smoker [9–12]. Salivary pH is commonly associated with an acidic environment of the oral cavity among individuals that smoke cigarettes [9, 13, 14] and also those with periodontitis [9, 15]. Cotinine, the nicotine metabolite, exhibits an extended serum elimination half-life of approximately 19 h and remains at a constant concentration in saliva; hence, it can be a reliable method to assess nicotine exposure in smokers. Previous studies have reported significant levels of cotinine in the saliva of e-cigarette smokers [16, 17], and salivary cotinine as a biomarker of nicotine exposure was reported to be significantly correlated with the frequency and dependence of e-cigarette smoking [18].

This study will contribute to the discrepancies and insufficient data in existing clinical and laboratory evidence to fully comprehend the effects of e-cigarettes on periodontal health. To the authors’ knowledge, there are no studies in the indexed literature that have compared the whole salivary cotinine and pH levels among cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers, and never-smokers. The present study is based on three hypotheses: (a) clinical periodontal parameters are poorer in cigarette and e-cigarette smokers than in never-smokers; (b) salivary pH levels are significantly higher in cigarette and e-cigarette smokers than in never-smokers; and (c) salivary cotinine levels are significantly higher in cigarette and e-cigarette smokers than in never-smokers. The present cross-sectional study aimed to compare clinical periodontal status, salivary pH, and cotinine levels in cigarette smokers, e-cigarette smokers, and never-smokers.

Methods

Ethical approval

The study was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee UKM Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia (UKM PPI/111/8/JEP-2020–615) and the Medical Research and Ethics Committee (MREC) (NMRR-20–1848-56186 (IIR)). Researchers have adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were given the option to withdraw from the study at any stage of the research.

Participants and grouping

Participants were recruited from government dental clinics by convenience sampling. The enrolment of male participants only was undertaken in this study [6, 7] to prevent potential bias in the assessment of periodontal parameters given the influence of hormonal fluctuations mainly endogenous oestrogen and progesterone among females that associated with periodontitis [19]. Individuals who reported smoking cigarettes, those who used electronic cigarettes, and those who had never smoked were included. Cigarette smokers were defined as individuals who have smoked five or more cigarettes per day for at least a year [6]. E-cigarette smokers were defined as individuals who have only smoked e-cigarettes for at least a year [6]. Never-smokers were defined as individuals who never smoked tobacco or used smokeless tobacco products [6].

Exclusion criteria

Dual smokers were those who smoke both tobacco and electronic cigarettes; Individuals with self-reported systemic illnesses, such as diabetes mellitus, heart conditions, liver problems, and renal conditions, individuals that reported to have used antibiotics, probiotics, steroids, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs within the past one month and third molars.

Questionnaire

A self-administered questionnaire translated into Malay language and the information regarding age, duration for cigarette and e-cigarette smoking, daily frequency and duration of cigarette and e-cigarette smoking were collected [20]. Trained research assistant (IAM) administered the questionnaire to all participants. The questionnaire underwent validation via a pilot study and refinement was carried out prior to data collection, involving 24 participants.

Patient involvement

Participants in this study were not involved in the research design, execution, reporting, and dissemination plans.

Sample size

The sample size was estimated using a formula of sample size calculation of single proportion test [21]. The prevalence was based on a pilot study with the same primary outcomes. The predicted sample size (n) was a total of 144 individuals and the expected prevalence of periodontal disease among e-cigarette smokers in Malaysia (in the proportion of one, 20.8%), based on a pilot study conducted before the actual research was conducted. It was estimated that at least 144 individuals would suffice in detecting a 0.05 difference in clinical periodontal parameters, salivary pH, and cotinine levels, where it would yield a study power of 80%, p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Assessment of periodontal parameters

The clinical examinations were performed by a trained and calibrated examiner (NWH) who was blinded to the study groups. An intra-examiner calibration was performed, yielding a kappa value of 0.9. The parameters of PI [22], GI [23], CAL, and PPD were measured at mesiobuccal, midbuccal, distobuccal, distopalatal/distolingual, midpalatal/midlingual, and mesiopalatal/mesiolingual which gave six different sites per tooth on both the maxillary and mandibular teeth. Plaque was stained on all teeth using an erythrosine disclosing tablet. The participants were instructed to rinse after all tooth surfaces were stained and scoring of plaque index was done. The participants were instructed to rinse after all tooth surfaces were stained and scoring of PI was done [22]. The PI was calculated by noting both mineralised deposits and soft debris on all dental surfaces other than the third molar [22]. The dental surfaces were each assigned a score between zero and three. Meanwhile, GI also was recorded with a score that was the same as the PI but with different score criteria [23]. The PPD was assessed to the nearest millimetre using the manual periodontal probe PCPUNC15 (Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA). Missing teeth were those that were fully impacted, broken, or had retained roots. The number of missing teeth was noted.

Collection of unstimulated saliva samples

The participants were directed to present themselves during the early morning period (between 7:30 a.m. and 8:30 a.m.) in a state of abstention from food for the collection of saliva specimens, where they were also instructed to refrain from cigarette and e-cigarette smoking the night before (approximately 10 h without nicotine exposure) [12]. The participants were instructed to refrain from brushing their teeth for at least 60 min before saliva collection [12]. They were also asked to refrain from alcohol for the previous 12 h, as it may affect the saliva composition [24]. Foods that were acidic or rich in sugar may affect the outcome of the cotinine test by reducing the pH of the sample and affecting bacterial growth. Participants were instructed to rinse with plain water before saliva collection to clear off food debris and waited for 10 min before starting sample collection to prevent sample dilution [13]. The participant was asked not to talk or swallow during the saliva collection [12]. All saliva samples were transferred to disposable Eppendorf tubes within 15 min of collection and frozen at − 80 °C until further analysis.

Salivary pH measurement

The saliva sample (1 ml) was analysed for salivary pH immediately after collection using a standard portable pH metre MW100 (Milwaukee Instruments, NC, USA). This is because the accuracy of pH measurements depends on the time interval after collection and analysis due to the continuous loss of carbon dioxide from saliva once it is exposed to air [25]. The portable pH meter was calibrated at pH 7 and pH 4 each time, following all manufacturer’s guidelines. According to Kazakov et al., systemic diseases, oral cancer, radiotherapy, medications, and diet all influence the pH measurement; thus, those present with the abovementioned parameters were excluded in this study [26].

Assessment of whole salivary cotinine level

A competitive enzyme immunoassay kit (Salimetrics, PA, USA) was used to assess the levels of cotinine. The saliva samples were defrosted, brought to room temperature, vortexed, and centrifuged at 1500 × g for 15 min. The saliva smoker samples were diluted 20 × . The sample from the never-smoker required no dilution. A multichannel pipette was used to add 100 μL of diluted Conjugate Solution as well as 100 μL of cotinine antiserum into the wells according to plate layout. The assay plate was mixed at 500 rpm for 1.5 h at 37 degrees Celsius in a warmed microplate incubator/shaker (IKA Shakers, Staufen, Germany). After washing, 200 μL of TMB Substrate Solution was pipetted to all wells. The Stop Solution was then added, and shaking was done for three minutes at 500 rpm on a plate rotator. Within 10 min after the colour of every well had become yellow, the optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA). Cotinine levels were biochemically calculated as the mean value of triplicate readings and the average optical density (OD). The four-parameter logistic curve fit was generated using MyAssays.com online software to obtain the standard curve and cotinine levels.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using computer-based software (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), Version 26 for Windows, IBM, Chicago, IL., USA). Qualitative data were expressed as numbers and percentages (%) and were analysed using the chi-square test for comparisons between groups. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test and box plots. Quantitative data conforming to a normal distribution were presented as the mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for the intergroup comparisons of clinical parameters (PI, GI, PPD, and CAL), salivary pH, and cotinine levels. For multiple comparisons, the Bonferroni post-hoc correction test was used. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The current cross-sectional observational study was structured from three hypotheses. Following the initial hypothesis, it was postulated that clinical periodontal parameters are poorer in cigarette and e-cigarette smokers than among never-smokers. It was also hypothesised that salivary pH levels are significantly higher in cigarette and e-cigarette smokers than in never-smokers. Salivary cotinine levels are hypothesised to be significantly higher in cigarette and e-cigarette smokers than in never-smokers. The findings presented partially align with the formulated hypothesis, revealing significant elevations in clinical periodontal parameters, such as probing pocket depth (PPD), clinical attachment level (CAL), and the percentage of pockets ≥ 4 mm, alongside higher salivary cotinine levels among cigarette and e-cigarette smokers in comparison to never-smokers. Moreover, the salivary pH exhibited significantly more acidic among the cigarette-smoking group. The present results are in accordance with the previous studies that confirmed similar findings [7, 8, 27]. One of the explanations for this outcome is that, as reported by an in-vitro study tobacco smoking exacerbates oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in the periodontal tissues [28, 29]. The current study also found that there was no statistically significant difference in PPD, CAL, and percentage of pockets ≥ 4 mm in e-cigarette smokers compared to never-smokers, which is in agreement with the previous study [7]. Nevertheless, it is interesting to note that the mean value of clinical periodontal parameters increased in e-cigarette smokers compared to never-smokers.

However, the results should be interpreted with caution as they are due to the history of cigarette smoking. Bleeding on probing is a classic indicator of disease progression. In the current study, it was observed that the gingival index was masked in people who smoke cigarettes and e-cigarette smokers compared with never-smokers. This could be explained by the in-vivo study of nicotine in cigarette smoke and e-cigarette smoke, has the ability to constrict the blood vessels in the gingival tissue [29].

The result of the present study showed that the pH value of saliva was significantly more acidic in cigarette smokers compared to non-smokers. Similar results have also been reported in previous studies [12, 30]. This is probably due to the fact that cigarette smoking is often associated with hyposalivation and a thicker serous salivary consistency [31], which leads to a reduction in the basal salivary flow rate, so that the reduced bicarbonate production contributes to the lowering of salivary pH [31]. Another explanation could be that salivary proteins, such as lactate dehydrogenase, salivary amylase [32], and acid phosphatase [33] are impaired in their ability to function as enzymes by smoking. The activity of salivary proteins decreases significantly after exposure to cigarette smoke [32]. The mean salivary pH in of e-cigarette smokers is more acidic than that of non-smokers, probably because the chemicals in e-cigarette vapour, such as aldehydes, cause a change in the physicochemical properties of saliva [33].

Salivary cotinine is a reliable biomarker for the validation of nicotine exposure compared to self-reported tobacco use [34, 35]. In the current study, e-cigarette smokers had high cotinine levels (349.56 ± 239.04 ng/ml), which is comparable to the results of other studies by Mokeem et al. [7] and Etter et al. [16], in which the mean cotinine levels were 221.6 ng/ml and 373 ng/ml, respectively. Compared to the current study, in which the mean smoking duration was four years (4.15 ± 2.76), lower mean salivary cotinine levels (81.1 ng/ml) were reported in an earlier study [17]. This could be due to the fact that the subjects may have been younger and had used e-cigarettes for shorter periods of up to three months. Compared to e-cigarette smokers, who vape almost exclusively on a daily basis, the average daily cigarette consumption in the cigarette smoker group was more than half a pack. According to an earlier study, the majority of daily e-cigarette smokers, and dual smokers, had high salivary cotinine levels of over 300 ng/ml [36, 37]. The mean cigarette consumption per day in the group of cigarette smokers in the present study was more than half a pack per day while almost all of the e-cigarette smokers vaped daily. According to the previous study, the duration of e-cigarette use was found to be the main factor associated with levels of cotinine in saliva [17]. The elevated levels of cotinine among smokers of cigarettes and e-cigarettes in this study may have been caused by factors, including exposure to second-hand smoke from acquaintances or a spouse [34], and the discrepancy between the nicotine labelling on e-liquid and the actual liquid [36, 37].

Cotinine levels in saliva have been reported to increase with high nicotine concentrations in e-liquids, and significant cotinine levels have also been found in nicotine-free e-liquids [17]. In this study, similarly high salivary cotinine was found which could indicate high nicotine levels in the e-liquids sold. The benefits of e-cigarettes as a smoking cessation aid remain unknown until strict regulations are conducted. In line with the increased nicotine concentration in the e-liquids, increasing and high cotinine levels in saliva were observed [17], and high cotinine levels in saliva indicate a potentially high cotinine content in e-liquids sold on the local market. These findings refute the assumption that e-cigarette smoking is less harmful than cigarette smoking, and its effectiveness as a smoking cessation aid is uncertain until strict regulation is enforced.

This research has some limitations. First, the inclusion of e-cigarette smokers who have smoked in the past would have some impact on periodontal tissue damage from previous diseases. Population studies among adolescents without a history of e-cigarette smoking are needed to further evaluate the impact of e-cigarette use at a population level. These studies should take into account that each e-cigarette smoker has different preferences in product choice. Secondly, this study is prone to selection bias, which makes it impossible for the results to be more interventional. Nevertheless, the current observational study provided insightful information that complements the available information on e-cigarette use in Malaysia.

Conclusions

The study showed that cigarette smoking induced greater periodontal damage compared to e-cigarette smoking and led to lower pH levels in saliva. Despite ongoing uncertainties, the rising popularity of e-cigarette use underscores the importance for clinicians to include it in smoking history assessments, raise awareness of potential risks to oral health, and integrate discussions on e-cigarette use into smoking cessation counselling. Moreover, the study revealed that e-cigarette smokers had similar cotinine levels to cigarette smokers, highlighting the need for regulatory measures to monitor nicotine levels in e-cigarette vapour and available e-cigarette products.
