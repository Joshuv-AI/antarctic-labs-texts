Filum terminale infected epidermoid cysts in pediatric age group; A case series
Authors: Walid A. Abdel Ghany, Iman H. Hewedi, George Halim, Mostafa G. Mahran, Sahar Talaat, Dina Amin Saleh, Mazen T. Al Karras, Mohamed E. Elhawi, Mohamed Ashraf Mahmoud, Marwa A. Nassef, Aly Ibrahim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpidermoid cysts are rare and account for only 1 % of primary spinal tumors. It's due to inclusion of ectodermal tissue during the third and fourth weeks of gestation. Infected epidermoid cysts are exceedingly rare with very few reports in literature. The clinical presentations include radicular symptoms, motor weakness, sphincteric disturbance, and repeated chemical meningitis. Surgery of this kind of tumor remained a challenge and of a considerable recurrence rate.MethodsThis is a retrospective study reviewing the records of six children who were treated for filum terminale infected epidermoid cysts. All patients underwent microsurgery, and the surgical outcomes were studied through a follow up period of at least 24 months.ResultsAll children had a low back dermal sinus with purulent discharge. One child was a recurrent presentation after the previous two surgeries for evacuation of pus collection, and the other five children were operated for the first time. All the six children had an associated neurological deficit; one child presented with active central nervous system infection, and one child had a history of meningitis and admission to hospital prior to our surgery. Regular follow up revealed no recurrence in any of the six patients. The five patients, who presented with motor weakness, showed significant improvement of the motor power with regular post-operative physical therapy.ConclusionEpidermoid cysts are rare benign lesions that may lead to significant morbidity when infected. The aim of microsurgical excision is to remove the cyst content and its capsule without inducing or increasing neurological deficit by the aid of intraoperative neuromonitoring and microsurgery techniques.

Introduction

Filum terminale epidermoid cyst is an extremely rare condition that accounts for about 1 % of all primary spinal tumors, 17 % of spinal tumors present during the first year of life, and 13 % of tumors of the first 15 years.1

Most of spinal epidermoid cysts are extramedullary; on the other hand, intramedullary cysts were reported only in 80 cases when reviewing the literature, principally in young patients, most frequently reported in thoracic (T4-T8) and lumbosacral.(4)

Defects of closure of the neural tube with inclusion of ectodermal tissue during the third and fourth week of gestation lead to appearance of the congenital forms These cysts are slow-growing tumors that lead to slowly progressive symptoms.

Clinical presentation varies according to the tumor location; progressive motor deficit, radicular symptoms, and in some rare cases, it could be diagnosed after chemical meningitis.2

Spinal cord intramedullary abscesses are rare and have a poor prognosis unless they are diagnosed and treated promptly. Abscess on top of an epidermoid cyst is an exceedingly rare condition with only very limited case reports in literature.3(7)4

Aim of the study

In this case series we review the outcomes and challenges of surgical excision of filum terminale infected epidermoid cysts in six children.

Patients and methods

This is a retrospective single-center case-series, including six children who were admitted to the department of Neurosurgery, Ain Shams University Hospitals from 2018 to 2023 for treatment of an infected filum terminale epidermoid cyst.

The complete medical records of the six patients were reviewed to identify the clinical features and surgical outcomes. Preoperative and postoperative clinical assessment and radiological findings including MR imaging at the lesion site were investigated. All patients underwent microscosurgery under full intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring with a special anesthesia and pain management protocol which consists of general anesthesia (inhalational sevoflurane for induction and maintenance) without long-acting muscle relaxants in order not to interfere with the neuromonitoring readings.13 Follow up period for at least 24 months. Surgical outcomes, operative findings, recovery from preoperative symptoms, postoperative complications, and recurrence of the tumor were studied. The study protocol was approved by the ethical committee review board.

Results

All six children presented to the neurosurgical outpatient clinic with a low back dermal sinus with purulent discharge. One child (16.6 %) was a recurrent presentation after previous two low back surgeries for evacuation of lumbar intrathecal pus collection, the last was 6 months prior to the recent admission. The other five children (83.3 %) were operated on for the first time. The six children had an associated neurological deficit in the form of sensory, motor or sphincteric disturbance. One child presented with an active CNS infection (meningitis and secondary hydrocephalus), and one child had a history of meningitis and admission to fever hospital five months prior to presentation. Surgical procedure and outcome

Anesthesia Protocol: All the procedures were performed under general anesthesia without long-acting muscle relaxants. The fine adjustment of the depth of anesthesia and the use of drugs that do not affect neuromuscular transmission are important issues because of the frequent use of intraoperative electrophysiological stimulation and recording. Volatile induction and maintenance of anesthesia with sevoflurane inhalational anesthetic were applied. Either the single-breath vital capacity technique or the tidal volume technique was used for induction, depending on patient response. Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane 1.8 to 2.5 minimal alveolar concentration (MAC), according to variations in blood pressure or heart rate compared with basal values.5

Preemptive Analgesia and Multimodal Pain Control Protocol: For perioperative analgesia, nalbuphine 0.1–0.2 mg/kg IM or IV was given 30 min before the induction of anesthesia. The maximum single dose is 20 mg, and the maximum daily dose is 160 mg in divided doses every 4 h. Paracetamol 15 mg/kg IV infusion was administered after the induction of anesthesia and before the start of surgery, and it could be repeated every 4–6 h with a maximum of 60 mg/kg, not exceeding 3 g/d.6 Antibiotic prophylaxis with ampicillin-sulbactam and ceftriaxone combinations were administered at the induction of anesthesia.

All children were operated in prone position. Excluding the recurrent case, who had multilevel lumbar laminectomy before, laminoplasty was done for the other five patients (83.3 %). Dissection of sinus tract was done from skin down to dura. After opening the dura, an encapsulated tumor was encountered, tumor capsule was identified, dissected of the arachnoid plane using microsurgical technique; dissection was done up towards the conus and down to the filum terminale distal attachment. Our rationale in dissection was to identify and dissect as much as could be capsule layers. Epidermoid capsule was found to be formed of multiple layers that denote the developmental stages of the tumor and/or repeated infections and healing processes.

Capsule was carefully dissected to encounter all layers and excised in large pieces due to its adherence to nerve roots. Intra operative neurophysiologic monitoring in the form of stimulation and continuous EMG recording from lower limb muscles and sphincters was applied in all cases. Profuse wash with warm saline of the tumor bed was done to ensure removal of all tumor debris and flakes then the dura was closed in a watertight way and closure of all layers of back muscles and fascia was performed. Microscopic examination of the specimens revealed infected granulation tissue with dense blood vessels and dense mixed inflammatory infiltrate made from macrophages, plasma cells, lymphocytes and many neutrophils, foreign body giant cells were also focally seen.

Follow up:

Regular follow up visits at three, six months, one and two years revealed no recurrence in any of the six children. The five children, who presented with motor weakness, showed significant improvement of the motor power with regular physical therapy.

Discussion

Neuroimaging

Magnetic resonance features of epidermoid cysts are hypointense or isointense in T1-weighted signal and hyperintense in T2-weighted signal. Contrast enhancement with Gadolinium remains limited, usually to the periphery of the cyst, which is related to the chronic inflammatory process and fibrosis, whereas peritumoral edema is usually lacking. Diffusion weighted images usually reveal restricted diffusion that can help to differentiate epidermoid cysts from arachnoid cysts.7(12) The image characteristics were all in concordance with the MRI images of our cases outlined in the figures below.

Clinical picture and associations

Our six cases had dermal sinuses, one case had spina bifida and another had VACTERL associated anomaly. Such associations once diagnosed should be operated upon once diagnosed as emphasized by Ak et al.8

In this series, all children presented with motor and sphincter deficit, and three of them had associated sensory manifestations such as radicular hypothesia, dysesthesia or paresthesia, this draws attention to take this condition into account when making a differential diagnosis in pediatric patients who present radicular symptoms and/or alteration of sphincter control in the context of a neuroinfection.

There are two forms of spinal epidermoid cysts; congenital and acquired, congenital forms are thought to be due to inclusion of ectodermal tissue during neural tube closure at the 3–5 weeks of gestation. While the acquired cysts are caused by the migration of dermal tissue during lumbar punctures or spine surgeries.9

These cysts usually become symptomatic by the 3rd and 4th decades of life. The diagnosis of spinal epidermoid cysts in children is very rare and is usually delayed to adulthood. 17 The presence of a dermal sinus in a back of a child may guide to an associated intramedullary dermoid or epidermoid cyst in about half of patients.

Neuroinfection

In this series, children presented in early childhood with discharging back dermal sinus, fever, deterioration of level of consciousness, and progressive weakness made the diagnosis possible at early childhood.

Chemical meningitis may develop because of rupture of these cysts, while bacterial meningitis may develop in the presence of associated dermal sinuses, as these sinuses are considered a route for infection, hence the development of bacterial meningitis or intramedullary abscesses.10

Gerlach et al,11 reported that large intraspinal abscesses may form because of infections of epidermoid cysts with a potentially devastating outcome as reported in our case number six that presented with huge cystic lesion (abscess) and sever neurological signs.

In this series, we reported six children presented with infected lumbar intradural epidermoid cysts. Five of them were de novo presentations while one boy (16.6 %) had a recurrent presentation of a discharging sinus. Two out of six children (33.3 %) had a history of bacterial meningitis upon admission to hospital for treatment.

By reviewing the literature, only very few case reports of intramedullary spinal abscesses on top of epidermoid cysts were found.12,13,14 In all these reports, they reported the importance of the recognition of cutaneous stigmata that may present with spinal dysraphism. Hung et al, recommended that any child with a congenital dermal sinus should be thoroughly examined and investigated because of the potential of intradural extension, and prophylactic surgery should be considered to prevent infections of the central nervous system.15

Surgery

Intraoperatively, these tumors were multilayered encapsulated tumors containing white flakes and pus. The tumor capsule was found adherent to nerve roots. Dissection of the tumor capsule layers from the nerve roots is done carefully under continuous EMG monitoring for preservation of neural function. The complete excision of the tumor and its capsule was performed in the five children with the first presentation. While subtotal excision of the tumor capsule was performed in the child with the recurrent presentation because of the adherence of some pieces of the capsule to the lumbar nerve roots near to the conus medullaris.

Neuromonitoring

We emphasize the role of intraoperative neuromonitoring in the form of real-time continuous stimulation and EMG recording that helped us to achieve maximal safe resection without causing additional neurological deficits. In contrast to Liu et al they reported a case where there was a recorded decrease in SSEPs during capsule dissection with partial recovery noted towards closure after dissection was halted.16

Intraoperatively laminoplasty was done in all our cases except case number (recurrent case) which ensures spinal stability on long-term with development and growth of those children. Morita et al, and others mentioned total and hemi laminectomies in their case series.

Histopathology

Histopathologic examination of epidermoid cysts reveals a lined stratified squamous epithelium surrounded by a layer of collagenous tissue. The cyst contains a considerable amount of fat and cholesterol which is formed by desquamation of keratin from the epithelial lining. Rupture of these cysts and dissemination of the cyst content may produce a vigorous inflammatory reaction which may precipitate chemical meningitis or septic meningitis in infected cysts.2(22)

Recurrence

The recurrence of spinal epidermoid cysts is reported in few studies, which is almost always caused by the incomplete excision of the original cyst. The preferred treatment for the recurrent symptomatic epidermoid cysts is reoperation, which becomes more difficult due to the formation of scarring tissue.

This is outlined in Morita et al, where the author describes a case where recurrence occurred and, in both surgeries, total excision of the capsule could not be achieved due to severe adhesions, and this supports our hypothesis about the necessity of careful dissection and radical excision of the capsule if safely possible.10

Finally, special attention should be paid in the treatment of recurrent lesions because of the possibility of malignant transformation. Adjuvant radiotherapy is usually preserved for lesions with histopathological signs of malignant transformation.16

Conclusion

Infected spinal epidermoid cysts are a very rare presentation in children which may produce serious neurologic manifestations. Complete excision of these cysts is the standard goal of surgical treatment. To our minds, the identification and meticulous dissection of the multilayered tumor capsule from the arachnoid and dura is the main prognostic factor to minimize the possibility of recurrence, which is not reported in similar published literature. Meticulous dissection of the capsule from the neural tissue under neurophysiologic monitoring is the best add-on to the surgical practice to prevent iatrogenic permanent neurologic deficits and to minimize the risk of recurrence, along with laminoplasty rather than laminectomy to prevent further spine deformity and disability.
