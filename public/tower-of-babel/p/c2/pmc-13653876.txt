Eco-phylogenetic diversity and relative pigment production in environmental Talaromyces purpurogenus isolates
Authors: Youssuf Gherbawy, Amera Alqahtani, Hanan Alomari, Kholoud Alzahrani, Pet Ioan, Helal Al-Harthi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Talaromyces purpurogenus is a filamentous fungus capable of producing red polyketide-like pigments with potential as natural alternatives to synthetic colorants. This study investigated the ecological distribution, phylogenetic diversity, and relative pigment-producing capacity of T. purpurogenus isolates recovered from agricultural soils, decaying fruits, spoiled grains/cereals, and stored feed/food materials. A total of 35 isolates were obtained, with the highest isolation frequencies observed in agricultural soils and spoiled grains/cereals. Molecular identification based on ITS rDNA and β-tubulin sequence similarity supported the assignment of the isolates to T. purpurogenus. Multilocus phylogenetic analysis revealed well-supported intraspecific subclades, suggesting notable genetic diversity among the examined isolates. Pigment production, semi-quantitatively evaluated spectrophotometrically at 500 nm (OD₅₀₀) following ethyl acetate extraction, varied significantly among isolates. Statistical analysis demonstrated a significant association between phylogenetic clustering and relative pigment production (p < 0.001), as well as between ecological origin and pigment production levels (p < 0.01), indicating that pigment-associated phenotypes are influenced by both genetic background and environmental factors. Strain PP05 exhibited the highest relative pigment production and was selected for optimization studies. Experimental analysis showed that pH, temperature, carbon and nitrogen sources, and incubation period significantly affected pigment production (p < 0.001), with maximum relative pigment production recorded at pH 5.0 and 24 °C in sucrose–peptone medium after 7 days. These findings provide an eco-phylogenetic perspective on pigment-associated variability in T. purpurogenus and highlight its relevance for future strain-selection and biotechnological studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10123-026-00847-8.

Introduction

The increasing demand for sustainable and environmentally friendly colorants has stimulated interest in natural pigments produced by microorganisms. Synthetic dyes widely used in textile, food, and cosmetic industries are often associated with environmental persistence and potential toxicity, prompting the search for safer and biodegradable alternatives (Morales-Oyervides et al. 2020). Fungal pigments have attracted particular attention due to their biodegradability, structural diversity, and ability to generate a wide spectrum of colors through secondary metabolism (Pimenta et al. 2021; Nicoletti et al. 2023).

Filamentous fungi such as Talaromyces, Penicillium, and Monascus are recognized producers of natural pigments, including polyketide-derived pigments and azaphilone-like compounds previously reported in these genera (Lebeau et al. 2017; Pimenta et al. 2021). These pigments have been investigated for applications in food, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, and textiles due to their color intensity and relative stability (Morales-Oyervides et al. 2017; Chintapenta et al. 2014; Venkatachalam et al. 2021). Among these fungi, Talaromyces purpurogenus (formerly Penicillium purpurogenum) has been reported as a producer of red polyketide-like pigments with potentially useful biochemical and technological properties (Yilmaz et al. 2012; Visagie et al. 2014). Several pigment compounds, including azaphilone-like and quinone-related metabolites, have previously been reported from this species and were associated with antioxidant, antimicrobial, and other bioactive properties (Mendez et al. 2011; Jin et al. 2018; Yue et al. 2022).

Recent studies on closely related species such as Talaromyces atroroseus have further demonstrated the potential of Talaromyces spp. for pigment production and optimization under controlled conditions (Salim et al. 2022; Ruiz-Sánchez et al. 2023; Xia et al. 2025). Among Talaromyces species, T. atroroseus has received particular industrial attention because of its high pigment productivity and favorable biosafety profile. These studies highlight the importance of strain selection, fermentation strategy, and environmental factors in influencing pigment production and functionality.

Advances in fungal taxonomy have clarified the placement of T. purpurogenus within Talaromyces section Purpurei and distinguished it from related taxa, facilitating more accurate characterization of pigment-producing strains (Yilmaz et al. 2012; Frisvad et al. 2013; Antipova et al. 2023). Despite increasing interest in this species, many studies have focused on reference or laboratory-adapted strains, whereas the diversity and pigment-associated phenotypes of environmental isolates remain relatively underexplored. Environmental substrates such as soils, plant residues, fruits, and stored food materials may harbor diverse strains with varying metabolic capacities and relative pigment production levels (Ogbonna et al. 2017; Sun et al. 2023). Differences in substrate composition and environmental conditions may also influence fungal secondary metabolism and contribute to ecological variation in pigment-associated phenotypes.

Pigment biosynthesis in T. purpurogenus is influenced by cultivation conditions, including carbon and nitrogen sources, pH, temperature, and fermentation mode (Santos-Ebinuma et al. 2013, 2014; Ugwu et al. 2021). Previous studies have shown that media containing sucrose and organic nitrogen sources often enhance red pigment production, while environmental parameters such as pH and temperature affect pigment stability and relative production levels (Parul et al. 2020; Venkatachalam et al. 2021).

The aim of this study was to investigate the ecological distribution, phylogenetic diversity, and relative pigment production of T. purpurogenus isolates recovered from diverse environmental substrates. Particular emphasis was placed on evaluating eco-phylogenetic variation and strain-level differences in pigment-associated phenotypes among environmental isolates.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and fungal isolation

Samples were collected from agricultural soils, decaying fruits, spoiled cereals, and stored animal feed in Taif, Saudi Arabia. Sampling sites included agricultural fields, local fruit markets, grain storage areas, and animal feed storage facilities located within different parts of the Taif region. Approximately 10–20 g of each sample was aseptically transferred into sterile containers and transported to the laboratory within 24 h. Fungal isolation was performed using the dilution plate technique (Scervino et al. 2011). Subsamples were serially diluted in sterile distilled water and plated onto Czapek–Dox agar (CDA; HiMedia, India) and potato dextrose agar (PDA; HiMedia, India) supplemented with chloramphenicol (100 mg L⁻¹) to inhibit bacterial growth (Venkatachalam et al. 2021). Plates were incubated at 28 ± 2 °C for 5–7 days. Colonies showing morphological characteristics consistent with Talaromyces/Penicillium-like fungi were subcultured onto fresh PDA plates for purification. Pure cultures were maintained on CDA slants at 4 °C for further analysis.

Morphological characterization

Colony morphology, including color, texture, and margin characteristics, as well as pigment diffusion, was recorded after 7 days of incubation on PDA and CDA. Microscopic features were examined using slide cultures stained with Lactophenol Cotton Blue to observe conidiophores, phialides, and conidia. Identification was performed following standard taxonomic keys for Talaromyces and related genera (Samson et al. 2010; Frisvad et al. 2013; Visagie et al. 2014).

Molecular identification and phylogenetic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from 7-day-old fungal mycelia grown on PDA using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method (Doyle and Doyle 1987). The internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region was amplified using primers ITS1 and ITS4 (White et al. 1990), while the β-tubulin (TUB) gene was amplified using primers Bt2a and Bt2b (Glass and Donaldson 1995). PCR products were purified and sequenced in both directions.

Sequences were compared with reference sequences in the NCBI GenBank database using BLASTn (Schoch et al. 2012), and representative sequences were deposited in GenBank. ITS and β-tubulin sequences were concatenated and aligned prior to phylogenetic analysis. Because ITS and β-tubulin markers may provide limited resolution within some Talaromyces species complexes, species assignment should be interpreted cautiously in the absence of additional loci such as CaM or RPB2.

Phylogenetic relationships were inferred using the Maximum Likelihood method implemented in MEGA12 (Kumar et al. 2024) under the Tamura–Nei substitution model (Tamura and Nei 1993). Branch support was evaluated using bootstrap analysis with 1000 replicates (Felsenstein 1985). Alternaria alternata (KP124348.1) was used as the outgroup.

Screening for Pigment Production.

All isolates were qualitatively screened for extracellular red pigment production on CDA and PDA after incubation at 28 ± 2 °C for 7 days (Santos-Ebinuma et al. 2013). Pigment production was initially assessed based on visible red coloration and diffusion into the culture medium.

Quantitative pigment analysis

Selected isolates were cultured in Czapek–Dox broth and incubated statically at 28 ± 2 °C for 7 days. Culture broths were filtered to remove mycelia, and extracellular pigments were extracted using ethyl acetate. The extraction was repeated until the organic phase became nearly colorless. Extracts were concentrated under reduced pressure and resuspended for analysis.

Pigment production was semi-quantitatively estimated spectrophotometrically by measuring absorbance at 500 nm (OD₅₀₀), which served as a relative proxy for extracellular pigment production (Lebeau et al. 2017). Because no purified pigment standard or calibration curve was used, OD₅₀₀ values were expressed as relative absorbance units (AU) rather than absolute pigment concentrations. Absorption spectra (350–600 nm) were also recorded to provide preliminary spectral characterization of the extracted pigments.

Fermentation parameter optimization

The isolate exhibiting the highest relative pigment production (PP05) was selected for optimization using a one-variable-at-a-time (OVAT) approach (Box et al. 1978; Rao et al. 2010). The effects of pH (4.0–7.0), temperature (20–32 °C), carbon sources (glucose, sucrose, mannitol, and starch), nitrogen sources (peptone, yeast extract, ammonium sulfate, and urea), and incubation period (4–14 days) were evaluated.

OVAT was selected as a preliminary and cost-effective strategy to identify major cultivation parameters influencing pigment production before applying multivariate optimization approaches. Although OVAT provides baseline optimization, it does not account for interactions among variables; therefore, response surface methodology (RSM) is recommended for further optimization (Bezerra et al. 2013; Myers et al. 2016). All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) (Miller 1959). Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp. 2019). Differences among groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test (p < 0.05) (Tukey 1949).

Chi-square (χ²) tests were used to examine associations between categorical variables, including source of isolation and OD-based pigment production classes, as well as phylogenetic clade and pigment production class (Pearson 1900). Pigment production classes were assigned according to OD₅₀₀ values as low, moderate, and high relative producers. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to visualize clustering patterns (Jolliffe 2002). PCA was used as an exploratory visualization method and not as a formal inferential test of ecological separation. Linear regression analysis was used to assess model fit between observed and predicted pigment production values (Draper and Smith 1998). Because some ecological groups contained relatively small sample sizes, statistical associations should be interpreted cautiously.

Samples collected from agricultural soils, decaying fruits, spoiled grains/cereals, and stored feed/food materials yielded a total of 35 fungal isolates morphologically consistent with Talaromyces species (Table 1). Variation in pigment production among isolates

Qualitative and semi-quantitative analyses revealed substantial variability in pigment production among the 35 isolates. Based on OD₅₀₀ measurements and relative pigment production levels (Table 2), isolates were categorized into high (34.3%), moderate (28.6%), and low (37.1%) OD-based pigment production classes (Fig. 2).

OD₅₀₀ values varied widely among isolates, ranging from low values in isolates such as PP04 and PP29 to comparatively high values in PP05, PP25, and PP35. Isolates with higher OD₅₀₀ values generally exhibited increased extracellular pigmentation.

Effect of ecological origin on pigment production

Marked differences in relative pigment production were observed according to source of isolation. Isolates from agricultural soils exhibited the highest median OD₅₀₀ values, followed by those from spoiled grains/cereals. Isolates from decaying fruits showed intermediate values, whereas isolates from stored feed/food samples consistently exhibited the lowest relative pigment production (Fig. 3).

A significant association was detected between phylogenetic clade and relative pigment production class (χ² = 43.27, df = 6, p < 0.001), indicating that pigment-associated phenotypes were associated with phylogenetic clustering patterns. Clade A was predominantly composed of high pigment-producing isolates, Clade B consisted mainly of low pigment producers, Clade C was dominated by moderate producers, and Clade D showed a mixed distribution.

When combined with ecological data, consistent patterns emerged. High pigment-producing isolates within Clade A were largely associated with agricultural soils, whereas Clade B isolates were predominantly derived from stored feed environments. Clade C isolates were distributed across multiple substrates, while Clade D showed no clear ecological preference. These associations should be interpreted cautiously because no direct genetic or biochemical characterization of pigment biosynthetic pathways was performed.

Model validation and optimal conditions

The regression model showed strong agreement between observed and predicted pigment production values (Fig. S6), indicating good model performance. Optimal conditions for relative pigment production were identified as pH 5–6, temperature 24 °C, sucrose as the carbon source, peptone as the nitrogen source, and an incubation period of approximately 7 days.

Discussion

The recovery of Talaromyces purpurogenus from agricultural soils, decaying fruits, spoiled grains/cereals, and stored feed/food samples demonstrates its ecological adaptability across diverse substrates. These findings are consistent with previous reports describing Talaromyces species as common inhabitants of soils, plant debris, and stored products (Yilmaz et al. 2012; Visagie et al. 2014; Nicoletti et al. 2023). The absence of significant differences in isolation success among substrates further suggests that T. purpurogenus is broadly distributed across diverse ecological niches.

Multilocus identification based on ITS and β-tubulin sequences provided improved phylogenetic resolution compared with ITS alone, supporting earlier taxonomic frameworks for Talaromyces (Yilmaz et al. 2012; Visagie et al. 2014). The concatenated ITS + β-tubulin phylogeny clearly resolved all isolates within a strongly supported T. purpurogenus clade, supporting their assignment to this species. Importantly, the multilocus analysis revealed several well-supported subclades, suggesting notable intraspecific genetic diversity. However, these phylogenetic patterns should be interpreted cautiously because additional loci such as CaM and RPB2 were not included in the present study.

A key finding of this study is the association between phylogenetic clustering and relative pigment production. Statistical analysis revealed a significant relationship between phylogenetic clade and OD-based pigment production class (χ² = 43.27, p < 0.001), indicating that pigment-associated phenotypes may be influenced by genetic background. Distinct phylogenetic clades exhibited different relative pigment production patterns, suggesting that pigment production in T. purpurogenus may partly reflect lineage-associated variation in secondary metabolism. Because no direct biochemical characterization was performed, references to azaphilone-like or polyketide-derived pigments should be considered preliminary and based on previous reports for related Talaromyces species. Similar genotype-dependent variation in pigment production has been reported in closely related species such as Talaromyces atroroseus (Salim et al. 2022; Xia et al. 2025), supporting the possible role of genetic background in shaping metabolic output. Among Talaromyces species, T. atroroseus has received particular industrial attention because of its high pigment productivity and favorable biosafety profile. In addition to phylogenetic effects, ecological origin significantly influenced relative pigment production (χ² = 27.31, p < 0.01). Isolates from agricultural soils and spoiled grains/cereals exhibited higher relative pigment production, whereas isolates from stored feed/food substrates consistently showed lower production. These patterns may reflect differences in nutrient availability and environmental conditions. Nutrient-rich substrates, particularly those rich in carbohydrates, are known to stimulate fungal growth and influence polyketide-associated metabolic pathways (Ogbonna 2019; Parul et al. 2020). Comparable observations have been reported in Talaromyces fermentation studies, where substrate composition strongly influences pigment production (Ruiz-Sánchez et al. 2023). In contrast, nutrient-limited environments may suppress secondary metabolism or favor energy conservation strategies (Visagie et al. 2014; Nicoletti et al. 2023). Although these ecological associations were statistically supported, the relatively small sample size within some ecological groups may limit broader ecological interpretation.

The integration of phylogenetic, ecological, and phenotypic data provides insight into relative pigment production in T. purpurogenus. The results suggest a hierarchical relationship in which phylogenetic background may influence pigment-associated potential, while environmental conditions modulate phenotypic expression. Such patterns could potentially reflect lineage-associated differences in metabolic regulation or substrate-dependent selection pressures. This eco-phylogenetic structuring highlights the importance of considering both genetic and environmental factors when evaluating functional traits in filamentous fungi. Similar observations have been reported in Talaromyces species, where integrating phenotypic screening with molecular identification provided a reliable strategy for selecting productive strains and understanding their ecological roles in natural and agricultural ecosystems (Abbas et al. 2024).

The observed intraspecific variability in relative pigment production (34.3% high producers versus 37.1% low producers) underscores the importance of environmental screening for identifying productive strains. Spectrophotometric screening based on OD₅₀₀ proved to be a rapid and effective approach for comparative isolate evaluation, although the measurements should be interpreted as relative absorbance-based estimates rather than absolute pigment concentrations. Combined with multilocus phylogenetic analysis, this strategy provides an efficient framework for selecting isolates for future biochemical and biotechnological investigations.

The optimal conditions identified for isolate PP05—pH 5.0, temperature 24 °C, sucrose as the carbon source, peptone as the nitrogen source, and a 7-day incubation period—are consistent with previous studies on Talaromyces pigments (Mendez et al. 2011; Santos-Ebinuma et al. 2013; Parul et al. 2020). Similar optimization strategies have been successfully applied in related species to enhance relative pigment production and process efficiency (Xia et al. 2025). The OVAT approach used in the present study provided a preliminary and cost-effective strategy for identifying major cultivation parameters influencing pigment production; however, future studies should employ multivariate optimization methods such as response surface methodology (RSM) to evaluate interactions among variables.

The identification of highly productive isolates highlights the relevance of T. purpurogenus for future biotechnological exploration. However, biosafety evaluation is essential, as some strains may produce mycotoxins such as citrinin (Yilmaz et al. 2012; Frisvad et al. 2013). Previous studies have also emphasized the importance of chemical characterization and safety assessment for industrial applications of Talaromyces pigments (Salim et al. 2022). No mycotoxin screening or detailed chemical characterization was performed in the present study; therefore, industrial or food-related applications cannot yet be recommended. Comprehensive profiling and toxicological evaluation are therefore required prior to practical application.

Conclusion

Systematic environmental sampling from agricultural soils, decaying fruits, spoiled grains/cereals, and stored feed/food materials yielded 35 Talaromyces purpurogenus isolates exhibiting substantial variability in relative pigment production. Multilocus phylogenetic analysis based on ITS and β-tubulin sequences supported the assignment of the isolates to T. purpurogenus and revealed notable intraspecific genetic diversity structured into distinct phylogenetic clades. A significant association between phylogenetic clustering and relative pigment production was observed, suggesting that pigment-associated phenotypes may be influenced by genetic background, while ecological origin further modulates phenotypic expression. Isolates from agricultural soils and plant-derived substrates exhibited higher relative pigment production compared with those from stored feed environments.

Semi-quantitative screening based on OD₅₀₀ identified isolate PP05 as the most productive strain under the examined conditions. Optimization experiments established favorable cultivation conditions at pH 5.0, 24 °C, sucrose–peptone medium, and a 7-day incubation period, resulting in maximum relative pigment production (OD₅₀₀ = 2.55 AU).

Overall, these findings provide an eco-phylogenetic perspective on pigment-associated variability in T. purpurogenus and highlight the value of environmental isolate screening for future strain-selection studies. Future research should focus on detailed chemical characterization, mycotoxin screening, multigene phylogenetic analysis, and advanced fermentation optimization to further evaluate the biotechnological relevance of these isolates.
