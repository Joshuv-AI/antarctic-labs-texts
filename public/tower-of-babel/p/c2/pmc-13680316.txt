A coculture system of Epicoccum nigrum with Mucor racemosus induces red pigment biosynthesis in vinegar mash
Authors: Fengli Wang, Fangyuan Xu, Xinle Liang, Wenjie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFungal pigments hold significant food industrial value due to their safe and sustainable properties. Here we identified a naturally occurring coculture pair Epicoccum nigrum SPDX618 and Mucor racemosus SPDX522 in vinegar mash that produced red pigments, which was not observed in either monoculture. Our results showed that E. nigrum acted as the pigment producer while M. racemosus served as the elicitor. Response surface methodology and pH stabilization strategy improved the yield of red pigment into 328.7 U/g. Through bioactivity-guided fractionation coupled with UHPLC-MS/MS and in silico structure reconstitution, four red pigments were preliminarily assigned as anthracycline-like compounds. RNA-seq analysis revealed a putative dual-PKS gene cluster (SPDX618009169–SPDX618009178) with exclusively upregulation in cocultured E. nigrum that is potentially involved in red pigment biosynthesis. Besides, the production of the yellow pigment epipyrone was reduced in cocultured E. nigrum, consistent with the downregulated transcription level of its biosynthesis genes. Comparative metabolomics analysis further revealed a broad downregulation in both of primary metabolism and secondary metabolism in coculture system, indicating that a competition interaction relationship also existed between E. nigrum and M. racemosus. Altogether, this study reports a fungal coculture system that exhibited red pigments production. These findings contribute to the understanding of microbial interaction-driven metabolite biosynthesis and may provide a foundation for future exploration of their potential application as a colorant.Key points• An Epicoccum nigrum–Mucor racemosus coculture system was identified to produce red pigment for the first time.• E. nigrum acts as the red pigment producer and M. racemosus serves as the elicitor.• RNA-seq analysis tentatively identified a putative dual-PKS gene cluster upregulated in coculture that is putatively involved in red pigment biosynthesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00253-026-13956-1.

Introduction

Fungi exhibit remarkable capacity for biosynthesizing structurally distinct secondary metabolites with multifaceted biotechnological applications, among which chromogenic compounds demonstrate exceptional commercial viability for sustainable production (Xu et al. 2023). Various pigment types, including melanins, anthraquinones, azaphilones, and carotenoids, have gained industrial importance in food coloration, agricultural applications, and pharmaceutical development (Kalra et al. 2020; Shen et al. 2023). To date, Monascus (Mapari et al. 2009), Penicillium (Sardaryan 2002), Talaromyces (Frisvad et al. 2013; Isbrandt et al. 2019), and Blakeslea (Liu et al. 2012) as well as their derived pigments, including monascorubrin, ankaflavin, mitorubrins, atrorosin and β-carotene, have achieved greatly industrial production by scaled-fermentation and have been authorized applications in food industry. Given the advances in metagenomics and culturomics from a variety of ecosystems including traditional fermented food microbiota, more and more fungi capable of producing pigment have been investigated. The filamentous fungus Aspergillus ustus, isolated from fermented broad bean paste, produces polyketide pigments, including pyrropyrazine diketone and dianthrone, which display attractive color and exhibit antimicrobial bioactivity (Zhou et al. 2023). The psychrotrophic polar fungus Geomyces sp. WNF-15A, isolated from Antarctic soil, synthesizes a water-soluble Antarctic Geomyces red pigment (AGRP) composed of polysaccharides, geomycamine, and derivatives, exhibiting a higher extinction coefficient than both commercial Monascus red pigment and cochineal red pigment (Long et al. 2024). The hydnoid fungus Sarcodontia setosa synthesizes novel quinone-type pigments setosic acid and 7,8-dehydrohomosarcodontic acid (Whaley et al. 2023). The exploration of novel pigment-producing fungi and pigments remains an active area of research.

Beyond well-established fermentation of sole species, cocultivation of multiple species has been extensively implemented in a variety of traditional food bioprocesses. Metabolic cross-talk within microbial consortia constitutes an indispensable biocatalytic driver in traditional fermented food ecosystems, critically determining textural, nutritional, and organoleptic properties of final products (Cheng et al. 2025; Nikoloudaki et al. 2024). Synergistic interactions encompassing bacterial-bacterial, bacterial-fungal, and fungal-fungal cross-talk are fundamentally involved in the fermentation ecosystems of traditional foods, which not only promotes quality and flavors but also benefits pigmentation to some extent. For example, red koji rice constitutes a symbiotic microbial consortium encompassing yeast cocultured with diverse strains of the genus Monascus capable of ethanol fermentation, pigmentation, and carbohydrate degradation activities (Moghadam et al. 2022).

In the context of synthetic microbiome design, the multispecies consortia that naturally evolved in traditional fermented foods are being reverse-engineered into modular and experimentally tractable model systems (Mohamed et al. 2022; Naz et al. 2026). These microbial networks provide a predictive blueprint for assembling resilient, functionally redundant fermentation communities and offer a low-risk alternative to innovation across the food industry. Zhejiang rosy vinegar is a traditional rice vinegar product, which is characterized by the natural surface fermentation over four months with a completely spontaneous complex microbiota and unique rosy appearance (Fang et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023). However, the source and the occurrence of its red pigment are still unclear. Monascus spp. have been isolated from this ecosystem, but Monascus red pigments are not detected in the final vinegar product and are not considered as the pigment signature (Tong et al. 2024).

In this study, we discovered a novel symbiotic association involving Mucor racemosus and Epicoccum nigrum from the traditional Zhejiang rosy vinegar mash that is capable of producing red pigments. In the coculture system, the pigment producer and the elicitor were identified, with the pigment structure tentatively characterized by tandem mass spectrometry complemented with SIRIUS computational prediction. Integrated analysis of transcriptome-metabolome interaction further revealed the regulatory network and symbiotic signaling molecules among two strains during the cocultivation. Our results deepen the understanding of Epicoccum–Mucor symbiosis and establish a framework for exploring the pigment biosynthetic gene cluster activated by interspecies interactions in fermented microbial consortia.

Materials and methods

Strains, media, cultivation conditions

Mucor racemosus SPDX522 (MrS522) and Epicoccum nigrum SPDX618 (EnS618) were isolated from traditional rosy vinegar in Zhejiang Province and stored at − 80 °C. Strains were first inoculated on potato dextrose agar (PDA) and incubated at 25 °C for 3 days, then punched with a cork borer to get 5-mm-diameter plugs. Two coculture configurations were then implemented: (i) 5-mm-diameter mycelial plugs pre-cultured from competing strains were positioned on opposing sides of 90-mm petri dishes with 3-cm separation (Oppong-Danquah et al. 2020); (ii) overlapping hyphal fragments (5 × 5 mm) from both strains were co-inoculated at the geometric center of the medium. Elucidating the red pigment producer strain in the coculture system was conducted as the previous description (Shang et al. 2017).

Optimization of fermentation process for high-yield pigment production in coculture

The 5-mm-diameter plugs of EnS618 and MrS522 PDA inoculum were inoculated into 100 mL of Czapek-Dox rice medium at inoculation ratios of 1:1, 1:3, 1:6, and 1:9, followed by incubation at 25 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for 5 days. The red pigment yield was then determined. Pigment production was expressed as the absorbance at 505 nm (A₅₀₅) of the methanol extract per unit biomass, in units of U/g. The color value was calculated using the following formula:

Subsequently, a one-factor-at-a-time experiment was conducted to screen the levels of each factor, followed by a Plackett–Burman (PB) design to identify key variables. The steepest ascent experiment was then employed to determine the central values of these factors. Finally, the optimal fermentation conditions were derived using Box–Behnken design (BBD) combined with response surface methodology (RSM). To address the issue of pH decline during fermentation, sodium acetate (NaAC) was supplemented at concentrations of 1, 3, 5, 10, 20, and 30 mmol/L under these optimized conditions for further testing.

Large-scale fermentation, extraction and isolation of pigments

For large-scale fermentation, a total of 30 L of culture was performed using a batch fermentation in 150 flasks, containing 200 mL medium per 500 mL flask. The rice-based microbial biomass was harvested and ultrasonicated with methanol (1:10, w/v; 3 × 60 min cycles) using a 40-kHz probe sonicator. The resulting crude pigment extract was partitioned thrice with little petroleum ether in a separatory funnel to remove nonpolar impurities.

The crude pigment extract obtained was further processed in a silica gel column (150 cm) packed with spherical particle size 75–200 μm (Sigma-Aldrich) using a gradient elution of dichloromethane (CH₂Cl₂) and (MeOH, 10:1 to 1:1, v/v). Compound 8 was isolated from the C1 fraction (yellow pigment) by eluting the column with a 3:1 (v/v) solvent ratio. The C2 fraction (red pigment) obtained via 1:1 (v/v) elution was further purified by semi-preparative HPLC using a mobile phase of MeOH and 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (FA) with a linear gradient from 85 to 100% MeOH over 100 min at 5 mL/min, diluted fractions were collected, dried, and then stored at 4 °C for further analysis on UHPLC-Triple-TOF/MS and MS/MS.

HPLC and UHPLC-Triple-TOF–MS/MS analysis

Chemical profiling of monoculture and coculture on rice medium was performed according to Shen et al. (2024). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a solvent system comprising MeOH (A) and water with 0.1% formic acid (B) at a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min, with a linear gradient from 10 to 100% A over 60 min. The UV absorption was monitored at 254 nm and 505 nm.

UHPLC-MS/MS analysis was performed following Oliveira et al. (2022) with modifications. Chromatographic separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC T3 column (C18, 1.8 µm, 3.0 × 150 mm) at 45 °C with a mobile phase of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B) at 0.35 mL/min. The gradient program was: 0 min 5%B; 15 min 50%B; 22 min 95%B until 25 min. MS and MS/MS data were acquired in IDA mode (± ESI) with source parameters: in the positive/negative mode, source voltage of 5.5 kV, the source temperature was 550 °C, collision energy (CE) was set at ± 40—± 20 V, the scan range of m/z of precursor ion and product ion were set as 80—1500 Da and 50—1500 Da. Controls (MeOH and blank medium) were analyzed identically. The structure of red pigments was predicted using SIRIUS 4 (Dührkop et al. 2019).

Genome sequencing

The genome of MrS522 and EnS618 was sequenced by LingEn Biology (Shanghai, China) using Nova-seq 6000 (Illumina). The preliminary genome assembly was performed using SMRT Link v5.0.1 (Ardui et al. 2018; Reiner et al. 2018). Assembly completeness was evaluated with BUSCO v3.0.2, and coding genes were predicted using the Augustus 2.7 software. Functional annotation of genes was conducted through the Gene Ontology (GO) (Ashburner et al. 2000), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (Kanehisa and Goto 2000), Clusters of Orthologous Groups (KOG), Non-Redundant Protein Database (NR), and Swiss-Prot databases. The initial prediction and analysis of proposed biosynthetic gene clusters were carried out using antiSMASH (Blin et al. 2023) (http://antismash.secondarymetabolites.org/).

Transcriptome sequencing and RT-qPCR

For RNA extraction, samples were prepared with monocultivation of MrS522 and EnS618 as well as cocultivation of two strains in 100 mL of Czapek and rice juice mixed liquid medium for 3 days at 25 °C. The cells were collected by centrifugation. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen) following previous method (Ma et al. 2026).

RNA-seq was performed by LingEn Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. A dual RNA-seq strategy was adopted to analyze transcriptomic data as previously described (Westermann et al. 2012). Raw reads were first quality-trimmed using Trimmomatic (Bolger et al. 2014) for data preprocessing. The processed clean reads from monoculture were mapped to the reference genome of EnS618 or MrS522, respectively. And the clean reads from the coculture were separately aligned to the reference genomes of EnS618 and MrS522. The sequence alignment was performed using HISAT2 software (Kim et al. 2015), and stringent alignment thresholds were applied to intercept erroneous cross-species read mapping, taking full advantage of the abundant single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertions/deletions (Indels) between two fungal genomes to eliminate interspecific mapping interference. Gene-level expression quantification was performed using featureCounts (v2.0.3) (Liao et al. 2014) with the parameters -M –primary. The -M option allows for the counting of multi-mapped reads, while –primary ensures that each multi-mapped read is assigned exclusively to its primary alignment site, which avoids redundant counting and reduces quantitative bias arising from ambiguous read mappings. The alignment quality was assessed using Qualimap (http://qualimap.bioinfo.cipf.es/) (Garcí­a-Alcalde et al. 2012), and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with edgeR under thresholds of |log₂FC|> 1 and FDR < 0.05. Functional enrichment of GO terms and KEGG pathways was analyzed using Goatools and KOBAS, respectively.

The reliability of species-specific transcript quantification results was further validated by quantitative qPCR using species-specific marker gene primers (Table S1). The qPCR reactions were performed using ChamQ™ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme).

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Untargeted metabolomic analysis was performed on monoculture and co-culture samples. Cellular extracts were analyzed using a QExactive™ HF Orbitrap mass spectrometer coupled to a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with analytical support provided by Lingen Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Raw UHPLC-MS/MS data were processed in Compound Discoverer 3.1 for peak alignment, quantitation (parameters: retention time tolerance 0.2 min, mass tolerance 5 ppm, signal-to-noise ratio ≥ 3, minimum intensity 105), and normalization to total spectral intensity. Molecular formulas were predicted based on adduct/fragment ion patterns. Metabolite identification was performed by matching peaks to mzCloud, mzVault, and MassList databases, followed by functional annotation via KEGG, HMDB, and LIPID Maps. Differential metabolite screening applied a t-test with thresholds of |log2(FoldChange)|> 1.0, p < 0.05, and VIP (variable importance in projection) ≥ 1.0 (Welling et al. 2021). Bioinformatic annotation of differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) was conducted using KEGG and HMDB databases. DAMs volcano plots were visualized using MetaboAnalyst (https://new.metaboanalyst.ca/home.xhtml) (Chong et al. 2018). DAMs lollipop plot and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were performed via the ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online/.) (Ji et al. 2022). The metabolite-pathway interaction network was constructed using Cytoscape (Shannon et al. 2003).

Statistical analysis

All data were derived from a minimum of three independent biological replicates and are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s test using SPSS Statistics 24.0, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. All graphs were generated with OriginPro 2025 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

Identification of a unique Epicoccum-Mucor symbiosis capable of red pigment production

Then two distinct strains were identified from the above plate and named as SPDX522 and SPDX618 (Fig. 1A). Out of our expectation, monoculture of either strain did not produce red pigment. But the red pigment produced again when cocultured SPDX522 and SPDX618, no matter separate or mixture inoculation in the same plate (Fig. 1B). To identify the producer of the red pigment, both strains were monocultured with the crude extract of each other. Then the whole genome sequencing and the multi-locus sequence alignment, including the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) with intervening 5.8S nuclear ribosomal (nrDNA), partial 28S large subunit nrDNA (LSU) and RNA polymerase II second largest subunit (RPB2) regions, were conducted to elucidate the taxonomy of isolates SPDX522 and SPDX618. The phylogenetic trees based on ITS-28S-Rpb2 joint sequences revealed that isolate SPDX522 exhibited high similarity with M. racemosus, while isolate SPDX618 was classified into E. nigrum (Fig. 1C and D). So the isolate SPDX522 was renamed as M. racemosus SPDX522 (MrS522), and the isolate SPDX618 was renamed as E. nigrum SPDX618 (EnS618). According to the NCBI Taxonomy database, MrS522 (accession number: SAMN54564409) belongs to the phylum Mucoromycota, while EnS618 (accession number: SAMN54564408) belongs to the phylum Ascomycota, reflecting their broad phylogenetic divergence within the fungal kingdom (NCBI 2025). These two species are distantly related phylogenetically.

Optimization of fermentation process for red pigment production

To establish an efficient red pigment production process, we first optimized the inoculation ratio of EnS618 and MrS522 in coculture system, determining a ratio of 1:3 (EnS618:MrS522) as optimal inoculation (Fig. 2A). This ratio likely achieved a balance between the inducer and producer, facilitating pigment production. Subsequently, single-factor experiment and Plackett–Burman design (PBD) were employed to screen key factors for red pigment production. The results showed temperature, rice concentration, and fermentation time were main factors, which further being optimized using Box-Behnken design and response surface methodology (Fig. S1, Tables S2–S6). The predicted optimal conditions are as follows (Fig. 2B): temperature 23.5 °C, fermentation time 92.8 h, and rice concentration 51 g/L, yielding a predicted pigment production of 163.5 U/g. Under these conditions, the pH decreased from 7.0 to 4.3 during fermentation (Fig. 2C). Besides, the transcription level of 45 pre bedicted BGCs in EnS618 was analyzed in monoculture and coculture. The that results showed 17 BGCs were downregulated and 3 BGCs got upregulated in the cocultured EnS618 when compared to monoculture (Fig. 4C). The PKS gene SPDX618009177 is a highly reducing polyketide synthase (HR-PKS) gene with KS/AT/DH/MT/ER/KR/ACP domains, while another PKS gene SPDX618009178 is a non-reducing polyketide synthase (NR-PKS) gene with SAT/KS/AT/ACP/MT/TD domains (Fig. 4E). RT-qPCR results confirmed that both PKS genes (SPDX618009177 and SPDX618009178) were upregulated in cocultured EnS618 (Fig. 4E).

Contrast to the rarely upregulated BGCs in cocultured EnS618, much more BGCs were downregulated (Fig. 4C), including the putative epipyrone cluster (SPDX618003815-SPDX618003818), which is consistent with the result of reduced epipyrone production in Fig. 3.

Discussion

This study reports for the first time that Epicoccum nigrum can be induced by Mucor racemosus to produce red pigments. Mucor-symbiotic systems are widely applied in traditional fermented food production. Sufu derived from the cofermentation with M. racemosus and Rhizopus oryzae demonstrated significantly higher key enzyme activities, biochemical quality, and textural (Wang et al. 2018). The coculture of M. indicus and R. oryzae enhanced key flavor compounds in Chinese turbid rice wine, and improved aroma intensity, taste harmony, and mouthfeel persistence and fullness (Xiang et al. 2019). It is acceptable that E. nigrum is the real producer of red pigments and M. racemosus as the inducer. Studies have shown that E. nigrum produces multiple secondary metabolites and pigments, including yellow and orange compounds such as carotenoids, azaphilones and epicolactone (Gribanovski-Sassu and Foppen 1967; Harwoko et al. 2020; Li et al. 2017, 2019; Shen et al. 2024). Besides, red pigments were rarely reported in E. nigrum. Rhodoxanthin and torularhodin were wine-red pigments produced by E. nigrum, but both were fat-soluble (Gribanovski-Sassu and Foppen 1967). Notably, the red pigments induced in our study are water-soluble, indicating the difference of Mucor-induced Epicoccum red pigments.

Although insufficient purified compound for NMR hindered absolute structural determination, preliminary analysis combining MS/MS data with SIRIUS suggested that the red pigments were putative anthracycline-like compounds. Similar to anthracyclines doxorubicin and epirubicin (Menna et al. 2006), compounds (1) and (4) share a tetracyclic framework. Anthracyclines are derived from the anthraquinone core scaffold, covalently linked to an aminosugar group and a tetrahydrofuran (THF) ring functional group, with their natural biosynthesis originating from Type II polyketide synthase (PKS) gene clusters (Lomovskaya et al. 1999). Notably, the fragments of these pigments appeared to show different side chains from classical anthracyclines, particularly in the tetracyclic core and the presence of phenylacetic acid moieties.

Based on the transcriptomic analysis and RT-qPCR results, genome mining proposed that a dual-PKS (SPDX61809177 and SPDX61809178) cluster might be responsible for their biosynthesis (Fig. 4), which waiting to be confirmed in the future. SPDX61809177 encodes a HR-PKS, whose modular architecture comprising KS/AT/DH/MT/ER/KR/ACP domains can be iteratively utilized to catalyze multiple elongation cycles. SPDX61809178 encodes a NR-PKS containing SAT/KS/AT/ACP/MT/TD domains and exhibits high homology to MpPKS5 from Monascus ruber NRRL 1597, a polyketide synthase located within the azaphilone biosynthesis gene cluster (Balakrishnan et al. 2014). Similar dual-PKS organization commonly occurs in fungi (Zabala et al. 2012) and functions either in a sequential or in a convergent manner to produce the polyketide products, including hypothemycin (Reeves et al. 2008; Zhou et al. 2010), asperfuranone (Chiang et al. 2009), lovastatin (Ma et al. 2009), chaetoviridins/chaetomugilins, asperpyranones (Williams et al. 2021), and sclerotiorin (Sousa et al. 2023).

Moreover, converse to the enrichment of red pigments, much more secondary metabolites in E. nigrum showed lower abundance when cocultured with M. racemosus, indicating that E. nigrum was suppressed by M. racemosus. Notably, the production of epipyrone was decreased in coculture when compared to E. nigrum SPDX618 monoculture. Epipyrone has been reported as one of the yellow compounds produced by E. nigrum (Lima et al. 2020). And epipyrone was reported to have various biological activities, including antifungal, antiviral and antiproliferative activity (Lee et al. 2024; Longobardi et al. 2026). The reduced production of epipyrone is consistent with the downregulation of epn gene expressions in the coculture. Epipyrone is known to be produced also by a polyketide pathway, and epnA encodes a HR-PKS as well (Fan et al. 2024; Lima et al. 2020). Epipyrones and the red pigments share precursors such as acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA, as well as cellular energy requirements. When the metabolic routing turns into red pigment production, the biosynthesis of epipyrone gets blocked in E. nigrum. Although the ecological function of those red pigments is still unclear, reduced epipyrone production that contains antifungal activity places E. nigrum at a competitive disadvantage, which is consistent with the above observations.

Besides, multi-omics analysis revealed the underlying physiological mechanisms of pigment induction during cocultivation. Alterations in phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) and prostaglandin levels suggest the activation of membrane-mediated hyphal recognition and GPCR signaling pathways. As the second most abundant glycerophospholipid in cell membranes, PE has been reported to be related to diverse cellular functions, including phosphatidylcholine biosynthesis, posttranslational modifications, energy metabolism, etc. (Calzada et al. 2016). And the abundance of PE could positively regulate autophagy (Rockenfeller et al. 2015). Besides, it is also known that fungal-derived prostaglandins modulate sporulation and secondary metabolism via G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR)-mediated signaling pathways (Tsitsigiannis and Keller 2007). Meanwhile, the elevated levels of methionine sulfoxide and nucleoside derivatives indicated accumulated intracellular oxidative stress (Davies 2025; Kim et al. 2017). These results indicated a membrane-mediated fungal-fungal hyphae interaction and competition occurred during coculture.

The broad suppression of the identified core metabolic pathways indicate a comprehensive inhibition of primary metabolism during coculture. Since intermediates of primary metabolites are the precursors of secondary metabolites, the downregulation of these metabolites and their pathways limits the precursor supply for secondary metabolite production. Consequently, metabolic resources appear to be strategically redirected toward the specific activation of the red pigment biosynthetic cluster, rather than being distributed across multiple secondary metabolic routes. This reflects a metabolic trade-off wherein the fungus prioritizes the production of a specialized defensive metabolite in response to interspecific competition, at the expense of broader secondary metabolic diversification. Such dynamic flux redistribution highlights how fungal cocultivation can rewire metabolic networks through substrate channeling and cross-pathway regulation, effectively unlocking otherwise silent biosynthetic potential.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we report a coculture system containing Epicoccum nigrum SPDX618 and Mucor racemosus SPDX522 from vinegar mash that constitutively produces anthracycline-like red pigments. EnS618 acts as the producer and harbors an upregulated dual-PKS gene cluster during coculture that might be responsible for red pigment biosynthesis, while MrS522 performs as the elicitor. Although red pigment biosynthesis got activated, much more secondary metabolites and primary metabolites were downregulated during coculture. This study primarily serves as a fundamental exploratory investigation into coculture-triggered metabolic reprogramming in fungi, laying a foundation for further research on the application potential of this coculture system and natural pigments.
