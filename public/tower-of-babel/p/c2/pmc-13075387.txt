Maternal Obesity, Gestational Diabetes Mellitus and Offspring's Body Mass Index Trajectories From Birth to Age 6 Years: Glucose in Relation to Women and Babies' Health (GrownB) Study
Authors: Cheng‐Tzu Hsieh, Xinyue Liu, Xiang Li, Rana F Chehab, Yeyi Zhu, Liwei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo examine whether offspring born to mothers with overweight/obesity, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) or both exhibit different body mass index (BMI) trajectories from birth to 6 years old.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingThe Glucose in Relation to Women and Babies' Health (GrownB) Study at the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) Medical Center.Population or SampleTwenty thousand four hundred fifty six mother–offspring pairs.MethodsMothers were classified as normal‐weight & non‐GDM (NWN), normal‐weight & GDM (NWG), overweight/obesity & non‐GDM (OWN) or overweight/obesity & GDM (OWG). Offspring BMI trajectories were estimated using linear mixed‐effects models in two age periods (0–24 months and 2–6 years), adjusting for confounding.Main Outcome MeasuresWe extracted offspring's birth weight, recumbent length (age < 2 years) or standing height (age ≥ 2 years), and weight data from routine clinical encounters and physical examinations from the UCLA electronic health records.ResultsOffspring of mothers with overweight/obesity had higher BMI trajectories than those of normal‐weight mothers across both 0–24 months (BMI mean differences overall 0–24 months: 0.28 [0.17, 0.39] kg/m2 for OWN vs. NWN; 0.53 [0.21, 0.85] kg/m2 for OWG vs. NWG) and 2–6 years (0.58 [0.45, 0.70] kg/m2 for OWN vs. NWN; 0.53 [0.20, 0.87] kg/m2 for OWG vs. NWG). Introduction

The prevalence of childhood obesity is continuing to increase in the United States (U.S.) [1]. Although the cause of the current childhood obesity epidemic is complicated and involves multiple factors at multiple levels (e.g., individual, family, neighbourhood and community), emerging evidence has indicated that early‐life exposures, including maternal metabolic conditions during pregnancy, play a critical role. In particular, maternal pre‐pregnancy overweight/obesity [2, 3] and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) [4] have both been identified as major risk factors for the development of obesity in childhood.

However, most previous studies only reported such associations at single age of offspring, without information regarding the children's growth trajectories [2, 3, 4, 5]. Children's growth is dynamic and nonlinear. In fact, their body mass index (BMI) rises rapidly to the peak in the first year of life, then declines to a nadir at approximately 4–8 years of age [6]. Emerging evidence has suggested that the overall growth trajectory patterns may be more informative for understanding the development of childhood obesity [7]. Up to date, only a few studies have examined the associations between pre‐pregnancy overweight/obesity or GDM with offspring BMI trajectories [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. In addition, most previous studies only focused on maternal pre‐pregnancy overweight/obesity or GDM, ignoring the strong correlation between these two conditions. Moreover, evidence from racially and ethnically diverse U.S. population is limited, despite well‐documented racial disparities in maternal pre‐pregnancy overweight/obesity [15] and GDM [16] prevalence in the U.S.

Therefore, we aimed to investigate the independent and joint associations of maternal pre‐pregnancy overweight/obesity and GDM with offspring childhood BMI trajectories in a large, racially diverse birth cohort in the U.S. We also examined whether these associations differ by race and ethnicity.

Methods

Data Source and Population

In current study, we used the data from the Glucose in Relation to Women and Babies' Health (GrownB) Study, which included 26 436 mother–offspring pairs who delivered live births and received care at the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) Medical Center between March 2, 2013 and August 31, 2021. We only included singletons birth and the first pregnancy during the study period (N = 20 998). We then excluded mothers with pre‐pregnancy diabetes (N = 330), offspring with missing birth weight (N = 205) and offspring without any height or weight measurements after birth (N = 7). The final analytical sample comprised 20 456 mother–offspring pairs (Figure 1). All information was extracted from the UCLA medical center electronic health records (EHR). UCLA health system applies Universal Consent process allows patients to consent to the use of their data and biological samples for research purposes. The study was approved by the UCLA Institutional Review Board.

Maternal Overweight/Obesity and Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM)

We extracted mother's height and weight measured by healthcare professionals at the first pre‐natal visit, with the median gestational age of 10.9 weeks (interquartile range: 5.4–12.1 weeks). Only 4.5% of women had their first prenatal visit after 13 weeks. We calculated BMIs using the weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. Those who had BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 were classified as being overweight/obesity. Mothers' weight (nearest 0.1 kg) was measured using calibrated clinical scales with mothers wearing light clothing and no shoes. Height (nearest 0.1 cm) was measured using a wall‐mounted stadiometer or length board with mothers properly positioned according to standard clinical procedures. We identified GDM using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes (ICD‐9‐CM: 648.8×; or ICD‐10‐CM: O24.4×). The UCLA Medical Center applied GDM screening between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation using a two‐step approach as recommended by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists since 2001 [17], with pregnant women screened first by a non‐fasting 50 g 1‐h oral glucose challenge test (GCT) then followed by a 100 g 3‐h oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) if the glucose value was ≥ 140 mg/L during the GCT. GDM was diagnosed as having ≥ 2 abnormal OGTT values using the Carpenter and Coustan criteria (i.e., fasting ≥ 95 mg/dL, 1‐h ≥ 180 mg/dL; 2‐h ≥ 155 mg/L; or 3‐h ≥ 140 mg/L). In the GrownB dataset, 53% of mothers had laboratory data available for GDM screening. The overall accuracy of all ICD codes was 97.2%, with a sensitivity of 96.2%, specificity of 97.3%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 72.5% and negative predictive value (NPV) of 99.7% [18, 19].

Offspring's Birth Weight and BMI

We extracted offspring's birth weight, recumbent length (age < 2 years) or standing height (age ≥ 2 years), and weight data from routine clinical encounters and physical examinations from the UCLA EHR for BMI calculation; each offspring had multiple measurements from birth to 6 years old in our EHR data. Height and weight measurements were collected by healthcare professionals following the established clinical protocols. Weight (nearest 0.1 kg) and recumbent length (nearest 0.1 cm) for offspring < 2 years were measured by trained healthcare professionals using calibrated infant scales and an infantometer (length board) during routine clinical care. For offspring ≥ 2 years, the measurement methods were the same as adults. The number and percentage of BMI measurements were shown in Table S1.

Covariates

We extracted data on all covariates from the UCLA EHR, including maternal age at delivery, self‐identified race and ethnicity (White, Black, Hispanic), Asian or Pacific Islander (API) and ‘Other race’ (i.e., American Indians or Alaska Native, multiracial, other and unknown), mother's marital status (yes or no), smoking and alcohol drinking status during pregnancy (yes or no), area deprivation index (ADI < 3.5 or ≥ 3.5; recognised as social constructs, higher ADI indicates economically disadvantaged), weight gain during pregnancy (continuous variable, mothers' weights at delivery minus by weights at the first pre‐natal visit, measured in pound), Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI = 0, 1 or ≥ 2; a clinical scoring system that quantifies a patient's overall disease burden, higher CCI indicates greater burden of mortality risk) and offspring's sex.

Statistical Analysis

All the mother‐offspring pairs were categorised by maternal pre‐pregnancy overweight/obesity and GDM status into one of the four groups: normal‐weight & non‐GDM (NWN), normal‐weight & GDM (NWG), overweight/obesity & non‐GDM (OWN) and overweight/obesity & GDM (OWG). We compared baseline characteristics of the four groups, using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means and standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables.

We applied linear mixed effects models with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparison and calculated the least‐square means of BMI and the 95% confidence intervals (CI) over offspring's age in each group. The dependent variable was offspring BMI, with repeated measurements from birth to 6 years of age. The independent variables were maternal pre‐pregnancy BMI and GDM groups (NWN, NWG, OWN and OWG). To better account for the different growth patterns due to infancy adiposity peak [20] and the changed from recumbent length (< 2 years old) to standing height (after 2 years old), we stratified the analysis into ‘birth to 24 months’ (analysed mean difference for the whole period and in 1‐month intervals) and ‘2–6 years old’ (analysed mean difference for the whole period and in 1‐year intervals) periods, and fit two models separately (‘birth to 24 months’ period model, and ‘2–6 years old’ period model). In both models, offspring age was included as fixed effect variable, and a subject‐specific random intercept with a random slope for age was specified to account for within‐child correlation of repeated BMI measurements. To determine the appropriate functional form of offspring age on BMI, we sequentially evaluated linear, quadratic, cubic and quartic polynomial terms for age. Model fit was compared using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and the model with the lowest BIC was selected. The final ‘birth to 24 months’ period model used a cubic polynomial of age for the fixed effects and linear for the random effect. In addition, we observed a single knot at 8 months from the crude offspring's BMI means (Figure S1). We therefore included a spline with a single knot at 8 months in the ‘birth to 24 months’ period model that allowed difference slopes before and after age 8 months (Formula S). The final ‘2–6 years old’ period model used a quadratic polynomial of age for the fixed effects and linear for the random effect (Formula S).

We adjusted for maternal age, offspring's sex, mother's race and ethnicity, mother's marital status, smoking and alcohol drinking status during pregnancy, area deprivation index, weight gain during pregnancy and Charlson Comorbidity Index as covariates in both ‘birth to 24 months’ and ‘2–6 years old’ models. Missing values in covariates were treated as a separate category in primary analysis. We also conducted several sensitivity analyses: (1) applied Robin's multiple imputations [21] (five imputations) for the missing covariates; (2) excluded mothers whose first prenatal visit occurred ≥ 13 weeks of gestation (N = 19 532); (3) excluded mothers who had pre‐pregnancy BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 (N = 19 932); and (4) removed gestational weight gain from the adjusted models. All statistical tests were two‐tailed and a p value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Participants Characteristics

Of a total 20 456 mothers, the mean maternal age at delivery was 33.2 (5.1) years; 40.7% were White, 23.1% were Hispanic, 17.6% were API, 13.5% were ‘Other race’ and 5.1% were Black. Overall, 10 672 (52.2%) mothers were NWN, 813 (4.0%) mothers were NWG, 7854 (38.4%) were OWN and 1117 (5.5%) were OWG. Compared to NWN group, mothers in OWN and OWG groups were more likely to be Hispanic and to live in neighbourhoods with higher area deprivation index (a higher level of socioeconomic disadvantage), and less likely to be married (Table 1). The mean offspring birth weight was highest for offspring in OWN group (3326.7 [558.1] grams), followed by OWG (3265.5 [580.6] grams), NWN (3237.9 [552.0] grams) and NWG (3145.3 [489.5] grams). From birth to 6 years old, the mean offspring BMI measurement times was 9.9 times (10.2).

Offspring BMI Trajectories From Birth to 24 Months of Age

For BMI trajectories during ‘birth to 24 months’, consistently higher BMI trajectories were observed among offspring with overweight/obesity mothers without or with GDM: BMI mean difference for the whole period was 0.28 (0.17, 0.39) kg/m2 for OWN vs. NWN (differences ranged from 0.06 to 0.54 kg/m2 [all p < 0.05, except for months 1–3]), and was 0.53 (0.21, 0.85) kg/m2 for OWG vs. NWG (ranged from 0.21 to 0.60 kg/m2 [all p < 0.05, except for months 1–4, 21 and 24]). We did not observe differences in BMI trajectories by GDM status (i.e., between offspring in NWG and NWN groups; −0.21 (−0.47, 0.04) kg/m2 [all p > 0.64]). Although we observed offspring exposed to both overweight/obesity and GDM (i.e., OWG) had consistently higher BMI trajectories than offspring in NWN group (BMI mean difference: 0.32 [0.10, 0.53] kg/m2; ranged from 0.16 to 0.54 kg/m2; all p < 0.05, except for months 0, 8, 9 and 21–24), there were no differences between OWG and OWN (all p > 0.21), indicating no joint effect between pre‐pregnancy overweight/obesity and GDM (p for interaction = 0.25; Figure 2; Table S2). In the subgroup analysis, similar BMI trajectories patterns were observed in White, Hispanic and API offspring. However, we could not observe clear patterns for Black offspring and 95% CIs at all ages were wide and overlapped cross groups (Figure 2). All the results were similar in our sensitivity analyses (Figure S2, Table S3).

Offspring BMI Trajectories From Age 2 to 6 Years Old

For BMI trajectories during 2 to 6 years, consistently higher BMI trajectories were observed among offspring with overweight/obesity mothers without or with GDM: BMI mean difference for the whole period was 0.58 (0.45, 0.70) kg/m2 for OWN vs. NWN (ranged from 0.45 to 1.12 kg/m2 [all p < 0.05]), was 0.53 (0.20, 0.87) kg/m2 for OWG vs. NWG (ranged from 0.34 to 0.75 kg/m2 [all p < 0.05]). We did not observe differences in BMI trajectories by GDM status (i.e., between offspring in NWG and NWN groups; 0.00 (−0.26, 0.25) kg/m2 [all p > 0.99]). Although we also observed offspring exposed to both overweight/obesity and GDM (i.e., OWG) had consistently higher BMI trajectories than offspring in NWN group (BMI mean difference: 0.53 [0.29, 0.77] kg/m2; ranged from 0.34 to 0.98 kg/m2; all p < 0.05), there were no differences between OWG and OWN (all p > 0.99), indicating no joint effect between pre‐pregnancy overweight/obesity and GDM (p for interaction = 0.78; Figure 3, Table S2). In the subgroup analysis by race and ethnicity, the BMI trajectories were similar as in the primary analysis for White, Hispanic and API. We did not observe any significant differences between BMI trajectories among Black offspring (Figure 3). All the results were similar in our sensitivity analyses (Figure S3, Table S3).

Discussion

In this large, racially diverse birth cohort with offspring followed up to 6 years, we found that offspring born to mothers with pre‐pregnancy overweight/obesity had higher BMI trajectories than offspring born to mothers with normal pre‐pregnancy weight. Although offspring born to mothers with both pre‐pregnancy overweight/obesity and GDM had the highest mean BMI and BMI trajectory over first 6 years of life, there was no significant difference regarding with or without GDM. The same patterns were evident among White, Hispanic and API offspring, but not apparent among Black offspring.

Previous seven studies (three in China [8, 9, 22], and one each in Spain [10], France [11], Australia [12] and Canada [13]) with follow‐up ages ranging from 2 to 10 years all reported higher BMI trajectories among offspring of mothers with pre‐pregnancy overweight/obesity, which are in the same line with our findings. To our best knowledge, current study may be the first to examine pre‐pregnancy overweight/obesity and offspring BMI trajectories in the U.S., with a substantially larger sample (20 456 vs. ≤ 4492 in previous studies) and including more racial and ethnic groups, therefore, proving more precise estimations and new insights related to racial and ethnic specific relationship.

Regarding the association between GDM and childhood obesity, previous findings are mixed, with the positive association being more likely to be shown when offspring reached adolescence or young adulthood [4, 23, 24]. Among studies focusing on children under age 5 [9, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29], only two found a positive association [9, 29], suggesting inconsistent evidence for children under 5 years. A recent study by Deng et al. using data from the landmark Hyperglycemia and Adverse Pregnancy Outcome (HAPO) Study Hong Kong field center followed offspring from birth through late childhood/adolescence (9–14 years) and reported that offspring born to mothers with GDM were more likely to have a rapidly increasing skinfold thickness trajectory [14], suggesting GDM was positively associated with offspring adiposity at age 9–14 years. It is noteworthy that it is not possible to make the direct comparison between our findings and the HAPO Hong Kong study because of several differences. First, the HAPO study used a one‐step approach (i.e., 75‐g 2‐h OGTT tests) to screen for GDM, while our study site applied a 2‐step approach (i.e., first 50 g GCT followed by the 100 g 3‐h OGTT tests among those who tested positively by CGT). Second, the study by Deng et al. performed group‐based trajectory modelling based on offspring skinfold thickness, while our study compared the offspring BMI trajectory between GDM and non‐GDM groups. Third, the study by Deng et al. followed offspring up to 9–14 years, while we only followed offspring up to age 6 years. Previous studies tend to suggest that the effect of maternal hyperglycemia on offspring growth and obesity risk may not show up in early childhood [23, 24]. Two previous studies using the multinational HAPO study also found that maternal hyperglycemia was not associated with offspring BMI at ages 2 and 5 years [30, 31] but positively associated with offspring BMI at ages 10–14 years [23, 32]. Lastly, mothers with elevated glucose in the original HAPO study were blinded and untreated. Thus, results from the HAPO Hong Kong study may reflect the unmitigated biological effects of maternal hyperglycemia.

Among studies have compared offspring BMI trajectories by maternal GDM status (five in China [8, 9, 14, 29, 33], two in the United States [34, 35] and one each in Australia [36] and Spain [10]), findings varied depending on follow‐up duration and adjustment for maternal pre‐pregnancy BMI. In a U.S. study using data from Exploring Perinatal Outcomes among Children (EPOCH) study [35], Crume et al. reported that the offspring born to mothers with GDM had the same BMI trajectory from birth to 27 months compared to those who born to mothers without GDM, but the difference was shown after 27 months and more apparent from 10 to 13 years of age. Notably, only sex and race/ethnicity were adjusted in their comparisons. In a following study using a subset of EPOCH sample with maternal pre‐pregnancy BMI [34], Hockett et al., found that there was not an association between exposure to GDM and offspring BMI trajectories during early childhood (approximately 27 months to 6 years) after adjustment for pre‐pregnancy BMI, which consistent with findings from our study. In a study conducted in Tianjin, China and followed offspring from birth to age of 8 years [9], Cao et al. found that GDM was only positively associated with the ‘late‐onset (i.e., normal BMI between 1 to 5 years of age but a higher BMI from 5 to 8 years)’ but not the ‘persistent (i.e., persistent high BMI 0‐8 years)’ patterns in offspring, after adjusting for pre‐pregnancy BMI. Other studies from China [29, 33] and Spain [10] following children up to Age 4 reported either null [10, 33] or attenuated associations after controlling for pre‐pregnancy BMI [29]. Taken together, available evidence tends to suggest that GDM may impact offspring's obesity risk shown in late childhood and afterward.

Another alternative explanation for the non‐significant associations between GDM and offspring BMI trajectories may be related to the effective clinical management of GDM such as dietary counselling, glucose monitoring or pharmacologic treatment which could bring maternal glucose to normal. Including GDM mothers who had effective glycemic control could attenuate the association between GDM and early childhood BMI trajectories. At UCLA Medical Center, the site of the current study, women diagnosed with GDM are receiving standard clinical care. As a result, the observed no association between GDM and offspring BMI in current study may reflect the mitigating effects of clinical management. Actually, we did observe that mean offspring birth weight among GDM groups was slightly lower than that among non‐GDM groups across BMI strata (OWG vs. OWN: 3265.5 vs. 3326.7 g; NWG vs. NWN: 3145.3 vs. 3237.9 g), which may reflect effective glycemic control and prevention of excessive fetal growth. Unfortunately, information on GDM treatments is not available in our EHR dataset. Further studies are warranted to investigate whether effective clinical management among mothers with GDM (successfully control maternal glucose level to normal) may mitigate the impact of GDM on childhood growth or adiposity development.

It is well documented that maternal overweight/obesity and GDM are highly correlated (i.e., could be modify or confound each other's effect) and may share biological pathways to offspring obesity such as fetal over‐nutrition [37]. A study conducted by Cao et al. [9] in China, has designed to examine both individual and combined associations of these two factors with offspring growth trajectories. They found that maternal overweight/obesity increased the likelihood of both ‘persistent’ and ‘late‐onset’ obesity trajectories, independent of GDM, whereas GDM alone associated with only the ‘late‐onset’ pattern. Interestingly, they found offspring exposed to both conditions had a higher risk of developing ‘late‐onset’ pattern, suggesting a joint effect [38]. Our study is the first to investigate the joint effect of pre‐pregnancy overweight/obesity and GDM on offspring BMI trajectory from birth to 6 years in the U.S. Although we did not detect the joint effect, it is plausible that we only followed offspring up to 6 years of age. The effect of GDM alone or the joint effects may become more apparent as offspring grow older, as suggested by Cao's study in China.

The mechanisms underlying the positive associations between maternal pre‐pregnancy overweight/obesity and offspring BMI trajectories are complex and could involve genetic predisposition, fetal over‐nutrition and inflammation [39]. The fetal over‐nutrition hypothesis intrauterine exposure to excess nutrients could lead to long‐lasting changes, through epigenetic mechanisms, in fetus fat tissue development, appetite control and energy utilisation, raising obesity risk later in life [39].

The strengths of the current study include a large sample size and a racial/ethnic‐diverse study population with adequate numbers of under‐studied minorities such as Hispanic and API. However, several limitations should be noted. First, despite the racial/ethnic diversity, only about 5% of participants were Black, which may result in inadequate statistical power to detect patterns among Black offspring. Nevertheless, the study still included more than 1000 Black mother–child pairs. Second, all participants were recruited from UCLA Medical Center, one of the largest healthcare systems in the United States, which may limit the generalisability of our findings to other populations. Third, although some important lifestyle covariates, such as breastfeeding, dietary factors and physical activity, were associated with childhood obesity risk, we did not have available data for those variables, leading to potential residual confounders.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in this racially and ethnically diverse cohort, consistently higher BMI trajectories from birth to 6 years of age were observed among offspring born to mother with pre‐pregnancy overweight/obesity, regardless of GDM status. The patterns were similar in White, Hispanic and API, while absent in Black. Our findings provide new insights in the U.S. population and support the importance of reducing maternal overweight/obesity before pregnancy, which will benefit both mothers and their offspring. Further studies with longer follow‐up periods are warranted to comprehensively compare the effects of maternal metabolic conditions on offspring obesity risk.
