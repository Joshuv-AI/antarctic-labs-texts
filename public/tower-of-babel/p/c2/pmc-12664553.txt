Targeting the mutant androgen receptor with PROTACs in spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy
Authors: Changwoo Lee, Janghoo Lim
Published: 2025

Spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy (SBMA, or Kennedy's disease) is a rare neuromuscular disorder caused by a CAG repeat expansion in exon 1 of the androgen receptor (AR) gene [1]. The resulting polyglutamine (polyQ)-expanded AR protein is prone to misfolding and aggregation, triggering progressive dysfunction and degeneration in motor neurons and skeletal muscle [2]. Because AR is X-linked, and ligand (androgen)-dependent nuclear translocation of the polyQ-AR is required for pathogenesis [3,4], the disease manifests almost exclusively in males. Although SBMA is a monogenic disease with a clearly defined toxic driver, therapeutic progress has been challenging. The field has searched for ways to mitigate toxicity by reducing androgen signaling or enhancing protein clearance, but clinical trials of androgen deprivation, such as leuprorelin [[5], [6], [7]] and dutasteride [8] have yielded limited benefit.

In this issue of Neurotherapeutics, Sangotra et al. [9] introduce a new approach: using proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) to selectively degrade the mutant protein. While the AR-directed PROTACs were originally developed for the treatment of prostate cancer, which have advanced to clinical trials [[10], [11], [12]], the authors repurposed these compounds to target polyQ-expanded mutant AR in SBMA. Among the AR degraders (ARDs) they tested, ARD-1676 was shown to efficiently promote proteasomal clearance of mutant AR in diverse disease models, including a polyQ-AR-overexpressing neuron-like cell line, patient iPSC-derived motor neurons and skeletal muscle cells, and a knock-in mouse model of SBMA. Furthermore, ARD-1676 partially normalized transcriptional dysregulation in patient-derived muscle cells within 24 ​h, suggesting that clearance of mutant AR rapidly alleviates downstream molecular pathology. Importantly, ARD-1676 treatment did not alter serum biomarkers of liver or kidney function in mice, supporting its safety profile. This is consistent with a previous report that ARD-1676 demonstrated antitumor efficacy in mouse xenograft models without causing weight loss or other signs of systemic toxicity [12]. Such evidence of target engagement across disease models, coupled with transcriptional-level functional benefits and an overall favorable safety profile, positions AR PROTACs as a promising therapeutic class for SBMA.

A central feature that distinguishes PROTACs from conventional inhibitors is their catalytic mode of action. Unlike traditional inhibitors that require continuous occupancy, PROTACs are heterobifunctional small molecules composed of a target-binding ligand linked to an E3 ligase-recruiting ligand, which together promote iterative cycles of ubiquitination and degradation [13]. This mechanism enables the selective and efficient clearance of pathogenic proteins at relatively low doses. Since its introduction in 2001 [14], PROTAC has been expanded to a wide range of therapeutic areas, including oncology, inflammation, and neurodegenerative diseases, such as tauopathies, α-synucleinopathies, and Huntington's disease (HD) [13]. The advantages of PROTACs include their pharmacological reversibility and the ability to target previously “undruggable” proteins [13]. Plus, these ARDs are orally bioavailable, making this approach more favorable for translation.

While Sangotra et al.’s findings highlight clear promise, further characterization and delivery improvements may broaden the impact of AR PROTACs. In SBMA knock-in mice, oral administration of ARD-1676 effectively reduced AR in muscle but not in the spinal cord, reflecting the limited blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetration of PROTACs. Because both skeletal muscle and spinal motor neurons contribute to disease pathogenesis [[15], [16], [17]], targeting both disease-relevant tissues has long been considered essential. Interestingly, a recent study showed that targeting mutant AR with antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) in skeletal muscle ameliorated motor neuron pathology, even at symptomatic stages, without directly reducing AR levels in the spinal cord, suggesting that peripheral interventions can indirectly modulate central pathology [18]. Further in vivo studies examining whether oral AR PROTAC administration can similarly mitigate spinal cord pathology will be of high interest. If additional CNS targeting proves necessary, strategies to enhance BBB permeability or alternative delivery routes could provide a more comprehensive therapeutic effect.

SBMA patients carry a single mutant AR allele on the X chromosome without a wild-type copy. This makes it both challenging and essential to mitigate the toxic gain-of-function of polyQ-expanded AR without abolishing the normal physiological functions of AR, since further loss of signaling could exacerbate features of androgen insensitivity. Careful optimization of dosing will, therefore, be critical to balance efficacy and safety. Relevant insights come from a study showing that transient suppression of mutant huntingtin (mHTT) by ASO delayed disease progression in HD mouse models, even after mHTT expression normalized [19]. This suggests that transient repression of a toxic protein can have sustained benefit. Thus, optimizing dosing intervals of the AR PROTACTs may prevent the progressive buildup of toxicity while minimizing loss-of-function effects (Fig. 1). In addition, strategies aimed at supplementing or restoring native AR function may be complementary [20]. Finally, thorough characterization of long-term safety and the risk of tolerance or compensatory adaptations will be critical for clinical translation.

Beyond SBMA, the demonstration of efficacy with an AR-directed PROTAC, together with recent advances using mHTT-targeting PROTACs in HD [21,22], highlights the broader potential of targeted protein degradation for other polyQ diseases. Spinocerebellar ataxias (SCAs), such as SCA1 and SCA3, share the fundamental problem of toxic protein accumulation [23], suggesting that degraders directed against mutant ataxin-1 or ataxin-3 could represent viable therapeutic strategies. As mentioned above, combining PROTACs with other modalities may also prove synergistic. Thus, the implications of this work extend well beyond SBMA, pointing to targeted protein degradation as a promising therapeutic avenue across the spectrum of polyQ and other related diseases.

Taken together, these findings represent an important advance toward addressing the long-standing therapeutic impasse in SBMA. By directly degrading the mutant AR, PROTACs may overcome the limitations of earlier androgen reduction, which have shown limited success in patients. Moving this strategy toward the clinic will require careful characterization and continued optimization. Nonetheless, this work underscores the promise of targeted protein degradation in SBMA and beyond, offering a foundation upon which AR PROTACs may one day deliver tangible benefit to patients.
