Soft microfingers with flexible tactile sensor using liquid metal for in situ evaluation of cellular spheroid stiffness
Authors: Satoshi Konishi, Kodai Koyanagi, Tomohiro Nakatsuka, Yuta Kaneko, Kosuke Kusamori, Makiya Nishikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inflatable soft microfingers integrated with liquid metal strain gauge sensors have been developed for the in situ evaluation of cellular spheroid stiffness. Cellular spheroids ranging from several hundred micrometers to a few millimeters in diameter, typical three-dimensional cellular aggregates, have been used as biological models. Cellular spheroids are evaluated using various methods, of which microscopic imaging is the most popular. This study focuses on mechanical characteristics, such as stiffness, related to the internal structure of cellular spheroids and reports an evaluation device for in situ stiffness measurement in pinching cellular spheroids. We demonstrated microfingers that pinch cellular spheroids and evaluated cellular spheroid stiffness so that different cellular spheroids (C3H10T1/2 and NIH3T3) could be compared and distinguished by their stiffness. Two microfingers (3.6 mm × 1.2 mm × 380 μm) made of polydimethylsiloxane are implemented for gentle pinching of an object. Flexible liquid-metal strain gauges were integrated into individual microfingers for stiffness evaluation while pinching an object without compromising the soft features of the microfingers. Our results demonstrate the potential of soft microfingers as an in-situ evaluation tool for cellular spheroids, which are anticipated to be an alternative biological model for various applications, such as drug screening.

Introduction

Cultured cells are widely used in tissue engineering, drug discovery, and regenerative medicine. Cells cultured in two-dimensional (2D) conditions, such as in a dish, are not representative of cells in a complex in vivo environment. The high failure rate of drug discovery using cultured cells is thought to be caused by the difference between 2D cultured cells and in vivo conditions1. Three-dimensional cellular aggregates have been developed as in vitro biological models to provide cultured cells in a state that approximately represents a living body. Various methods such as 3D bioprinting technology2,3 have been reported for 3D cellular aggregates. Three-dimensional bioprinting technology provides complicated layer-by-layer structures of cell-laden functional tissues. Gelatin-based 3D bioprinting technology has been used for spheroid formation3. There are several methods for culturing cells in spheroids in addition to 3D bioprinting, including hanging droplet technology4–10. A microfluidic-based hanging-drop culture system for the aggregation of spheroids has been reported, in which a tapered tube was designed for the high stability of droplets during liquid exchange6. 3D culturing techniques enable the functionalization of mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) spheroids9. MSC spheroids provide cell–cell interactions, enhanced phenotypic profiles, and trophic and immunomodulatory functionalities for effective MSC-based products. A spheroid is typically several hundred micrometers in size, depending on the nutrient and oxygen supply to the spheroid core. As mentioned above, spheroids are cultured in microwell plates or hanging drop systems using conventional pipetting or microfluidics. Various methods have been used to manipulate cells and cellular aggregates11–14.

As an advanced tool for dealing with cellular aggregates, we designed a 3D microfluidic device for a U-shaped bottom microwell plate, enabling the manipulation of a cellular spheroid cultured in a microwell plate15. It was inserted into a microwell to generate a flow and transport cellular spheroids into a trap in the device. We then proposed microfingers for damage-free manipulation of a cellular spheroid by taking advantage of the small, soft, and safe properties of microfingers16. Microfingers are made of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) and driven by pneumatic balloon actuators (PBAs)17. Paired and bonded microfingers facing each other were designed to pinch cellular spheroids between them. The microfingers were normally closed in the initial state and opened by the PBAs. In addition to the pinching function, the microfingers can release cellular spheroids at a desired location. The microfingers were subsequently functionalized with an optical fiberscope for in situ observation18.

Recently, the mechanical properties of cells and tissues have gained attention for further understanding of cellular behavior19–25. Cell and tissue mechanics in cell migration have been reviewed and discussed in terms of passive and active mechanics and the mechanical basis of durotaxis, plithotaxis, and mechanotransduction19. A force sensor probe that uses a quartz crystal resonator was reported to achieve the mechanical characterization of a HepG2 spheroid20. Changes in the stiffness of the HepG2 spheroids were evaluated over different culture periods. Changes in cell mechanics as cellular physical factors have been examined in terms of tumor suppression21.

Micropipettes, atomic force microscopy (AFM), and microtweezers have been used for the evaluation of cellular mechanics22–24. The mechanical behavior of living cells has been studied using micropipette suction22, and the surface tension and viscosity of neutrophils have been evaluated using micropipette aspiration. The single-cell mechanics of the living and dead cells with and without fixation were evaluated using AFM23. Microtweezers were used for the stiffness analysis of the 3D spheroids24. Nanometric probe-integrated microfluidic chips have been used for on-chip cellular force measurements25.

This paper presents additional functions of soft microfingers for the in situ evaluation of cellular spheroid stiffness based on previous results. We previously reported a preliminary device for evaluating cellular spheroid stiffness26. The reported pinching device was driven by a piezo-bimorph actuator instead of PBAs, and integrated with a Si strain gauge sensor. We also reported the development of soft microfingers using a PBA composed of PDMS for cellular aggregate manipulation16. A 200 µm cellular aggregate was successfully pinched and released without causing serious damage. We demonstrated microfingers that pinch cellular spheroids and evaluated the cellular spheroid stiffness. Device Design

The microfingers were formed by bonding two microfingers, as shown in Fig. 2(a). Each microfinger was divided into tip, sensing, and wiring sections (Fig. 2(b)). The parameters for the structural dimensions defined in Fig. 2(b) were determined based on the following considerations. In the basic design, each microfinger is composed of three PDMS layers, as shown in Fig. 2(c). The upper PDMS layer is used as an inflatable PBA film. The middle layer functions as the base structure of the PBA and as a pressure separation wall. A liquid metal strain gauge sensor was integrated into the lower layer. The middle layer, as a pressure-separation wall, restricted undesired inflatable deformation of the strain gauge sensor in the lower layer. In addition, parylene(poly-p-xylylene)-C was deposited on the surface of the upper inflatable film of the PBA for adhesion control owing to the detachment of the object by the opening motion of the microfingers. Sensing range estimation for evaluation of cellular spheroid stiffness

It is useful to estimate the stiffness of the cellular spheroids to evaluate cellular spheroid deformation by pinching between microfingers. Therefore, the stiffness of the cellular spheroids was estimated by measuring their deformation against pushing force using a load cell. The restoring force Fr was measured by the load cell when a cellular spheroid was compressed by the load cell in the same manner as used in a previous study26. In this study, two types of cellular spheroids, prepared as previously reported27 using the mouse mesenchymal stromal cell line C3H10T1/2 (JCRB9080; Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (JCRB), Osaka, Japan) and the mouse fibroblast cell line NIH3T3 (RCB2767; RIKEN Bioresource Center (RIKEN BRC), Ibaraki, Japan), were used to discriminate stiffness. Design of soft microfingers for cellular spheroid application

Based on a preliminary evaluation of the cellular spheroid stiffness, the dimensions of the microfinger (length L, width W, and thickness H) (Fig. 2) were designed. These parameters were determined by the allowable pinching force Fallowable to prevent damage to the cellular spheroids. The allowable deformation rate for cellular spheroids was defined as 50%. Thus, the allowable pinching force Fallowable was designed to be the pinching force required to achieve 50% deformation rate in NIH3T3 spheroids, which was relatively elastic between the two types. The average diameters of the C3H10T1/2 and NIH3T3 spheroids were 300.4 μm and 299.4 μm, respectively. Consequently, the diameter of the cellular spheroid was assumed to be approximately 300 μm in this study. The allowable pinching force Fallowable=73 µN was led by substituting r = 150 μm, δ = 150 μm, ν = 0.5, and Ec=5.2 kPa into Eq. (1). Stoppers on inner surfaces of microfingers were positioned to ensure the gap between the two fingers create a gap of larger than 150 μm (50% deformation rate) (Fig. 2).

The finger length L was determined to be 3.6 mm from Eq. (6), considering the limitations in the manufacturing accuracy. The finger width W was determined to be 1.2 mm following an aspect ratio L:W = 3:1. The length of the PBA l was designed to be 2.5 mm, considering the length of the microfinger and stopper. The balloon width w was set to 300 μm to ensure sufficient area for securing a space for the liquid metal strain sensor.

Finally, a liquid-metal strain sensor was designed considering its flexible structure. The liquid metal injected into the PDMS channel functions as a strain sensor that deforms together with the microfinger. The structure of the channel filled with liquid metal can be divided into sensing and wiring sections (Fig. 2(b)). The electrical resistance of the wiring section was designed to be sufficiently lower than that of the sensing section. A narrow and long channel was designed to increase the resistance within the limited space of the microfinger. Simultaneously, the pressure loss in the channel, which depends on the channel dimensions, was also considered to enable liquid metal injection into the microchannel.

Design results

The microfingers open and close owing to the bending motion of the two opposing microfingers. The pushing force against a cellular spheroid by microfingers depends on the balance between the stiffness of each microfinger and the force generated by the inflatable balloons. Microfingers were designed to pinch a cellular spheroid without exceeding 50% deformation rate. A pair of microfingers for cellular spheroids with a size of 300 μm in diameter were designed as 3.6 mm long, 1.2 mm wide, and 380 μm thick by taking into account specifications of a cellular spheroid, such as size and elasticity.

Results and discussion

Fabrication of microfingers for in situ cellular spheroid stiffness evaluation

Figure 3 shows the fabricated microfingers (3.6 mm × 1.2 mm × 380 μm). A complete view of the fabricated microfingers, implemented using electrical interconnections, is shown in Fig. 3 (a). Each PDMS microfinger was integrated with a PBA and a liquid metal strain gauge sensor. Two sets of wires were connected to the strain gauges on the microfingers. Figure 3 (b) shows the serpentine strain gauge integrated into a microfinger. Figure 3 (c) and (d) show side views of the motion of microfingers before and after driving. The two microfingers were initially in contact via stoppers, which defined a gap between the microfingers (Fig. 3 (c)). The opening gap was 156 μm such that a cellular spheroid with a diameter of 300 μm would not cause more than 50% deformation. The initial gap can be adjusted using stoppers by considering the size of the cellular spheroids. The microfingers were bent in the opposite direction and opened by applying pressure from the inflatable balloons (Fig. 3 (d)). The opening gap by applied pressure of 55 kPa was approximately 491 μm, which was sufficient for cellular spheroids with a diameter of 300 μm.

Characterization of microfingers

Microfingers pinch and deform cellular aggregates. The pinching force, which depends on the deflection, was measured using a load cell, whereas the deflection was estimated from the captured side view. The measured values of the pinching force depending on the deflection of the microfinger are shown in Fig. 4, together with the approximate line and values calculated using Eq. (4). The approximate line was obtained by least squares fitting. These datasets are in good agreement. The slope of the linear fit of the measured pinching force was 0.8983, whereas that of the theoretical value was 0.9315. For a cellular spheroid with a diameter of 300 µm, microfingers begin to contact when the deflection is 75 µm since the gap between microfingers results in 300 µm in total. The pinching force at a deflection of 75 μm of the microfinger was estimated to be 67.4 µN from the approximate curve in Fig. 4. The requirement for pinching force without damage to cellular spheroids was estimated to be 75 µN, which caused a 50% deformation rate of the NIH3T3 spheroid16. Consequently, the pinching force obtained at a deflection of 75 µm from the microfinger was sufficiently small to avoid damaging the pinching of the cellular aggregate.

Subsequently, the bending motion of the microfinger was evaluated by applying pressure to the PBA. Figure 5 shows the relationship between the applied pressure and the opening gap between the two fingers. The gap was measured by increasing the pressure from 0 to 55 kPa and then depressurizing it from 55 to 0 kPa. The gap between microfingers in the no-load state with a pressure of 0 kPa was 156 μm since the initial gap was defined by the stopper preventing damage. The gap slightly decreased to between 0 and 15 kPa, owing to the dual-mode characteristics of the PBA itself17. The gap could be linearly controlled by applying pressure, and a slight hysteresis was observed. The gap of 491 μm at a pressure of 55 kPa was sufficient for pinching cellular spheroids with a diameter of 300 μm. Consequently, a pressure range of 15–55 kPa was used in this study.

As previously mentioned, the pressure range for the PBA was set from 15 to 55 kPa in this study by considering its characteristics. The microfingers were opened by the deformation of the inflating balloons between them. The resistance change rate also exhibited different characteristics between 0 and 15 kPa compared to those above 15 kPa. This behavior at a low applied pressure was due to the bending motion on the balloon side. In the range of 15–55 kPa, the resistance changed linearly depending on the applied pressure. The gauge factor of the strain sensor was calculated to be 0.46 (Figs. 5 and 6). Consequently, the bending motion of the microfinger can be estimated by monitoring the resistance change in the strain sensor.

In situ evaluation of cellular spheroid stiffness by soft microfingers

Sequential operations for in situ evaluation of cellular spheroid stiffness were performed using the developed microfingers, as shown in Figs. 7 (a)–(c). Cellular spheroids with diameters of approximately 300 μm were prepared for the experiments. The microfingers were positioned above the cellular spheroids and opened by applying a pressure of 55 kPa (Fig. 7 (a)). The microfingers descended and approached the target spheroid (Fig. 7 (b)). The microfingers were closed by decreasing the applied pressure (Fig. 7 (c)). The deformed cellular aggregate elastically repulsed the pinching microfingers and generated a restoring force that caused strain in the microfingers. Strain gauge sensors integrated into the microfingers measured the strain generated in them.

In this study, C3H10T1/2 and NIH3T3 spheroids were prepared and compared to demonstrate stiffness discrimination. The average diameters of C3H10T1/2 spheroid and NIH3T3 spheroid were approximately 300 μm. The allowable deformation rate for pinching cellular spheroids without damage was defined as 50%. The deformation rate did not exceed 50% by the developed microfingers with stoppers restricting the gap by up to 150 μm.

The evaluation results of the resistance change rate of the strain sensor in pinching the C3H10T1/2 and NIH3T3 spheroids are shown in Fig. 8 in comparison with the load-free operation. The C3H10T1/2 spheroid was stiffer than the NIH3T3 spheroid based on the preliminary evaluation of Young’s modulus. The microfingers were opened first and gradually closed by depressurization, as shown in Fig. 8. During depressurization, the microfingers returned from the initial opening state to the closing state at the stoppers. Compressive strain was generated in the microfingers during the initial opening state. The strain decreased, and the resistance change rate increased with depressurization, as shown in Fig. 8, where the resistance change rate was calculated based on the reference resistance value in the opening state at a pressure of 55 kPa.

Fig. 8Measurement of in situ evaluation of stiffness of different cellular spheroids. Two types of C3H10T1/2 and NIH3T3 spheroids were prepared and compared in terms of stiffness. Load-free operation was evaluated as reference.

Measurement of in situ evaluation of stiffness of different cellular spheroids. Two types of C3H10T1/2 and NIH3T3 spheroids were prepared and compared in terms of stiffness. Load-free operation was evaluated as reference.

When the pressure was decreased from 55 to 35 kPa, the rate of resistance change increased in proportion to the pressure reduction because the microfingers opened wider than the diameter of the cellular spheroid. The microfingers were not in contact with the cellular spheroids. When the pressure reached approximately 30 kPa, the opening gap was about 300 μm and came into contact with the cellular spheroid. When the pressure was reduced from 25 to 10 kPa, the cellular spheroid deformed owing to the pinching force of the microfingers. The microfingers moved close to each other until the restoring force was balanced by the elastic force of the deformed cellular spheroid. PBA for microfinger has a specific characteristic generating small bending motion in different direction at low pressure range17. The behavior of resistance change rate below 10 kPa in Fig. 8 was due to the characteristic. Therefore, the resistance change rate at 10 kPa or more of applied pressure was regarded as available for stiffness evaluation. Examining the mean rate of change in the resistance at 10 kPa, the rate of pinching the stiffer spheroids was lower. It was approximately 0.15% for the C3H10T1/2 spheroid, and more than 0.2% for free pinching from the object. Evaluation results for NIH3T3 spheroids showed a resistance change rate of 0.19%, which was an intermediate value between the rate for C3H10T1/2 spheroids and for free pinching from objects. The difference in resistance rate between the two was 0.04%, which indicated a difference in the stiffness of the cellular spheroids. Thus, it is possible to qualitatively discriminate between spheroids of different relative stiffness.

In this study, inflatable soft microfingers were integrated with a liquid metal strain gauge sensor for in situ evaluation of cellular spheroid stiffness. The stiffness of a cellular spheroid depends on its internal structure. Two microfingers (3.6 mm × 1.2 mm × 380 μm) made of PDMS were combined by bonding. The microfingers were opened by PBAs and closed by decreasing the applied pressure on the PBAs so that the microfingers could pinch a cellular spheroid. The deformed cellular aggregates were elastically repulsed against the microfingers and generated a restoring force, causing strain in the microfingers. Strain gauge sensors integrated into the microfingers were used to measure the strain. Stiffness of cellular spheroids with a diameter of approximately 300 μm was evaluated by developed microfingers. Different cellular spheroids (C3H10T1/2 and NIH3T3) were compared and discriminated based on their stiffness. The C3H10T1/2 spheroid was found to be stiffer than the NIH3T3 spheroid as a result of preliminary evaluation of Young’s modulus using a load cell. As a result of the evaluation using microfingers, stiffer C3H10T1/2 spheroids showed a resistance change rate of 0.15%, whereas that showed by NIH3T3 spheroids were 0.19%. The difference in the resistance rates between the two spheroids indicated a difference in the stiffness of the cellular spheroids. As a result, it was possible to discriminate different spheroids using a strain gauge sensor integrated into the microfingers. Further application to various cellular aggregates is expected to expand the possibilities of the developed microfingers for the in situ evaluation of biological objects.

Methods

Fabrication of microfingers integrated with PBAs and a strain sensor

The two microfingers were fabricated separately and bonded together to form the pinching device. Each microfinger was integrated with PBAs and a strain sensor. The stopper structures were attached to the microfingers. Microchannels for both the PBA and liquid metal-based sensors were fabricated by molding PDMS (Silpot 184, Dow Corning Inc., USA) using a photoresist (SU-8) mold on a Si substrate. The prepolymer-to-curing-agent ratio was 12:1 for PBA layer and 10:1 for others. Three layers of the PBAs, sensor channels, and intermediate buffers were bonded using vacuum ultraviolet (VUV) light. Parylene C was coated onto the inner surfaces of the microfingers for surface adhesion control. The two microfingers were bonded using a VUV. The bonded PDMS films were fabricated using PDMS interconnections. Galinstan (eutectic gallium indium stannum, Zairyo-ya.com, Japan) was injected into the microchannels of the sensor and wired through interconnections.

Experimental setup

A load cell (LVS-5GA, Kyowa Electronic Instruments, Japan) was used for the preliminary evaluation. The restoring force of the cellular spheroids was evaluated using force measurement equipment. The measurements were performed by pressing the cellular spheroid with a probe attached to the load cell, and the restoring force from the deformed cellular spheroid was measured using the load cell.

A digital microscope (VHX 500 F, KEYENCE Co., Japan) was used to measure deflection d in the bending test. The digital microscope was also used to measure opening gap between microfingers depending on the applied pressure (Fig. 5). A syringe pump (MODEL33, HARVARD APPARATUS, USA) was used to drive the microfingers. The applied pressure was monitored using a manometer (PG-100-102GP, NIDEC COPAL ELECTRONICS Co., Japan). The electrical resistance of the liquid-metal-based strain sensor was measured using a digital multimeter (34460 A, Keysight Tech., Japan). The cellular spheroids were prepared with PBS at a temperature of 37 ℃ in a dish.
