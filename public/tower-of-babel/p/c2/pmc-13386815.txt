Evaluation of a simulation-based interprofessional education program using a high-fidelity simulator: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Huaili Luo, Hong Jiang, Yuan Huang, Jingjing Tan, Qunhua Fan, Xiaochun He, Feifei Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInterprofessional education (IPE) has been increasingly recognized as an essential approach for improving collaboration among healthcare professionals. Simulation-based learning offers an authentic and safe environment for implementing IPE. However, evidence regarding structured integration of high-fidelity simulation and interprofessional training in cardiovascular nursing education remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a simulation-based IPE model on clinical competence, collaborative attitudes, and critical thinking among nursing trainees.ObjectiveTo evaluate the application effect of an interprofessional education model utilizing a high-fidelity simulation in the clinical teaching of cardiovascular nursing training.MethodsA quasi-experimental study using a non-equivalent cohort design was conducted in a tertiary teaching hospital. Nursing trainees from two consecutive training cycles were allocated to either a traditional single-profession high-fidelity simulation group (control, n = 45) or a simulation-based interprofessional education group involving collaboration with medical trainees (intervention, n = 45). The intervention was implemented in a structured three-stage format comprising scenario introduction, simulation practice, and facilitated debriefing. Outcomes were assessed using theoretical and operational examinations, the Jefferson Scale of Attitudes Toward Physician–Nurse Collaboration, the Chinese version of the Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory (CTDI-SCV). Independent-sample t tests and χ² tests were applied for group comparisons.ResultsCompared with the control group, nursing trainees in the interprofessional simulation group achieved significantly higher post-intervention theoretical scores (82.65 ± 4.32 vs. 71.23 ± 5.87, p < 0.01) and operational performance scores (90.36 ± 3.89 vs. 78.45 ± 4.63, p < 0.01). Significant improvements were also observed in collaborative attitudes, particularly in the domains of shared education and teamwork, nurse autonomy, and overall collaboration scores (all p < 0.001). Additionally, critical thinking scores across cognitive, rational, and systematic domains were significantly higher in the intervention group following training (all p < 0.001).ConclusionInterprofessional simulation-based training was associated with improvements in critical thinking and collaborative attitudes among nursing trainees. These findings suggest that incorporating interprofessional simulation into training programs may support the development of competencies required for team-based clinical practice.Keywords: Interdisciplinary education, Medical and nursing collaboration, High-fidelity simulation teaching, Medical education

Introduction

As healthcare systems become increasingly complex, effective collaboration among healthcare professionals is essential for delivering safe and high-quality patient care [1]. Policy initiatives, including national strategies to reform medical education, have underscored the need to establish cross-disciplinary and cross-professional educational mechanisms to prepare healthcare professionals for integrated practice environments [2]. However, despite these initiatives, traditional medical and nursing education programs often occur within professional silos, limiting opportunities for learners to develop interprofessional competencies.

Interprofessional education (IPE) has been proposed as an effective approach to address these challenges by enabling learners from different disciplines to learn with, from, and about each other [3, 4]. Accumulating evidence suggests that IPE may enhance collaborative attitudes, communication skills, and mutual respect among healthcare trainees. Nevertheless, existing IPE initiatives often face challenges related to implementation feasibility, curricular integration, and contextual authenticity. Many programs rely on short-term workshops or isolated skill-based activities, which may limit sustained impact on learners’ professional identity and clinical reasoning [5].

High-fidelity simulation has emerged as a powerful pedagogical tool in medical and nursing education, offering immersive, realistic clinical scenarios in a safe and controllable environment [6]. Simulation-based learning enables repeated practice, immediate feedback, and structured reflection, thereby facilitating experiential learning processes. From the perspective of experiential learning theory, effective learning occurs through a cyclical process involving concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation [7]. Integrating simulation into IPE may therefore provide an ideal platform for operationalizing collaborative competencies within authentic clinical contexts.

Although a growing body of research, including systematic reviews, has demonstrated the effectiveness of simulation-based IPE in improving collaborative competencies, important gaps remain. Specifically, evidence from structured, curriculum-integrated IPE programs conducted in Chinese tertiary hospital settings is still limited. In addition, relatively few studies have simultaneously evaluated multiple core outcomes, including knowledge acquisition, clinical skills, collaborative attitudes, and critical thinking within a single intervention. Addressing these gaps may provide more comprehensive insights into the educational value of simulation-based IPE in real-world clinical training environments.

Therefore, this study employed a high-fidelity simulation platform to develop a structured IPE program based on typical acute and critical cardiovascular scenarios, and compared its effectiveness with that of traditional single-profession simulation-based training, aiming to provide empirical evidence for introducing a structured IPE model in clinical nursing education.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a single-center, quasi-experimental design using a non-equivalent cohort approach. The study was conducted at the medical simulation center of a tertiary Grade A teaching hospital (the highest level in the Chinese hospital classification system) in China between January 2023 and December 2023. The control group (January-June 2023) received traditional single-profession high-fidelity simulation training. The intervention group (June–December 2023) participated in a structured simulation-based IPE program involving collaboration with clinical medical trainees.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Mianyang Central Hospital (Approval No. S20240261-01). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation in the study.

Participants

Participants were standardized nursing trainees. In the Chinese medical education system, standardized nursing trainees refer to newly graduated nurses undergoing standardized residency training. All participants in this study were in their first year of clinical training and had completed foundational nursing education but had limited independent clinical experience.

The cardiovascular clinical training program is a structured rotational training module focusing on the management of common and critical cardiovascular conditions, including both theoretical instruction and simulation-based practice.

All eligible nursing trainees during the study period were invited to participate in this study. No trainees declined participation, and the participation rate was 100%. Participants were allocated to groups based on their training cohort.

In the intervention group, clinical medical trainees also participated in the interprofessional simulation sessions. These medical trainees were final-year undergraduate medical students undergoing clinical rotations in the cardiovascular department. A total of 5 medical trainees participated in the intervention. Prior to the simulation sessions, medical trainees received a brief orientation on interprofessional collaboration and their roles within the simulation scenarios to ensure effective participation.

Participants were standardized nursing trainees enrolled in the cardiovascular clinical training program during the study period. Inclusion criteria were:

Completion of prerequisite foundational nursing coursework;

Completion of prerequisite foundational nursing coursework;

Eligibility for participation in simulation-based training;

Eligibility for participation in simulation-based training;

Voluntary participation with signed informed consent.

A total of 90 nursing trainees were included, with 45 in the control group and 45 in the intervention group. Although medical trainees participated in the interprofessional simulation scenarios in the intervention cohort, outcome assessments were conducted exclusively among nursing trainees.

Sample size calculation

Using PASS software, with a significance level of α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 37 participants per group. Considering a potential dropout rate of 20%, the final target sample size was set at 45 participants per group.

Intervention

Simulation environment

All training sessions were conducted in a fully equipped simulation center replicating a real cardiovascular ward. The setting included patient monitoring systems, defibrillators, respiratory support devices, emergency medications, and a high-fidelity simulator capable of dynamic physiological responses.

Scenario development

A total of 6 simulation scenarios were developed based on typical acute and critical cardiovascular conditions, including acute left heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, secondary ventricular fibrillation or sudden cardiac arrest, cardiac tamponade, hypoglycemic coma, post-interventional vagal reflex complications, and acute chest pain. Each scenario was designed to reflect realistic clinical situations encountered in cardiovascular wards and incorporated dynamic patient condition changes requiring timely assessment and intervention. All scenarios were developed by a multidisciplinary team consisting of two senior cardiovascular physicians and two experienced nursing educators, and were reviewed to ensure content validity and clinical relevance.

Intervention

Instructional design and session structure

The training followed a structured three-phase approach based on simulation best practice standards. Prebriefing, including orientation to the simulation environment, clarification of learning objectives, and establishment of psychological safety. Simulation activity involving team-based management of dynamic clinical scenarios. Debriefing structured reflective discussion focusing on clinical reasoning, communication, and teamwork. Debriefing was conducted using the PEARLS framework, a widely recognized structured debriefing model in simulation-based education. Each debriefing session was facilitated by trained instructors and focused on reflective discussion of clinical reasoning, team communication, and decision-making processes. All facilitators received standardized training in simulation-based education and debriefing techniques prior to the study to ensure consistency in delivery. Each debriefing session lasted approximately 30–40 min and was conducted immediately after each simulation activity. All participants were required to attend one debriefing session per simulation scenario.

Control group

In the control group, nursing trainees participated in single-profession high-fidelity simulation sessions. Role assignments were distributed among nursing trainees (e.g., primary nurse, assisting nurse, family member), and learning objectives focused primarily on nursing assessment, technical procedures, and emergency response protocols.

Intervention group

In the intervention group, trainees engaged in simulation sessions alongside clinical medicine trainees, assuming their authentic professional roles. The instructional emphasis extended beyond technical skills to include interprofessional communication, shared decision-making, and clarification of professional responsibilities. Structured debriefing encouraged reflection on collaborative dynamics and clinical reasoning processes.

To ensure internal validity and comparability between groups, the same simulation scenarios, learning objectives, and case progression were used for both the control and intervention groups. The only difference between groups was the presence or absence of interprofessional collaboration during the simulation process.

Prior to the simulation sessions, medical trainees received a structured orientation that introduced the principles of interprofessional collaboration, role responsibilities in cardiovascular care, and expectations for teamwork and communication during the simulation. This orientation aimed to ensure that medical trainees were adequately prepared to engage in collaborative decision-making and to interact effectively with nursing trainees during the simulation activities.

Outcome measures

This study employed the following four tools to assess the teaching effectiveness:

Jefferson Healthcare Collaboration Attitude Scale: This scale, developed by Hojat et al. [8], was used to assess collaborative attitudes. The scale consists of 15 items across four dimensions: shared education and teamwork, caring versus curing, nurse autonomy, and physician dominance. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 15 to 60. Higher total scores indicate more positive attitudes toward interprofessional collaboration. The scale has demonstrated good reliability in previous studies, with a reported Cronbach’s α of 0.848.

The scale consists of 15 items across four dimensions: shared education and teamwork, caring versus curing, nurse autonomy, and physician dominance. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 15 to 60. Higher total scores indicate more positive attitudes toward interprofessional collaboration. The scale has demonstrated good reliability in previous studies, with a reported Cronbach’s α of 0.848.

Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory-Chinese Version (CTDI-SCV): The Chinese version of the Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory was revised and validated by Dong Wen based on the original CTDI developed by Facione. The CTDI-SCV consists of 18 items across three domains: cognitive thinking, rational thinking, and systematic thinking. Items are rated on a 6-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating a stronger critical thinking disposition. Previous studies have demonstrated good reliability and validity of the CTDI-SCV, with a reported Cronbach’s α of 0.91 [9], with higher scores indicating a stronger critical thinking disposition.

Theoretical assessment and practical training scores.

Theoretical assessment and practical training scores.

Both the theory and skill tests had a maximum score of 100. This met the required standard, achieving a score of 80 or above on the theory test and 85 or above on the skill test. The theoretical examination questions and skill evaluation checklist were developed by a panel of experts consisting of senior cardiology nursing educators and clinical specialists. The content was reviewed to ensure consistency with the standardized cardiology nursing training curriculum. Prior to implementation, the assessment tools were reviewed by three senior clinical nursing educators for content validity, and pilot testing was conducted with a small group of trainees to ensure clarity and feasibility. Specifically, the theoretical examinations were graded by independent nursing educators who were not involved in the training program. Skill assessments were conducted by two senior clinical instructors who were not involved in the teaching of either group. The assessors were blinded to the group allocation of the participants to minimize potential evaluation bias.

The practical skills examination was assessed using a standardized scoring system based on the hospital’s unified clinical skills assessment criteria. These criteria are routinely used for training and evaluation within the institution and are aligned with established clinical practice standards.

To reduce potential assessment bias, evaluators were not involved in the teaching sessions and were blinded to group allocation. In addition, practical examinations were assessed independently by two trained evaluators, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

The questionnaire was distributed centrally after the course and collected on the spot.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 29.0 software. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent-sample t tests, while within-group pre–post comparisons were conducted using paired t tests. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using the χ² test. Effect sizes, Cohen’s d, were calculated for key comparisons to complement p-values. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study was conducted based on a simulation platform, integrating the IPE concept system into the clinical teaching context of cardiovascular nursing training. The findings indicate that, compared with traditional single-profession simulation, the interprofessional approach was associated with significantly higher post-intervention scores across multiple domains, with large to very large effect sizes. These results suggest that interprofessional simulation may provide meaningful advantages in fostering both cognitive and collaborative competencies.

In terms of knowledge acquisition and skill improvement, the significant improvement in theoretical and operational scores of the nursing trainees in the intervention group indicates that IPE high-fidelity simulation teaching is conducive to promoting the integration and transfer of learning content [10, 11]. The findings can also be interpreted within the framework of Experiential Learning Theory. In this study, simulation activities provided concrete experiences through realistic clinical scenarios. The changes in the condition were dynamically presented through the high-fidelity simulation system, enabling the trainees to complete assessment, decision-making, and operation in an environment close to the real clinical pressure, while structured debriefing supported reflective observation by enabling participants to review their decisions and actions. Interprofessional interaction may have facilitated abstract conceptualization, as trainees reconciled differing professional perspectives and integrated new insights into their understanding. This contextualized learning helps to break the non-coherent learning pattern of knowledge and skills, prompting the trainees to actively integrate theoretical knowledge and practical experience during problem-solving [12]. At the same time, cross-professional division of labor and collaboration enabled the nursing trainees to participate in team decision-making based on their real professional roles, avoiding the cognitive biases caused by role playing in traditional single-professional simulations [13]. This alignment with the experiential learning cycle provides a coherent theoretical explanation for the observed improvements.

The change in the attitude of healthcare professionals’ cooperation was one of the important findings of this study. After the teaching, the intervention group significantly improved their scores in the dimensions of “joint education and teamwork” and “nurse’s work autonomy”, while the degree of acceptance of “physician dominance” significantly decreased. This indicates that cross-professional high-fidelity simulation teaching helps to form a more balanced and positive concept of healthcare collaboration [14]. It shows that cross-professional education helps to enhance the professional identity and collaboration awareness of nursing students [15]. During the simulation process, nursing students participate in the key aspects of clinical management through continuous observation of the patient’s condition, information feedback, and nursing decisions, and can intuitively perceive their professional value in patient safety and continuous care [16]. Traditional high-fidelity teaching is mostly limited to training of a single profession, and students have difficulty forming a clear understanding of team collaboration [17]. Nursing students often lack real interactions with doctors, and their understanding of equal collaboration between healthcare professionals is vague [18]. In this study, direct interaction with clinical medicine students helps them understand the diagnostic thinking and decision-making logic of doctors, thereby reducing role stereotypes and, to a certain extent, reducing the acceptance of “physician dominance”. It promotes the formation of mutual respect and collaboration awareness.

An unexpected finding in this study was the decline in Jefferson Scale scores observed in the control group following the intervention. This pattern warrants careful consideration. One possible explanation is that single-profession simulation may inadvertently reinforce traditional hierarchical perceptions of professional roles, particularly in the absence of structured interprofessional interaction. Without opportunities for collaborative engagement, participants may rely more heavily on pre-existing assumptions about physician–nurse relationships. Alternatively, this decline may reflect increased awareness of professional roles and boundaries following training, leading to more critical or differentiated responses to the questionnaire items at post-test. Given these possibilities, future studies are needed to further explore this phenomenon and to determine whether it represents a true effect of single-profession training or a measurement-related artifact.

The observed improvements in critical thinking. The intervention group demonstrated an approximately 20-point higher total score compared with the control group. Given the scale range, this represents a substantial improvement and is accompanied by a very large effect size (Cohen’s d > 1.5), indicating a meaningful enhancement in higher-order cognitive abilities relevant to clinical reasoning and decision-making. This may be explained by the unique mechanisms inherent in interprofessional interaction, closely related to “problem-driven learning“ [19]. While both groups participated in simulation-based learning, the interprofessional format may have provided additional cognitive stimulation through exposure to diverse professional perspectives. Exposure to different professional perspectives may generate cognitive conflict, prompting learners to re-examine their assumptions and reconsider clinical decisions. These mechanisms are consistent with socio-cultural perspectives on learning, including the Zone of Proximal Development, where knowledge is constructed through interaction with others. Nursing trainees observing the diagnostic reasoning and decision-making processes of medical trainees may provide a cognitive model that they can internalize. Interprofessional interaction requires participants to articulate their reasoning to individuals from other disciplines. This may promote more explicit, structured thinking during clinical problem solving. This teaching model is well-suited to the learning needs and cognitive characteristics of students, and the research results are consistent with those of domestic and international studies [20, 21]. Taken together, these findings suggest that IPE may enhance critical thinking not only through experiential learning but also through socially mediated cognitive processes that are less prominent in single-profession training.

Beyond the measured outcomes, the findings of this study may have broader implications for clinical practice. Interprofessional simulation has been associated with improvements in team communication, shared understanding of roles, and alignment of professional attitudes, all of which are critical components of effective healthcare delivery. Enhanced collaboration and communication may contribute to improved patient safety by reducing errors related to miscommunication and fragmented care. These interpretations are consistent with findings from recent evidence syntheses, such as the systematic review by Shuyi AT [22], which reported that interprofessional education can positively influence collaborative competencies, communication, and professional attitudes among healthcare learners.

Research limitations

This study has several limitations. The quasi-experimental design using consecutive training cohorts introduces the risk of history and maturation bias, as the intervention group completed training later in the year. This study was a single-center study with a relatively limited sample size. It is worth noting that the achieved sample size met and exceeded the a priori calculation, and therefore was adequate for detecting the expected effects. Future studies should employ randomized designs, multi-center samples, and standardized measurement tools to further validate and extend these findings.
