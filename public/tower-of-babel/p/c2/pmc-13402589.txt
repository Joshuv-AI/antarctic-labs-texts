Chlorella vulgaris and Dunaliella salina-enriched Artemia salina enhances growth, pigmentation, gut morphology and immune-related pathways in goldfish (Carassius auratus)
Authors: Ahmed E. Elshafey, Bin Wen, Jian-Zhong Gao, Jun-Nan Huang, Xu Can, Malik M. Khalafalla, Attia A. Abou Zaid, Mohamed M. Abdel-Rahim, Mustafa Shukry, Ibrahim Albokhadaim, Samir Alhojaily, Saad Shousha, Ahmed O. Alameen, Yassir A. Almofti, Bahaa AL-Trad, Mahmoud Kandeel, Yamen Hegazy, Zai-Zhong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The objective of this research was to investigate the impacts of supplementing both unenriched and enriched live Artemia salina with Chlorella vulgaris and Dunaliella salina in goldfish (Carassius auratus) diets on various parameters, including growth performance, feed utilisation, blood parameters, immunity, histology, and β-carotene levels. The experiment was conducted for 90 days, and all groups of goldfish (initial weight = 2.51 ± 0.01 g/fish, N = 180) received commercial diet supplements (unenriched Artemia (UE), Chlorella-enriched Artemia (CE), Dunaliella-enriched Artemia (DE), and Artemia enriched with both Chlorella and Dunaliella (CDE)). Compared to other fish groups, goldfish given CDE had better final weight, weight gain, average daily gain, specific growth rate, relative growth rate, final length, and length gain, and a lower feed conversion ratio. CDE had the best hematological, antioxidant, and immunity parameters (Immunoglobulin M levels, lysozyme activity, phagocytic activity, and phagocytic index). In addition, goldfish given CDE had the greatest β-carotene content at 8.90 ± 0.4 mg/100 g. Goldfish given enriched Artemia, specifically CDE, exhibited increased intestinal villus length, width, and goblet cells, as determined by histomorphometric analysis (p < 0.05). Additionally, upregulation of MCH, MCH-R, POMC, IGF-1, and TNF-α genes was observed in CDE. In conclusion, supplementing goldfish diets with CDE has several advantages, including improved growth performance, blood parameters, immunity, histology, β-carotene levels, and gene expression related to growth and immunity. The study’s results indicate that Artemia enhanced with Chlorella and Dunaliella can improve the health, growth performance, and development of goldfish in aquaculture.

Introduction

Goldfish (Carassius auratus) is Goldfish (Carassius auratus) are among the most commercially important freshwater ornamental fish species, where pigmentation intensity, growth performance, and immune robustness directly influence economic value and production sustainability1,2. In ornamental aquaculture, dietary manipulation represents the primary tool for improving these traits, particularly pigmentation, which is strongly associated with carotenoid deposition in skin and scales3–5. Live feeds, especially Artemia salina nauplii, are widely used during early life stages due to their high digestibility and feeding-stimulating properties6–8. Newly hatched Artemia nauplii are widely used as live prey in aquaculture, prized for the ease of hatching their long-lasting dormant cysts9,10 However, Artemia nauplii are naturally deficient in long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (LC-PUFAs) such as EPA and DHA, as well as certain bioactive compounds required for optimal fish development11,12. Consequently, enrichment strategies have been developed to improve Artemia nutritional quality prior to feeding13. Moreover, fish cannot synthesize PUFA and LC-PUFA components14. To bridge this nutritional gap, bio-enrichment of Artemia with microalgae is a standard practice. Microalgae can feed or enrich Artemia nauplii with PUFAs, switching prey to predator15–17.

Microalgae are considered promising enrichment agents due to their dense biochemical composition and functional bioactive compounds18. Chlorella vulgaris is characterized by high-quality protein content, essential amino acids, immunomodulatory polysaccharides, and antioxidant molecules19. In contrast, Dunaliella salina is recognized as one of the richest natural sources of β-carotene and lipid-derived antioxidants 26,84. Both microalgae have independently demonstrated positive effects on fish growth performance, immune response, and pigmentation19–21. Due to their foundational role and rich nutritional profile, microalgae are a primary food source in aquaculture for larval fish, shrimp, and mollusks. Specific strains like Chlorella and Dunaliella are valued as excellent supplements for enriching live feeds like brine shrimp, given their high content of proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and vitamins22,23.

While numerous studies have established the value of enriching Artemia with single microalgal species such as Chlorella vulgaris or Dunaliella salina13,24, the potential for a synergistic interaction when combining Chlorella and Dunaliella for enrichment remains poorly understood and inadequately justified.

The potential synergistic or complementary effects of combining Chlorella vulgaris and Dunaliella salina remain insufficiently explored. From a biochemical perspective, Chlorella primarily contributes protein, micronutrients, and immunostimulatory compounds 25,29, whereas Dunaliella supplies carotenoids and specific lipid fractions involved in oxidative balance and pigmentation enhancement25,26. Their complementary nutritional profiles suggest a possible additive or synergistic modulation of physiological pathways related to pigmentation (MCH, MCH-R, POMC), growth (IGF-1), and immune regulation (TNF-α), which are known to respond to dietary bioactive components27–29.

Although previous studies have demonstrated the beneficial effects of single-microalgal enrichment strategies in ornamental fish29, including our earlier work on Spirulina- and canthaxanthin-enriched Artemia 13, the potential synergistic impact of dual microalgal enrichment remains poorly understood. To date, no comprehensive study has evaluated the integrated physiological, histomorphometric, biochemical, and molecular responses to Artemia simultaneously enriched with Chlorella vulgaris and Dunaliella salina. In particular, whether combined enrichment enhances growth performance, intestinal absorptive capacity, antioxidant defense, β-carotene deposition, and multi-tissue gene expression beyond single-microalgal treatments remains unclear.

We hypothesized that Artemia enriched concurrently with Chlorella vulgaris and Dunaliella salina would exert superior biological effects compared with single enrichment strategies, owing to complementary biochemical contributions and potential synergistic modulation of growth- and immunity-related pathways. Specifically, integrating the high-quality proteins and immunostimulatory compounds of Chlorella with the carotenoids and antioxidant molecules of Dunaliella may enhance nutrient utilization, gut morphology, pigmentation pathways, and immune-related gene regulation more effectively than either microalga alone. Therefore, the present study was designed not only to assess nutritional enhancement but also to provide a multi-level biological interpretation linking dietary enrichment, intestinal absorptive capacity, antioxidant defense, immune modulation, pigmentation pathways, and growth-related gene expression in goldfish.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

All fish-related procedures in this study were executed in accordance with national and institutional ethical requirements. Fish handling and experimental steps were carried out exclusively by trained personnel under the supervision and approval of the Animal Ethics Committee at Kafrelsheikh University, Egypt (KFS-IACUC/174/2024). Ethical conduct was continuously monitored and aligned with the core principles and reporting requirements of the ARRIVE guidelines to ensure responsible and humane use of animals in research30.

Preparation and analysis of Artemia

Enrichment of Artemia

Artemia cysts (AQUA MASTER, China) were hatched in aerated seawater (salinity: 30–32 ppt) at 28 ± 1 °C under continuous illumination (approximately 2000 lx). After 24 h, newly hatched nauplii were collected and transferred to clean, aerated seawater for enrichment.

For enrichment, approximately 54,000 Artemia metanauplii were stocked in 1 L plastic containers (final density ≈ 54 individuals/mL). Enrichment was conducted for 24 h under continuous aeration and light conditions at 28 ± 1 °C. Four treatments were prepared: The first is Artemia without any enrichments (UE); the second uses 1 × 107 mL− 1 algal cells of Chlorella vulgaris (CE); the third uses 1 × 107 mL− 1 algal cells of Dunaliella salina (DE); and the fourth uses 1 × 107 mL− 1 algal cells of C. vulgaris and D. salina (CDE)31. Microalgae cultures were maintained under standard laboratory conditions, and cell density was determined using a Neubauer hemocytometer under light microscopy before enrichment. After enrichment, Artemia metanauplii were harvested, rinsed with clean seawater, and immediately fed to goldfish juveniles.The enrichment density (approximately 54 individuals/mL) was selected based on standard Artemia enrichment protocols that optimize microalgal uptake while maintaining adequate aeration and survival32.

Chemical composition of Artemia

A commercial analytical laboratory analyzed the dried samples (50 g) of each unenriched (UE). It enriched Artemia (CE, DE, and CDE) for 24 h at 28 °C to analyze protein, fat, ash, fiber, moisture, and carbohydrates. Replicate samples from the 50 g were used for the analysis using the following techniques33. In addition to measuring phosphorus and calcium34, a pump calorimeter was used to determine the total energy (CAL2K Oxygen Bomb Calorimeter System)35.

Fatty acid profiling of Artemia by GC-MS

The methodology outlined by Chakraborty et al. was followed to perform quantitative and qualitative analyses of fatty acids. 31. Fatty acids extracted through transesterification were analyzed using a Perkin-Elmer AutoSystem XL gas chromatograph. The oven temperature was programmed as follows: initially held at 110 °C for 1.0 min, then increased at a rate of 45 °C/min to 210 °C, followed by a rate of 30 °C/min to 250 °C, 25 °C/min to 285 °C, and finally 5 °C/min to 290 °C. Ultrahigh-purity helium was used as the carrier gas, and the injector temperature was maintained at 285 °C. Fatty acid compositions were expressed as the weight% of total fatty acids in each sample. Data were analyzed using Perkin-Elmer Chromstation software (version 4.02).

Experimental design

Goldfish were obtained from local fish breeders in Damro, Kafr-Elsheikh, and sent to the glass tank lab at Egypt’s Kafrelsheikh University’s Faculty of Aquatic and Fisheries Sciences in oxygenated polyethylene bags. The fish were randomly distributed among 12 rectangular glass tanks (60 × 30 × 40 cm³), each containing 45 L of water and an air stone to ensure sufficient dissolved oxygen (DO). The experiment employed 180 goldfish across four groups, each containing three replicates (tanks per treatment in triplicate) of 15 fish per replicate. The average initial weight of the fish was 2.51 ± 0.01 g per fish. The proximate composition of the commercial diet (Skretting Egypt, 10th of Ramadan, El Sharqia, Egypt) contained 90% DM, 44% crude protein, 9% ether extract, 7% ash, 3.38% crude fiber, 36.62% NFE, and 4520 kcal/kg gross energy and was given to all groups. Each group was supplemented with a different type of Artemia: unenriched Artemia (UE), Chlorella-enriched Artemia (CE), Dunaliella-enriched Artemia (DE), and a combination of Chlorella and Dunaliella-enriched Artemia (CDE). The commercial dry diet was offered at a daily feeding rate of 4% of total tank biomass, twice daily (08:00 and 16:00) for 90 days (Fig. 1). The feeding ration was adjusted weekly based on the bulk weight of fish in each tank to account for growth-related changes in body weight. Feed was distributed in multiple portions during each feeding session to ensure complete consumption. No visible feed residue remained during the feeding period., alongside 300 Artemia supplementation according to Besen, et al.36. In addition to the dry diet, fish were supplemented with Artemia (UE, CE, DE, or CDE, depending on the treatment). The dry matter (DM) content of each Artemia type was determined by drying representative subsamples to constant weight. Using a Sartorius balance ENTRIS64-1 S, hatched Artemia were weighed daily post-harvest before being fed to goldfish.

Growth parameters of goldfish

The growth parameters were estimated using the following equations to evaluate the productive growth of goldfish supplemented with different enriched Artemia salina after 90 days of feeding:

WG = Wf – Wi.

Where WG: Weight Gain, Wf: Final weight (g), and Wi: Initial weight (g).

ADG (g/fish/day) = (Wf - Wi) / t.

Where ADG: Average daily gain (g/fish/day), t: Total experiment duration (90 days).

SGR (% day-1) = 100 × (lnWf − lnWi) / t.

where ln is the natural logarithm, Wf is the final weight (g), Wi is the initial weight (g), and t is the experimental period (days).

SGR: Specific growth rate (%) and ln: Natural logarithm.

RGR (%) = 100 × (Wf-Wi)/ Wi.

Where RGR: Relative growth rate (%).

LG (cm) = Lf – Li. Where LG: Length gain (cm), Lf: Final length (cm), and Li: Initial length (cm).

K = 100 × (Wf / Lf3).

Where K: Condition factor.

SR (%) = 100 × (Nf / Ni).

Where SR: Survival rate (%), Nf: Final fish number, and Ni: Initial fish number.

FI: Feed intake (g/fish), defined as the total amount of dry feed and live feed supplied per fish during the experimental period.

Feed intake (FI) was calculated on a dry-matter (DM) basis and included both the commercial dry diet and Artemia supplementation.

Total feed intake was calculated as:

Total FI (g DM) = Dry feed intake (g DM) + Artemia intake (g DM).

Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated as:

FCR = Total FI (g DM) / Body weight gain (g).

Blood samples

At the end of the experiment. The fish were fasted for 24 h before sampling, then anesthetized with tricaine methanesulfonate at 100 mg/L before sample collection37. Blood for plasma was collected in EDTA tubes, while blood for serum was collected in plain tubes and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C; serum was stored at − 20 °C until analysis. This method was previously described by Duman, et al.38.

Hematology and Antioxidant Analyses

To analyze various blood parameters, including RBCs, Hb, PCV, WBCs, Heterophil, Lymphocyte, Monocytes, Eosinophil, and Basophil, the methods outlined by Anderson and Siwicki39 were followed. The activity levels of (SOD), (CAT), (GPx), and (MDA) were determined at 450 nm using a commercial ELISA kit (In-fi0025, In-fi0006, In-fi0020, and In-fi0008 from Inova Biotechnology, China, respectively). The manufacturer’s procedures were strictly followed, as described by Hao, et al.40.

Immunity assay

The phagocytic index, serum lysozyme activity, serum IgM levels, and phagocytic activity were all assessed. To determine serum lysozyme activity, a commercially available ELISA kit was used according to the procedure described by Demers and Bayne41. Serum IgM concentrations were measured using a commercial fish IgM ELISA kit (Cusabio®, Wuhan, Hubei, China). Phagocytic activity and the phagocytic index were assessed by preparing whole blood smears and adding a culture of Candida albicans to the samples. The mixtures were incubated in a water bath with gentle shaking, then stained with Giemsa solution. Phagocytic activity was determined by calculating the percentage of macrophages containing intracellular yeast cells. In contrast, the phagocytic index was calculated as the average number of Candida cells engulfed per phagocytic cell42.

β-carotene analysis

Fish were euthanized humanely, and the entire body of each fish was rinsed with distilled water, blotted dry, weighed, and immediately stored at − 80 °C until analysis Chen, et al.43. Each whole-body sample was minced and homogenized using a mechanical homogenizer. A known weight of homogenized tissue was transferred into a 50 mL centrifuge tube, and 20 mL of acetonitrile–ethyl acetate (1:1, v/v) containing 0.1% butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) was added for extraction to prevent carotenoid oxidation. The mixture was vortexed and centrifuged, and the supernatant was transferred to a polypropylene tube. Subsequently, 10 mL of hexane was added to extract carotenoids into the organic phase.

The extract was purified using an Oasis WAX cartridge, and β-carotene was eluted with 4 mL of methanol–ammonium hydroxide. The eluate was evaporated under reduced pressure and reconstituted in either 40 mL of mobile phase or 2 mL of acetonitrile, depending on the required concentration. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 5 min, 50 µL of the supernatant was injected into the HPLC system for quantification. β-Carotene quantification was performed using reverse-phase HPLC following previously established methods for carotenoid analysis in fish44, with chromatographic conditions adapted from El-Bilawy, et al.45. The method was validated for fish whole-body matrix before analysis. Calibration curves prepared with analytical-grade β-carotene standards showed excellent linearity (R² > 0.99). Recovery from spiked fish tissue samples ranged between 90 and 105%, confirming the suitability of the method for quantification in fish tissue. The β-carotene concentration was expressed as mg per 100 g wet weight.

Biometric indices and histopathological examination

For histomorphological examinations, the following indices were measured:

HSI (%) = 100 × (liver weight/body weight).

VSI (%) = 100 × (viscera weight/body weight).

ISI (%) = 100 × (intestinal weight/body weight).

The samples were fixed in 10% formalin for three days. After fixation, they were dehydrated through a graded series of absolute alcohol rinses and then embedded in paraffin. Longitudinal Sect. 5 μm thick were prepared on glass slides and cut with a Leica Rotary Microtome (RM 2145, Leica Microsystems). Regular staining of the slides with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) was followed by histomorphometric analysis using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health), as described by Bancroft and Layton46.

QRT-PCR gene expression analysis for color, growth, and immunity

Target genes were selected to represent key biological pathways relevant to the study objectives: MCH, MCH-R, and POMC are involved in pigmentation and neuroendocrine regulation; IGF-1 plays a central role in growth and somatic development; and TNF-α is a well-established cytokine involved in immune regulation. This selection enables integrated evaluation of pigmentation, growth, and immune modulation in response to dietary enrichment83.

To assess gene expression levels, tissue samples (hypothalamus, skin, pituitary, liver, and head kidney). Approximately 50 mg of each tissue was homogenized in TRIzol reagent (iNtRON Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s protocol for total RNA extraction. RNA purity was evaluated spectrophotometrically using a NanoDrop system (BioDrop), and samples with A260/280 ratios between 1.9 and 2.1 were considered acceptable for downstream applications. RNA integrity was verified by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis stained with ethidium bromide, confirming the presence of distinct 28 S and 18 S rRNA bands.

Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using a reverse transcription kit (High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kits) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using the SensiFAST SYBR Lo-Rox kit on a mic-PCR system (Bio-Molecular Systems), following the Minimum Information for Publication of Quantitative Real-Time PCR Experiments (MIQE) guidelines.

Gene-specific primers were used for melanin-concentrating hormone (MCH) in the hypothalamus, melanin-concentrating hormone receptor (MCH-R) in the skin, proopiomelanocortin (POMC) in the pituitary, insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) in the liver, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) in the head kidney (Table 1). Primer efficiency was determined using standard curve analysis, within the acceptable range for quantitative PCR. Amplification specificity was confirmed by melt curve analysis, showing a single peak for each primer pair. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method47. β-actin was used as the reference gene. Its expression stability across tissues and treatment groups was verified and showed no significant variation (p > 0.05), confirming its suitability for normalization, in accordance with established reference gene validation practices.

Water quality

Water quality parameters were assessed weekly, including dissolved oxygen (DO), pH, temperature, electrical conductivity (EC), and total dissolved solids (TDS). All measurements were taken from the center of each aquarium using a multiparameter probe meter (HI9829–03042, HANNA® Instruments, https://www.hannainst.com/(. Total ammonia nitrogen concentrations were measured separately using a portable colorimeter (Martini MI 405).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed considering the tank as the experimental unit (n = 3 per treatment). For growth performance, feed utilization, survival rate, and biometric indices, data were calculated per tank and analyzed using tank means. For hematological, antioxidant, immune, histological, and gene expression parameters, multiple fish were sampled per tank; however, individual fish measurements were averaged within each tank before statistical analysis. Therefore, the unit of replication for all statistical comparisons was the tank, not the individual fish. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Anderson–Darling and Levene’s tests, respectively. Differences among treatments were evaluated using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with the tank considered the experimental unit (n = 3 per treatment).

Results

Chemical Composition of Artemia

Table 2 results demonstrate a considerable disparity in the average proximate composition of the supplemental meal (unenriched and enriched Artemia). The enriched Artemia, specifically the combination of Chlorella and Dunaliella (CDE), had substantially higher (p < 0.05) mean values of crude protein, crude lipid, ash, fiber, calcium, and energy content associated with Artemia. Meanwhile, the mean values of moisture, carbohydrate, and phosphorus were highest in (DE), (UE), and (CE), respectively. Enriching Artemia with (CDE) resulted in a significant (p < 0.05) improvement in its nutritional value, as evidenced by increased protein, lipid, ash, fiber, calcium, and energy content.

Growth parameters of goldfish

The statistics on the development and survival of goldfish after 90 days of feeding are thoroughly examined in Table 4. The findings show that the goldfish given CDE exhibited a significant difference (p = 0.018). Final weight, weight increase, specific growth rate (SGR), relative growth rate (RGR), length gain, average daily gain (ADG), and overall growth performance were all noticeably higher in goldfish that were fed the CDE diet compared to the other groups (UE, CE, and DE). The CDE treatment had a notable positive impact (p < 0.05) on promoting growth and development. Additionally, the feed conversion ratio (FCR) was significantly lower in the CE, DE, and CDE groups compared to the control (p < 0.05), indicating improved feed efficiency. The condition factor (K value) was also lowest in the CDE group, indicating better overall health status in the fish. No significant differences in survival rates were detected among groups (ANOVA, p = 0.276), suggesting that the different feeding regimes did not affect goldfish survival.

Analysis of color, growth, and immunity-related gene expression using QRT-PCR

The relative mRNA expression profiling showed substantial upregulation of MCH (p < 0.0001), MCH-R (p < 0.0001), POMC (p = 0.0002), IGF-1 (p = 0.0002), and TNF-α (p < 0.0001) genes in response to different treatments (CE, DE, and CDE). These findings suggest that the use of Chlorella plus Dunaliella (CDE) together resulted in better improvements in the general color, growth, and immunity of goldfish compared to the single use of each (CE or DE) alone, as presented in Fig. 5. Regarding the relative expression levels of MCH in the hypothalamus (Fig. 5, A), MCH-R in the skin (Fig. 5, B), and POMC in the pituitary (Fig. 5, C), these three genes are crucial for pigmentation in goldfish. CDE treatment showed the highest upregulation, followed by DE and CE. Additionally, the relative expression levels of IGF-1 in the liver (Fig. 5D) indicate that fish growth is enhanced. TNF-α in the head kidney (Fig. 5, E) indicates fish immunity, with CDE treatment exhibiting the highest upregulation compared to other treatments.

Discussion

The present study extends previous enrichment-based nutritional strategies by demonstrating that combining Chlorella vulgaris and Dunaliella salina in Artemia enrichment elicits integrative physiological and molecular responses that exceed those reported for single-microalgal supplementation. Unlike earlier studies that focused primarily on growth or pigmentation outcomes independently29, the current work integrates growth performance, antioxidant status, immune parameters, gut histomorphometry, and gene expression profiling across multiple tissues. This integrative approach provides new insight into how dual-microalgal enrichment may simultaneously modulate nutrient utilization, intestinal absorptive efficiency, immune responsiveness, and pigmentation-related pathways.

Live food is a natural source of nutrition that can provide a complete and balanced diet for fish, containing essential vitamins, minerals, and fatty acids that are important for their growth, development, improved coloration and appearance, reduced risk of disease transmission, and overall health13,48. Enriched Artemia, in particular, is a popular live food that has been shown to offer several advantages for feeding ornamental fish49. Enriched Artemia contains high levels of carotenoids, pigments responsible for goldfish’s red, orange, and yellow coloration50. Feeding goldfish with enriched Artemia can help intensify their colors and improve their overall appearance29,51.

Enriched Artemia in the diet of goldfish can significantly impact their nutrient intake. Specifically, Artemia enriched with Chlorella vulgaris and Dunaliella salina had higher protein, lipid, ash, calcium, and total energy values than unenriched Artemia (UE). The higher protein content in CDE is likely due to both C. vulgaris and D. salina being rich sources of protein (58% and 60%, respectively)52. The high lipid content of D. salina, which can reach 14% 52, results in CDE and DE having higher lipid content than in other treatments. Furthermore, the higher ash and calcium levels in CDE may be since both C. vulgaris and D. salina are rich in minerals, including calcium. C. vulgaris has been found to contain up to 15% calcium53, while D. salina can contain up to 12% calcium54. Interestingly, the phosphorus values were higher in CE than in CDE. Chlorella’s phosphorus uptake rate was 3.8 times higher than that of the control, indicating that chlorella contains a high phosphorus content of55. Akbarali et al. (2014) reported analogous findings, indicating that Artemia supplemented with soybean oil, sesame oil, and rice oil possesses superior nutritional value compared to unenriched Artemia. These findings suggest that enriched Artemia can provide goldfish with a comprehensive, balanced diet, including vital nutrients such as protein, lipids, minerals, and energy.

The enrichment of Artemia with C. vulgaris and D. salina has been found to lead to elevated concentrations of saturated fatty acids (SFAs), monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), as well as the omega-3 fatty acids docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), and the omega-6 fatty acid arachidonic acid (AA). Chlorella is rich in PUFAs, particularly alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), a precursor to the omega-3 fatty acids DHA and EPA56. Conversely, Dunaliella is rich in omega-6 fatty acids and arachidonic acid (AA)57. A similar outcome was reported in another study on the fatty acid profile of Artemia enhanced with microalgae58. When Artemia is supplemented with Chlorella and Dunaliella, it consumes these microalgae and incorporates their nutrients into its tissues. This results in a higher concentration of these nutrients in the Artemia, which can then be passed on to the fish that consume them.

The goldfish fed enriched Artemia, particularly those fed CDE, exhibit improved growth performance, as evidenced by final weight, weight gain, ADG, SGR, RGR, final length, and length gain. The nutritional value of CDE played a significant role in promoting growth and development. Survival rates did not differ significantly among treatments (p > 0.05), suggesting that enrichment did not markedly affect survival under the present experimental conditions. This outcome is comparable to Siddik, et al.59, which explains the benefits of Chlorella and Dunaliella when feeding fish. Moreover, Artemia enriched with vitamin C can boost the growth and survival of Sepia pharaonis larvae, as reported by Vajargah, et al.60. Enriched Artemia with Chlorella and Dunaliella enhances growth performance in goldfish due to the presence of essential nutrients, including high-quality protein52, minerals, and fatty acids61. Using enriched brine shrimp can be an effective feeding strategy in ornamental fish stores, providing the nutrients necessary for optimal growth and development in goldfish.

Measuring hematological parameters is useful for assessing fish’s nutritional status and health62. Goldfish fed Artemia enriched with Chlorella and Dunaliella exhibited higher RBC, Hb, and PCV levels, as well as greater SOD, CAT, and GPX activities. Carotenoids and other antioxidants are abundant in Chlorella and Dunaliella63, Tocopherols64 and ascorbic acid65 protect cells from oxidative damage by scavenging damaging free radicals66. An enhancement in hematocrit level in goldfish fed with grape seed proanthocyanidin extract was observed. It also had antioxidants, as reported by Jahanbakhshi, et al.67. Enrichment of Artemia with Chlorella and Dunaliella can facilitate the formation of blood cells and antioxidants, thereby enhancing the development and function of blood cells, resulting in improved blood parameters in fish.

Enrichment of Artemia with Chlorella and Dunaliella can provide essential nutrients that enhance immune cell function, thereby improving immune responses in goldfish. In addition, the increased phagocytic activity and phagocytic index in goldfish fed enriched CDE with higher protein and mineral content may cause artemia. These nutrients are essential for the formation and function of immune cells, such as macrophages68, which phagocytose foreign invaders and pathogens20. Moreover, the higher levels of IgM and lysozyme activity in goldfish fed enriched Artemia may be due to the presence of bioactive compounds, such as carotenoids and polysaccharides, in Chlorella and Dunaliella69,70. These compounds have been shown to enhance immune function and stimulate the production of immunoglobulins and lysozymes in fish71. The immunological state of goldfish that feed CDE is similar to the result of Mehrgan, et al.27, which shows a high level of immunity when goldfish are fed 0.2% sodium butyrate plus 0.2% sodium propionate. Enrichment of Artemia with Chlorella and Dunaliella can promote immune function and protect against pathogenic infections in goldfish.

Goldfish fed CDE had the highest levels of β-carotene, while goldfish fed DE, CE, and UE had the lowest. Goldfish are known for their bright and vibrant colors, mainly because their skin and scales contain carotenoid pigments72. β-carotene is one such carotenoid that plays a crucial role in the coloration of goldfish73,74. Enriching Artemia with Chlorella and Dunaliella can increase the β-carotene content of the food, as both of these microalgae are rich sources of this pigment25. Goldfish can effectively absorb and metabolize β-carotene from their food. When they consume CDE, the β-carotene is digested and absorbed before being transported to the skin and scales75. The pigment is then deposited in these areas, contributing to the fish’s vibrant coloration76. Studies have shown that goldfish can efficiently convert dietary β-carotene into retinol, the active form of vitamin A77. Goldfish fed Artemia enriched with Chlorella and Dunaliella are likely to record high levels of β-carotene due to increased availability, efficient absorption, and potential synergistic effects.

Goldfish fed CDE had the highest values for the visceral somatic index (VSI), hepatosomatic index (HSI), and intestinal somatic index (ISI), followed by DE, CE, and UE. Additionally, goldfish fed with CDE showed good intestinal condition in all parts. Chlorella and Dunaliella are rich sources of fatty acids and antioxidants, for example, carotenoids and vitamins C and E78, which may have protected the internal organs from oxidative stress and damage. This may have improved organ function and higher VSI, HSI, and ISI values. Furthermore, fatty acids can help maintain the integrity of the intestinal mucosa and reduce inflammation, boosting nutrient uptake and digestion79. Our results match those of Diab et al. 80, who found that Red Tilapia fed with Roselle Calyces extract can improve intestinal health. The healthy intestinal features observed in goldfish fed Artemia enriched with Chlorella and Dunaliella may be ascribed to the augmentation levels of antioxidants and beneficial fatty acids in the enriched food and the immunomodulatory effects of bioactive compounds.

The observed increase in villus height and intestinal indices in goldfish fed enriched Artemia suggests an expansion of intestinal absorptive surface area, commonly associated with enhanced nutrient assimilation capacity. Increased villus length has been linked to improved digestive efficiency and greater surface area available for enzymatic activity and nutrient transport in fish80. The improvement in intestinal morphology observed in the CDE group may therefore partially explain the enhanced growth performance and improved feed conversion ratio recorded in this treatment.

Additionally, the higher goblet cell density observed in enriched groups may indicate enhanced mucosal protection and improved intestinal barrier function. Goblet cells secrete mucins that contribute to intestinal integrity and microbial balance, thereby supporting digestive efficiency and immune defense 89. Improved intestinal health can facilitate more efficient nutrient absorption, which may contribute to improved somatic growth and metabolic performance. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that dietary bioactive compounds, including microalgae-derived nutrients, can modulate intestinal architecture and enhance growth outcomes in fish81. However, it should be noted that histomorphometric changes represent structural adaptations and do not directly quantify nutrient absorption rates. While increased villus height and goblet cell density are commonly associated with improved absorptive capacity, functional assays, such as enzyme activity measurements or nutrient transport analyses, would be required to confirm enhanced digestive efficiency at the physiological level.

The upregulation of MCH, MCH-R, POMC, IGF-1, and TNF-α genes in goldfish fed Artemia enriched with Chlorella vulgaris and Dunaliella salina may be associated with the enhanced nutritional profile of the enriched feed, particularly the increased availability of proteins, lipids, vitamins, and bioactive compounds81,82. Both microalgae are recognized as nutrient-dense sources that can support physiological and metabolic processes in fish81,82. The observed upregulation of MCH, MCH-R, and POMC was associated with improved pigmentation outcomes in the enriched groups. Although these transcriptional changes are consistent with modulation of pigmentation-related pathways, gene expression data represent correlative molecular responses rather than direct evidence of causal mechanisms. Changes at the mRNA level do not necessarily translate into proportional alterations in protein abundance or functional activity. Previous studies have shown that environmental or nutritional factors can modulate these genes; for example, Shin and Choi82 reported upregulation of MCH, MCH-R, and POMC in response to LED light exposure. Similarly, Jahanbakhshi, et al.83 demonstrated increased IGF-1 expression following dietary supplementation with selenium nanoparticles and selenomethionine. In the present study, the increased hepatic IGF-1 expression observed in enriched groups was associated with enhanced growth performance. While IGF-1 is widely recognized as a key regulator of somatic growth, the current findings indicate an association rather than direct mechanistic confirmation. Further analyses at the protein or endocrine level would be required to establish a definitive causal relationship. The upregulation of TNF-α in the head kidney suggests activation or modulation of immune-related signaling pathways, consistent with previous findings linking dietary interventions to TNF-α expression changes Rashmeei, et al.32. However, TNF-α transcription may reflect broader immunological activation or physiological adaptation rather than a direct enhancement of disease resistance. Therefore, these transcriptional changes should be interpreted as indicators of altered immune-related activity rather than definitive proof of improved immune competence.

Overall, the gene expression results support a coordinated molecular response to dual microalgal enrichment that aligns with the observed improvements in growth performance, antioxidant status, pigmentation, and intestinal morphology. Nevertheless, additional mechanistic investigations, including protein quantification and functional immune assays, are necessary to elucidate the causal pathways underlying these associations fully.

These findings underscore the importance of utilizing high-quality, nutrient-rich feed in aquaculture to promote the health, growth, and development of fish, as well as their immune system. Treatments UE to CDE had no notable discrepancies in water parameters. The result is similar to that of Noureldin et al.84. This demonstrates the impact of various monochromatic LED wavelengths on water quality. The findings underscore the importance of using high-quality, nutrient-dense feed in aquaculture to improve fish health, growth, and development, as well as their immune systems.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that dual enrichment of Artemia with Chlorella vulgaris and Dunaliella salina is associated with improved growth performance, enhanced pigmentation, better intestinal morphology, and modulation of immune- and growth-related gene expression in goldfish juveniles. The combined enrichment strategy yielded more consistent and integrated physiological responses than single-microalgal treatments, suggesting potential synergistic effects of complementary bioactive compounds.

From an applied perspective, these findings support the use of microalgae-enriched live feed as a practical nutritional strategy to enhance the quality of ornamental fish, particularly in terms of coloration, growth efficiency, and overall health. Improved feed conversion and pigmentation may translate into economic benefits for ornamental fish producers by increasing market value and reducing feed costs.

Future studies should investigate optimal enrichment dosages and duration, assess long-term physiological and reproductive effects, and evaluate the applicability of dual-microalgal enrichment in other ornamental fish species. Further mechanistic investigations, including protein-level analysis and functional immune assays, would also help clarify the biological pathways underlying the observed responses.

Overall, dual microalgal enrichment represents a promising and biologically integrative strategy for improving ornamental fish production.
