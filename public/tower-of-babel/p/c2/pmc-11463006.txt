The perceived long-term impact of peer teaching in the skills lab. A descriptive interview study
Authors: Marijke Avonts, Nele R. Michels, Olivier M. Vanderveken, Benedicte Y. De Winter, Katrien Bombeke
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTeaching is an essential skill for future doctors. Peer-assisted learning (PAL)–where students take up a teaching role at an early stage of their training–is widely used in medical curricula. No studies have explored the long-term perceived impact of peer teaching. Therefore, we aimed to determine how former peer teachers reflected on PAL and its perceived long-term impact. In this longitudinal descriptive study, we conducted 42 semi-structured interviews with 11 former peer teachers at the University of Antwerp. Five of them were interviewed 6 months after PAL; 6 of them 9 years after PAL. This latter group was also interviewed during PAL in a previous study. We conducted secondary analyses of previously collected interviews, consistently comparing the findings with new data gathered from the current interviews. Using realist thematic analysis, topic summary themes were generated. Former peer teachers reflected on their PAL experience with a lot of satisfaction. Those meanwhile working as residents are still passionate about teaching. Peer teachers experienced a gain in clinical examination- and professional skills in the long-term, facilitating a smoother transition to their internship. Our findings suggest that PAL selects diverse but committed students. The longitudinal data demonstrate how PAL initiates or stimulates different skills in diverse students, including public speaking, teaching, time management, and self-efficacy in specific clinical skills. Regarding the chicken-and-egg question, our study encompasses both longitudinal cases illustrating the pre-existing skill theory and cases demonstrating how achieved competences were induced by PAL. Former peer teachers experienced long-term benefits of PAL, which eased their transition into the internship. PAL has the potential to ignite a lasting passion for teaching, providing diverse and unique learning opportunities not only for the most talented and ‘pre-selected’ medical students but also for a range of dedicated future clinicians.

Introduction

Teaching is an essential skill for future doctors [1,2] as an important part of a doctor’s activity will be teaching patients, students, and colleagues. Peer-assisted learning (PAL) – where medical students take up a teaching role at an early stage of their training – is widely used in medical curricula. As medical students will have a teaching role later, Dandavino et al. have suggested that PAL should begin at the medical-student level. Another aspect however, is that teaching is an essential aspect of the physician – patient interaction, and medical students become more effective communicators as a result of such training [2].

What are the effects of PAL participation on peer teachers? Eight reviews [3–10] have addressed this question in the past decade. We can conclude that there is still some disagreement as to whether the performance of student teachers improves in exams or not and we need to be mindful of a selection bias. All reviews reported improved learning outputs for student-teachers within several domains, including better knowledge retention, improved skills, improved leadership, improved communication capabilities and increased confidence [5,7,10,11].

In a previous study we concluded that higher academic achieving students are more likely to volunteer for a peer tutoring program in the Skills Lab, confirming a form of selection bias at the start of the program [12]. In a subsequent longitudinal interview study, we revealed how peer teachers developed self-efficacy in their clinical and teaching skills, how they became a role model and how they became mature students with a professional identity by integrating different CanMEDS roles [13].

The following questions arose: ‘Do they master those skills better because they develop these during PAL? Or is there a selection beforehand: students with certain talents choose to become a peer teacher?’ and ‘Are former peer teachers better prepared for their challenging internship because of the acquired maturity?’

The long-term effects of peer teaching remain poorly understood [3,6,8,9]. Peer teachers stated that they feel prepared and motivated for future teaching roles [9,14] and to be involved in teaching in the future [15,16]. Can they live up to their intentions?

No studies have explored how former peer teachers reflected on their teaching period after a few years and how PAL in medical school aided them with their further medical career.

Materials and methods

Context

During medical school, students at the University of Antwerp can apply for a voluntary peer teaching program in the Skills Lab during their 4th and/or 5th year (equaling the 1st and 2nd Master Year, Figure 1). This is 1 or 2 years before the students start a full year of clinical internships and after obtaining their Bachelor’s degree (Figure 1). Figure 1.Timeline of the 6-year medical curriculum at university of Antwerp.(Ba: Bachelor; Ma: Master; PT: Peer Teachers)

Timeline of the 6-year medical curriculum at university of Antwerp.

We cite the concept of our PAL program from our previous study [11]: “The selection of the students was based on a completed application form, in addition to a curriculum vitae and a cover letter explaining their motivation to become a peer-teacher. When the peer teachers were selected (by 2 faculty members from the Skills Lab), they would choose 1 or 2 topics out of a list of all OSCE stations to teach: heart and lung, abdomen, basic life support (BLS) and first aid, suturing, intramuscular (IM) injection and drawing blood, gynecology, musculoskeletal examination, neurology, eye examination and ear, nose, throat (ENT) examination, taping, and examination of a neonate. Subsequently, the selected students received training sessions lasting approximately 2 hours for each teaching topic from an experienced staff member. During this training, they practiced the necessary clinical, physical, and technical skills and received advice on how to teach and provide feedback (didactic skills).

In this peer teaching program students teach the different physical examination skills to their fellow students (3rd to 5th year) and coach them during extracurricular practice sessions. This provides added, voluntary training sessions for students, in addition to the official skills training. It also supports students in mastering the necessary skills and provides an ideal preparation for the OSCE (Objective Structured Clinical Examination) that takes place in the 3rd and 5th years of medical school. Students need to successfully pass these high stakes OSCEs before they can enter a Master’s program or a fulltime clinical internship year.

During the peer skills sessions, 5 to 8 peer teachers present for up to 30 to 50 students. Each peer teacher provides 3 to 5 sessions a year. For each topic a coordinator is appointed for the group of peer teachers. This student is responsible for all the communication between the peer-teachers and the faculty concerning the organization and practical aspects of the training sessions. These coordinators are supervised by the faculty staff member(s) from the Skills Lab.”

Study participants

Junior peer teachers

We mailed a group of junior former peer teachers (n = 68) who recently performed their PAL program, from October 2021 until December 2022. Sixteen participants (n = 16) agreed to participate in this study in time as we scheduled the interviews on a tutor day most students had to attend. From this group, we purposively conducted in-depth interviews with five students to ensure a diverse sample encompassing various genders and teaching topics. As we noticed data saturation after these interviews, the other students were not contacted anymore.

Senior peer teachers

We mailed all peer teachers (n = 13) included in our former interview study [13], 6 agreed to participate. Their initial interviews were obtained during their 2-year PAL period, from October 2014 until May 2016. So, the recent interviews took place 9 years later.

Ethics

Ethics approval was received from the Institutional Review Board of Antwerp University Hospital and the University of Antwerp (20/46/607). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects involved in this study. The participants were aware that this interview was part of the PhD of MA and knew who (MA, KB) were analyzing the data.

Study design

We aimed to investigate what the experienced impact is from being a peer teacher over the long term. To achieve this objective, we opted for a longitudinal qualitative interview study.

We assembled data using semi-structured interviews, guided by a pre-determined set of open questions (Tables 1 and 2) with the opportunity for the interviewer to further explore particular themes or responses. The interviews lasted for about 30 minutes, took place live (at the University) or online (by videocall) and were performed by MA. During the interviews, she noted distinctive details, non-verbal expression. As our native language is Dutch, all interviews were held in Dutch. For the purpose of publication, the quotes were translated by a native English speaker (BM) who has a passive knowledge of the Dutch language. The translated quotes were proofread by MA who concluded that the meaning of the quotes was similar to the original quotes.

The senior peer teachers were also longitudinally interviewed during their PAL period in a previous study [13], from which we also use data in this current study (see further, secondary analyses). These interviews were performed by 2 experienced interviewers (LS and KB). We alternated short face-to-face interviews (15 to 30 minutes in a private room at the University) with interviews by email. The latter consisted of an initial mailing, followed by a response by the interviewer with specific questions to collect all the relevant information. All students had at least 1 face-to-face interview.

Analyses

Leaning more towards a ‘realist’ research perspective (‘small-q’) [17], we used a descriptive thematic analysis for generating themes within our qualitative data. Realism posits a broad assumption that entities with a genuine, objective existence exist in the external world [18]. However, it acknowledges the indirect apprehension of these entities, as they are filtered through the lenses of our brain, language, culture, and methodologies [19].

In this approach, we explored both the experiences of peer teachers as conveyed in the interviews, and the underlying causal powers [18].

In our Thematic Analysis we based our steps on the practical guide of Maguire and Delahunt [20] (Figure 2) also taking Bhaskar’s stratified ontology in account, as explained by Wiltshire and Ronkainen in their paper on Realist TA [18]. They present the comprehensible metaphor of the different layers with how you look at a flower from directly above: ‘The petals are in clear view (the empirical domain), but the rest of the flower (the actual domain) is not. The stem will become visible if we look from another angle. The soil in which the flower is growing (the real domain) is out of the reach of our visual field. This means that we can only know anything about its properties by observing the effects that they have on the flower and builing a theory about it.’ (Table 3). After initial open coding, we identified relationships (axial coding) between the individual codes by inductive reasoning (MA and KB).

Next, we chose a ‘core concept’ for analysis (selective coding) and built ‘themes’ to better understand what the former peer teacher learned from PAL (MA and KB). The different themes were revised toward a general thematic framework. The topic summary themes were chosen because of their recurrence in the interviews, their relevance to our research questions, and/or their innovativeness.

To further investigate the theme of reverse causality, we explicitly asked the junior peer teachers how they experienced this. (Table 6, question 3). For the senior peer teachers, we also performed a secondary analysis of the interviews from the time they were actively peer teaching (coding by MA). This secondary analysis specifically focused on whether the peer teachers developed skills through peer teaching, or if these skills were already present beforehand.

During the process we had several meetings to promote critical reflection with all investigators (MA, NM, OV, BDW, KB). This researcher triangulation method was performed to enhance trustworthiness. We performed a thematic analysis with the aid of NVivo 12.

To shed some light on the perspective of the researchers who performed the analysis, we would like to add that MA (female) is an MD and PhD student. She was a teacher and examinator of the medical-technical skills and the coordinator for the peer teachers when the senior peer teachers were active. At the time of the current interviews, she was only performing research. KB (female) is an MD, PhD and coordinator, teacher, and evaluator of the communication skills. Doing so, she has a teaching and assessing role with the peer teachers but not for the skills who were part of the PAL program. She is an experienced qualitative researcher.

LS (female) is a psychologist and a trained, experienced, and independent interviewer. She was part of the Skills Lab as she was coordinating the standardized/simulated patients. Doing that, she had inside information about how the Skills training is organized but not a teaching nor assessing relation with the students.

We adopted the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (COREQ) guideline [21]. The 32-item checklist is available on request.

Reflecting on PAL experiences

All the former peer teachers were very satisfied with their time as peer teachers. They described it as a valuable chapter in their past.

The senior peer teachers were exposed to quotes from their past interviews. They stated how remarkable it was to recognize their own words and how their quotes from the past still described their functioning as a person.

The junior peer teachers were also very positive about PAL. They mentioned having learned a lot, such as clinical skills, public speaking and managing their plans. Involvement of teaching

All former senior peer teachers, except one, were at the final stage of their residency. They all mentioned that they were passionate about teaching the next generation of medical students, including interns and junior residents. As experienced clinicians, they faced some challenges in balancing teaching and clinical practice, with increasing time constraints and a diminishing motivation for longer hours, especially when interns display a lack of enthusiasm for learning.

They still remembered how they felt as an intern and used this as a driving force to effectively coach the junior students. They hope to be able to continue doing this as a specialist clinician.

For some peer teachers, the experience of being a peer teacher altered how they viewed integrating teaching into their future career. As a student they had the idea that only professors can combine teaching with a medical practice, but gradually they realized this is not a necessity.

They became determined to implement teaching in their professional career, as they experienced how rewarding this can be. One of the peer teachers did have a passion for teaching before he started PAL. Being a peer teacher made him realize he wanted to choose a specialty where teaching is an important factor. Long-term impact of peer teaching

Former peer teachers explained that they were able to master their clinical skills and became more self-confident. This seemed to last for a while.

They experienced an advantage during the preparation of the OSCE in Master 2 and the start of the internship. Later, their clinical experience expanded, and all the clinical skills became a routine.

For some former peer teachers who did not enjoy giving presentations, being a peer teacher instilled enough confidence in their speaking skills. As residents, they would no longer avoid doing presentations, trusting their capability to do it well, even if they don’t necessarily find enjoyment in it.

The peer teachers indicated how PAL gave them a head start in their internship year. Former peer teachers on the topic of suturing felt more adept in the operating room than their peers. Additionally, some peer teachers were impressed by the confidence displayed during their initial staff presentations or how seamlessly they conducted their first patient examinations.

What came first: the chicken or the egg: the question of reverse causality

Are peer teachers already better at certain competencies than other students before PAL or does PAL initiate these? Liam [8] expressed it this way: ‘In any case there is a selection of committed and motivated students who may have a talent for the relevant skills’. This is in line with the statements of the junior peer teachers where we explicitly asked this question.

They were all committed students who were very active in their free time with activities such as coaching, volunteering, and scouting (Tables 6 and 7), but certainly not all of them had a ‘connection’ with teaching.

Their commitment was diverse and so were the peer teachers as a person. There were timid, introvert peer teachers who had consciously chosen this project to stretch their boundaries. On the other hand, there were extravert students who indicated from the beginning that they already mastered skills such as presenting and teaching. This diversity was also reflected in their further education: ranging from doctoral students to both surgical and internist disciplines as general practice.

All students continued their development during PAL, but everyone’s learning process was unique. Some of them clearly indicated how PAL initiated certain skills.

Some expressed that they acquired skills uniquely through PAL, such as public speaking and finding their place in a team. These skills provide benefit to them now as a resident. Initially, others only wanted to pass on their experience, but they discovered how they were energised by teaching. Now they want to combine teaching with clinical practice.

Other peer teachers grew mainly in pre-existing qualities (self-confidence, skills) and did not experience the influence of peer teaching so intensely. Discussion

Our longitudinal follow-up approach of former peer teachers provided a unique impression of how these peer teachers evolved. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the long-term impact of a PAL program in the Skills Lab in medical school.

The former peer teachers reflected on PAL as a valuable experience, and it stimulated them to pursue teaching in the future. They also indicated having gained skills that helped them in their medical career.

Former peer teachers reflected on their PAL experience with a lot of satisfaction, as already mentioned in our previous study. Peer teachers not only enjoy teaching but they also feel satisfied by helping others [13]. This enthusiasm has also been demonstrated by other authors [16,22,23].

In this study the peer teachers mentioned that these feelings persisted when they reflected on this period, up to 9 years later. Pasquinelli & Greenberg (2008) stated that students who learned to teach during medical school, took up this role quickly once they became a resident [24]. This is exactly what our former peer teachers also did and reported.

Besides being satisfied of their PAL experience, those students who were already training as residents were still passionate about teaching, even 9 years after their PAL sessions. This was remarkable, as several authors have concluded that as the workload of residents increases that their enjoyment of teaching decreases. Busy residents rank teaching as a lower priority than competing clinical needs [25]. Our residents admitted that their enthusiasm decreased a little bit over time, but they remained passionate teachers.

Our previous study reported that peer teachers gained self-confidence in their clinical and teaching skills [13]. This study adds that this self-confidence persisted and even helped to ease the start of their internship, as the transition can be challenging and is a source of stress and anxiety for medical students [26]. This is of concern, as stress can impact cognitive functioning and learning [27].

Concerning the question of reverse causality, we noted that PAL selected diverse but committed students. The longitudinal data illustrated how every peer teacher followed their own personal path, where PAL either initiated or enhanced skills such as public speaking, teaching, and self-efficacy in clinical skills. These findings suggest that not only the most talented, ‘pre-selected’ medical students, but also a variety of students can benefit from becoming a peer teacher. This is in contrast with the assumption that high achievers self-select for peer tutoring [11,28,29]. Iwata et al. even suggested that only those with high academic potential may be able to gain extra skills or knowledge through PAL.

There are limited studies that have conducted interviews with peer teachers after participating in PAL. Most of them focused on the benefits, competencies, and perceptions during PAL [30–32]. Only Hall et al. questioned former peer teachers (in anatomy dissection) on the long-term aspects, with some having graduated in the meantime. The most reported benefit was an improvement in confidence for public speaking and better communication skills [23]. The experience as a peer teacher was also successfully used in their applications for specialist training [23]. This is in line with our findings.

All former peer teachers told us that they had no idea how to combine teaching with a clinical career. Through PAL, they engaged with other physicians who successfully integrated teaching into their clinical practice. This exposure provided them with a more diverse perspective on what it means to be a ‘teaching’ physician. In conclusion, PAL contributes to the cultivation of a generation of residents and future specialists equipped with the capability and enthusiasm to educate interns and fellow residents. Being a good teacher does not happen automatically. Good doctors don’t always make good teachers. Without formal preparation, residents are likely to adopt ineffective teaching strategies [33]. Students taught by peer teachers who received teacher training obtain higher examination scores than students taught by untrained peer teachers [14]. Therefore, it is highly recommended that PAL is implemented during medical school.

We also need to emphasize the limitations and strengths of our study. The transferability may be limited as we only interviewed peer teachers at one institution and only within the Skills Lab. To support the transferability of the findings to other educational contexts, we transparently described our specific educational context and the participant characteristics. However, more research is needed to study the effect of different types of peer teachings like small group collaborative learning and large class lectures.

Our design captured their perceptions, opinions, and evolutions, but direct observation of these students was lacking. Integrating observations with interviews could have offered a more comprehensive understanding and a first-hand view of their development.

On the other hand, we were able to incorporate perspectives from former peer teachers who participated recently, as well as those who were involved nine years later, offering both short-term and long-term follow-up insights. Employing a qualitative approach and ensuring privacy (with MA having no teaching or assessing roles towards the interviewed students) facilitated the in-depth discussion of potentially sensitive issues. By staying closely connected to their experiences, we unveiled the processes through which these former peer teachers learned and evolved.

Conclusion

Our study investigated the unexplored topic of the long-term perceived impact of peer teaching, providing valuable insights into the learning process. We conclude that peer teachers gained skills in the long-term, allowing a smoother transition to their internship and fostering a lasting passion for teaching. Additionally, we observed that PAL attracts diverse yet committed students. Each peer teacher undergoes a unique learning process, where PAL either initiates or enhances skills such as public speaking, teaching, and self-efficacy in clinical skills.
