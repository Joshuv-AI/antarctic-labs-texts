The evolution of digital surgery and the role of the surgeon
Authors: Jesse C. Selber, Forrest Bohler, Syena Moltaji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This article outlines the evolution of digital surgery, tracing its trajectory from past to present and into the future. The authors discuss the rise of robotic surgery, the role of robotic surgery within plastic surgery, and the future of artificial intelligence in surgery.

Part 1: The rise of robotic surgery

Minimally invasive surgery has long been desirable due to its association with reduced morbidity.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 However, laparoscopy, though mastered by some, has inherent limitations in precision and visualization.6, 7, 8 Robotic surgery has eliminated many of these obstacles, enabling the full spectrum of intra-abdominal and intrathoracic procedures to be performed with a minimally invasive approach.9, 10, 11

As William Gibson famously said, “The future is already here; It’s just not evenly distributed.”12 This sentiment perfectly encapsulates the state of surgical technology. While laparoscopy will remain relevant for straightforward intra-abdominal procedures in low-resource environments, robotic systems are becoming increasingly dominant (Figure 1).13,14 With over 7000 robotic systems in circulation and more than 10 million robotic procedures performed globally, it's evident that robotic surgery is supplanting laparoscopy in many regions.15,16 Just as it took the automobile 10 years to overtake the horse and buggy as the primary means of transportation in American cities, we will continue to see laparoscopy co-exist with robotic surgery for years to come.Figure 1Trends in surgical approaches.Figure 1

Trends in surgical approaches.

This technological transformation provides the backdrop for the emergence of robotic surgery within plastic and reconstructive surgery. In transitioning from a fellowship following a categorical general surgery residency to an integrated training program, plastic surgery training has excluded general surgical skill acquisition and, by extension, minimally invasive techniques.17,18 Moreover, the specialty's frequent focus on skin manipulation and superficial structures has made open approaches more practical and minimally invasive techniques less relevant.19 These factors have slowed the adoption of robotic systems in the field of plastic surgery.

Part 2: Robotic surgery in plastic and reconstructive surgery

In the early 2000s, I (JCS) was fortunate to be at the University of Pennsylvania during the development of transoral robotic surgery.20 I recognized that while these resections were extensive, reconstruction was lagging due to the difficulty of insetting flaps into very confined spaces.21 In 2009, I completed my fellowship training and began performing transoral robotic reconstructions at MD Anderson Cancer Center (Houston, TX). I presented this work later that year at the American Association of Plastic Surgeons and was met with both interest and understandable skepticism. Given the limited number of head and neck reconstructions performed by plastic surgeons, this technique has remained niche. Nonetheless, the ability to achieve precise flap inset without mandibular splitting is a significant advantage in selected cases (Figure 2).21 With the advent of the Single Port robot (SP) (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA), transoral resections and reconstructions is far more ergonomic because all the instruments deploy from a single cannula, limiting proximal collisions with dental structures.Figure 2A) post-operative result of traditional transoral approach for oral access with mandibulotomy B) post-operative result of transoral robotic approach for oral access.Figure 2

In 2010–2012, I initiated robotic harvest of the latissimus dorsi and rectus abdominis muscles.22, 23, 24 When the skin island is deemed unnecessary, the muscle-only latissimus can be harvested robotically with a high degree of precision and without a large donor site incision (Figure 3).22,23 This is applicable in partial and implant-based breast reconstruction, as well as in free tissue transfers. I presented this work at The European Association of Plastic Surgeons (EURAPS) in Munich in 2012 and was again met with a mix of enthusiasm and skepticism.25 Though not widely adopted due to the technical complexity and the inherent urgency of trauma cases, robotic latissimus harvest remains in use at select centers.26,27 The multi-port robotic system (Xi, DV5, Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA) has the disadvantage of requiring instruments to be far enough apart to avoid collisions, yet remain confluent in a single space, making use in potential spaces more difficult. Because the latissimus dorsi harvest requires the creation of a potential space, instead of an actual body cavity, the SP device is better suited to this application (this is also why the SP has been the preferred robotic interface for Robotic Nipple Sparing Mastectomy).Figure. 3. In contrast, the robotic rectus muscle harvest has gained wider spread popularity.28, 29, 30, 31 As robotic abdominoperineal resections (APRs) have become the standard for colorectal malignancy, the need for a minimally invasive reconstructive option has emerged.32,33 A laparotomy to harvest the rectus defeats the purpose of a minimally invasive APR, making the robotic rectus an elegant solution to the unsolved problem of pelvic dead-space obliteration and wound healing optimization while maintaining an MIS approach (Figure 4). Around this time and informed by my experience with the robotic rectus, I recognized that the deep inferior epigastric pedicle could be accessed more directly and with less damage to the overlying neuro-musculature from within the abdominal cavity. This insight led to the development of the robotic deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP) flap in 2018 (Figure 5).34 Until that point, the DIEP flap technique had remained largely unchanged since its introduction by pioneers such as Bob Allen, Isao Koshima, and Phil Blondeel in the late 1990s and early 2000s.35,36 In the years since its publication in 2020, the robotic DIEP has gained global traction and is now performed on every continent in the world.34,37Figure 5Robotic-assisted DIEP port placement setup.Figure 5

In 2008, I began exploring the da Vinci platform for microsurgical anastomoses, driven by the belief that robotic augmentation could enhance the precision and consistency required for microsurgery. I completed my first clinical case in 2010 using the DaVinci Si (Intuitive Surgical), an early milestone that reinforced my conviction about the role robotics would play in the future of our field. My syllogism was simple:•Microsurgery requires high levels of precision•Robotic platforms excel at high levels of precision•Robotic platforms should excel at microsurgery

During this time I, along with a small group of like-minded physicians, co-founded the Robotic Assisted Microsurgical & Endoscopic Society (RAMSES), based on the shared vision that robotic microsurgery would eventually supersede conventional techniques.43 Microsurgery is uniquely suited to this transformation, as it combines the need for human cognitive function with a demand for technical precision, an ideal scenario for robotic integration. RAMSES soon became a hub for innovators, including the teams behind MUSA (Microsure, Einhoven, Netherlands) and SYMANI (MMI, Florence, Italy).44,45 In 2013, the MUSA prototype was presented at the RAMSES meeting in Orlando, and the following year in Geneva, Hanna and Giuseppe introduced their early work on SYMANI. I have worked with both these teams over the last decade and both systems have since gained approval in Europe, Asia, and the United States (MMI only), drawing increasing interest from leaders across surgical subspecialties. More systems are being designed and build as we speak.

Although current iterations may not yet surpass manual microsurgery, they lay the groundwork for a transformative shift.

Part 3: Artificial intelligence and the future of surgery

If you had fallen asleep in 2022 and awakened in 2025, the most profound change in the tech-scape would be the exponential leap in artificial intelligence (AI). Once a low-fidelity tool, AI has become the most powerful force reshaping our world.46,47 With semiconductors now more valuable than oil, the race for AI dominance has supplanted the nuclear arms race, this time between the U.S. and China.48,49

In healthcare, AI threatens to displace traditional diagnostic roles, and at the same time promises to be the answer to profound labor shortages among providers. Pattern recognition, long the hallmark of experienced physicians, is now performed more effectively by algorithms.50, 51, 52, 53 Radiology and pathology are likely the first fields to transition fully to AI-based interpretation.51,54, 55, 56 Even beyond these fields, AI will begin to shoulder cognitive pattern recognition tasks across all specialties, accelerating decision-making, and eroding the traditional medical learning curve, which historically relied on trial, error, and patient exposure.54,57,58

While this upheaval may displace many professional roles, it also creates an opportunity to re-emphasize the holistic, humanistic side of medicine. In surgery, AI's impact will occur in three phases:

Predictive analytics

Much like sports analytics revolutionized professional athletics (as seen in “Moneyball”), predictive analytics will become central to surgical practice.59,60 Just as athletes now review gameplay footage to improve performance, surgeons can analyze recorded procedures to understand decision-making and technique.61,62

Robotic platforms, by inserting a computer between the operator and the effector, create an opportunity for machines learning from recorded surgical videos as training data.63 Intuitive's Case Insights platform already delivers feedback on motion efficiency, suturing performance, and operative time comparisons.64 Surgeons can use this data to refine their technique and improve outcomes.

Augmented reality and navigation

Incorporating visual systems and overlays allows surgeons to see more than the human eye can perceive. From simple enhancements like indocyanine green angiography to complex scene segmentation and guidance, augmented reality improves situational awareness.65,66 Navigation becomes the logical extension, guiding the surgeon through optimal procedural paths.

The latest Intuitive system, the DV5, exemplifies this shift with increased compute capacity, enabling real-time analysis and feedback.67 MMI is pursuing a parallel path through SYMANI and its digital platform, Synaptix, approved by the FDA in September 2025, which integrates predictive analytics, AR, and AI to provide a similar set of capabilities and inch closer to autonomy.

Autonomous surgery

The true inflection point will be when AI not only guides but independently performs surgery. All the ingredients are now present. The Smart Tissue Autonomous Robot (STAR) platform at Johns Hopkins has already executed autonomous cholecystectomies and bowel anastomoses in porcine models.68,69

While widespread deployment may take time, and clinical application in humans has yet to be tested, autonomous surgery is now widely regarded as inevitable. But technological progress alone is not enough. The complete removal of the human hand from the scalpel will require not only clinical validation, but also societal acceptance, an emotional and philosophical shift that may prove just as formidable as the technical barriers. For many, the notion of a machine performing surgery without direct human control remains a conceptual red line, something imagined but not believed. Yet, if and when evidence demonstrates that outcomes from autonomous systems surpass those of even the most experienced surgeons, the standard of care will evolve. At that point, it will no longer be a matter of human excellence setting the bar, but rather, whether we as a profession are willing to accept that the bar has already been raised. What I once believed—that the executive function of the human mind combined with robotic precision was the pinnacle of surgical innovation—has evolved. The reality is that machines will soon surpass humans in defined technical tasks. We, as a specialty, will need to recapture what we learned in medical school – that being a doctor is about taking care of the person, and just as humans are not expected to have Xray vision to detect fractures, we may also not be expected to suture 1 mm vessels together inside the human body. These types of transformations can occur right under our nose. I currently live in Michigan, and found a colleague surprised to hear that self-driving Ubers were commonplace in Arizona and California (again: The future is here, it’s just not evenly distributed).

As Bill Gates once said, “We always overestimate the change that will occur in the next 2 years and underestimate the change that will occur in the next 10.”70 When I began speaking about robotic microsurgery in 2009, I overestimated its pace of adoption but not its ultimate presence in our field. I will not make the same mistake in 2025 by attempting to predict the timeline of autonomous surgery. What I will say is that the future is not only coming—major components of it are already here. Our challenge is to recognize that in this moment, we must engage with it and prepare for what is now an inescapable shift in surgical practice.

Human paradigm shift

Plastic surgery has entered a phase where, for well-bounded subtasks, the machine can outperform us. The appeal is not gadgetry; it’s outcomes that matter to patients: less pain, faster return to function, and less morbidity when the reconstruction itself is meant to restore well-being.32, 33, 34,37,39, 40, 41 That reality doesn’t diminish the surgeon—it redefines the job. If a platform can isolate perforators faster, measure perfusion more consistently, or perform a more precise anastomosis, then surgical excellence shifts toward what only humans do well: setting goals with patients, communicating under uncertainty, exercising judgment about trade-offs, and inventing the next instrument or workflow by drawing connections across domains.

Artificial intelligence is the ultimate force multiplier. Today it accelerates the parts of reconstructive planning and execution that are slow, subjective, or fragile: 3D mapping perforators on CTA/MRA rather than scrolling through cuts (Figure 8); turning ICG angiography from a “looks good” impression into quantitative thresholds; and extending postoperative vigilance with automated image- and signal-based flap monitoring that escalates human attention only when needed.61, 62, 63, 64, 65, 66Figure 8Surgical theatre rendering of DIEP perforators.Figure 8

Across medicine, the writing is on the wall: for pattern-recognition tasks, algorithms increasingly match or surpass clinicians.49, 50, 51, 52, 53, 54, 55 What once masqueraded as wisdom, has now shown itself to be repetition, and it does not need to come at the patient’s expense. In other words, the old adage, “Good Judgement comes from Experience and Experience Comes from Bad Judgement” will not be as true as it once was. Let the machine do what it’s good at (stable camera, motion scaling, quantitative perfusion, continuous monitoring) while we do what only a clinician can (for now): align reconstruction with patient values; adjudicate trade-offs when anatomy, oncologic needs, and life context collide; and decide when “less morbidity” now might mean “higher patient satisfaction” later.28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34,37,39, 40, 41,64, 65, 662)Design the choreography of human–machine teaming. Autonomy will come in pockets. The technical ingredients already exist in preclinical models, and selective autonomy will likely arrive first for constrained, repetitive maneuvers under tight supervision.67,68 But the threshold for removing the human hand—or more importantly, the human mind—from reconstruction is not just technical; it is clinical, ethical, and societal. Our job is to shape the evidence and the expectations so that when autonomy does outperform humans for specific subtasks, we integrate it safely and humanely.

Plastic surgery has always been a dialogue between hands and ideas. In the digital era, it becomes a three-way conversation: human intelligence, machine intelligence, and patient goals. In the future, optimizing outcomes and efficiency using technology will be the ability to orchestrate this trio so that cold precision serves warm purpose—and the patient receives all the benefits of super human, robotic precision, complemented by the care of human hands and hearts.
