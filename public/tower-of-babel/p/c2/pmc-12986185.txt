Development of a Conditional Replication System Using a Lassa Virus Glycoprotein Complex-Encoding Retroviral Vector for Isolating Resistant Variants to Inhibitors in BSL-2
Authors: Manya Bakatumana Hans, Anita Moendat Fanto, Tsutomu Fukuda, Koushirou Suga, Masatomo Iwao, Hideki Hayashi, Masaru Yokoyama, Hironori Sato, Olivier Tshiani Mbaya, Osamu Kotani, Yoshinao Kubo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A high-risk infectious disease or a Category A pathogen, Lassa virus (LASV), requires strict containment, classified as biosafety level 4 (BSL-4) conditions, which restricts research on the virus due to the scarcity of BSL-4 facilities. Thus, replication-defective pseudotyped retroviral vectors have been widely used as safe materials for neutralizing activity assays of drugs and antibodies in BSL-2. Here, we established a novel retroviral vector system encoding LASV glycoprotein complex (GPC) that can exclusively replicate in cells expressing the Gag-Pol protein of murine leukemia virus (MLV) under BSL-2 conditions. Using this conditional replication system, we successfully isolated LASV GPC variants resistant to either an anti-LASV compound, lamellarin α 20-sulfate, or a neutralizing antibody derived from a Lassa fever survivor. In the lamellarin α 20-sulfate-resistant variants, K125E and H13R amino acid substitutions cooperatively conferred resistance. The K125E enhanced infectivity and simultaneously conferred a lethal effect on cells in the conditional replication system, while the H13R mitigated the latter effect, thereby enabling stable expression of LASV GPC in cells. In the neutralizing antibody-resistant variants, I403T substitution was responsible for the resistance by impairing antibody binding. This study provides a valuable BSL-2-based platform for isolating LASV GPC variants resistant to inhibitors and characterizing their mutations.

1. Introduction

Lassa virus (LASV) is an Old World arenavirus endemic to West Africa, including Nigeria, Sierra Leone, Liberia, Guinea, Togo, and the Benin Republic. This disease has been reported with a high burden and causes an estimated 200,000 to 300,000 infections and thousands of deaths yearly [1,2,3,4]. Lassa fever is a disease characterized by a wide spectrum of clinical symptoms ranging from mild febrile illness to severe hemorrhagic fever, thereby posing diagnostic and treatment challenges in endemic regions [5]. The virus represents a major public health threat due to its high fatality rate among severely ill hospitalized patients and high-risk groups such as pregnant women and children, its potential to trigger outbreaks, and the lack of available vaccines or treatments [6,7,8].

LASV is classified as a Category A pathogen that requires specific containment in biosafety level 4 (BSL-4) facilities [9]. However, such facilities remain scarce worldwide, particularly in affected endemic countries, thereby restricting research on LASV. Consequently, no approved therapeutic drugs or vaccines are currently available [6,7,8]. To overcome these limitations, replication-defective retroviral vectors pseudotyped with LASV glycoprotein complex (GPC), which can be handled in BSL-2 laboratories, are widely employed to investigate the pathobiological functions of LASV GPC [10,11].

Isolating resistant variants prior to the clinical use of therapeutic drugs and neutralizing antibodies is crucial for their clinical application [12,13]. Understanding the mechanisms of resistance not only elucidates how inhibitors and neutralizing antibodies block LASV infection at the molecular level but also helps identify mutations that should be monitored after clinical approval. However, since LASV GPC-pseudotyped retroviral vectors are replication-defective, they cannot be used to isolate resistant variants. To overcome these limitations, a conditional replication system of LASV-pseudotyped retroviral vector is required to isolate variants resistant to GPC-targeting inhibitors [14,15].

To construct a replication-defective LASV GPC-pseudotyped retroviral vector, human 293T cells are typically co-transfected with three plasmids [16]: a murine leukemia virus (MLV) Gag-Pol expression plasmid, a LASV GPC expression plasmid, and a retroviral vector genome expression plasmid containing the packaging signal. The Gag-Pol and LASV GPC expression plasmids, which lack the packaging signal, provide the viral structural and envelope proteins necessary for infectious virion assembly. In contrast, the retroviral vector genome expression plasmid encodes the RNA genome that is packaged into vector particles but does not contribute to viral protein production in the producer cells. Consequently, target cells infected with the resulting pseudotyped retroviral vector express no viral protein, as only the vector genome is delivered and expressed in the infected cells.

When 293T cells were transfected with a Gag-Pol expression plasmid and a retroviral vector genome expression plasmid encoding the LASV glycoprotein complex, LASV GPC and vector genome RNA were produced from the latter plasmid and utilized for pseudotyping and genome packaging, respectively (Figure 1). Target cells infected with this LASV GPC-encoding retroviral vector express the LASV GPC and the retroviral vector genome RNA; in the absence of Gag-Pol, the vector cannot undergo further replication. When this retroviral vector is inoculated into Gag-Pol-expressing cells, it is hypothesized that new LASV GPC-pseudotyped and GPC-encoding retroviral vector can be produced again from the infected cells. Therefore, this LASV GPC-encoding retroviral vector may replicate conditionally, only in cells that express Gag-Pol. Owing to this replication restriction, the conditional replication system can be safely manipulated under BSL-2 conditions.

This conditional replication system using LASV GPC-pseudotyped retroviral vector is useful for isolating GPC variants that are resistant to antiviral drugs or neutralizing antibodies. In this study, LASV GPC variants resistant to either the GPC-targeting antiviral compound lamellarin α 20-sulfate or a neutralizing antibody derived from a Lassa fever survivor were successfully isolated using this system.

2. Results

2.1. Development of a Conditional Replication System of LASV-Pseudotyped Retroviral Vector

Human 293T cells were transfected with a MLV Gag-Pol expression plasmid and a retroviral vector genome expression plasmid encoding the LASV GPC. Culture supernatants from the transfected cells were used to inoculate either normal TE671 cells or TE671 cells stably expressing Gag-Pol [17]. Following inoculation, the cells were treated with puromycin, as the retroviral vector genome additionally encodes the puromycin resistant gene (Figure 1). Supernatants from the resulting puromycin-resistant cell cultures were filtered through 0.45 μm membranes to remove residual cells, and subsequently used to inoculate into normal TE671 cells. When these inoculated cells reached to about 80% confluence, they were subjected to puromycin selection. Two weeks post-selection, the cells were stained with methylene blue to visualize surviving cells. Puromycin-resistant cells were detected only in TE671 cells inoculated with supernatants derived from the inoculated Gag-Pol-expressing TE671 cells, but not in those inoculated with supernatants from the inoculated normal TE671 cells (Figure S1A). These results indicate that infectious retroviral particles carrying the puromycin resistant gene were generated exclusively in Gag-Pol-expressing cells. This finding supports the functionality of a conditional replication system of LASV-pseudotyped retroviral vector, in which vector propagation is dependent on the presence of Gag-Pol in target cells.

To evaluate whether a conditional replication system could also be established for amphotropic MLV vector, a retroviral vector genome expression plasmid encoding the amphotropic MLV envelope (Env) protein was constructed using pMX-puro vector [18] (Figure S1B). Human 293T cells were co-transfected with the Gag-Pol expression plasmid and amphotropic Env-encoding retroviral vector genome expression plasmid (pMX-MLV Env). Culture supernatants from the transfected cells were then used to inoculate Gag-Pol-expressing TE671 cells. Following puromycin selection, no puromycin-resistant cells were observed, indicating that infectious vector particles were not produced under these conditions.

To investigate the reason for the absence of infectious vector particles, Western blot analysis was performed using cell lysates from transfected 293T cells and probed with either anti-LASV GP1 or anti-MLV SU antibody. Two types of viral glycoprotein expression plasmids were used: pTargeT-based expression plasmid (pT-LASV GPC and pT-MLV Env) and retroviral vector genome expression plasmids (pMX-LASV GPC and pMX-MLV Env) [18]. MLV Env protein was detected in 293T cells transiently transfected with pT-MLV Env, but not with pMX-MLV Env (Figure S1C). In contrast, LASV GP1 protein was readily detected in cells transfected with either pT-LASV GPC or pMX-LASV GPC (Figure S1D). These results indicate that the failure to establish a conditional replication system for the amphotropic MLV vector was due to the lack of Env protein expression from the retroviral vector genome expression plasmid, consistent with a previous report [15]. Without Env expression, infectious vector particles cannot be generated, thereby preventing successful vector propagation.

2.2. Conservation of LASV GPC Sequence During Conditional Replication in the Absence of Selective Pressure

MLV-based retroviral vector encoding the LASV GPC were produced by transfecting 293T cells, and subsequently used to inoculate Gag-Pol-expressing TE671 cells in the absence of chemical inhibitors or neutralizing antibodies (Figure 2A). The inoculated cells were cultured for 9 days before selection with puromycin. Once the puromycin-resistant cells reached confluence, the culture supernatant was harvested and used to inoculate fresh Gag-Pol-expressing TE671 cells. This process was repeated for a total of 3 sequential passages.

Total RNA was then extracted from the final population of puromycin-resistant cells, and the LASV GPC coding region was amplified by RT-PCR. The amplified products were cloned into the pTargeT plasmid, resulting in 4 LASV GPC expression clones (C2, C43, C48, and C71). Their nucleotide sequences were determined (Figure S2). Among the 4 clones, clone C48 contained a single nucleotide substitution that resulted in an amino acid change from asparagine to aspartic acid at position 127 (N127D), compared to the wild type (Wt) LASV GPC sequence (Figure 2B and Figure S3). Clone C43 harbored another nucleotide substitution, but it was synonymous and did not alter the amino acid sequence. Clones C2 and C71 showed no mutations in the LASV GPC nucleotide sequence. These findings indicate that the LASV GPC sequence remains largely conserved during conditional replication in the absence of selective pressure, with only minor variations observed after multiple passages.

To evaluate the transduction efficiency of replication-defective MLV vector harboring LASV GPC variants isolated from the conditional replication system, 293T cells were co-transfected with the MLV Gag-Pol expression plasmid, a luciferase-encoding MLV vector genome expression plasmid, and each of the LASV GPC variant expression plasmids (C2, C43, C48, and C71). Culture supernatants from the transfected cells were then used to inoculate normal TE671 cells, and luciferase activities in cell lysates from the inoculated cells were measured. Luciferase activity in cells inoculated with the C48 LASV GPC-containing vector was approximately two-fold higher than that observed with the Wt LASV GPC vector (Figure 2C). In contrast, vectors containing GPC from clone C2, C43, and C71—whose amino acid sequences were identical to the Wt—showed luciferase activity comparable to that of the Wt vector. Western blot analysis confirmed that expression levels and proteolytic processing of the LASV GPC were not affected among the different clones (Figure 2D).

A C48 GPC-encoding MLV vector genome expression plasmid was constructed. Human 293T cells were transfected with this plasmid together with a Gag-Pol expression plasmid, and the culture supernatants were used to inoculate Gag-Pol-expressing TE671 cells to generate a conditional replication system for the C48-encoding MLV vector. The culture supernatants were inoculated into normal TE671 cells, and cultured in the presence of puromycin. Puromycin-resistant colonies were subsequently counted. Although the number of puromycin-resistant colonies obtained with C48 was comparable to that with Wt (Figure S4), the luciferase-encoding C48-pseudotyped MLV vector exhibited 2-fold higher luciferase activity in the inoculated cells than the Wt (Figure 2C). The N127D amino acid substitution was also reported during the replication of a recombinant LASV with a codon-deoptimized GPC [19]. These findings indicate that the N127D mutation in LASV GPC moderately enhances the infectivity, and suggest that such an infectivity-enhancing mutation can arise during the replication of LASV GPC-encoding MLV vector to confer a fitness advantage in cell culture.

2.3. Isolation of LASV GPC Variants Resistant to Lamellarin α 20-Sulfate

We previously reported that lamellarin α 20-sulfate inhibits infection mediated by the glycoproteins of human immunodeficiency virus type 1, Ebola virus, and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus type 2 by targeting the viral glycoproteins [20,21]. To evaluate whether this compound also inhibits LASV GPC-mediated infection, a luciferase-encoding LASV GPC-pseudotyped MLV vector was inoculated into TE671 cells in the absence or presence of lamellarin α 20-sulfate (50 μM). Luciferase activity in cell lysates from inoculated cells treated with the chemical was decreased, demonstrating that lamellarin α 20-sulfate inhibits LASV GPC-mediated infection (Figure S5).

To isolate lamellarin α 20-sulfate-resistant LASV GPC variants, the GPC-encoding MLV vector was inoculated into Gag-Pol-expressing TE671 cells in the presence of lamellarin α 20-sulfate (50 μM) (Figure 3A). Three days after inoculation, the cells were selected with puromycin. Once the puromycin-resistant cells reached confluence, their culture supernatant was transferred to fresh Gag-Pol-expressing TE671 cells in the presence of the chemical (50 μM). Following puromycin selection, the resulting culture supernatant was again used to inoculate fresh Gag-Pol-expressing cells under the same drug conditions. The LASV GPC-coding region was subsequently amplified by RT-PCR, and 7 GP expression plasmids based on pTargeT mammalian expression vector were constructed (L1, L5, L6, L7, L15, L20, and L23) (Figure 3B).

Their nucleotide sequences were determined (Figure S6). Clone L6 and L20 each contained two amino acid substitutions, H13R and K125E (Figure 3B and Figure S7), thereby clone L20 was not used in the following experiments. The remaining clones were unique, with L5 and L15 carrying amino acid changes at N-linked glycosylation sites. In total, 26 nucleotide substitutions were identified, 18 of which resulted in amino acid substitutions. The relatively high ratio of nonsynonymous mutations indicates selective pressure on LASV GPC (Figure S8). In addition, frequent G-to-A substitutions were observed, suggesting that APOBEC3G may be involved in the generation of LASV GPC variants [22,23] (Figure S9).

To identify LASV GPC variants resistant to lamellarin α 20-sulfate, 293T cells were transfected with the luciferase-encoding MLV vector genome expression plasmid and MLV Gag-Pol expression plasmid together with each LASV GPC variant expression plasmid. Culture supernatants from the transfected cells were inoculated into normal TE671 cells in the absence or presence of the chemical (50 μM), and luciferase activity was measured in the inoculated cells. No variant exhibited resistance to lamellarin α 20-sulfate at 50 μM, as luciferase activity of inoculated cells was consistently reduced in its presence. Interestingly, however, luciferase activity in TE671 cells inoculated with the MLV vector pseudotyped with the L6 GPC was approximately 30-fold higher than that with the Wt GPC (Figure 3C).

To assess the sensitivity of Wt and L6-mediated infection to lamellarin α 20-sulfate, dose-dependent effects were examined. Treatment of target cells with lamellarin α 20-sulfate at concentrations of 50 μM similarly reduced luciferase activity in cells inoculated with either the Wt or L6 GPC-containing vector (Figure S10) as the above result. In contrast, treatment at 25 μM attenuated L6-mediated infection less efficiently than Wt-mediated infection, showing that the L6 variant exhibits reduced sensitivity to lamellarin α 20-sulfate compared with Wt.

To analyze the expression of these LASV GPC variants, Western blotting was performed using lysates from transfected cells. Consistent with the nucleotide sequences of L5 and L15, which carry substitutions at the N-linked glycosylation sites, the precursor GPCs of these variants migrated faster in electrophoresis than the Wt GPC (Figure 3D). The levels of mature GP1 in L5, L6, L7, and L15 were reduced compared with the Wt, suggesting that the processing of these variants was impaired. Notably, although the L6 GPC induced markedly higher infectivity, its processing was also suppressed.

2.4. K125E Is Responsible for High Infectivity and Reduced Sensitivity to Lamellarin α 20-Sulfate

The L6 clone harbors two amino acid substitutions, H13R and K125E. To determine which substitution accounts for the increased infectivity, LASV GPC mutant expression plasmids containing each substitution were constructed. Human 293T cells were transfected with MLV Gag-Pol expression plasmid and the luciferase-encoding MLV vector genome expression plasmid together with each GPC mutant expression plasmid. Culture supernatants from the transfected cells were inoculated into normal TE671 cells, and luciferase activity in cell lysates was measured. Strikingly, luciferase activity in cells inoculated with the K125E mutant-containing MLV vector was more than 100-fold higher than that with the Wt vector (Figure 4A), indicating that the K125E substitution is responsible for the increased infectivity. In contrast, luciferase activity in cells inoculated with the H13R mutant vector was lower than that with the Wt vector.

To analyze GPC mutant expression, Western blotting was performed using lysates from transfected cells. Comparable levels of precursor GPC were detected in Wt, L6, and K125E (Figure 4B). In contrast, the levels of mature GP1 in L6, H13R, and K125E were lower than those in Wt. In cells transfected with H13R mutant, the precursor was not detected; instead, digested fragments were observed, suggesting that the H13R mutation renders LASV GPC more susceptible to proteolytic digestion. Although the L6 variant carried the H13R substitution, the precursor level of L6 was comparable to that of Wt, and digested fragments were not detected. Thus, either the H13R or K125E single-substitution has the ability to alter differentially the processing of LASV GPC.

To confirm that the K125E mutation enhances LASV GPC-mediated infection, a conditional replication system of L6 or K125E GPC-expressing MLV vectors was constructed. Human 293T cells were transfected with the Gag-Pol expression plasmid and either the L6 or K125E GPC-encoding MLV vector genome expression plasmid. Culture supernatant was inoculated into Gag-Pol-expressing TE671 cells. After the inoculated cells were cultured for 9 days, the culture supernatants were serially diluted and inoculated into normal TE671 cells. Following puromycin selection, puromycin-resistant colonies were counted. The number of puromycin-resistant colonies in the conditional replication system with L6 GPC was higher than that with Wt (Figure 4C). Interestingly, no puromycin-resistant colonies were detected in the conditional replication system with K125E GPC, despite the K125E substitution enhancing LASV GPC-mediated infection by more than 100-fold in transiently produced replication-defective vector system.

To assess the effect on cell viability, TE671 cells were transfected with each LASV GPC expression plasmid together with a plasmid encoding nano luciferase N-terminally fused to the IL-6 signal peptide (sNLuc). Two days after transfection, sNLuc activity in the culture supernatants was measured. Among all LASV GPC expression plasmids tested, sNLuc activity in K125E-transfected cells was the lowest (Figure S11A), suggesting that one possible explanation is K125E-mediated cytotoxicity.

When the replication-defective MLV vector containing the K125E GPC was constructed by transient transfection, the K125E-containing vector exhibited more than a 100-fold higher transduction efficiency than the Wt-containing vector (Figure 4A), indicating that the K125E GPC does not directly impair viral particle production or infection. Therefore, if K125E had no adverse effect on cell viability, the conditional replication system using K125E would be expected to show higher replication efficiency and to generate a greater number of puromycin-resistant colonies, because it utilizes the same MLV proteins and vector genome backbone (pMX-puro) as the replication-defective MLV vector. However, no puromycin-resistant colonies were observed, and the conditional replication system using K125E GPC could not be established (Figure 4C). During the 9-day culture of inoculated Gag-Pol-expressing TE671 cells and the subsequent 3-week puromycin selection of inoculated normal TE671 cells, K125E-expressing cells failed to survive, suggesting that K125E GPC everts an adverse effect on cell viability (Figure S11B). In contrast, the inhibitory effect of K125E-mediated cytotoxicity on MLV vector infection in the transient transfection system was minimal, because viral vectors were harvested only 2 days after transfection, before severe cytotoxic effects became apparent. Although other unknown mechanisms that may contribute to the absence of puromycin-resistant colonies in the conditional replication system using K125E cannot be completely excluded, it is most likely that the K125E-mediated cytotoxicity is the primary causal factor.

Notably, the number of puromycin-resistant colonies was higher in the L6 GPC-expressing conditional replication system than in the Wt system, and sNLuc activity was higher in L6-transfected cells than in K125E-transfected cells, although the L6 GPC carries the K125E substitution. These results suggest that the additional H13R mutation in the L6 GPC mitigates the inhibitory effect of K125E substitution on cell viability, thereby allowing for stable expression in infected cells.

2.5. Effects of K125E and H13R Mutations on GPC Structure and Biochemical Properties

To better understand the effect of the K125E substitution on the molecular interaction between LASV GPC and its receptor, we constructed a molecular model of the LASV GPC trimer bound to α-dystroglycan (α-DG), as described in Section 4. The model showed that the K125 amino acid residue is located near the α-DG binding pocket (Figure 5A), suggesting a potential role of this residue in regulating receptor-binding efficiency. Indeed, in silico mutagenesis of the Wt GP1-α-DG complex model showed that most of the single mutations at position 125 altered the binding affinity of GP1 to α-DG (Figure 5B). When the change in binding free energy upon mutation (ΔΔG) is positive (ΔΔG > 0), the mutation destabilizes the LASV-GPC–α-DG interaction, resulting in reduced binding affinity. Conversely, a negative ΔΔG (ΔΔG < 0) indicates that the mutation stabilizes the interaction and increases binding affinity. Among them, the single K125E substitution in the GP1-α-DG complex induced a mild reduction in the binding affinity of GP1 for the glycan receptor (Figure 5B, arrow). This finding is consistent with our experimental data (Figure 5C); binding of HIV-1 vectors containing LASV GPC to TE671 cells at 4 °C was slightly reduced in the K125E mutant compared with Wt (right panel), although the luciferase activity in TE671 cells inoculated with the K125E GPC-containing HIV-1 vector was markedly higher than that in cells inoculated with the Wt GPC-containing vector (left panel). This substitution effect likely involves the reduction in the positively-charged-patch surface surrounding the receptor binding pocket (Figure 5D); the changes in chemical property around the receptor binding pocket would reduce electrostatic attractions between GPC and the negatively charged glycan receptor. Taken together, these results suggest that although the amino acid residue at position 125 plays a key role in determining the binding efficiency of GP1 to α-DG, the experimentally observed enhancement in vector infectivity by the single K125E substitution (Figure 4A) is unlikely to occur at the GP1-α-DG binding step.

Interestingly, the K125E-induced inhibitory effect on α-DG binding in silico was mitigated by the additional mutation H13R (Figure 5B, H13R/K125E). The H13 residue is positioned at the end of the stable signal peptide (SSP) region, which noncovalently associated with the GPC trimer, and plays an essential role in its proper folding and stability [26,27]. Therefore, we further examined the potential effects of K125E on the stability of the GPC trimer. When the change in free energy of LASV-GPC and α-DG complex upon mutation (ΔΔG) is positive (ΔΔG > 0), the mutation destabilizes the LASV-GPC–α-DG structure. Conversely, a negative ΔΔG (ΔΔG < 0) indicates that the mutation stabilizes the structure complex. Either H13R or K125E single mutation was predicted to slightly reduce trimer stability (Figure 5E, H13R and K125E). Notably, the combinational mutation of H13R and K125E reversed the effects of the single mutations (Figure 5E, H13R/K125E). Together with the positional information and the experimental results on the vector replication, H13R appears to be a compensatory mutation that repairs the lethal effects of the K125E single mutation.

2.6. K125E Is Responsible for Reduced Sensitivity to Lamellarin α 20-Sulfate

To determine which amino acid substitution is responsible for the reduced sensitivity of L6 GPC to lamellarin α 20-sulfate, dose-dependent effects on each mutant-mediated infection were examined. Treatment of target cells with lamellarin α 20-sulfdate at a concentration of 50 μM, similarly reduced luciferase activity in cells inoculated with either the Wt-, L6-, or K125E-containing vector (Figure 6A). In contrast, treatment at 25 μM attenuated L6- and K125E-mediated infection less efficiently than Wt-mediated infection, showing that the K125E substitution is responsible for this reduced sensitivity.

We conducted in silico docking simulations using the LASV GPC structure. Both commercially available and academic docking programs generally provided reliable predictions for a set of appropriate ligand-binding poses, whereas the ranking of these docking poses based on binding affinity is not always accurate [25]. Therefore, we utilized the Dock tool in MOE to characterize the top 100 docking poses, which were ranked according to the degree of binding affinity and steric hindrance. Among these top 100 binding poses, 43 were localized to the head region of LASV GPC (Figure 6B). Lamellarin α 20-sulfate was predicted to bind to a positively charged region of LASV GPC through electrostatic attraction (Figure 6C). Consistently, the K125E amino acid substitution, which markedly disrupts this positively charged patch, reduced the binding affinity of lamellarin α 20-sulfate to LASV GPC (Figure 6D). Together with the experimental data shown in Figure 6A, our in silico data suggest that the amino acid residue 125 of GPC plays a critical role in regulating the electrostatic environment of the putative lamellarin α 20-sulfate binding pocket, and that the K125E substitution decreases lamellarin α 20-sulfate-susceptibility and binding affinity by reducing electrostatic attraction between GPC and the compound.

2.7. Isolation of LASV GPC Variants Resistant to a Human Neutralizing Antibody

We attempted to isolate LASV GPC variants resistant to a commercially available human neutralizing antibody 37.7H derived from a Lassa fever survivor [13,28,29]. Human 293T cells were transfected with the MLV Gag-Pol expression plasmid and pMX-LASV GPC, and the resulting culture supernatant was inoculated into Gag-Pol-expressing TE671 cells in the presence of the neutralizing antibody (0.25 μg/mL) (Figure 7A). Three days post-inoculation, the cells were treated with puromycin. When puromycin-resistant cells reached confluence, their culture supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm filter, and inoculated into fresh Gag-Pol-expressing cells in the presence of the neutralizing antibody (1 μg/mL). The inoculated cells were subjected to the same treatment protocol. The culture supernatant from the resulting puromycin-resistant cells was again filtered and inoculated into fresh Gag-Pol-expressing cells in the presence of the neutralizing antibody (2 μg/mL). After puromycin selection, RNA was extracted from the puromycin-resistant cells. LASV GPC-encoding region was amplified by RT-PCR, and 11 GPC variant expression plasmids were constructed from the PCR product.

Nucleotide sequences of the GPC variants were determined (Figure S12). All variants contained nucleotide changes, with a total of 28 nucleotide changes identified, 21 of which resulted in amino acid replacements (Figure S13). The relatively high ratio of nonsynonymous to total nucleotide changes suggests strong selection pressure on the LASV GPC sequence by the neutralizing antibody (Figure S14). Similar to the result obtained under lamellarin α 20-sulfate selection, G-to-A substitutions were the most frequent, implying a possible contribution of APOBEC3G to the observed mutations [22,23] (Figure S15). Among the variants, NA2 and NA9 carried the C231Y and I286T amino acid substitutions (Figure 7B and Figure S13). NA8 and NA11 shared identical amino acid sequences, harboring T281A and I403T substitutions. For subsequent analyses, therefore, variants NA2, NA3, NA5, NA8, NA10, NA108, NA112, NA125, and NA130 were used. Notably, NA125 contained the I403T substitution, but lacked T281A. In addition, NA5, NA10, and NA130 each contained the G208D substitution.

To evaluate the infection efficiency conferred by each variant, 293T cells were transfected with the Gag-Pol expression plasmid, luciferase-encoding MLV vector genome expression plasmid, and each GPC variant expression plasmid. Culture supernatants from the transfected cells were then inoculated into normal TE671 cells, and luciferase activity in the cell lysates was measured. Luciferase activity in cells inoculated with the NA2, NA8, NA108, or NA130 variant-containing MLV vector was comparable to that observed in cells inoculated with a luciferase-encoding MLV vector lacking viral glycoproteins (control vector) (Figure 7C). In contrast, luciferase activity in cells inoculated with NA3, NA5, NA10, NA112, or NA125 vector was significantly higher than that of the control. Since both NA5 and NA10, which harbor the G208D substitution, supported transduction, the additional S56G substitution is likely responsible for the loss of infectivity observed in NA130. Furthermore, because the NA125 variant containing I403T substitution alone was able to mediate transduction, the T281A substitution appears to be the determinant for the impaired infectivity of NA8 and NA11.

The impact of the neutralizing antibody on infection mediated by NA3, NA5, NA10, NA112, and NA125 variant GPC-carrying vectors was examined. Luciferase activity in cells inoculated with the Wt, NA3, NA5, NA10, and NA112 vectors was markedly reduced in the presence of the neutralizing antibody (1 μg/mL), whereas luciferase activity in cells inoculated with the NA125 vector remained unaffected (Figure 7D). These findings demonstrate that the NA125 variant is resistant to the neutralizing antibody. Since NA125 harbors a single amino acid substitution, I403T, this mutation is responsible for the observed resistance.

To assess the protein expression and processing of LASV GPC variants, Western blotting was performed using lysates from transfected cells (Figure 7E). In the NA2 variant, a higher level of precursor protein was detected, whereas mature GP1 protein was not detected, indicating that impaired GPC processing accounts for its lack of infectivity. In NA8 and NA130, precursor levels were reduced, and mature GP1 was undetectable, suggesting that insufficient GPC expression and defective processing underlie their failure to mediate infection. For NA5, which supported vector infection, precursor GPC levels were comparable to those of Wt and mature GP1 level was higher. NA112 also mediated luciferase gene transduction, although the levels of precursor GPC and mature GP1 were both lower than Wt. In contrast, mature GP1 protein was barely detectable in NA3 and NA125, consistent with their reduced infection efficiency relative to Wt. According to higher levels of precursor and GP1 expression compared to Wt, NA10 mediated higher transduction efficiency.

2.8. I403T Impairs Interaction Between the Neutralizing Antibody and LASV GPC

To elucidate the mechanism by which the I403T mutation confers resistance to the neutralizing antibody, an in silico analysis of the interaction between LASV GP2 and the antibody was performed. Using information on the reported X-ray crystal structure [28,29], a complex model of LASV GPC trimer and Fab fragment of antibody 37.7H used in this study was constructed using the homology modeling method. The complex model showed that the I403 residue is located at the binding interface of GP2 and antibody 37.7H (Figure 8A). In the binding interface, interactions between 37.7H heavy chain and GP2 alpha helix were identified at multiple sites, in which GP2 I403 residue is positioned next to E404 residue that directly forms hydrogen bond with S56 residue of the 37.7H heavy chain CDR2 (Figure 8B). Importantly, an in silico mutagenesis study predicted that the GP2 I403T mutation causes reduction in binding affinity to the Fab (Figure 8C, arrow). Thus, the I403T substitution rendered resistance to the neutralizing antibody, probably by disrupting antibody binding to the variant.

To confirm the above result, the Wt and I403T GP proteins were immunoprecipitated using the 37.7H neutralizing antibody, which binds to native LASV GP but not to the denatured protein [29]. The precipitates were then analyzed by Western blotting using the anti-GP1 antibody. Direct Western blotting of cell lysates from transfected 293T cells revealed similar levels of the precursor GP for both the Wt and I403T mutant (Figure 8D), consistent with the above observation (Figure 7E). In contrast, Western blot analysis of the immunoprecipitates showed that the precursor level of the I403T mutant was significantly lower than that of the Wt GP. These results indicate that I403T substitution reduces the affinity between LASV GP and 37.7H neutralizing antibody, in agreement with the in silico analysis.

2.9. Noninfectious GPC Variants Are Involved in Resistance to Neutralizing Antibody

Among the 11 GPC variants isolated under neutralizing antibody selection, 6 variants failed to induce transduction. However, in the absence of inhibitors and in the presence of lamellarin α 20-sulfate, all variants were capable of conferring transduction to the MLV vector. This raises the question why noninfectious variants were present in the inoculated cells under neutralizing antibody pressure. It is possible that these noninfectious variants play a role in conferring resistance to the antibody.

To test this hypothesis, 293T cells were transfected with the Gag-Pol expression plasmid and the luciferase-encoding MLV vector genome expression plasmid together with the pcDNA3.1, Wt LASV GPC, or the noninfectious GPC variant NA2 in various combinations and at different ratios. Because the nucleotide sequence of NA2 was identical to that of NA9, these variants were likely selected under the neutralizing antibody pressure. Therefore, NA2 was used in this experiment.

The resulting culture supernatants were inoculated into normal TE671 cells in the absence or presence of the neutralizing antibody (0.25 μg/mL). Transduction by MLV vectors produced from cells transfected with Wt GPC expression plasmid alone, or with the Wt GPC expression plasmid plus pcDNA3.1, was reduced by the antibody (Figure 9A). Luciferase activity in cells transduced with MLV vectors produced from cells co-expressing Wt and NA2 GPC was lower than that of vectors carrying Wt GPC alone, but consistently higher than that of the control inoculation with MLV vector lacking viral glycoproteins. Notably, transduction by MLV vector carrying both Wt and NA2 GPC was not inhibited by the antibody. These findings indicate that noninfectious GPC variants can contribute to resistance against neutralizing antibody.

To assess the expression of LASV GPC, Western blotting was performed using lysates from the transfected cells. When 293T cells were co-transfected with 0.25 μg of the Wt GPC expression plasmid and 0.75 μg of pcDNA3.1, the precursor GPC level was decreased (Figure 9B). In contrast, when 293T cells were co-transfected with 0.25 μg of the Wt GPC and 0.75 μg of the NA2 variant expression plasmids, comparable levels of the precursor were detected, as expected. These results demonstrate that the noninfectious NA2 variant competes with Wt GPC for binding to the neutralizing antibody, thereby diminishing its neutralization activity.

3. Discussion

The conditional replication system using the LASV GPC-expressing MLV vector was successfully established, and LASV GPC variants resistant to lamellarin α 20-sulfate and the 37.7H neutralizing antibody were isolated. Because this system replicates exclusively in MLV Gag-Pol-expressing cells, it can be safely operated under BSL-2 conditions. This study provides the first demonstration of conditional replication using a pseudotyped MLV vector.

Our conditional replication system is safer than other replication-competent recombinant viruses containing LASV GPC. When the glycoprotein-coding region of the VSV genome is replaced with the LASV GPC sequence, the resulting chimeric virus can replicate in normal cells [30,31]. In addition, the ML29 reassortant virus, which contains the L segment of the Mopea viral genome and S segment of the LASV genome, can also replicate in normal cells [32,33]. These engineered viruses expressing LASV GPC can be utilized to isolate variants resistant to inhibitors. However, the conditional replication system using LASV GPC-encoding MLV vector developed by our research group provides a much more safety than these two chimeric viruses, because replication of our system is strictly restricted to MLV Gag-Pol-expressing cells, whereas the others can replicate in almost all cell types. Thus, our system represents a uniquely safe and practical platform for studying LASV GPC and isolating variants resistant to GPC-targeting inhibitors under BSL-2 conditions.

Carr et al. developed an experimental system to isolate inhibitor-resistant variants using a LASV GPC mutant library containing comprehensive substitutions at each amino acid residue within a replication-defective HIV-1 vector [13]. However, because viral replication does not occur in their system, it is difficult to identify complementing mutations like H13R. In contrast, our conditional replication system using the LASV GPC-expressing MLV vector overcomes this limitation, providing a clear advantage for detecting such compensatory mutations.

The expression of MLV Env protein from the MLV vector used in this study appears to require additional factors. While the conditional replication system of the LASV GPC-encoding MLV vector was successfully constructed, that of the MLV Env-encoding vector was not. Specifically, MLV Env protein was not detected in cells transfected with the MLV Env-encoding MLV vector genome expression plasmid (pMX-MLV Env). It is known that splicing is required for Env protein expression [34,35]. In the present plasmid, MLV Env protein was not expressed due to the absence of appropriate splicing donor and acceptor sites. In contrast, the expression of LASV GPC may not depend on splicing.

The K125E amino acid substitution dramatically enhanced infectivity and reduced sensitivity to lamellarin α 20-sulfate compared to the Wt GPC, consistent with the in silico analysis. In silico studies revealed the role of the K125 residue in regulating the GPC binding ability to the authentic receptor of LASV, α-DG, consistent with a previous study [36]. However, the in silico mutagenesis analysis, in combination with the experimental results, strongly suggested that the critical infection step for the marked enhancement in the viral infectivity by the K125E substitution does not reside in the step of GP1 binding to the α-DG receptor. A possible explanation for the finding may be the presence of an alternative receptor for the viral entry, or a coreceptor that stabilizes the GP1-α-DG complex. For example, the K125E substitution, which markedly alters the electrostatic property around the receptor binding pocket, could have shifted the receptor tropism of LASV GPC. In this regard, several studies have shown that cellular factors other than α-DG can serve as LASV receptors [37,38,39,40,41,42]. An alternative possibility is that the K125E substitution enhances other steps of the replication cycle. For example, the extensive electrostatic changes on the GP1 surface caused by the K125E substitution might influence the pH-dependent structural change in GPC and thereby enhance the membrane-fusion step in the cells after endocytosis of virions. Further study is necessary to address each of the issues.

It is noteworthy that although the H13R substitution in the SSP itself did not account for the enhanced infectivity, it was essential for the LASV K125E substitution to gain viral competence of maintaining continuous replication in cells. Our experimental data suggest that the H13R substitution relieves the K125E-substitution-induced negative effect on cell viability. In this regard, it should be noted that the electrostatic interactions between SSP, GP1, and GP2 play a key role in the pH-dependent conformational changes in GPC for membrane fusion and thus infectivity of arenaviruses including LASV [43,44,45,46,47]. It is conceivable that the H13R substitution influences the electrostatic interactions, because the guanidinium group of arginine remains positively charged over a wide pH range, and therefore arginine has a greater influence on the electrostatic interactions than histidine does.

Notably, the LASV SSP H13 residue is positioned near the GP1 and GP2 domains within the folded GPC (Figure S16). Thus, it is possible that the H13R substitution influences the networks of electrostatic interactions around the interfaces of SSP, GP1, and GP2, thereby indirectly altering the balance between GPC stability, membrane fusion, and viral infectivity [43,44,45,46,47]. Consistently, our study indicates that the H13R substitution alone can induce mild destabilization of WT GPC and concomitantly reduce viral infectivity. Intriguingly, the H13R substitution reverted the destabilization of the K125E GPC and simultaneously the defectiveness of the K125E variant. These results suggest that not only local but also overall electrostatic environment of GPC surface is important for maintaining the vital balance between GPC stability and viral infectivity.

Together with the results of the selection experiments with lamellarin α 20-sulfate, our experimental and in silico site-directed-mutagenesis studies strongly suggest a survival strategy of LASV under the selective pressure of the anti-LASV compound; both GP1 K125E and SSP H13R residues likely played a key role in viral adaptation under the anti-LASV compound, where virus had needed to gain the K125E to evade the antiviral compound and concomitantly the H13R to eliminate the lethal effect of the K125E.

The I403T substitution is responsible for resistance to the 37.7H neutralizing antibody, consistent with a previous report [13]. LASV GPC variants resistant to 37.7H antibody have already been isolated in a study using a GPC mutant library containing comprehensive substitutions at each amino acid residue within a replication-defective HIV-1 vector. Substitutions at positions 398 to 404 confer resistance to the neutralizing antibody by disrupting the GP2-antibody interaction [13]. These findings demonstrate that our conditional replication system using the LASV GPC-expressing MLV vector provides a robust platform for identifying and characterizing resistance-associated mutations.

Noninfectious LASV GPC variants were selected under neutralizing antibody pressure. These noninfectious variants compete with infectious GPC for antibody binding, thereby diminishing the neutralizing activity of the antibody. This phenomenon arises from MLV vector particles that simultaneously package both a noninfectious variant-encoding genome and an infectious GPC-encoding genome (Figure S17). Cells infected with such MLV vectors are expected to produce three types of progeny particles: those containing only infectious GPC-encoding genomes, only noninfectious GPC-encoding genomes, or a mixture of both. MLV vector particles derived from cells expressing only noninfectious GPC-encoding genomes cannot transduce target cells. Particles produced from cells expressing only infectious GPC-encoding genomes are efficiently neutralized by the antibody. In contrast, the inhibitory effect of the neutralizing antibody on transduction by particles produced from cells expressing both infectious and noninfectious GPC is diminished. As a result, noninfectious GPC-encoding genomes persist during repeated inoculation in the presence of the neutralizing antibody. Because MLV-based vectors contain two genome copies per particle, they can exploit this mechanism to resist neutralizing antibodies. In contrast, LASV particles, which contain only a single genome encoding GPC, cannot utilize this mechanism to evade antibody-mediated neutralization.

The total GPC protein levels of several variants isolated in this study were lower than those of the Wt. The amounts of mature GP1 in L5, L6, L7, L15, NA3, NA10, NA112, and NA125 were reduced compared with the Wt, suggesting impaired GPC processing. However, the precursor protein did not accumulate. These findings suggest two possible mechanisms: (i) the variant proteins may be more prone to degradation, and (ii) the nucleotide change—not the amino acid substitutions—may diminish the expression levels of these clones.

Our conditional replication system is useful for elucidating molecular mechanisms underlying resistance to GPC-targeting inhibitors. First, we showed that the H13R substitution at the SSP of LASV GPC and the K125E substitution at GP1 need to cooperatively function to generate a viral phenotype to grow in the presence of lamellarin α 20-sulfate, and that the K125E plays a key role in increasing viral infectivity. Secondly, we showed that I403T substitution in GP2 is sufficient to confer a viral phenotype to grow in the presence of the neutralizing monoclonal antibody 37.7H. Thus, our conditional replication system using LASV GPC-expressing MLV vector is helpful in identifying GPC mutations that are responsible for the variant replicable in the presence of anti-LASV GPC agents and thereby elucidating further their role in the resistance.

4. Materials and Methods

4.1. Cells

Human 293T and TE671 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) (FUJIFILM Wako, Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 8% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (CAPRICORN Scientific, Ebsdorfergrund, Germany), 100 unit/mL penicillin (FUJIFILM Wako), and 100 μg/mL Streptomycin (FUJIFILM Wako) at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Human 293T and TE671 cells were purchased from ATCC and have been maintained in our laboratory for over 20 years. TE671 cells stably expressing MLV Gag-Pol protein were kindly provided by Dr. F. Cossett [17]. For maintenance, cells in 6 cm dishes were washed with 2 mL PBS, detached from culture dishes by 0.25% trypsin treatment (1 mL) (FUJIFILM Wako), and 1/10 of the total cell suspension (0.3 mL) was passaged into new 6 cm dishes every three days.

4.2. Plasmids

A humanized sequence encoding the GPC of the Josiah strain (AAA46283.1) [48] was synthesized (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and subcloned into the pTargeT vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), as the humanized GPC induces more efficient infection [19]. The resulting plasmid was digested with BamHI and NotI (TaKaRa Bio, Otsu, Japan), and the LASV GPC sequence-containing fragment was purified using QIAEX II Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen Hilden, Germany) following 1%-agarose gel electrophoresis (Advance, Tokyo, Japan). The purified fragment was then ligated into the pMX-puro plasmid vector [18] using T4 DNA ligase (TaKaRa Bio, Otsu, Japan) at room temperature overnight to construct the LASV GPC-encoding MLV vector genome expression plasmid (pMX-LASV GPC). E. coli HB101 cells (TaKaRa Bio) were transformed with the ligation mixture, and plated onto LB agar plates (FUJIFILM Wako) containing ampicillin (100 μg/μL) (FUJIFILM Wako). After overnight incubation at 37 °C, 12 colonies were cultured in 2 mL LB medium containing ampicillin. Plasmid DNA was purified from each colony using the QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (Qiagen), and E. coli cells harboring plasmids containing the LASV GPC sequence were identified by restriction enzyme analysis. E. coli cells carrying the LASV GPC-containing plasmid were then cultured in 50 mL of LB medium containing ampicillin, and plasmid DNA was purified using the NucleoBond Xtra Midi Kit (TaKaRa Bio). The same procedure was applied to purify other plasmids. Consequently, the LASV GPC sequence was flanked by pTargeT-derived sequences in pMX-LASV GPC. The MLV Gag-Pol expression plasmid was purchased from TaKaRa Bio.

Total RNA was extracted from MLV Gag-Pol-expressing cells inoculated with the LASV GPC-encoding MLV vector in 6 cm-dishes using 1 mL of TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 0.2 mL of chloroform (FUJIFILM Wako). Following vigorous mixing, the sample was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 1 min (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). The upper aqueous phase was transferred to a new microtube, and 2-volume of ethanol (FUJIFILM Wako) were added. After centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 10 min, the RNA pellet was dissolved in DEPC-treated water (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Total RNA (500 ng) was subjected to reverse transcription using the reverse transcriptase (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) to synthesize cDNA. Using this cDNA as a template, PCR was performed with LA Taq DNA polymerase (TaKaRa Bio) to amplify the LASV GPC sequence. The enzyme possesses proofreading activity, thereby ensuring high-fidelity amplification. The nucleotide sequences of the PCR primers are 5′-TTG CTA GCC TCG AGA CGC GTG-3′ and 5′-GCA GTC GAC GGT ACC CCC GGG-3′. These primers annealed to the pTargeT-derived sequences flanking the LASV GPC-encoding region in the MLV vector genome. The resulting PCR product was ligated into the pTargeT plasmid vector using the ligase provided in the pTargeT Mammalian Expression Plasmid Kit (Promega), and the BamHI-NotI fragment was subsequently subcloned into the pMX-puro plasmid vector as described above.

The Renilla luciferase-encoding MLV vector genome expression plasmid was constructed as follows: A Renilla luciferase expression plasmid was purchased from Promega, and the luciferase coding sequence was amplified by PCR. The PCR product was ligated into the pTargeT vector. The resulting plasmid was digested with BamHI and NotI, and the fragment was subcloned into the pMX-puro vector.

An expression plasmid encoding nano luciferase N-terminally fused to the IL-6 signal peptide was purchased from Promega.

4.3. Site-Directed Mutagenesis

Since the H13 residue is located in the N-terminal region of the LASV GPC coding sequence, the H13R mutant was constructed by PCR using primers, one of which contains a nucleotide substitution introducing the H13R mutation. The nucleotide sequences of the PCR primers were 5′-ATG GGG CAG ATC GTG ACC TTC TTT CAG GAA GTG CCA CGC GTA ATC GAA GAG GTG ATG AAC-3′ and 5′-TCA CCG CTT CCA CTT CAC AGG-3′ (synthesized by Sigma-Aldrich). The underlined nucleotide represents the substitution induces the H13R mutation. The PCR product was subcloned to pTargeT plasmid.

To construct the K125E mutant, 5′-phosphorylated PCR primers were synthesized (Sigma-Aldrich). Their nucleotide sequences were 5′-GGC GAA TTC GGG TCC TTG CTA GCC-3′ and 5′-CAG GTT CTT CTC ATG GGC ATC GCT-3′. The first primer introduced a nucleotide change that destroys the unique BamHI site of the pTargeT plasmid (underlined), while the second primer contained a nucleotide substitution resulting in the K125E mutation. The region between the two primers was first amplified by PCR, and the amplified fragment subsequently functioned as primers to amplify the entire plasmid in the same reaction. The PCR product was purified using QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen), and the purified DNA fragment was digested with BamHI to eliminate the original plasmid, and was then transformed into E. coli HB101 competent cells (TaKaRa Bio). The presence of the mutation was confirmed by sequencing using BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle sequencing Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

4.4. Western Blotting

Cell lysates were prepared from cells cultured in 6 cm dishes using Glo Lysis Buffer (200 μL) (Promega). Protein concentrations were measured by UV absorbance (Eppendorf), and equal amounts of protein from each sample were subjected to SDS-PAGE (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). The membrane was then blocked with 10% skimmed milk (BD Difco, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). To detect the LASV GPC, the membrane was incubated with a 1/500-diluted mouse anti-LASV GP1 monoclonal antibody EBS-I-301 (PROGEN, Heidelberg, Germany) followed by 1/1000-diluted HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG antibody (Bio-Rad). For detection of actin protein as a loading control, the membrane was incubated with a 1/2000-diluted mouse anti-actin antibody sc-47778 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), followed by 1/1000-diluted HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG antibody. Finally, antibody-bound proteins were visualized using an HRP substrate (Bio-Rad) by C-DiGit scanner (LI-COR, Lincoln, NB, USA). The intensities of GPC precursor, GP1, and actin protein bands were measured by its software. Normalized precursor and GP1 levels were calculated.

4.5. Sequencing

Nucleotide sequencing was performed using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the sequencing products were purified using the DyeEx 2.0 Spin Kit (Qiagen). Nucleotide sequences were determined using an automated DNA sequencer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA).

4.6. Conditional Replication System of LASV GPC-Encoding MLV Vector

The MLV Gag-Pol expression plasmid (1 μg) and the LASV GPC-encoding MLV vector genome expression plasmid (1 μg) were mixed with 5 μL of FuGENE transfection reagent (Promega) in a total volume of 100 μL DMEM without FBS and antibiotics. The mixture was added to 293T cells cultured in 6 cm dishes 2 days after passage and cultured for 1 day. The culture supernatant of the transfected cells was replaced with fresh medium, and culture was continued for an additional day. The culture supernatant from the transfected cells was filtered through a 0.45-μm filter (Millipore) to remove residual cells and inoculated into Gag-Pol-expressing TE671 cells in 6 cm dishes in the presence of lamellarin α 20-sulfate or the 37.7H neutralizing antibody (Absolute Antibody, Oxford, UK). Lamellarin α 20-sulfate was synthesized in our laboratory [20,21]. Two days after inoculation, all inoculated cells were transferred to 10 cm dishes. When the cells reached confluence, puromycin was added at a final concentration of 2 μg/mL (Sigma-Aldrich). After the puromycin-resistant cells reached confluence, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium without puromycin, and the cells were cultured for an additional day. The culture supernatant from the puromycin-resistant cells was reinoculated into fresh Gag-Pol-expressing cells in the presence of each inhibitor in 6 cm dishes. The inoculation procedure was repeated three times.

4.7. Renilla Luciferase-Encoding, Replication-Defective MLV Vector

The MLV Gag-Pol expression plasmid (1 μg), the pTargeT-based LASV GPC expression plasmid (1 μg), and the Renilla luciferase-encoding MLV vector genome expression plasmid (1 μg) were mixed with 5 μL of Fugene transfection reagent (Promega) in a total volume of 100 μL DMEM buffer. The mixture was added to 293T cells cultured in 6 cm dishes 2 days after passage and cultured for 1 day. The culture supernatant of the transfected cells was replaced with fresh medium, and culture was continued for an additional day. The culture supernatant from the transfected cells was then inoculated into normal TE671 cells in the presence or absence of lamellarin α 20-sulfate or the 37.7H neutralizing antibody. As a control, normal TE671 cells were inoculated with culture supernatant from 293T cells transfected with the Gag-Pol expression plasmid and luciferase-encoding MLV vector genome expression plasmid without the viral glycoprotein expression plasmid. Luciferase activity in cell lysates was measured using Renilla Luciferase Assay System (Promega) 2 days after inoculation to evaluate transduction efficiency.

4.8. HIV-1 Vector Binding Assay

Human 293T cells were transfected with the HIV-1 Gag-Pol-Tat-Rev expression plasmid (1 μg) [49] and the LacZ-encoding HIV-1 vector genome expression plasmid (1 μg) [50], together with either the LASV GPC Wt or K125E mutant expression plasmid (1 μg) in 6 cm dishes. One day after the transfection, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium (2 mL), and the cells were further cultured for 1 day. The amount of HIV-1 p24 in the culture supernatants was quantified by ELISA (ZeptoMetrix, Buffalo, NY, USA), and equal amounts of p24-containing supernatants were used to inoculate TE671 cells. The inoculated cells were cultured for an additional 2 days and stained with X-Gal (FUJIFILM Wako). The number of blue-stained cells was counted to estimate transduction titers.

For binding assays, target TE671 cells were incubated with equal amounts of LASV GPC-pseudotyped HIV-1 vector at 4 °C for 2 h. After washing 5 times with PBS, cell lysates were prepared, and the amounts of HIV-1 p24 in the lysates were quantified by ELISA (ZeptoMetrix).

4.9. In Silico Analysis of the LASV Glycoprotein Complex with α-Dystroglycan

A structural model of the glycoprotein complex (GPC) of LASV was constructed by the AlphaFold 3 program [51] using the reported amino acid sequence of the LASV (AAA46283.1) [48]. The obtained LASV GPC trimer model gave a predicted TM-score (pTM) of 0.81, which is in the “Confident” range. A glycosylated LASV GPC trimer model was constructed from the reported glycosylated arenavirus GPC structure (PDB code: 8EJD) [52] and AlphaFold 3-predicted GPC model using the “Structure Superposition” tool in the Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) (Chemical Computing Group, Montreal, Quebec, Canada). A glycosylated LASV GPC model binding α-dystroglycan (α-DG) was constructed from the reported GPC structure (PDB code: 7PVD) [53] using MOE. The LASV GPC and α-DG complex model was used for site-directed mutagenesis of the lamellarin α 20-sulfate-resistant variants, K125E and H13R amino acid substitutions. Modeling of the GPC mutants and calculation of changes in the GPC and α-DG complex stability and binding affinity to α-DG by the mutations were done using the Protein Design tool of MOE, along with the MM/GBVI program to calculate the binding affinity as described previously [54]. The results were expressed by the change in the free energy of binding (ΔΔG). The energy change values in the results represent the numerical values of the relative binding affinities between the K125E and H13R mutants and the wild type (Wt) of GPC. When the change in binding free energy upon mutation (ΔΔG) is positive (ΔΔG > 0), the mutation destabilizes the LASV-GPC–α-DG interaction, resulting in reduced binding affinity. Conversely, a negative ΔΔG (ΔΔG < 0) indicates that the mutation stabilizes the interaction and increases binding affinity.

The structural properties of α-DG binding areas on the LASV GP1 were evaluated using the “Protein Patch Analyzer” tool in MOE as previously described [24]. A trimer model of the LASV GPC with or without the K125E mutation was used for the patch analyses. Briefly, the Protein patch Analyzer tool was applied to search for the electrostatic patches (a minimal patch area of 40 Å2 in size) that were potentially involved in interaction with negatively charged glycan.

4.10. In Silico Analysis of the LASV GPC Bound to Neutralizing Antibody 37.7H

A three-dimensional model of the LASV GPC and antibody 37.7H Fab fragment was constructed by the homology modeling method using the reported amino acid sequence of LASV GPC (AAA46283.1) [48] and the reported LASV GPC and 37.7H complex structure (PDB code: 5VK2) [55]. The CDR boundaries were defined using IMGT annotation tool within MOE. Using the same method as above, LASV GPC models harboring the 37.7H-resistant I403 mutations were constructed using MOE, and the change in GPC binding affinity for the 37.7H antibody was calculated. Modeling of the GPC mutants and calculation of changes in binding affinity to 37.7H antibody by the I403 mutants were performed using the Protein Design tool of MOE, along with the MM/GBVI program, as described previously [54]. The results were expressed by the change in the free energy of binding (ΔΔG). The energy change values in the results represent the numerical values of the relative binding affinities between each mutant and the wild-type GPC. More negative values indicate mutations with higher affinity.

4.11. Docking Simulation Between LASV GPC and Lamellarin α 20-Sulfate

Physicochemically and thermodynamically possible binding modes of lamellarin α 20-sulfate to the LASV GPC model were assessed using the Docking application of MOE. The structure of lamellarin α 20-sulfate has been reported in the previous study [20,21]. The structures of the full-length GPC and lamellarin α 20-sulfate were defined as the receptor and ligand, respectively. Subsequently, docking simulations were performed to comprehensively search possible binding poses, and the top 100 poses were generated. These poses were ranked based on docking scores using the Dock tool in MOE [56], which assesses the steric and thermodynamic validity of each binding pose. In addition, the LASV GPC-lamellarin α 20-sulfate complex model was used for site-directed mutagenesis analyses of lamellarin α 20-sulfate-resistant variant carrying the K125E and H13R amino acid substitutions. Modeling of the GPC mutants was generated, and changes in binding affinity to lamellarin α 20-sulfate were calculated. Modeling of the GPC mutants and calculation of changes in binding affinity to lamellarin 20-sulfate by the K125E and H13R mutants were performed using the Protein Design tool of MOE, along with the MM/GBVI program, as described previously [54]. The results were expressed by the change in the free energy of binding (ΔΔG). The energy change values in the results indicate the numerical values of the relative binding affinities between the mutant and Wt GPC. More negative values indicate mutations with higher affinity.

4.12. Immunoprecipitation

Human 293T cells were transfected with either the Wt or I403T GPC expression plasmid (1 μg) in 6 cm dishes. Cell lysates were prepared using 500 μL of immunoprecipitation (IP) buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The human 37.7H neutralizing antibody (4 μL) was added to each lysate, and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h. Protein G-Sepharose bead solution (50 μL) (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) was then added, followed by incubation at 4 °C for 2 h. The beads were washed five times with 500 μL of IP buffer, and 30 μL of SDS sample buffer (Bio-Rad) was added. After heating at 95 °C for 3 min, the samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE.

4.13. Viability of LASV GP-Expressing Cells

TE671 cells were transfected in 6 cm dishes with each LASV GP expression plasmid (1 μg) together with a plasmid encoding nano luciferase fused to the IL-6 signal peptide (Promega) (1 μg) using the Fugene transfection reagent (5 μL) (Promega). The culture medium was replaced with fresh medium 24 h after transfection. Luciferase activity in the culture supernatants was measured 2 days after transfection. A reduction in luciferase activity is interpreted as an indication of LASV GP-induced cytotoxicity or suppression of cell growth.

4.14. Statistical Analysis

Significant differences between two groups were evaluated using Student’s t-test. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. These statistical analyses were performed using Excel software version 16.106.3.

5. Conclusions

The conditional replication system using the LASV GPC-encoding MLV-based vector was successfully established in this study. This system represents a practical tool for isolating LASV GP variants resistant to GPC-targeting inhibitors. In addition, the system should be useful to elucidate molecular mechanisms underlying the resistance. We are currently extending this approach to construct conditional replication systems for other high-risk viruses including Ebola virus, thereby providing a versatile platform for studying BSL4 pathogens under lower biosafety conditions.
