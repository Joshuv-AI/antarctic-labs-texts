Making WHO’s work relevant to the Member States in SEA region
Authors: Anand Krishnan
Published: 2023

The World Health Organization (WHO) has constantly faced existential questions. While its constitution defines many functions, the primary one is ‘to act as the directing and co-ordinating authority on international health work’. The flagship initiative by WHO SEARO seems a good fit to this mandate. For these flagship areas, WHO managed to elicit high degree of political commitment through high-level and inter-ministerial meetings. A unique feature of WHO among UN agencies is the establishment of regional organizations. This effort reflects the ability to harness the autonomous nature of the regional office. These flagship areas make the most use of WHO’s core competencies, provide significant value addition and enhance its prestige in the global health space. These flagship programmes, modelled on Roll Back Malaria and the Tobacco Free Initiative of Dr Gro Harlem Brundtland in mid-nineties, under whom the current Regional Director was schooled, reflect a clear prioritization and bring a much-needed focus to the WHO’s work and leverage its leadership position and the convening and co-ordinating power. WHO works closely with Member States responding to their needs and has been criticized for spreading itself too thin to make a real impact on the ground. This approach tries to counter that criticism.

Evaluating such efforts are always challenging, given that it is difficult to know where WHO’s efforts end and where national efforts begin. While WHO facilitates many national initiatives, the credit of implementing them justifiably goes to the Member States. This is both WHO’s strength and its weakness. While responding to the country’ voiced needs should be given due importance, an initiative of this nature also ensures that important public health priorities are addressed effectively. A full evaluation of such an initiative requires a close look at its funding mechanisms, reorganization, or redistribution of WHO resources including human and feedback from the Member States and other levels of WHO. What are the implications of such an approach for future Regional Directors in SEARO or for other regions? Have these multi-country initiatives established robust platforms that can deliver in future despite changing priorities globally and regionally or can these be reshaped to address the newer challenges?

While most flagship areas would choose themselves, the AMR Flagship is interesting in the sense that at the time of its inception, this was neither a global priority nor of Member States. The ability of the Regional Office to identify it and successfully advocate it speaks for its credibility. At the opposite end, the UHC experiment provides an example of limitation of this approach where WHO can do little except to nudge countries. While we do see many initiatives in Member States to achieve UHC, the contribution of the flagship to these initiatives is not easy to quantify. Despite the flagship, public health expenditures remain low in the region.

Another aspect that these flagships have highlighted is the capacity of WHO to facilitate, support and conduct monitoring and surveillance as well as certification of elimination of diseases. It does this by developing frameworks, sharing tools, building capacity, and synthesizing the data. The establishment and administration of the SEAR Health Relief Fund provides a remarkable example of the non-partisan ability of WHO to gather and manage funds to address critical needs of Member States. However, the health emergencies flagship did not facilitate a better response to the pandemic which raises question of these flagship platforms being able to respond effectively to a future threat.

However, reading these set of papers one gets a feeling that the adoption of the flagship approach has made the WHO’s work more relevant to the Member States. They demonstrate the possibilities given the right choice of the priority areas and a smart strategy to deliver them effectively.
