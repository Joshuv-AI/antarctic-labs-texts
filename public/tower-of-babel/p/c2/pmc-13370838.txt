A Fidelity Assessment of Early Psychosis Intervention Services in British Columbia, Canada
Authors: S Riedlinger, T Ehmann, S Tamburri, D J Lang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionEstablished standards and guidelines for early psychosis intervention (EPI) in British Columbia (BC) and across Canada define the intent and scope of individual EPI programs. EPI programs are a critical mental health service both in BC and internationally, providing a range of pharmacological, psychological, and social supports under one service. If EPI principles and practices are applied, positive outcomes are expected, including greater reduction of psychotic symptoms compared to standard treatment, improved treatment adherence, fewer recurrent episodes of psychosis, and greater client satisfaction. Low fidelity, or difficulty adhering to the principles associated with the EPI model, may jeopardize attaining these positive outcomes.MethodsEvaluation of program fidelity to established EPI practice standards and guidelines was conducted at 16 mental health service sites in BC that provide care to young adults with early psychosis. Fidelity assessment was performed via utilization of the EPI Fidelity Scale (EPI‐FS).ResultsBased on the EPI‐FS, two sites (12.5%) met criteria for high fidelity, nine (56.3%) attained moderate fidelity, three (18.8%) attained low fidelity, and two (12.5%) attained very low fidelity. Specialized EPI programs were linked to higher fidelity, while many generic mental health and substance use centres had difficulty following the EPI model. Identified weaknesses included provision of psychological therapy, family education, ongoing program evaluation, and supervision and mentorship for clinicians. Sites with sufficient resources and staff development opportunities had higher overall fidelity.ConclusionThese findings underscore the importance of equitable resource allocation and the need for specialized EPI services, especially in underserved areas in BC.Keywords: early psychosis intervention, fidelity assessment, fidelity scale, first episode psychosis, program evaluation

Introduction

Early Psychosis Intervention (EPI) is a team‐based model of care that is optimized to provide timely recognition and assessment, comprehensive interventions, and wide‐ranging supports that improve individual functioning and autonomy for clients early in the course of a psychotic illness (Durbin et al. 2019; McGorry et al. 2008). Early intervention is known to improve long and short‐term clinical outcomes, reduce costs to the medical system, improve functioning, decrease secondary morbidities, and restore young people to the appropriate developmental stage disrupted by the onset of psychosis (Bertelsen et al. 2008; Bird et al. 2010; Correll et al. 2018; Hastrup et al. 2013; Kane et al. 2016; Mihalopoulos et al. 2009; Petersen et al. 2005). Despite empirical support for early psychosis interventions, services often lack optimal breadth, intensity, or quality of interventions (McGorry et al. 2018; O'Connell et al. 2021; Powell et al. 2021). As a result, fidelity scales have been developed worldwide to assess adherence of early intervention services to the core components and principles of the EPI model of care, including the First Episode Psychosis Services Fidelity Scale (FEPS‐FS; Addington et al. 2016), revised to the FEPS‐FS 1.0 (Addington 2021), the Early Assessment and Support Alliance (EASA) fidelity scale (Melton et al. 2012), the NAVIGATE Fidelity Index (Mueser et al. 2019), the Danish Specialized Early Intervention (SEI) fidelity scale (Melau et al. 2019; Nordentoft et al. 2015), and the Early Psychosis Prevention and Intervention Centre (EPPIC) Model Integrity Tool (EMIT; Hughes et al. 2014; Killackey et al. 2018). Measuring fidelity is important as optimal outcomes are achieved when programs have higher fidelity to the model of care (Addington et al. 2018; Essock et al. 2015; O'Connell et al. 2021; Rosenblatt et al. 2024). Additionally, it can provide accountability in allocation of resources, can inform the training and supervision of clinical staff, and can establish a framework for continuous improvement through identification of services' strengths and weaknesses.

EPI services were implemented across the province of British Columbia (BC), Canada (population 5.22 M), with the goal that everyone affected by a first episode psychosis would receive an equal chance of achieving maximal recovery by receiving optimal care (BC Stats 2022; Ehmann et al. 2010). The British Columbia Early Psychosis Intervention Advanced Practice Program (BC EPI APP) is a provincial committee of service provider stakeholders that was created by the BC Ministry of Health (MoH) to support EPI services after the BC Standards and Guidelines for Early Psychosis Intervention Programs were written in 2010 (Ehmann et al. 2010). Members include regional and governmental bodies, academics, families, alumni of EPI, NGOs, and contractors. The BC EPI APP provides standardized foundational training for clinicians, operates public websites, acts as a hub for cross‐regional communication and collaboration, and facilitates service development and uptake of optimal practices for EPI in the five health authorities in BC: Vancouver Coastal Health, Fraser Health, Island Health, Interior Health, and Northern Health. Drawn from internationally recognized best practices and the BC Standards and Guidelines for EPI Programs, these optimal practices include pharmacological interventions, psychosocial support, such as functional and vocational interventions, empirically supported therapies offered by qualified practitioners, and comprehensive education to clients, families, and the public. Despite the desirability of optimized EPI services, multiple barriers to provision of EPI were initially revealed in the original program evaluation cycle (Ehmann and Hanson 2016). In the intervening decade, these barriers have likely changed and evolved. Following infusion of funding from the BC MoH, the EPI Fidelity Scale (EPI‐FS) and Self‐Assessment Users' Guide (see Appendix A for a sample of the scale) was created by the BC EPI APP and tailored to the BC Standards and Guidelines for EPI Programs to conduct a more up to date fidelity assessment. Development of the scale is described in a separate document available on the epitrainingbc.ca website.

While many mental health service sites in BC provide treatment to young adults with first episode psychosis and report that they provide EPI care, only a subset of these sites are specialized EPI programs. At the time of this study, four were standalone EPI sites, providing specialized EPI care in their own mental health centre, and an additional four were semi‐standalone EPI sites, with a specialized EPI team within a larger, more generic mental health centre. The standard generic mental health and substance use (MHSU) centres throughout the province may have some staff with EPI expertise. This EPI fidelity assessment was conducted by the BC EPI APP to determine if mental health service sites in BC that provide care to young adults with early psychosis followed the practices outlined in the BC Standards and Guidelines for EPI Programs utilizing the EPI‐FS. It provided a baseline assessment of services before substantive additional funding for quality improvement was provided by the BC MoH.

Methods

Instrument

The EPI‐FS is a 28‐item instrument that assesses EPI practices and service operations (See Appendix A for a sample of the scale). The items are grouped into four sections: Recognition and Access (4 items), Assessment and Engagement (5 items), Interventions and Required Services (11 items), and Service Structure and Staff (8 items). Each item is anchored and scored from 0–3, with scores of 2–3 considered meeting fidelity. Thirteen of the 28 items required a certain number of subcomponents or “targets” to be met to be assigned a score for that individual item. Sixteen items for rural sites (catchment population < 100 000) and 17 items for urban sites (catchment population > 100 000) were designated as “essential” by the BC EPI APP, with services identifying as providing EPI care expected to show fidelity to all essential items. These items were derived from a separate initiative done in 2020 using a Delphi approach to create a consensus as to what activities are core to BC EPI programs. A tentative level of fidelity rating was developed using total scores to rank sites as having high fidelity (> 75% of the total maximum score), moderate fidelity (50%–74%), low fidelity (39%–49%), or very low fidelity (< 39%).

Program Assessment of EPI Fidelity

Twenty‐three mental health service sites across BC that provide treatment to individuals aged 18–30 years old with early psychosis were identified by the BC EPI APP as potential participants for this fidelity assessment. Senior management in each Health Authority was asked to facilitate participation and, in several cases, they declined to direct smaller generic services from participating. Subsequently, twenty mental health service sites were officially invited to participate in the fidelity assessment as a quality improvement process. The EPI‐FS was emailed to managers of each site to be completed by the manager and at least one other clinical staff through self‐assessment, interviews, and review of administrative records. Participating sites were instructed in how to use the self‐report EPI‐FS through the EPI‐FS Self‐Assessment Users' Guide, a virtual training session, and follow‐up meetings. Five items on the EPI‐FS required a review of 10 charts, following unbiased selection, of clients who had been in the service for at least 9 months. Most items required multiple sources of information to determine a score for the item. Data sources are listed in priority sequence on the EPI‐FS with the most important source underlined. Two or more clinical staff, including the clinical manager of the site, independently decided scores for each item and then together developed a consensus score.

Data Analysis

Three main EPI fidelity scores for each site were extracted from the EPI‐FS, including total score (0–84), number of items meeting fidelity (0–28), and number of essential items meeting fidelity (0–17). Total score was calculated from the sum of all 28 items, with each item scoring 0–3, for a maximum total score of 84. The total score percentage was calculated, and sites were ranked into a level of fidelity rating as described above. The mean of the three main scores across all sites was calculated, as were the percentage of sites meeting fidelity for each item. The statistical software program, Wizard 1.0, was used for all of the following analyses, including examining each variable's distribution to determine suitability of using parametric or nonparametric statistical tests. The three main EPI fidelity scores were compared by: (a) health authority (the distribution of the five health authorities in BC referenced earlier are viewable in Figure 1; ANOVA), (b) catchment population (number of people residing within the geographical catchment area of each mental health service site based on 2021 BC Census data; Spearman correlation), (c) urban versus rural sites (catchment population > 100 000 people for urban and < 100 000 for rural; t‐test), and (d) mental health service site designation as a standalone EPI site (a specialized EPI program in its own mental health centre), semi‐standalone EPI site (a specialized EPI team within a larger, more generic mental health centre), or generic mental health and substance use (MHSU) centre (a standard mental health centre that may have some staff with EPI expertise; ANOVA). Correlational analyses (Pearson, or Spearman when the variable was not normally distributed) were done to determine which individual items were associated with higher total scores, and therefore may be key contributors to higher levels of program fidelity.

Results

The self‐report EPI‐FS was completed by managers and clinical staff at 17 out of 23 sites (73.9%) that were identified as potential participants, or 17 out of 20 sites (85.0%) that received an official invitation to participate. One of the 17 sites that completed the assessment was not included in data analysis as they scored themselves zero on all but two items, explaining that all clients with first episode psychosis were referred to a different site with more resources while they were in the process of developing an EPI program and that they completed the EPI‐FS for educational purposes only. Sites from all of the five health authorities in BC participated. The distribution of the 16 analysed sites across BC and their designation as a standalone EPI site, semi‐standalone EPI site, or generic MHSU centre is seen in Figure 1. The catchment population the analysed sites served ranged from 28 000 to 924 000 people, with a mean of 273 000. Catchment population for each site is shown in Table 1. The catchment population of the sites that did not participate in the assessment ranged from 8000 to 65 000, and these sites were all generic mental health and substance use (MHSU) centres. Overall, the catchment population of the 16 analysed sites totaled approximately 4.36 million people or 83.5% of the 2021 BC population.

The three main EPI fidelity scores and the overall fidelity rating for each site are shown in Table 1. Sites were re‐named to letters A through P. Total scores ranged from 27 to 68 (possible range 0–84), with a mean of 47.12 (SD = 11.92). Using total scores as a tentative designation of fidelity, two sites (12.5%) met criteria for high fidelity (i.e., > 75% of the total maximum score), nine (56.3%) attained moderate fidelity, three (18.8%) low fidelity, and two (12.5%) very low fidelity. The mean total score for all 16 sites was 56.1%, giving the province overall a ranking of moderate EPI fidelity. Of the four sites with the highest total score, three were standalone EPI sites and one was a semi‐standalone EPI site. The mean total score was significantly different between the three mental health service site designations, at 59.75 (SD = 11.92) for standalone EPI sites, 48.75 (SD = 14.83) for semi‐standalone EPI sites, and 40.0 (SD = 8.05) for generic MHSU centres (F (2,13) = 6.36, p = 0.01). No significant differences were found in total scores when sites were grouped by health authority (F (4,11) =1.58, p = 0.25) or between sites designated as urban versus rural (t(14) =1.54, p = 0.15). When catchment population was considered, rather than just using the categorization of urban versus rural, a positive trend in total score was found but failed to reach significance (r(14) =0.46, p = 0.07).

No site met fidelity for all 28 items on the EPI‐FS (i.e., scored 2–3 for each item). Only three sites met fidelity on over 75% of the items. The mean number of items meeting fidelity was 15.63 (SD = 5.31), with a range from 7 to 25 (possible range 0–28). The mean number of items meeting fidelity was significantly different between mental health service site designation (F(2,13) = 7.17, p = 0.008), at 21.75 (SD = 4.38) for standalone EPI sites, 15.50 (SD = 6.43) for semi‐standalone EPI sites, and 12.62 (SD = 3.60) for generic MHSU centres. Neither health authority (F(4,11) = 1.69, p = 0.22), nor rural versus urban grouping (t(14) = 1.05, p = 0.31) significantly differed in the number of items met. The number of items meeting fidelity was not significantly correlated with catchment population (r(14) = 0.42, p = 0.10).

Only one site met fidelity for all essential items on the EPI‐FS. Mean score for essential items meeting fidelity across the 16 sites was 10.13 (SD = 3.67), with a range from 4–17 (possible range 0–17). Mean number of essential items meeting fidelity was significantly different between mental health service site designation (F(2,13) = 4.74, p = 0.03), at 13.75 (SD = 3.98) for standalone EPI sites, 10.50 (SD = 4.04) for semi‐standalone EPI sites, and 8.12 (SD = 2.21) for generic MHSU centres. Number of essential items meeting fidelity was not correlated with catchment population (r(14) = 0.40, p = 0.15). Urban sites met fidelity for approximately 2.1 more essential items than rural sites, but the difference was not significant. No significant differences were found across health authorities.

The highest scoring items, with 75% or more sites meeting fidelity, were: Ongoing Assessment of Potential Self‐harm (100.0%), Well Defined Service Criteria (93.8%), Timely Response (93.8%), Outreach and Home Treatment (93.8%), Links to Inpatient Services (81.3%), Regular Client Reviews/Weekly Team Meetings (75.0%), and Service Intensity (75.0%). Discussion

The province of British Columbia, Canada, overall received a ranking of moderate fidelity to the EPI model of care, as defined by the BC Standards and Guidelines for Early Psychosis Intervention Programs, utilizing the EPI‐FS. Despite high fidelity at some sites, there was notable variability of fidelity across the province. This variability indicates that the goal of ensuring all clients with early psychosis, regardless of where they live in BC, have equitable access to care is not yet being met.

Standalone and semi‐standalone EPI sites scored significantly higher than generic mental health and substance use (MHSU) centres on the three main EPI‐FS scores, suggesting that a specialized EPI program, with staff who focus solely on providing specialized interventions to clients with early psychosis, is linked to higher fidelity to the EPI model of care. The BC EPI APP expected sites to meet fidelity to all items designated as an “essential” component of the EPI model to be classified as an EPI program; however, only one site met fidelity for all essential items. This finding highlights the need for additional resources, education, and support in individual programs. This finding also suggests that reassessment of appropriate expectations and what is considered “essential” by the BC EPI APP may be needed. Reevaluating these expectations is supported by fidelity research in different jurisdictions that shows that while many EPI programs strive to implement all essential components, full fidelity is rarely achieved (Durbin et al. 2019; Jumper et al. 2024; Meneghelli et al. 2023; O'Connell et al. 2021).

EPI services in BC performed well in several key areas. Despite the growing population of BC and recent strains on the publicly funded medical system, it was encouraging that timely response to referrals was maintained throughout the province, as early intervention is a fundamental practice of EPI that truncates duration of untreated psychosis and reduces risks and suffering (Correll et al. 2018; Melle et al. 2004; Petersen et al. 2005; Srihari et al. 2012). Links with inpatient services were another area of strength, which can improve continuity of care and assist with the transition from inpatient to outpatient treatment (Steffen et al. 2009). Outreach and home treatment are effective methods of engaging clients and making treatment more accessible, and this was maintained in most mental health service sites assessed (Lynch et al. 2016; Melle et al. 2004). As expected, all of the sites met fidelity for assessment of potential self‐harm and all but one site met fidelity for having well defined service criteria.

Service levels varied between sites, with programs experiencing challenges in several areas. Many sites scored low for having sufficient resources, as well as ongoing supervision and mentorship for clinicians, which could contribute to increased staff attrition. Education to the public and service providers about signs of early psychosis was identified as an area of improvement, which is concerning as this is a key factor to reduce the duration of untreated psychosis by increasing referrals and identifying clients as quickly as possible (Joa et al. 2008). Similarly, the provision of family education was only acceptable in less than a quarter of sites. Family education and involvement have been shown to not only reduce client disengagement but also have direct beneficial effects on both the client and family (Bird et al. 2010; Demarais et al. 2024; Onwumere et al. 2011; Mascayano et al. 2021). Evidence‐based psychological therapies, such as Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT), Cognitive Remediation, and Family Interventions, were infrequently provided to clients in BC despite being a component of the EPI model that can improve outcomes (Onwumere et al. 2011; Revell et al. 2015; Singh et al. 2021). Some clients that receive care through EPI services in BC are at Ultra High Risk (UHR) for developing psychosis and there is evidence that psychological interventions can reduce the transition to psychosis (Mei et al. 2021). The weakness in the provision of evidence‐based psychological therapies may be particularly impacting this sub‐population. Finally, few sites integrated substance use treatment, despite evidence that supports integration within EPI services (Crockford and Addington 2017). It is possible that the low fidelity may be attributed to the historical separation of psychosis and substance use services in BC, combined with insufficient concurrent disorder expertise.

The individual item correlations to total score provide further insights into the attributes of higher fidelity sites. Sites that engage and provide education to families, have more staff development opportunities, provide quality psychological interventions, and have sufficient operational resources were most likely to have the highest total fidelity scores. Comprehensive assessments, best practice pharmacotherapy, quality staff supervision, and the capacity to do program evaluation were also correlated with higher fidelity to the EPI model of care.

A previous fidelity assessment of EPI services in BC by the BC EPI APP occurred from 2012 to 2015, with nine of the 16 sites from this assessment participating (Ehmann and Hanson 2016). External reviewers visited each site and sites also self‐assessed using a tool, called the Program Form, that roughly maps onto the four sections of the EPI‐FS. The Program Form utilized a yes or no response to unanchored, less specific questions compared to the EPI‐FS. The relative standings among the nine long‐standing services providing EPI care in BC were mostly unchanged over the past decade, with the two highest scoring sites remaining in their respective ranking and the four lowest scoring sites again scoring the lowest. Different fidelity instruments were used, subsequently limiting the comparability of sites' fidelity over time. In both assessments, the BC EPI APP has found that the way EPI was implemented has led to well‐established and well‐resourced sites, such as standalone EPI sites dedicated only to providing EPI care, often scoring higher, while more generic MHSU centres located in smaller communities where only a few clients with early psychosis are treated each year score lower.

The evaluation of EPI services has become more frequent in recent years using a variety of different fidelity scales, including in Canada (Durbin et al. 2019), the United States (Addington et al. 2020; Essock et al. 2015; Jumper et al. 2024), Australia (Killackey et al. 2018; Williams et al. 2021), Denmark (Melau et al. 2019; Nordentoft et al. 2015), and Italy (Meneghelli et al. 2023). Durbin et al. (2019) conducted a fidelity assessment of nine EPI programs across Ontario, Canada, using the First Episode Psychosis Services Fidelity Scale (FEPS‐FS). Results were similar to ours, including notable variability in fidelity across services and gaps in the delivery of evidence‐based psychological therapies and substance use treatment. Addington et al. (2020) conducted a fidelity assessment in the United States of 36 first‐episode psychosis services using a modified version of the FEPS‐FS with remote independent reviewers. They found very high rates of evidence‐based psychotherapy and family engagement, a noticeable difference compared to our assessment. Conversely, many of these first‐episode psychosis services struggled with providing outreach to clients, one of the highest scoring items on the EPI‐FS. Comparison of our assessment to both of these studies is limited, however, given the use of an alternative fidelity scale with different criteria for meeting fidelity.

Limitations

There are several limitations with this fidelity assessment. This was the first assessment utilizing the recently developed and unvalidated EPI‐FS. Although it was tailored specifically to the BC Standards and Guidelines for EPI Programs, other well‐studied fidelity scales exist and may have produced different results. The self‐assessment format of the EPI‐FS also carries the risks of inconsistent interpretation of criteria and/or strategic bias where sites may have over‐rated due to fear of repercussions to reputation or under‐rated to help obtain additional funding. Specific criteria for fidelity scoring using clear cut‐points for most items and targets on the scale, as well as training sessions for raters, ideally mitigated some of this risk. This limitation is expected to be resolved by using external reviewers in the next planned BC EPI fidelity assessment. Additionally, not all outpatient mental health services across the province that provide care to clients with early psychosis participated despite being considered or officially invited. Sites that declined to participate may have done so due to lack of available staff time and resources at these sites. Additionally, sites in larger communities were more likely to participate. Certain groups, such as Indigenous clients and families, may be disproportionately living in smaller communities that did not participate. The health inequities seen when comparing urban centres to very small and remote communities may not have been fully appreciated (Public Health Agency of Canada 2018). The sites that were assessed provide care for adults; therefore, clients under the age of 18 were not included. With a sample size of 16 sites, statistical power to detect some differences may have been limited. The inclusion of smaller and mid‐sized community programs, as well as Child and Youth Mental Health services, will be an important next step in future EPI fidelity assessments in BC.

Conclusion

This BC EPI fidelity assessment represents the first attempt to quantify mental health service sites fidelity to the BC Standards and Guidelines for EPI Programs using the EPI‐FS. Rather than assuming quality of service, a fidelity assessment provides data to assess whether the EPI model is being implemented as intended, identifies strengths and weaknesses of individual programs, provides accountability to funders, and ensures the public, clients, and providers can rely on services to provide a high standard of care. It was also intended to deepen participants' understanding of the EPI model and assist managers with planning and implementation decisions, including staffing and service structure.

There was considerable variability in fidelity levels of programs in BC providing mental health services for young adults with early psychosis. Specialized EPI programs were more likely to meet fidelity compared to generic mental health and substance use centres. Sites with sufficient resources, staff development opportunities, and clinical supervision and mentorship had higher fidelity, indicating that having the appropriate funding to support clinicians with a comprehensive understanding of the EPI model helps ensure high‐quality early psychosis interventions are provided effectively. Overall, these findings demonstrate a need for greater levels of support within individual mental health service sites, highlight the importance of establishing specialized EPI programs, and contribute to international efforts to ensure that EPI services demonstrate commitment to self‐evaluation and adherence to EPI practices and principles.
