Rational design of polymer film morphology via structure-performance linkage for enhanced condensation performance
Authors: Jun Soo Kim, Minjeong Kang, Seokwan Roh, Donghyeong Lee, Wontae Jang, Sung Gap Im, Youngsuk Nam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dropwise condensation is essential for heat transfer in power generation, thermal management, and water harvesting, yet remains limited by surface modifications that often fail to maximize the advantages of dropwise mode. Here, we develop a morphology-based design strategy using conformal, pinhole-free fluoropolymer films deposited by initiated chemical vapor deposition. By controlling film thickness and thermal annealing, we identify a structure–performance relationship linking nanoscale aggregate morphology, wettability, and condensation dynamics. Thin films (≤ 100 nm) contain smaller, more densely distributed aggregates and lower roughness than 500 nm films, resulting in higher nucleation densities. Thermal annealing increases crystalline ordering while reducing contact angle hysteresis, enabling droplet removal at smaller diameters. These coupled effects increase surface renewal by combining rapid droplet nucleation with frequent sweeping. Under pure-vapor conditions, the morphology-controlled films improve heat transfer by ~50% compared with hydrophobic self-assembled monolayers, providing design principles for polymer coatings in condensation-based thermal systems.

Introduction

Water vapor condensation is a crucial process across various industries, including power generation1, water desalination/harvesting2, and thermal management3. For instance, approximately 70% of the world’s electricity is produced using a steam-based power cycle that utilizes steam condensers4. Therefore, improving condensation efficiency leads to energy savings and reduced greenhouse gas emissions, resulting in a low carbon footprint. Most industrial condensers employ hydrophilic metal surfaces that promote filmwise condensation, in which a continuous water film forms. The low thermal conductivity of this film limits heat transfer, yielding low condensation heat transfer coefficients (hc ~ 10–20 kW m⁻² K⁻¹)5,6. Performance can be markedly improved by promoting dropwise condensation, wherein nucleated droplets coalesce and depart under gravity. Rapid surface renewal from liquid–vapor to solid–vapor states continually exposes fresh nucleation sites, enhancing heat and mass transfer by a factor of about 4–6 compared with filmwise condensation (hc ~ 40–60 kW m⁻² K⁻¹). Gains of this magnitude could increase steam-plant efficiency by ~2%, equivalent to ~1000 TWh of additional electricity annually7,8. Accordingly, research on enabling dropwise condensation has expanded rapidly in recent years. Among the available approaches, surface modification is the most intensively studied, and within this category, the predominant strategy is to apply low-surface-energy hydrophobic coatings to hydrophilic metal surfaces9.

Various hydrophobic coating methods have been proposed to achieve dropwise condensation, including silane- or thiol-based self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) and fluorinated polymers. Both approaches show enhanced droplet repellency, allowing condensed droplets to fall easily owing to the low surface energy of the fluorinated group (10–18 mN m−1)10,11. SAMs are typically ultrathin (1–10 nm) with uniform coverage, which is favorable for thermal resistance, but they degrade rapidly by hydrolysis upon exposure to water and revert to filmwise condensation12,13. On the other hand, fluorinated polymers such as polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) and fluorocarbon films (CFx, x ~ 2) exhibit high thermal stability14 and favorable mechanical/chemical robustness15, rendering them effective in promoting stable dropwise condensation16. However, when deposited as thick films (typically several hundred nanometers to a few micrometers), the intrinsically low thermal conductivity (~0.2 W m−1 K−1) of the polymer film itself offsets the thermal advantages gained from dropwise mode17. By contrast, thinning the fluoropolymer layers often introduces nanoscale defects, including dewetting, blistering, and delamination, that precipitate a transition to filmwise condensation18–21. This problem is particularly severe for fluorinated polymers because their low surface energy hinders uniform wetting and adhesion on the substrate. As they can only be processed using low-surface-energy fluorinated solvents, incomplete spreading and peeling frequently occur during thin-film formation. The deposition of thin polymer films also increases surface heterogeneity, such as roughness, which in turn results in high contact angle hysteresis and significantly retards droplet mobility, slowing droplet removal and thereby reducing condensation performance18. Therefore, achieving a thin, defect-free film with chemical and mechanical homogeneity is essential for promoting enhanced dropwise condensation.

To address the aforementioned limitations, recent studies have increasingly focused on the fabrication of ultra-thin, defect-free, and chemically homogeneous low-surface-energy coatings with enhanced smoothness, and thus capable of sustaining reliable dropwise condensation22–24. Initiated chemical vapor deposition (iCVD), a vapor-phase polymer deposition method, has emerged as a promising technique for fabricating conformal, pinhole-free polymer films under solvent-free, near-room-temperature conditions. Compared with other coating methods, iCVD offers many distinct advantages, including precise thickness control, large-area uniformity, scalability, and economic feasibility25–27. Owing to these advantages, iCVD has been widely adopted in applications requiring precise surface functionality, such as electronics28, antifouling coatings29,30, and energy devices31,32. In particular, conventional deposition methods often suffer from poor wetting and delamination during processing of fluoropolymers, due to their low surface energy and the need for fluorinated solvents, issues that iCVD effectively overcomes through direct, conformal vapor-phase deposition33–36. The ability to directly polymerize fluoroalkyl-containing monomers in the vapor phase enables the formation of robust, uniform, and conformal low-surface-energy coating layers, which are suitable for promoting dropwise condensation.

Condensation is a multiscale process that begins with nanoscale droplet nucleation, progresses through growth and coalescence, and culminates in macroscale departure when gravity overcomes the pinning force from contact angle hysteresis37,38. Surface roughness and chemical properties of the substrate strongly influence both the initial droplet nucleation density and the contact angle hysteresis, which mainly controls the droplet sweeping behavior39–41. Therefore, the relationships among surface structure, wettability, and condensation dynamics must be rigorously investigated in the design of functional coatings. In 2014, a notable study employed iCVD to synthesize a copolymer film of 1H,1H,2H,2H-perfluorodecyl acrylate (PFDA) and divinylbenzene (DVB), demonstrating the potential for promoting dropwise condensation42. While this work validated the applicability of iCVD-deposited fluoropolymers for condensation applications, it primarily focused on enhancing mechanical and chemical durability through crosslinking. Moreover, the reported heat transfer coefficient (hc ~ 37 kW m⁻² K⁻¹) was similar to values measured for conventional fluorosilane-treated surfaces. To further improve the dropwise condensation performance, a systematic understanding is necessary, especially in terms of how film thickness and fabrication conditions determine the surface chemical/physical state; how those states govern nucleation density, droplet coalescence, and departure across scales; and whether stable dropwise condensation can be induced in operating windows representative of real-world applications. These aspects remained largely unaddressed in prior fluoropolymer studies using other coating methods as well. By rigorously mapping the structure–property–performance linkages of fluoropolymers through controlled variation in film thickness and fabrication conditions, condensation performance can be substantially enhanced.

Here, we examine the fundamental relationship among nanoscale morphology, surface characteristics, condensation behavior, and dropwise condensation performance in iCVD-deposited pPFDMA (poly(3,3,4,4,5,5,6,6,7,7,8,8,9,9,10,10,10-heptadecafluorodecyl methacrylate)) films to understand how nanoscale film properties govern multiscale condensation behavior and to optimize heat transfer performance. We begin by elucidating the effect of film thickness on surface morphology: thinner pPFDMA films (≤100 nm) are smoother and exhibit ~3× higher nucleation densities than a thicker film (500 nm), owing to dense distribution of small semi-crystalline aggregates that act as nucleation sites. Then, we examine thermal annealing (80 °C, 1 h), which contracts aggregates through crystalline reorganization, lowering roughness and contact angle hysteresis. Transient condensation analysis shows that thin films exhibit lower droplet departure diameters and higher droplet sweeping frequencies. Together, these results elucidate a fundamental structure–property–performance relationship for dropwise condensation. Moving beyond the conventional paradigm of simply minimizing surface roughness to reduce contact-line pinning, we establish an aggregate-centric framework. We reveal that semi-crystalline aggregates exert a competing dual effect: they inherently impede droplet mobility by increasing hysteresis, yet paradoxically serve as essential, geometrically favorable nucleation sites. By explicitly addressing this unresolved nucleation–mobility tradeoff, we demonstrate that these performance-dictating parameters can be decoupled. Specifically, film thickness is the primary lever for maximizing nucleation density, whereas thermal annealing selectively mitigates the pinning penalty without sacrificing active nucleation sites. Pure vapor condensation tests on copper tubes confirm this enhancement: annealed thin pPFDMA films exhibit heat transfer coefficients up to ~88 kW m⁻² K⁻¹, over 50% higher than those on conventional dropwise-inducing surfaces, including silane-based self-assembled monolayers and prior iCVD coatings. These results provide clear, predictive design principles for engineering fluoropolymer coatings that balance nucleation and droplet shedding, offering a scalable pathway toward high-performance condensation systems for industrial applications.

Results

Surface characteristics

To promote enhanced dropwise condensation, we deposited a low-surface-energy fluoropolymer, pPFDMA (poly(3,3,4,4,5,5,6,6,7,7,8,8,9,9,10,10,10-heptadecafluorodecyl methacrylate)) with fluoroalkyl chains via iCVD (Fig. 1a). Briefly, vaporized PFDMA monomer and initiator (tert-butyl peroxide, TBPO) species are introduced into the reactor chamber with a hot filament (~140 °C) on the top and a cooled substrate (~35 °C) at the bottom. The initiator injected into the heated filament decomposes thermally to form radicals, which react with the adsorbed monomer on the cooled substrate to undergo surface polymerization. The thickness of the deposited polymer can be tuned precisely by adjusting the process time. Detailed information on the sample preparation and thickness measurement processes is provided in the Methods and Supplementary Note 1. After polymer deposition, the samples underwent thermal annealing. The thickness-dependent roughness of iCVD-deposited pPFDMA films arises from thin-film growth kinetics and the intrinsic crystallization behavior of fluoropolymers. At early stages, strong substrate–adsorbate interactions promote two-dimensional (2D) layer-by-layer growth (Frank–van der Merwe)44, yielding uniform, low-roughness coatings in thinner films. As thickness increases, cohesion between polymer chains dominates, triggering a transition to three-dimensional (3D) island growth45, in which molecules preferentially aggregate rather than wet the underlying layer. This unusual roughening phenomenon stems from the inherently strong ordering/packing tendency of perfluorinated side chains, leading to localized crystalline domains46. Such aggregate formation is a natural characteristic of semi-crystalline polymers undergoing solidification, whether from melt or solution47. Importantly, the resulting semi-crystalline islands, consistently observed in AFM images, vary with thickness: thin films contain small, densely packed aggregates, whereas thicker films exhibit larger, more isolated domains, which accounts for the pronounced increase in surface roughness.

Enhanced dropwise condensation requires surfaces with both low surface energy and smooth morphology6,41. To achieve this, we thermally annealed iCVD-deposited pPFDMA films to induce crystalline reorganization and reduce roughness and contact angle hysteresis (CAH). After 1 h of annealing, surface roughness decreased from 2.1 to 1.2 nm (25 nm film), 4.2 to 2.2 nm (100 nm), and 26 to 21 nm (500 nm), with no further reduction observed beyond this duration (Fig. 2g), establishing 1 h as the optimal time. To examine roughness reduction in detail, in-situ environmental AFM was performed to measure roughness at the same position during annealing. As shown in Fig. 2b, across all samples, the aggregates, highlighted by the yellow circles, exhibit the same features before and after annealing and are observed to reduce in size, thereby directly linking the roughness reduction to aggregate contraction.

To quantitatively assess aggregate reduction, histogram analysis was performed. As shown in Fig. 2c–e, the distribution shifts toward the left for all samples after 1 h of thermal annealing, indicating a decrease in the overall average aggregate height. The aggregate count also varied with film thickness, ranging from 0–160 in the 500 nm sample to 0–2300 in the 25 and 100 nm samples. These results indicate that thicker films contain fewer but taller aggregates, whereas thinner films feature smaller aggregates with higher density.

Changes in surface roughness substantially affect wettability. As shown in Fig. 2f, g, thermal annealing reduced CAH across all samples, with a clear correlation: decreased roughness led to reduced hysteresis. This is due to the contraction of semi-crystalline aggregates, which act as local pinning sites owing to their chemical heterogeneity42,48. Because condensers typically operate in vertical orientations, CAH governs the minimum droplet departure diameter. As shown in Fig. 2h, annealed surfaces exhibited smaller departure diameters: reduced by 0.5 mm (25 nm), 0.6 mm (100 nm), and 0.02 mm (500 nm). The limited improvement in the 500 nm film stems from a significant decrease in the advancing angle (from 160° to 140°), thereby weakening water repellency. As shown in Supplementary Note 4, when the advancing angle is high, the influence of hysteresis on the droplet departure diameter is less pronounced. In contrast, when the advancing angle is low, the effect of hysteresis becomes more significant. Hence, despite hysteresis reduction, the 500 nm film showed minimal change. In contrast, thinner films demonstrated ~25% smaller departure diameters, indicating enhanced droplet mobility and faster sweeping potential.

To investigate the thermal behavior of pPFDMA thin films during annealing, in-situ spectroscopic ellipsometry (iSE) was performed on a 100 nm-thick film heated from –30 °C to 120 °C at 10 °C min−1 (Fig. 3a). The film thickness first increased, then decreased above 69 °C, and rose again beyond 82 °C, corresponding to three distinct thermal phases: amorphous (Phase Ⅰ, <69 °C), cold crystallization (Phase Ⅱ, 69–82 °C), and melting (Phase Ⅲ, >82 °C). Despite the long perfluorinated side chains of PFDMA, the as-deposited films remain in an amorphous-rich state due to the nature of iCVD polymerization, which proceeds via surface-mediated free-radical reactions with predominantly physical adsorption27. Upon slow heating above the glass transition temperature (Tg ~ 45 °C), the polymer chains gain sufficient mobility that enables structural reorganization and leads to cold crystallization (i.e., crystallization upon heating above glass transition temperature (Tg) in previously amorphous polymers), as indicated by the thickness decrease in Phase Ⅱ. During cold crystallization, the surface roughness is reduced, as shown in Fig. 2a, indicating the system’s evolution toward a more thermodynamically stable state. This process is fundamentally driven by minimizing the total Gibbs free energy, which encompasses both reducing excess surface energy associated with the initial high-roughness topography and eliminating defects within the semi-crystalline aggregates. In Phase Ⅲ, the polymer begins to acquire sufficient thermal energy to melt the crystallites. This transition disrupts ordered chain packing and increases thickness due to volume expansion in the disordered molten phase. As shown in Fig. 3c, only a melting transition at 82 °C (Tm) is observed upon heating the once-annealed sample, with no cold crystallization. Upon cooling at 10 °C min−1, the crystallization transition occurs near 83 °C (Tc). This indicates that the thermal annealing (80 °C, 1 h) modifies the film’s thermal history, suppressing cold re-crystallization even under repeated heating and cooling. Constant-temperature iSE (Fig. 3b) confirms steady surface reorganization over 1 h, as shown by the gradual thickness reduction and saturation.

Figure 3d shows XRD patterns of pPFDMA films (25, 100, 500 nm) before and after 1 h annealing at 80 °C. Prior to annealing, no distinct diffraction peaks were observed in the 2–15° range, indicating an amorphous-rich state. Post-annealing, clear peaks near 2.9° and 5.8° emerged, consistent with lamellar ordering of semicrystalline fluorinated polymers and previously reported smectic B-like structures in pPFDA films34, where bilayer periodicities arise from vertically aligned perfluorinated side chains. Notably, the intensity of the diffraction peaks increases with film thickness, particularly the peak near 18°, indicating enhanced out-of-plane crystallinity. The suppressed crystallinity in thinner films is presumably due to kinetic trapping, in which limited chain mobility during the iCVD process hinders lamellar alignment50. Consistent with these studies, we infer that thicker films favor vertical polymer chain ordering due to reduced confinement effects and enhanced mobility during polymer deposition. While the metastable structure in thinner films reflects limited out-of-plane crystallinity, it yields a large population of smaller aggregates densely distributed across the surface. This high aggregate density can subsequently increase the density of water droplet nucleation sites. Upon thermal annealing, these kinetically trapped small structures reorganize into a more stable morphology with increased in-plane crystallinity. Notably, perfluoroalkyl-functionalized surfaces exposed to water can reconstruct into hydrophilic, acrylate-rich layers, thereby raising hysteresis51. Thermal annealing counteracts this effect by stabilizing a fluorine-rich outer layer, thereby lowering interfacial energy and enabling stable dropwise condensation. This reorganization links increased crystallinity to improved condensation behavior by explaining the lower hysteresis and smaller departure diameters observed after annealing.

To further investigate the polymer’s structural transformation upon heating, we conducted in-situ XRD measurements with a controlled temperature ramp (2 °C min−1). Figure 3f shows that diffraction peaks emerge at approximately 2.9° and 5.8°, beginning at ~69 °C, marking the onset of cold crystallization (Phase II). These peaks continue to develop up to 82–83 °C, in good agreement with the ellipsometry data, indicating that the initially amorphous chains reorganize into semicrystalline domains. Above 85 °C, the intensity of these crystalline peaks diminishes and eventually disappears, demonstrating that the polymer has entered a fully melted state (Phase III). These observations confirm that polymer crystallization occurs within the temperature range of 70–83 °C, highlighting the importance of selecting an appropriate annealing temperature to effectively induce structural reorganization of the polymer that can potentially affect condensation performance.

The in-situ XRD further examined the time-resolved crystallization behavior during isothermal annealing at 80 °C (heating rate: 10 °C min⁻¹, 10–minute measurement intervals). Figure 3g shows that crystallization began at 80 °C, with diffraction peaks appearing at 2.9° and 5.8°. Peak intensities increased progressively over time, reaching saturation after ~50–60 min, consistent with the ellipsometry results shown in Fig. 3b. In addition to selecting an appropriate thermal annealing temperature, these findings underscore the importance of selecting a sufficient annealing time to ensure full structural reorganization.

Overall, the macroscopic wetting behaviors observed on the pPFDMA films can be mechanistically linked to the microscopic structural evolution induced by thermal annealing. The causal relationship operates through a sequential thermodynamic and morphological transition. First, thermal energy drives the surface reconstruction of the polymer, allowing the highly fluorinated side-chains to reorganize and pack more densely, thereby increasing the overall degree of crystallinity. This transition to a more ordered, crystalline-dominant state induces contraction and partial flattening of the aggregate-bearing morphology. Consequently, nanoscale structural relaxation suppresses the vertical amplitude of the asperities, thereby reducing surface roughness. Ultimately, this smoothed topographical landscape minimizes the density of geometric pinning sites, effectively lowering the energy barrier for contact line motion and resulting in a significant reduction in CAH. This establishes a clear structure-property linkage where localized crystallization-driven structural reorganization directly dictates the macroscopic droplet mobility.

Nucleation density

Condensation involves multiscale phenomena, ranging from nanoscale nucleation to macroscale droplet coalescence and sweeping. We investigated the effects of pPFDMA film thickness and thermal annealing on the overall condensation process. For early-stage condensation observation, we conducted in-situ droplet nucleation analysis using environmental scanning electron microscopy (ESEM). Surface temperature was controlled via a Peltier stage, and once steady-state conditions were achieved, water vapor was injected to initiate condensation (see the Methods for detailed methodology).

ESEM analysis (Fig. 4a, b) revealed that 25/100 nm films exhibited ~3× higher nucleation densities than 500 nm films, with dense, uniformly distributed droplets. Thermal annealing slightly increased nucleation density but with no statistical significance, indicating minimal impact on initial nucleation. This trend is attributed to the number density of semi-crystalline aggregates: thinner films contain numerous small aggregates, while thicker films exhibit fewer, larger ones (Fig. 2c–e), creating a ~ 10–15× difference in aggregate number density. These aggregates lower the nucleation energy barrier42,52. Specifically, the densely packed small aggregates on thin films form abundant locally concave nanocavities that significantly reduce the Gibbs free-energy barrier for nucleation via geometric confinement (see Supplementary Note 11). Furthermore, while thermal annealing slightly increases the nanoscale aggregate count, this modest increase is overshadowed by rapid microscale droplet coalescence, resulting in a negligible change in the observable nucleation density (see Supplementary Note 11 for a detailed theoretical thermodynamic analysis). It should be noted that surface aggregates, although often responsible for increased roughness and, consequently, higher CAH, can facilitate frequent condensate nucleation by providing energetically favorable sites for droplet nucleation, effectively lowering the nucleation energy barrier. Therefore, it is essential to identify the optimal balance between minimizing hysteresis to allow efficient droplet shedding and maximizing nucleation density. In our case, annealed thin polymer films simultaneously exhibit low contact angle hysteresis owing to their minimal surface roughness and enhanced nucleation density, attributed to the abundant presence of smaller aggregates, rendering thinner films (≤100 nm) particularly suitable for achieving enhanced dropwise condensation.

We also utilized optical microscopy (OM), which, despite offering lower resolution than ESEM, allows observation of initial condensation without any interference from electron beam heating53. The experiments were performed under ambient conditions, because previous research confirmed that nucleation density measurements are quantitatively consistent, regardless of the presence of non-condensable gases54 (see Supplementary Note 5 for further experimental details). Despite its lower spatial resolution, optical microscopy provided results fully consistent with those obtained from ESEM. Both results revealed that thinner pPFDMA films (25/100 nm) possess higher nucleation densities than the 500 nm sample. Therefore, these complementary measurements indicate that nucleation density is primarily thickness-controlled, with only modest sensitivity to annealing.

To verify transferability to a practical metallic condenser substrate, we performed time-resolved OM condensation analysis on pPFDMA-coated Cu substrates. Despite the intrinsic microscale roughness of Cu, the same thickness-dependent nucleation-density trend was preserved. The slightly higher projected-area-normalized nucleation densities on Cu than on Si indicate only a modest substrate-dependent offset, likely arising from the increased surface area of the rough Cu substrate. These results support that the thickness-dependent nucleation-density trend is primarily associated with the pPFDMA-mediated nanoscale aggregate morphology, while Cu roughness contributes mainly to a substrate-dependent offset (see Supplementary Note 18).

Transient droplet dynamics

To investigate how surface roughness and nucleation density influence transient condensate droplet dynamics, vertical condensation experiments were conducted using pPFDMA-deposited Si wafer substrates. Experiments were conducted in a controlled environmental chamber (Fig. 5a) under pure water vapor to minimize the presence of NCGs (non-condensable gases), which act as a diffusional mass transfer barrier8,55. We thoroughly evacuated the test chamber prior to water vapor injection, reducing both the chamber pressure and the NCG concentration to below 0.5 Pa and 0.5%, respectively. Due to the highly dynamic nature of condensation under pure vapor conditions, manual measurement of droplet number or size becomes infeasible. Therefore, we utilized a vision transformer-based model, the Segment Anything Model56, to analyze time-sequenced images captured during the experiment, where images were acquired at 1 frame per second using a CMOS (Complementary Metal Oxide Semiconductor) camera equipped with a telecentric lens (see Supplementary Note 9 for methodological details of computer vision).

The time-resolved droplet number data for samples with varying thickness and annealing conditions are shown in Fig. 5c. Figure 5d, e illustrates the macroscale condensation cycle for the 100 nm film before annealing, proceeding through (i) coalescence and growth, (ii) droplet sweeping, and (iii) renucleation. During coalescence, droplets merge and grow, decreasing the total count. Once gravitational force overcomes pinning, sweeping occurs, followed by rapid renucleation on the exposed solid–vapor interface, increasing the droplet count. Since heat and mass transfer peak during renucleation, frequent cycling is key to enhancing condensation performance. These patterns are observed across all samples.

Regions of sharp droplet count drop-and-rebound correspond to sweeping events, from which we extracted departure diameters and sweeping frequencies (Fig. 5f, g). For 25 and 100 nm films, reduced roughness and hysteresis resulted in ~27% smaller departure diameters and a 1.37× higher sweeping frequency than the 500 nm film. Annealing further decreased departure diameters by 31% (25 nm) and 33% (100 nm), with corresponding increases in frequency of 11% and 30%, respectively. In contrast, the 500 nm film exhibits a slight increase in departure diameter of only 2% after annealing, accompanied by a corresponding decrease in sweeping frequency of about 2%. Collectively, these results establish a clear pathway: reducing hysteresis leads to smaller departure diameters and, in turn, more frequent sweeping. The significance of this increased sweeping frequency lies in its role in accelerating surface renewal. More frequent sweeping events rapidly clear the condensing surface of large, thermally resistive droplets, thereby re-exposing fresh nucleation sites. This dynamic coupling between rapid droplet removal and continuous surface renewal is a primary driver for achieving higher heat transfer coefficients, as corroborated by recent modeling studies that identify droplet sweeping frequency as a key parameter governing macroscale thermal performance57.

To further deconstruct the effect of controlling thickness and annealing, we analyzed the macroscale-averaged droplet number density (Fig. 5h). The thinner (25/100 nm) films consistently support a significantly higher density of droplet formation than the 500 nm film, where the difference is particularly stark in the sub-100 µm diameter regime. This observation points to a dual enhancement mechanism: a higher intrinsic density of nucleation sites coupled with more efficient droplet removal via low hysteresis. Although annealing slightly increased the number density, ESEM and OM revealed no change in nucleation site density. Together, rapid droplet removal on annealed thin pPFDMA films is promoted by two concurrent effects: post-annealing reductions in hysteresis, which lower the departure diameter, and the thickness-dependent persistence of small, densely packed semi-crystalline aggregates, which sustains a high nucleation site density. As a result, the annealed 25 and 100 nm coatings exhibit higher droplet sweeping frequencies, indicating optimal conditions for enhanced condensation heat transfer.

Condensation heat transfer performance

To evaluate heat transfer performance and industrial scalability, we measured the condensation heat transfer coefficient of pPFDMA on metallic tubes, the configuration most widely used in practice and well suited to high-fidelity measurements. The experiments were conducted in the same controlled environmental chamber and under pure water vapor conditions used in the transient droplet dynamics experiments described above. Tube samples used in the experiment were made of copper (Cu), a widely used industrial condenser material. Since previously measured contact angle values were obtained using silicon substrates, additional measurements were performed on flat copper samples coated with pPFDMA. Here, the thickness labels (25, 100, and 500 nm) refer to the nominal deposition thicknesses calibrated on flat Si reference substrates. Unlike silicon substrates, the copper samples exhibited approximately twice the CAH, resulting in an increase in CAH from ~20° to ~40° (Supplementary Note 2). Notably, after thermal annealing, hysteresis values of the 25 nm and 100 nm films on the Cu surface decreased significantly to 19.6° and 19.3°, respectively, whereas the hysteresis of the 500 nm film remained nearly unchanged at 43.6°. As discussed in Supplementary Note 18, cross-sectional analysis confirmed that the actual coating thickness on Cu remained close to these nominal values (±5–10%). For comparative reference in the condensation performance experiment, we also measured the contact angle of the HTMS ((heptadecafluoro-1,1,2,2-tetrahydrodecyl)-trimethoxysilane, Gelest)-treated surface, which is one of the most widely used SAMs studied in prior research. Consistent with previous literature11,13,58, HTMS exhibited a considerably higher hysteresis of ~42°, far higher than that observed for thin, annealed pPFDMA.

Visualization data from the condensation experiments are shown in Fig. 6b. Stable dropwise condensation was successfully induced on all pPFDMA-coated samples. Without thermal annealing, the condensed droplets appeared relatively flat and large, with higher departure diameters driven by higher CAH, resulting in slower droplet sweeping. Once annealed, the droplets on the 25 nm and 100 nm samples were more spherical, with a much smaller diameter (2–2.5 mm) due to reduced hysteresis. In contrast, droplets on the annealed 500 nm sample remained large (3–4 mm), with hysteresis nearly unchanged.

The condensation heat transfer coefficients (hc) were calculated by measuring the flow rate and inlet/outlet temperature differences during the experiments, and subsequently accounting for the conduction through the tube wall, internal convection within the tube, and thermal conduction through the polymer coating layer (see Supplementary Note 6). As presented in Fig. 6c, heat transfer coefficients for the thinner (25 nm and 100 nm) pPFDMA films prior to annealing were measured to be approximately 55–65 kW m−² K−¹, similar to the performance of the HTMS-coated samples. After annealing, these values increased significantly by ~35–40% to 75–88 kW m−² K−¹. On the other hand, the 500 nm pPFDMA samples showed almost unchanged hc ranging from 37 to 45 kW m−² K−¹ regardless of annealing. It should also be noted that the effect of coating thickness was excluded from the calculated heat transfer coefficients by removing the polymer’s conduction term. Additionally, to compare our results with a previous study that utilized p(PFDA-co-DVB) films deposited via iCVD for condensation experiments, we deposited coatings under identical conditions and conducted condensation tests42. The measured condensation heat transfer coefficients ranged from 47 to 59 kW m⁻² K⁻¹. These values exceed those previously reported (~37 kW m⁻² K⁻¹) and are comparable to those of the 25 and 100 nm pPFDMA films without annealing (see Supplementary Fig. 10).

To validate the measured experimental values, theoretical predictions were calculated using the droplet distribution and single-droplet thermal resistance model, as indicated by the dotted lines in Fig. 6c (see Supplementary Note 6 for the calculations). Experimental results showed good agreement with theoretical predictions. For a direct experimental benchmark of filmwise condensation under matched conditions, we performed condensation experiments on a bare Cu tube using the same setup and operating conditions. The bare Cu surface exhibited stable filmwise condensation, yielding 16–17 kW m⁻² K⁻¹, which is in close agreement with the Nusselt laminar filmwise condensation prediction for our tube geometry and test conditions. Nusselt’s filmwise condensation model was also employed to enable direct comparison with the filmwise mode, indicating 4.5–5.5× higher condensation performance for pPFDMA films.

Figure 6d benchmarks the performance of our surface design against representative literature values for tube-type condensers under quiescent vapor conditions (no direct injection), plotting the heat transfer coefficient as a function of subcooling temperature. Conventional polymer or carbon-based coatings have been limited by significant thermal resistance, particularly at the microscale, with heat transfer coefficients of 30–60 kW m⁻² K⁻¹. Even on slippery surfaces engineered for low CAH, such as lubricant-infused surfaces, performance typically reaches a ceiling of 60–70 kW m⁻² K⁻¹. Compared with established benchmarks, the optimized iCVD-deposited pPFDMA films exhibit ~1.5 times higher condensation heat transfer under our test conditions. This improvement arises from the concurrent effects of increased nucleation site density and reduced hysteresis achieved through nanoscale control of film thickness and annealing. These results indicate a practical route to overcoming prior limitations in condenser coatings through iCVD-based structural optimization.

Discussion

In summary, we demonstrated the potential of nanoscale fluorinated polymer films (pPFDMA) deposited via iCVD to promote enhanced, stable dropwise condensation. By systematically investigating the impact of polymer thickness and thermal annealing on surface properties and condensation dynamics, we clarified key structure–performance relationships essential for optimizing the heat transfer performance. We found that thinner polymer coatings (≤100 nm) exhibit substantially higher nucleation densities and lower surface roughness compared to thicker (500 nm) films. Early-stage condensation experiments revealed that the higher droplet nucleation observed on thinner films stems from an abundance of small, densely packed semi-crystalline aggregates that serve as preferential nucleation sites. Furthermore, the thermal annealing process notably reduced surface roughness and contact angle hysteresis in thin films, driven by surface reconstruction and crystallization. This process reorganized the amorphous/semi-crystalline phase into a crystalline-dominant phase, resulting in markedly reduced droplet departure diameters and significantly enhanced droplet sweeping frequencies.

Together, these results establish an experimentally supported guideline within the investigated parameter space: aggregate morphology can exert coupled but distinct influences on nucleation and droplet mobility, and can be tuned through thickness control and thermal annealing to jointly increase nucleation density and reduce hysteresis. Crucially, this shifts the prevailing paradigm of condensation surface design from merely minimizing surface roughness to intentionally engineering the nanoscale aggregate landscape. Unlike previous studies that treat roughness solely as a source of detrimental contact-line pinning, we elucidate the competing dual effects of semi-crystalline aggregates: they inherently restrict droplet mobility yet paradoxically serve as essential, low-energy nucleation sites. By explicitly identifying this unresolved trade-off, our decoupled approach isolates film thickness as the primary lever for maximizing active nucleation density, while using thermal annealing to selectively relax aggregate topography and mitigate pinning without compromising those sites. Our experimental evidence confirms that thin, thermally treated pPFDMA films (25/100 nm) satisfy these dual requirements for optimal performance.

The pure vapor experiments were conducted on copper tubes in controlled environmental chambers under pure water vapor conditions, providing performance validation for an industrially relevant condenser material. Heat transfer coefficients for annealed thin polymer films reached up to 88 kW m−² K−¹, outperforming those of other benchmark dropwise-inducing surfaces, such as lubricant-infused surfaces (LISs) and other fluoropolymers. Such performance enhancement, arising from optimized nanoscale roughness and aggregates that minimize droplet adhesion and maximize nucleation site density, enabled thin, annealed films to achieve hc up to 1.5﻿× higher than those of HTMS and previously studied iCVD-deposited crosslinked polymers. Notably, although the 100 nm film is 4× thicker than the 25 nm film and therefore imposes a larger conduction penalty (see Supplementary Note 10), it still exhibits comparable heat transfer performance. In addition, long-term pure-vapor vacuum condensation durability tests (see Supplementary Note 12) show that both ≤100 nm coatings (25 and 100 nm) maintain stable dropwise condensation for at least 4 weeks, whereas the HTMS reference degrades rapidly and transitions toward filmwise condensation. These results indicate that the high-performance ≤100 nm regime is not a fragile operating window, and suggest that the 100 nm coating is an especially attractive practical design point because it preserves the performance benefit while providing a larger nominal thickness margin, even though no measurable durability difference between the 25 nm and 100 nm films was observed within the present 4-week test window. In addition, consistent with prior delamination studies18 and the discussion in Supplementary Note 10, the greater thickness of the 100 nm film is expected to make it theoretically more resistant to blistering- or delamination-driven failure during extended operation.

Overall, our findings underline the importance of tuning and optimizing nanoscale polymer coating parameters, including thickness control and thermal annealing, to achieve stable, high-performance dropwise condensation. Within the scope of the substrates and conditions tested here, the durability data further indicate that iCVD-enabled conformal and continuous film formation, together with aggregate-level morphology control, can support sustained dropwise condensation under harsh pure-vapor conditions. More broadly, these results provide a mechanistic, experimentally validated framework that may guide future scale-up and extended-lifetime demonstrations for practical condenser geometries and operating environments. These insights offer valuable guidelines for the rational design and scalable deployment of iCVD-deposited polymer films in a wide range of thermal management and industrial condensation applications.

Methods

Deposition of nanoscale pPFDMA film

The hydrophobic fluorinated polymer films of pPFDMA (poly(3,3,4,4,5,5,6,6,7,7,8,8,9,9,10,10,10-heptadecafluorodecyl methacrylate)) were deposited on the substrate using initiated chemical vapor deposition (iCVD). The monomer, PFDMA (97%), and the initiator, tert-butyl peroxide (TBPO, 98%), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received without further purification. PFDMA and TBPO were stored in separate cylinders, with PFDMA heated to 80 °C to facilitate vaporization and TBPO maintained at room temperature. The flow rate of PFDMA and TBPO were set to 0.10 sccm and 0.20 sccm, respectively. The chamber pressure during deposition was maintained at 80 mTorr, with a substrate temperature held at 35 °C. The filament was heated to 140 °C to thermally decompose the initiator. For thermal annealing, the sample coated with pPFDMA was placed in a convection oven (OF4-05P, Lab Companion) at 80 °C for 1 h.

Thickness measurement

The spectroscopic ellipsometer (M2000U with Auto Angle ESM-300 Base, J. A. Woollam) was used to measure the thickness of the deposited pPFDMA on a Si wafer substrate. From 400 to 1000 nm spectral range, the optical constants of the pPFDMA layer on the silicon substrate layer were fitted and measured using the Cauchy model. For in-situ thickness analysis, the sample was heated from −30 °C to 120 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C min−1 using the heating stage.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM)

The atomic force microscopy (FlexAFM, Nanosurf) scan was conducted to measure surface roughness and changes in polymer aggregates due to thermal annealing using a cantilever (PPP-NCHR, Nanosensors, tip radius <10 nm, resonance frequency 330 kHz, and force constant 42 N m−1). Dynamic force mode (tapping mode) was used to minimize physical damage from the cantilever tip, scanning a 256 × 256 pixel image at a rate of 1.25 Hz. The obtained images were post-processed using Gwyddion software. The height of each aggregate was extracted from a 256 × 256 pixel AFM image, and aggregates were categorized into height intervals of 0.5 nm (e.g., 1–1.5 nm, 1.5–2 nm, etc.). Gaussian distribution fitting was applied to the extracted height distributions to quantitatively compare aggregate morphology.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The crystallinity of the pPFDMA was determined through X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Smartlab, Rigaku) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at 45 kV and 200 mA. Grazing-incidence (GID, 2θ) diffraction was performed at an incidence angle of 0.5° to characterize the crystalline structure of pPFDMA films. This measurement provides a penetration depth of tens to hundreds of nanometers, ensuring sensitivity to the nanoscale structure of the polymer layer. The scanning speed was set at 2° min−1 with a step size of 0.02°. Background removal was performed by interpolation, and the data were smoothed with the Savitzky–Golay method in OriginLab. For in-situ XRD measurements, a temperature-controlled heating stage was used to monitor the substrate’s thermal behavior. The θ/2θ method was used for in-situ analysis. Temperature-dependent crystalline evolution was characterized using a heating ramp of 2 °C min⁻¹ with 2 min settling time before scanning. To assess surface reorganization saturation state, samples were heated at identical rates to 80 °C, followed by isothermal treatment for 60 min.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

The surface chemistry was examined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (K-Alpha+, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the Al Kα X-ray source (hv = 1486.6 eV). The pressure was maintained below 10−7 Pa during the analysis. The XPS results were post-processed using CasaXPS software.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

To check the surface topography of the coated sample, the field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, SU8230, Hitachi) was used at a voltage of 7 kV and a current of 10 nA. To examine the cross-section of the coating, a focused ion beam (FIB) system (Helios Nanolab 450 F1, FEI) was utilized under operating conditions of 20 kV and 10 nA.

Environmental scanning electron microscopy (ESEM)

For in-situ observation of the initial nucleation of condensate droplets, environmental scanning electron microscopy (ESEM) was conducted using an FEI Quattro S instrument. Samples were attached to a 45° inclined aluminum-based stub using carbon conductive tape. The stub was placed onto a Peltier stage to precisely control the surface temperature. The vapor pressure and surface temperature were first maintained at 200 Pa and 2 °C, respectively. Droplet nucleation does not occur under these conditions because the vapor pressure is below the saturation pressure at the surface. After reaching a steady state (~5 min), the vapor pressure was gradually increased until droplet formation began, then sustained at 850 Pa. Due to the rapid progression of initial nucleation, nucleation density calculations were performed using data obtained within 1–10 s after the onset of condensation, thereby minimizing droplet coalescence. The beam potential of 10 kV was used to capture the image.

Pure vapor condensation experimental setup and procedure

Condensation heat transfer performance was evaluated using a vacuum-controlled environmental chamber designed to eliminate non-condensable gases that degrade heat transfer efficiency, as shown in Supplementary Note 6. The experimental setup accommodated both flat and tubular sample geometries using identical chamber configurations. A rotary vacuum pump equipped with a liquid-nitrogen cold trap was employed to reduce the chamber pressure to below 0.5 Pa. Water vapor was generated in a sealed stainless-steel reservoir with external band heaters and pressure-regulation systems. The pressure was monitored using a Pirani gauge during pump-down and capacitance transducers during condensation experiments to accurately determine the saturation temperature. Temperature measurements were made with calibrated T-type thermocouples (±0.1 K) at coolant inlet/outlet and surface locations. Coolant was supplied via a temperature-controlled chiller with flow rate monitoring. All sensor data were acquired using synchronized data-acquisition systems and analyzed using the data reduction procedures described in Supplementary Note 6.

For flat samples, specimens were mounted on aluminum cold plates integrated with copper cooling tubes. The cold plate backside was insulated with low-outgassing materials, and samples were attached using high-conductivity copper tape and a PEEK bolt. Surface temperature was measured directly via thermocouples, with all exposed surfaces covered to prevent parasitic condensation. Tube samples (6.35 mm O.D. / 4.55 mm I.D.) were connected via tee fittings, with thermocouples positioned at the flow centerline to minimize wall effects and reduce uncertainties in the heat transfer coefficient.

Condensation visualization employed high-resolution imaging systems: flat samples used telecentric lens systems for computer vision post-processing, while tubular samples used macrophotography systems for dynamic observation. Heat transfer coefficients were calculated from measured temperature differences, flow rates, and thermodynamically determined saturation conditions.

Prior to each run, the chamber interior and all fittings were solvent-cleaned (IPA, acetone), and the exterior walls were heated (40–45 °C) and insulated to prevent unwanted condensation. The sample was mounted through bellows and tube fittings (insulated with Teflon-coated glass fiber and polyimide tape) and instrumented with thermocouples. Water in the vapor reservoir was boiled above 100 °C for 20 min (with the chamber vented to purge dissolved gases), then maintained at 100 °C. The chamber was then sealed, the cold trap was cooled with liquid nitrogen, and the oil rotary pump was run until the pressure fell below 0.5 Pa (an additional hour of pumping minimized outgassing). With the vacuum established, the sample was supplied with water at 8 °C for the flat sample and 25 °C for the tube sample until a steady state (24.5–25.5 °C inlet for 10 min). The pump was isolated and leak-tested using IPA spray. Finally, the vapor valve was opened and the target pressure set via the needle valve. Temperature and pressure were logged every second (LabVIEW) for 30 min (flat samples) or 15 min (tube samples), and the final 10 min of data were averaged to compute heat transfer coefficients.
