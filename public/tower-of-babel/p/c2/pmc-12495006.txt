Evaluating the prognosis of prostate cancer through metabolic pathways
Authors: Qiang Su, Yi Hu, Bin Dai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe pathogenesis of prostate cancer (PCa) is strongly influenced by metabolism. Thus, we explored candidate genes with metabolism-related functions that can be used to predict PCa prognosis.MethodsTo create a training set and two validation sets, RNA data and clinical parameters were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). Risk score (RS) was constructed based on metabolism-related gene signatures. The predictive power of the RS was evaluated. A nomogram related to biochemical recurrence-free survival (BCRFS) was built and evaluated. Finally, an enrichment analysis using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed to identify enriched Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) categories. The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) platform was employed to identify the expression of important genes at the protein level.ResultsFive gene signatures from 860 metabolism-related genes were selected. RS were constructed using the five-gene signatures, which showed high prognostic power for biochemical recurrence (BCR). The nomogram effectively predicted BCRFS. According to GO analysis, DNA damage is primarily associated with genes involved in metabolism. The five-gene signatures were primarily enriched in sulfur metabolic pathways, as analyzed by KEGG. With the progression of prostate cancer malignancy, the expression of HAGHL, and INPP5E also increased.ConclusionThe study establishes a robust, metabolism-based prognostic model for prostate cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03514-y.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most frequently diagnosed cancers among men globally [1]. According to estimates by the American Cancer Society, PCa accounts for 30% of new cancer cases and 11% of cancer-related deaths in men [2]. Despite improvements in PCa care, it is estimated that patients treated with radical therapy are likely to experience biochemical recurrence (BCR) within ten years [3–5]. According to the European Guidelines, an increase in prostate-specific antigen (PSA) beyond 0.2 ng/ml was defined as BCR [6]. To improve the prognosis of patients with BCR, it is important to conduct more intensive follow-ups and utilize a more personalized adjuvant therapy. Early salvage therapy is recommended for patients at high risk for BCR, while low-risk individuals can postpone treatment [7]. The clinical features of the patient, including TNM stage, surgical margin (SM), Gleason score (GS), and PSA levels, should still be considered when making a diagnosis [8]. However, these clinical parameters are not efficient in accurately predicting the progression of PCa [9]. For example, there is a difference in prognosis between patients with similar clinical characteristics.

In addition to their ability to multiply unrestrainedly, tumor cells exhibit distinctive metabolic characteristics compared to normal cells. For instance, different types of cancer are associated with metabolic dysregulation [10]. Meanwhile, there are also differences in progression rates among patients with disease progression, and not all patients who experience progression will develop metastasis or die. Aside from that, metabolic activity could also reflect the biological characteristics of PCa [11]. Therefore, metabolic gene signatures are essential for identifying patients at high risk of developing the disease.

Advances in high-throughput technologies, such as microarrays, have made it possible to construct prognostic gene signatures that are more accurate than traditional clinicopathologic characteristics. These gene signatures can not only serve as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers but also provide clues to the mechanisms underlying BCR. In this study, our objective was to construct and then validate an innovative metabolism-related prognostic model to identify patients who are most likely to experience disease progression. By using univariate Cox regression analysis, metabolic genes associated with BCRFS were identified. Then, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-Cox regression was performed to select predictive metabolic gene signatures to establish a risk score (RS) that can identify high-risk BCR. Furthermore, we analyzed the relationship between RS and PCa progression. The prognostic power of RS was validated in the validation dataset. For clinical application, a nomogram was established to improve risk stratification for BCR by combining RS with clinicopathologic parameters. Lastly, biological processes and signaling pathways were explored through the analysis of selected gene signatures.

Methods

Collecting and preprocessing data

A workflow chart of the statistical analysis in this study was illustrated in Fig. 1. Eligible data sets met the following criteria: (a) biospecimens collected from PCa patients were pathologically confirmed; (b) a minimum of 30 biospecimens was included in each data set; (c) clinicopathological characteristics included age, pathological stage T, and GS. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) prognostic information was missing; (b) metabolism-related gene expression data were missing. Based on screening, it was determined that three datasets would be used for this study. A total of 608 PCa samples (480 cases from TCGA, 36 cases from GSE46602, and 92 cases from GSE70769, used as training and validation sets, respectively) with complete prognostic information were downloaded from the TCGA data portal (https://tcga-data.nci.nih.gov/tcga/) and the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The probe identifiers were annotated with gene symbols using a matching annotation list. In cases where multiple probes mapped to one gene symbol, the relative levels of gene expression were averaged. The corresponding clinicopathological characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Clinical and pathological characteristics of training and validation datasets

Establishment of metabolic gene signatures

Genes associated with metabolism were extracted from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) of c2 (c2.cp.kegg.v7.2.symbols.gmt). Combined with clinicopathological parameters, 860 metabolism-related genes were analyzed relative to their expression values within three datasets. As part of our study, we developed a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model using the R package ‘survival’ to determine independent prognostic factors (http://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/survival/). The ‘glmnet’ algorithm identifies the most significant metabolic genes associated with prognosis after performing LASSO-Cox regression analysis (https://cran.r-project.org/package=glmnet) [12]. A Lasso coefficient weighted with the expression values of each gene signature was used to calculate RS.

RS model validation

According to their respective median values of RS, a low-risk and high-risk BCR group was assigned to the training and validation data sets. Using the R packages ‘survival’ and ‘survminer’, we created K-M survival plots. By using the log-rank test, we evaluated the prognostic capability of RS. Furthermore, heat maps and risk plots were built using the R package ‘pheatmap’ (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pheatmap/). Following this, univariate and multivariate Cox analyses were computed using R packages ‘foreign‘(https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=foreign). For evaluating the predictive power of RS for BCRFS, we used the ‘survivalROC’ R package to construct a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=survivalROC). Using the R package ‘beeswarm’, correlation analysis was performed between RS and clinical factors (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=beeswarm).

Developing a nomogram

In order to determine whether RS and clinical data are predictive for BCR, the Harrell’s Concordance index (C-index), and Area under curve (AUC) are constructed by combining RS and clinical data. The C-index was used to evaluate a compound nomogram for forecasting BCRFS cumulative probabilities first, third, and fifth years in the training set. In the remaining steps, nomograms based on R package ‘rms’ (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=rms) were created that incorporate RS and prognostic clinicopathologic features. Each dataset was evaluated according to the C-index. Lastly, an analysis of the accuracy of the nomogram was completed by building the calibration and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (t-ROC) curves.

GO functional enrichment and KEGG pathway

In the training set, metabolism-related gene expression value matrices were developed for low-risk and high-risk groups. To explore the main functions and pathways involved in metabolism-related genes, GSEA software v4.1.0 (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea) was used for the analysis of KEGG pathways and GO terms analysis. GO analysis was applied to confirm biological process (BP), cellular component (CC) and molecular function (MF). The GSEA results were ranked according to the normalized enrichment score (NES). NES > 1 and P-value < 0.05 were considered significant in our study.

Human protein atlas (HPA) analysis

We evaluated the RS gene expression in prostate tumor tissues categorized as low grade and high grade. Immunohistochemical images of differential protein expression in prostate tissue were obtained using the HPA database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/, accessed on June 23, 2025).

Statistical analyses

In order to perform the statistical analysis, R software (version 3.6.3) was used. In this study, BCRFS was evaluated using the logarithmic rank test and based on the Nearest Neighbor Estimation (NNE) method. Estimated potential predictive properties of BCRFS parameters were derived using multivariate and univariate Cox proportional hazard models. Based on t-ROC curves and AUC values, we assessed the predictive ability of RS for BCRFS in the first, third, and fifth years. As part of a correlation analysis, the Wilcox test was used to compare the two groups. In each cohort, the C-index was calculated to assess RS’s predictive ability. An evaluation of an individual nomogram’s prediction accuracy was conducted using calibration plots, C-indexes, and AUCs. Statistical significance requires a p-value below 0.05.

Results

Biomarker identification for metabolic genes

First, univariate Cox regression analysis was used on expression data of 860 metabolism-related genes in the training set. Secondly, there were 181 candidates identified with a critical p-value below 0.01. With log(λ) = -2.5657, we selected 0.0769 as the optimal λ value (Fig. 2A). To construct RS using LASSO-Cox regression, five genes related to metabolism were selected with non-zero coefficients (HAGHL, PCCA, CYP3A4, CHST13, and INPP5E) (Fig. 2B).

For both univariate and multivariate Cox analyses of BCRFS, the RS was an independent prognostic factor after taking into account clinicopathological characteristics (training set: hazard ratio [HR] = 3.241, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.868–5.623, P < 0.001; validation set: HR = 3.241, 95% CI 1.868–5.623, P < 0.001). Clinical factors (GS and pathological T stage) in the training cohort (n = 480) were significantly related to BCR. On the contrary, neither age nor pathological N stage correlated significantly with BCR (Table 2). In the GSE46602 set ( n = 36), there was a significant association between high preoperative PSA and BCR, while clinical factors (age, PSM, pathological T stage, and GS) failed to identify BCR (Table 3). For the GSE70769 group (n = 92), there was a notable association between high preoperative GS, pT, PSM, RS, and BCR in univariate Cox analysis, whereas clinical factors such as PSA failed to identify BCR (Table 4). Three cohorts experienced significant mortality differences between the two risk groups, according to the survival analysis (Fig. 4(a) and (b)). Based on the t-ROC analysis, it was also found that RS predicted BCRFS strongly. At first, third, and fifth-year follow-up, the AUC ranged from 0.709 to 0.734, 0.568–0.742, and 0.607–0.681 respectively (Fig. 4A and C). Significant variation was revealed between RS and BCR or GS in three datasets(Fig. 4A and C). Of the five new markers, INPP5E proved superior. It had an AUC range of 0.645 to 0.718 in the training set and showed robust diagnostic capability in all three datasets. Its diagnostic effectiveness was nearly equivalent to the conventional Gleason score (Table S1).

Developing and validating nomograms

Among the training set models, the multivariate prognostic module (GS, RS, and pathological T stage) achieved the highest C-index of 0.769, followed by the RS model (0.739) and conventional clinicopathological model (0.705). In validation sets,: the multivariate prognostic module also has the highest C-index of 0.744 (Table 5). In this study, RS was evaluated more favorably than clinicopathological variables for predicting BCR risk. In the training set, the nomogram was constructed using the multivariate prognostic module to enhance risk stratification and facilitate clinical application (Fig. 5(a)). As demonstrated in calibration plots, a nomogram prediction of BCRSF was highly correlated with actual BCRFS after the first, third, and fifth years (Fig. 5(b)). Additionally, a t-ROC plot was also constructed to assess individual nomograms’ prediction power. All BCRFS nomograms demonstrated excellent predictive ability, with AUC values of 0.726, 0.742, and 0.750, respectively Fig. 5(c).

Metabolic-related gene function and pathway analysis

An in-depth analysis of metabolic functions was performed on 860 metabolism-related genes using GO functions and KEGG pathways. Based on the GO enrichment analysis, three categories were identified: BP (Biological Process), CC (Cellular Component), and MF (Molecular Function). The enriched terms comprised 59 BPs, 5 CCs, and 16 MFs. 6 KEGG pathways were also identified. The selected metabolism-related gene signatures were enriched in terms of ‘sulfur metabolism’, ‘cellular response to DNA damage stimulus’, ‘carbon-sulfur lyase activity’, and ‘nuclear protein-containing complex’ (Fig. 6).

Immunohistochemistry results

We confirmed the immunohistochemistry (IHC) representation of four RS genes: HAGHL, PCCA, CYP3A4, and INPP5E. IHC images were obtained from the HPA database. High-grade prostate cancer tissues exhibited stronger immunohistochemical staining for HAGHL and INPP5E than low-grade tissues. Discussion

It is a well-established fact that tumors develop dependencies or addictions to specific metabolic processes, yet few studies have examined PCa progression from this angle. Consequently, identifying metabolic gene signatures associated with PCa is crucial. In addition to gene signature studies that demonstrate their potential as in-depth predictive factors for PCa recurrence, our study nicely complements the metabolic approach [13–16].

This study aimed to evaluate gene expression profiles associated with metabolism in PCa as a predictor of progression. A total of 480 samples from TCGA were used as a ‘training set’, while 36 samples from GSE46602 and 92 samples from GSE70769 served as ‘validation sets’. To identify potential prognostic genes, a univariate Cox analysis and a LASSO-Cox analysis were conducted on the training set. Interestingly, five metabolism-related genes were found to be significantly associated with BCR. Consequently, a polygenic RS was developed, derived from a five-gene signature consisting of HAGHL, PCCA, CYP3A4, CHST13 and INPP5E. These genes were strongly correlated with the BCRFS of PCa patients. Notably, the RS can serve as an effective predictive marker for BCRFS of PCa patients and is significantly correlated with PCa progression. In the BCR group, the RS increases with increasing GS, and it is higher than in the non-BCR group, suggesting that the RS may also serve as a marker for PCa progression. Finally, a model for predicting BCRFS in patients with PCa was constructed using a nomogram.

In training cohorts, BCRFS was independently predicted by RS, pathological T stage, and GS in both univariate and multivariate COX analyses. This study found that a GS of 8–10 is a poor prognostic factor, which is broadly in line with previous studies [17–22]. Additionally, a negative association was observed between age at diagnosis and BCR [23]. However, GS and pathological T stage did not seem to be associated with BCR. The inconsistency might be attributed to participants’ differences in behavioral characteristics and environmental factors. Future studies should place greater emphasis on clinicopathological parameters that incorporate environmental status and patient characteristics.

Several metabolic genes have been reported to be associated with PCa. Among them, CYP3A4 (cytochrome P450 3A4) was identified as a prevalent risk gene in our study. The Cytochromes P450 enzymes are monooxygenases that convert heme into thiols. This enzyme participates in an electron transport pathway that is dependent on NADPH in liver microsomes. In addition to oxidizing biologically active compounds, it can also oxidize structurally unrelated compounds such as steroids, fatty acids, and xenobiotics. CYP3A4 is an enzyme required for abiraterone metabolism, which has been approved by the FDA to treat castration-resistant prostate cancers [24]. CYP3A4 inhibitory compounds may further enhance the therapeutic efficacy of anticancer drugs in DU-145 prostate adenocarcinoma cells [25]. CYP3A4 can be considered prognostic and diagnostic markers of prostate cancer [26]. Genetic variants of CYP3A4 have been reported as contributing to prostate cancer in Korean men [27]. However, negative results for CYP3A4 in the HPA database’s immunohistochemical analysis may be attributed to data limitations. According to HPA results, HAGHL and INPP5E are significantly linked to the development of prostate cancer. PCCA staining is strong in both high-grade and low-grade prostate tumor tissues. The aforementioned conclusions can validate our research outcomes. While the HPA database lacks staining images for CHST13 immunohistochemistry, we intend to carry out experiments in the future to confirm this gene’s expression in prostate cancer. There has been limited research on the roles of HAGHL, PCCA, CHST13, and INPP5E in PCa as compared to CYP3A4. As part of our future research, we are going to verify the relationship between these genes and PCa progression by using prospective surveillance models.

Several studies have investigated the relationship between PCa metabolism and BCR. A recent study has shown that BCR is associated with purine and pyrimidine synthesis pathways [28]. A review reported that the PCa metabolome was characterized by an increased expression of genes involved in the tricarboxylic acid cycle, which could induce new adipogenesis and cholesterogenesis [29]. Further research has indicated that the metabolic pathway may play a key role in PCa progression [11]. These results are consistent with our findings. Collectively, these studies suggest a critical role for a gene signature related to metabolism in the progression of PCa; however, the precise mechanisms and candidate biomarkers necessitate experimental exploration and verification. According to Tang et al., the PRDX5-Nomo model achieved a C-index of 0.726 in the TCGA dataset and 0.75 in the GSE70769 dataset [30]. Our results were 0.769 and 0.744, respectively. Although the two approaches yield similar results, they emphasize different elements. Our analysis focuses on metabolism genes, whereas Tang’s research leans more towards Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS).

In previous studies, KEGG and GO analyses were instrumental in analyzing biological processes [31]. GO annotation was implemented to explore potential biological behaviors of target genes, while KEGG analyses highlighted meaningful biological pathways. In this study, the GO annotation identified ‘cellular response to DNA damage stimulus’ as the key BP with statistical significance. Additionally, cellular component analysis of metabolism-related genes indicated significant involvement in ‘nuclear protein containing complex’. Molecular function analysis demonstrated that carbon-sulfur lyase activity was the key factor. KEGG analysis illustrated that the key genes were enriched in ‘sulfur metabolism’. Ultimately, consistent with previous studies, BCR was significantly associated with the metabolic process [11]. The findings indicated that the biological functions of selected metabolism-related genes are closely correlated with the BCR of PCa. Previous research has found that the sulfur metabolism pathway plays a crucial role in the progression and survival of PCa cells. One of the key components of this pathway is the transsulfuration pathway, which involves the conversion of homocysteine to cystathionine. This conversion is regulated by the enzyme cystathionine-β-synthase (CBS). Recent studies have shown that heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) directly regulates this pathway by binding to the CBS gene and upregulating its mRNA levels. This regulation is significant because targeting CBS has been found to decrease PCa growth and induce tumor cell death, while largely sparing benign prostate cells. Furthermore, the combined inhibition of HSF1 and CBS results in a more pronounced inhibition of PCa cell proliferation and a reduction in transsulfuration pathway metabolites. This suggests that targeting the sulfur metabolism pathway, specifically through HSF1 and CBS, could provide an effective therapeutic strategy against advanced prostate cancer [32].

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the clinical and gene expression data were sourced from public databases rather than our own. Secondly, the study is constrained by its retrospective design and small sample size. Thirdly, the biological functions of selected metabolism-related genes necessitate extensive experimental exploration and validation. Although this study provides a validated prognostic model, the functional roles of HAGHL, PCCA, CYP3A4, CHST13, and INPP5E call for experimental examination. Despite these limitations, our study may hold significant value in predicting progression in PCa.

Conclusions

Generally, the findings of this study have led to the discovery of novel metabolism-related genes that contribute to PCa progression. Clinical characteristics and metabolism-related gene signatures have been used to develop the prognostic nomogram. With its excellent accuracy, the nomogram has a greater predictive value than traditional clinicopathological parameters for risk stratification of PCa patients.
