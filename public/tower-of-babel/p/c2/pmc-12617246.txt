Efficacy of Dietary Cottonseed Bioactive Peptides Compared to Diclazuril or Salinomycin on Reducing Adverse Effects of Coccidiosis Challenge in Broiler Chickens
Authors: Mohammad Reza Yazdani, Farshid Kheiri, Mostafa Faghani, Ali Sharifzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundBioactive peptides present antimicrobial, antioxidative and immunomodulatory activities with beneficial effects on broiler chickens.ObjectivesThis experiment was conducted to examine the influence of dietary supplemental cottonseed bioactive peptides (CBP) on growth performance, oocysts per gram of faeces (OPG), intestinal morphology, immune responses and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) gene expression in broilers exposed to coccidial infection.MethodsA total of 420 broiler chicks (Ross 308) were assigned to seven dietary treatments in a completely randomized design during 10–45 days of age. Experimental treatments included: (1) CON+, broiler chickens without challenge; (2) CON–, birds challenged with coccidiosis; (3–5) the CON– birds fed diets containing 4 g/kg, 5 g/kg or 6 g/kg CBP, respectively; (6) the CON– birds fed diets containing 0.2 g/kg diclazuril; (7) the CON– birds fed diets containing 0.5 g/kg salinomycin.ResultsInfection alleviated daily feed intake and growth of broilers fed a basal diet during 31–38 days of age (p < 0·05), which ameliorated after supplementation of 6 g/kg CBP (p < 0.05). Dietary 4 or 6 g/kg CBP, diclazuril or salinomycin compensated for the impairment in the feed conversion ratio of Eimeria spp. challenged broilers during 31–45 days of age (p < 0.05). Coccidiosis challenge increased OPG; however, the OPG value decreased when the diet contained CBP or anti‐coccidiosis drugs (p < 0.05). Antibody titer against sheep red blood cells improved in challenged birds fed 6 g/kg CBP (p < 0.05).ConclusionsDietary CBP decreased the negative effects of coccidiosis, whereby supplemented birds had similar immune responses or even better growth performance than birds receiving anticoccidials.Keywords: bioactive peptides, broiler chickens, cottonseed, gene expression, growth performance

Introduction

Coccidiosis is one of the most common causes of economic loss in the poultry industry, costing about $11.93 billion annually (Blake et al. 2020). Seven species of Eimeria spp. (Eimeria acervulina, Eimeria brunetti, Ecklonia maxima, Eimeria mitis, Eimeria necatrix, Eremophila praecox and Eimeria tenella) are known to be responsible for coccidiosis in domestic birds. Coccidiosis infection could damage the epithelial cells of broiler chickens and result in intestinal osmotic stress (Kettunen et al. 2001; Perez‐Carbajal et al. 2010), consequently impairing nutrient absorption and disrupting weight gain (Vermeulen et al. 2001). Anticoccidial feed additives prevent coccidiosis in the broiler industry, although the resistance against anticoccidial drugs hampers their effectiveness for poultry production (De Gussem 2007). Furthermore, the European Medicine Agency regulation (EU2019/6) focused on minimizing antimicrobial resistance, including anticoccidial drugs. Despite the global concern over residual drugs in the meat (Martins et al. 2022), the ban on using antimicrobials as growth promoters has declined animal health and growth performance (Wallace et al. 2010). Previous findings have shown that coccidiosis disease is affected by nutritional modulations such as therapeutic or herbal feed additives (Idris et al. 2017). Therefore, another strategy to improve the immune defence against coccidiosis is nutritional modification.

Research on the influence of bioactive peptides in poultry nutrition has attracted much attention over the last decade. Bioactive peptides are protein fragments produced from animal or plant origin by microbial fermentation, enzymatic, alkali or acid hydrolysis (Muir et al. 2013). These active compounds present antimicrobial, antioxidative, immunomodulatory and antihypertensive activities (Hartmann and Meisel 2007; Yang et al. 2004; Zambrowicz et al. 2015). Soybean bioactive peptides were reported to beneficially influence the feed conversion ratio (FCR) in broiler chickens owing to the development of their small intestine morphology, enhanced intestinal enzyme activity and improvements in nutrient digestibility (Abdollahi et al. 2018). Comparably, dietary supplementation of cottonseed bioactive peptides (CBPs) increased the growth performance of broiler chickens due to improved daily feed intake (DFI) and intestinal morphology (Landy et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2022) as well as enhanced absorption of dietary protein following the upregulation of the PepT1 gene (Liu et al. 2022). In addition, bioactive peptides have been shown to increase the proportional weight of lymphoid organs (Abdollahi et al. 2018; Landy et al. 2020) and enhance antibody titer against sheep red blood cells (SRBC) as well as Newcastle disease virus (NDV). Moreover, CBP improved serum immune‐related attributes in yellow‐feathered broilers (Liu et al. 2022). It has been demonstrated that including soybean bioactive peptides in the diet ameliorated the detrimental effect of coccidial challenge on growth performance and immune responses in broiler chickens (Osho et al. 2019). However, information on the effect of CBP compared to anticoccidial drugs on broiler chickens exposed to Eimeria spp. infection is limited. We hypothesized that supplementation of CBP in the diet of broiler chickens may decrease the negative effects of coccidiosis challenge on growth performance and immune responses compared to anti‐coccidiosis substances. Therefore, the current study was conducted to evaluate the effects of CBP (4, 5 and 6 g/kg) compared to diclazuril and salinomycin on growth performance, intestinal morphology, humoral immune responses and IL‐6 gene expression in Eimeria spp. infected broiler chickens.

Material and Methods

Ethics Committee Authorization

All animal procedures were approved by the Ethical Committee of Shahrekord Azad University's Ethical Committee (approval ref no. 2018‐032).

Birds, Diets and Management

A total of 420 one‐day‐old broiler chicks (Ross 308) were obtained from a commercial hatchery, weighed on arrival and used in this experiment. Five replicate pens of 12 chicks each were randomly allotted to seven treatment groups with a completely randomized design across 10–45 days of age. The experimental treatments included broiler chickens without coccidial challenge (CNT+) or with coccidial challenge (CNT−), and five groups of CNT− birds offered with diets containing CBP at either 4, 5 and 6 g/kg using Fortide (Chengdu Mytech Biotech Co. Ltd., Chengdu, Sichuan, China) compared to dietary supplemental 0.2 g/kg diclazuril (Qilucoxid; Rooyan Darou, Pharmaceutical Co., Tehran, Iran) or 0.5 g/kg salinomycin (Salinocox; Rooyan Darou Pharmaceutical Co., Tehran, Iran). Experimental diets were formulated to meet nutrient requirements provided by Ross Manual (Aviagen 2014; Tables 1, 2, 3) across different periods of the experiment and fed in mash form. The CBP, diclazuril and salinomycin were incorporated by partially replacing maize and soybean meal on a weight‐equivalent basis. Chicks housed in wire floor pens (length 120 cm × width 120 cm × height 80 cm) were covered with paper rolls and had free access to feed and water throughout the trial. Each pen was equipped with a separate feeder and a manual drinker. Broilers were kept in a temperature‐controlled house at 32°C from Days 1–7, 29°C from Days 8–14, 26°C from Days 15–21 and 22°C from Day 22 to the end of the trial. The lighting program was considered as 23 h light and 1 h darkness. Birds in the challenge group were separated from those in the no‐challenge group by partitioning the sections with heavy‐duty black contractor nylon sheets (Tolou Plast Persian, Tehran, Iran). This was to prevent cross‐contamination of litter with Eimeria spp. Additionally, entry and exit from each section were strictly controlled and followed a systematic process—from the no‐challenge group to the challenge group and to exit from the facility.

Coccidial Infection

After 14 days from the start of the experiment, all birds except CON− were selected and orally inoculated with a mixture of Eimeria spp. species (E. acervulina, E. tenella, E. maxima and E. necatrix) on Day 24 of age. The non‐challenged group was used to verify the effect of coccidial challenge in the experiment. Each broiler chicken received an oral administration of 1 mL of sporulated Eimeria spp. oocysts (each inoculum contained 100,000 oocysts of E. tenella, E. maxima, E. necatrix and E. acervulina, at the proportions of 8:1.5:0.25:0.25, respectively). Oocysts of the species were acquired from the Laboratory of Parasitology, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, University of Tehran. These fields Eimeria spp. species’ oocysts were isolated from dropping, litter samples, and the intestine of broiler chickens from commercial broiler flocks in Iran. The oocysts were stored in potassium dichromate solution to allow sporulation of the oocysts (Ghasemi et al. 2010).

Data Collection and Sampling

Live weight of broilers was measured in replicates at 10, 17, 24, 31, 38 and 45 days of age to determine daily weight gain (DWG) in different weeks of the experiment. The DFI was calculated by subtracting feed left over from the quantity supplied daily. The FCR is defined as the ratio of the feed intake to weight gain. Mortality was recorded to adjust DFI, DWG and resultant FCR values. On Day 45 of the experiment, two birds that approximated the mean body weight of their respective pen were individually weighed and slaughtered off‐site by exsanguination through severing the carotid arteries and jugular veins. Carcass, breast, thigh, abdominal fat, heart, liver, gizzard, small intestine and cecum were excised, weighed and calculated as a percentage of live body weight.

Morphology of Jejunum

Two birds of each pen were slaughtered on Day 28, and segments of the small intestine were sampled from the jejunum, midway between the point of entry of the bile ducts and Meckel's diverticulum and the ileum. Noted intestinal samples were taken to determine parameters such as villus height and width, crypt depth, villus height: crypt depth ratio and epithelial cell thickness. Segments measuring 1.5 cm in length were flushed with saline solution (1% NaCl) and placed in 100 gL−1 buffered formalin (PH 7.0). The fixed intestinal samples were embedded in paraffin, then sectioned (5 µm) and stained with haematoxylin–eosin. Intestinal samples were examined using an optical microscope (Olympus CX31, Tokyo, Japan). A total of 10 intact, well‐oriented villus–crypt units were selected for each intestinal cross‐section (30 cross‐sections/treatment, for a total of 300 measurements/treatment). The measurement of villus height (µm) was taken from the tip of the villus to the villus crypt junction, and crypt depth was measured from the base upward to the region of transition between the crypt and villus. The ratio of villus height to crypt depth (V/C) was then calculated. The thickness of the epithelial cells was defined as the distance between the base and tip of the epithelial cell. The average values for each cross‐section were used for further analysis.

Lesion Scoring and Faecal Oocyst Measurements

On Day 28 of age, three different sections of the chicken small intestine (i.e., duodenum, jejunum, ileum and caecum) were removed post‐slaughtering and examined for lesions. Lesion scores were observed and recorded based on the methodology of Johnson and Reid (1970). The lesion scoring was based on a scale of 0–4, where 0 corresponds to normal status with no gross lesions, 1 to small scattered petechiae, 2 to numerous petechiae, 3 to extensive haemorrhage and 4 to extensive haemorrhage that gives a dark colour to the caeca intestine.

For oocysts per gram (OPG) of faeces measurements, sampling was conducted by procuring approximately 400‐ to 500‐g excreta samples from each replicate pen. Oocyst quantification was performed on excreta samples from each subgroup and assessed daily from Days 10–14 post‐infection. Random collection of each pen sample was imperative to estimate oocyst excretion accurately. All areas of the pen floor were sampled, and a representative amount of excreta was collected. Samples were collected daily, placed in distinct airtight plastic bags, homogenized thoroughly with a domestic mixer, and kept refrigerated until the total quantity of oocyst counts was ascertained (Christaki et al. 2004). Homogenized samples were subjected to a 10‐fold dilution with tap water followed by an additional dilution using saturated saline solution at a ratio of 1:10. Oocyst counts were evaluated utilizing McMaster chambers and conveyed as the number (×104) of OPG of excreta (Christaki et al. 2004).

Humoral Immunity, Total Serum Antioxidant Capacity and Albumin to Globulin Ratio

On Day 9 of age, the commercially available oil‐adjuvant injectable emulsion against NDV and influenza disease virus (IDV) was used (H9N2 subtype) for vaccinating broiler chicks (VAXXON ND_FLU, IZO SRL) and they were injected subcutaneously with 0.2 mL per chick. Also, chicks were orally vaccinated against Newcastle disease with Avishield ND (La Sota strain, Dechra Co., UK) at 19 days of age. On Day 30, the same wing‐banded birds were subjected to blood collection by syringes containing heparin to avoid blood clot formation to determine antibody titers against IDV and NDV. Serum was separated by centrifugation (2000 × g for 15 min), and antibody titer against IDV and NDV was separately measured using the haemagglutination inhibition method (HI). The HI antibodies were then transformed to log2. On Day 45 of age, two birds were selected randomly from each pen and bled from the wing vein, followed by serum separation achieved through centrifugation at 2000 × g for 15 min (SIGMA 4‐15 Lab Centrifuge, Germany). Total serum antioxidant capacity (T‐AOC) was assayed in serum samples using a BioAssay Systems commercial kit (Re et al. 1999). Moreover, the serum samples were analysed for total protein and albumin concentrations using commercial kits (Pars Azmoon). The globulin concentration within the serum was calculated by subtracting albumin concentration from the total protein concentration in the serum (Abedpour et al. 2017).

Quantitative Real‐Time PCR

On Day 45 of the experiment, two broiler chickens from each pen were slaughtered following 4 h of feed withdrawal. Samples from the hepatic tissue were collected and promptly snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, which was subsequently utilized to assess the mRNA levels of IL‐6. Total RNAs were extracted from the liver using the IRAIZOL reagent (RNA Biotechnology Co., Isfahan, Iran). The quantification of RNA concentration was conducted via a spectrophotometer nano‐drop (MD‐1000) at a wavelength of 250 nm. According to the manufacturer's recommended protocol, complementary (c) DNA was synthesized from 1 µg of RNA samples with a MiScript II RT kit (QIAGENE, Germany). All primers were synthesized and purified by Sigma Company. The GAPDH served as the reference gene to normalize the expression of the target gene. The primer pairs for the amplification of GAPDH cDNA fragments are listed in Table 5. Quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR) was performed to ascertain the levels of inducible myostatin mRNA. Two microliters of the 10‐fold dilution reverse transcription products were employed for PCR in a final volume of 25 µL containing 0.4–0.8 µM primers and 12.5 µL of QuantiTect SYBR Green master mix (Life Technologies, Cat # 4367659). Cycling parameters were as follows: 5 min at 94°C, then 35 cycles at 94°C for 30 s, annealing temperature for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s and extension for 1 min at 72°C. To verify amplification specificity, the PCR products from each primer were subjected to melting curve analysis followed by agarose gel electrophoresis. The final concentration of IL‐6 was computed using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001).

Statistical Analysis

Prior to the execution of the analysis of variance, the normality of the sample data was thoroughly assessed using the UNIVARIATE procedure of SAS 9.2. Because data about faecal oocyst measurement did not align with the assumptions of normality, they were analysed using the non‐parametric Kruskal–Wallis test via SAS 9.2 (SAS Institute Inc.). Data corresponding to the other recorded traits were subjected to analysis of variance procedures appropriate for a completely randomized design using the General Linear Model procedure of SAS 9.2 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Each pen was treated as an experimental unit for various parameters. For all statistical analyses, significance was established at p ≤ 0.05 unless otherwise stated. The Fisher's protected least significant difference test was employed to compare multiple treatment groups. Means were reported alongside their corresponding standard error of means.

Results

Growth Performance and Carcass Measurements

Data on the effect of experimental treatments on pre‐ and post‐challenged growth performance of broiler chickens are summarized in Table 6. The pre‐challenge results show that dietary supplementation of 5 g/kg and 4–6 g/kg CBP improved DFI and DWG of broiler chickens, respectively, compared to the birds fed a basal diet during 10–17 days of age (P<0·05). Additionally, dietary inclusion of CBP decreased the FCR of broiler chickens during 17–24 days of age (p < 0.05). Infection with Eimeria spp. decreased DFI and DWG of broiler chickens fed a basal diet during 31–38 days of age (p < 0.05). Moreover, FCR deteriorated after exposure of birds in the CNT treatment group to coccidiosis during 31–45 days of age (p < 0.05). Impairments in DFI and DWG caused by Eimeria spp. infection ameliorated after dietary supplementation of 6 g/kg CBP (p < 0.05). The detrimental effect of Eimeria spp. infection on the FCR of broilers was minimized when the basal diet was supplemented with salinomycin during 31–45 days of age (p < 0.05). Moreover, dietary supplementation of 4 or 6 g/kg CBP or 0.2 g/kg diclazuril decreased the adverse effects of coccidiosis challenge on FCR during 38–45 days of age (p < 0.05). During the third week of the experiment, Eimeria spp. infection did not affect growth performance, while supplementation of CBP or diclazuril decreased the FCR of broiler chickens (p < 0.05). Likewise, Eimeria spp. infection failed to affect growth performance during the entire rearing period, whereas supplementation of 4 or 6 g/kg CBP decreased the FCR of broiler chickens compared to infected or non‐infected birds that received abasal diet (p < 0.05).

Discussion

In the present experiment, the supplemental in‐feed CBP was compared to diclazuril or salinomycin in order to investigate their effects on broiler chickens without or with coccidial challenge.

Infection with Eimeria spp. impaired growth performance, more evident during 31–45 days of age. It clearly shows that broiler chickens were successfully exposed to coccidiosis disease. Furthermore, results on oocyst shedding verified the Eimeria spp. infection in challenged broilers. Coccidiosis applies its effect through inflammatory conditions in the gut and consequently decreases growth rate (Memon et al. 2021). Deleterious effects of coccidial challenge on DWG and FCR of broiler chickens have been reported in other studies (Memon et al. 2021; Osho et al. 2019). Supplementation of CBP ameliorated the negative impact of the Eimeria spp. challenge on the growth performance of broiler chickens, which was as efficient as diclazuril and salinomycin. The immune‐protective effect of CBP and the following improvement of host defence against Eimeria spp. challenge helps broilers to recover from coccidiosis disease (Osho et al. 2019). Moreover, diclazuril and salinomycin have been shown to exert powerful anticoccidial action (Abdelrahman et al. 2014; El‐Banna et al. 2005). The results of this study agree with the findings of Osho et al. (2019), who reported that soybean bioactive peptides alleviated the negative effects of coccidia challenge in broiler chickens. However, feeding broilers with CBP‐supplemented diets led to comparable or even superior FCR to unchallenged broiler chickens fed a basal diet during the overall experiment. Similarly, the beneficial effects of CBP on DWG and FCR were evident during the pre‐challenge period. In line with the result of the current study, the DWG of broiler chickens was reported to increase when the diet was supplemented with soybean bioactive peptides (Mathivanan et al. 2006). Furthermore, Abdollahi et al. (2018) reported that dietary inclusion of soybean bioactive peptides improved FCR of broiler chickens. Moreover, Nie et al. (2015) observed greater DWG and improved FCR in chickens that received diets containing fermented cottonseed meal during 43–64 days of age and the entire rearing period. Similarly, findings of Sa'adoon and Abbas (2023) indicated the improved DWG and DFI of birds fed 2.5–7.5 g/kg soybean bioactive peptides in their feed. Improvements in productive traits of broilers fed CBP‐included diets might be attributed to enhanced activity of digestive enzymes (Karimzadeh et al. 2016), which led to increased nutrient digestibility (Nie et al. 2015). In contrast to the result of this experiment, Y. Wang, Deng, et al. (2017) showed that fermented CBPs did not affect growth‐related traits of broiler chickens. The contradictory results on the effect of bioactive peptides might be due to different properties and inclusion rates of test products (Mateos et al. 2014).

There was no effect of Eimeria spp. infection on carcass traits of broiler chickens in this study, whereas CBP supplementation to the diet of challenged broilers increased thigh yield. Similar results have been reported by Nie et al. (2015), who observed the increased thigh muscle in birds that received diets that included cottonseed meal fermented by C. tropicalis and S. cerevisiae. Similarly, Sa'adoon and Abbas (2023) reported that 2.5 and 5 g/kg supplementation of soybean bioactive peptide to the feed numerically increased thigh yield compared to broilers fed a basal diet.

Villus height determines the available surface area for digesta absorption (Montagne et al. 2003), while crypt depth is considered a criterion to assess the proliferative potential of the intestine (Iji et al. 2001). Accordingly, V/C is an index to estimate the absorptive capacity of the small intestine (Montagne et al. 2003). Many studies reported adverse effects of coccidial infection on the morphology of the small intestine (Amerah and Ravindran 2015; Laika and Jahanian 2017). In the current experiment, numerical reduction in villus height and width and lower epithelial thickness were observed in challenged chickens fed a basal diet. The Eimeria spp. infection is associated with noxious effects on the intestinal morphology, which diminishes the absorptive surface, and nutrient absorption (Ruff and Edgar 1982). Dietary inclusion of CBP or anti‐coccidiosis substances alleviated negative effects of infection on gut morphology of broiler chickens. These results show that bioactive peptides induce beneficial effects on gut integrity and consequently mitigate performance reduction (Bao et al. 2009; Wen and He 2012) as efficiently as diclazuril or salinomycin. Consistent with this experiment's results, Osho et al. (2019) reported that dietary supplementation with 4 g/kg soybean bioactive peptides ameliorated the negative effects of coccidial challenge on the jejunum morphology of broiler chickens. Furthermore, Karimzadeh et al. (2016) found increased villus height and V/C in birds receiving diets containing canola bioactive peptides.

Lesion scoring is a significant metric for assessing the pathogenic potential of Eimeria spp. parasites (Allen et al. 2000; Yvorĕ et al. 1980). The drop in the lesion score in birds who received diets containing CBP might be due to modulation of gut microflora and improvement of host immune responses (D. Wang et al. 2009; Z. Wang, Wang, et al. 2017). This agreed with the results of Muneeb et al. (2025), who found a reduction in lesion scores of broilers received diets containing antimicrobial peptides. In line with our expectations, the challenge with coccidiosis increased OPG value in broiler chickens. Results of the present experiment are consistent with those reported by El‐Banna et al. (2005), who expressed diminishing effects of water‐soluble or diclazuril on OPG excretion in broiler chickens. Comparable results for diclazuril and salinomycin were observed in the work of other researchers (Behnamifar et al. 2019; El‐Sawah et al. 2020). On the other hand, information on the effect of bioactive peptides on OPG of challenged broiler chickens is limited. Reduction in OPG value in the faeces of broiler chickens fed CBP‐containing diet in this study shows a healthier gut condition with greater resistance against Eimeria spp. infection compared to unchallenged broiler chickens. Although fewer OPG shed was observed in salinomycin‐administered broilers, CBP could be as effective as diclazuril to combat OPG shedding.

In the present experiment, dietary inclusion of CBP and anticoccidials retrieved numerical reduction in antibody titer against SRBC after coccidial challenge. Following our results, Osho et al. (2019) reported that including soybean bioactive peptides in the diet improved plasma levels of IL‐6, TNF‐α and IgA in broiler chickens infected with Eimeria spp. Bioactive peptides increase the immune response of broiler chickens, probably through their immune‐stimulating traits. It is believed that the underlying effects of bioactive peptides applied through direct stimulation of Toll‐like receptors lead to the production of certain cytokines associated with immune responses (Osho et al. 2019). Moreover, Landy et al. (2020) reported the increased antibody titer against SRBC and NDV in response to dietary inclusion of CBP. On the other hand, T‐AOC increased in birds fed diets containing CBP, suggesting that improved immunity could be partly due to enhanced T‐AOC. Generally, the findings of previous studies show that small peptides increase antioxidant enzyme activity and improve antioxidant capacity in the animal body (Moure et al. 2006). Liu et al. (2022) reported that dietary supplementation of CBP increased serum T‐AOC in broiler chickens fed diets supplemented with 3% CBP. Antioxidant properties of CBP may be applied through certain electron donors so that they react with free radicals and generate a more stable product to terminate the free radical chain (Liu et al. 2022). In line with our results, Landy et al. (2020) reported increased T‐AOC in broilers fed diets added with up to 5 g/kg CBP.

Interleukins play a crucial role in the activation and regulation of immune cells in the body's immune responses and contribute to maintaining homeostasis (Van de Veerdonk et al. 2011). Coccidial challenge did not affect gene expression of IL‐6 in this study, which is against the results that reported the augmented IL‐6 gene expression in response to the Eimeria spp. challenge (Liu et al. 2022; Osho et al. 2019). However, supplementation of 6 g/kg CBP to the diet of birds infected with coccidiosis increased the expression of the IL‐6 gene compared to broiler chickens fed a basal diet. On the contrary, Osho et al. (2019) reported that dietary inclusion of 4 g/kg soybean bioactive peptides attenuated IL‐6 gene expression in Eimeria spp.‐infected broiler chickens, suggesting an immune‐stimulating effect of soybean bioactive peptides. In agreement with the results of this experiment, Liu et al. (2022) found that 3% dietary supplementation of CBP increased serum IL‐6 concentration. According to the results of the current research, CBP may affect IL‐6 at the gene level, but based on contradictory reports, more investigations in this field are warranted.

Conclusion

In conclusion, coccidial challenge impaired the growth performance of broiler chickens and increased oocyst excretion in their faeces. The negative effects of coccidiosis were ameliorated following dietary supplementation of CBP so that supplemented birds performed similarly or even better than birds receiving anticoccidials. This effect was in part related to promotions in the intestinal morphometric features and also could be attributed to the enhanced activity of digestive enzymes. Dietary inclusion of CBP retrieved the numerical adverse effect of coccidiosis challenge on the antibody titer against SRBC. Increased serum T‐AOC in reaction to CBP‐added diets improved the immune response of broiler chickens. Expression of IL‐6 was not influenced by Eimeria spp. infection, but feeding diets supplemented with 6 g/kg CBP increased IL‐6 gene expression, suggesting that CBP might apply an inflammatory‐related influence at the gene level.
