Latissimus Dorsi Transfer Combined With Dynamic Biceps Rerouting for Massive Irreparable Rotator Cuff Tear
Authors: Yue Geng, Zhuangdai Zhang, Ye Tian, Pengfei Liu, Yu Liu, Xusheng Bo, Jiangtao Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Treating patients with irreparable massive rotator cuff tears, particularly those with loss of external rotation and active elevation function, has always been a challenging clinical problem. Latissimus dorsi transfer has shown long-term clinical reliability, especially as a preferred surgical option for relatively young patients. However, isolated tendon transfer appears insufficient to restore the range of motion in patients with bidirectional deficits. There are various surgical techniques using the long head of the biceps tendon to mimic superior capsular reconstruction, among which dynamic biceps rerouting is a superior capsular reconstruction with more tension that can reconstruct the balance of vertical pairs acting on the shoulder. Latissimus dorsi transfer combined with dynamic biceps rerouting can compensate for the bidirectional functional deficiencies of the rotator cuff and minimize the risk of latissimus dorsi tendon rupture and related complications.

Massive irreparable rotator cuff tears (MIRCTs) may represent as many as 20% to 40% of total rotator cuff tears, and often because of severe tendon degeneration and fatty infiltration, the native tendons cannot be directly repaired to the greater tuberosity.1 Most patients experience severe functional loss, especially the combined loss of active elevation and external rotation, and consequently bidirectional pseudoparesis or pseudoparalysis.2 If left unrepaired, in time, the humeral head displacement will increase the abnormal loading of the joint surfaces and then lead to irreversible arthritis of the shoulder joint.3 At present, reverse total shoulder arthroplasty is an effective option for MIRCTs, but for young and relatively active patients, tendon transfer surgery may be a good alternative4 (Table 1).Table 1Indications and ContraindicationsIndications Chronic posterosuperior rotator cuff tendon tear with retraction to level of glenoid Intact subscapularis tendon and function Combined loss of active forward flexion and external rotation Rotator cuff arthropathy of stage 1-2 per Hamada classification Stage 3 or 4 fatty infiltration of posterosuperior rotator cuff musculature Young and relatively active patientContraindications Subscapularis tear of type III or higher per Lafosse classification Loss of active adduction or internal rotation Rotator cuff arthropathy of stage 3 or higher per Hamada classification Deltoid dysfunction Permanent and complete axillary nerve palsy Poor compliance with rehabilitation

This article describes a surgical procedure for arthroscopic latissimus dorsi transfer (LDT) at the infraspinatus footprint combined with dynamic biceps rerouting (DBR) with lateral decubitus positioning for the treatment of MIRCT. DBR can assist in repairing supraspinatus tendon tears while providing a greater ability to lower the humeral head.5 This combined surgical technique aims to maximize the restoration of shoulder biomechanical balance and minimize the risk of associated complications. Surgical Technique

This article focuses on DBR combined with arthroscopic LDT. Our surgical technique is presented in Video 1.

Anesthesia and Patient Setup

The surgical procedure is performed with the patient under general and locoregional anesthesia (interscalene block). The patient is placed in the lateral decubitus position (Fig 1A),with the body leaning backward by 20° to 30° and the affected limb abducted by 45° to 60° and flexed forward by 15° to 35°, with a traction weight of 3 to 5 kg. This position facilitates the transition from an arthroscopic portal to an open axillary portal. The advantage is that the patient’s head does not interfere with the operation and reduces the risk in patients with hypotension, bradycardia, and so on. In addition, during tendon harvest, the affected limb can be kept in the proper overhead position without the use of an arm holder (Fig 1B).Fig 1Patient positioning for arthroscopy-assisted latissimus dorsi transfer and dynamic biceps rerouting: right shoulder in lateral decubitus position. Protocol Approval

Step 1: Creation of Operational Portal and Diagnostic Arthroscopy

First, a posterior portal, 1 cm below the standard posterior portal, and standard anteroinferior and anterosuperior portals are created (Fig 2A). This posterior portal is convenient for debridement of the subdeltoid bursa and the interval between the teres minor and posterior deltoid. Arthroscopy is used to evaluate pathology to confirm the indications for latissimus dorsi (LD) tendon and long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) transfer (Fig 2B). After subacromial bursectomy and resection of the remaining rotator interval (RI) using a shaver and a radiofrequency device, the retracted rotator cuff is fully released and the base of the coracoid is exposed to avoid postoperative pain and stiffness (Fig 2C). The greater tuberosity is skeletonized, the receptor transfer area is identified, and a new bicipital groove is created (Fig 2D).Fig 2Creation of operational portal and diagnostic arthroscopy. A posterolateral working portal is established, and the subdeltoid bursa and the interval between the teres minor and posterior deltoid are cleared to create the LDT passage space. Dissection continues medially, and the infraspinatus fascia must be widely opened. The vertical fibers of the long head of the triceps are dissected in an inferomedial orientation until the horizontal fibers of the teres major are visualized (Fig 2E). A knot pusher is inserted with stitching from the posterior portal, with care taken to avoid injury to the axillary nerve, for future LDT passage (Fig 2F).

Step 2: LD Tendon Harvesting Through Arthroscopic and Axillary Small Incisions

An accessory portal is established between the anterosuperior portal and the anterosuperior-distal portal to perform progressive debridement to expose the LD tendon. Along the course of the LHBT, the superior border of the pectoralis major muscle and the circumflex vessels (the 3 sisters) can be identified (Fig 3 A and B); below the 3 sisters is the inferior border of the subscapularis tendon. Just below the inferior border of the subscapularis tendon, the superior border of the LD tendon is visible. If necessary, the pectoralis major superior insertion can be appropriately released to fully release the inferior insertion of the LD tendon. Care must be taken during the release to avoid damaging the axillary nerve and radial nerve; both nerves can be visualized under arthroscopy (Fig 3C). The medial and lateral borders of the LD tendon insertion part are flattened using 2 high-strength suture threads of different colors (Fig 4E). The wire loop previously placed in the knot pusher is taken out. The high-strength suture pulls the LD tendon proximally between the triceps and deltoid through the wire loop (Fig 4F), with care taken to visually prevent the LD tendon from twisting during transfer.

Step 3: Arthroscopic Transport and Fixation of LHBT and LD tendon

Returning to the arthroscopic position, the surgeon uses an suture grasper through the anterior portal to retrieve and tighten the suture limbs to confirm the intra-articular excursion of the LD (Fig 5A). The transverse humeral ligament is released, and the LHBT is exposed (Fig 5B). The transfer location for the LHBT is selected based on the remaining supraspinatus tendon, and a longitudinal groove is created in the insertion area of the supraspinatus tendon, whose depth and width can accommodate the LHBT (Fig 5C). The receptor site for the biceps transfer is 1 to 2 cm behind the intertubercular groove. If the tension of the remaining supraspinatus tendon in the front is high, the receptor site for the biceps transfer is 1 cm behind the intertubercular groove. If the tension of the remaining posterior supraspinatus tendon is high, the receptor position for the biceps transfer is 2 cm behind the intertubercular groove. The surgeon should confirm that the LHBT can be pulled out of the native groove and located in the new LHBT groove. An anchor (Rejon, Zhejiang, China) is placed in the new LHBT groove, the sutures are passed through the LHBT, and the LHBT is firmly fixed in the new LHBT groove (Fig 5D). Rotator cuff repair can be performed using this anchor or new anchors. After repair of the remaining supraspinatus tendon and successful transposition of the LHBT, the transfer target location of the LDT and the future anchor position are determined. The different-colored sutures are inserted into the anchor bolt (Arthrex, Naples, FL). The anchor nail is placed at the target position; the remaining stitching is tightened at the same time.

Postoperative Care and Rehabilitation

A compressive bandage is applied to the axillary fold. The patient uses an abduction pillow to maintain 60° of abduction and neutral rotation for 6 weeks. Active exercises begin 6 weeks after the operation. Magnetic resonance imaging should be performed 2 to 3 months after the operation according to the patient’s condition to confirm the healing of the tendon. The rehabilitation protocol should be adjusted based on rotator cuff healing.

Discussion

MIRCT has always been a significant treatment challenge. According to the general principles guiding tendon transfer, an isolated tendon transfer might not be effective in restoring the bidirectional deficit. A single tendon should be transferred for a single function.6

LDT is a well-established method for the treatment of massive irreparable posterosuperior cuff tears in young patients without significant glenohumeral arthritis, which can significantly improve shoulder function and relieve pain.7,8 Gerber et al.9 have confirmed that treatment of posterosuperior rotator cuff tears with LDT shows good long-term efficacy. Moreover, biomechanical studies by Burkhart et al.10,11 have confirmed the significant role of maintaining the balance of force pairs acting on the shoulder, stabilizing it in the transverse plane. To rebalance the force pairs acting on the shoulder in the transverse plane, the LD tendon transfer is performed at the infraspinatus footprint (posterior) instead of the supraspinatus footprint (superior). At the same time, this footprint reduces the transfer distance of the LD, and the use of all-suture anchors for fixation reduces the risk of tendon rupture.12

DBR is a dynamic superior capsular reconstruction with more tension.5 DBR can reconstruct the balance of vertical pairs acting on the shoulder. A biomechanical study by Han et al.13 has confirmed that the biceps rerouting technique can restore shoulder stability without overconstraining range of motion. At the same time, the rerouted LHBT can be used as a decompression device and internal splint for the humeral head and can protect rotator cuff healing.14 Many studies have found that the retear rate of patients receiving biceps rerouting is significantly lower than that of those treated conventionally.15,16 These studies have shown that biceps rerouting is an important method for treating MIRCT and restoring the stability of the shoulder.

The described surgical procedure addresses the bidirectional deficiency of the rotator cuff using an arthroscopy-assisted LDT with DBR. In addition, the use of the lateral decubitus position in this technique has the advantage of easily exposing the axillary incision and releasing the LD tendon. At the same time, this position reduces the surgical risk for the patient. Compared with single-tendon transplantation, this surgical procedure restores the bidirectional mechanical balance.17 For other surgical methods (reverse shoulder arthroplasty18 and combined subacromial balloon spacer placement19)—especially subacromial balloon spacer placement (in which the deployment of a balloon spacer can reduce subacromial friction during shoulder abduction by lowering the head of the humerus and facilitating humeral gliding against the acromion during movement20)—this technique not only reduces the use of extracorporeal grafts (prostheses or balloons) and lowers the risk of rejection and infection but also eliminates the risk of ineffective results due to balloon displacement or rupture, as well as the need for reoperation to adjust or remove the balloon.21,22 Moreover, there is limited evidence regarding the clinical safety and long-term benefits of using subacromial balloon spacers.

In conclusion, this study presents arthroscopic LDT at the infraspinatus footprint combined with DBR with lateral decubitus positioning. The aim is to compensate for the bidirectional functional deficiencies of the rotator cuff and minimize the risk of LDT rupture and related complications.
