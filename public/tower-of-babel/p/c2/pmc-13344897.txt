Relationships between longitudinal retinal amyloid imaging and amyloid PET in the A4 Trial
Authors: Sonal Sukreet, Michael C. Donohue, Jennifer Ngolab, Alison Belsha, Jennifer Salazar, Paula Cohen, Sandhya Jaiswal, Veasna Tan, Neelum T. Aggarwal, Jessica Alber, Ken Johnson, Gregory A. Jicha, Christopher H. van Dyck, Srinath Ramanan, James J. Lah, Stephen Salloway, Steven R. Verdooner, Michael S. Rafii, Paul S. Aisen, Reisa A. Sperling, Robert A. Rissman

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAlzheimer's disease (AD) is associated with retinal amyloid‐related changes, which may help identify amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) positive (+) individuals. Previously, in a small cross‐sectional study, we reported higher retinal spot counts (RSCs) in preclinical amyloid PET (+) individuals screened for the Anti‐Amyloid Treatment in Asymptomatic Alzheimer's Disease (A4) trial compared to control individuals enrolled in the Longitudinal Evaluation of Amyloid Risk and Neurodegeneration (LEARN) trial before drug treatment.METHODSEligible volunteers had retinal scans 48 hours after consuming curcumin. Scans were processed and quantified via NeuroVision. Participants were grouped by amyloid status and treatment to assess the effect of solanezumab on RSC.RESULTSRSC did not differ significantly over time between groups and was not modified by treatment, diverging from the cross‐sectional retinal amyloid findings observed in A4/LEARN.DISCUSSIONCurcumin‐based retinal amyloid labeling shows promise but needs standardized protocols and validation in larger cohorts to understand its relationship to amyloid PET.

INTRODUCTION

Early diagnosis of age‐dependent neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's disease (AD), the leading cause of dementia in the United States, could provide more time to treat and impede disease‐associated cognitive loss. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Anti‐amyloid therapeutics have been shown to significantly slow the rate of cognitive decline in individuals with early symptoms of AD. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Part of the success of these trials lay in the use of biomarkers relevant to AD pathology such as amyloid beta (Aβ) and phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) to screen at‐risk individuals independent of clinical symptoms. 8 Great strides have been made in developing brain imaging, such as positron emission tomography (PET) and biofluid assays, to reliably detect AD biomarkers and assign AD status. 1 Blood‐based biomarkers exhibit high validity and accuracy and are included as recommended assays in the Alzheimer's Association's updated clinical practice guidelines. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 No single biomarker assay can provide a definitive diagnosis without additional assessment; yet, a combination of biomarker tests can enhance diagnostic accuracy and specificity. 16 Additionally, the current assays may have limited accessibility to medical professionals (mass spectrometry, brain imaging), be invasive to participants (cerebrospinal fluid [CSF] and blood), or be susceptible to metabolic processes (CSF and blood). Therefore, testing additional diagnostic assays across different modalities could provide valuable insights into AD progression and improve overall diagnostic accuracy.

The brain and neurosensory retina exhibit similarities in function, structure, and origin, 17 , 18 , 19 suggesting diseases related to the brain may manifest in the retina. Furthermore, the eye's structure enables real‐time monitoring of AD‐susceptible components, such as neural tissue and blood vessels. Cross‐sectional studies in AD populations have reported retinal abnormalities similar to AD pathology, including vascular changes, thinning of retinal layers, and retinal deposits. 20 , 21 , 22 Histological analyses reported detecting Aβ deposition in post mortem AD retinal tissue, suggesting that retinal alterations are independent of aging alone. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 While detection of retinal Aβ is not universal across studies, complementary biophysical and biochemical tests support the presence of Aβ in the AD retina. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 Further studies on AD retinal tissue revealed pericyte loss and activated microglia, while proteomics analysis of post mortem AD retinas identified increased inflammatory markers, similar to those observed in the AD brain. 27 , 34 , 35 Subsequent human studies have indicated increased retinal deposits hypothesized to be related to Aβ, observed through confocal scanning laser ophthalmoscope in both preclinical and prodromal AD populations. 36 , 37 Visualized retinal deposits have been associated with decreased brain volume, both in the hippocampus and overall gray matter. 37 , 38 The combination of vascular analysis and retinal deposit quantification is associated with verbal recall. 39 In summary, these findings position the retina as an accessible, non‐invasive AD biomarker that could facilitate proper diagnosis and evaluate therapeutic efficacy.

With advances refining existing retinal imaging modalities to detect AD‐associated infarctions, defining a context of use (COU) would help solidify the eye as an AD biomarker. 21 , 22 A recent meta‐analysis of cross‐sectional retinal imaging studies in AD biomarker‐confirmed individuals found associations of retinal thinning and vascular reduction with AD, suggesting that eye‐based AD biomarkers could support an AD diagnosis. 40 Next steps in developing this COU include understanding how AD‐associated retinal alterations change over time. Another potential COU could use the eye's structure to monitor disease progression and/or therapeutic efficacy. Few longitudinal retinal trials have reported retinal changes over time in the retinal vasculature between experimental and control groups, although the affected regions differ across studies. 41 , 42 In conjunction with the Anti‐Amyloid Treatment in Asymptomatic Alzheimer's Disease (A4) and Longitudinal Evaluation of Amyloid Risk and Neurodegeneration (LEARN) studies, we conducted a longitudinal assessment of curcumin‐labeled spots in the retinas of high‐amyloid PET individuals and their amyloid‐low counterparts. 43 Building on baseline measurements taken before the start of the trial, we scanned participants’ retinas annually for the duration of the study. 36 To measure the effects of drug‐associated retinal amyloid, we analyzed the number of retinal spots in individuals receiving solanezumab versus placebo. The findings from this study provide further insight into the potential utility of retinal imaging as an AD biomarker and contribute to the growing body of research evaluating non‐invasive techniques for early detection of AD pathology.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We reviewed the literature using PubMed for studies evaluating retinal amyloid detection as a biomarker for Alzheimer's disease (AD). Histopathological studies and small cross‐sectional imaging investigations suggest that amyloid beta can be detected in the retina and may correspond to cerebral amyloid burden measured by positron emission tomography (PET). However, longitudinal evidence assessing whether retinal amyloid measures track disease progression or respond to anti‐amyloid treatment in large, well‐characterized cohorts remains limited. Interpretation: In the Anti‐Amyloid Treatment in Asymptomatic Alzheimer's Disease (A4) and Longitudinal Evaluation of Amyloid Risk and Neurodegeneration (LEARN) trials, curcumin‐enhanced retinal imaging was applied longitudinally in amyloid PET positive and negative individuals, including participants treated with solanezumab. Despite prior cross‐sectional findings showing increased fluorescent retinal spots in amyloid‐positive individuals, longitudinal analyses demonstrated no significant differences over time between groups and no treatment‐related effects. These results suggest that retinal fluorescent spots did not reliably reflect cerebral amyloid status or therapeutic response. Future directions: Future studies should validate retinal biomarkers in larger cohorts, refine imaging methodologies, and evaluate their utility alongside established AD biomarkers.

METHODS AND MATERIALS

Study design

Details on the A4 and LEARN trials, as well as the recruitment process for the main trials and the current retinal study, have been previously published. 43 , 44 Briefly, recruitment into the retinal imaging addendum was voluntary, and A4 and LEARN volunteers were briefed on the trial's details before providing informed consent. Exclusion criteria included any condition exacerbated by fundus imaging, a curcumin allergy, or any condition deemed grounds for exclusion by the principal investigators. A total of 42 participants were initially recruited for the current study. Amyloid PET standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) levels and thresholds were obtained from the parent A4/LEARN trials. Details on the curcumin dosing protocol and retinal scan procedure have been previously published. To enhance the target signal, participants consumed 80 g of curcumin before the scan session. Starting 2 days before each visit, participants ingested one packet (20 g) of Longvida curcumin twice daily, along with an Ensure nutritional drink, for a total of 40 g per day. Retinal scan sessions were included in the site visit tests at the end of the session. Using the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)‐approved RETIA 2 imaging system, both eyes were scanned unless cataracts obstructed retinal visualization. The central and superior retinal regions were imaged 18 times per eye, capturing six autofluorescent images at three distinct points. Each eye was scanned for at least 180 seconds, with 90 seconds allocated to each region.

Study sites

Eleven A4 sites participated in the current retinal imaging study: (1) Alzheimer's Therapeutic Research Institute, (2) Rush University Medical Center, (3) George & Anne Ryan Institute for Neuroscience and the University of Rhode Island, (4) University of Kentucky Sanders‐Brown Center on Aging, (5) Alzheimer's Disease Research Unit, (6) Yale School of Medicine, (7) Emory University, (8) Butler Hospital, (9) Warren Alpert Medical School of Brown University, (10) Alpert Medical School and the Center for Alzheimer's Research and Treatment, (11) Brigham and Women's Hospital.

Retinal scan analysis

All retinal scans were processed using NeuroVision Imaging, Inc.’s proprietary automated image analysis software, which assesses scan usability. The image analysis approach implemented here is also substantially equivalent in methodology to the retinal autofluorescence measurement approach used in an FDA‐cleared device (K181501). 45 Factors influencing overall scan quality included focus, contrast, illumination variation, eye motion, fixation, and obstruction. Eight high‐quality scans were selected for quantification of retinal spots. Retinal amyloid spots (RSC) were identified using an automated image processing pipeline previously described in detail. 36 , 37 Briefly, spots were defined using a fixed intensity threshold based on the minimum pixel intensity relative to the local background intensity, such that a “spot” could comprise a single pixel or a cluster of contiguous pixels that met the thresholding criteria. The measurement region was standardized and centered on the fovea to ensure consistency across participants. Readers are referred to the prior publication for a full description of the spot‐definition criteria, segmentation methodology, and measurement repeatability data. 36 , 37

Statistical analysis

Spaghetti plots were generated for each eye to show retinal spot counts for each study/treatment group as a function of the weeks elapsed since baseline measurements. Means and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for RSC by group were also plotted across study visits, accounting for eye side (left vs. right eye). Linear mixed models (LMMs) for spot counts were developed that incorporated participant‐specific random intercepts and slopes. LMMs were selected to maintain consistency with prior analyses of retinal imaging outcomes and to facilitate estimation of longitudinal trends. An LMM was chosen over a generalized LMM because it provides greater estimation stability and reduces the risk of numerical convergence failure that can occur with overparameterized models in small, unbalanced datasets.

The first model used fixed effects for time‐by‐treatment, age, apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 status, education, baseline amyloid PET SUVR, and sex. The second model accounted for study groups (A4 vs. LEARN) and subgroups (A4: solanezumab vs. placebo), with an additional fixed effect to account for baseline differences between treatment groups. We estimated the Pearson correlation coefficient separately for the following: (1) between the first retinal scan and the baseline amyloid PET Centiloid (CL), (2) in aggregate, and (3) within each treatment arm. We also estimated the correlation between the last amyloid PET scan with last retinal scan for each individual. Imaging data were collected across multiple sites using standardized acquisition protocols to minimize inter‐site variability. Site‐related variability was further evaluated during each model development and we used the Akaike information criterion (AIC) to select the preferred model. Additional details on the statistical analysis used in this paper have been previously published. 36

DISCUSSION

Extending the findings of our previous study, 36 we investigated longitudinal changes in fluorescent spots detected in the retina and their relationship with amyloid pathology, as measured by RSC and CL in high‐ and low‐amyloid PET scans from the A4 and LEARN cohorts. Additionally, we accounted for solanezumab's effects on retinal fluorescent spots within the A4 group. Neither time (Figures 1, 2) nor treatment (Figure 3) mediated any significant change in RSC between the control and experimental groups. While trends emerged between eyes, variation within the samples hindered any definitive conclusions (Figure 1B). Despite previous research suggesting that retinal amyloid accumulation correlates with cerebral amyloid levels in AD, our findings did not demonstrate a significant association between RSC and PET CL values across the cohorts. Hence, the current retinal imaging approach used in our study is insufficient to distinguish between amyloid‐positive and amyloid‐negative individuals, and further refinement is required to establish it as a reliable biomarker for AD.

One major limitation of the current study is its small sample size, which may have contributed to the wide variation in RSC. While the main A4 and LEARN trials accumulated a high number of participants, several factors contributed to the low numbers in the retinal imaging component. The raw images taken from the test sites undergo quality control, with eight scans selected to undergo further quantification. 36 , 37 , 39 When certain eyes failed to meet quality‐control standards, they were excluded from the analysis, accounting for the missing retinal scans (Table 2). Also, because participation in the retinal imaging substudy was voluntary, not all individuals enrolled in A4 were included, which contributed to the relatively small sample size. As participants drop out over time, which is common in longitudinal studies, this further reduced the number of available observations. Together, these factors may have introduced bias related to missing data and should be considered when interpreting the robustness and generalizability of the findings. Low participant retention throughout the study's duration also contributed to the low sample size and wide variation (Figure 1A). Differences in demographic characteristics, such as age, sex, and APOE distribution, particularly the predominance of younger age and males in the A4 solanezumab group and the ε4/ε4 carriers in the placebo group, may have influenced treatment group comparisons independently of the drug effect and therefore introduced potential bias in interpreting outcomes. Additionally, the wider Cl observed in LEARN suggests greater variability and reduced precision of the estimate, which may again reflect factors such as a smaller sample size, greater participant heterogeneity, or the underlying baseline imbalances.

Furthermore, administration of solanezumab did not significantly impact retinal amyloid deposition over time, aligning with previous findings that it neither slowed cognitive decline nor reduced amyloid plaque burden in a preclinical AD cohort. 43 , 44 Interestingly, while the A4 cohort exhibited higher cerebral amyloid levels compared to the LEARN cohort, as evidenced by significantly elevated SUVR and CL values, they did not translate into corresponding increases in retinal amyloid deposition. This discrepancy suggests that retinal amyloid accumulation may not directly mirror cerebral amyloid pathology because the retina may represent a brain region rather than the whole brain or that current retinal imaging techniques lack the sensitivity to detect subtle changes. Consideration should also be given to whether unilateral retinal pathology or inter‐eye differences in image quality warrant separate analyses of each eye or a combined analysis. Disease progression may differ temporally or regionally between eyes, and averaging measurements when both eyes are available could potentially provide a more stable estimate of retinal change. However, in this study, individual eyes were analyzed separately to avoid masking potentially meaningful asymmetry or early unilateral changes that may be clinically relevant. This approach allows greater sensitivity for detecting eye‐specific pathology, though it may also introduce additional variability that should be acknowledged when interpreting the results. Meta‐analysis highlighted that while several retinal biomarkers are associated with AD, small study sizes and heterogeneity in imaging methods and reporting hinder the determination of their utility as AD biomarkers. 40 The field is still relatively new, and committees have been formed to determine best practices. Future studies with larger, more diverse cohorts and standardized imaging protocols may help elucidate whether retinal amyloid accumulation reliably indicates disease progression. Additionally, integrating other retinal biomarkers, such as vascular changes or neurodegenerative markers, may provide further insights into the relationship between retinal and cerebral pathology in AD. 21 , 22

The inconsistency of the RSC between eyes in both amyloid PET status comparisons (Figure 2C‐D) and especially A4 treatment comparisons (Figure 3E‐F) may be explained by inherent differences between eyes. In our previous study, we observed high within‐subject variation of RSC between eyes, with no discernible preference for either side. 36 Retinal diseases can manifest unilaterally, although the reasons for this differential susceptibility are poorly understood. The wide range of retinal spots may be partially explained by biological variance, which a greater sample population can mitigate. Additionally, differences in ocular pathology and individual variability in retinal structure could further confound measurements. 46 While we did not observe a significant effect of time on RSC, the moderate negative correlation in the left eye of the solanezumab group at the last scan session suggests a possible treatment‐related effect that warrants further investigation, and longer follow‐up may be necessary to determine whether this finding represents a sustained or clinically meaningful change over time. Previous studies have indicated that systemic anti‐amyloid therapies may impact retinal pathology, although the implications of these effects remain unclear. 47 , 48 The observed weak negative trends in the retinal spots over time in the solanezumab and placebo groups suggest that retinal AD pathology may progress independently from cerebral AD pathology. 47 , 48

Also, here RSC were in the hundreds, which is numerically higher than values reported in prior studies, in which counts in the range of 20 to 50 have been described. These differences are likely attributable to methodological variation across studies, including differences in camera field of view, image resolution, segmentation algorithms, and the specific criteria used to define an individual spot. In our pipeline, even a single pixel meeting the intensity threshold is counted as a distinct spot, and the standardized fovea‐centered measurement region captures a defined retinal area consistently across participants. Accordingly, absolute spot counts should not be compared directly across studies using different acquisition or analysis pipelines; rather, within‐study relative differences and longitudinal trends are the most meaningful metrics for interpreting amyloid burden from retinal imaging.

We used curcumin as a fluorescent label to detect potential retinal amyloid structures as curcumin has demonstrated high affinity for fibrillar amyloid, making it a valuable tool for identifying amyloid pathology. 26 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 48 While preliminary dose–response studies suggested an optimal dose, standardized protocols to determine appropriate dosages tailored to the cohort's needs would facilitate the harmonization of results across studies. 26 Additionally, technological advances in detecting blue‐light autofluorescence have shifted current studies away from using curcumin to assess retinal amyloid (NCT03699644), and, together with machine learning–based modeling of retinal scans, they offer a potential solution to several of these challenges. Further, a key limitation of the present study is the inability to independently confirm the molecular composition of the retinal fluorescence signal. The RSC here may reflect a mixture of retinal fluorophores and deposits, not all of which are necessarily amyloid specific, and non‐amyloid contributions to the signal may have attenuated the observed retina–PET correlations. The weak correlations should therefore be interpreted as reflecting limited alignment between this specific retinal autofluorescence metric and PET amyloid burden.

Another important interpretive consideration is the disease stage of this study cohort. Participants were drawn primarily from the A4 study and were cognitively normal or nearly so at enrollment, with a mean Mini‐Mental State Examination of ≈ 29, representing a pre‐symptomatic stage of AD. This stands in contrast to prior work, in which retinal amyloid labeling has been evaluated in individuals at the mild dementia stage, a population in which greater amyloid burden and more pronounced between‐group differences might be expected. 26 The absence of a significant difference in retinal amyloid measures between PET‐positive and PET‐negative participants in the present study should therefore be interpreted in this context and does not preclude the utility of curcumin‐based retinal imaging as a biomarker in more advanced or symptomatic disease. Notably, within the LEARN cohort, which includes amyloid‐negative individuals, a longitudinal trend in retinal amyloid measures was observed, suggesting early subclinical pathological changes detectable by this method. Taken together, these findings suggest that the sensitivity of retinal amyloid labeling to distinguish disease status may be greater in symptomatic populations, and future studies should prospectively evaluate this approach across the full clinical spectrum of AD, from preclinical to dementia stages, to better define the stage at which retinal amyloid imaging achieves optimal diagnostic discrimination.

Irrespective of the weak correlation observed in our study, curcumin retinal amyloid labeling remains a promising approach for the non‐invasive detection of AD‐related pathology, but further standardization of imaging acquisition, signal characterization, and analytical protocols is necessary before clinical implementation. Larger cohorts with longer longitudinal follow‐up could better define the relationship between RSC and amyloid PET, both cross‐sectionally and over time. In conclusion, the small sample size, multi‐site imaging variability, signal characterization, and cohort heterogeneity between A4 and LEARN participants may have contributed to the variability observed in retinal spot counts and should be considered when interpreting the findings, as these factors may limit the strength and generalizability of the inferences drawn.
