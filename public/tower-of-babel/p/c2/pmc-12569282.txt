Prototype-oriented class-conditional clustering transport for unsupervised domain adaptation
Authors: Liangda Yan, Jianwen Tao, Tao He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) plays a vital role in machine learning to tackle the homogeneous data distribution scenario. While most previous studies have concentrated on between-domain transferability, they often neglect the rich within-domain semantic structures, thus empirically narrowing the discriminative performance of the learning model. In this work, we introduce a novel approach known as prototype-oriented Class-conditional clustering transport (CLUST) for UDA. This method utilizes clustering objectives and deep prototype learning to improve UDA performance. CLUST aims to generate more diverse and reliable probabilistic outputs by maximizing informational entropy and ensuring semantic consistency. Specifically, CLUST reduces both class-conditional feature clustering transport costs and prototype clustering transport costs, fostering intra-domain feature aggregation and robust domain class structure alignment. Furthermore, CLUST maintains consistent probability predictions for same-class samples, preserving semantic structural consistency within the target data. Theoretically, we analyze the CLUST architecture concerning the target learning generalization error bound, confirming its theoretical soundness and robustness. Extensive experiments demonstrate CLUST’s effectiveness across various challenging scenarios. The experimental results show that CLUST delivers state-of-the-art or comparable performance, highlighting its robustness and practicality across diverse UDA contexts.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) is a pivotal learning paradigm in machine learning and computer vision 1,2. It focuses on transferring knowledge from a fully annotated source domain to an unlabeled target domain, a task hindered by significant domain discrepancies3. The lack of explicit prior knowledge in the target domain renders UDA a major challenge in domain adaptation (DA) research4–6. Recent deep learning models have made remarkable progress in UDA by learning robust, domain-invariant features5–14. Advanced UDA methods commonly use adversarial8–10,15 or kernelized training techniques7,11–13,16 to synchronize source and target domain distributions. The underlying principle is that deep networks can optimize the feature space to remedy marginal domain discrepancies, enabling source-trained classifiers to perform effectively in the target domain12.

Despite these advancements, practical application of domain adversarial training and direct distribution alignment faces several key challenges17. First, metrics like Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)18 and Wasserstein distance4,19, crucial for aligning domain marginal distributions with domain-specific features, are highly sensitive to outliers in small sample batches20–28. Second, although UDA assumes a shared label space, inter-domain label proportion variations complicate feature representation alignment, potentially reducing discriminative power in domain-invariant feature learning29–32. Moreover, traditional UDA requires simultaneous access to both domain datasets, which is infeasible in privacy-sensitive scenarios23,28,33–38. To this end, Tanwisuth et al.17,39 recently proposed a Prototypes-oriented Conditional Transport (PCT) method based on optimal transport40–48 and class-specific prototypes25,39,49–58. While the PCT method has shown certain performance improvements over several recent state-of-the-art techniques, it still manifests multiple limitations in its formulation and practical application. For example, in its present form, PCT fails to encode the semantic structure of the target data24,25,59–61. Moreover, the bidirectional matching of features and prototypes in PCT doesn’t fully resolve the mode collapse problem55,62,

Can a prototype-based clustering transport framework, enhanced by class-conditional optimization and teacher-supervised self-training, mitigate the limitations of existing UDA methods—specifically outlier sensitivity, label proportion bias, and data co-access requirements—while improving alignment stability and generalization across diverse domains? To address this question, we propose the following testable hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1:

A class-conditional clustering transport mechanism that explicitly models intra-class feature homogeneity and cross-domain prototype alignment will reduce outlier sensitivity in distribution metrics, leading to more stable domain alignment compared to marginal distribution alignment methods (e.g., MMD, Wasserstein distance).

Hypothesis 2:

Integrating cost-based optimization of class-conditional feature and prototype transport costs will enhance discriminative power in feature learning, even under extreme inter-domain label proportion variations, outperforming PCT in scenarios with imbalanced class distributions.

Hypothesis 3:

A teacher-supervised self-training strategy, combined with prototype-oriented clustering, will enable effective UDA in source-free scenarios (no direct access to source data), achieving comparable performance to traditional methods that require simultaneous source-target data access.

To validate these hypotheses, we present a novel Class-Conditional LUStering Transport (CLUST) model by employing a teacher-supervised self-training strategy27,60,62. This study makes three significant contributions to the field of Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA):

Novel prototype-oriented clustering transport framework: We introduce a cutting-edge class-conditional clustering transport method for UDA. This innovative approach delves deep into the class-conditional clustering structure both within individual domains and across multiple domains. It accomplishes this through the introduction of two distinct types of class-conditional clustering transports. The class-conditional feature clustering transport focuses on the fine-grained manipulation of features within a class, while the domain-prototype clustering transport deals with the alignment of domain-level prototypes, thereby providing a comprehensive solution for domain adaptation.

Related work

Unsupervised domain adaptation

Recently, Liu et al.14 provided a comprehensive review of deep UDA, focusing on its methodologies, applications, and future directions. Mainstream deep UDA methods primarily use adversarial training8–10,15 or kernelized training12,13 to diminish the distribution disparities between the training and test datasets. A commonly adopted statistical criterion in preceding works2,5,12,13,63,64, dubbed as Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)18, is employed to measure and further minimize the distribution distance between different domains. Motivated by the ideas of generative adversarial networks (GANs)15, Ganin et al.9 extended the learning paradigm by additionally designing a domain discriminator to achieve invariant-domain features from both source and target domains. This approach has become a classical schema in UDA research6,9,10. Essentially, adversarial based DA methods achieved the adaptation performance by diminishing the Jensen Shannon (JS) divergence between two different domains15. The utilization of Wasserstein criterion, originating from optimal transport40,45, further improved the distance measurement effect between domains4,19.

Recent methods13,59,60,64,65 delved into domain-specific fine-grained features in a class-conditional manner42. By aligning target data with class prototypes, the adaptation process can respect the class structure and potentially achieves better generalization on the target domain49–51,53,54. For instace, in51, Tian et al. utilized task-adaptive bidirectional transport costs, along with learnable marginal priors, to optimize the Conditional Transport (CT) objective17,46.

Except the two-domain setting in vanilla UDA, there also exists an interesting scenario, i.e., source-free adaptation (SFA), where the source domain is inaccessible due to privacy protection, and the adaptation learning is performed solely based on target domain and some pre-trained model on source data23. Recently, SFA gradually gains much attention in DA research23,28,34–38. Moreover, the issue of class proportion shift between domains also is a common challenge in many applications but has received limited exploration. Researchers have approached this problem from various perspectives over time17,30,65,66.

Self-training (pseudo-labeling)

Drawing on Semi-Supervised Learning (SSL) achievements, entropy minimization strategies67–70 enable networks to infer unlabeled target data. However, overconfidence in wrong predictions remains a problem26. Iterative pseudo-labeling71, or self-training25,28,72 , originally from SSL, has become an alternative to domain adaptation27,28,73,74. It generates pseudo-labels for unlabeled data and trains models with source and target pseudo-labels. Recent studies use category centroids for pseudo-label generation38, and common practices like label-sharpening20, label spreading75, and the teacher-student paradigm enhance pseudo-label quality20,60,62.

To handle pseudo-label noise, methods27 such as adding confidence regularization terms or validating prediction confidence have been proposed23. Threshold-based and re-weighting techniques23,27 aim to address domain-shift issues in self-training, but they are sensitive and require task-specific tuning with inconsistent results.

Entropy and confidence help select reliable pseudo-labels without domain shift, but their effectiveness drops under distributional shift. Using all pseudo-labels risks error spread and suboptimal convergence26. The Cycle Self-Training approach72 exposes standard self-training’s limitations in UDA. Moreover, most self-training methods alternate between fixed - label learning and periodic updates, hampering adaptability.

Task-specific prediction consistency

Nevertheless, the existence of a domain discrepancy often results in a dispersed target distribution, posing a challenge. In such scenarios, despite the effective separation of target features stemming from the source model’s outputs25, the prototypes encounter difficulties in accurately relabeling data points whose features reside at the fringes of clusters. This limitation arises from the insufficient coverage of the entire target domain distribution by the limited pool of pseudo-labeled data. The training algorithm of CLUST

Training procedure

To address the optimization problem in Eq. (15), we split the training process into four steps:

Intra-domain prototype learning: calculate classifier (i.e., prototypes ) on source domain and perform it to assign pseudo labels to target samples; calculate classifiers (i.e., prototypes ) on target-only and source-target data, and update with respect to the gradient descent of overall objective function. Optimizes the learning of class prototypes within source domain, and provides initialization of target pseudo labels; iteratively computes target class prototypes, distilling the learned prototype knowledge into a mean-teacher model60,62, thus establishing a teacher classifier for self-training the target prototypes. This not only predicts pseudo labels for target features but also provides semantic information for prototype-oriented clustering transport.

Class-conditional clustering transport: In order to leverage the intricate class structures within the feature space, CLUST employs a dual-pronged strategy. Firstly, it endeavors to minimize a class-conditional feature clustering transport loss, thereby compelling features belonging to the same class to coalesce into tight clusters while maintaining a discernible distance from features of disparate classes. This mechanism fosters intra-class compactness and inter-class separability. Secondly, CLUST minimizes a class prototype clustering transport loss. This step aligns the clusters formed by the same class across domains, ensuring that their representations are comparable and coherent. By bridging the domain gap at the level of class prototypes, CLUST enhances the discriminability of the target domain representations, enabling more accurate classification and generalization to unseen target instances.

Task-specific prediction consistency: Based on the discriminative semantic structure mined, using Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence to measure and constrain the semantic consistency of the target outputs, further enhancing the discriminative and robust nature of target feature learning.

Information maximization regularization: Introduces an information maximization regularization term to enhance the diversity and learning confidence of CLUST outputs22. Incorporating the principle of information maximization alongside prototype-guided self-training approaches yields notable enhancements in performance, particularly as the level of pseudo-label noise escalates25.

The training details of the proposed CLUST are outlined in Algorithm 1. In each training iteration, we cycle through four distinct steps, and the training process halts once the convergence criterion is satisfied. To address the inaccuracies inherent in self-labeling, we assign pseudo labels exclusively to target examples with maximized scores exceeding 0.6. Additionally, we perform target example resampling for labeling in every iteration to prevent overfitting caused by pseudo labels. Moreover, the CLUST training mechanism is robust against pseudo label noise. We iteratively leverage both labeled source examples and pseudo-labeled target examples to learn the embedding function. This approach not only maintains high accuracy in the source domain but also significantly reduces class-level and sample-level differences. As this iterative process continues, the accuracy in the target domain steadily improves.

conclusion

This paper presents a holistic framework for UDA, named CLUST, which leverages a class conditional clustering transport mechanism between target features and class prototypes from both domains. Compared to relying solely on prototypes, our CLUST offers richer insights and ultimately leads to a more compact target feature space. Additionally, performance for CLUST can be further enhanced by distilling knowledge from a self-training model. This strategy utilizes pseudo-labels not only for final predictions but also for extracting semantic-level features. These domain-invariant features are further optimized under constraints of both information maximization and prediction consistency, without relying on labels in the target domain. When integrated with the recent Mean Teacher self-training method, our approach establishes a new state-of-the-art performance for UDA, delivering stable learning gains and achieving substantial improvements, especially in the presence of pseudo-label noise.

Through comprehensive experimentation across diverse UDA scenarios, we demonstrate the effectiveness of our proposed CLUST. Our algorithm attains competitive performance with state-of-the-art methods on several prevalent benchmarks, without necessitating additional source parameters. While we have validated the robustness of our approach under class imbalance, we have not yet evaluated its performance in more challenging settings such as low signal-to-noise ratio, extreme class imbalance, and cross-modal adaptation scenarios. These scenarios, which are common in real-world applications, could further reveal the boundaries and potential weaknesses of the prototype-based unsupervised domain adaptation framework. Our future research will further focus on addressing these limitations.
