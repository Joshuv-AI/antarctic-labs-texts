Impact of cardiac rehabilitation exercise management on improving exercise tolerance among patients with chronic heart disease using a telemetry-based biosignal measurement device: protocol for a multicentre randomised controlled trial in Japan (iCARE-MATE study)
Authors: Junya Yamagishi, Takatomo Watanabe, Jun Matsumoto-Miyazaki, Yuki Sahashi, Daichi Watanabe, Ryuta Asada, Hiroyuki Okura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCardiac rehabilitation (CR) improves the outcomes of patients with chronic heart disease. However, participation in hospital-based programs is limited, partly because of accessibility issues. Notably, most CRs are performed unsupervised at home, making it challenging for healthcare providers and patients to monitor progress accurately or provide tailored feedback. Wearable devices are increasingly used to track physical activity; however, many require smartphone operations, which can be difficult for older adults. The purpose of this study is to evaluate whether a simple telemetry system for activity tracking and feedback could improve exercise tolerance in outpatients with chronic heart disease undergoing unsupervised CR at home.Methods and analysisThis open-label, multicentre, randomised controlled trial will include 140 patients with cardiovascular diseases aged ≥20 years with percent of peak oxygen uptake (%peak VO2) <80%. Eligible patients will undergo a 2–4-week observation period followed by cardiopulmonary exercise testing. Participants with <3% peak VO2 change over 6 months will be randomised in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention (device) or control (no device) group. The intervention group will use a wearable device to track daily activities and receive exercise guidance based on these data every 4 weeks. The control group will not use the device or receive feedback. The primary outcome is the 12-week change in peak VO2 from baseline between the groups.Ethics and disseminationThis study was designed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Gifu University Graduate School of Medicine Ethics Committee approved this study (approval number: 2022–081), and all participants will sign an informed consent form.Trial registrationThis study has been registered at the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCT; registration number, 1042220058; URL, https://jrct.mhlw.go.jp/en-latest-detail/jRCT1042220058; date of registration, 26 August 2022).

Introduction

Background and rationale {9a, 9b}

Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) improves prognosis, exercise capacity and quality of life in patients with chronic heart disease1 2 and is strongly recommended by major cardiovascular societies.35 However, despite its proven benefits, patient participation in outpatient CR remains low due to limited accessibility, especially among older adults.6 7 The COVID-19 pandemic further disrupted facility-based programmes, underscoring the need for flexible and scalable alternatives.8

Home-based CR has shown comparable safety and efficacy to centre-based models,9 and recent studies support its safety even in unsupervised settings.10 Additionally, remote delivery of CR has been associated with cost-effectiveness, making it a promising strategy for broader implementation.11

Recent advances in wearable technologies have facilitated home-based CR through activity monitoring and remote feedback.12 13 While earlier digital CR interventions often targeted younger populations, recent evidence confirms their feasibility even in older adults.14 15 However, many tools require smartphone or app operation skills. Therefore, older adults, who constitute the majority of patients requiring CR, tend to have lower participation rates due to limited access to digital technology.16 17 As a result, many digital CR studies have been conducted with younger populations, limiting their applicability to older adults.

To address this, we developed a telemetry-based system using a wearable device that tracks basic activity metrics and transmits data without the need for smartphone interaction. This allows healthcare professionals to visualise activity and provide individualised feedback, particularly for older adults with limited digital proficiency.

We hypothesise that using wearable devices that are easy to operate for exercise feedback, combined with appropriate monitoring and tailored guidance, will improve the effectiveness of home-based CR and enhance exercise tolerance in patients with chronic heart disease.

Methods and analysis

Patient and public involvement {11}

This randomised controlled trial will enrol outpatients with chronic heart disease from multiple collaborating institutions in Japan. Eligible participants must be 20 years or older and have reduced exercise tolerance (percent of peak VO2 (%peak VO₂) <80%). Exclusion criteria include difficulty undergoing cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET), New York Heart Association (NYHA) class III or IV heart failure, dementia, severe psychiatric illness and inability to use the wearable device independently.

Participants will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group, which receives daily exercise management using the telemetry-based device (iAide2), or the control group, which receives standard care. At 12 weeks, the control group will also begin using the device.

The primary outcome is the change in peak VO₂ measured by CPET at 12 weeks. Secondary outcomes include changes in clinical biomarkers (eg, BNP, NT-proBNP), body composition, psychological and cognitive status (HADS, MMSE, IPAQ), heart structure and function (echocardiography) and autonomic nervous activity analysed by HRV during standing load. Outcomes will be assessed at baseline, 12 weeks and 24 weeks.

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this study. However, participants will receive feedback on their personal data and outcomes from the wearable device as part of the intervention.

Trial design and setting {12, 13}

This study is a multicentre, randomised, open-label, parallel-group, controlled trial designed to evaluate the effectiveness of a wearable telemetry-based biosignal monitoring system in improving exercise tolerance among patients with chronic heart disease undergoing home-based CR.

The trial is being conducted at nine hospitals and six outpatient clinics across Japan, primarily located in Gifu Prefecture. After an initial 2–4-week prerandomisation observation period to assess the stability of exercise capacity and mitigate the Hawthorne effect, eligible participants with a change in %peak VO₂ <3% will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group (use of the wearable device with feedback) or the control group (no device and no feedback during the initial 12-week period). Randomisation will be stratified by age (>70 years or ≤70 years), primary diagnosis (myocardial infarction, chronic heart failure or other), participating institution and history of facility-based CR. Participants in the control group will cross over to the intervention condition from week 12 to week 24 for ethical reasons.

Participants demonstrating a ≥3% improvement in %peak VO₂ during the observation period will be excluded from the primary outcome analysis and assigned to a reference group. These participants will use the wearable device but will not receive feedback, thereby enabling an exploratory analysis of the device’s impact in the absence of professional guidance.

The inclusion of both urban and suburban clinical sites ensures that the findings will be broadly applicable to diverse outpatient settings and enhances the generalisability of the study results to real-world clinical practice.

Intervention and comparator {15a, 15b, 15c, 15d}

Intervention (15a)

Participants randomised to the intervention group will receive a wearable, smart-band-type medical telemetry device (iAide2, TOKAI Corporation, Gifu, Japan). This device continuously measures physiological parameters, including resting heart rate, step count and walking intensity, via accelerometry. Participants are instructed to wear the device during waking hours daily. Data from the device are automatically transmitted to a centralised research database when the device is placed on a portable home docking station.

Based on accumulated activity data, participants receive personalised exercise feedback and guidance from CR therapists following a standardised protocol based on the frequency, intensity, time, type (FITT) principles at least once every 4 weeks. All therapists participating in the study will attend a 2-hour training session to ensure consistency in data interpretation and exercise guidance. During the session, they will share how to set aerobic exercise and resistance training using the FITT-VP (volume, progression) framework. For aerobic exercise, the frequency (F) is set to 3–5 days per week, intensity (I) to 3–6 metabolic equivalents (METs), duration (T) to 30–60 min per session and type (T) to walking. The volume (V) is set to 8000–10 000 steps, with progressive modification (P) increasing by 1–5 min at a time. For resistance training, the frequency (F) was set to daily, the intensity (I) to bodyweight load and the duration (T) to 5–10 sets per day. The type of exercise (T) was resistance training (pretraining). The volume (V) was started at 5–10 repetitions per set, with gradual progression (P) adjusted according to symptoms.

Comparator (15a)

Participants in the control group will receive standard care without the wearable device or feedback during the initial 12-week period. After the primary outcome assessment at week 12, participants in the control group will cross over to the intervention phase and receive the same device and exercise feedback protocol as the intervention group from week 12 to week 24.

Strategies for adherence and monitoring (15c)

To promote adherence, device usage is simplified; no smartphone operation is required, and data transmission is automated via the docking station. Compliance is monitored through automatic logs of daily wear time and data transmission frequency. Therapists evaluate device usage, adherence to exercise instructions and sedentary time during feedback sessions (conducted at least once every 4 weeks), based on both the frequency of device use and data such as heart rate and step count obtained from the device. If non-compliance issues are identified (eg, reduced physical activity levels or irregular device use), physical therapists provide personalised feedback and motivational support.

Concomitant care (15d)

All participants are permitted to continue standard medical therapies, including medications and non-study-related physical therapy, as prescribed by their treating physicians. No concomitant rehabilitation programmes involving structured exercise feedback or telemonitoring are allowed during the study period to avoid contamination. Any additional interventions will be recorded and monitored.

Outcomes {16}

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is the between-group difference in the change in peak VO₂ (mL/kg/min) from baseline to 12 weeks, measured by CPET. Peak VO₂ is a well-established objective marker of aerobic exercise capacity and prognosis in patients with chronic heart disease.1 2

CPET will be conducted using an electronically braked cycle ergometer with breath-by-breath gas analysis. Participants will undergo a standardised ramp protocol following a warm-up, with incremental loading (10 W/min) until symptom-limited maximal exertion. The test will measure VO₂, carbon dioxide production (VCO₂), ventilation (VE), respiratory rate and ventilatory equivalents (VE/VO₂ and VE/VCO₂). Heart rate, blood pressure and pulse oximetry will be continuously monitored.

The type and timing of any missing or partial data will be recorded, and outcome data (eg, CPET results, questionnaires, laboratory findings) will be obtained if the participant consents to continued data collection. Reasons for dropout will be documented.

Sample size {19}

The sample size was calculated to detect a clinically meaningful between-group difference in peak VO₂ (mL/kg/min) after 12 weeks of intervention. Based on prior studies of similar populations and interventions, a conservative estimate of the expected improvement in peak VO₂ was set at 1.5 mL/kg/min in the intervention group, assuming no change in the control group.2123

A common SD of 3.0 mL/kg/min was assumed for both groups, based on previous randomised controlled trials evaluating exercise-based interventions in patients with cardiovascular disease. Using a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05 and a statistical power (1−β) of 80%, the required sample size per group to detect the anticipated effect size of 1.5 mL/kg/min was 63 participants.

To account for an estimated dropout rate of 10%, the final target enrolment was set at 70 participants per group, resulting in a total planned sample size of 140 participants.

The sample size calculation was performed using standard formulae for comparing two means in independent groups and assumes equal allocation (1:1 randomisation).

Recruitment {20}

Participant recruitment began following approval from the Gifu University Graduate School of Medicine Ethics Committee on 29 July 2022 (approval number: 2022–081). Recruitment is being conducted across 15 sites, including nine hospitals and six outpatient clinics located primarily in Gifu Prefecture, Japan.

The planned recruitment period spans from 29 July 2022 to 31 December 2024, allowing sufficient time to enrol the target sample size of 140 participants (70 per group), as determined by the sample size calculation. Each site has been selected based on its existing CR infrastructure and prior experience with clinical research, ensuring feasibility in participant identification, enrolment and follow-up.

Eligible patients are identified by attending cardiologists or rehabilitation staff during routine outpatient visits or CR sessions. Informational brochures and verbal explanations are provided to potentially eligible patients, and written informed consent is obtained by trained study personnel. A centralised web-based registration and randomisation system is used to streamline participant management.

The use of multiple sites and the inclusion of both hospital-based and community clinic settings are expected to enhance the generalisability of the findings and support timely recruitment. Regular site monitoring and monthly recruitment reports are used to track enrolment progress and implement corrective actions if targets fall behind schedule.

Sequence generation (21a)

Participants will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group (wearable device with feedback) or the control group (no device or feedback for 12 weeks). The random allocation sequence will be generated using a computer-based, permuted block randomisation method with variable block sizes to ensure allocation balance while minimising predictability.

Participating institution.

The random sequence will be generated by an independent statistician not involved in participant recruitment, data collection or outcome assessment.

Type of randomisation and restrictions (21b)

The study uses stratified block randomisation with randomly varying block sizes to prevent predictability. Stratification ensures balanced allocation across key prognostic variables. The specific details of block sizes and sequence parameters are stored in a secured, access-controlled file and will not be disclosed to investigators involved in enrolment or assignment.

Allocation concealment mechanism (22)

Allocation concealment will be ensured through the use of a centralised web-based randomisation system (hosted on REDCap) accessible only by authorised staff. On confirming eligibility, study coordinators will input participant data into the system, which will then return the group assignment in real time. This mechanism prevents foreknowledge or manipulation of assignment.

Implementation (23)

Eligible participants will be identified and consented by site investigators. After completion of the baseline assessment and confirmation of stable %peak VO₂ (<3% change) during the prerandomisation observation period, the allocation will be carried out through the centralised system. The allocation sequence will not be accessible to those enrolling participants or delivering the intervention.

Blinding {24a}

Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and care providers is not feasible. Participants in the intervention group are aware of their device use and receive feedback, whereas control participants initially receive no such input. Similarly, physical therapists who deliver exercise guidance cannot be blinded to group assignment.

Statistical methods {27a, 27b, 27c, 27d}

Analysis of primary and secondary outcomes (27a)

The primary analysis will compare the change in peak VO₂ (mL/kg/min) from baseline to 12 weeks between the intervention and control groups. A linear mixed-effects model will be used, incorporating trial site as a random effect and including fixed effects for treatment group, age category (>70 years vs ≤70 years), primary diagnosis and prior CR. The treatment effect will be tested using a two-sided t-test on the regression coefficient for the group variable. A p value <0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

If model assumptions (eg, normality of residuals) are violated, natural log transformation of the outcome will be considered.

Secondary continuous outcomes (eg, biomarker levels, questionnaire scores, body composition) will be analysed using similar mixed-effects models or paired t-tests, as appropriate. Categorical outcomes will be compared using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests.

Analysis populations (27b)

The full analysis set will include all randomised participants who have undergone at least one postbaseline efficacy assessment and will be used for the primary analysis under the intention-to-treat principle.

The per-protocol set (PPS) will exclude participants with major protocol deviations, such as eligibility violations or failure to adhere to the intervention. Analyses using the PPS will be conducted as sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the primary findings.

Handling of missing data (27c)

Missing data for the primary outcome will be imputed using the last observation carried forward method, provided the participant has at least one postbaseline measurement. The pattern and extent of missing data will be examined, and multiple imputation may be conducted as a sensitivity analysis if missingness exceeds 10% or appears non-random.
