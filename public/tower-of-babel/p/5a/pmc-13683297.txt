The efficacy of transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation as an adjunctive therapy for perioperative pain management after total knee arthroplasty: an umbrella review
Authors: Meihui Huang, Peiyuan Tang, Ting Wen, Yusheng Li, Bangbao Lu, Xucheng Yang, Wenfeng Xiao, Kai Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTotal knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a cornerstone intervention for end-stage knee pathologies, yet acute postoperative pain remains a critical barrier to rehabilitation. Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), a non-pharmacological adjunct, has potential for pain mitigation, but its efficacy lacks robust evidence from umbrella reviews.MethodsThis umbrella review systematically evaluated meta-analyses (MAs) published through September 2025 in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science. Overlap among primary studies was assessed using the GROOVE tool, and evidence quality was graded via the AMSTAR 2 and GRADE frameworks. This umbrella review is registered on the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under accession no. CRD420251126630.ResultsEleven MAs were included in this review. The results showed that TENS significantly reduced acute postoperative pain at 24 h (MD = − 0.42, 95% CI − 0.59, − 0.25, P < 0.00001, I2 = 0%) and 72 h (MD = − 0.84, 95% CI − 1.17, − 0.52, P < 0.00001, I2 = 0%), but no significant difference was observed at 2 weeks (MD = 0.97, 95% CI − 0.66, 2.60, P = 0.24, I2 = 91%). Opioid consumption was lower in the TENS group (SMD = − 0.58, 95% CI − 1.06, − 0.11, P = 0.02, I2 = 53%). Improvements were also seen in range of motion (SMD = 0.40, 95% CI 0.11, 0.68, P = 0.006, I2 = 0%), Hospital for Special Surgery scores (MD = 4.18, 95% CI 2.91, 5.45, P < 0.00001, I2 = 0%), and length of hospital stay (SMD = − 0.20, 95% CI − 0.38, − 0.03, P = 0.02, I2 = 0%). TENS was associated with a reduced incidence of nausea and vomiting.ConclusionsTENS is an effective adjunct for acute pain management after TKA, reducing opioid use and improving early functional outcomes. Future research should aim to standardize TENS application protocols and evaluate long-term efficacy and safety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10195-026-00957-z.Keywords: Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, Total knee arthroplasty, Postoperative pain, Opioid consumption, Umbrella review

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a crucial surgical intervention for patients with degenerative or inflammatory conditions causing irreversible joint damage. TKA replaces damaged articular surfaces with prosthetic components, effectively alleviating pain, correcting deformity, restoring mechanical alignment, and significantly enhancing functional mobility and quality of life [1, 2]. With the progressive aging of the global population, the demand for TKA has risen substantially. Epidemiological studies project that the annual number of TKA procedures performed worldwide will exceed 3 million by 2030, representing a considerable public health and economic burden [3, 4].

Despite high success rates for implant survival and long-term functional improvement, acute postoperative pain management remains a central challenge in the perioperative care of TKA patients. Inadequately controlled pain after surgery can lead to a range of adverse outcomes, including delayed mobilization, increased risk of thromboembolic events, muscle atrophy, joint stiffness, prolonged hospitalization, and chronic pain syndromes [5]. Conventional postoperative pain management typically includes opioids, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and local anesthetic nerve blocks. However, opioids are associated with respiratory depression, sedation, nausea, constipation, and the potential for misuse and dependency. NSAIDs may contribute to gastrointestinal bleeding, renal impairment, and cardiovascular complications. Similarly, regional nerve blocks can lead to muscle weakness and falls [6, 7]. These inherent drawbacks have prompted increasing interest in complementary, non-pharmacological analgesic techniques aimed at minimizing opioid consumption and enhancing patient safety [8].

Among these adjunctive therapies, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) has emerged as a promising modality that is easy to use, inexpensive, and associated with minimal side effects [9]. TENS involves the delivery of low-frequency electrical currents through electrodes placed on the skin surface, which modulate pain perception via several theorized mechanisms [10, 11]. In recent years, there has been growing clinical and research interest in the use of TENS for acute postoperative pain management. Several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses (MAs) have investigated its efficacy, with many demonstrating statistically significant reductions in early postoperative pain scores, reduced opioid consumption, and earlier participation in physical therapy [12–17].

This umbrella review aims to address this gap by systematically appraising and synthesizing the evidence from MAs on the use of TENS for acute pain management following TKA. By evaluating the strength and quality of the collective evidence, this review seeks to provide a definitive conclusion on the effectiveness of TENS in reducing pain, decreasing opioid requirements, improving functional recovery, and minimizing opioid-related adverse events in the early postoperative period after TKA.

Methods

An umbrella review evaluates and synthesizes data from multiple MAs on a given topic. We followed the procedures outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for conducting umbrella reviews. This review was registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251126630) and followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) (Additional file 1) and the AMSTAR 2 (A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews 2) guidelines [18, 19].

Search methodology

Four databases, including Embase, PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science, were searched through September 2025. The search strategy combined subject headings and free-text terms, including transcutaneous electric nerve stimulation, total knee arthroplasty, postoperative pain, systematic review, and meta-analysis (Additional file 2).

Study selection

Inclusion criteria: (1) Study design and reporting: Only MAs that adhered to the PRISMA guidelines were included to ensure methodological rigor and transparent reporting; (2) Intervention and comparison: Systematic reviews with MAs that directly compared TENS as an adjunctive therapy versus conventional pain management after TKA, with TENS defined as non-invasive delivery of low-frequency electrical currents; (3) Minimum study requirement: MAs had to include at least two RCTs for any given outcome measure to ensure sufficient data for meaningful synthesis and to minimize the risk of biased estimates; (4) Population: Studies involving adult patients who underwent primary unilateral or bilateral TKA for osteoarthritis or other end-stage knee pathologies; (5) Outcomes: MAs reporting at least one of the following outcomes: postoperative pain intensity, opioid consumption, range of motion (ROM), Hospital for Special Surgery score (HSS), length of hospital stay (LOS), or adverse events.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Study type: Narrative reviews, letters, editorials, conference abstracts, study protocols, and non-systematic reviews were excluded. Studies that did not perform a quantitative synthesis were also excluded; (2) Data limitations: MAs with incomplete or insufficient data for outcome extraction or effect size calculation were excluded; (3) Accessibility: Studies for which the full text was unavailable or could not be retrieved through institutional access or author correspondence were excluded; (4) Language: Non-English publications were excluded; (5) Intervention specificity: Other electrical modalities were explicitly excluded to ensure intervention homogeneity. Neuromuscular electrical stimulation used higher‑intensity currents primarily for muscle strengthening rather than analgesia; peripheral nerve stimulation was an invasive technique requiring temporary or permanent electrode implantation adjacent to the target nerve; electroacupuncture involved percutaneous needle insertion at acupoints, which differs fundamentally from surface‑electrode‑based TENS in both delivery and mechanism; (6) Duplicate: If multiple MAs were published by the same author group using overlapping datasets, the most comprehensive or recent publication was included to avoid duplication (Additional file 3).

Overlapping discovery and processing

Given that two or more MAs evaluating the same outcome may share primary studies, we assessed overlap using the Graphical Representation of Overlap for Overviews (GROOVE). This tool quantifies overlap by calculating the Corrected Covered Area (CCA), which represents the percentage of primary studies shared between two meta‑analyses [20]. Based on the CCA, GROOVE classifies overlap into four levels: very high (> 15%), high (10% to < 15%), moderate (5% to < 10%), and slight (< 5%). Two authors independently conducted overlap assessment using the GROOVE tool, identifying overlapping areas of original studies across MAs through graphical visualization. In the event of discrepancies in overlap determination, a third author was consulted to adjudicate and reach a final decision. When overlap was present, the following measures were applied: (1) if the overlap involved Cochrane reviews and non-Cochrane reviews, the results of the Cochrane reviews were preferred; (2) if a high degree of overlap (≥ 10%) was found between two or more non-Cochrane reviews, the results of the meta-analysis with the highest AMSTAR 2 score were given priority. If the AMSTAR 2 scores were consistent, the results of the meta-analysis with the highest number of RCTs included or the most recent publication were given priority; (3) if slight or moderate overlap (< 10%) was found, all MAs were included, as the impact on summary estimates was considered minimal. Importantly, all outcomes demonstrated overlap rates < 15%, with most falling into the moderate or slight categories, supporting the validity of our pooled analyses (Additional file 4).

Obtaining data and evaluating its quality

Two authors (MH & PT) independently extracted the data and assessed quality; a third author (YL) resolved any disagreements. The main results extracted in this study include: postoperative pain, opioid consumption, HSS, ROM, LOS, and adverse events. The gender ratio of included studies, average age, number of included RCTs, and number of knees in the TENS and CI groups undergoing total knee replacement were also extracted. Using the AMSTAR 2, two authors independently evaluated the methodological quality of the included MAs. In case of disagreement, a senior author adjudicated to reach a consensus. According to the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE), we evaluated the evidence level for each outcome and rated it as high, moderate, low, and very low (Additional file 5). In addition, we classified the evidence into four categories: I (convincing evidence), II (highly suggestive evidence), III (suggestive evidence), IV (weak evidence), and NS (not significant). Detailed criteria for the classification of evidence are shown in Additional file 6.

Data synthesis

Statistical heterogeneity among the studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q chi-squared test and the I2 statistic. The I2 values were interpreted as follows: < 25%, low heterogeneity; 25–50%, low to moderate heterogeneity; 50–75%, moderate to substantial heterogeneity; > 75%, substantial heterogeneity. A P-value of < 0.05 for the Q-test was considered indicative of significant statistical heterogeneity. The choice of statistical model for pooling data was based on the degree of heterogeneity. A fixed-effect model was applied when heterogeneity was not significant (typically I2 < 50% and P ≥ 0.05 for the Q-test), assuming that all studies share a common true effect size. A random-effects model was adopted in the presence of substantial heterogeneity (typically I2 ≥ 50% and P < 0.05), accounting for the possibility that the true effect size may vary across studies. The results of all meta-analyses were visualized using forest plots. A two-sided P-value of < 0.05 for the overall effect estimate was considered statistically significant. Significant clinical heterogeneity was explored through subgroup analyses or sensitivity analyses where appropriate.

Results

Search results

The initial search included 42 systematic reviews. After removing 11 duplicates, 31 records were screened. Of these, 9 were excluded based on title and abstract, leaving 22 full-text reports assessed for eligibility. A further 11 were excluded through reading the full text, resulting in 11 MAs included in this umbrella review. Figure 1 shows the literature screening procedure.

Study characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the basic characteristics of the 11 MAs included in this systematic review. The included MAs were published between 2014 and 2025, and the temporal distribution showed a noticeable concentration of studies in recent years. Regarding the scope of literature coverage, four MAs integrated a relatively modest number of studies (< 10), whereas seven demonstrated more comprehensive coverage. Demographic reporting among the MAs was inconsistent but informative. Eight MAs provided explicit gender ratios for participants enrolled in their constituent primary studies, with female participants generally outnumbering male participants. Eight MAs reported the mean age of participants, which consistently fell within the senior population range of the mid-60s to early 70s. Geographically, the corresponding authors of the MAs were predominantly from China (n = 7), with the remaining MAs originating from Belgium, the US, Saudi Arabia, and Italy [21–31]. Methodological quality, as critically appraised using the AMSTAR 2 tool, revealed a mixed picture. Five MAs were rated as high quality, two as moderate, and four as low quality (Additional file 7). The outcomes measured across these MAs were comprehensive, focusing on the primary outcome of postoperative pain at various time points and on secondary outcomes including opioid consumption, HSS score, ROM, LOS, and the incidence of adverse events (Additional file 8).

Results of the umbrella review

Postoperative pain

Figure 2 shows the forest plots of postoperative pain. Seven MAs reported the primary outcome of postoperative pain at various time points [22–24, 27, 29–31]. The AMSTAR-2 ratings for five studies were high, one was moderate, and one was low. GROOVE analysis revealed overlap rates of < 15% across all included MAs for postoperative pain. GRADE assessment indicated low certainty for 24-h and 72-h pain outcomes, but very low certainty for the 2-week outcome due to risk of bias and publication bias. Subgroup analyses showed that postoperative pain at 24 h in the TENS group was significantly lower than that in the CI group (MD = − 0.42, 95% CI − 0.59, − 0.25, P < 0.00001, I2 = 0%) and was similar at 72 h (MD = − 0.84, 95% CI − 1.17, − 0.52, P < 0.00001, I2 = 0%). However, at 2 weeks, the forest plot indicated no statistically significant difference (MD = 0.97, 95% CI − 0.66, 2.60, P = 0.24, I2 = 91%). The substantial heterogeneity observed is likely attributable to variations in post-discharge rehabilitation protocols, discontinuation of TENS therapy after hospitalization in most included studies, differing surgical approaches, the transition of pain mechanisms from acute inflammatory to chronic neuropathic, and inconsistent use of rescue analgesics across studies. Overall, the TENS and CI groups were not statistically different, and heterogeneity was substantial (MD = − 0.24, 95% CI − 0.63, 0.15, P = 0.23, I2 = 87%).

Forest plots of postoperative pain

Opioid consumption

Figure 3 shows the forest plots of opioids consumption. A total of four MAs reported opioid consumption [23, 24, 30, 31]. The AMSTAR-2 ratings of three studies were high, and the rating of one was moderate. GROOVE analysis revealed overlap rates of < 15% across all included MAs for opioid consumption. GRADE assessment revealed moderate to very low certainty across contributing studies, primarily due to risk of bias and publication bias. Forest plots showed that opioid consumption in the TENS group was significantly lower than that in the CI group (SMD = − 0.58, 95% CI − 1.06, − 0.11, P = 0.02, I2 = 53%).

Forest plots of opioid consumption

Hospital for Special Surgery score

Figure 4 shows the forest plots of HSS score. Two MAs reported the HSS scores [27, 29]. The AMSTAR-2 rating of two studies was high. GROOVE analysis revealed overlap rates of < 15% across all included MAs for HSS score. GRADE assessment showed very low certainty due to concerns about risk of bias and publication bias. Forest plots showed that HSS score in the TENS group was significantly higher than that in the CI group (MD = 4.18, 95% CI 2.91, 5.45, P < 0.00001, I2 = 0%).

Forest plots of Hospital for Special Surgery score

Range of motion

Figure 5 summarizes the ROM forest plots. Two MAs reported ROM [24, 30]. The AMSTAR-2 rating of one study was high and of the other was moderate. GROOVE analysis revealed a 0% overlap rate across all included MAs for ROM. GRADE assessment indicated low to very low certainty. Forest plots showed that ROM in the TENS group was significantly higher than that in the CI group (SMD = 0.40, 95% CI 0.11, 0.68, P = 0.006, I2 = 0%).

Forest plots of range of motion

Length of hospital stay

Figure 6 shows the forest plots of LOS. Two MAs reported LOS [23, 30]. The AMSTAR-2 ratings of the two studies were high and moderate, respecitvely. GROOVE analysis revealed overlap rates of 0% across all included MAs for LOS. GRADE assessment indicated low to very low certainty. Forest plots showed that LOS in the TENS group was significantly shorter than that in the CI group (SMD = − 0.20, 95% CI − 0.38, − 0.03, P = 0.02, I2 = 0%).

Forest plots of length of hospital stay

Adverse events

In addition, two MAs reported adverse events, specifically nausea/vomiting [23, 29]. The AMSTAR-2 ratings of the two studies were high. GRADE assessment indicated a decline due to risk of bias and publication bias; however, because of the large effect, the rating was upgraded to low quality. The results were not amenable to direct pooling in a forest plot due to heterogeneity in effect measures. Li et al. reported RD = − 0.0980, 95% CI − 0.180, − 0.016, P = 0.02, I2 = 0%; and Zhang et al. reported OR = 0.300, 95% CI 0.2, 0.45, P < 0.00001, I2 = 0%. Both results (RD < 0, OR < 1) were statistically significant, suggesting that TENS may reduce the risk of nausea and vomiting.

Discussion

Osteoarthritis is a highly prevalent disease that leads to severe functional impairment in its end stage, often requiring TKA [32]. This umbrella review systematically synthesized evidence from 11 MAs evaluating the efficacy of TENS in acute pain management following TKA. Our findings show that TENS contributes to statistically significant reductions in 24-h and 72-h postoperative pain scores, reductions in opioid consumption, improved functional recovery metrics including ROM and HSS scores, and reduced incidence of opioid-related adverse events, particularly nausea and vomiting. However, the benefits appear most pronounced in the immediate postoperative period, with diminishing effects at the 2-week mark and notable heterogeneity in longer-term outcomes. These results position TENS as a valuable component of multimodal analgesia protocols, supporting the current trend toward opioid-sparing approaches in orthopedic enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways.

The forest plots generated in this review provide nuanced insights into the magnitude, consistency, and temporal pattern of TENS effects across multiple outcome domains. The analysis of pain scores revealed a consistent pattern of early benefit associated with TENS use. At 24 h postoperatively, the pooled MD was − 0.42 (95% CI − 0.59 to − 0.25, P < 0.00001), while at 72 h, the effect size increased to − 0.84 (95% CI − 1.17 to − 0.52, P < 0.00001). Both time points exhibited negligible heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), indicating remarkable consistency across studies. These reductions, while statistically significant, must be interpreted within the context of clinical relevance. The enhanced effect at 72 h may reflect cumulative benefits of repeated TENS applications and reduced central sensitization during this critical period when the inflammatory response peaks. The absence of significant benefit at 2 weeks (MD = 0.97, 95% CI − 0.66 to 2.60, P = 0.24) with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 91%) suggests that TENS primarily affects acute pain mechanisms rather than altering the longer-term healing process. This heterogeneity may stem from variations in rehabilitation protocols, cessation of TENS therapy after hospitalization, differing surgical approaches, or evolving pain mechanisms beyond the acute phase. The finding underscores that TENS should be conceptualized primarily as an acute pain management strategy rather than as a modifier of chronic recovery trajectories.

The reduction in opioid consumption (SMD = − 0.58, 95% CI − 1.06 to − 0.11, P = 0.02) represents one of the most clinically significant findings of this review. The moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 53%) reflects variations in opioid prescribing practices, drug potency calculations, and administration protocols across studies. The opioid-sparing effect of TENS aligns perfectly with current initiatives to minimize opioid exposure following surgery, which is particularly relevant in the context of the opioid epidemic. Reduced opioid use directly translates to decreased risk of opioid-related adverse events, which our review confirmed through significantly lower rates of nausea and vomiting. This secondary benefit contributes substantially to patient comfort, early oral intake, and potentially shorter recovery times.

The improvements in ROM (SMD = 0.40, 95% CI 0.11 to 0.68, P = 0.006) and HSS scores (MD = 4.18, 95% CI 2.91 to 5.45, P < 0.00001) show that pain reduction translates to meaningful functional benefits. Improved early ROM is particularly crucial as it predicts long-term functional outcomes and may prevent arthrofibrosis. The HSS score improvement of > 4 points exceeds the minimal clinically important difference for this instrument, suggesting patients experience tangible improvements in joint function, stability, and daily activities. These functional benefits likely stem from both better pain control and possible neuromodulatory effects on muscle activation patterns.

Although the observed reduction in hospital stay was modest (SMD = − 0.20, 95% CI − 0.38, − 0.03, P = 0.02, I2 = 0%), it still carries important clinical and economic implications. In the context of ERAS pathways, even a half-day reduction in hospital stay can lower healthcare costs, improve bed turnover, and reduce the risk of hospital-acquired complications. By addressing these multiple discharge barriers simultaneously, TENS facilitates a smoother and faster recovery trajectory. These findings suggest that incorporating TENS into multimodal analgesia protocols may serve as a cost-effective strategy to accelerate hospital discharge without compromising patient outcomes.

These clinical findings are consistent with the established neurophysiological mechanisms of TENS analgesia. According to gate control theory, activity in small-diameter (C/Aδ) nerve fibers mediates pain, whereas stimulation of large-diameter sensory (Aβ) fibers inhibits pain perception. The theory hypothesizes a physiological gating mechanism within the spinal cord's dorsal horn. This gate modulates the transmission of nociceptive signals to the brain. TENS reduces pain by selectively activating cutaneous Aβ fibers, thereby augmenting this gating mechanism and producing segmental inhibition of nociceptive transmission via small fibers [33]. In addition, new research shows that TENS activates the central nervous system, modulating activity in the lateral prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, parahippocampal and hippocampal regions, thalamus, and amygdala [34]. TENS also alters the release of neurotransmitters such as norepinephrine, acetylcholine, dopamine, and serotonin, which are involved in pain control [35]. Furthermore, TENS has been shown to stimulate the release of endogenous opioids, including β-endorphins and enkephalins, which bind to μ-opioid receptors in the central nervous system, thereby producing analgesia [36]. This mechanism may explain the reduction in opioid consumption observed in our analysis. A recent meta-analysis also suggests that TENS modulates neuroinflammatory responses by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β and promoting anti-inflammatory mediators such as IL-10 in synovial tissue and the spinal cord [36]. This anti-inflammatory effect may contribute to enhanced functional recovery and reduced joint stiffness. Understanding these mechanisms supports the clinical observation that TENS is most effective in the acute postoperative period (24–72 h) when inflammation peaks, with diminishing effects at 2 weeks as inflammatory processes subside.

While this umbrella review provides comprehensive insights into the efficacy of TENS after TKA, several limitations in the available evidence must be acknowledged. The methodological quality of the included MAs varied considerably according to AMSTAR 2 ratings, with only five deemed high quality, two moderate, and four low, potentially introducing bias and limiting the robustness of conclusions. Variability in TENS administration protocols, including electrode placement, stimulation parameters such as frequency, intensity and duration, and timing of initiation, contributes to heterogeneity in study outcomes. Furthermore, many trials are limited by small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, and inadequate blinding, raising questions about the robustness and generalizability of findings. This heterogeneity, while appropriately managed through random effects models, limits the ability to identify optimal TENS protocols and may mask subgroup effects. Although primary study overlap was generally low to moderate, some duplication may persist. This could potentially lead to overestimation of effect precision, though the impact is likely minimal given the low overlap rates. Nevertheless, this methodological factor should be considered when interpreting the pooled estimates. Geographical bias is also a concern, as 7 of the 11 MAs had corresponding authors from China, which may affect the generalizability of findings to other regions because of differences in surgical and rehabilitation practices. The certainty of evidence according to GRADE assessments was concerningly low for several key outcomes, including postoperative pain (low to very low), opioid consumption (moderate to very low), HSS score (very low), and ROM (low to very low). This reflects serious concerns regarding risk of bias and publication bias across the included MAs. Clinicians and policymakers should interpret the findings with appropriate caution, recognizing that further research is likely to impact the confidence in these effect estimates. Moreover, the evidence predominantly focuses on short-term outcomes, with limited data on long-term efficacy beyond 2 weeks, hindering understanding of whether TENS sustains benefits or merely postpones pain. Systematic reporting of adverse events was notably lacking, preventing a comprehensive assessment of the safety profile, and no economic evaluations were available to assess cost-effectiveness.

Based on these limitations, several future research directions are recommended. There is a need to standardize TENS protocols through dose-response and component-network analyses to establish optimal stimulation parameters. Mechanistic studies incorporating neuroimaging and biomarker assessments could elucidate analgesia pathways and identify treatment responders. Extending follow-up durations would help evaluate long-term impacts on chronic pain, functional recovery, and opioid use. Research on personalized stimulation strategies based on genetic and physiological profiles may enhance efficacy, and studies exploring integrative approaches with other non-pharmacological interventions could reveal synergistic effects. Additionally, there is currently a lack of health economic evaluations. However, it is essential to assess cost‑effectiveness and identify barriers to clinical adoption. Future research should explicitly evaluate the cost‑effectiveness of TENS as an adjunct to multimodal analgesia following TKA, considering device costs, personnel training, and any associated reductions in hospital length of stay or opioid‑related adverse events.

These findings support the integration of TENS into multimodal analgesia protocols for TKA patients, particularly within the first 72 postoperative h, given its demonstrated benefits in reducing pain and opioid consumption, improving early function, and having a favorable safety profile. Clinicians should apply TENS early with appropriate electrode placement and adequate intensity to achieve comfortable paresthesia, preferably through regular rather than as-needed sessions, especially during rehabilitation activities. Institutions are encouraged to develop standardized protocols, training programs, and monitoring procedures to support implementation within enhanced recovery pathways. In conclusion, despite existing limitations, consistent evidence supports the use of TENS as an effective adjunct therapy post-TKA. Future efforts should focus on protocol optimization, mechanistic clarification, long-term outcome assessment, and economic evaluation to strengthen the evidence base and guide clinical practice.

Conclusion

TENS is an effective adjunct for acute pain management after TKA, reducing opioid use and improving early functional outcomes. Future research should aim to standardize TENS application protocols and evaluate long-term efficacy and safety.
