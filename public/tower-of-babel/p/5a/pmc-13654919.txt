Mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes alleviate post-resuscitation brain injury by inhibiting neuronal ferroptosis partly via the PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway
Authors: Lulu Li, Lan Ying, Lu He, Qijiang Chen, Huijuan Yang, Jinyu Zhu, Yong Liu, Li Jiang, Licai Liang, Jiefeng Xu, Mao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost-resuscitation brain injury is the main contributor to the death and disability of cardiac arrest (CA) victims. Neuronal ferroptosis is involved in the pathogenesis of brain injury after resuscitation, which becomes an effective therapeutic target. Recent studies demonstrated that mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (MSC-Exo) were a promising approach for the alleviation of regional cerebral ischemia reperfusion injury; however, its effectiveness in post-resuscitation brain injury remains to be investigated. In this study, we investigated whether MSC-Exo could alleviate post-resuscitation brain injury by inhibiting neuronal ferroptosis and its regulatory mechanism.Methods and resultsIn vivo, a clinically relevant swine model of CA and resuscitation was established. In parallel, SH-SY5Y neuronal cells exposed to hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) were used as the in vitro model. We found that MSC-Exo administration provided potent protection of neuronal cells against H/R injury in vitro, and effectively alleviated brain injury after CA and resuscitation in vivo. Furthermore, we showed that the protective effects of MSC-Exo administration were associated with the upregulation of protein arginine methyltransferase 1 (PRMT1), solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) expression, and subsequent inhibition of neuronal ferroptosis in in vitro and in vivo studies. Mechanistically, MSC-Exo administration promoted the recovery of PRMT1 expression, and then the latter bound to the promoter of SLC7A11 and enhanced its transcriptional activity after H/R injury in neuronal cells. Additionally, the miR-320a-3p/tripartite motif-containing 21 (TRIM21) axis was a possible mechanism by which MSC-Exo administration activated the PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway in vitro.ConclusionsMSC-Exo administration effectively alleviated post-resuscitation brain injury after CA and resuscitation, which was related to the inhibition of neuronal ferroptosis partly through the activation of PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway.

Cardiac arrest (CA) is always a critical public health theme with high incidence and low survival rates in the world [1–3]. Following successful cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) from CA events, the ensuing post-CA syndrome would result in multiple organ dysfunction and further poor clinical outcomes [4]. Among which, post-resuscitation brain injury is considered to be one of the main contributors, which can cause high rates of in-hospital mortality and post-hospital disability [5]. Although targeted temperature management is recommended by the CPR guideline to implement for post-resuscitation brain protection [6]; nevertheless, a plenty of clinical practices manifest that many survivors still can’t obtain the recovery of neurological function from the treatment of temperature control [7, 8]. Thus, it is needed to explore more effective therapeutic strategy for use in the clinical setting.

Recently, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have emerged as an effective treatment for alleviating post-resuscitation brain injury in a series of preclinical studies [9, 10]. Especially, one study has preliminarily manifested that MSCs administration exhibits effective therapeutic effects on inflammation, oxidative injury and cell death, and thereby produces more potent post-resuscitation brain protection compared with targeted temperature management [11]. However, the clinical implementation of MSCs administration is impeded by several concerns, including its inconvenience of storage and transportation, instability of cellular activity, potential risk of tumorigenicity, and so on [12]. Nevertheless, MSC-derived exosomes (MSC-Exo), which contain a diversity of bioactive substances (such as nucleic acids, proteins and lipids), can serve as an effective alternative of MSCs to exert comparable therapeutic benefits [13]. In the models of regional cerebral ischemia reperfusion (I/R) injury, several studies have demonstrated that MSC-Exo administration can provide effective brain protection through the regulation of inflammatory and metabolic pathways and the inhibition of programmed cell death [14, 15]. Currently, it remains unknown whether MSC-Exo administration is equally effective in protecting the brain against global I/R injury resulted from CA and resuscitation. Thus, it is necessary to explore the effectiveness of MSC-Exo administration in alleviating post-resuscitation brain injury and its potential mechanism.

In the present study, we employed the in vitro hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) neuron model and in vivo CA/CPR swine model to explore the therapeutic effects of MSC-Exo administration. Our results showed that MSC-Exo administration produced effective neuronal protection in both in vitro and in vivo experiments. In addition, considering cell ferroptosis has been confirmed to be a key form of neuronal death in the brain after resuscitation and also become an effective therapeutic target for post-resuscitation brain protection [16, 17], the regulatory effect of MSC-Exo administration on brain ferroptosis was also investigated in this study. Our results showed that MSC-Exo administration significantly inhibited H/R-induced neuronal ferroptosis in vitro and CA/CPR-induced cortical ferroptosis in vivo. In the mechanistical study, we identified that protein arginine methyltransferase 1 (PRMT1) might be a key regulator of solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) expression, which could interact with the SLC7A11 promoter to enhance its transcriptional activity [18]. We further demonstrated that MSC-Exo administration promoted the recovery of PRMT1 expression, then the latter promoted SLC7A11 and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) expression, and finally inhibited neuronal ferroptosis. Additionally, the miR-320a-3p/tripartite motif-containing 21 (TRIM21) axis was a possible mechanism by which MSC-Exo administration activated the PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway in vitro. Thus, our study suggested that MSC-Exo administration alleviated post-resuscitation brain injury by inhibiting neuronal ferroptosis partly through activating the PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway.

Methods

MSC-Exo preparation and identification

First, human embryonic stem cell-derived MSCs (Hangzhou Nextexo Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) were obtained based on our previous study [10]. Second, the passage 4 generation of MSCs were cultured using a chemically defined proliferation medium. After cell density at 80% confluence was achieved, the supernatant of MSCs was collected to centrifuge at 4, 000 g for 20 min. Third, tangential flow filtration (VF05H4, Sartorius, Gottingen, Germany) was used to process cell supernatant at a ratio of concentration of 50: 1 to achieve its ultrafiltration and concentration. Fourth, a filter membrane (pore size 0.22 μm) was used to remove those particulates and impurities from cell supernatant. Finally, size exclusion chromatography (qEV10, Izon Science, Christchurch, New Zealand) was employed to purify the supernatant to obtain MSC-Exo. The typical morphology of MSC-Exo was confirmed using transmission electron microscope (Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), which displayed a bilayer membrane structure and a cup shape (Fig. 1A). The size distribution of MSC-Exo was evaluated using nanoparticle tracking analysis (ZetaView, Particle Metrix, Inning am Ammersee, Germany), which indicated that the size of nanoparticles was mainly ranged from 30 to 200 nm (Fig. 1B). In addition, western blot analysis confirmed specific expression of exosomal markers (HSP70, TSG101, CD63 and CD9) and absence of Calnexin in MSC-Exo (Fig. 1C).

Cell study

SH-SY5Y neuronal cells were provided by Meixuan Biotechnology Inc. (Shanghai, China). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium containing a concentration of 10% of fetal bovine serum, and placed in a cell incubator with the setting of 95% air and 5% CO2. The in vitro H/R model was established by 8 h of hypoxia and then 24 h of reoxygenation. During hypoxia, neuronal cells were cultured with glucose-deprived media under the condition of 94% N2, 5% CO2, and 1% O2. During reoxygenation, the culture of cells was recovered with complete media under normal condition. Four concentrations of 10, 20, 40, and 80 µg/ml were initially administered to observe the protective effects of MSC-Exo on neuronal cells against H/R injury, and then the optimal concentration (80 µg/ml) was chosen to use for the remaining cell experiments.

To confirm the uptake of MSC-Exo by neuronal cells, the exosomes were labeled with DiR (deep-red fluorescence) following the manufacturer’s instruction, and then 80 µg/ml of DiR-labeled exosomes were incubated with neuronal cells in a 12-well culture plate under normal condition. At 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h after co-culture, the nuclei of neuronal cells in the designated wells were stained using DAPI for 10 min, respectively. Subsequently, the images of exosomes’ cellular uptake were observed with the fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The results showed that the fluorescence of DiR-labeled exosomes in neuronal cells was gradually intensified following the extension of co-culture duration, which indicated that MSC-Exo were successfully absorbed into neuronal cells (Fig. 2).

To investigate the role of PRMT1 in MSC-Exo-mediated neuronal protection after H/R injury, the knockdown of PRMT1 gene in SH-SY5Y neuronal cells was established. After neuronal cells were seeded into the plates, and then they were transfected by short interfering RNA (siRNA) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) for a total of 48 h. Four different RNAi duplexes (Meixuan Biotechnology Inc., Shanghai, China) were used with the following sequences: PRMT1 siRNA1 sense GGCAGUACAAAGACUACAAGA, antisense UUGUAGUCUUUGUACUGCCGG; PRMT1 siRNA2 sense GGAAGCAGACGGUGUUCUACA, antisense UAGAACACCGUCUGCUUCCAG; PRMT1 siRNA3 sense GCAACUCCAUGUUUCAUAACC, antisense UUAUGAAACAUGGAGUUGCGG; control siRNA sense AUGAGAUAAGCUUAAGAUCGC, antisense GCGAUCUUAAGCUUAUCUCAU. Transfection efficiency was examined by western blot, in which the PRMT1 siRNA with best knockdown efficiency was used in the following experiments. In addition, the overexpression of PRMT1 gene was established using lentiviral transduction in SH-SY5Y neuronal cells. The lentivirus expressing PRMT1 and a control lentivirus expressing GFP were synthesized by Shanghai TranSheep Bio (Shanghai, China). Neuronal cells were transfected with the lentivirus for 72 h, and then selected using the puromycin. The multiplicity of infection in transfection was 20. The transfection efficiency was examined by western blot.

Swine study

The healthy male domestic swine weighing 38 ± 2 kg were purchased from Shanghai Jiagan Biotechnology Inc. (Shanghai, China), and fed under standard condition including room temperature (20–25°C), humidity (60–80%), spontaneous water intake, regular feeding, cleaning and disinfection. This swine study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. The in vivo CA/CPR model was established by 12 mins of CA and then 6 mins of CPR according to the methods of our previous studies [10, 16]. In brief, the swine received intramuscular and intravenous anesthesia, and then they were intubated and ventilated with tidal volume (10 ml/kg), respiratory frequency (12/min), and FiO2 (0.21). Subsequently, two pressure-monitoring catheters were inserted from right femoral artery and vein into thoracic aorta and right atrium to measure aortic and right atrial pressures, and a pacing electrode was inserted from right external jugular vein into right ventricle to induce CA. After that, the swine’ baseline measurements were obtained and they were randomly divided into one of the three groups: Sham, CA/CPR, or CA/CPR + MSC-Exo. After 12 min of untreated CA, CPR was initiated with mechanical chest compression (depth 5 cm, rate 100/min) and mechanical ventilation (tidal volume 7 ml/kg, respiratory frequency 10/min, FiO2 1.0). After 2 min of CPR, the epinephrine (20 µg/kg) was given and then repeated every 3 min. After 6 min of CPR, a single 150-J biphasic electrical defibrillation was delivered. If an organized rhythm was obtained accompanied with persistent mean arterial pressure of > 50 mmHg, the swine was regarded as successful resuscitation. If not, CPR was immediately resumed for 2 min, and followed by another electrical defibrillation. This protocol was repeated until successful resuscitation or for a maximum of 5 cycles. At 5 min after successful resuscitation, a dose of 2.5 × 1010 /kg of MSC-Exo diluted in 20 ml of normal saline was intravenously administered within 30 min in the CA/CPR + MSC-Exo group. Thereafter, all the resuscitated swine were monitored for 4 h and then observed for an additional 20 h. All the measurements of swine study including neurological evaluation, blood and tissue samples’ detection were finished by those technicians who were blinded to the study.

Cell Viability and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay

For the measurement of cell viability, neuronal cells were seeded in a 96-well culture plate, then the CCK-8 assay kit (Share-Bio Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was added into the wells to incubate at 37 °C for 3 h, and finally the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). For the measurement of LDH release, the supernatant of neuronal cells was collected from the culture plate, then mixed with the LDH assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) to incubate at 37 ℃ for 1 h, and finally measured at 450 nm using the microplate reader.

Apoptosis detection

For apoptosis detection in cell study, neuronal cells were harvested and resuspended, then incubated with the Annexin V-A/Propidium iodide-A apoptosis kit (Share-Bio Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 20 min, and finally detected by flow cytometry (Dakewe Biotech, Shenzhen, China). For apoptosis detection in swine study, tissue samples were harvested from cerebral cortex at 24 h after resuscitation, then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sliced into 5-µm sections, and finally stained with the TUNEL assay kit (Boster Biological Technology Co, Wuhan, China). The photographs of three random fields were captured at 200× magnification with the help of optical microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and then the percentage of TUNEL-positive cells was calculated in each photograph.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay

For the measurements of cytosolic and lipid ROS in cell study, neuronal cells were washed by phosphate buffer saline, and then incubated with 10 µM of 2′, 7′-dichloroflurorescein diacetate (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and 5 µM of BODIPY 581/591 C11 (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ) at 37 °C for 30 min, respectively. After that, fluorescence intensity of cell samples was measured using flow cytometry (Dakewe Biotech, Shenzhen, China). For the measurement of tissue ROS in swine study, tissue samples harvested from cerebral cortex were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, sliced into 5-µm sections, incubated with 2′, 7′-dichloroflurorescein diacetate (1:500, Solarbio, Beijing, China) at 37 °C for 30 min. After that, the photographs of three random fields were captured at 200× magnification with the fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and their fluorescence intensity was analyzed with the Image J image software (NIH, Bethesda, MD).

Ferrous Iron (Fe2+), malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione (GSH) assay

The samples were harvested from neuronal cells after H/R injury and cerebral cortex at 24 h after resuscitation, respectively. Subsequently, the contents of Fe2+, MDA and GSH were measured with the cell ferrous iron colorimetric assay kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China), and the MDA and GSH detection kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, respectively. Determinations were normalized with protein content.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM) analysis

The ultrastructure of neuronal ferroptosis was observed using TEM (Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). First, the samples of neuronal cells were fixed in an electron microscopy fixative solution to preserve its ultrastructural integrity. Second, cell samples were washed with phosphate buffer saline and gradually dehydrated with a graded ethanol series. Third, the samples were infiltrated with resin and polymerised at 60 °C for 48 h, then mounted on copper grids, and finally were photographed and observed under TEM.

Brain injury biomarkers and neurological function

Serum biomarkers of brain injury including neuron specific enolase (NSE) and S100β protein (S100β) were measured with their enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (Meixuan Biotechnology Inc., Shanghai, China) at baseline, and at 1 h, 2 h, 4 h and 24 h after resuscitation. Neurological function was evaluated using neurological deficit score (NDS) and cerebral performance categories (CPC) at 24 h after resuscitation, in which the NDS was scored from 0 (no observed neurologic deficit) to 400 (death or brain death) [19], and the CPC was scored from 1 (normal cerebral function) to 5 (dead) [20].

Western blot

The samples were harvested from neuronal cells after H/R injury and cerebral cortex at 24 h after resuscitation, respectively. Subsequently, the proteins in the samples were extracted with the RIPA lysate, then centrifuged at 12, 000 rpm at 4 °C for 20 min, and finally quantified with the quantitation kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). After that, the proteins were separated with the SDS-PAGE and transferred with the PVDF membrane, then incubated with primary anti-PRMT1 (1:1000, Proteintech, Rosemount, IL), anti-SLC7A11 (1:1000, Proteintech, Rosemount, IL), anti-GPX4 (1:1000, Proteintech, Rosemount, IL), anti-TRIM21 (1:1000, Proteintech, Rosemount, IL) and anti-GAPDH (1:1000, BBI Life Science Corporation, Shanghai, China) at 4 °C for 24 h, and finally incubated with the secondary antibody (1:5000, BBI Life Science Corporation, Shanghai, China) at room temperature for 1 h. The protein was visualized with ECL luminescent substrates and analyzed with the Image J software (NIH, Bethesda, MD). The relative expression levels of PRMT1, SLC7A11 and GPX4 were all standardized by GAPDH.

Co-immunoprecipitation

To investigate whether PRMT1 was degraded through the ubiquitination, co-immunoprecipitation was performed. First, neuronal cells were harvested after experiencing normal culture, H/R injury, or miR-320a-3p mimics administration (Quanyang Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), in which the MG132 (5µM) was added to the culture medium of neuronal cells at 4 h before sample collection. The samples were then lysed and centrifuged to extract the proteins. Second, protein A/G agarose beads (P2108, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) were pre-incubated with an anti-PRMT1 antibody (1: 50, Proteintech, Rosemount, IL) and a negative IgG antibody (1: 50, Proteintech, Rosemount, IL) at room temperature for 1 h. Third, the beads were incubated with the protein samples at 4 ◦C for 12 h. After that, the beads were washed and then resuspended in loading buffer and boiled at 95 ◦C for 5 min to denature the proteins. Finally, the processed proteins were measured using ubiquitin antibody (1:1000, Zenbio, Chengdu, China) with the method of western blot.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

To investigate whether PRMT1 bound to the SLC7A11 promoter, ChIP assay was performed with the EZ-Magna ChIP kit (Millipore, Billerica, MA). First, neuronal cells were harvested after experiencing normal culture or H/R injury, then fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min to cross-link deoxyribonucleic acids and proteins, and finally the latter’s reaction was terminated with 0.125 M glycine. Second, the samples were washed and centrifugated at 800 g for 5 min, then the sonication was used to shear the cross-linked chromatin into fragments (200–1000 bp), and finally the latter was immunoprecipitated with an anti-PRMT1 antibody (1: 50, Proteintech, Rosemount, IL) and a negative IgG antibody (1: 50, Proteintech, Rosemount, IL). Third, the samples of deoxyribonucleic acids were purified using magnetic beads, and then measured with ChIP-qPCR. The primers of SLC7A11 promoter were as follows: forward ACAGCTTTTGTTGCTCACTACG, reverse CCTGCCTGTCACACCAACTT.

Luciferase reporter assay

To investigate whether PRMT1 regulated the transcriptional activity of SLC7A11 promoter, the HEK293T cells were co-transfected with PRMT1-overexpressing plasmids or vector control, Firefly luciferase vector fused with wild-type SLC7A11 promoter, and Renilla luciferase vector at a ratio of 1: 1: 0.5. The transfection of PRMT1 overexpression plasmids (Shanghai TranSheep Bio, Shanghai, China) were finished with Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The promoter region of SLC7A11 was fused into a luciferase reporter (pGL4.10, Shanghai TranSheep Bio, Shanghai, China). The activities of Firefly and Renilla luciferase in each well were assessed by the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI), and then measured with a fluorescence spectrophotometer (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). The ratio of Firefly to Renilla luciferase activity was calculated, and the relative SLC7A11 luciferase activity was normalized to the value of corresponding vector control. To confirm the binding between miR-320a-3p and TRIM21, the wild-type or mutated TRIM21-3’-UTR sequences were cloned into the psiCHECK2.0 vector (TongYong Bio, Anhui, China), and HEK-293T cells were then transiently co-transfected by the psiCHECK2.0 plasmids with either miR-320a-3p mimics or negative controls. After that, the activities of Firefly and Renilla luciferase were similarly assessed using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System, then measured with a fluorescence spectrophotometer, and finally the relative TRIM21 Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with the SPSS 24.0 statistical software (IBM, Armonk, NY). Initially, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to evaluate the distribution of continuous variables. Levene’s test was applied to assess the homogeneity of variances. When the data were confirmed to be normally distributed and their variances were homogeneous, they were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and then compared with the Student’s t-test between two groups or ANOVA among more than two groups. If a significant difference was observed in the overall comparison of groups, the comparisons between any other two groups were performed with the Bonferroni test. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

MSC-exo administration protected neuronal cells against H/R injury

To investigate the protective effects of MSC-Exo administration on neuronal cells after H/R injury, SH-SY5Y cell lines were employed to establish the in vitro H/R model using 8 h of hypoxia and 24 h of reoxygenation. We observed that MSC-Exo administration increased the viability of neuronal cells after H/R injury in a concentration-dependent manner within the tested range (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, the optimal concentration of 80 µg/ml of MSC-Exo administration was chosen to investigate its inhibitory effects on neuronal LDH release, apoptosis and ROS production after H/R injury. We observed that LDH release, apoptosis ratio and cytosolic ROS production were significantly increased in the H/R and H/R + MSC-Exo groups compared with the NC group; however, the increases in them were markedly lower in the H/R + MSC-Exo group compared to the H/R group (Fig. 3B and F). These results indicated that MSC-Exo administration effectively alleviated neuronal injury and meanwhile promoted its survival after H/R injury. MSC-exo administration inhibited neuronal ferroptosis after H/R injury

To investigate the inhibitory effect of MSC-Exo administration on neuronal ferroptosis after H/R injury, the same concentration of 80 µg/ml of MSC-Exo was administered after H/R injury in SH-SY5Y neuronal cells. We observed that the contents of Fe2+, lipid ROS and MDA were significantly increased while GSH content was markedly decreased in the H/R and H/R + MSC-Exo groups compared with the NC group; however, MSC-Exo administration effectively reversed the changes of those ferroptosis-related products mentioned above compared to the H/R group (Fig. 4A and E). In addition, TEM analysis indicated that ultrastructural characteristics of cell ferroptosis including increased mitochondrial membrane density, decreased cristae abundance, reduced mitochondrial size and the appearance of vacuoles were observed after H/R injury in SH-SY5Y neuronal cells. Nevertheless, the ultrastructural changes of neuronal ferroptosis mentioned above were markedly milder in the H/R + MSC-Exo group than in the H/R group. (Fig. 4F). These results indicated that MSC-Exo administration alleviated H/R-induced neuronal injury partly through the inhibition of cell ferroptosis.

MSC-exo administration upregulated neuronal PRMT1 expression and therefore promoted PRMT1-mediated SLC7A11 promoter transcriptional activation after H/R injury

To investigate the potential mechanism of MSC-Exo administration for regulating neuronal ferroptosis after H/R injury, the PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway was chosen to evaluate in our in vitro H/R model. Initially, the effects of MSC-Exo administration on the expression levels of PRMT1, SLC7A11 and GPX4 were measured using western blot. We observed that all of them were substantially downregulated in the H/R and H/R + MSC-Exo groups compared with the NC group; however, MSC-Exo administration potently promoted their expression levels’ recovery compared to the H/R group (Fig. 5A and D). Subsequently, the regulatory effect of PRMT1 on the transcriptional activity of SLC7A11 promoter was explored using ChIP-PCR assay and dual-luciferase reporter assay. Our ChIP-PCR assay indicated that the interaction was existed between PRMT1 and SLC7A11 promoter under normal condition and also after H/R injury in neuronal cells, which hinted that PRMT1 could bind to the SLC7A11 promoter (Fig. 5E and G). However, only two independent biological replicates were performed in the ChIP-PCR assay, the statistical comparison couldn’t be performed to determine the difference of the interaction between different experimental conditions. After that, our dual-luciferase reporter revealed that PRMT1 overexpression effectively enhanced SLC7A11 promoter’s transcriptional activity under normal condition in HEK 293T cells (Fig. 5H). These results indicated that MSC-Exo administration inhibited H/R-induced neuronal ferroptosis through the activation of the PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway.

MSC-exo administration reversed H/R-induced neuronal PRMT1 expression downregulation possibly via regulating the miR-320a-3p/TRIM21 Axis

To further confirm the potential mechanism of MSC-Exo administration for upregulating neuronal PRMT1 expression after H/R injury, the key miRNA in the MSC-Exo was analyzed and then its mechanism for regulating PRMT1 expression was explored. First, the top 10 miRNAs including miR-21-5p, miR-221-3p, miR-320a-3p, miR-423-5p, miR-100-5p, miR-26a-5p, miR-191-5p, miR-22-3p, miR-143-3p and miR-146a-5p were confirmed based on our previous study [21]. Second, one key molecule, TRIM21 was chosen due to its specific role in promoting the ubiquitination-mediated degradation of PRMT1 protein [22]. Third, TRIM21 mRNA was predicted to have the targeted binding site with miR-320a-3p by TargetScan software (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, dual-luciferase reporter revealed that relative luciferase activity was significantly decreased by miR-320a-3p mimics in TRIM21 wild-type HEK-293T cells (Fig. 6B). Fourth, we observed that neuronal TRIM21 expression was significantly upregulated while PRMT1 expression was markedly downregulated in the H/R and H/R + miR-320a-3p mimics groups compared with the NC group; however, miR-320a-3p mimics effectively reversed neuronal TRIM21 upregulation and PRMT1 downregulation compared to the H/R group (Fig. 6C and E). Finally, ubiquitination assay showed that the level of PRMT1 ubiquitination was increased after H/R injury; however, miR-320a-3p mimics inhibited H/R-induced PRMT1 ubiquitination (Fig. 6F). These results indicated that MSC-Exo administration might secrete miR-320a-3p to inhibit TRIM21 mRNA translation, and then inhibit the latter to mediate PRMT1 degradation through the ubiquitination. Thus, the miR-320a-3p/TRIM21 axis was a possible mechanism by which MSC-Exo administration activated the PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway. PRMT1 knockdown weakened the inhibitory effect of MSC-exo administration on neuronal ferroptosis after H/R injury

To further investigate whether PRMT1 participated in the inhibitory process of MSC-Exo administration on neuronal ferroptosis after H/R injury, the knockdown of PRMT1 gene in SH-SY5Y neuronal cells was established and then confirmed by western blot (Figs. 7 A-7B). Similarly, the expression levels of PRMT1, SLC7A11 and GPX4 were substantially upregulated, the contents of Fe2+, lipid ROS and MDA were potently decreased, and the content of GSH was markedly increased in the H/R + MSC-Exo group compared with the H/R group, which indicated that MSC-Exo administration could activate the PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway and therefore inhibit H/R-induced neuronal ferroptosis. Additionally, PRMT1 knockdown further potently downregulated PRMT1, SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression, increased Fe2+, lipid ROS and MDA contents, and decreased GSH content compared to the H/R group, which indicated that PRMT1 knockdown could inactivate the PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway and thereby aggravate H/R-induced neuronal ferroptosis. Finally, PRMT1 knockdown markedly weakened the regulatory effects of MSC-Exo administration on neuronal PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway and ferroptosis in the H/R + MSC-Exo+siPRMT1 group compared with the MSC-Exo group (Fig. 7C and K). Overall, these results indicated that PRMT1 might be a key regulator of MSC-Exo administration for inhibiting H/R-induced neuronal ferroptosis.

PRMT1 Overexpression Inhibited H/R-Induced Neuronal Ferroptosis by Regulating the SLC7A11/GPX4 Pathway

To further confirm whether PRMT1 overexpression replicated the inhibitory effect of MSC-Exo administration on neuronal ferroptosis after H/R injury, the overexpression of PRMT1 gene in SH-SY5Y neuronal cells was established and then confirmed by western blot (Fig. 8A and B). Consequently, neuronal SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression were significantly downregulated, and meanwhile Fe2+, lipid ROS and MDA contents were markedly increased and GSH content was effectively decreased in the H/R and H/R+oePRMT1 groups compared with the NC group; however, PRMT1 overexpression significantly recovered neuronal SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression, and meanwhile markedly decreased Fe2+, lipid ROS and MDA contents and increased GSH content compared to the H/R group (Fig. 8C and J). These results indicated that PRMT1 overexpression could effectively replicate the inhibitory effect of MSC-Exo administration on H/R-induced neuronal ferroptosis by regulating the SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway.

Baseline and CA/CPR Characteristics in the Swine Study

Initially, 24 healthy male white domestic swine were used in this study. During the establishment of CA/CPR model, 3 swine in the CA/CPR group and 3 swine in the CA/CPR + MSC-Exo group were not resuscitated. Finally, the data of those unresuscitated 6 swine were excluded, and the remaining 18 swine were included for data analysis. At baseline, no differences in body weight, heart rate, mean arterial pressure, end-tidal carbon dioxide, and arterial pH, partial pressure of carbon dioxide, partial pressure of oxygen, and lactate were observed among the three groups (Fig. 9A and H). During CPR, coronary perfusion pressure was maintained at an even level in the CA/CPR and CA/CPR + MSC-Exo groups. Finally, coronary perfusion pressure and other CA/CPR characteristics including duration of CPR, dosage of epinephrine, and number of defibrillations that were required for establishing spontaneous circulation were not significantly different between the two groups (Fig. 9I and L). These results indicated that baseline and CA/CPR characteristics were consistent in those swine experiencing the CA/CPR procedures.

MSC-exo administration provided effective brain protection after CA and resuscitation

To investigate the protective effects of MSC-Exo administration on brain injury after CA and resuscitation, a clinically relevant, large-animal swine model was established using 12 min of CA and 6 min of CPR. We observed that post-resuscitation brain injury biomarkers including NSE and S100β were significantly increased in those resuscitated swine compared with the Sham group. However, the serum levels of NSE starting 2 h after resuscitation and the serum levels of S100β starting 1 h after resuscitation were markedly lower in the CA/CPR + MSC-Exo group than in the CA/CPR group (Fig. 10A and B). In addition, post-resuscitation neurological function including NDS and CPC were substantially abnormal in those resuscitated swine compared with the Sham group. Nevertheless, the scores of NDS and CPC at 24 h post-resuscitation were effectively decreased in those swine treated with the MSC-Exo compared to the CA/CPR group (Fig. 10C and D). Pathologic analysis manifested that cell apoptosis occurred in cerebral cortex after resuscitation in the CA/CPR and CA/CPR + MSC-Exo groups, and their apoptosis ratios were markedly higher when compared with the Sham group. Most importantly, MSC-Exo administration potently decreased post-resuscitation cortical apoptosis when compared to the CA/CPR group (Fig. 10E and F). These results indicated that MSC-Exo administration was an effective approach for protecting the brain against CA and resuscitation in swine.

MSC-exo administration activated the PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway and further inhibited cortical ferroptosis after CA and resuscitation

To investigate the effects of MSC-Exo administration on cortical PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway and ferroptosis after CA and resuscitation, cerebral cortex was further measured at 24 h after resuscitation. As we saw in the in vitro H/R model, the expression levels of PRMT1, SLC7A11 and GPX4 in cerebral cortex were substantially downregulated in those swine experiencing the CA/CPR procedures compared with the Sham group. However, all of them were markedly upregulated in those swine receiving MSC-Exo administration compared to the CA/CPR group (Fig. 11A and D). In addition, the contents of Fe2+, ROS and MDA were significantly increased and the GSH content was markedly decreased in cerebral cortex in those swine experiencing the CA/CPR procedures compared with the Sham group. Nevertheless, MSC-Exo administration effectively reversed the changes of those ferroptosis-related products in cerebral cortex compared to the CA/CPR group (Fig. 11E and I). Together with the data of cell experiments, we speculated that MSC-Exo administration inhibited post-resuscitation neuronal ferroptosis partly by activating the PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the therapeutic potential of MSC-Exo administration in post-resuscitation brain injury in the setting of CA and resuscitation. We demonstrated that the administration of MSC-Exo significantly alleviated H/R-induced neuronal injury in vitro and CA/CPR-induced brain injury in vivo. Subsequently, neuronal ferroptosis was confirmed to be a feasible therapeutic target, which was markedly inhibited by MSC-Exo administration in the in vitro and in vivo experiments. After that, we revealed that PRMT1 might participate in the process of neuronal ferroptosis inhibition induced by MSC-Exo administration, which could enhance SLC7A11 transcriptional activity and further upregulate SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression to suppress the ferroptosis. Furthermore, we found that MSC-Exo administration upregulated neuronal PRMT1 expression, and the latter’s overexpression could replicate the inhibitory effect of MSC-Exo administration on H/R-induced neuronal ferroptosis by regulating the SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway. Additionally, we found that the miR-320a-3p/TRIM21 axis was a possible mechanism by which MSC-Exo administration activated the PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway in vitro.

So far, post-resuscitation brain injury remains the leading cause of poor prognosis in CA victims, and its existing treatment strategies’ efficacy is limited [23]. Recently, MSC-Exo administration is proven to own a series of therapeutic effects (such as anti-inflammation, anti-oxidation, anti-apoptosis, repair and regeneration) and thereby attempted to use in a variety of diseases [24]. Especially, MSC-Exo administration also exhibits significant therapeutic potential in various acute and chronic brain injury. In the setting of ischemic stroke, Shen et al. [14] demonstrated that umbilical cord MSC-derived exosomes exerted effective neuroprotective effects by suppressing inflammatory pathways and modulating metabolic processes. In the setting of traumatic brain injury, Williams et al. [25] demonstrated that bone marrow MSC-derived exosomes improved neurologic outcomes by inhibiting apoptosis and promoting neural plasticity. In the setting of Parkinson’s diseases, Zhang et al. [26] demonstrated that olfactory mucosal MSC-derived exosomes improved improve clinical and behavioral outcomes by alleviating oxidative injury through enhancing TP53INP1-mediated mitophagy. Based on research evidence mentioned-above, we attempted to explore the effectiveness of MSC-Exo administration in alleviating global brain I/R injury induced by CA and resuscitation. First, MSC-Exo derived from human embryonic stem cells was preferentially chosen due to its several advantages (high quality, strong immunomodulatory function, great repair capability) over other sources (such as umbilical cord, bone marrow, etc.) [27, 28]. Second, the dose of MSC-Exo administration (2.5 × 1010 /kg) was used based on two previous swine studies [25, 29], in which it could effectively alleviate brain pathological injury and thereby promoted neurological recovery after traumatic brain injury. Our results showed that MSC-Exo administration effectively alleviated H/R-induced neuronal LDH release, apoptosis, oxidative stress and promoted its survival in vitro, and potently decreased CA/CPR-induced cortical apoptosis, brain injury and improved neurological dysfunction in vivo. Thus, our data supported that MSC-Exo administration could become a promising treatment for post-resuscitation brain protection.

Recently, growing evidence reveals that cell ferroptosis has emerged as one important form of regulated cell death contributing to the pathological process of multiple central nervous system injuries [30]. Especially, experimental studies have demonstrated that cell ferroptosis is a feasible therapeutic target by which several protective agents including sulforaphane, baicalein and liraglutide effectively alleviate post-resuscitation brain injury [16, 31, 32]. In theory, these pathological stimuli result in the accumulation of free iron ions in cells, and the latter catalyze the Fenton reaction, then promote the formation of lipid ROS and peroxides to destroy normal structure of cell membranes, and finally trigger cell ferroptosis [30]. In addition, the SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway plays pivotal role in neutralizing lipid peroxides, and has been considered as an important approach for the suppression of cell ferroptosis [33]. However, whether neuronal ferroptosis is a feasible target of brain protection produced by MSC-Exo administration in the setting of CA and resuscitation remains unclear. Furthermore, the regulatory effect of MSC-Exo administration on the SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway also requires investigation in the setting of central nervous system injuries. In the present study, our results showed that MSC-Exo administration effectively decreased neuronal Fe2+, ROS and MDA production and increased its GSH content after H/R injury in vitro and after resuscitation in vivo. In addition, MSC-Exo administration effectively promoted the recovery of neuronal SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression in the in vitro and in vivo experimental models. Thus, our data supported that the inhibition of cell ferroptosis mediated by the SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway was involved in the mechanism of post-resuscitation brain protection produced by MSC-Exo administration.

PRMT1, one of main members of the PRMT family, can regulate various cellular functions through the methylation of histone and non-histone proteins, such as gene transcription, deoxyribonucleic acids’ damage response, signal transduction, etc [34]. Recently, a few studies have preliminarily manifested that PRMT1 participates in the protective process of vital organs against I/R injury. In the setting of hepatic I/R injury, PRMT1 expression was confirmed to be decreased accompanied with increased aminotransferase in the injured liver, and its upregulation could be involved in the protective role of sitagliptin in alleviating hepatic injury [35, 36]. In the setting of cerebral I/R injury, Liu et al. [37] demonstrated that PRMT1 overexpression could alleviate cerebral injury by inhibiting neuronal necroptosis and apoptosis through suppressing the activation of receptor-interacting protein kinase 1. In the setting of CA and resuscitation, Wu et al. [38] demonstrated that PRMT1 could be a protective target by which palmitic acid methyl ester alleviated post-resuscitation neuronal inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction. Currently, the role and mechanism of PRMT1 in mediating post-resuscitation brain protection remain to be investigated. However, two studies have revealed that PRMT1 can exert the effective inhibitory effect on cell ferroptosis, and its mechanism is possibly related to the enhancement of transcriptional activity of SLC7A11 promoter [18, 39]. Based on these research evidence, we attempted to explore the potential role of PRMT1 in mediating post-resuscitation neuronal ferroptosis inhibition produced by MSC-Exo administration. Our results showed that neuronal PRMT1, SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression was downregulated accompanied with the occurrence of neuronal ferroptosis after H/R injury; however, MSC-Exo administration promoted the recovery of PRMT1 expression, then the latter’s upregulation enhanced SLC7A11 promoter’s transcriptional activity, and finally upregulated SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression to inhibit neuronal ferroptosis. Further data showed that the miR-320a-3p/TRIM21 axis was a possible mechanism by which MSC-Exo administration activated the PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway. Overall, our data supported that MSC-Exo administration might inhibit post-resuscitation neuronal ferroptosis partly through activating the PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway.

Limitations

There were some limitations in our study. First, although standard characterization of MSC-Exo was confirmed by its typical morphology, size distribution, and specific markers in this study; however, the effects of the exosomes derived from other cell types (e.g., fibroblasts) weren’t simultaneously observed in the experiments. Thus, it was difficult to confirm that those observed effects were specific to MSC-derived exosomes. Second, although our study preliminarily confirmed the protective effects of MSC-Exo administration on post-resuscitation brain injury, but only one single dose of MSC-Exo was chosen to administer in an ideal treatment window. Thus, its optimal dose and more clinically realistic timing require to be investigated in the future studies. Third, considering that brain injury was still severe at 24 h post-resuscitation in the CA/CPR and CA/CPR + MSC-Exo groups, the evaluation of longer-term survival and neurological function were needed to fully confirm post-resuscitation brain protection produced by MSC-Exo administration in the following studies. Fourth, although PRMT1 were confirmed to regulate SLC7A11 promoter’s transcriptional activity and further enhance the inhibiting effect of MSC-Exo administration on neuronal ferroptosis after H/R injury in vitro; however, its effect wasn’t verified in the in vivo CA/CPR model. Fifth, a positive control, such as the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1, was lacking in this study. Consequently, we couldn’t fully confirm that the ferroptosis was the dominant form of regulated cell death and also the primary pathway by which MSC-Exo administration alleviated neuronal injury in our experimental model.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that MSC-Exo administration could be an effective therapeutic intervention against post-resuscitation brain injury in the setting of CA and resuscitation, in which the protective effects were possibly achieved by inhibiting neuronal ferroptosis partly through activating the PRMT1/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway.
