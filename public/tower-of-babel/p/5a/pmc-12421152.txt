INDIGO randomised controlled digital clinical trial: INvestigating DIgital outcomes and quality of life in cancer survivors – a study protocol

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThere are estimated to be 3.4 million patients in the UK living after a diagnosis of cancer. We know very little about their quality of life or healthcare usage. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are tools which help to translate a patient’s quality of life into measurable categories, but how to do this at scale remains underexplored. The study employs a randomised design to assess different engagement strategies for optimising participation, data linkage and questionnaire completion in Northwest London and then nationally, with appropriate research approvals.Methods and analysisWe have designed and implemented an online, patient-completed, randomised observational trial. We will pilot it in Northwest London before national roll-out, using initially the General Practice (GP) record of a cancer diagnosis and then exploring the use of social media. The primary objective is to explore the feasibility of recruiting participants via self-identification or contact from the primary care research network and obtaining consent to link participants’ PROMs responses to their cancer registry records. Data collection occurs through a secure platform, with participants directly responsible for data entry. There is no formal target sample size because this is a feasibility study, and we want to explore how many patients we can recruit. Analyses will be conducted using descriptive statistics, repeated measures multilevel modelling and machine learning techniques. If a substantial difference in responses between randomisation arms is detected, ineffective strategies will be removed. If no clear difference is observed, recruitment will continue with periodic reviews based on response rates and data completeness.Ethics and disseminationThe Study Coordination Centre has obtained approval from the London—Surrey Research Ethics Committee and Health Research Authority. Introduction

It is estimated that in 2020 there were 3.4 million people in the UK living with or beyond a diagnosis of cancer.1 There have been studies which have sought to develop and optimise patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) in cancer care.2,6 Patient-reported outcome collection to date has been in the context of clinical trials assessing treatment or post-treatment.7,11 With tight entry criteria, patients in trials are not representative of the incident population and are limited in how much we can learn about the ‘real-world’ experiences of patients.12,15 Clinical trials tend to recruit younger and fitter patients, with a disparity in gender, and so offer a biased view of patient experience.16,18 There is a gap in our knowledge when we consider outcomes and service use for people who are living with or beyond cancer and who have completed their initial treatment. The purpose of this study is to explore the feasibility of collecting PROMs and service use in people who are no longer receiving routine care. We hope to improve our understanding of their health status-related quality of life outcomes.

PROMs are tools which help to translate patients’ quality of life and results of their treatment into categories that clinical teams can measure and act on.2 4 5 There have been studies looking at long-term outcomes, but these studies often focus on patients diagnosed with one cancer type19 20 or patients who were still on follow-up 1–5 years from diagnosis.7 19 20 There have been few large-scale studies exploring which healthcare services patients use in the long term. Those that have been performed often focus on costs, not qualitative measures.21

Historically, PROM studies involved paper-based questionnaires with response rates of 54%–66%. They are associated with significant costs in terms of administration and data processing. Secure digital platforms are being increasingly used to reduce costs.22,25 They offer an ability to edit, update and share questionnaires more easily, allowing iterative approaches to questionnaire design to maximise utility and minimise responder burden. Although clinical and administrative data are widely collected, PROMs data are largely missing and are not included within the National Cancer Patient Experience Survey (NCPES).26 The National Cancer Patient Experience Survey (NCPES) focuses on patients’ experience of healthcare services; therefore, it does not include any validated PROM questionnaires (eg, European Quality of Life 5 Dimensions 5 Level Version - EQ-5D-5L).

Public Health England successfully linked patients’ responses from NCPES to the national cancer registry with patients’ year of birth, sex, ethnicity and postcode.27 Linking patient-reported outcomes or experiences to national cancer registry data provides context to those measures. We therefore aim to be able to assemble a dataset that has treatment details and PROMS data, without undue burden for patients.

Reaching patients who are no longer receiving treatment or being followed up for their cancer has been a barrier to research. By using the primary care research infrastructure and social media, we hope to describe which methods of recruitment work across different demographic groups, tumour types and at different times post-treatment. We aim to explore outcomes in the short term (>1 year) and long term (>10 years) following a cancer diagnosis. The INDIGO study ("INvestigating DIgital Outcomes") is an innovative pan-cancer trial, in line with National Health Service (NHS) plans to transform digital health data collection,28 intending to recruit people diagnosed with any type of cancer aged over 16, able to understand, read and write written English and have online access. It will run as a pilot in Northwest London and will be scaled nationally.

Aims and outcomes

Our main aim is to develop a pragmatic evidence base to support the collection of patient-reported data for people diagnosed with cancer.

Our primary objectives are to assess the feasibility of recruiting participants via self-identification or contact from the primary care research network (PCRN) and obtaining consent to link participants’ PROMs responses to their cancer registry records.

Our secondary objectives are to assess the effectiveness of different methods of communication to trigger self-enrolment and to assess which PROM performs best in combination with EQ-5D-5L.

Our tertiary objectives are to assess the feasibility of collecting, filtering, grouping and interpreting free-text responses. We will also assess participants’ willingness to be followed longitudinally with repeated sampling.

Recruitment

We wish to assess the feasibility of recruiting into a self-enrolment digital PROMs study via participant self-assessment of suitability or contact from the PCRN, as no evidence exists yet.

Communication

We want to assess the different methods of communication which trigger participant self-enrolment. We shall explore their impact on participation rate across demographic groups. This will provide evidence currently missing from the literature.

The digital platform can provide real-time data on recruitment. The trial management group (TMG) will determine when recruitment appears to be plateauing to trigger the next stage of recruitment.

Understanding the most efficient and preferred PROMs questionnaires

EQ-5D-5L is used in research to assess health-related quality of life (HRQOL) while trying to minimise participant burden. It is useful for comparison across populations but has limited scope to explore subtle aspects in specific-disease populations. We want to assess which of three PROMs performs best in combination with EQ-5D-5L. We do not know which PROM tools are most acceptable to participants to describe their situation in the context of this study. We will randomise participants to different questionnaires and ask for their feedback.

The use of a digital platform allows us to trial PROMs that traditionally have been difficult to complete on paper without researcher support. We will test the feasibility of administering a Patient Generated Index (PGI) digitally without support. PGI has usually been administered face-to-face with a research team member guiding participants. We aim to understand if participants can complete it and if completion rates and quality are improved or impaired by using field validation to restrict the possible responses.

Analysing free-text data responses

We want to assess the feasibility of collecting, filtering, grouping and interpreting free-text responses. No evidence exists of the feasibility of collecting large-scale free-text responses in the context of PROMs collection in patients living with and beyond cancer. We will explore the performance of simple AI-based methods to classify free-text responses, and if successful, the tools may enable large-scale analyses of free text in larger studies.

Linkage to datasets

We will explore the feasibility of linking patients’ PROM data to their NHS records using their name, date of birth, sex assigned at birth and postcode. We will be testing this acceptability by exploring consent rates to linkage. We will investigate if the timing of this question impacts on consent rates. Participants will be randomised 50:50 to be asked near the start or end of the survey.

We wish to assess its technical feasibility. No evidence exists of the feasibility of obtaining participant-level consent at scale to generate linked datasets.

Methods and analyses

Design

INDIGO is an online, observational, randomised questionnaire-based study that collects self-reported data from cancer patients. It was codeveloped with two highly experienced cancer patients and uses the secure online platform (‘Qualtrics’), conforming to the ISO 27001 certification, already used in the NHS.29

The survey contains core components presented to every participant and two points of randomisation within the survey handled by the platform with no intervention from the clinical trial team.

Study setting

This is a national trial in the UK aiming to start in December 2023 and to finish recruitment in December 2025. The pilot phase involves the recruitment of participants in Northwest London to test the survey, responses and ability to reach the eligible population. To do the latter, we shall partner with our Local Health and Care Record Exemplar to compare aggregated figures of our population demographics with the cancer patients diagnosed in Northwest London (eg, how many women/men recruited vs how many diagnosed). Following analyses and any amendments required to improve accessibility, usability and data quality, the study will then open across England and the devolved nations.

Recruitment has two stages. The first stage is recruitment via GP practices, using Egton Medical Information Systems (EMIS) and SystmOne searches (designed by Northwest London Clinical Research Network (CRN) to meet trial eligibility criteria and filtering out eligible patients who have declined Short Message Service (SMS) contact or being approached about research studies). The clinical research networks will recruit GP practices who will perform patient identification by running primary care GP database searches. The eligible patients are then sent an SMS message inviting them to participate and a link which takes them to the information sheet and consent to participate if they wish.

The second stage of recruitment uses social media. By using general and targeted posts on social media, the aim is to encourage participation from people who meet the eligibility criteria. There will be a mixture of free and paid-for posts across different platforms, for example, Meta, Twitter and Google. The posts will contain a link that takes them to the information sheet and consent to participate if they wish. We will also partner with several different organisations to help spread knowledge of the study more widely.

There is no formal target sample size because this is a feasibility study, and we want to explore how many patients we can recruit. However, we know that there are 3.6 million long-term cancer survivors; therefore, a 10% recruitment rate would yield a sample size of 360 000 patients, although we expect research participation to be lower than this. We have obtained research approval to recruit up to 2.4 million patients although we do not expect to reach this number.

Core components

Consent

In the context of this project, we will use electronic consent. Participants will have the possibility to contact a member of the study team via email if they have any questions before consenting.

As per the Health Research Authority and the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (HRA-MHRA) guidance on e-consenting, we will use ‘simple electronic signatures’ at initial enrolment. Potential participants will be provided with information which they can review online or download. The participant will consent on a digitally administered form. As this is a low-risk study, participants will be allowed to read the information on the study, consent and participate in the same sitting. We believe this will minimise a potential barrier to participation that will exist if participants are forced to return to the platform. The participant flow was developed with patient public involvement contributors to balance participant burden with trial recruitment.

A cooling-off period will be possible. They will be able to read the information, return to consent and/or return to complete the survey up to a week later. There is not a fixed time point for completion, and questionnaires are available until the end of the study.

During completion, the participant will be free to stop at any point and leave the survey without providing any explanation.

Participants who refuse to consent to the study will not have access to the questionnaires. They will have the opportunity to tell us why they declined, should they wish. This question will not be mandatory, and participants can leave the page without giving reasons.

A second and separate set of consent questions will be asked of participants to link the survey responses to NHS data.

At the end, participants will be asked about future contact and their willingness to hear the results of the study, participate in future studies and receive a short follow-up survey a week postcompletion. If they consent, they will be asked to enter a valid email address. It can be their own email address or that of a trusted friend or a family member. The follow-up survey aims to check for any distress caused by participation and if any healthcare resource utilisation has been triggered.

Quality of life questionnaires

We have chosen to explore the value of different PROM tools by completion rates and the level of value to the participant.

EQ-5D-5L will be administered to all participants. Participants will then be randomly allocated one further PROM, drawn equally from EORTC Core Quality of Life questionnaire (EORTC QLQ-C30), Social Difficulties Inventory (SDI) and PGI (figure 1). Other sections

Once the participant has decided to participate, they self-enrol and provide online consent. It is acceptable to get help from their friends or family.

The participants will be required to enter demographic data (eg, employment status, outward postcode and education), including protected characteristics (eg, age, sex, gender and gender reassignment, ethnicity, disability, sexual orientation and marital status) because it is not often reported how these may affect patients during and after their treatment. It will allow the trial team to be transparent about any participant imbalance and to identify which groups are under-represented.

Then a core set of questions will follow and focus on their cancer diagnoses, asking about the year(s) of diagnosis, treatments and outcomes (eg, remission). The focus turns to their community service use to understand the frequency of visits to GP or other health professionals, the use of supplementary medication and/or non-medical help. Finally, a set of questions will focus on other health problems and their height and weight (available in inches and centimetres and in stones and kilograms) to calculate their BMI. These questions are to allow us to explore variables which may be potential covariates when examining PROM scores.

The participants will not be identifiable from the information collected.

Randomisation

Randomisations are handled by the secure online platform and aim to answer the completion rates and participant satisfaction.

The first randomisation relates to the positioning of the question asking for consent to link their responses to NHS data. The participants will be randomised to be asked this early or at the end. The aim of this is to answer:

Do participants give their consent to link their responses to cancer registry data in the context of a self-enrolment digital study?

Do participants give their consent to link their responses to cancer registry data in the context of a self-enrolment digital study?

Does its positioning affect participation or survey completion rates?

Does its positioning affect participation or survey completion rates?

Participants are informed that providing the data to allow linkage to registry datasets will mean that they disclose personal identifiable data and could be identified.

The second randomisation relates to the second validated questionnaire. All patients will receive the EQ-5D-5L and will be randomised to receive one of EORTC QLQ-C30, SDI or PGI. Participants will always be equally randomised to each of the second PROM questionnaire.

The third randomisation only applies to the 33% of participants randomised to receive the PGI. This relates to how the PGI is constructed. We wish to explore how to best present PGI on a digital platform: using inbuilt rules and constraints (eg, preventing out-of-range numbers from being entered) or without any validation functions. To explore this, we will therefore further randomise the participants who have been randomised to receive the PGI. This will be a 50:50 split; therefore, 16.5% of the total participants will be in each arm of this randomisation.

End of the survey

This study does not impact treatment, and no changes to a participant’s care or treatment are made. A helpline is offered by a cancer charity to support any participant following reflection on their quality of life and the impact of their cancer and its treatment30. After the submission of their answers, participants can choose to download the questions and their answers. The ‘thank you’ page contains links to online resources approved by the trial team in case reflecting on their quality of life has triggered any health-seeking behaviour.

The participant will be able to decide if there is any future contact and its nature. To check if participation caused any difficulties for the participant or triggered any healthcare resource utilisation, there is an optional follow-up two-question survey 7 days later. Participants are asked if they consent to receive the same survey 12 months later, so we can assess any change over time.

Data management

Participants can complete the questionnaire with or without providing identifiable data and consenting to linkage with national cancer datasets. Participants are in control as to how much personal identifiable data they disclose. Participants are explained where personal identifiable data could be captured so they can make an informed choice as to what they choose to disclose.

Storage arrangements

Data will be collected using Qualtrics, which is accessed under a licence agreement. Qualtrics is a secure online platform that has already been used in the NHS29 and was used for research during the coronavirus pandemic in 2020.31 32

When appropriate, we will download participant consent and data to NHS computers operating within a secure environment using the Health and Social Care Network (HSCN—previously known as N3). Personal identifiable data and anonymous data will be stored with the same levels of security. For patients who agreed to have their data linked to cancer registries, their data will be shared with NHS using HSCN and NHS.net environments.

A trial number will be assigned to patients who consent to have their data linked. This will allow us to receive participants’ pseudo-anonymised health records in an ISO-27001:2013 certified research environment at Imperial College London, compliant with the NHS England Data Security and Protection Toolkit.

The cancer registries had active input in the creation and editing of the consents requested throughout the questionnaire to ensure they would accept the request for data at the time of linkage. To receive the data, an application will be made through NHS England's Data Access Request Service (DARS). Requests will be made after the pilot phase to test the proposed methodology and then once the trial has recruited nationally.

Recruitment timeline

Following REC-HRA approval, the first phase of the study will start in Northwest London in December 2023 and will be considered as the pilot study. The pilot study will run until July 2024; a new phase will start at the end of 2024 with the recruitment of eligible participants via the rest of the CRNs/Regional Research Delivery Networks (RRDNs) in England. Finally, the second stage of the second phase will open in 2025 when the trial will be advertised via social media channels. Any protocol amendments will be submitted for sponsor and Research Ethics Committee (REC) approval as standard but are implemented centrally on the platform and therefore do not require changes or notification for the recruiting sites.

Statistical methods

Sample size for pilot phase

There are 37 000 people in Northwest London with a coded diagnosis of cancer in their primary care records. To show a 20% difference between arms of the trial 80% (±3%) versus 60% (±3%) in terms of agreement to linkage to cancer registries or completion of questions, we will require 300–500 subjects per randomisation. A 100% participation rate will yield 37 000 participants. Using the NHS England figure of 45%–55% participation rate, we would have approximately 18 000 participants (although this is unlikely as the 18-month survey is much earlier in people’s cancer journey). If we have a participation rate like that of other primary care trials that use SMS to contact potential participants, this will recruit 1850 participants.

Other statistical methods

There is a paucity of evidence in this area on which to base statistical calculations. There are very few digital PROMs studies, and we are not aware of any self-identification/self-enrolment PROMs studies. Therefore, our recruitment is based on the above considerations.

In the possible, but unlikely, situation where there is an overwhelming difference between arms of the randomisation, the Expert Advisory Group (EAG) will conclude if there is evidence on which to accept or reject the null hypothesis. The questionnaire will be adapted to remove that randomisation. If there is no clear difference at interim analysis, the trial will continue recruiting with the TMG determining the next analysis point based on recruitment rates and initial completion metrics.

All analyses will be conducted between Imperial College Healthcare NHS Trust and Imperial College London. All data will be handled in accordance with national data protection and information governance guidance.

We will seek explicit consent to store the enrolment log, consent form and coded data for 10 years following completion of the study. This will mean that participant data is stored for a maximum of 12 years if they are recruited at the very start of the 2-year recruitment period. The data will be analysed with conventional statistical methods (eg, repeated measures multilevel modelling), which will inform machine learning methods to be employed.

Monitoring and quality assurance

Data will be provided and entered directly by participants, so only the data subjects will be responsible. Aggregated data will be fed back to the Trial Management Team and the Expert Advisory Group. Furthermore, a trial email address has been created to answer participants’ queries, requests and/or warnings about the questionnaire.

Harms

Imperial College London negligent and non-negligent insurance applies. The study shall be subject to inspection and audit by Imperial College London under their remit as sponsor. This is a low-risk study which does not change treatment; therefore, the potential risk of harm to the patients or staff is minimal.

Patient and public involvement

This study was codeveloped over 18 months with two patient experts in PROMs and data research in cancer care. They supported the approach whereby participants decide how much they can share. They provided insight into the nature and delivery of the questions. It was iteratively developed and tested with over 30 members of the public recruited via a range of channels, for example, local Patient and Public Involvement (PPI) groups, Cancer Research United Kingdom (CRUK).

Ethics and dissemination

The Study Coordination Centre has obtained approval from the London—Surrey Research Ethics Committee and Health Research Authority (23/PR/0405). We will publish and disseminate the results in local, national and international meetings, in peer-reviewed journals, on social media and on websites.

The sponsor and the funders had no role in study design, conduct, analysis or reporting, although some staff employed by the main funder were also members of the EAG and therefore helped guide study conduct and analysis.
