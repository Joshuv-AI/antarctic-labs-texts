Effects of nitrogen allocation and photosynthetic proteins response in peanut leaves on photosynthesis under conditions of water scarcity and nitrogen deficiency
Authors: Yang Xu, Zhimeng Zhang, Qing Guo, Hao Feng, Feifei Qin, Guanchu Zhang, Hong Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeaf nitrogen allocation and photosynthetic proteins response can affect net photosynthetic rate (Pn), ultimately influencing crop yield under diverse environmental stresses. However, the internal relationship between Pn with leaf nitrogen allocation and photosynthetic proteins response under nitrogen or water scarcity in peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.) remains elusive. Here, comprehensive physiological property and proteomic analyses of peanut were conducted, revealing that both nitrogen and water scarcity remarkably impeded leaf growth and reduced Pn. Nitrogen deficiency significantly reduced the total nitrogen content per unit leaf area (Narea), chlorophyll content, and Pn, whereas drought stress caused a greater decline in photosynthetic nitrogen use efficiency (PNUE). The allocation of leaf nitrogen to photosynthetic components, including the carboxylation system and electron transport system in leaves, was significantly reduced when subjected to individual or combined deficiency. Proteomic analyses exhibited that several key photosynthetic proteins underwent a decrease under both single and combined water and nitrogen deficiency conditions. Thereby, Pn may decline due to the disruption of nitrogen allocation and down-regulated expression of photosynthetic proteins under these stress conditions. Our findings establish a benchmark for future research exploring the roles of leaf nitrogen allocation and photosynthetic proteins in the plant’s response to nitrogen or water deficiency.Keywords: Peanut, Nitrogen deficiency, Water scarcity, Photosynthesis, Proteomic analysis, Net photosynthetic rate

Background

Peanuts (Arachis hypogaea L.) are important oil and cash crops in the world, providing an excellent source of protein, calories, vitamins, and minerals for human beings [1, 2]. For plant yield, photosynthesis is the material basis and is determined by several environmental factors, among which water and nitrogen are two crucial factors [3–5]. Water scarcity leads to a decrease in crops’ internal water potential, reducing the stomatal size and density of crop leaves, hindering the entry of CO2, resulting in a loss of net photosynthetic rate (Pn), and limiting crop photosynthesis [6–8]. Stomatal closure is the main reason to reduce photosynthesis under mild or moderate drought stress, while the inactivation of photosynthetic enzyme activity under severe drought stress also leads to a decrease in photosynthetic rate [9]. Nitrogen serves as an important structural component of nitrogen-containing compounds, including amino acids, proteins, nucleic acids, chlorophyll, and enzymes. Consequently, it exerts a direct or indirect influence on plant photosynthesis by modulating leaf chlorophyll content, photosynthetic rate, and the enzymes involved in photochemical reactions and photorespiration [10–12]. Nitrogen also occupies a pivotal position in the photosynthetic process, with more than half of the leaf nitrogen allocated to the photosynthetic system [13], and plant growth is significantly correlated with nitrogen content within this system [14]. Thus, water and nitrogen play a vital role in plant photosynthesis, thereby considerably affecting crop growth and yield.

Crop photosynthesis exhibits a close correlation with leaf nitrogen allocation and photosynthetic nitrogen use efficiency (PNUE), and improving PNUE and achieving efficient utilization of nitrogen within the photosynthetic system can markedly increase crop nitrogen use efficiency and yield [15, 16]. Numerical simulation results reveal that optimizing the allocation of nitrogen among photosynthetic proteins could enhance crop photosynthetic capacity by up to 60%, without necessitating additional nitrogen input [17–19]. Under conditions of low nitrogen and drought stress, the coordination of nitrogen allocation also plays a crucial role in maintaining the stability of PNUE [12, 20]. Nitrogen allocation between photosynthetic and non-photosynthetic proteins reflects a trade-off between productivity and environmental adaptability [21, 22]. Specifically, investing more leaf nitrogen in structural proteins diminishes photosynthetic capacity and PNUE, while also increasing leaf longevity [23, 24]. It is speculated that under stress conditions, crops allocate more nitrogen towards non-photosynthetic systems to adapt to the stressful environment and coordinate crop growth, thereby potentially reducing photosynthesis and PNUE [12, 25]. In addition to this, leaf nitrogen in photosynthetic apparatus consists of nitrogen allocation to light-harvesting (nitrogen in light-harvesting chlorophyll–protein complexes), to electron transport system (total nitrogen content of cytochrome f, ferredoxin NADP reductase, and the coupling factor), and to carboxylation system (Rubisco) [26]. Notably, nitrogen allocation was subject to a positive effect of photosynthesis [26]. We hypothesize that drought and nitrogen may influence peanut photosynthesis via regulating nitrogen allocation across different photosynthetic apparatus within peanut leaves.

High-throughput proteomics offer a comprehensive understanding of the molecular changes that plants undergo in response to nitrogen deficiency or drought stress [18, 27–29]. Some studies found that, beyond proteins involved in nitrogen and carbohydrate metabolism, photosynthetic proteins also undergo significant alterations [30–32]. Under nitrogen deficiency conditions, proteins involved in photosynthesis, electron transport chain, and carbon fixation underwent notable changes in oil-seed rape leaves [16]. Photosynthetic proteins constitute a substantial portion of the identified proteins in wheat leaves under water stress, with a general decrease in the abundance of proteins associated with photosynthetic complexes, ATP synthase complexes, carbon fixation, and carbon rearrangement [33]. These studies provide invaluable insights into the protein-level changes in photosynthesis under nitrogen deficiency or drought stress. The effects of nitrogen and water on crop growth are intricate and often interact with each other. However, proteomic adjustments under combined drought and nitrogen deficiency in peanut remain largely unexplored. We hypothesize that drought and nitrogen may influence peanut photosynthesis by regulating some specific photosynthetic protein expression. In our study, briefly, we aim to: (i) investigate peanut photosynthetic and proteomic responses to single and combined drought and nitrogen deficiency, (ii) analyze leaf nitrogen allocation patterns and PNUE, (iii) explore key proteins involved in stress adaptation, thereby providing a scientific foundation for improving photosynthetic utilization to increase peanut production.

Results

Effects of various treatments on the physiological traits of peanuts

Four distinct treatments were conducted to analyze the physiological characteristics of peanuts. The control group, receiving adequate watering and nitrogen application, was denoted as WWNA. The nitrogen deficiency group was designated as WWNN, drought group as DSNA, and the water and nitrogen dual-deficiency group as DSNN. Compared to the WWNA treatment, WWNN, DSNA, and DSNN all significantly reduced the number and dry weight of peanut leaves, with severity following the order DSNN > WWNN > DSNA (Fig. 1a, b). Under nitrogen-application conditions, drought stress caused a notable decrease of 7.03% in leaf number and 17.95% in leaf dry mass compared with well-watered conditions (WWNA_vs_DSNA; Fig. 1a, b). The combined treatment of drought stress and nitrogen deficiency substantially reduced the leaf number by 11.32% and the leaf dry mass by 32.91% compared to the well-watered condition (WWNA_vs_DSNN; Fig. 1a, b). However, without nitrogen fertilizer application, drought stress alone did not affect the physiological characteristics of peanut leaves (WWNN_vs_DSNN; Fig. 1a-d). Compared to plants fertilized with nitrogen, nitrogen deficiency markedly decreased the leaf area per plant by 36.29% under normal water supply and by 30.84% under drought stress, respectively (WWNA_vs_WWNN, DSNA_vs_DSNN; Fig. 1c). Interestingly, the changes in specific leaf mass (SLM) exhibited an inverse trend to those observed in individual leaf area, showing increases of 13.41% and 18.81% under well-watered and drought stress conditions, respectively (WWNA_vs_WWNN, DSNA_vs_DSNN; Fig. 1d). Additionally, compared to nitrogen application, nitrogen deficiency led to a substantial decline in chlorophyll content in peanut leaves, with reductions of 39.05% under normal water supply and 41.58% under drought stress, respectively (WWNA_vs_WWNN, DSNA_vs_DSNN; Fig. 1e). These results unveiled that water had a distinct effect on the number and dry mass of peanut leaves, whereas nitrogen exerted a substantial influence on all measured indicators. Notably, no interaction between water and nitrogen was observed for any of these physiological indicators. These findings indicate that the effect of nitrogen fertilizer on peanut leaves surpasses that of water.

Effects of different water and nitrogen treatments on the photosynthetic nitrogen utilization efficiency of peanut leaves

Compared to the WWNA treatment, the WWNN and DSNN treatments exhibited significant reductions in leaf nitrogen content, by 44.57% and 36.11%, respectively. Conversely, the single drought stress treatment showed a 14.15% increase in leaf nitrogen content (WWNA_vs_DSNA; Fig. 2a). Notably, nitrogen deficiency led to a notable decrease in specific leaf nitrogen Narea under identical water conditions, with reductions of 37.06% under well-watered conditions and 33.14% under drought stress (WWNA_vs_WWNN, DSNA_vs_DSNN; Fig. 2b). Furthermore, nitrogen deficiency, water deficiency, and water nitrogen dual-deficiency treatments significantly reduced the net photosynthetic rate (Pn) of peanut leaves by 40.86%, 26.87%, and 50.97%, respectively (Fig. 2c). When subjected to water scarcity but with nitrogen application, the groups showed a Pn that was 49.15% higher than that of the groups experiencing both water and nitrogen dual-deficiency (DSNA_vs_DSNN; Fig. 2c). Under drought stress conditions, photosynthetic nitrogen use efficiency (PNUE) decreased significantly, with reductions of 31.36% and 30.52% recorded for the DSNA and DSNN treatments, respectively (Fig. 2d). Interestingly, water had a significant effect on all indicators except for Narea, while nitrogen had no considerable impact on PNUE. Water-nitrogen interaction only significantly influences Pn.

Effects of different water and nitrogen treatments on leaf nitrogen allocation in peanut leaves

Compared to WWNA, nitrogen deficiency, drought stress, and water nitrogen dual-deficiency notably reduced the total nitrogen content of photosynthetic components (Nphoto), with decreases of 33.65%, 32.90%, and 32.72%, respectively (Fig. 3a). The leaf photosynthetic nitrogen within the carboxylation system (Ncb) were substantially lower subjected to nitrogen deficiency, water deficiency, and water nitrogen dual-deficiency, reduced by 33.84%, 41.43%, 29.59% (Fig. 3b). Similarly, the nitrogen content within the electron transport system (Net) also decreased significantly under these stress conditions, with reductions of 19.05%, 28.76%, and 16.49%, respectively (Fig. 3c). Notably, there was no significant difference in the nitrogen of light capture (Nlc) between the water-deficiency treatment and the control, and the Nlc under the water nitrogen dual-deficiency treatment was the lowest, exhibiting a 47.97% reduction (WWNA_vs_DSNN; Fig. 3d). The nitrogen content of non-photosynthetic components (Nnon-photo) increased 129.53% with the drought stress treatment (WWNA_vs_DSNA; Fig. 3e). Except for Nlc, water treatment significantly influenced the other nitrogen forms, while nitrogen treatments had a pronounced effect on Nphoto, Nlc, and Nnon-photo components (Fig. 3). Additionally, there was a significant interactive effect of water and nitrogen on Nphoto, Ncb, and Nlc (Fig. 3).

These observations underscore the substantial influence of Ncb and Nphoto on the Pn and PNUE of peanut leaves.

Identification and analysis of differentially expressed photosynthetic proteins via proteomics

A total of 8,214 proteins were quantified across all three replicate samples in this study. Among these, 1,135 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified across the four treatments, with a notable overlap of 68 DEPs common to all (Fig. 4a). Specifically, 345, 757, and 481 DEPs were identified in the comparisons of WWNA_vs_DSNA, WWNA_vs_DSNN, and WWNA_vs_WWNN, respectively (Fig. 4a). Notably, 61 proteins were linked to photosynthesis. To better understanding of these photosynthetic proteins under the WWNA, WWNN, DSNA, and DSNN treatments, all 61 were subjected to Principal Component Analysis (PCA; Fig. 4b). The PCA results revealed striking differences in the proteome regulation patterns concerning the photosynthetic proteins under the four treatments. PC1 accounted for 41.94% of the variance in photosynthetic proteins, clearly distinguishing DSNA and DSNN from the WWNA. PC2 explained 13.20% of the variance, effectively separating WWNN and DSNN from the control group (Fig. 4b). Furthermore, a hierarchical clustering analysis was conducted to obtain a holistic perspective on the expression changes of the 61 photosynthetic proteins under the four treatments (Fig. 4c). The samples are clearly divided into two distinct clusters: one cluster contains samples only subjected to drought stress, while the other cluster groups the remaining samples (Fig. 4c).

Proteomics analysis. a Venn analysis of the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in different treatments. b Principal component analysis (PCA) of photosynthetic proteins in the WWNA versus the WWNN, DSNA, and DSNN treatments. c Hierarchical clustering of photosynthetic proteins exhibiting differential expression patterns in response to different treatments

The responses of the carboxylation and Calvin cycle proteins to the stress treatments were characterized. Specifically, in WWNN, a decline was observed in one Transaldolase/Fructose-6-phosphate aldolase, whereas the Rubisco LSMT substrate-binding protein exhibited an increase (Table 2). Similarly, in DSNA, two Transaldolase/Fructose-6-phosphate aldolases demonstrated a reduction, coupled with an elevation in one Rubisco LSMT substrate-binding protein (Table 2). Furthermore, in DSNN, two Ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase (RuBPCase) proteins displayed an increase, whereas two Transketolase, thiamine diphosphate binding domain, two Transaldolase/Fructose-6-phosphate aldolase, and one lactate/malate dehydrogenase protein exhibited a decrease (Table 2). One Transaldolase/Fructose-6-phosphate aldolase (A0A445ERB8) showed a consistent down-regulated trend across all three treatments (Table 2).

Discussion

Drought stress and nitrogen deficiency are crucial constraints hindering peanut growth [34, 35]. Moderate nitrogen application can mitigate the inhibitory effect of drought stress on growth and substantially alleviate its detrimental consequences [36–38], while some studies indicate that the application of nitrogen fertilizer may heighten plants’ sensitivity to drought stress [39]. In our study, drought stress, nitrogen deficiency, and dual-deficiency significantly decreased the peanut leaf biomass and Pn, whereas the magnitude of reduction was less pronounced with nitrogen application than without nitrogen application under drought stress (DSNA_vs_DSNN; Figs. 1a, 2c). We propose that increased nitrogen availability can ameliorate the negative effects of water limitation in peanuts. Intriguingly, nitrogen deficiency also increased the specific leaf mass (Fig. 1d). However, the underlying reasons for this phenomenon’s occurrence, along with its potential biological role in stress adaptation, need further in-depth investigation. Additionally, the chlorophyll content of leaves was affected by nitrogen deficiency, whereas it remained unaffected by drought stress (Fig. 1e), which is attributed to the fact that nitrogen application furnishes the necessary proteins for chlorophyll synthesis. Under drought stress conditions, the nitrogen content and specific leaf nitrogen Narea reached their peak levels (Fig. 2a, b), suggesting that the reduction in leaf dry mass under drought stress leads to a concentration of nitrogen content and specific leaf nitrogen. This observation underscores the intricate response and adaptation mechanisms of plants to their growth environment. The relevant conclusion remains scarcely cited in the context of drought stress, but is referenced in scenarios where nitrogen supply is inadequate. Under nitrogen deficiency, crops tend to curtail leaf expansion and utilize more nitrogen to maintain a relatively high chlorophyll content and photosynthetic rate, consequently enhancing carbon fixation [40]. This strategic adjustment underscores the plants’ resilience and ability to optimize resource allocation in adverse environmental conditions.

Numerous studies across various plant species have extensively examined the intricate interplay between water and nitrogen on plant photosynthesis [41–44]. Nitrogen is allocated between photosynthetic and non-photosynthetic proteins in plants. This reflects a delicate equilibrium between productivity and environmental adaptability. In the previous studies, plants invest more nitrogen to non-photosynthetic fractions under stressful conditions [12, 21]. In this study, under single and combined drought and nitrogen deficiency, the allocation of leaf nitrogen to photosynthetic components including Nphoto, Ncb, and Net, decreased uniformly (Figs. 3a-c and 5). However, under drought stress, the application of nitrogen fertilizer constrained the transport of nitrogen to Ncb and Net, causing Nlc levels to rise almost to those observed in the control group (Figs. 3a-d and 5). Nphoto, Ncb, and Net exhibit strong, statistically significant positive correlations with PNUE as shown in Table 1. A decrease in nitrogen allocation to these photosynthetic components can lead to a reduction in PNUE and photosynthetic capacity. Moreover, the reduction of nitrogen allocation to the Ncb or Net may disrupt the balance among light energy capture, electron transfer, and energy utilization. These alterations may be the primary cause of photosynthetic inhibition and PNUE reduction under these stressful conditions, which aligns with the results in the previous study [36, 45, 46]. Nitrogen deficiency led to a marked decline in Ncb under well-watered conditions and in Nlc under both water conditions compared to nitrogen application conditions (Fig. 3b, d). Additionally, the Pn also exhibited a higher level under nitrogen application than under nitrogen deficiency (Fig. 2c). Consequently, the application of nitrogen further underscores the critical role of coordinated nitrogen allocation among various components of the photosynthetic apparatus in enhancing Pn.

In this study, an in-depth analysis of proteome data revealed that the DEPs were predominantly involved in the Calvin cycle, photosynthetic electron transport, and light-harvesting system (Tables 2, 3 and 4). Notably, a higher number of photosynthetic DEPs were identified under nitrogen deficiency compared to drought stress, with dual deficiency exhibiting the highest abundance of these proteins (Fig. 4a; Tables 2, 3 and 4). However, changes in photosynthetic protein levels were more pronounced in wheat leaves subjected to drought treatment than to nitrogen deficiency [30]. It’s worth mentioning that this study was conducted in a pot experiment with peanuts, a legume crop capable of absorbing nitrogen through nitrogen fixation, which may explain the discrepancy in results compared to wheat. Previous studies have indicated that DEPs related to photosynthesis positively regulate ramie growth [31]. In this study, photosynthetic DEPs including Transaldolase/Fructose-6-phosphate aldolase (A0A445ERB8) in the carboxylation reaction, NADPH-dependent FMN reductase (A0A444YAP9), and NAD(P)H-binding protein (A0A445AJ72) involved in electronic transfer and bioenergy, as well as the PsaE protein (A0A444YMB3) in light energy capture system, exhibited a consistent decline across these stress treatments (Tables 2, 3 and 4). Transaldolase and fructose-6-phosphate aldolase represent two special enzymes situated at the junction of carbohydrate synthesis and the photosynthetic carbon cycle and have the potential to control carbon flux in the Calvin cycle [30]. NADPH-dependent FMN reductase and NAD(P)H-binding protein are key participants in redox reactions integral to the electron transfer chain during photosynthesis. The PsaE protein, as a subunit of PSI, is essential for the proper assembly and structural stability of the PSI complex. Thus, the observed reductions in the expression levels of these critical proteins under single and combined drought and nitrogen deficiency conditions may offer a plausible explanation for the marked decline in photosynthesis.

In our study, we employed pot experiments to investigate the combined effects of drought stress and nitrogen deficiency on peanut growth, leaf physiology, photosynthesis, nitrogen allocation, and proteomic changes. The impacts of nitrogen and water on crops growing under field conditions are more intricate, with these two factors typically interacting with each other. Thus, the discrepancies between pot experiments and field conditions pose certain constraints to directly translating our findings to field practical implications. Moreover, peanuts, as legumes, can conduct biological nitrogen fixation [47], which may add another complexity to the intricate interaction between water and nitrogen. Evidence suggests that increased nitrogen availability can ameliorate the adverse effects of water limitation in peanuts within our study. Based on this, we propose optimizing nitrogen fertilization timing (e.g., split applications during the flowering-pegging stage) to match periods of high photosynthetic demand, mitigating nitrogen loss under drought in peanut agronomic management. This data contributes to an improved understanding of the molecular mechanisms through which nitrogen allocation and photosynthetic proteins modulate photosynthesis in peanut leaves under nitrogen or water deficiency. The findings may establish a benchmark for enhancing the growth and yield of peanuts and even legume crops more broadly by precisely regulating nitrogen fertilization.

Conclusions

The pot experiment demonstrated that nitrogen deficiency and water scarcity reduced the allocation of leaf nitrogen to photosynthetic components, including Ncb and Net in peanuts, subsequently leading to the suppression of PNUE and Pn. Nitrogen application can ameliorate the decrease in Pn under combined water and nitrogen deficiency conditions. Throughout this process, the down-regulated expression of photosynthetic proteins, including Transaldolase/Fructose-6-phosphate aldolase, NADPH-dependent FMN reductase, NAD(P)H-binding protein, and PsaE protein, which are involved in the Calvin cycle, electron transport chain, and light-harvesting complexes, could also potentially impair peanut photosynthesis. These alterations resulted in the impeded peanut leaf growth and reduced dry matter under nitrogen deficiency and water scarcity, a finding that was further confirmed through physiological characteristics analysis. Optimizing nitrogen fertilization timing to match periods of high photosynthetic demand, mitigating nitrogen loss under drought stress, may represent an effective strategy for enhancing the yield of peanuts, potentially extending this benefit to a broader range of legume crops.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

“Huayu 25”, a drought-resistant peanut variety [48], was planted in test pots at Laixi Experimental Station of Shandong Peanut Research Institute (36°48ʹ47ʺN, 120°30ʹ17ʺE) for the study. Soil samples were collected from the 1–10 mm depth of experimental fields in Laixi, revealing the following soil indicators: an alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen content of 65.70 mg kg−1 (a medium N supply level), available phosphorus at 41.12 mg kg−1, available potassium at 87.53 mg kg−1, and a balanced pH of 6.31. The pots with a diameter of 23.5 cm and a height of 41.5 cm were filled with 25 kg of soil. Four peanut seeds were planted in each pot and then were positioned in a growth chamber under optimal growth conditions photoperiod of 16 h light/8 h dark, photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) of 300 μmol m−2 s−1 and temperature of 28℃/18℃ (day/night) [49]. Water was added every two days to maintain the soil water content at 75% of field capacity using the weighing method under normal conditions consistent with our previous study [50]. In case of drought stress, the water content was reduced to 45% of the field water capacity at flowering-pegging stage (from 40 days after sowing) for ten days. The nitrogen fertilizer application was set to no nitrogen (0 kg ha−1) or nitrogen application (90 kg ha−1). Nitrogen was applied in the form of urea (N content 46%), along with the application of 100 kg ha−1 P2O5 and 135 kg ha−1 K2O. All the treatments received the identical amounts of phosphorus and potassium fertilizers. These fertilizers were applied as base fertilizers, and other field managements were consistent with those used in typical high-yield fields. Subsequently, the samples were collected and photosynthetic characteristics were measured ten days post-stress when the stress symptoms had become obvious. Following this, the peanuts were re-irrigated with water to restore and maintain the soil water content at 75% of field capacity until harvest [51].

The study incorporated four distinct treatments: (1) a control group that received adequate watering and nitrogen application (75% field water capacity, with nitrogen application at 90 kg ha−1, WWNA); (2) a nitrogen deficiency group that was well-watered but did not receive nitrogen fertilizer (75% field water capacity, with no nitrogen application; WWNN); (3) a drought group subjected to drought stress but still receiving nitrogen fertilizer (45% field water capacity, with nitrogen application at 90 kg ha−1, DSNA); (4) a dual-deficiency group that experienced both drought stress and nitrogen deficiency (45% field water capacity, with no nitrogen application, DSNN). Each treatment group consisted of three biological replicates, with each replicate comprising leaf samples from three individual plants within the same treatment. The experimental pots were arranged as a randomized block design in a growth chamber.

Measurements of photosynthetic characteristics

On a sunny day after experiencing drought stress for ten days, leaves from the third-to-last position on the main stem, exhibiting uniform growth, were meticulously selected for measurement. Between the hours of 9:00 and 11:30, the CIRAS-2 portable photosynthesis analyzer (PP SYSTEMS, USA) was utilized to measure the Pn under specific conditions: the light intensity was set at 1,200 μmol m−2 s−1, the leaf chamber temperature was maintained at 28℃, and the CO2 concentration was fixed at 400 μmol mol−1. Concurrently, the CO2 response curve was measured under identical light intensity and leaf chamber temperature conditions as those used for determining Pn. The CO2 concentration was sequentially adjusted to 400, 300, 200, 150, 100, 50, 400, 600, 800, 1,000, 1,200, 1,500, and 1,800 μmol mol−1, and Pn and intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci) values were recorded at each point to construct the Pn-Ci curve. Ultimately, parameters such as Rubisco maximum carboxylation efficiency (Vc max) and maximum electron transfer rate (Jmax) were calculated based on the biochemical model and linear regression equation proposed by Farquhar et al. [52].

Leaf dry mass measurement

After the photosynthesis measurement had been completed, six independent peanut plants were collected in the morning (9:00 to 11:00 am). These plants were thoroughly washed to eliminate any adhering soil. The leaves of plants were taken individually and put in labeled paper bags for the dry mass calculation. The leaves were placed in an oven at 105 °C for 30 min, then set to 65 °C for 72 h. The leaf dry mass was then determined using a balance with 0.01 g precision.

Measurement of total nitrogen content in leaves

After the photosynthesis measurement has been completed, carefully detach the third-last leaves and transport them back to the laboratory. The leaves were subjected to a drying process at 105℃ for 30 min and then at 75℃ for 48 h. Subsequently, dried samples were weighed and finely ground until they passed through a 0.5-mm sieve, and the powdered leaves were then prepared for digestion using a mixture of H2SO4-H2O2. Ultimately, the nitrogen content within the digested solution was precisely determined utilizing a flow analyzer (AA3, Hamburg, Germany).

Leaf area measurement

The leaf area was assessed utilizing the leaf discs method described as a previous study [53]. Peanut leaves were initially weighed to determine their mass. Subsequently, a punch with a diameter of 1 cm was employed to select uniform leaves for punching, allowing for the calculation of the unit weight of the leaf area.

Measurement of chlorophyll

The chlorophyll content was measured as previously described [54]. Briefly, approximately 0.1 g of leaf tissue was cut and soaked in 25 mL of 95% ethanol for 48 h. Absorbance readings at wavelengths of 665 nm and 649 nm (UV-759S, SHIMADZU, Kyoto, Japan) were then taken to accurately calculate the concentrations of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and total chlorophyll (a + b).

Calculation of nitrogen allocation in leaf photosynthetic organs

According to the previous study in C3 plants [55], leaf photosynthetic organs are delineated into three primary systems based on their biological functions: the carboxylation system (predominantly Rubisco), the electron transport system (comprising photosynthetic electron transfer and photosynthetic phosphorylation components), and the light-harvesting system (including PSI, PSII, LHCII, and other light-harvesting pigment-protein complexes). The formulas for calculating the nitrogen content of each component are outlined below:

In Eq. (1), Vcr denotes the Rubisco-specific activity, representing the rate of CO2 fixation per unit of Rubisco protein, with a value of 20.78 μmol CO2 g−1 Rubisco s−1. Vcmax signifies the maximum rate of carboxylation, and 6.25 is the coefficient used to convert nitrogen into protein. In Eq. (2), Jmc stands for the maximum electron transfer rate per unit of cytochrome f (Cytf), assigned a value of 155.6 μmol e−1 μmol Cytf s−1, and 8.06 indicates the number of μmol Cytf per g of nitrogen in the biological energy conversion carrier. In Eq. (3), Cc stands for the chlorophyll content of the leaves (mmol m−2), and CB is the conversion factor from nitrogen to chlorophyll, set at 2.15 mmol g−1. The total nitrogen content of photosynthetic components (Nphoto) is the sum of Ncb, Net, and Nlc. Narea represents the total nitrogen content per unit leaf area, and the nitrogen content of non-photosynthetic components (Nnon-photo) is derived by subtracting Nphoto from Narea.

Proteomic analysis

Leaves from the third-to-last position on the main stem of different treatments were harvested, frozen immediately in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80℃ until protein extraction. For protein extraction and digestion, samples from different treatments were finely powdered in liquid nitrogen and then transferred to 300 μL of 8-M urea, with a 10% protease inhibitor added relative to the lysate. After centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 20 min, the supernatant was carefully collected. Subsequently, four volumes of cold acetone were added, and the mixture was precipitated overnight at − 20℃. After another centrifugation step at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4℃, the supernatant was discarded. Then, 1 mL of pre-cooled acetone was added to resuspend the precipitate. Following a final centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4℃, the supernatant was discarded once again, and an appropriate volume of lysis buffer was added for reconstitution. From this reconstituted solution, 10 μL was aliquoted for quantification, while the remainder was frozen at − 80℃. The protein concentration was determined using the Bradford method. Protein quantification was performed according to the kit protocol. After protein quantification, SDS-PAGE electrophoresis was performed. Samples that exhibit adequate protein content, display clear SDS-PAGE gel patterns, and show plentiful bands, even with low abundance, are suitable for utilization in subsequent proteomic analysis.

An aliquot of 100 µg of extracted proteins from each sample was then subjected to reduction. The proteins were incubated with a final concentration of 5 mM DTT at 37℃ for 1 h, before being allowed to return to room temperature. A final concentration of 10 mM iodoacetamide was added, and the mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 45 min. Subsequently, the sample was diluted fourfold with 25-mM ammonium bicarbonate, and trypsin was added in a 50:1 ratio of protein to trypsin. The mixture was then incubated at 37℃ overnight, and formic acid was added on the second day to adjust the pH to below 3 to effectively terminate the enzyme digestion process.

For desalination, a C18 desalination column was employed. Initially, the desalination column was activated with 100% acetonitrile and equilibrated with 0.1% formic acid. The sample was then loaded onto the column, followed by a thorough wash with 0.1% formic acid to remove impurities. Finally, the column was washed with 70% acetonitrile, and the permeate was collected and freeze-dried for proteomics analysis.

For Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) quantitative proteomics, the mobile phase A (formic acid: water = 1:1,000 [v/v]) and phase B (acetonitrile: formic acid = 1:800 [v/v]) were prepared. The freeze-dried powder was dissolved in 10 µL of phase A solution, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4℃. A 400-ng aliquot of the supernatant was then injected for LC–MS/MS analysis. The liquid phase gradient settings were configured as follows: 0–7 min, 8%–12% phase B; 7–55 min, 12%–30% phase B; 55–65 min, 30%–40% phase B; 65–66 min, 40%–95% phase B; 65–80 min, 95% phase B. The flow rate was held constant at 600 nanoliters per minute (nL min−1), facilitating label-free MS analysis using Thermo Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometry. The scan events were set as a full MS scan of 350–1550 m/z at a mass resolution of 120,000, followed by a collision-induced dissociation MS/MS scan that focused on the 20 most intense ions selected from the previous full MS scan with an isolation window. The normalized collision energy was set to 30%, with an activation duration of 45 ms. The second stage employed linear ion trap fast mode for data acquisition, with the automatic gain control finely tuned to 50,000, and the maximum injection time strictly maintained at 45 ms.

Proteome Discoverer 2.4 software was used to search the raw data. The parameters are as follows: Precursor ion mass tolerance was ± 15 ppm; Fragment ion mass tolerance was ± 0.5 Da; Enzyme cutting site was trypsin/P; The maximum missed cleavage site was 2; Carbamidomethylation of cysteines as fixed modification, and oxidation of methionines and protein N-terminal acetylation as variable modifications. Protein quantification was standardized based on the total peak area, achieved by dividing the peak area of each protein in a sample by the cumulative peak area of all proteins within that same sample. For protein functional annotation, the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/) was used as the search repository. P-values and Fold change (FC) for the proteins among the different groups were calculated using the R package “t-test”. The thresholds of fold change (> 1.5 or < 0.67) and p-value < 0.05 were used to identify differentially expressed proteins. The LC–MS/MS data were processed using a thorough database search considering biological modifications and amino acid substitutions under the ParagonTM algorithm [56] and the Pro GroupTM algorithm with ProteinPilot version 4.5 software (AB Sciex). Gene Ontology (GO) Enrichment Analysis (http://www.geneontology.org/) was conducted to analyze the differential expression of proteins functional classification. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/) was used to perform pathway enrichment analysis on differentially expressed proteins.

Data analysis

All calculations and plotting were performed utilizing the BMKCloud Platform. Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS 19.0 software with the two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) between water and nitrogen addition treatment in R 4.0.2. Data processing and image preparation were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8.3 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, NM, USA). Multiple comparisons were made using least significant difference (LSD) tests at p < 0.05. The relationships between nitrogen forms and Pn, PNUE, and Narea were analyzed by Pearson correlation analysis. All experiments have three biological replicates, and the data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE).
