Arthroscopic Posterior Labral Repair in Professional Contact Athletes Restores Shoulder Stability and Enables Successful Return to Pre‐injury Competition
Authors: Ayham Jaber, Lorenz Fritsch, Michael Nocek, Christopher J. Hawryluk, Maximilian Hinz, Matthew T. Provencher, Peter J. Millett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate return‐to‐competition rates, posterior shoulder instability recurrences, and revision surgeries following arthroscopic posterior labral repair in this elite cohort.MethodsA retrospective review of prospectively collected data included professional contact athletes undergoing primary arthroscopic posterior labral repair by a single surgeon (June 2016 to February 2024). Inclusion required age ≥18, active professional status at surgery, and minimum 12‐month follow‐up. Patients with concomitant cuff repairs or bony procedures were excluded. Outcomes included return‐to‐competition (defined as the first postoperative professional game), time to return‐to‐competition, instability recurrence, games per season, and career longevity using public performance data.ResultsSeventeen professional male contact‐sport athletes (mean age 24 ± 3.7 years) met inclusion criteria. Ten were National Hockey League hockey players and 7 were National Football League football players. All (100%) returned to their pre‐injury level at a median of 173 days (range 89‐370 days). At mean follow‐up of 45 ± 22.9 months, 1 athlete experienced recurrent instability, which was managed nonoperatively. No athlete retired postoperatively. Fourteen of 17 played more games per season following surgery, with a mean increase of 26.6% ± 21.8%.ConclusionsArthroscopic posterior labral repair is a highly effective surgical intervention for restoring shoulder stability and enabling a reliable return to play among professional contact‐sport athletes. In this cohort of elite‐level, professional competitors, most athletes returned to their pre‐injury level of competition and continued playing without issue, with only 1 case of recurrent instability that did not require revision surgery at a minimum 1‐year follow‐up.Level of EvidenceLevel IV, retrospective therapeutic case series.

Posterior shoulder instability (PSI) is a relatively uncommon but increasingly recognized shoulder pathology. 1 Although it accounts for only 2% to 10% of all glenohumeral instability cases, PSI is likely underdiagnosed due to its subtle presentation and the difficulty in detection compared to anterior instability. 2 , 3 Athletes in high‐demand, contact sports such as football, hockey, and rugby are especially vulnerable, given the repetitive posteriorly directed forces encountered during sport‐specific maneuvers. 4 Epidemiologically, PSI is more prevalent in athletic and military populations than in the general population. The Incidence rates ranging from 0.1 to 4.3 per 1000 person‐years in athletes, compared with 0.05 per 1000 person‐years among the general population. 1 , 3 Posterior subluxations have been shown to account for more than half of all shoulder subluxations in National Football League (NFL) athletes, particularly among defensive players. 5

While conservative management remains the first‐line treatment following posterior shoulder dislocation, surgical stabilization is often required in athletes who experience persistent instability, pain, or functional limitations. 6 Arthroscopic posterior labral repair is the preferred initial surgical approach, offering a minimally invasive technique with good outcomes. 4 , 7 Most published outcomes are reported in mixed populations or nonelite athletes, or outcomes in professional athletes following stabilization due to anterior shoulder instability, 8 leaving a gap in the literature regarding outcomes in professional athletes in the setting of PSI. Filling this gap is essential for aligning patient and surgeon expectations, particularly as an increasing number of athletes enter professional or semi‐professional sports as early as their college years. 9 The purpose of this study was to evaluate return‐to‐competition (RTC) rates, PSI recurrences, and revision surgeries following arthroscopic posterior labral repair in this elite cohort. We hypothesized that this procedure would result in high RTC rates and a low incidence of recurrent shoulder instability.

METHODS

This retrospective review of prospectively collected data was approved by the Vail Health Hospital Institutional Review Board (reference: 2024‐243‐SPRI). It was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Professional athletes who underwent primary surgery for posterior labral lesions (Figure 1), performed by the senior author (P.J.M.) between June 2016 and February 2024, were eligible for inclusion. Additional inclusion criteria were age >18 years at the time of surgery and athletes who did not retire from their professional career prior to surgery and had at least 12 months of follow‐up. Athletes who retired from their professional careers prior to surgery and those with concomitant fractures were excluded. “Professional athletes” were defined as those who received compensation for their specific sports participation and competed at the highest or most elite level of their respective sport.

Surgical Technique

Following the administration of general anesthesia, the patient is positioned in the beach‐chair position to allow for sterile preparation and draping. A physical examination is conducted to assess glenohumeral instability using the posterior load‐and‐shift and jerk tests, which confirm the diagnosis.

Standard arthroscopic access is established using a posterior viewing portal. A diagnostic arthroscopy is then performed to visualize the labral tear along with any associated pathology. Two anterior portals, one directly below the clavicle beneath the biceps tendon and the other lower in the rotator interval, are then established. The camera is transitioned to the anterosuperior portal to allow optimal evaluation of the posterior labrum. A posterolateral working portal is created approximately 2 cm inferior and lateral to the standard posterior portal. Cannula placement is adjusted based on desired access, more medial for capsular and labral passage and more lateral for anchor placement. An anteroinferior portal is also created parallel to the glenoid, facilitating suture shuttling from posterior to anterior.

Through the posterolateral portal, the labrum is mobilized with an elevator, and the glenoid surface is debrided with a shaver to promote healing. Repair begins at the 6 o'clock position using a 15° angled drill guide and a 2.4‐mm drill. Anchors were placed at the 6, 4:30, and 3 o'clock positions, with approximately 5‐6 mm between anchors. The anchors varied during the course of the study, but most recently, knotless all suture‐based anchor systems were used. These anchors are then inserted and secured with a mallet. The repair suture is retrieved via the anterosuperior portal using a tape retriever.

A crescent lasso loop is passed through the capsule and chondrolabral junction, capturing ample tissue and shifting the capsule during fixation. The shuttling wire is passed through the anteroinferior portal, allowing the repair suture to be advanced through the capsulolabral tissue and secured through the anchor's self‐locking mechanism. The suture is tensioned to secure the repair and trimmed flush with the labrum. Subsequent anchors are placed as needed using the same technique. A probe is used to confirm adequate restoration posteriorly. Figure 2 demonstrates a posterior labral tear and the labral repair with knotless all suture anchors.

For capsular repair, the cannula is retracted just above the capsule, and a suture lasso is used to pierce the capsule medial to the defect. The shuttling wire is passed through the anteroinferior portal. A No. 1 polydioxanone suture is threaded through the loop and pulled back. A 22° angled suture retriever is then used to retrieve the suture laterally, and the stitch is tied and trimmed to complete the posterior capsular repair.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

The operated shoulder is placed in a sling for 6 weeks postoperatively. During this period, the primary goals are to protect the repair, manage pain, and maintain joint mobility. Passive range‐of‐motion exercises begin immediately, but the posterior capsule is not stretched for 4 weeks. Active strengthening and stabilization exercises are introduced gradually, with full active range of motion and weight‐bearing activities typically resuming after 3 months. RTC is usually permitted around 4 months postoperatively depending on clinical factors and time of the year.

Patient Characteristics and Operative Data

Patient characteristics, including age at the time of surgery, sex, laterality of the injury, handedness, and time between onset of symptoms as well as injury‐ and surgery‐related information, including injury etiology (traumatic versus insidious onset), duration between injury and surgery, extent of labral tear, type of concomitant pathologies as well as intra‐ and postoperative complications were assessed.

Outcome Measurements

Using search engines (e.g., www.google.com), sports websites (e.g., www.espn.com), and websites of the individual teams, the length of professional play both before injury and after surgery, the level of and time to RTC, and recurrence rates were evaluated, as previously described. Successful RTC was defined as competing again for at least 1 game/competition at the same level as pre‐injury, as was done in previous studies.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 28.0 (IBM‐SPSS, New York, USA). Categorical variables are reported as numbers and percentages. Distribution of the collected continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro‐Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation whereas non‐normally distributed continuous variables are reported as median (range). The t‐test was used for group comparisons of continuous variables. Due to the rarity of this injury in professional athletes, all eligible athletes were included, and a power analysis was not carried out.

RESULTS

In total, 20 professional athletes underwent surgery by the senior author for the treatment of posterior labral lesions, of whom 17 patients met the inclusion criteria. Three athletes were excluded due to undergoing concomitant procedures that did not concern the biceps‐labral complex (1 athlete with a rotator cuff repair, 1 athlete with distal clavicle excision, and 1 athlete who received a glenoid bone graft) due to 20% bone loss. The mean age at the time of surgery was 24 ± 3.7 years. All athletes were male and played contact sports professionally at the time of surgery; hockey in the NHL, 59%, n = 10, or football in the NFL, 41%, n = 7. Thirteen of 17 athletes (77%) underwent surgery during their offseason. The mean time between injury and surgery was 3.73 ± 5 months. The onset of symptoms was traumatic in 16 athletes (94%) and atraumatic or insidious onset in 1 athlete (6%). Nine athletes had posterior subluxation (53%). Seven athletes (41%) had a posterior dislocation that necessitated reduction. One athlete (6%) did not suffer from instability but had posterior pain and weakness in external rotation that was accompanied by a paralabral cyst (Table 1). The mean extent of the labral tear was 127.5° (range 90°‐360°). The median number of anchors used for the labral repair was 4 (range, 3‐10). The SLAP complex was involved in 7 patients (41%) who all underwent concomitant SLAP. Other concomitant procedures included repair of a reverse humeral avulsion of the inferior glenohumeral ligament (rHAGL, 6%, n = 1), paralabral cyst decompression (6%, n = 1), adjunct reverse remplissage (13%, n = 2). Return to Competition/Instability Recurrence

All athletes returned to competition. RTC or return to unrestricted activity with full contact (if cleared in the offseason) was, on average, 190.1 days, with a median of 173 days (range 89‐370 days) (Figure 3). At a mean follow‐up of 45 ± 22.86 months, none of the athletes had retired. At the time of the study, athletes played for a median of 3.0 (range, 2‐7) seasons before surgery and continued playing for a median of 3.0 (range, 1‐7) seasons after surgery. Fourteen of the 17 athletes played more games per season after surgery. On average, those 14 athletes played 26.6% ± 21.8% more games after surgery per year in their respective sport. The 3 athletes that played fewer games per year played 8.5%, 26%, and 81% fewer games per season after surgery. The patient who played 81% fewer games following surgery had an unrelated injury that prevented his play. He incurred an ankle injury during the training camp and was placed on Injured Reserve. This was the year directly after his shoulder surgery. One patient suffered an instability recurrence in the form of posterior dislocation during his fourth game after returning to competition. Magnetic resonance imaging showed an intact labral repair, with a tear in the posterior capsule adjacent to the posterior labral repair. He was treated conservatively with autologous bone marrow aspirate concentrate and platelet‐rich plasma injections. He returned after 1.5 months and played 68 games without recurrence.

DISCUSSION

The main finding of the study is the high rate of return to professional competition among elite contact‐sport athletes following arthroscopic posterior labral repair. All participants successfully resumed their pre‐injury level of sport and returned to competition within an average of approximately 6.1 months. There was 1 instance of recurrent instability, which was managed successfully with nonoperative treatment. No athletes retired after a median follow‐up period of nearly 4 years. Several players were injured in the preseason then missed the entire season. They returned to competition the next season. It is likely they were able to return to playing sooner than their first day of competition.

Outcomes of surgical stabilizations for PSI are relatively less established when compared with anterior instability. They also vary widely. In a long‐term follow‐up study, Rothrauff et al. reported long‐term outcomes (mean follow‐up of 15.4 years) following posterior arthroscopic capsulolabral repair in a mixed‐sport population. Although improvements in pain and function remained over time, only 35% of patients returned to sport at their pre‐injury level, and 60% returned to sport at any level. Notably, just 22% of shoulders were involved in the same sport at final follow‐up. 2 Pavlik and colleagues found that although 97.3% of amateur athletes returned to some form of sport, only 51.4% returned to their pre‐injury level. 10 They noted that athletes with traumatic injuries had a significantly higher RTS rate compared with those without a clear traumatic event. This rate is significantly lower than that reported in this cohort. The findings suggest that professional athletes may benefit from more structured sport‐specific postoperative rehabilitation programs.

NFL players typically incur posterior shoulder instability because of repetitive, posteriorly directed axial loads, which are common in contact sports like American football. This mechanism is particularly prevalent in defensive players, such as linemen, who frequently perform blocking and tackling activities that apply stress to the posterior labrum and capsule. The high incidence of posterior subluxations in these athletes can be a result of microtrauma but is often due to repetitive insult that leads to posteroinferior labral tears and other associated injuries. Additionally, the contact nature of the sport contributes significantly to the occurrence of these injuries, as most instability events in the NFL result from a contact mechanism. 11 The proposed mechanism involves macrotrauma, in which a sufficiently strong force is applied to the joint. These acute, traumatic events usually lead to capsulolabral detachment. 3 Arner and colleagues reported outcomes following posterior capsulolabral repair in 56 American football players, 2 of whom were NFL players; the rest were collegiate or high school athletes. At a mean follow‐up of 44.7 months, 93% returned to sport and 79% returned to sport at the same level. 12 These findings align more with the findings in this study. Looking at overhead sports, Kercher and colleagues reported results of posterior labral repairs in 32 baseball players, 6 of whom were professionals, at a minimum 2‐year follow‐up. Ninety‐four percent of athletes returned to play, 61% at their pre‐injury level, and 6% did not return. 13 A systematic review and meta‐analysis by Gouveia and colleagues reported a pooled RTS rate of 88% after surgical management of posterior shoulder instability, with only 68% returning to their pre‐injury level. Notably, arthroscopic procedures had higher RTS rates compared with open surgeries (90% vs 80%), and contact athletes showed a 94% RTC rate. 7 In this cohort, 1 patient had a mini‐open procedure with glenoid bony augmentation using an iliac crest autograft.

In a systematic review, Matar and colleagues reported RTS rates ranging from 57.9% to 100% following surgical treatment for PSI, which included 23 studies. Similar to this study, the pre‐injury level ranged from 47.4% to 100% and time to RTS varied between 4.3 and 7.7 months. 4

Limitations

The present study is limited by its retrospective design, small sample size, and lack of a control group. Additionally, there is large variability in concomitant procedures that could potentially confound the results. Although these findings are positive, they represent a selective population of professional athletes managed by a single high‐volume surgeon, which may limit generalizability. Furthermore, our results do not include patient‐reported outcome measures, and the minimum follow‐up is short at 12 months. Because all the subjects were male, we could not disaggregate the data by sex to determine its impact.

CONCLUSIONS

Arthroscopic posterior labral repair is a highly effective surgical intervention for restoring shoulder stability and enabling a reliable return to play among professional contact‐sport athletes. In this cohort of elite level, professional competitors, most athletes returned to their pre‐injury level of competition and continued playing without issue, with only 1 case of recurrent instability that did not require revision surgery at a minimum 1‐year follow‐up.
