Urinary Soluble CD163: A Dynamic Biomarker for Monitoring Treatment Response and Relapse in IgA Nephropathy

IgA nephropathy (IgAN) is the most prevalent primary glomerulonephritis, with many unanswered questions, varied clinical presentations, and, quite often, 30%–40% of patients progressing to kidney failure in 20 to 25 years of diagnosis.1 Until 2021, KDIGO guidelines2 did not recommend any specific therapy, as regulators focused on hard endpoints like doubling creatinine or kidney failure. Later, these guidelines were changed to include proteinuria reduction, which led to the approval of drugs like targeted release budesonide and sparsenton for IgAN. Except for proteinuria, there are currently no noninvasive biomarkers available to monitor disease progression or to initiate disease-modifying therapy. The disease itself is diagnosed through renal biopsy, which shows dominant or codominant IgA deposits in histopathology. Even the risk of disease progression is assessed using the MEST (mesangial [M] and endocapillary [E] hypercellularity, segmental sclerosis [S], interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy [T], and crescents [C]) according to the revised Oxford Classification score from the renal biopsy. Even the immunosuppressants have improved outcomes but are associated with so many complications, ex: testing trial, so for precision therapy we need a noninvasive biomarker to guide us to stop or to initiate the immunosuppression.3 This concept was started way back in 2008, a study by Harada et al.,4 NDT, 2008, infiltration of glomeruli by fibroblast-specific positive cells: FSP1 cells > 32.6/HPF strongly correlates with steroid unresponsiveness. Clinicians cannot repeat the invasive renal biopsies regularly. Hence, there is the urgent dearth of noninvasive biomarkers to identify the disease progression, disease response, and disease relapse.

Initial studies by Xie et al.5 found that macrophage infiltration (CD206+ and CD68+ cells) in the kidney biopsy specimen was correlated with the response to steroids, but it is a snapshot that can be done once on a kidney biopsy specimen; it cannot identify the progressive dynamics inside the glomerulus. Recently, urinary soluble CD163 has come into the limelight in many autoimmune disorders, such as systemic vasculitis and lupus nephritis, which is the marker of macrophage invasion of the kidney, especially the M2c variant of macrophages. Whenever the autoimmune injury occurs in the kidney, the FcRγ-dependent massive influx of macrophages occurs in the glomerular and tubular compartments. Macrophages are two types: M1, classically activated, and M2, alternatively activated. M2 is further divided into M2a and M2b, which are involved in wound healing, and M2c, which are regulatory macrophages. In the kidneys, M2c variants are predominant in IgA.6 CD163, a 130 kDa transmembrane protein and a member of the cysteine-rich scavenger receptor superfamily type B, is predominantly the surface marker of the M2c variety of macrophage. When it is cleaved by metalloproteinases, soluble CD163 is expelled into the urine. According to the recent study by Li et al.7 on urinary soluble CD163 in IgAN, a testing trial Chinese cohort documented a correlation to both disease activity and response to immunosuppression in the patients with high urinary sCD163 levels, but there were so many unanswered questions to use u-sCD163 as a biomarker for treatment selection and disease monitoring in IgAN, like can the magnitude of change in u-sCD163 predict the response to glucocorticoids? Can it be used as a surrogate marker of relapse like proteinuria? Can a decrease in its value precede remission, like Phospholipase A2 receptors in membranous nephropathy? Can it be used as a biomarker even in renal transplant patients to monitor the disease recurrence?

All the queries were partially answered by the recent study published in this issue of the KI report by8 a prospective cohort study of high-risk IgAN patients. u-sCD163 levels were strongly correlated with the CD163 levels in the kidney biopsy specimens; the higher the u-sCD163 levels, the better the response to the treatment with immunosuppression and the more positively correlated with the proteinuria and hematuria and negatively correlated with the estimated glomerular filtration rate, similar to the work by the same authors in a previous study (Figure 1).5 The results were stratified according to the baseline characteristics; the patients with heavy proteinuria, low glomerular filtration rate, and high histological scores were having high tertiles of u-sCD163 levels and higher responses to immunosuppression. Incorporating the u-sCD163 into the MEST C score has resulted in higher predictions. Even in the longitudinal follow-up, those patients with relapse had a rise in levels of u-sCD163 2.8 months before proteinuria (clinical relapse), and it can be used like phospholipase A2 receptors levels in membranous nephropathy. Therefore, u-sCD163 levels can be used for monitoring disease activity, treatment response, and disease relapse. However, this study was conducted at a single center and only involved a Chinese population; additionally, renal transplant patients were not shown to be included in the study population. The study only tested treatment responses with corticosteroids and mycophenolate mofetil, not with newer therapies such as budesonide, APRIL/BAFF inhibitors, or CD38 inhibitors, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, there is a pressing clinical need for dynamic biomarkers beyond proteinuria, which can also serve as a surrogate for active inflammation.

An important issue with the IgAN is that it is one of the important areas in nephrology undergoing major research from pathophysiology to innovation of major biomarkers and newer treatments. The development of transcriptomic medicine9 has highlighted the consistently upregulated genes (CCL2, CXCL10, HAVCR1, and COL1A1) and altered pathways (NF-kB, TGF-Beta, JAK/STAT, and complement) that are associated with the clinical deterioration. Transcriptomic medicine is also coming up with the so many noninvasive biomarkers for prediction and also for precision therapy in the field of IgAN. The pathogenesis of IgAN is explained by the 4-hit hypothesis. Hit 1: Increase in circulating IgA1 that lacks galactose at its hinge region (galactose-deficient IgA1 [Gd-IgA1]); Hit 2: IgG and IgA autoantibody production; Hit 3: With subsequent immune complex formation; Hit 4: Deposition of immune complexes in the mesangium and injury by different mediators of inflammation like cytokines, chemokines, and the complements.

So many novel therapies are targeting these hits, including APRIL/BAFF inhibitors for Hit 1, Nefecon for Hits 1-3, antibodies against CD38+ plasma cells for Hit 2, and various complement inhibitors targeting the alternate complement pathway for Hit 4, which are all in the pipeline. So the biggest question here is whether u-sCD163 parallels the other noninvasive biomarkers of transcriptomic medicine, like urinary microRNA, and whether the levels alter with the newer therapies acting on different hits of IgA pathophysiology. Though the effect of any hit ultimate pathogenesis is macrophage infiltration, u-sCD163 will rise in all the cases of IgA, so logically it can be a biomarker for all cases, but it must be proved through the future studies. Further studies are needed to synthesize the evidence to generalize the findings of this study and to include u-sCD163 into the clinical decision-making.

Future studies are needed to prepare modern weapons, such as incorporating novel biomarkers like u-sCD163, complement components, and microRNA sequencing into clinical decision-making, as well as developing newer drugs that act at different levels of pathogenesis to combat the battle against the most common primary glomerulonephritis, IgAN.
