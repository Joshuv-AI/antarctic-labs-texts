Ovarian microrna transcriptome of Macrobrachium rosenbergii after eyestalk ablation: unveiling molecular switches in reproductive regulation
Authors: Huwei Yuan, Haihua Cheng, Fei Peng, Yang Xu, Xilian Li, Yunpeng Fan, Qiang Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMacrobrachium rosenbergii is an economically important freshwater prawn species in China. The X-organ–sinus gland complex in crustacean eyestalks regulates reproduction through neurohormones, with ablation significantly promoting ovarian maturation. However, the miRNA mediated molecular mechanisms underlying this eyestalk ovary regulatory axis remain poorly characterized in M. rosenbergii.ResultsRNA sequencing of 9 ovarian samples produced 207 million paired-end reads (62.25 Gb total), averaging > 6 Gb per sample with Q30 scores exceeding 94.42%. The reads showed 84.32% alignment efficiency to the reference transcriptome. Transcriptomic analysis identified 769 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and 176 differentially expressed miRNAs (DEMs), which were significantly enriched in reproduction related pathways including steroid biosynthesis and Wnt signaling.​ Integration analysis revealed 11 functionally important miRNA-mRNA regulatory pairs targeting key ovarian development genes. qRT-PCR validation confirmed the high reliability of sequencing data.ConclusionsTranscriptomic analysis of ovarian mRNA and miRNA in eyestalk-ablated M. rosenbergii revealed the molecular mechanisms of eyestalk mediated ovarian regulation.​​ Eyestalk ablation significantly upregulated vitellogenin genes and ecdysteroid biosynthesis genes, ​​demonstrating its dual regulatory role in reproduction and molting.​​ The findings indicate potential roles of the eyestalk in reproductive regulation via specific pathways, with the identified DEGs and DEMs serving as a valuable resource for future investigations.Keywords: Eyestalk ablation​, MicroRNA, Ovary, Macrobrachium rosenbergii​

Introduction

Macrobrachium rosenbergii (M. rosenbergii) belongs to the family Palaemonidae and the genus Macrobrachium. It is the largest freshwater prawn in the world, often referred to as the ‘king of freshwater prawn‘ [1]. M. rosenbergii is favored for its fast growth rate, large size, short farming cycle, high nutritional value, and good market acceptance. As an important economic prawn species, M. rosenbergii occupies a significant position in the global aquaculture industry.

In crustaceans, the eyestalks are not only visual organs but also closely associated with the endocrine system. Hanström first revealed a structure located in the eyestalks of crustaceans, which plays an important role in physiological regulation, naming it the sinus gland(SG) [2]. The SG and the X organ form the X organ-SG complex (XO-SG), which is an important neuroendocrine regulatory organ in crustaceans, analogous to the hypothalamic-pituitary system in vertebrates [3]. The eyestalks are the primary endocrine centers for the production and release of various hormones in crustaceans [4]. Experiments involving the ablation and reimplantation of the eyestalk in crustaceans have demonstrated that the XO-SG nervous system regulates various physiological activities, including osmoregulation, molting, and reproduction [5–7].

Previous studies have shown that eyestalk ablation typically stimulates ovarian maturation and spawning in crustaceans [8]. The various neurohormones they secrete can influence the development and maturation of the ovaries, thereby regulating the reproductive cycle [9]. The ovaries, as the core organs of the reproductive system, are responsible for the production, maturation, and release of eggs, and their functional status directly affects the reproductive efficiency of crustaceans. Therefore, the interaction between the eyestalks and the ovaries constitutes a key component in the reproductive regulation of crustaceans. The phenomenon that the ablation of the eyestalks significantly affects ovarian function suggests that a deeper investigation into the interaction mechanisms between the eyestalks and ovaries is crucial for understanding and improving the reproductive performance of crustaceans.

MicroRNA (miRNA) is a class of endogenous small non-coding RNA molecules, approximately 22 nucleotides in length. In recent years, miRNAs have been confirmed to play a critical role as important regulators of gene expression in various physiological processes, such as molting and reproduction, in multiple insects and crustaceans [10]. In Locusta migratoria, miR-184 influences molting by targeting the CYP303A1 gene. Injection of miR-184 analogs results in abnormal molting in locusts, consistent with the outcomes observed after interference with CYP303A1 [11]. In Aedes aegypti, miR-8 is crucial for oocyte development in females. It activates the Wingless signaling pathway, thereby regulating the development of vitellogenin and oocytes [12]. In M. rosenbergii, it has been found that the overexpression of miR-184 can significantly downregulate the expression of the MrIAG gene and detect a series of differentially expressed genes associated with sex differentiation, gonadal development, growth, and molting [13]. The above findings suggest that miRNAs exercise important regulatory functions during molting or reproduction, however, they remain relatively understudied in crustaceans.

In the current study, we constructed ovarian mRNA and miRNA transcriptome libraries following eyestalk ablation in M. rosenbergii. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and miRNAs (DEMs) were identified through transcriptome comparison, and annotations were performed using multiple databases. The accuracy of the transcriptome data was validated through quantitative real time PCR (qRT-PCR). From the perspective of the regulatory molecular mechanisms of the eyestalks on the ovaries, the first transcriptomic analysis of mRNA and miRNA in the ovaries of M. rosenbergii after eyestalk ablation was established. This study provides essential genomic resources for elucidating the regulatory functions of eyestalks in crustacean reproduction.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and RNA extraction

The experimental M. rosenbergii were provided by the Changxing M. rosenbergii Breeding Base, Zhejiang Institute of Freshwater Fisheries. The prawns were acclimated in a circular water system for one week, fed twice daily with M. rosenbergii specific feed. Ninety individuals (12.6 ± 1.3 g, ovaries developed to mature stage) were randomly divided into experimental and control groups (n = 30). For the experimental group, unilateral and bilateral eyestalk removal was performed using the eyestalk ablation method. The control group remained untreated. Seven days post surgery, ovarian tissues were collected from 8 individuals in each group (triplicate samples). Tissues were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent RNA extraction. Total RNA was isolated from ovaries using the RNAiso Plus Kit (TaKaRa, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA contamination was removed from RNA samples with DNase I (Sangon, Shanghai, China). RNA quality and purity were assessed via gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop ND2000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA), respectively. The A260/A280 ratio of all samples fell within 1.9–2.1.

cDNA and MiRNA library construction and sequencing

The cDNA and miRNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina sequencing platform by Genedenovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China). Total RNA (3 µg, concentration > 300 ng/µL) was enriched using mRNA Capture Beads. After magnetic bead purification, mRNA was fragmented via high-temperature treatment. The fragmented mRNA served as templates for synthesizing the first strand of cDNA in a reverse transcriptase reaction system. During the synthesis of the second strand, end repair and poly(A) tail addition were concurrently completed. Subsequently, adapters were ligated to the cDNA fragments and purified using Hieff NGS® DNA Selection Beads. Libraries were amplified by PCR and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform. Small RNA fragments (18–30 nt) were isolated from total RNA samples via agarose gel electrophoresis and gel extraction. After ligating both 3′ and 5′ adapters, the double-adaptor-ligated small RNAs underwent reverse transcription and PCR amplification. The amplified cDNA products were purified by agarose gel electrophoresis to isolate DNA fragments (~ 140 bp), thereby completing the miRNA library construction. All constructed libraries were quality controlled using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and qPCR. Subsequently, the end libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform.

Sequencing data quality control

Raw reads derived from mRNA sequencing were initially processed using ​fastp to remove low-quality bases, yielding high quality clean reads [14]. Subsequently, ​Bowtie2 was employed to align the clean reads against a ribosomal RNA (rRNA) database, allowing the removal of rRNA contaminated reads [15]. The remaining unmapped reads were retained for downstream transcriptomic analysis. For genome alignment, paired-end sequencing data were mapped to the M. rosenbergii reference genome (NCBI_GCF_040412425.1) using ​HISAT2 [16]. Gene or transcript expression levels were quantified using ​FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of exon model per Million mapped fragments), a standardized metric for transcript normalization [17]. Low quality tags in raw miRNA sequencing data were removed through quality control filters, including reads lacking 3’ adapter sequences, reads containing 5’ adapter contaminants, reads with insert lengths below 18 nt, and reads containing polyA sequences. Clean tags were aligned to the M. rosenbergii reference genome and small RNA databases using ​Bowtie2 [18], filtering out ribosomal RNA (rRNA), small conditional RNA (scRNA), small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA), small nuclear RNA (snRNA), and transfer RNA (tRNA). Known miRNAs were identified using ​miRDeep2 [19]. Novel miRNA prediction was performed based on the hairpin secondary structure of miRNA precursors via ​miRDeep2. Transcript expression levels for both known and novel miRNAs were normalized to transcripts per million (TPM).

Identification and target gene prediction of DEGs and DEMs

To identify differentially expressed transcripts across groups, the edgeR package (http://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/edgeR.html) was used. DEGs were identified as mRNAs with a fold change ≥ 2 and FDR < 0.05, while DEMs were determined by fold change ≥ 2 and p < 0.05. The target gene prediction was conducted using two computational tools: Miranda (v3.3a) and TargetScan (Version 7.0). The final set of miRNA target gene predictions was determined by taking the intersection of target gene candidates identified through both computational approaches.

Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs and target genes of DEMs

After identifying the DEGs and their corresponding target genes of DEMs, we performed functional enrichment analysis by conducting Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Processes term analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis on both DEGs and DEM targeted genes. Firstly, all genes were annotated with GO terms from the Gene Ontology database (https://www.geneontology.org/). Term specific gene counts were determined, and significantly enriched GO terms in genes were identified. Pathway based analysis facilitates the understanding of genes’ biological functions. The KEGG (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/) serves as the primary public repository for pathway related annotations. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed significantly enriched metabolic pathways or signal transduction pathways.

qRT-PCR validation

To validate the accuracy of sequencing results, 10 DEGs and DEMs were randomly selected from the transcriptome for verification. Primers for mRNA qRT-PCR were designed using the Primer-BLAST online tool, while miRNA primers were designed with miRprimer2 software. All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. (Table 1). qRT-PCR was performed on the Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The relative expression levels of ten ovarian development related genes ​​(CYP315A1, CYP307A1, Foxl2, HR3, E75, Fzd2, HR4, Vg4, Vg-like, and Gst1)​​ were measured by qRT-PCR in both experimental and control groups. The mRNA qRT-PCR reaction system and cycling conditions followed our previous study [20]. The relative expression levels of DEGs were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method, with ​​β-actin serving as the endogenous control​​ for mRNAs [21]. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test compared group differences. Results are expressed as mean ± SD, where groups labeled with distinct letters differ significantly (p < 0.05). Analyses used SPSS 20.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Sequencing data and comparative efficiency

A total of ​​207,489,800 paired-end reads​​ (62.25 Gb of transcriptomic data) were obtained from sequencing nine ovarian mixed samples of M. rosenbergii after eyestalk ablation. Each sample yielded ​​ >6 Gb of clean reads​​, with an ​​average read length of 150 bp​​. The sequencing data exhibited high quality, with ​​Q30 scores​​ ranging from ​​94.42% to 96.45%​​ and ​​GC content​​ between ​​40.95% and 41.19%​​. The alignment rate to the reference genome exceeded ​​84.32%​​ (Table 2).

Ovarian mRNA expression analysis and functional enrichment after eyestalk ablation

Following eyestalk ablation, we performed pairwise comparisons of ovarian transcriptomes and identified numerous DEGs. The control (C) vs unilateral eyestalk ablation (UEA) comparison yielded 250 DEGs (86 upregulated, 164 downregulated). The C vs bilateral eyestalk ablation (BEA) comparison revealed 769 DEGs (248 upregulated, 521 downregulated). The UEA vs BEA comparison showed 75 DEGs (27 upregulated, 48 downregulated) (Fig. 1 A). DEGs were annotated using GO and KEGG databases for gene classification and pathway analysis. GO annotation assigned 591 DEGs to at least one of three GO categories. In Biological processes, predominant terms were ‘Cellular process’ and ‘Metabolic process’. For Molecular function, key terms were ‘Binding’ and ‘Transporter activity’. In cellular components, ‘protein-containing complex’ showed significant representation (Fig. 1 B). Pathway analysis provided further insight into the biological functions of DEGs. KEGG analysis revealed 201 DEGs annotated to 276 signaling pathways. Among these, the ten key pathways potentially regulating ovarian development either directly or indirectly through hormonal secretion included: fatty acid degradation, circadian entrainment, salivary secretion, cholesterol metabolism, retinol metabolism, steroid biosynthesis, insulin signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, estrogen signaling pathway, and insect hormone biosynthesis (Fig. 1 C).

Ovarian MiRNA expression analysis and target gene annotation after eyestalk ablation​​

After eyestalk ablation, pairwise comparisons of ovarian miRNA transcriptomes were performed. A total of 614 miRNAs were detected, including 283 known and 331 novel miRNAs. Among them, 176 DEMs were identified, with 53 upregulated and 123 downregulated. In the C vs UEA comparison, 89 DEMs were found (27 upregulated, 62 downregulated). The C vs BEA comparison revealed 68 DEMs (15 upregulated, 53 downregulated). The UEA vs BEA comparison showed 19 DEMs (11 upregulated, 8 downregulated) (Fig. 2). The target genes of DEMs were annotated using GO and KEGG databases. In the Biological Process, the top three enriched GO terms were ‘cellular process’, ‘metabolic process’ and ‘biological regulation’. For Molecular Function, the predominant terms included ‘binding’, ‘catalytic activity’ and ‘transporter activity’. Within Cellular Component, ‘cellular anatomical entity’ and ‘protein-containing complex’ were the most representative terms (Fig. 2B). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the target genes of DEMs were annotated to 368 signaling pathways. Among these, significant enrichment was observed in pathways directly or indirectly associated with ovarian development, including the Hippo signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, Calcium signaling pathway, Chemokine signaling pathway, TGF-beta signaling pathway, and Insulin secretion (Fig. 2 C).

Ovarian miRNA-mRNA dynamics after eyestalk ablation

Computational analysis predicted 64 miRNAs regulating 300 target genes, generating 832 miRNA-mRNA pairs. Based on DEM expression levels and functional relevance of predicted targets, we identified 11 ovarian development-related miRNA-mRNA pairs and constructed their interaction network.​ The results revealed that each of the four target genes (Hr3, Fzd1, Sad, and CaMK1) was ​​uniquely regulated by a single miRNA, with the following interactions: ​​novel-m0160-5p targets Hr3​​, ​​novel-m0304-3p regulates Fzd1, ​​novel-m0070-5p modulates Sad​​, and ​​novel-m0031-3p controls CaMK1​​. Furthermore, ​​novel-m0165-3p​​ and ​​novel-m0182-3p​​ regulate RERG, while ​​ novel-m0095-3p​​ modulates CTSB and E75. ​​novel-m0038-5p​​ targets three genes: SMAD3, Hr4, and GstD1. Additionally, the Spo gene is regulated by three miRNAs: ​​miR-200-y​​, ​​miR-23-y​​, and ​​novel-m0089-5p ​​(Fig. 3).

Clustering and expression patterns of genes associated with ovarian development​

​​Transcriptomic analysis identified 45 ovarian development related genes. Among these, seven genes (CYP315A1, TMPRSS11D, CYP307A1, Vg1b, LRP4, CCNK, Vg-like) showed low expression in controls but were upregulated after eyestalk ablation. Five genes (Vg4, TUBA3, RDH12, SFRP5, CACNB2) exhibited decreased expression following UEA but increased after BEA compared to controls. Ten genes (CRHBP, TRPA1, IAGBP, LIPH, PDH1, TUBB, CDKN1B, PSPH, ALDH1A1, DCLK1) displayed reduced expression post UEA but no significant changes after BEA. Twenty-three genes (FSTL3, FZD2, CALML4, TRIM65, ACLY, HR3, HR4, PRKG1, RSPO3, TIM, GPCR Mth1, RERG, STK17A, E75, CaMK1, SMAD3, Foxl2, PRKCABP, STAT5B, TUBA8, CTSB, DMRT1, GST1) were highly expressed in controls but downregulated post-ablation. Heatmap analysis revealed distinct expression patterns and clustering characteristics of these genes (Fig. 4 A).​.

To assess the expression trends of ovarian development related genes across the three groups, trend analysis was performed to quantify their expression patterns. Two significant clusters were identified (p < 0.05). The results showed that 20 genes exhibited a consistent downregulation trend, with expression levels gradually decreasing from the control group to the UEA group and further to the BEA group. Additionally, 12 genes displayed a similar pattern of downregulation from the control group to the UEA group, but no significant change was observed between the UEA and BEA groups (Fig. 4 B).​

qRT-PCR validation of DEGs

To validate the reliability of the RNA-Seq data, ten key candidate DEGs were selected for qRT-PCR verification. The results demonstrated that the expression trends of these ten DEGs detected by qRT-PCR were consistent with the RNA-Seq data, confirming the high credibility of the sequencing data (Fig. 5).​

Discussion

​​The eyestalk is a crucial neuroendocrine organ in crustaceans, functionally analogous to the hypothalamic-pituitary system in vertebrates. Its X-organ-sinus gland (XO-SG) complex secretes various neuropeptides (e.g., gonad-inhibiting hormone, GIH; molt-inhibiting hormone, MIH) that negatively regulate ovarian development. In this study, we performed eyestalk ablation and conducted ovarian transcriptome sequencing to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying eyestalk-mediated ovarian regulation. The sequencing data quality assessment demonstrated that all samples exhibited Q30 scores > 94% and mapping rates > 84% against the reference genome, indicating high sequencing quality and data utility. Comparative transcriptomic analysis revealed significantly more DEGs between the control and BEA groups than other pairwise comparisons, while the numbers of DEMs showed no significant variation. This phenomenon may be attributed to the distinct regulatory mechanisms: the neuroendocrine system primarily mediates rapid reproductive regulation through mRNA transcriptional control, whereas miRNAs exert fine-tuning effects via their characteristic one-to-many targeting mode.

Transcriptomic analysis revealed significant upregulation of numerous genes associated with vitellogenesis and steroid hormone biosynthesis, demonstrating that the eyestalk neuroendocrine system maintains ovarian quiescence by suppressing these genes. Eyestalk ablation significantly upregulated multiple vitellogenin genes (Vg1b, Vg4, Vg-like). These results agree with previous findings in Exopalaemon carinicauda [22]. Notably, Vg1b expression increased 8.7-fold in the BEA group compared to controls. This change was substantially higher than the 2.3-fold increase observed in the UEA group. The dose dependent response suggests eyestalks exert graded inhibitory effects on vitellogenesis. These findings support the conclusion that eyestalks suppress ovarian development, as previously demonstrated in Marsupenaeus japonicus [23]. Furthermore, activation of the 20-hydroxyecdysone (20E) synthesis pathway provides additional evidence for the coordinated regulation between reproduction and molting processes in crustaceans.​ In the Insert hormone biosynthesis pathway, CYP307A1 and CYP315A1 expression levels increased by 5.2-fold and 6.8-fold, respectively. These genes encode cytochrome P450 enzymes that are crucial for ecdysteroid biosynthesis [24]. These results align with previous findings in Portunus trituberculatus, further confirming the inhibitory effect of eyestalks on these enzymes [25]. Interestingly, the upregulation of these genes coincided with vitellogenin genes activation. This observation suggests potential coordinated regulation between molting and reproduction in crustaceans.

In the current study, we identified 331 novel miRNAs that significantly expanded the miRNA database of M. rosenbergii. Of particular interest is novel-m0038-5p, which simultaneously targets three crucial genes: SMAD3, Hr4 and GstD1. In insects, the TGF-β signaling pathway regulates juvenile hormone synthesis during metamorphosis. SMAD3 serves as a key transcription factor in this pathway and plays an indispensable role [26]. Hr4 regulates 20E synthesis and mediates 20E signaling during the molting process in Macrobrachium nipponense [27]. Novel-m0095-3p targets two key genes: E75 and CTSB. E75 belongs to the nuclear receptor gene family and plays a critical role in regulating molting in Tetranychus urticae [28]. In Bombyx mori, CTSB is induced by 20E and functions in metamorphosis regulation [29]. This one-to-many miRNA regulatory pattern has been widely reported in multiple crustacean species, including Eriocheir sinensis, M. rosenbergii, and Scylla paramamosain [30–32]. Additionally, we identified that miR-200-y, miR-23-y, and novel-m0089-5p collectively regulate CYP307A1 expression. These findings suggest these miRNAs may participate in steroid hormone metabolism through CYP307A1 regulation. This discovery provides new insights into the unique endocrine regulatory network in crustaceans.

Our study characterized the dynamic mRNA and miRNA transcriptome changes in M. rosenbergii ovaries following eyestalk ablation. These analyses revealed that the neuroendocrine system appears to coordinately regulate both vitellogenin genes and steroid hormone biosynthesis genes, likely contributing to ovarian development control. The identification of multiple novel miRNAs and their target gene networks establishes a foundation for understanding the molecular mechanisms of reproductive regulation in M. rosenbergii. However, the miRNA-mRNA regulatory interactions identified in this study were predicted through computational analysis and require experimental validation. Future work will include: confirmation of targeting relationships using dual luciferase reporter assays, and functional investigation of candidate genes through overexpression and knockdown approaches. Moreover, the biological significance of the enriched pathways in ovarian development needs to be systematically examined by integrating hormone level measurements with mechanistic studies of critical genes within these pathways.Given that M. rosenbergii is an economically important prawn species requiring prolonged vitellogenin accumulation, a strategy targeting multiple Vg genes simultaneously may be necessary to enhance ovarian development.​.
