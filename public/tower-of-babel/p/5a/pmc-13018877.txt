Enhanced cell contact and planarian tissue extract increase DNA replication and viability for neoblast cultivation
Authors: Wenya Zhang, Ling Yang, Nan Li, Kai Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe development of an in vitro culture system for planarian neoblasts remains at an early stage. Although modified neoblast enrichment method and basic culture conditions have been established, it is unclear which micro- and macro-environments can functionally improve neoblast behavior in vitro. Neoblasts tend to aggregate in culture, and when cultured as aggregates in the U-bottom plate or on feeder layers, they show increased DNA replication, potentially due to improved cell-cell interactions. At the macro-environment level, the addition of planarian extract to the medium reduces cell death, increases lipid droplet formation, and decreases the intracellular reactive oxygen species levels. An optimized culture condition has thus been established using U-bottom plates supplemented with planarian extract for the first 3 days, effectively incorporating cues of micro- and macro-environment. By modulating both micro- and macro-environmental factors, this study provides a foundation for further optimization at the metabolic and genetic levels in subsequent studies.

Introduction

Schmidtea mediterranea, a member of the phylum Platyhelminthes and order Tricladida, possesses a robust capacity for whole-body regeneration, making it an important model organism for studying tissue regeneration.1,2,3,4 This regenerative capacity is attributed to neoblasts, which are adult pluripotent stem cells distributed throughout the organism.5,6,7 While numerous cellular and molecular studies have revealed the mechanisms underlying neoblast proliferation and differentiation during regeneration,4,8,9 the development of an in vitro system, such as primary cell culture or cell lines, remains crucial for advancing this field. Such a platform would enable precise functional studies of neoblasts under controlled micro- and macro-environmental conditions.10 Establishing an in vitro culture system would overcome current limitations, allowing for direct investigation of neoblast dynamics, experimental manipulation in defined environments, and accelerated screening of gene functions.

Prior efforts have established fundamental methodologies for neoblast culture, including suitable basal culture conditions and improved strategies for neoblast enrichment.11,12,13,14 After screening various basal media, it was found that X1FS cells cultured under 5% CO2 in KnockOut DMEM maintained pluripotency for 2 days in vitro. However, the X1FS cell population contains only 20%–30% piwi-1+ cells, indicating limited enrichment efficiency.14 In addition to Hoechst 33342 and Calcein AM, alternative fluorescence-activated cell sorting methods based on the higher DNA content in cycling neoblasts have been developed using other dyes, achieving comparable enrichment efficiency. For instance, SiRNeoblasts enriched with SiR-DNA and Cell Tracker, and CRNeoblasts enriched with CellROX and LysoTracker, showed effective neoblast isolation.14,15,16 It has been validated that SiRNeoblasts maintain both proliferation capacity and pluripotency following transplantation, enabling them to rescue neoblast-depleted hosts.14 This approach overcomes the proliferation deficiency of X1 cells and the low neoblast proportion in X1FS population, offering a promising strategy for neoblast enrichment in culture. Despite these advancements, key challenges remain, such as ensuring cell survival and promoting sustained proliferation.

Traditional 2-dimensional (2D) stem cell culture conditions fail to meet the demand of research requiring precise control over cell state, such as the mimic of developmental processes and organ formations.10 Various cultural methods have been practiced based on the in vivo environment, which can be categorized into micro- and macro-environmental conditions. The adoption of the scaffold-free 3D culture method enhances modeling fidelity for tissue-level complexity. In this way, the microenvironment can be more accurately recapitulated within cell aggregates, including extracellular matrix (ECM) with improved topological structure and elasticity, as well as enhanced cell-cell interactions that facilitate exchange of biological signals between neighboring cells through direct contact or paracrine signaling.17 Typically, such aggregates form through the self-organization of single cells. For example, embryonic bodies are formed through the spontaneous aggregation of embryonic stem cells and are widely utilized in the generation of organoids, such as blastocyst-like structures18 and gastruloids,19 which more closely resemble in vivo tissues and serve as important models for embryonic development. Similarly, dissociated planarian cells tend to aggregate in vitro20 and neoblasts also exhibit a propensity for aggregation outside the body.21 However, the functional significance of neoblast aggregates remains poorly understood. Clarifying this issue may yield critical insights for optimizing neoblast culture conditions.

The biochemical environment where neoblasts are located is highly complex. Several studies have sought to analyze the ECM and its function using various approaches. Components such as matrix metalloproteinases, collagen, integrin, and proteoglycans have been shown to play essential roles in planarian tissue regeneration.22,23,24,25 The planarian matrisome has been identified in silico at the transcription level26 and ECM proteomes have been analyzed via liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry following decellularization.25 However, due to the intricate nature of extracellular biochemical components, a comprehensive understanding remains incomplete. As an alternative, tissue extract containing diverse components has traditionally been used as a supplement in cell culture.27,28,29,30,31 These complex extracellular components in planarians can be included in planarian tissue extracts, which can be added to culture media as a whole to simulate the in vivo macro-environment.

In this study, we investigated the effects of aggregate culture and planarian extract on neoblasts in vitro. The regulation of both micro- and macro-environmental conditions resulted in increased DNA replication, lipid droplet accumulation, and decreased reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. Our results indicate that culturing neoblasts as aggregates within the U-bottom plates and supplementing the medium with the planarian extract during the first three days enhances DNA replication and reduces cell death. By adjusting both micro- and macro-environmental factors, this study lays a foundation for further optimization at the metabolic and genetic levels in subsequent studies.

To determine whether neoblasts spontaneously aggregate in vitro, dispersed SiRNeoblasts were seeded on flat-bottom plates and observed for aggregate formation over 2 days. A progressive self-accumulation of cells was observed, with aggregates increasing in size from day 1 to day 2 in culture (Figures 1B, Video S1). To compare gene expression profiles between intra- and extra-aggregate cells, aggregates and non-aggregated cells were mechanically separated using glass capillaries and subjected to transcriptomic analysis. The results revealed upregulation of hist1h2bl, polr2l, and mt-nd3, which are associated with DNA replication and oxidative phosphorylation, in aggregated cells compared with non-aggregated counterparts (Figures 1C and S1A). To assess differences in DNA replication capacity, F-ara-EdU staining was performed. A higher proportion of F-ara-EdU+ cells was detected within aggregates compared with that in non-aggregated cells, indicating enhanced DNA replication activity (Figures 1D, 1E, and S1B). These findings suggest that aggregate culture provides a beneficial environment for neoblasts in vitro.

U-bottom culture facilitates aggregation and DNA replication

To further investigate the impact of aggregation on neoblasts, we used both U-bottom plates and AggreWell-400 plates pre-treated with anti-adherence rinsing solution to facilitate the sedimentation and aggregation of all cells within individual wells. This approach enables the assessment of functional effects associated with neoblast aggregation. SiRNeoblasts were seeded into ultra-low attachment U-bottom plates at a density of 4.1 × 103 cells/well or into AggreWell-400 plates at ∼70 cells/microwell to promote aggregate formation. After sedimentation, cells gradually coalesced and formed a single multicellular aggregate within 1 day (Figure 1F). Genes such as hist1h2blL, polr2l, and mt-nd3, which were upregulated in self-assembled aggregates on flat plates, were similarly upregulated in aggregates formed in U-bottom plates, but not in those formed in AggreWell plates (Figures S1C and S1D). Aggregates formed in the U-bottom plate exhibited consistent effects on cellular responses comparable to those of spontaneously formed aggregates on flat surfaces, whereas AggreWell-generated aggregates did not elicit these effects, but instead suppressed core cellular processes, including spliceosome and ribosome biogenesis processes (Figure S1F). Consequently, the U-bottom plate was selected for further investigation of aggregation effects.

We further investigated how aggregate formation influences DNA replication in the U-bottom plate. To investigate cells that had undergone DNA replication, F-ara-EdU was added to the culture medium during cell seeding, and the proportion of F-ara-EdU+ cells was measured after 1 and 3 days of culture. Compared to the plastic flat plate, neoblasts cultured in the U-bottom plate showed a significantly higher proportion of F-ara-EdU+ cells at both 1 and 3 days (Figures 1G, 1H, and S1E). To elucidate the underlying mechanism of enhanced DNA replication in the U-bottom plate, transcriptome analysis was performed. Compared to the plastic flat condition, 222 genes were upregulated in neoblasts cultured in the U-bottom plate at 1 day in vitro (Figure S1G). Functional enrichment analysis of these genes showed that the DNA replication pathway was enriched in the upregulated genes, including cdk1, mcm4, pura, snrpf, and snrpd1 (Figures S1H and S1I). Notably, the expression levels of cdk1, mcm4, and pura in neoblasts cultured for 1 day in the U-bottom plate were even higher than those in freshly isolated neoblasts (Figure S1I). In addition, pathways related to the cellular energy processes, such as oxidative phosphorylation and the citrate cycle, were enriched in the upregulated genes, suggesting a critical role in supplying the energy required for DNA replication (Figures S1H, S1J, and S1K). In conclusion, aggregate formation in the U-bottom plate enhances DNA replication. These aggregates functionally mimic the DNA replication-promoting effect of spontaneous cellular self-assembly.

DNA replication enhancement in feeder layer conditions

Cell aggregates generate a unique microenvironment that is absent in dispersed cell cultures. Their formation primarily exerts two biological effects: first, aggregation promotes cell-cell contact and signal transduction; second, cellular aggregates limit direct substrate contact for cells, thereby altering their perception of substrate stiffness. To investigate the effects of cell-cell contact and substrate stiffness on neoblast properties, we cultured cells separately on feeder layers and soft substrates.

Establishing a single-layer neoblast-neoblast contact is technically challenging. Instead, we cultured neoblasts on feeder cell layers. Dispersed SiRNeoblasts were seeded on either human foreskin fibroblasts (HFF1) or mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) (Figures 2A and 2B). Under these conditions, neoblasts resided atop a monolayer of feeder cells, preserving the inherent rigidity of the culture plate while allowing direct cell-cell contact between neoblasts and feeder cells. Comparative analysis identified 214 and 205 rescued genes that were downregulated in plastic flat plate cultures compared with non-cultured neoblasts, but upregulated under HFF1 and MEF feeder culture conditions, respectively (Figures S2B and S2C). Functional enrichment analysis of these rescued genes revealed significant upregulation of pathways related to nucleobase-containing small-molecule metabolic process, nucleoside metabolic process, and nucleoside phosphate metabolic process (Figures S2D and S2E). Specifically, genes critical for supplying materials for DNA replication, including uck2, ak7, nudt24, aprt, and uckl1, were restored under feeder layer culture conditions (Figure 2D). In addition, one pathway enriched in upregulated genes in neoblasts cultured on HFF1 feeder layer was related to ECM, named NABA basement membranes (Figure S2F). Positive regulation of ATP metabolic process was also enhanced (Figure S2F). Genes associated with NABA basement membranes (hmcn1, col4a6, col15a1, and fkrp) and ATP metabolic process (fkrp, pfkm, mt-cyb, and mt-nd5) exhibited higher upregulation in neoblasts cultured on the HFF1 feeder layer than in those cultured on MEF (Figures S2G and S2H). Enhanced cell-cell contact, energy supply, and nucleotide metabolism may collectively contribute to the increased DNA replication observed in feeder layer cultures. Then, to verify that direct cell-cell contact mediates increased DNA replication, Transwell co-culture and conditioned medium assays were used to physically separate neoblasts from feeder cells. Under these non-contact conditions, F-ara-EdU incorporation was markedly reduced to the level of the plastic plate culture condition. However, when SiRNeoblasts were cultured on fixed feeder cells that had lost physiological activity, no F-ara-EdU+ cells were detected. These findings demonstrate that DNA replication promotion depends on both direct cell-cell contact and the biological activity of feeder cells (Figures S2I and S2J).

DNA replication enhancement is independent of substrate stiffness

To investigate the effect of soft substrates, we prepared polyacrylamide (PA)-based substrates due to their biocompatibility in cell culture and tunable stiffness, which can be modulated by adjusting crosslinking reagent ratios.33 The bulk elasticity of whole live worms was measured using a rheometer, and the PA formulation was subsequently adjusted to match the native bulk stiffness of the whole organism (∼100 Pa) (Figures 3A–3D, S3A, and S3B). Simultaneously, we confirmed that PA substrates did not significantly compromise planarian cell viability by culturing bulk cells on PA gels of varying elasticity for short durations (Figure S3C). SiRNeoblasts were seeded at the same cell density on both plastic flat plates and PA gels of 100 Pa and cultured for 7 days (Figures 3E and 3F). The proportion of F-ara-EdU+ cells did not increase at day 1 and instead showed a decrease at day 3 (Figures 3G and 3H). In addition to suppressing DNA replication, the soft substrate also increased cell death and reduced the proportion of piwi-1+ cells after 7 days (Figures S3D–S3I). To elucidate the underlying cause of the deteriorated cell state observed in neoblasts cultured on PA gels of 100 Pa, transcriptome analysis was conducted. Among the genes downregulated in cells cultured on PA gels of 100 Pa compared to those on plastic flat plates, oxidative phosphorylation-related genes, including atp6, nd4, uqcrh, mt-nd1, mt-nd3, and mt-co1, were the most significantly enriched among the top 10 annotated hits (Figures 3I and 3J). These findings indicate that substrate elasticity regulates the state of neoblasts by modulating mitochondrial function, thereby influencing energy metabolism.

Planarian extract improves cell viability in U-bottom cultured neoblasts

Compared to conventional culture conditions in plastic flat plates, multicellular aggregation in a U-bottom plate and feeder culture exhibit enhanced DNA replication, which generates physiological microenvironments in vivo, such as cellular contact. In addition to DNA replication, what are the impacts of different microenvironments on cell death and retention of piwi-1+ cells? U-bottom plates exhibited a significant increase in cell death emerging at 7 days (Figure 4B). In the organoid culture process, cells in the center of the organoid sphere tend to die as a result of the exhaustion of nutrients and oxygen, as well as the accumulation of metabolic waste.34 Higher cell death ratios in neoblast aggregates after culture for 7 days may also originate from the metabolic predicament within the compressed cell microenvironment. Compared to the plastic flat plate culture condition, cell death in HFF1 and MEF feeder culture conditions is decreased within 3 days. With respect to piwi-1+ cells, although their numbers progressively declined irrespective of the culture method (Figures S4B–S4F), a significant reduction was observed compared with the plastic flat plate at 7 days under the U-bottom or HFF1 feeder culture condition and at 3 days under MEF feeder culture condition (Figures S4B–S4F). The attenuation of neoblast marker expression was more pronounced in MEF feeder co-cultures than in other conditions (Figure S4F). Conversely, the expression levels of certain fate-specific transcription factors,35 particularly those associated with neuronal subtypes, were elevated in neoblasts cultured on the feeder layer (Figures S4H and S5). This promotion of differentiation was not observed in the U-bottom plate, likely due to the direct influence of feeder cells on neoblast behavior (Figure S4G).

Based on the cell death and the reduction in piwi-1+ cells, we aimed to further optimize the culture conditions on the macro-environment level by modifying the medium composition. To achieve this, we extracted components from planarians and supplemented the culture medium with this extract, which contains in vivo components such as metabolites and proteins. The planarian extract was prepared by grinding live worms (Figure 4C). The gap between the grinding rod and the container is 0.09–0.16 mm, a size larger than the diameter of individual cells, which minimizes cellular disruption during the grinding process. The extract was subsequently filtered through a 0.1-μm strainer to avoid contamination. Based on our previous finding that cell-cell contact promoted DNA replication in neoblasts, we aimed to integrate both micro- and macro-environmental factors to evaluate the state of neoblasts in vitro. Considering the negative impact of MEF cells on the maintenance of piwi-1+ cells, we supplemented the culture medium with the planarian extract under both U-bottom and HFF1 feeder layer conditions, using plastic flat plates as the control. However, the planarian extract was found to be toxic to HFF1 cells (Figure S6A). Consequently, we compared the states of neoblasts cultured under five different conditions (Figure S6B). The extract significantly reduced the cell death of neoblasts in both the plastic plate and the U-bottom plate within 7 days (Figures 4D and 4G). Notably, it rescued the increased cell death observed in the U-bottom plate and outperformed the HFF1 feeder layer in preventing cell death (Figures 4D and 4G). Importantly, the addition of extract did not interfere with the U-bottom plate’s promoting effect on DNA replication (Figure 4E). To evaluate the impact of planarian extract supplementation under the HFF1 feeder layer, the extract was heated to 37°C to eliminate its toxicity to feeder cells (Figure S6D). Heated extract can also decrease cell death in the feeder layer culture condition with no significant effect on piwi-1+ cell ratio and F-ara-EdU+ cell ratio (Figures S6E–S6H). However, this condition did not confer a further significant advantage over the condition of U-bottom plates with planarian extract. Given its operational simplicity, including the absence of cell recovery and frequent replating, the U-bottom plate supplemented with planarian extract was selected as the optimal culture condition for subsequent experiments. These results indicate cross-scale environmental optimization achieved by integrating aggregate culture with the supplementation of planarian extract.

The optimized culture condition includes the U-bottom plate and planarian extract

There was observed to be a significant decrease in the piwi-1+ cell proportion following 7 days of extract treatment, whereas no significant difference was detected under different conditions with or without extract supplement at 3 days (Figures 4F and S6I). To reduce the adverse effects of the extract on neoblasts, we shorten the treatment duration to 3 days. Thus, shorter extract exposure partially alleviates the suppressive effect on piwi-1+ cell maintenance. Meanwhile, the inhibition of cell death remained sustained under the 3-day extract treatment condition (Figure 4G). Upon confirmation of the optimized culture system, phenotyping comparisons of cultured neoblasts were conducted between pre- (plastic flat plate without extract) and post-optimization conditions. Compared to the plastic flat plate without extract, the number of F-ara-EdU+ cells in the U-bottom plate with extract condition increased by approximately 41% at day 1 and 16% at day 3 (Figure 5J). The addition of planarian extracts not only reversed the increased cell death in the U-bottom plate but also improved cell viability compared to the pre-optimized culture condition over 7 days (Figures 5K and 5L). Additionally, there are significantly more H3P+ cells within neoblasts cultured under the optimized condition, indicating enhanced mitotic entry (Figures 5M and 5N). Although no significant difference in the size of piwi-1+ cell populations was observed, recipients receiving cells from the optimized condition exhibited a higher frequency of piwi-1+ cell population formation at 8 days post-transplantation (Figures S7C–S7F). In summary, the optimized culture condition improves DNA replication and cell viability.

Lipid droplet accumulation and attenuated ROS may mediate cytoprotection in the optimized culture condition

Transcriptomic analysis revealed that treatment with planarian extract enhanced processes related to cellular fatty acid metabolism (Figures 6A and S8F). Notably, cells exhibited morphological changes following culture optimization. A significant accumulation of intracellular particles was observed in cells cultured in the U-bottom plates supplemented with the extract (Figure 6B). Flow cytometry analysis further showed that these cells exhibited increased internal complexity, which arose progressively over time in culture (Figures S8B–S8E). Comparative analysis across four culture conditions—plastic flat plate or U-bottom plate, with or without planarian extract—indicated that intracellular particle accumulation was specifically induced by the presence of the planarian extract (Figures 6B and S8A–S8E). In morphology, these particles appeared as regularly shaped, spherical structures with a high index of refraction (Figures 6B and S8G), features similar to lipid droplets.36 Given the observed enhancement of fatty acid metabolism upon extract treatment, we used Nile red staining to confirm the identity of these particles as lipid droplets (Figure 6C). Indeed, planarian extract obviously increased Nile red fluorescence in cultured neoblasts (Figure 6D). Notably, lipid droplets were also observed in freshly isolated neoblasts prior to culture (Figure S8H). In summary, planarian extract promotes lipid droplet accumulation in cultured neoblasts and enhances fatty acid metabolism. To verify the relationship between decreased cell death and lipid droplet accumulation, the DGAT1 inhibitor A922500 was used to reduce lipid droplet accumulation (Figure 6E). A922500 treatment significantly increased cell death (Figure 6F). Additionally, Nile red High cell population showed a higher piwi-1+ cell ratio (Figures S8I–S8L). A922500 can also decrease the piwi-1 cell ratio (Figure S8M). Lipid droplets are reported to play an important role in antioxidant function in the stem cell niche of Drosophila.37 We hypothesize that the extract inhibits cell death via antioxidant mechanisms. First, we confirmed that ROS levels increased during culture. Given that mitochondria are a major source of ROS, MitoSOX staining was performed to stain neoblasts before and after 3 days of culture. The proportion of MitoSOX-positive cells increased significantly from 16% to 46% after 3 days in culture (Figure S9A). Cells exhibiting high MitoSOX signals were predominantly among the unhealthy population destined for cell death, whereas healthy cells exhibited minimal or no MitoSOX signal (Figures S9B and S9C).

To assess the antioxidant effect of the optimized culture condition, we compared the transcriptomes of neoblasts cultured under optimized conditions (U-bottom plate with extract) and non-optimized conditions (plastic flat plate without extract) after 7 days. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that processes related to the response to stimuli were downregulated under optimized conditions (Figure 7A). Specifically, antioxidant-related genes, including idh1, gclm, txn2, prdx2, and sod1, were downregulated, suggesting a potential reduction in intracellular ROS levels under the optimized condition (Figure 7B). We further validated the decreased ROS levels using CellROX, a fluorescence indicator of intracellular ROS. The fluorescence intensity of CellROX was significantly decreased in neoblasts cultured under the optimized condition, both at 3 and 7 days (Figures 7C and 7D). Additionally, the proportion of the CellROXhigh cell population was markedly decreased under the optimized culture condition (Figures 7E–7G and S9D). Notably, in the absence of the extract, neoblasts cultured in either the plastic flat plate or U-bottom flat exhibited comparable ROS levels and the expression level of antioxidant genes. ROS suppression was observed exclusively upon extract addition. We attempted to utilize antioxidants to reduce ROS within cells and detect cell death. However, antioxidant treatment did not decrease either ROS levels or cell death (Figures S9E and S9F). To evaluate cell apoptosis, we used Annexin V stain and found that the CellROXhigh cell population showed reduced apoptotic signals in the optimized condition (Figure 7H). In the Annexin V-negative cell population, ROS levels were lower under the optimized culture condition compared to non-optimized conditions (Figure 7I). Discussion

Research on establishing an in vitro culture system for planarian neoblasts remains in its early stages. In this study, we aimed to optimize neoblast culture conditions by culturing neoblasts in aggregates and investigating the effects of the microenvironment on neoblast behavior in vitro. Regarding the macro-environment, we supplemented the culture medium with planarian extract and examined its impact. Ultimately, we established an optimized culture condition by integrating beneficial factors from both micro- and macro-environments: the use of ultra-low attachment U-bottom plates combined with planarian extract treatment for the first 3 days. Under this condition, the number of F-ara-EdU+ cells increased by 41% after 1 day of culture (Figure 5J). To investigate why DNA replication of neoblasts is enhanced in U-bottom plates, we evaluated DNA replication in two specific conditions: on a soft substrate (100 Pa elasticity) and in co-culture with feeder cells (to simulate cell-cell contact). After 3 days of culture, feeder cell co-culture increased the number of F-ara-EdU+ cells, whereas 100-Pa soft substrate reduced their abundance. Moreover, other cell types, including neural stem/progenitor cells, myoblasts, and epithelial cells, were found to aggregate in vitro, with aggregated cells exhibiting higher proliferation ability.38,39,40,41 In the case of neural stem/progenitor cells, enhanced proliferation within aggregates has been linked to cell-cell interactions mediated through MAPK signals.38 Additionally, in the optimized neoblast culture condition, planarian extract not only mitigated the increased cell death associated with U-bottom plate culture but also reduced cell death by 5%–10% compared to the condition before optimization. In summary, the results show that neoblasts can respond to different environments in vitro. Culture condition in this study improves the S-phase entry and survival of neoblasts in the short term, representing a step toward better culture conditions. While our data show increased F-ara-EdU incorporation and H3P+ cells under optimized conditions, suggesting that an improved culture environment supports more robust cell cycle progression by enhancing entry into S phase and M phase, it remains unknown whether cultured neoblasts can complete the full cell cycle. In future studies, as the in vivo microenvironment and the mechanisms regulating functions of neoblasts are increasingly elucidated, like specific niche regulating pluripotency and cell division, the optimization of neoblast culture conditions by recapitulating the native niche may ultimately enable functionally long-term maintenance of neoblasts outside the organism.

Is the clustering of neoblasts an inherent characteristic in the planarian body? In vivo, neoblasts exhibit migratory behavior. After partial lethal irradiation, neoblasts migrate to regions where they have been deleted due to tissue loss, as observed in previous studies.42 During early regeneration, neoblasts accumulate at the wound site within 18 h, even in regions initially devoid of neoblasts, such as the region anterior to the photoreceptors.43,44 These observations indicate that neoblasts are capable of migration and aggregation during regeneration. However, it remains unclear whether this aggregation at the wound site is necessary for initiating the subsequent regeneration process. The formation of cell-cell contacts through aggregation in vitro may simulate the microenvironment of the wound site, where neoblasts naturally migrate and cluster. The in vitro findings suggest that the accumulation of neoblasts may promote their S-phase entry at the wound site, a process critical for normal tissue regeneration. Regarding the size of aggregates and cell types contributing to aggregate formation, the current study did not explore their specific effects in detail. Future research could further optimize cell density in U-bottom wells to explore this parameter more thoroughly. Whether the co-aggregation of different cell types, such as neoblasts and parenchymal cells,45 can achieve better optimization by better simulating the in vivo environment should be explored in future research.

Our study revealed that metabolic processes in cultured neoblasts were dynamically regulated by external factors. Transcriptome analysis revealed that energy metabolism, including oxidative phosphorylation and citrate cycle, was promoted in aggregates cultured in the U-bottom plate. The ATP metabolic process was also improved in neoblasts cultured on the HFF1 feeder layer. In human mesenchymal stem cell culture, the transition from 2D to 3D conditions was accompanied by the activation of OXPHOS and glycolysis.46 Enhanced energy metabolism may satisfy the increased energy demands during DNA replication in cultured neoblasts. ECM stiffening can induce mitochondrial fusion.47 It has been reported that substrate stiffness regulates mitochondrial morphology through integrin-kindlin-2/PINCH-1 signaling. Mitochondrial dynamics are closely linked to cellular metabolism.48 In planarians, knockdown of opa1, a gene critical for mitochondrial fusion, impairs neoblast pluripotency and tissue regeneration.49 Transcriptome analysis of neoblasts cultured on a 100-Pa soft PA gel revealed downregulation of OXPHOS-related genes, including atp6, nd4, uqcrh, mt-nd1, mt-nd4, and mt-co1 (Figures 3I and 3J). The compromised cell state under soft culture conditions may result from mitochondrial responses to the extracellular soft substrate, leading to dysregulated energy metabolism, particularly OXPHOS. In the future, precise measurement of planarian mesenchyme elasticity, where neoblasts reside, will enable a more refined dissection of how substrate stiffness regulates neoblast behavior. In addition, planarian extract influenced the metabolic profile of cultured neoblasts, potentially through the provision of fatty acids.50 Fatty acid metabolic process-related genes were upregulated in neoblasts with extract treatment. Fatty acids play a key role in regulating cell state. During the primed-to-naive pluripotency transition of mouse epiblast stem cells, lipid remodeling facilitates the transition and influences cell fate decisions.51 Moreover, lipid droplet accumulation increased after the addition of the extract during the culture of neoblasts. Lipid droplets are crucial for metabolic flexibility.52 It shows that the inhibition of lipid droplets can increase cell death and decrease piwi-1+ cell maintenance (Figures 6F and S8M), indicating that extract-mediated lipid droplet accumulation plays a broad role in shaping the overall state of neoblasts in vitro through metabolic regulation. Notably, lipid droplets were also observed in freshly isolated neoblasts. Whether these lipid droplets function as an energy reserve or protect cells from the damage caused by free fatty acids oxidation37,53 remains to be determined. Additionally, planarian extract decreased ROS levels in cultured neoblasts. Since excessive ROS can trigger cell death via mitochondria, death receptor, and endoplasmic reticulum pathways,54 the extract-induced reduction in ROS level may be a downstream consequence of coordinated metabolic reprogramming that coincides with improved cell survival. Therefore, these results suggest that modulating the metabolic state, such as adjusting oxygen levels55 and enhancing energy metabolism through OXPHOS or lipid metabolism,56 can influence the cell state of neoblasts. This represents a promising avenue for improving neoblast culture methods. In addition, future progress in a deeper understanding of neoblasts’ native metabolic state will also be essential for the improvement.

Limitations of the study

Despite exploring multiple aspects, including both micro- and macro-environmental factors, we have not identified conditions that effectively sustain piwi-1+ cell proportions, which warrants further investigation in future studies. Additionally, the mechanism by which planarian extract regulates cellular state through cellular metabolism remains to be elucidated in future studies.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Schmidtea mediterranea asexual CIW4 strain was maintained in 1× Montjuïc water at 20°C in the dark as previously described.57 The worms were fed once or twice a week with beef liver paste. Worms for experiments were starved for 5-10 days. In this study, only the asexual strain was used, and the sexual strain was not included. The influence of sex on the study results was not evaluated.

Culture of SiRNeoblasts in different conditions

The culture medium,32 comprising KnockOut DMEM (ThermoFisher, 10829018), 100× non-essential amino acid solution (Gibco, 11140050), 100× vitamin solution (Gibco, 11120052), 100× sodium pyruvate (Gibco, 11360070), 100× Pen/Strep (Gibco, 15070063), and 20% heat-inactivated FBS (Sigma, F8318), is referred to as KSF20 in this study.

The tissue culture plates were prepared as follows. The plastic flat plate (WHB, WHB-96) was treated with 50 μg/mL poly-D-lysine (BD Biosciences, 354210) overnight at 4°C. The wells were subsequently washed twice with 200 μL KSF20 before seeding cells. The U-bottom plate (Corning, 3788) was treated with 50 μL anti-Adherence Rinsing Solution for 10 min, followed by two washes with 200 μL KSF20. For the Aggrewell plate (AggreWell™400 24-well plate, 34415), 500 μL anti-Adherence Rinsing Solution was added, and the plate was centrifuged at 1,300× g for 10 min. The wells were rinsed twice with 1 mL KSF20.

Cells were seeded at a consistent density of 5.6×104 cells/cm2 among four conditions: the plastic flat plate, PA gel, HFF1 feeder layer, and MEF feeder layer. Specifically, 70 μL of cell suspension containing 1.8×104 SiRNeoblasts was added to each well of the 96-well plate for the plastic flat plate, HFF1 feeder layer, and MEF feeder layer. For the PA gel condition, 30 μL of cell suspension containing 6×104 SiRNeoblasts was seeded onto the gel in a 24-well plate. The plate was undisturbed for 30 min to allow cells to sediment onto the gel. Then 800 μL of KSF20 was added along the sidewall of each well to minimize disturbance of the settled cells. For the U-bottom plate, 70 μL of cell suspension containing 4,100 SiRNeoblasts was added to each well. For the Aggrewell plate, 800 μL of cell suspension containing 8.4×104 SiRNeoblasts was added to each well, followed by centrifugation at 100× g for 3 min to ensure uniform distribution and sedimentation of approximately 70 SiRNeoblasts per microwell.

For the lipid droplet inhibitor, 50 μM A922500 (TargetMol, T6365) was added to the culture medium when seeding cells before culture. It continues to treat cells for 3 days. For antioxidant treatment, 50 μg/ml AAP2 (2-Phospho-L-ascorbic acid trisodium salt, Sigma, 49752), 100 μM BME (2-Mercaptoethanol, Gibco, 21985023), 1 mM GSH (L-Glutathione reduced, Sigma, V900456), and 1 mM NAC (N-acetyl cysteine, Sigma, A7250) were used. The treatment continues for 3 days. The medium containing antioxidants was changed at 1.5 days.

Cells were cultured in an incubator (Memmert, ICH110C) maintained at 22°C with 5% carbon dioxide and 80% humidity. The culture medium was replenished at a volumetric ratio of 1:2 (spent: fresh) every three days, and the feeder layer was replaced every 4-5 days. For the optimized culture method in the U-bottom with planarian extract, the medium in the well was refreshed by replacing two-thirds of the volume with extract-free medium (KSF20 medium) at day 3. For a longer culture duration, the medium was refreshed every three days by replacing two-thirds of the volume with extract-free medium (KSF20 medium).

Feeder cell layer preparation

Mitotically inactivated HFF1 (ATCC, SCRC-1041) and MEF (embryonic day 13.5) cells treated with 10 μg/mL Mitomycin C (Sigma, M5353) for 3 hours were recovered one day prior to seeding planarian cells. HFF1 was obtained from a commercial source and was used without further authentication. Primary MEF cells were isolated from E13.5 embryos of ICR mice. No mycoplasma contamination was detected. Frozen vials of HFF1 and MEF cells were rapidly thawed in a 37°C water bath, suspended in pre-warmed DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% Pen/Strep, and centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 5 min to remove DMSO. 1-3×104 HFF1 or MEF cells were seeded per well in a 96-well plastic flat-bottom plate and cultured with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% Pen/Strep. After one day of culture at 37°C and 5% CO2, the medium was removed, and the well was washed twice with KSF20 to remove unattached cells. Then, feeder cells were cultured in KSF20 medium at 22°C and 5% CO2 in the incubator used for planarian cell culture. When seeding SiRNeoblasts, the medium was removed and replaced with the cell suspension. The interval between feeder plating and neoblast seeding is 24-30 hours.

Method details

Polyacrylamide gel preparation

Coverslips were prepared to facilitate the attachment of PA gel. 12 mm coverslips (Fisher Scientific, 1254580) were placed on a glass surface. 0.1 M NaOH solution was added on top of the coverslips, which were subsequently dried in an oven at 60°C. Next, 3-aminopropyltrimethoxysilane (APTES, Macklin, A800088) was evenly spread over the glass surface for 6 min, after which the excess liquid was removed. The coverslips were submerged in Milli-Q water for 10 min and washed three times. 0.5% glutaraldehyde solution (Macklin, G810413) was prepared by diluting the stock solution with 100 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.0). This solution was evenly applied to the coverslips and incubated for 30 min. After incubation, the glutaraldehyde solution was removed, and the coverslips were washed three times with Milli-Q water for 10 min each. Before use, the treated coverslips were gently dried with Kimwipes and sterilized under UV irradiation.

To prepare the PA gel, 40% acrylamide solution (Leagene, PE0145) and 2% bis-acrylamide solution (Leagene, PE0147) were mixed in sterile water. A fresh 10% APS solution (Macklin, A801035) was prepared by dissolving the powder in water and then sterilizing it with a 0.22 μm strainer. To activate polymerization in 1 mL gel solution, 1 μL TEMED (Sigma, T7024) and 10 μL 10% APS solution were added. After thorough mixing, 50 μL of the solution was immediately transferred onto a glass slide. A treated coverslip, with the treated side facing the solution, was placed over the mixture. The gel was allowed to polymerize for 5 to 15 min in a laminar flow hood. Once polymerized, the gel attached to the coverslip was carefully removed from the glass with a razor blade. The gel was rinsed with 100 mM HEPES and stored in HEPES at 4°C until use. The elasticity of the PA gel can be adjusted by changing the amount of acrylamide and bis-acrylamide in the solution.

Elasticity measurement

The rheometer (TA, ARES-G2) was used for bulk elasticity measurement of whole live worms with non-invasive operation. An 8.0 mm stainless steel parallel plate was selected. The temperature of the plate was set to 22°C. Compression mode was employed with an axial force of 0.03 N and a sensitivity of 0.01 N. An oscillation time scan was performed for 120 seconds with 20 data points collected. A strain of 5% and a frequency of 1 Hz were applied. The gap was set to 0.5 mm. The geometry gap was zeroed before measurement. For the measurement of live planarians, 10-20 live worms were placed at the center of the plate. Their positions of the worms were adjusted to ensure proper filling between the upper geometry and the lower plate. Once the worms were placed well, measurement was started immediately after removing the Montjuïc water. The measurement was completed within three minutes. After the measurement, the worms remained in a normal state of survival. To prevent mechanical perturbation and maintain physiological characteristics, each individual batch of planarians was used for no more than three consecutive measurements. For the measurement of PA gel, the gel was removed from the coverslip and trimmed to match the diameter of the upper geometry.

SiRNeoblasts enrichment through fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)

SiRNeoblasts enrichment was performed according to a previously established method.32 Briefly, tails of 6-8 mm worms in length were cut and dissociated to single cells in 1× Calcium Magnesium free buffer with BSA (CMFB) solution. Tissue dissociation was facilitated by pipetting every 3-5 min. Complete tissue dissociation was achieved within 20 minutes, with no visible tissue fragments. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm strainer and subsequently centrifuged at 290 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The cell pellet was resuspended in isotonic planarian medium (IPM) solution, and the cell density was adjusted to 4–6× 106 cells/mL. Cells were then stained with SiR-DNA dye (Cytoskeleton, CY-SC007) for 1 hour at a final concentration of 1 μM and Cell Tracker Green dye (ThermoFisher, C7025) for 10 min at a final concentration of 2.5 μg/mL at room temperature. The cell suspensions were centrifuged at 290× g for 10 min at 4°C, and the cells were resuspended in IPM. DAPI (ThermoFisher, D3571) was added to the cell suspension at a final concentration of 1 μg/mL before sorting. The cell sorter BD FACSAria Fusion SORP was used for cell sorting. SiRNeoblasts were collected in a 15 mL tube containing 10 mL of KSF20.

Planarian bulk cell preparation

Dissociated cell suspension was filtered sequentially through a 70 μm filter, followed by two passes through a 35 μm filter. After centrifugation at 290× g for 10 min at 4°C, the cells were resuspended in KSF20 for culture.

Planarian extract preparation

The method for obtaining planarian extract was adapted from a previously reported protocol with modifications.58 A Tenbroeck Tissue Grinder (WHEATON, 357421) was used for extract preparation. Prior to use, the grinder was cleaned by sonication for 20 min, rinsed with 75% ethanol, and subsequently washed three times with sterile water. It was further rinsed twice with 500 μL KnockOut DMEM. Worms measuring 4-6 mm in length were wiped with Kimwipes to remove excess moisture before being transferred into the grinder tube. 500 μL KnockOut DMEM was added, and the pestle was inserted, pressing it firmly to the bottom of the tube. The pestle was rotated three complete turns counterclockwise, followed by three full clockwise rotations. The tube was then removed from the pestle, and this process was repeated 10-20 times until no visible planarian tissue remained. The homogenized mixture was transferred to a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube and centrifuged at 5,000 × g at 4°C for 15 min. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube, and the pellet was discarded. The supernatant was filtered with a 0.1 μm syringe-driven filter to remove contaminants. To minimize the batch effects, protein concentration was quantified using absorbance at A280 on a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. The protein concentration of the extract was adjusted to 0.6-0.7 mg/mL with KnockOut DMEM. Then the extract was used to prepare complete medium by supplementing it with 100× non-essential amino acid solution, 100× vitamin solution, 100× sodium pyruvate, 100× Pen/Strep, and 20% heat-inactivated FBS. In the complete medium, the final volume fraction of the extract is 76%. For example, 1 mL of complete medium with extract includes 760 μL planarian extract, 10 μL each of four supplements, and 200 μL FBS. Planarian extract should be prepared freshly. In each independent experiment, different batches of planarian extract were used.

Flow cytometry analysis for cell death

For cell collection, cultured cells were resuspended and transferred to 1.5 mL tubes. The volume of the cell suspension was adjusted to 50 μL per sample.

For dead cell analysis, cells were stained with propidium iodide (ThermoFisher, P3566) at a final concentration of 1 μg/mL for 5 min before analysis.

For Annexin V signaling detection, 12.5 μL 5× Annexin Binding Buffer (ThermoFisher, V13246) and 3.1 μL Annexin V-Alexa Fluor 350 (ThermoFisher, A23202) were added to the cell suspension and mixed thoroughly. Cells were stained for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Subsequently, 62.5 μL IPM was added, and the samples were placed on ice. Cells were stained with propidium iodide at a final concentration of 1 μg/mL for 5 min before analysis. All samples were analyzed within 2 hours using the CytoFLEX LX Flow Cytometer (Beckman) and data were processed with FlowJo software. Samples without staining with PI as a blank group for confirming the gating position of PI+ cell population. Gating methods were the same for the analysis of all samples.

F-ara-EdU assay

F-ara-EdU (5 mg, CCT-1403-5, Vectorlabs) was dissolved in 50 μL DMSO to prepare a 100 mg/mL stock solution, which was stored at -20°C. A working solution was prepared by diluting the stock solution with sterile water at a ratio of 1:36. This working solution was mixed with culture medium at a ratio of 1:10 before use. Following 1 or 3 days of culture, cells were collected by pipette resuspension. The resulting cell suspension was transferred to a 384-well plate and centrifuged at 290 × g for 5 min. Cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde solution diluted in 0.4× PBS. After the in situ hybridization, F-ara-EdU+ cells were stained using the Click-IT assay. The reaction solution contained 1× TBS, 2 mM CuSO4, 5 μM Azide, 50 mM Ascorbic acid, and 1 μg/mL DAPI. 50 μL of reaction mixture was added to each well, and cells were stained for 1 hour. After staining, cells were washed three times with PBST (0.1% Triton-X), each for 10 min.

ROS and lipid droplets detection

For ROS detection, MitoSox (ThermoFisher, M36008) and CellRox (ThermoFisher, C10444) were used at a final dilution of 1:1000 in culture medium for 30 min at room temperature. After staining, the cells were centrifuged at 290 × g for 10 min to remove the dyes and then resuspended in culture medium for flow cytometry.

For lipid droplet detection, Nile Red was used at a final concentration of 2 μM for 30 min at room temperature. Before staining, cells were centrifuged at 290 × g for 10 min and then resuspended in KnockOut DMEM without supplements. After staining, the cells were centrifuged at 290 × g for 10 min to remove the dyes and then resuspended in culture medium for flow cytometry.

Cell fluorescent in situ hybridization and immunofluorescence staining

Cells were collected through resuspension with a pipette and then reseeded to a 384-well plate, which was centrifuged at 290× g for 5 min. The cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde solution (Sigma, F8775) diluted in 0.4× PBS for 20 min, followed by two washes with PBST (0.1% Triton X-100), each for 5 min. After blocking with prehybridization buffer for 1 hour at 56°C, cells were hybridized with a DIG-labeled piwi-1 riboprobe at 56°C for at least 16 hours. After washing with 2× SSCW (0.1% Tween-20) and 0.2× SSCW (0.1% Tween-20) at 56°C, cells were blocked with horse serum at room temperature for 30 min. The cells were then incubated with anti-digoxigenin-POD (Roche Diagnostics, 11207733910) at room temperature for 2 h. After washing with PBSTX (0.1% Triton X-100), the signal was developed with tyramide-conjugated Cy3 (Sigma, PA13101) or Cy5 (Sigma, PA15101) at room temperature for 10 min. After washing with PBSTX (0.1% Triton X-100), cells were stained with DAPI.

For PIWI-1 immunofluorescence staining, the PIWI-1 antibody (gift from Dr. Jochen Rink) was used at a dilution of 1:5,000. After incubation at room temperature for 2 hours, the cells were washed with PBSTX (0.1% Triton X-100). Subsequently, Alexa 647-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Abcam, ab150119) was applied for staining for 45 min. Finally, the cells were washed with PBSTX (0.1% Triton X-100).

Cell transplantation

For the transplantation of no cultured cells,14 4,100 freshly sorted SiRNeoblasts were transplanted into one lethal-irradiated CIW4 planarian. SiRNeoblasts were cultured under the plastic flat plate without planarian extract or the U-bottom plate with planarian extract for 3 days. Then, 4,100 cultured cells (the original cell number at the time of seeding) were transplanted into one lethal-irradiated CIW4 planarian. After 8 days post-transplantation, the hosts were fixed for co-staining of piwi-1 FISH and H3P IF.

Cell imaging and analysis

DMi8 inverted microscope was used for live cell imaging. Live cell panoramic super-resolution microscopy (MH-PanoView, Pellucid Optics Technology (Nantong) Co., LTD) was used for optical diffraction tomography (ODT) imaging.

For cell staining imaging, a Confocal High-Content Imaging System (Perkin Elmer, Opera Phenix Plus) was used, which was equipped with a 20× air objective (NA=0.4, WD=8.28). For Figure 1E, imaging was performed at the focal plane with the strongest DAPI signal to ensure representative imaging. The ratio was calculated as (total number of positive cells)/(DAPI+ cells) in each field. For other experiments, the aggregates were dissociated into single-cell suspensions by gentle pipetting before fixation and staining. To calculate the positive cell ratio of F-ara-EdU and piwi-1, cells within the whole well were collected after culture in different conditions. Then, entire well fields were imaged and used for calculation after staining. Image analysis was carried out using Harmony software. In Figures 1G and 1H, 2C; 3G, 3H; 4E; 5E, 5F, 5J; S1E; S2A, S2I; S6F and S6G, the positive cell ratio of F-ara-EdU+ for each sample was calculated as (total number of F-ara-EdU+ cells)/(initial number of seeded cells). In Figure 4F; 5G, 5H; S3D, S3E; S4B, S4C; S6H, S6I and S8M, the positive cell ratio of piwi-1 for each sample was calculated as (total number of piwi-1+ cells)/(initial number of seeded cells). In Figure 5M, the positive cell ratio of H3P+ for each sample was calculated as (total number of H3P+ cells)/(initial number of seeded cells). The imaging method and analysis method were the same for all samples. All data are represented as mean ± SEM. Prism software was used to generate the bar charts and line graphs.

The ratios of F-ara-EdU+, H3P+, and piwi-1+ cells are calculated relative to the initial number of seeded cells, rather than to viable cells, because our goal was to evaluate how many of the originally plated cells ultimately engage in DNA replication under different culture conditions. Differential cell death across conditions can confound the interpretation. This calculation method provides a more consistent and functionally relevant measure of cultural performance.

Separation of aggregates and non-aggregated cells

After 48h of culture, aggregates and non-aggregated single cells were separately harvested using glass capillaries (inner diameter, 80 μm) with CellCelector (Sartorius, CellCelector Flex and FlowBox). After washing in 0.4× PBS (0.04% BSA), samples were processed for Smart-seq.

Smart-seq and data analysis

The library was generated according to a previously described method.59 Cultured cells were collected and washed twice with 0.4× PBS containing 0.04% BSA. Cell lysis was carried out using 2× lysis buffer (Plastech, XR2301) supplemented with RNase inhibitor (Vazyme, R301). Reverse transcription was performed using 1M Betaine and LNA-modified TSO (template switching oligo). PCR amplification was performed with KAPA HiFi HotStart polymerase (Roche, KK2601) and ISPCR primers. The resulting PCR products were purified with AMPure XP bead (Beckman, A63881). Tagmentation was performed with Tn5 transposase (Vazyme, TD501), followed by indexing PCR with dual barcodes (i5/i7). Sequencing was performed on an DNBSEQ-T7.

The sequencing data were aligned to the 2014 version of the reference transcriptome as described previously.60 kallisto 0.46.2. R 4.0.5 and tximport 1.18.0 were used for alignment. Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 at the website https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn.61 Function enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes was conducted using Metascape62 and the website developed internally by the HPC Center. The heatmap and Venn analysis were performed at the website http://vip.sangerbox.com/home.html.63 The volcano plots were performed using the OmicStudio tools at https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool.64

Quantification and statistical analysis

Microsoft Excel, Prism 8 and RStudio were used for statistical analysis. Data was represented as mean ± SEM. All significance analysis of the proportion of F-ara-EdU+ cells was conducted using Generalized linear models with binomial distribution in RStudio. For cell death, piwi-1+ cell ratio, H3P+ cell ratio and ROShigh cell ratio, p-values between two groups were calculated by unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests. Adjusted p-values (padj) for comparisons among more than two groups were determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test or Dunnett’s T3 test. ∗, 0.01 < p < 0.05. ∗∗, 0.001 < p < 0.01. ∗∗∗, 0.0001 < p < 0.001. ∗∗∗∗, p < 0.001. n.s., no significance.
