Latent transition analysis of end-of-life care trajectories among cancer patients in South Korea: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Goun Park, Wankyo Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the prolonged and often fragmented course of care before death among patients with cancer, end-of-life care research has largely relied on cross-sectional snapshots rather than the longitudinal trajectories needed to inform quality improvement. This study aimed to identify distinct end-of-life care trajectories among Korean cancer decedents.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used a nationwide dataset of all 75,784 individuals who died from cancer in South Korea in 2016 and examined health care utilization trajectories during the final year of life. Health care utilization during the final year of life was summarized using 5 binary indicators: chemotherapy or radiotherapy; high-intensity treatment (intensive care unit admission, mechanical ventilation, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, and vasopressor use); acute-care hospital admission; long-term care hospital (LTCH) admission; and hospice use. These indicators were analyzed using latent transition analysis to identify end-of-life care states, transition probabilities between states, and the most frequent trajectories.ResultsLatent transition analysis identified 5 end-of-life care states: high anticancer therapy use state, high acute-care hospital admission state, high LTCH admission state, minimal inpatient care state, and high hospice use state. Near the end of life, two primary trajectories emerged, collectively accounting for 36.0% of all patients: transitioning from either minimal inpatient care or high anticancer therapy use state into the high acute-care hospital admission state. High state persistence was observed across subsequent intervals; 90.2% of individuals remained in the high LTCH admission state, and 99.1% remained in the high hospice use state.Conclusions and relevanceThese findings suggest that end-of-life anticancer therapy, LTCH admission, and hospice use are concurrently or sequentially linked to acute-care hospital utilization. The marked state persistence, coupled with the late-stage initiation and limited duration of hospice care, underscores the critical need for earlier, proactive palliative care integration and timely hospice referrals before high-utilization patterns become entrenched.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14949-6.

Background

Among patients with cancer, care near the end-of-life is often intensive and fragmented, involving transitions across multiple institutions and fluctuating use of anticancer treatment, intensive care, and vasopressors [1–4]. These patterns can impose substantial burdens on patients and caregivers, increase health care costs, and diminish quality of life. Consequently, understanding end-of-life care trajectories and the quality of care delivered along those trajectories has become an important focus of research [5–7].

Previous studies of end-of-life care among patients with cancer have primarily relied on cross-sectional indicators, such as aggressive treatment in the last month of life, hospice use, place of death, and the number of health care transitions. These studies have consistently shown that many patients with cancer receive high-intensity treatment near death and that most deaths occur in hospitals [4, 8–15]. However, such indicators provide only a static snapshot of care and do not show how patients move across care settings over time or how patterns of care evolve as death approaches.

For patients with cancer, clinically meaningful end-of-life trajectories encompass not only institutional transfers but also shifts in treatment intensity. As patients approach the end of life, transitions in care—such as the cessation of anticancer therapy, escalation to high-intensity treatment, or acute-care hospital admissions—frequently occur, significantly impacting their quality of life [4, 16–18]. Consequently, research focusing exclusively on care-setting transitions may fail to encapsulate the multifaceted nature of end-of-life care pathways. While prior studies have explored these trajectories, they are frequently limited to specific hospice-related transitions [19–21], isolated institutional transfers [9, 14, 22–25], or simplified representations of patient pathways [7]. Thus, current evidence offers insufficient insight into the sequence, timing, and prevalent patterns of end-of-life care trajectories in this population.

This gap is especially salient in South Korea, where a privately-dominated delivery system and a fee-for-service payment model often lead to fragmented care and excessive service utilization [26]. Such structural factors hinder the continuity and timely decision-making essential for high-quality end-of-life care. Consequently, deteriorating patients often undergo multiple institutional transfers characterized by poor coordination [27–29]. Although inpatient hospice care became reimbursable under the National Health Insurance system in 2015, hospice and palliative care in South Korea have remained concentrated in terminal-stage care and inpatient care settings, with regional and socioeconomic barriers to access persisting [30, 31]. As a result, acute-care hospitals remain a major site of end-of-life care; in 2021, 74.8% of all deaths in South Korea occurred in medical institutions, underscoring the high-intensity, hospital-centered nature of end-of-life care [32].

This study addressed existing evidence gaps by utilizing nationwide claims data and latent transition analysis to identify discrete end-of-life care states and transitions among Korean cancer patients. Moving beyond static metrics, we adopted a dynamic, pathway-oriented approach to characterize common trajectories of health care utilization preceding death. Elucidating these patterns provides a critical foundation for implementing timely supportive care interventions and enhancing the continuity and coordination of care in terminal illness.

Methods

Data sources and study population

We used National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) claims data linked to national cause-of-death data. The dataset included demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status based on health insurance premium quintiles (including Medical Aid), diagnoses (ICD-10), dates of health care use, procedures, prescribed medications, and medical expenditures for the entire population [33].

The study population included all decedents in 2016 whose primary cause of death was cancer (ICD-10 codes C00–C97). To analyze health care utilization trajectories during the final year of life, we restricted the cohort to individuals with at least one cancer-related health care encounter in 2015. Of the 76,420 patients meeting these criteria, 636 were excluded due to missing covariate data (specifically, health insurance premium quintiles or residential region). The final analytic cohort comprised 75,784 patients. The selection process is detailed in Fig. 1. To ensure the robustness of our findings, we conducted supplementary analyses using 2019 data (eTable 3 and eFigure 2 in the Supplement).

Measures

Latent state indicators

We categorized end-of-life care into five prespecified domains derived from established administrative claims-based quality indicators: (1) anticancer therapy (chemotherapy or radiotherapy); (2) high-intensity treatment (ICU admission, vasopressor use, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, mechanical ventilation, hemodialysis, or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation); (3) acute-care hospital admission; (4) long-term care hospital (LTCH) admission; and (5) hospice use [16–18, 34]. These indicators were originally developed and validated to assess the quality of end-of-life cancer care, subsequently endorsed by the National Quality Forum (NQF), and incorporated into the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) quality assessment frameworks. Furthermore, these metrics have been extensively utilized in prior end-of-life research involving cancer populations [35–37].

To minimize measurement overlap among the indicators used to construct the latent states, acute-care hospital admission was operationalized independently from anticancer therapy, high-intensity treatment, and hospice use. Specifically, an acute-care hospital admission was defined as an inpatient episode within a tertiary hospital, general hospital, or hospital that did not include claims for chemotherapy, radiotherapy, high-intensity treatment, or hospice care. This distinct classification prevents the double-counting of a single hospitalization episode across multiple indicators, thereby enhancing the distinctiveness and interpretability of the resulting latent states.

Furthermore, we distinguished long-term care hospital (LTCH) admissions from acute-care hospital admissions. While hospitalization is traditionally used as an indicator of aggressive end-of-life care, this metric typically refers to acute-care settings; conversely, long-term or residential institutions are generally considered distinct care environments [4, 8, 16, 19, 38–40]. In South Korea, LTCHs primarily provide extended stay and custodial care rather than high-intensity treatment. Given that LTCH utilization among cancer patients has risen substantially near the end of life—yet remains conceptually distinct from acute-care utilization—we analyzed these as discrete categories [41–43]. Comprehensive coding definitions and construction rules are detailed in eTable 1 and eFigure 1 in the Supplement.

Covariates

Covariates were modeled as predictors of initial and transition probabilities, rather than the latent states themselves, to account for their influence on trajectory shifts over time. Selection of these variables was guided by Andersen’s behavioral model of health services use and existing literature [44–53]. The final model included: age (continuous); sex (female vs. male); residential region (Seoul metropolitan area vs. non-capital area); socioeconomic status (high: insurance premium quintiles Q4–Q5; low: Q1–Q3 and Medical Aid beneficiaries); and cancer type (hematologic [ICD-10 C81–C96] vs. solid cancer). Because the primary objective was to characterize end-of-life trajectories rather than quantify specific covariate effects, our results focus on latent state identification and transition patterns. Additionally, since end-of-life healthcare utilization among patients with cancer may differ by age and sex, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by age group and sex (eTables 4–9 in Supplement).

Study period

The observation period was defined as the final year of life. Although end-of-life decline may unfold over an extended period, the most clinically meaningful deterioration and escalation in care utilization typically occur within the final months of life [1, 3, 6, 19, 54–58]. Accordingly, we employed a 1-year observation window to capture health care utilization across both the period of relative decline and the phase of rapid terminal deterioration [59]. To examine temporal shifts in care as death approached, the final year was partitioned into five discrete intervals: 365–181, 180–91, 90–61, 60–31, and 30–0 days preceding death [60–68]. These unequal intervals were selected to reflect the typical cancer illness trajectory, which is characterized by relative functional stability followed by an abrupt decline near death [1, 54, 69–75]. By increasing the granularity of assessment as death approached, we sought to more accurately resolve the intensified care needs and clinical volatility inherent to the terminal phase.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted between November 2023 and April 2025 using R (version 4.0.3) and SAS (version 9.4). Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. To identify unobserved end-of-life care states and estimate trajectories over the final year of life, we utilized latent transition models implemented via the LMest package (version 3.1.2) [76].

For each decedent, five binary indicators—anticancer therapy, high-intensity treatment, acute-care hospital admission, LTCH admission, and hospice use—were dichotomized (1 = use, 0 = no use) within each of the five non-overlapping intervals (365–181, 180–91, 90–61, 60–31, and 30–0 days before death). To describe the most frequent trajectories, the model focused on three primary parameters: initial probabilities (the likelihood of starting in a state), item-response probabilities (the clinical characteristics of each state), and transition probabilities (the likelihood of moving between states).

We identified the most prevalent patient-level trajectories based on estimated state sequences. Models with two to five latent states were evaluated and compared using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and entropy, alongside clinical interpretability. To ensure the conceptual stability of the latent states over time, we imposed longitudinal measurement invariance by constraining item-response probabilities to be equal across all intervals [77].

Results

Cohort characteristics and end-of-life care utilization in the last year of life

The final analytic cohort comprised 75,784 cancer decedents. Of these, 46,816 were men (61.8%), and the mean age was 70.47 (SD, 13.23) years (Table 1). Overall, 31,342 individuals (41.4%) resided in the capital area, and 23,320 (30.8%) were in the highest income group (fifth insurance premium quintile). Cancer type was classified as hematologic versus solid tumors. Within the final analytic cohort, 71,361 patients (94.2%) were diagnosed with solid tumors and 4,423 (5.8%) with hematologic malignancies (Table 1).

Among 75,784 cancer decedents in 2016, we examined end-of-life care utilization across five intervals in the final year of life. The proportion receiving chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy was 30.2% during days 365–181 before death and remained similar during days 180–91 (30.3%), but declined thereafter to 19.8% (days 90–61), 17.4% (days 60–31), and 9.3% (days 30 − 0), with the steepest decreases observed in the final 3 months and particularly the last month of life. In contrast, the use of high-intensity treatment (ICU admission, mechanical ventilation, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, hemodialysis, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, and vasopressors) remained below 10% through most of the year (7.7% in days 365–181; 8.5% in days 60–31) but increased sharply to 25.6% in the final 30 days. Acute-care hospital admission excluding anticancer therapy and high-intensity treatment increased from 30.5% in days 365–181 to 49.6% in days 30–0. Long-term care hospital admission also rose as death approached, reaching 19.3% in the final 30 days. Hospice use was rare early in the final year (0.3% in days 365–181) and remained low through days 60–31 (4.6%), before increasing to 16.8% in the last 30 days, suggesting that hospice care was largely concentrated in the final month of life (Table 2).

Results of latent transition analysis

Identification and characterization of latent states

Model comparisons including 2 to 5 latent states indicated that the 5-state model had the lowest AIC and BIC and an entropy of 0.80 (eTable 2 in Supplement). Accordingly, 5 latent states were selected and labeled based on item-response probabilities (Fig. 2). State 1 was characterized by a high probability of receiving chemotherapy or radiotherapy (84%) and was labeled the high anticancer therapy use state. State 2 was labeled the high acute-care hospital admission state, characterized by a 61% conditional probability of acute-care admission and a modest 17% probability of receiving high-intensity treatment. State 3 was characterized by a high probability of LTCH admission (98%) and was labeled the high LTCH admission state. State 4 showed low probabilities (< 10%) across all indicators and was labeled the minimal inpatient care state. State 5 was characterized by a high probability of hospice use (86%) and was labeled the high hospice use state (Fig. 2).

Initial and transition probabilities

At the beginning of the one-year period before death, the initial probabilities showed that 35.2% of cancer patients were in the high anticancer therapy use state (state 1), 8.1% were in the high acute-care hospital admission state (state 2), and 4.3% were in the high LTCH admission state (state 3). More than half (52.3%) were in the minimal inpatient care state (state 4), while only 0.2% of patients were in the high hospice use state (state 5) (Table 3).

The estimated self-transition probability for the high anticancer therapy use state (state 1) was 0.606, indicating that, conditional on being in this state at one interval, patients had a 60.6% probability of remaining in the same state at the next interval. However, 25.6% of the high anticancer therapy use state (state 1) transitioned to the high acute-care hospital admission state (state 2) (Table 3).

The high acute-care hospital admission state (state 2) also showed substantial persistence, with 82.2% of individuals remaining in the same state; 7.2% transitioned to the high LTCH admission state (state 3), and 9.6% transitioned to the high hospice use state (state 5). The probability of transitioning to the minimal inpatient care state (state 4) was less than 0.1%. The minimal inpatient care state (state 4) likewise showed moderate persistence, with 64.9% remaining in the same state; 20.9% transitioned to the high acute-care hospital admission state (state 2), and 10.6% transitioned to the high anticancer therapy use state (state 1) (Table 3). Once individuals entered the high hospice use state (state 5), nearly all (99.1%) remained in that state. The high LTCH admission state (state 3) also exhibited high persistence, with 90.2% remaining in the same state (Table 3).

The distribution of 5-interval trajectories was concentrated: the 15 most common pathways accounted for 60.9% of decedents (46,151/75,784). The single most frequent trajectory was remaining in the minimal inpatient care state (state 4) throughout the follow-up period (state 4-state 4-state 4-state 4-state 4; 8.9%). Two additional common patterns showed a late shift from the minimal inpatient care state to the high acute-care hospital admission state (state 2), specifically state 4-state 4-state 4-state 4-state 2 (6.8%) and state 4-state 4-state 4-state 2-state 2 (5.2%). Similarly, trajectories beginning in the high anticancer therapy use state (state 1) often transitioned to the high acute-care hospital admission state (state 2) as death approached (state 1-state 1-state 2-state 2-state 2, 5.0%; state 1-state 1-state 1-state 1-state 2, 4.5%). High state persistence was particularly pronounced for the high LTCH admission state (state 3), as reflected by the state 3-state 3-state 3-state 3-state 3 trajectory (2.7%). The high hospice use state (state 5) was relatively uncommon and typically emerged only in the final interval (e.g., state 4-state 4-state 4-state 2-state 5, 2.3%; state 1-state 1-state 2-state 2-state 5, 1.5%) (Table 4), most often following the high acute-care hospital admission state (state 2) (Fig. 3; Table 4).

Discussion

Using nationwide, population-based data and latent transition analysis (LTA), this study characterized end-of-life healthcare utilization trajectories among cancer decedents in South Korea. Five distinct latent states were identified: high anticancer therapy use, high acute-care hospital admission, high long-term care hospital (LTCH) admission, minimal inpatient care, and high hospice use. Although each state was defined by distinct utilization patterns, significant overlap was observed across states. Notably, a non-negligible probability of acute-care hospital admission persisted within every state. These findings suggest that end-of-life anticancer therapy, LTCH admission, and hospice use are frequently linked—either concurrently or sequentially—to acute-care hospital utilization.

We also identified end-of-life care pathways that converged toward the high acute-care hospital admission state as death approached. In the initial period of the analysis, approximately 87% of patients were positioned in either the high anticancer therapy use state or the minimal inpatient care state. Over time, individuals in both groups most frequently transitioned to the high acute-care hospital admission state. This pattern aligns with existing literature documenting a sharp increase in acute-care hospital admissions near death in South Korea [11, 78–80].

Patients in the high anticancer therapy use state were more likely to transition to the high acute-care hospital admission state than to the high hospice use state. This suggests that when chemotherapy or radiotherapy ceased to be the dominant mode of care, subsequent management primarily centered on acute-care hospital admission rather than a transition to hospice care. This trajectory aligns with prior literature linking late-stage anticancer therapy to lower hospice utilization, increased acute-care admissions, and higher rates of in-hospital death [81–83]. Furthermore, these patterns contrast with US findings, which indicate stable or declining end-of-life care intensity, alongside relatively low rates of chemotherapy use and hospital admission during the final month of life [7].

Among patients in the high acute-care hospital admission state, some transitioned to high LTCH admission state (7.2%) or high hospice use state (9.6%). This suggests transfers occur when ongoing active disease-directed treatment becomes difficult to sustain. In Korea, acute-care hospitals prioritize high-intensity treatment, and prolonged stays are often not feasible, leading to early discharge and frequent transfers [27, 84]. Compared with prior studies, hospice entry in Korea appears to be more tightly channeled through acute-care hospitals [20, 21].

Transitions from the minimal inpatient care state rarely proceeded directly to either the high LTCH admission state or the high hospice use state; instead, they occurred substantially toward the high acute-care hospital admission. Conversely, transitions from the high acute-care hospital admission state back to the minimal inpatient care state were nearly non-existent. While the inherent limitations of claims data preclude a full elucidation of the underlying clinical or personal reasons for this pattern, our findings align with prior evidence demonstrating that hospitalization and healthcare utilization intensify as death approaches [15, 25, 33, 79].

Apart from transitions, the probability of remaining in the same latent state across adjacent intervals was 82.2% for the high acute-care hospital admission state, 90.2% for the high LTCH admission state, and 99.1% for the high hospice use state. Care-setting changes near the end of life are complex processes shaped by patient and family preferences, institutional arrangements, and a lack of support for coordinated care transitions [14, 19, 23, 27, 28]. This descriptive pattern is often driven by policy environments that inadequately facilitate the delivery of continuous and integrated care [27, 28, 85, 86].

This state persistence was particularly pronounced for the high LTCH admission and high hospice use states. This high persistence of the high LTCH-admission state may reflect a care pathway in which patients remain in LTCHs for prolonged periods, with intermittent acute-care hospital visits as their condition deteriorates, before ultimately dying [41, 43]. The high hospice use state also demonstrated near-complete persistence. This likely reflects late referrals in a clinical context where hospice care is frequently perceived as the cessation of all treatment [85]. Consequently, hospice care in Korea may function as a brief terminal add-on rather than a mechanism for the sustained de-escalation of aggressive medical care [85, 87, 88].

In summary, end-of-life cancer care in Korea remains heavily centered on acute-care hospitals. This landscape is characterized by high state persistence—particularly within high LTCH admission state—alongside a concentrated, late-phase utilization of hospice care. To address these patterns, policy priorities should target the prevailing shortage of early goals-of-care discussions and promote proactive advance care planning.

Strengths and limitations

The use of a large-scale nationwide sample and LTA, which reconstructs transitions rather than a single-point snapshot, is key strength of this study. However, we highlight several limitations.

First, the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) claims data lack detailed clinical information, such as cancer stage, performance status, symptom burden, patient and family preferences, and decision-making processes. Consequently, unmeasured heterogeneity in these clinical characteristics may have confounded both the composition and the transition probabilities of the latent states.

Second, to compare latent states across intervals, we assumed longitudinal measurement invariance by constraining item-response probabilities to be equal over time. This may mask time-specific clinical nuances of end-of-life care, particularly since the clinical meaning and objective of healthcare utilization can shift as death approaches. Our latent states should therefore be interpreted as time-comparable abstractions of a complex clinical reality across the entire study period.

Third, we defined five unequal time intervals over a one-year period to capture the typical trajectory of advanced cancer, which is characterized by relative functional stability followed by an abrupt decline near death. Although this approach was carefully designed to capture the accelerating decline near death while managing model complexity, alternative specifications—such as six equal monthly intervals—are warranted in future research to evaluate the sensitivity of these findings. Finally, given the descriptive design of this study, the observed associations cannot be interpreted as causal.

Conclusion

In this nationwide cohort of cancer decedents in South Korea, end-of-life care trajectories largely converged on acute-care hospital admission as death approached, whereas hospice utilization remained relatively low and concentrated near death. Trajectories showed state persistence, with high persistence after entry into the high LTCH admission state or the high hospice use state.

These findings underscore the necessity of integrating palliative care earlier and facilitating timely hospice referrals while enhancing care coordination across acute-care hospitals, LTCHs, and hospice providers.
