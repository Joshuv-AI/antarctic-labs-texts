Revised cardiac risk index and adverse outcomes in patients undergoing surgical stabilization of rib fractures
Authors: Yu-Hao Wang, Chien-An Liao, Ling-Wei Kuo, Sheng-Yu Chan, Szu-An Chen, Yu-San Tee, Chi-Tung Cheng, Chien-Hung Liao, Chih-Yuan Fu, Shih-Ching Kang, Chi-Hsun Hsieh, Thomas M. Scalea, Marcelo A. F. Ribeiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical stabilization of rib fractures (SSRF) is increasingly performed in patients with severe rib fractures or flail chest. It has been shown to reduce ventilator days and pulmonary complications. Nevertheless, SSRF carries perioperative morbidity, particularly pulmonary and cardiac events, and the optimal preoperative cardiac risk stratification tool for this population has not been established. This study evaluated the association between the Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI) and in-hospital mortality and cardiopulmonary complications in SSRF patients.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used the ACS-TQIP National Trauma Data Bank (2019–2023) to identify adult patients undergoing SSRF. Patients with AIS ≥ 2 in non-thoracic regions, blunt cardiac injury (AIS ≥ 2), or aortic injury were excluded. Because SSRF is an intrathoracic procedure, each patient carried a minimum RCRI of 1; patients were stratified into RCRI = 1, RCRI = 2, and RCRI ≥ 3. The primary outcomes were in-hospital mortality and a composite of cardiopulmonary complications (myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, pneumonia, ARDS, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism). Modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors estimated adjusted risk ratios (RRs), with RCRI = 1 as the reference and adjustment for demographics, regional and chest AIS, fixation details, and comorbidities.ResultsAfter exclusions, 6,139 patients were analyzed (RCRI = 1, n = 4,678; RCRI = 2, n = 1,208; RCRI ≥ 3, n = 253). Adverse outcomes increased stepwise with higher RCRI in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses. Compared with RCRI = 1, RCRI ≥ 3 was associated with significantly higher adjusted risks of in-hospital mortality (RR 3.34, 95% CI 2.06–5.43, p <0.001) and cardiopulmonary complications (RR 3.42, 95% CI 2.08–5.62; both p < 0.001), as well as myocardial infarction (RR 9.28, 95% CI 2.84–30.34, p < 0.001) and pneumonia (RR 5.85, 95% CI 2.72–12.59, p < 0.001). RCRI = 2 was also associated with increased mortality (RR 2.13, 95% CI 1.50–3.04, p < 0.001) and cardiopulmonary complications (RR 1.72, 95% CI 1.20–2.48, p = 0.003).ConclusionsRCRI is a simple, readily available bedside tool that independently stratifies the risk of in-hospital mortality and cardiopulmonary complications in patients undergoing SSRF. Incorporating the RCRI into preoperative assessment may help identify high-risk patients and guide perioperative management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03999-6.

Background

Rib fractures are a frequent consequence of blunt thoracic trauma and can impair respiratory mechanics [1–3]. Over the past decades, surgical stabilization of rib fractures (SSRF) has become increasingly utilized, particularly in patients with flail chest, with its benefits supported by multiple randomized controlled trials [4, 5]. Previous studies have demonstrated that SSRF can reduce the duration of mechanical ventilation, decrease the incidence of pneumonia, and shorten hospital length of stay compared with nonoperative management [6]. Despite these advantages, SSRF is associated with notable postoperative risks. The most common complications are pulmonary in nature, such as pneumonia, respiratory failure, and the need for tracheostomy [7]. Since cardiopulmonary complications represent a major source of morbidity following SSRF, identifying patients at higher risk before surgery is important. Preoperative cardiac risk stratification may help guide perioperative management, optimize patient selection, and ultimately improve surgical outcomes. However, current literature on cardiac risk assessment in SSRF patients is limited, and further investigation is needed to address this gap.

The Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI) is a widely used tool designed to estimate the risk of major adverse cardiac events, including myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, and mortality, within 30 days after noncardiac surgery [8, 9]. It has been validated across various surgical and trauma populations, such as emergency abdominal surgery [10], severe traumatic brain injury [11], and traumatic hip fracture [12], where higher RCRI scores have been linked to worse outcomes. Although the RCRI has been shown to be independently associated with increased complications in patients with rib fractures, prior studies have not specifically focused on those undergoing SSRF [13]. SSRF is often performed in patients with significant comorbidities, and preoperative cardiac risk may play an important role in predicting postoperative cardiopulmonary complications and mortality [14]. Therefore, evaluating the utility of the RCRI in this surgical population could provide valuable insight into perioperative risk stratification and guide targeted management strategies.

Given the lack of published data on cardiac risk assessment in patients undergoing SSRF, the present study aimed to evaluate the association between preoperative RCRI and in-hospital complications in this population. We hypothesized that higher RCRI scores would be associated with an increased risk of mortality and cardiopulmonary complications. Using a large national trauma registry, we sought to determine whether the RCRI can serve as an effective preoperative stratification tool to identify high-risk patients, thereby guiding clinical decision-making and optimizing perioperative care.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective cohort study using data from the American College of Surgeons Trauma Quality Improvement Program (ACS-TQIP®) National Trauma Data Bank (NTDB) from 2019 to 2023.

Data sources and collection

The ACS-TQIP NTDB is a national trauma registry that captures detailed clinical information on patients admitted to participating centers with at least one serious injury, defined as an Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) score of 3 or greater. Data are abstracted by trained personnel at each site following standardized protocols, and the program ensures accuracy through routine audits and periodic quality checks.

Study population

Patients who underwent SSRF were identified using ICD-10 procedure codes, as detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Patients younger than 18 years were excluded. To focus on thoracic trauma, we excluded patients with AIS scores ≥ 2 in non-thoracic regions (head, face, abdomen, extremities, and external). Additionally, patients with blunt cardiac injury (AIS ≥ 2) or aortic injury were excluded.

Baseline characteristics included demographics (age, sex, race), comorbidities, Injury Severity Score (ISS), regional AIS scores, rib injury patterns (bilateral, flail chest), associated thoracic injuries (hemothorax, pneumothorax), and operative details (timing of surgery, number of ribs fixed). Regional AIS was defined as the highest AIS severity among all diagnoses within each anatomical region (head, face, chest, abdomen, extremities, and external), and AIS for rib-fracture was defined as the AIS severity score derived specifically from rib-fracture diagnosis codes within the chest region. Clinical outcomes included in-hospital mortality, cardiopulmonary complications, hospital length of stay (LOS), intensive care unit (ICU) LOS, ventilator days, and in-hospital adverse events such as pulmonary embolism and pneumonia. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included cardiopulmonary complications, hospital LOS, ICU LOS, and duration of mechanical ventilation.

Calculating the revised cardiac risk index

The RCRI is a clinical tool developed to estimate the risk of major adverse cardiac events—such as myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, and mortality—within 30 days after noncardiac surgery [8, 9]. The RCRI consists of six variables: (1) history of ischemic heart disease, (2) history of cerebrovascular disease, (3) congestive heart failure, (4) renal insufficiency, (5) diabetes mellitus, and (6) high-risk surgery. The diagnosis-based operationalization of the RCRI, substituting the diagnoses of diabetes mellitus and renal insufficiency for the original insulin-treatment and serum-creatinine criteria, was proposed and validated by Lindenauer et al. and has been widely adopted in subsequent studies and guidelines [15]. Accordingly, and consistent with prior applications of the RCRI in trauma populations, diabetes mellitus was defined by its documented diagnosis irrespective of insulin use, and renal insufficiency was defined as the presence of acute kidney injury or chronic kidney disease [11, 13]. High-risk surgery is defined as intraperitoneal, intrathoracic, or vascular surgery, or other procedures classified as high risk according to the American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association guidelines [16]. In this study cohort, SSRF was considered an intrathoracic procedure and therefore classified as a high-risk surgery; therefore, all patients had an initial RCRI score of 1. Patients were further categorized into three groups: (1) RCRI = 1, (2) RCRI = 2, and (3) RCRI ≥ 3. Because the number of patients with four or more RCRI components was small, and because prior perioperative studies have consistently combined RCRI ≥ 3 as the highest-risk category, we grouped patients as RCRI = 1, RCRI = 2, and RCRI ≥ 3 to preserve statistical power while maintaining a clinically meaningful stratification [9].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.0). Continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

To evaluate the association between cardiac risk and the specified adverse outcomes, modified Poisson regression models with robust standard errors were used [17]. Patients with an RCRI score of 1 served as the reference group. Covariates were pre-specified rather than selected on the basis of the outcome data. Selection was guided by a priori clinical plausibility as potential confounders, the avoidance of collinearity with the exposure (notably, the components of the RCRI were not entered as covariates, so as not to adjust away the exposure of interest), and the need to keep the model parsimonious given the number of events. On this basis, models were adjusted for age, sex, the Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) score for the chest excluding rib fractures, the AIS score for rib fractures, timing of rib fixation, the number of ribs fixed, and the comorbidities chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, smoking status, dementia, and cirrhosis. Cases with missing values were excluded from the complete-case analysis. For outcomes with a low number of events (myocardial infarction and pneumonia), the events-per-variable ratio was insufficient for stable maximum-likelihood estimation, and complete or quasi-complete separation was observed. For these outcomes, Firth penalized-likelihood regression was therefore applied to reduce small-sample bias and to yield finite, stable effect estimates [18, 19]. Results are presented as adjusted risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals. A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 22,087 patients who underwent SSRF were identified. After excluding pediatric patients (n = 79), and those with an AIS score ≥ 2 in non-thoracic regions as well as patients with severe cardiac injury (AIS ≥ 2, n = 86) or aortic injury (n = 360), 6,139 patients with isolated chest injuries remained for analysis. Based on the RCRI, patients were categorized into three groups: RCRI = 1 (n = 4,678), RCRI = 2 (n = 1,208), and RCRI ≥ 3 (n = 253) (Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics by RCRI group are summarized in Table 1. Patients with higher RCRI scores were older (median age 62 vs. 68 vs. 72 years, p < 0.001) and had a higher prevalence of comorbidities, including hypertension, history of angina, previous myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, chronic renal failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and diabetes mellitus (all p < 0.001). Gender and racial distributions were similar across groups. The prevalence of dementia, peripheral vascular disease, disseminated cancer, cirrhosis, and advanced directives limiting care also increased with higher RCRI categories. Conversely, current smoking and alcohol use disorder were more frequent in patients with an RCRI score of 1 (p < 0.05).

In an exploratory component-level analysis (supplementary Table 2), the renal criterion (chronic kidney disease or acute kidney injury) and congestive heart failure were the RCRI components most strongly associated with both in-hospital mortality (adjusted RR 8.07 and 2.83, respectively) and cardiopulmonary complications (6.41 and 1.78), whereas diabetes, cerebrovascular disease, and ischemic heart disease showed weaker, non-significant associations after adjustment.

Among patients who developed a cardiopulmonary complication, 41 of 166 (24.7%) died during the index hospitalization, and 41 of the 163 in-hospital deaths (25.2%) were accompanied by a cardiopulmonary complication. Among patients who died, the median interval from rib fixation to in-hospital death was 10 days (IQR 6–17), and no death occurred before fixation.

Discussion

SSRF has been increasingly adopted in recent years, supported by evidence demonstrating improved outcomes such as reduced ventilator days, decreased pulmonary complications, and earlier return to work [4, 5]. However, as with any surgical intervention, SSRF carries inherent risks, including cardiopulmonary complications [20]. While the procedure has gained widespread attention and adoption, relatively few studies have examined these risks in detail or provided a clear method to stratify them. The present study highlights a simple and quick tool to assess the risk of cardiopulmonary complications following SSRF.

Multiple perioperative risk stratification tools have been developed, including the NSQIP [21] and VASQIP calculators [22], P-POSSUM [23], E-PASS [24], SURPASS [25], the NELA risk calculator [26], the Charlson Comorbidity Index [27], and the machine learning–based POTTER score [28]. However, few of these have been designed specifically for trauma patients. Several established scoring systems—including the Trauma Score (TS), the Revised Trauma Score (RTS), the Trauma and Injury Severity Score (TRISS), and the New Injury Severity Score (NISS)—were developed using large aggregate trauma datasets to predict outcomes following injury [29, 30]. While valuable, these models are primarily tailored to patients with multiple injuries rather than to those undergoing a single, defined surgical procedure.

A variety of scoring systems have been developed to predict adverse outcomes related to rib fractures or blunt thoracic trauma, including the Thoracic Trauma Severity Score (TTSS) [31], the STUMBL score [32], the Rib Fracture Score (RFS) [33], the Chest Trauma Score (CTS) [34], the RibScore [35], and the Revised Injury Battle Score (RIBS) [36]. In a recent meta-analysis, the TTSS and STUMBL scores demonstrated superior predictive accuracy in both development and external validation studies compared with other models [37]. The TTSS incorporates demographic, clinical, and radiographic data, including age, arterial blood gas analysis, and chest imaging (number of rib fractures, presence of HTX/PTX (hemothorax/pneumothorax), and severity of pulmonary contusion) [31]. The relative complexity of this composite scoring system can make rapid bedside risk assessment challenging. The STUMBL score is simpler, relying on age, number of rib fractures, anticoagulant use, chronic lung disease, and oxygen saturation level [38]. However, these scoring systems are designed to estimate the risk of complications and mortality following chest trauma or rib fractures and do not specifically address the risk profile after SSRF.

The RCRI is a widely used tool for estimating the risk of major adverse cardiac events—including myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, and mortality—within 30 days after noncardiac surgery [8, 9]. It has been incorporated into multiple guidelines for perioperative cardiac protection. The most recent 2024 AHA/ACC/ACS/ASNC/HRS/SCA/SCCT/SCMR/SVM Guideline for Perioperative Cardiovascular Management for Noncardiac Surgery specifically recommends referring to the multiple risk stratification model including RCRI when considering postoperative and anesthesia-related cardiopulmonary risk [16]. Our findings demonstrate that the RCRI can also be effectively applied in the trauma setting, specifically in patients undergoing SSRF.

Beyond estimating risk, identifying SSRF patients at elevated cardiac risk may help direct subsequent perioperative care. The 2024 AHA/ACC/Multisociety guideline endorses validated risk-prediction tools such as the RCRI for estimating perioperative major adverse cardiac events, and patients identified as higher risk may in turn be candidates for guideline-recommended measures such as preoperative natriuretic peptide or troponin assessment and postoperative surveillance for myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery—although these recommendations are keyed to clinical and surgical risk criteria rather than to a specific RCRI threshold [16]. Notably, risk stratification informs monitoring and decision-making rather than dictating specific pharmacologic therapy: although early observational studies suggested a potential benefit of beta-blockade in patients at higher RCRI-defined risk, these findings were not replicated in randomized trials, and current guidelines no longer recommend initiating beta-blocker therapy on the basis of RCRI score [15, 39]. Whether RCRI-guided perioperative interventions improve outcomes in patients undergoing SSRF therefore remains unproven and warrants dedicated investigation.

Our study was designed to assess risk stratification within patients undergoing SSRF rather than to compare operative with non-operative management. Whether the observed mortality and cardiopulmonary complication rates differ as a consequence of surgery cannot be determined from these data, because patients selected for SSRF differ systematically from those managed non-operatively (confounding by indication), and the benefits of SSRF over non-operative management in appropriately selected patients have already been demonstrated in randomized trials. A rigorous operative-versus-non-operative comparison would require a dedicated, propensity-based design and represents an important direction for future work.

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective registry-based analysis, it can establish associations but not causality and is subject to the selection bias, misclassification, and missing data inherent to the NTDB, which also lacks granular clinical information such as laboratory results, intraoperative variables, preoperative cardiac functional status, medications, frailty, and cause of death; residual confounding from unmeasured factors therefore cannot be excluded. The database also does not allow reliable characterization of the additional operative procedures patients underwent, because the procedure coding captures a broad range of interventions including minor and bedside procedures; injury severity, which correlates with the number of such procedures, was adjusted for in all models. Importantly, TQIP records complications as in-hospital events without dating them relative to SSRF, so we could not determine whether a given event (for example, myocardial infarction, pneumonia, or acute kidney injury) occurred before or after rib fixation. Consequently, the temporal sequence—and therefore any causal relationship—between the RCRI, the surgical procedure, and these complications cannot be established. Because patients with higher RCRI scores underwent fixation later, they had a longer interval during which complications could accrue, and although we adjusted for the timing of rib fixation, our findings should be interpreted as associations with in-hospital rather than strictly postoperative events. In addition, the RCRI was operationalized using diagnosis-based definitions consistent with prior administrative and trauma studies rather than the original Lee criteria (notably incorporating acute kidney injury into the renal criterion), and estimates should be interpreted accordingly. Missing covariate values were handled by complete-case analysis without multiple imputation, and the cohort comprised patients treated at U.S. ACS-verified trauma centers participating in TQIP, which may limit generalizability to other settings. Prospective studies with more granular, temporally resolved data are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusions

In this large, national cohort of patients undergoing SSRF, higher RCRI scores were independently associated with increased risks of in-hospital mortality and cardiopulmonary complications. The RCRI, a simple and widely accessible bedside tool, may facilitate preoperative risk stratification in the trauma setting. Incorporating the RCRI into perioperative decision-making for SSRF could help identify high-risk patients and guide targeted management strategies.
