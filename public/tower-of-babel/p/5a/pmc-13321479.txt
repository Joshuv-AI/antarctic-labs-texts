Mechanical versus manual chest compressions during ECPR in the catheterization laboratory
Authors: Stepan Maruniak, Mykhailo Todurov, Serhii Sudakevych, Andrii Khokhlov, Borys Todurov, Justyna Swol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiac arrest occurring in the catheterization laboratory (CathLab) carries a high risk of mortality, especially when effective circulation cannot be restored immediately. Although extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) is increasingly used in this context, there is still limited evidence comparing different chest compression strategies. This study aimed to assess clinical outcomes associated with mechanical versus manual chest compression approaches during ECPR in the CathLab.MethodsWe performed a retrospective single-center observational cohort study from 2018 to 2025, including patients who experienced cardiac arrest in the CathLab and received ECPR. Patients were divided into two groups according to the chest compression strategy used during resuscitation: the mechanical chest compression group (mechanical CPR) and the manual chest compression group (manual CPR). The primary endpoint was low-flow time. Secondary endpoints included procedural characteristics and and clinical outcomes (survival to hospital discharge, favorable neurological outcome (CPC 1–2)).ResultsA total of 58 patients were included in the analysis, including 22 patients in the mechanical CPR group and 36 in the manual CPR group, with comparable baseline characteristics between groups. Low-flow duration was significantly shorter in the mechanical CPR group compared with the manual CPR group (34 [22–53] min vs. 41 [28–63] min, p = 0.024). No significant differences were observed between groups regarding return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) prior to cannulation (9.1% vs. 13.9%, p = 0.698), survival to ICU discharge (27.3% vs. 22.2%, p = 0.756), survival to hospital discharge (22.7% vs. 19.4%, p = 0.752), or favorable neurological outcome at discharge (22.7% vs. 13.9%, p = 0.481). Univariable analysis demonstrated that favorable neurological survival (CPC 1–2) was significantly associated with the presence of a shockable rhythm (90.0% vs. 47.9%, p = 0.017), shorter low-flow duration (35 [20–44] min vs. 42 [30–61] min, p = 0.012), shorter cannulation time (12 [7–13] min vs. 14 [11–18] min, p = 0.038), and ROSC prior to cannulation (40.0% vs. 6.3%, p = 0.013).ConclusionsIn patients with cardiac arrest undergoing ECPR in the CathLab, mechanical chest compressions were associated with shorter low-flow duration but were not associated with improved survival or neurological outcomes compared with manual CPR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12245-026-01296-w.

Introduction

Cardiac arrest in the catheterization laboratory (CathLab) is a life-threatening emergency associated with high mortality rates [1]. In patients with refractory cardiac arrest, prolonged low-flow duration is strongly associated with poor neurological and survival outcomes [2–4]. Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) has emerged as a second-line treatment strategy for selected patients with refractory cardiac arrest and can be implemented directly in the CathLab environment [5]. However, during advanced life support and ECPR initiation, delivering high-quality chest compressions remains technically challenging. Restricted access to the patient due to fluoroscopy equipment, ongoing coronary angiography or percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), radiation exposure to healthcare personnel, and simultaneous cannulation may compromise the quality and continuity of manual chest compressions [6].

In this setting, mechanical chest compression devices such as the LUCAS device (LUCAS™ 2 Chest Compression System, Stryker Corporation, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) may provide automated and consistent compressions with controlled depth and rate while minimizing interruptions during invasive procedures [7]. Although previous randomized trials performed mainly in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest settings did not demonstrate a clear survival benefit of mechanical over manual CPR [8], evidence regarding the optimal chest compression strategy during ECPR initiation in the CathLab remains limited.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare mechanical and manual chest compressions during ECPR in the catheterization laboratory and evaluate their impact on procedural characteristics and clinical outcomes.

Methods

Design and study population

This retrospective single-center study observational cohort study was conducted at a tertiary cardiac center with an established ECPR program. Consecutive adult patients who underwent ECPR in the CathLab between December 2018 and January 2025 were screened for eligibility. According to the institutional protocol, cannulation for ECPR was considered in line with contemporary practice for patients with witnessed cardiac arrest and immediate initiation of CPR, and failure to achieve sustained return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) despite ≥ 15 min of advanced life support. Additional eligibilty criteria included an initial shockable rhythm or pulseless electrical activity of suspected cardiac origin, a no-flow time ≤ 5 min with an anticipated low-flow time ≤ 60 min, and the absence of major comorbidities or contraindications to ECPR, such as advanced malignancy, irreversible neurological injury, or active uncontrolled bleeding. Eligibility for ECPR was determined by a multidisciplinary resuscitation team comprising interventional cardiologists, anesthesiologists, and perfusionists. Patients were excluded from the analysis if CPR was initiated outside the CathLab.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics committee (protocol №9 from 11 March 2026). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with all applicable institutional and national regulations. Given the retrospective design, the requirement for informed consent was waived. Patient confidentiality and privacy were strictly maintained throughout the study.

Study groups

Patients were divided into two groups according to the chest compression strategy used during resuscitation: the mechanical chest compression group (mechanical CPR) and the manual chest compression group (manual CPR). Mechanical compressions were delivered using the LUCAS device (LUCAS™ 2 Chest Compression System, Stryker Corporation, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) while manual compressions were performed by medical staff in accordance with resuscitation guidelines. The LUCAS device was typically applied after recognition of cardiac arrest and initiation of advanced life support measures; prophylactic placement of the LUCAS backplate before arrest in anticipation of high-risk procedures was not routinely performed.

Manual chest compressions were performed by medical staff wearing standard lead protection in accordance with institutional radiation safety protocols. In the manual CPR group, chest compressions were frequently performed with the C-arm positioned over the patient during fluoroscopy-guided procedures. Femoral cannulation, wire positioning, and fluoroscopic confirmation were generally performed during ongoing chest compressions whenever feasible in order to minimize low-flow time. Consequently, fluoroscopy was often used during active manual chest compressions in the manual CPR group.

The choice between mechanical and manual CPR was depended on device availability and left at the discretion of the treating resuscitation team. The Cardiohelp system and extracorporeal circuit (Getinge Group, Gothenburg, Sweden) were prepared and maintained on standby in the CathLab allowing immediate cannulation. Data collection

Clinical and procedural data were retrospectively collected from institutional sources, including the intstitutional ECPR registry, CathLab records, anesthesia documentation, intensive care unit charts, and electronic medical records. Baseline characteristics comprised age, sex, body mass index, cardiovascular risk factors and comorbidities, indication for coronary angiography, presence of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), initial cardiac arrest rhythm, witnessed arrest status, and laboratory values prior to cannulation (including lactate and arterial pH). Procedural variables included the type of chest compression strategy (mechanical vs. manual), duration of no-flow time (the time between cardiac arrest and the start of on-site resuscitation, i.e., the period without circulation), duration of low-flow time (the time from the start of resuscitation until successful establishment of blood flow), duration of cannulation time (the time from puncture to successful establishment of blood flow), performance and success of PCI, as well as fluoroscopy time and contrast volume.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoint of the study, predefined before data analysis, was low-flow duration. Procedural outcomes included indication for procedure, transient return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) prior to cannulation, no-flow time, cannulation time, successful percutaneous coronary intervention, fluoroscopy time, and contrast volume. Clinical outcomes included major bleeding, limb ischemia, survival to Intensive Care Unit (ICU) discharge, survival to hospital discharge, and favorable neurological outcome at discharge.

Transient ROSC prior to cannulation was defined as temporary restoration of spontaneous circulation. All patients included in the study had refractory cardiac arrest requiring ECPR support.

Successful percutaneous coronary intervention was defined as restoration of TIMI 3 flow in the treated culprit vessel with residual stenosis < 30% and without immediate procedure-related major complications.

Favorable neurological outcome was defined as a Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) score of 1–2 at hospital discharge [9]. CPC scores at hospital discharge were directly extracted from clinical documentation and follow-up records in which neurological outcomes had been routinely assessed and documented by the treating clinical team.

Major bleeding was defined as bleeding requiring surgical or radiological intervention and/or transfusion of > 3 red blood cell units within 24 h [10]. Limb ischemia was defined as hypoperfusion of the cannulated limb requiring intervention, including distal perfusion cannula placement, fasciotomy, or surgical revision.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate based on their distribution. Distribution normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test together with visual inspection of histograms and quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Comparisons between the groups were performed using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis for individual variables, with available data used for each specific analysis. No imputation methods were applied due to the limited extent of missing data and the retrospective design of the study. The extent of missing data for each variable is reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Additionally, univariate comparisons were performed between patients with favorable neurological outcome (survival with CPC 1–2) and the remaining study population to identify factors associated with neurologically favorable survival. Given the limited sample size and number of outcome events, multivariable regression analysis was not performed to avoid model overfitting and unstable estimates.

Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA, 2015).

Results

A total of 58 patients who underwent ECPR in the CathLab were included in the retrospective analysis. In the mechanical CPR group, 22 patients received mechanical chest compressions using the LUCAS device (LUCAS™ 2 Chest Compression System, Stryker Corporation, Kalamazoo, MI, USA), whereas 36 patients underwent manual chest compressions during resuscitation (manual CPR group) [Fig. 2]. In 11 patients, CPR had already been initiated prior to arrival in the catheterization laboratory, and they were excluded from further analysis.

Discussion

In this study, we compared the characteristics of patients who received mechanical versus manual chest compressions prior to ECPR in the catheterization laboratory. The main finding was that the use of mechanical CPR was associated with shorter low-flow duration, while cannulation time showed no statistically significant difference between groups. These findings suggest that the primary benefit of mechanical CPR in the CathLab setting may be procedural rather than directly prognostic.

Consistent with prior meta-analyses, low-flow duration remains one of the strongest predictors of survival following refractory cardiac arrest [11, 12]. In our cohort, the absolute decrease in low-flow time in mechanical CPR group was modest, which may explain why improvements in workflow did not translate into a measurable survival benefit in this relatively small sample.

Another important finding was that, despite the significantly shorter low-flow duration in the mechanical CPR group, cannulation time itself was not significantly different between groups. This observation suggests that the potential advantage of mechanical chest compressions may be related more to optimization of the overall ECPR workflow – including team coordination, procedural logistics, and uninterrupted preparation for extracorporeal support – rather than to facilitation of the cannulation procedure itself. In the present study, cannulation time was defined as the interval from vascular puncture to establishment of extracorporeal blood flow (“needle-to-pump”). It is possible that a broader definition incorporating the entire logistical process, such as decision-to-cannulation-to-pump time, might better capture workflow-related benefits associated with mechanical CPR in the CathLab environment. However, these data were not systematically available in the current retrospective analysis.

Beyond procedural metrics, mechanical chest compression devices may provide important operational and safety advantages during high-intensity resuscitation scenarios in the CathLab. By creating a more stable procedural environment, mechanical CPR may reduce rescuer fatigue, improve workspace organization, facilitate communication and task allocation within the multidisciplinary team, and allow operators to focus on technically demanding procedures such as PCI and fluoroscopy-guided cannulation. These aspects are particularly relevant in the CathLab setting, where manual chest compressions are frequently complicated by restricted workspace, C-arm positioning, prolonged use of lead protection, and radiation exposure [1].

In our institution, manual CPR was often required because of limited availability of mechanical chest compression devices. During the study period, only a single LUCAS device was available, and it was frequently located in the emergency department rather than permanently stationed in the angiography suite. As a result, immediate access to mechanical CPR during cardiac arrest in the CathLab was not always feasible. In addition, manual chest compressions performed during fluoroscopy may compromise compression quality because of limited operator mobility and rescuer fatigue, while simultaneously exposing healthcare personnel – particularly the head, neck, and upper extremities – to increased radiation exposure despite standard protective equipment. Interruptions of either fluoroscopy or chest compressions during vascular access and wire manipulation may further prolong low-flow duration or compromise procedural safety. These workflow-related constraints likely contributed to the longer low-flow and cannulation times observed in the manual CPR group.

Therefore, the potential advantages of mechanical CPR in the CathLab may extend beyond direct patient-centered outcomes and include improved procedural efficiency, workflow stability, team endurance, and radiation safety during prolonged fluoroscopy-guided ECPR procedures. However, these provider- and system-related outcomes were not formally assessed in the present study and should be explored in future prospective investigations.

Despite these procedural advantages, no significant differences in survival or neurological outcomes were observed between the groups. Large prehospital randomized trials and meta-analyses also failed to show a survival benefit for mechanical compared with manual chest compressions [6–8]. In a systematic review and meta-analysis including over 111,000 patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, Larik et al. found no significant differences between mechanical and manual chest compressions in terms of ROSC, survival to hospital discharge, or short- and long-term survival. However, manual CPR was associated with a significantly higher likelihood of favorable neurological outcomes (OR 1.41; 95% CI 1.07–1.84; p = 0.01) [8].

More recently, in a large population-based study of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest patients undergoing intra-arrest transport, Mok et al. reported no significant association between mechanical and manual chest compressions in terms of favorable neurological outcome at hospital discharge (6.6% vs. 7.1%) [13]. Similarly, rates of ROSC at hospital arrival (18% vs. 24%) and survival to hospital discharge (7.0% vs. 7.8%) did not differ significantly between the mechanical and manual CPR groups [13].

In contrast, a recent emergency department–based comparative study, Vafaei et al. reported higher rates of ROSC in the mechanical CPR group (41.7% vs. 27.8%) as well as improved early (6-hour) survival (29.2% vs. 15.7%) compared with manual CPR. However, this early advantage did not persist at 24 h, with no significant difference in survival between the groups (20.8% vs. 14.8%; p > 0.05) [14]. However, this study was non-randomized and sequential in design, with potential risk of residual confounding which limited the causal interpretation [14]. This pattern helps contextualize our findings: while mechanical CPR may enhance short-term workflow or early physiological parameters in selected settings, long-term outcomes remain largely determined by the overall ischemic burden, the quality of post-resuscitation care, and the timeliness of treating the underlying reversible cause.

Chest compressions – whether manual or mechanical – are inherently traumatic and often lead to clinically significant injuries. The higher incidence of CPR-related injuries observed with use of the LUCAS device likely reflects a combination of factors, including the delivery of consistent force at a fixed anatomical location and its more frequent use during prolonged resuscitation efforts. In addition, inappropriate application of the device itself has been described as a potential direct source of harm [15]. Gaisendrees et al. reported significantly higher rates of CPR-related injuries in patients receiving mechanical CPR (83% vs. 55%); however, in this study it was accompanied by longer CPR duration [16]. The use of mechanical chest compressions was associated with a higher incidence of traumatic and hemorrhagic complications in ECPR cohorts including rib fractures, pulmonary bleeding, and vascular injuries [16]. CPR-related trauma may become relevant if the patient is receiving ECPR, requiring full systemic anticoagulation.

Autopsy data suggest that overall injury severity and incidence may not differ significantly between mechanical and manual CPR, despite longer resuscitation times in the mechanical CPR [17]. Notably, Karasek J et al. analyses demonstrate that CPR-related trauma is highly prevalent regardless of technique, occurring in up to 85–87% of patients, with rib and sternal fractures representing the most common findings [17]. Taken together, these data indicate that CPR-related injuries are largely driven by the duration and intensity of resuscitation rather than the compression modality itself, although specific injury patterns and their clinical consequences may vary depending on the technique and clinical context. More recent imaging-based analyses performed by Tsuchida T at al. further indicate that while overall complication rates may be comparable, certain injury patterns such as hemothorax may be more frequent with mechanical devices, and complication risk is more strongly associated with CPR duration, age, and patient-related factors rather than device use per se [18].

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective, single-center design and relatively small sample size limit statistical power and restrict generalizability, particularly for survival and neurological outcomes. The non-randomized allocation to mechanical versus manual CPR introduces potential selection bias and confounding by indication, as mechanical CPR was likely preferentially used in more complex or prolonged resuscitation scenarios. Temporal bias cannot be excluded, given the increasing adoption of mechanical CPR over time alongside improvements in team experience and ECPR workflows. Residual confounding is likely despite multivariable adjustment, as relevant variables, such as CPR quality metrics, completeness of revascularization, and details of post-resuscitation care, were not fully captured. In addition, several potentially relevant procedural and physiological variables were not systematically documented because of the retrospective study design. Detailed data regarding interruptions of chest compressions during device application, fluoroscopy-guided cannulation, or wire manipulation, as well as continuous CPR quality metrics such as invasive arterial pressure or end-tidal CO₂ monitoring, were inconsistently recorded and therefore could not be reliably analyzed. Similarly, dynamic intra-arrest physiological parameters, including serial lactate and oxygenation measurements during CPR, were incompletely documented across the study population.

Conclusions

In patients undergoing ECPR in the CathLab, low-flow time was shorter in the mechanical CPR group, indicating higher procedural efficiency. However, this advantage did not translate into improved survival or neurological outcomes compared with manual chest compressions and shows that ECPR outcomes were determined by the rapid restoration of effective circulation and successful revascularisation, rather than the method of chest compression itself.
