Fetal echocardiography in an Arab population: indications and findings from a tertiary care center
Authors: Ahmad Aljumaa, Sameer Desai, Reem Elmokattaf, Lama Abdulaziz Alabduljabbar, Lina Almohammadi, Waleed Alamoudi, Sara Alnajjar, Aljohara Khalid Alrobeishy, Ismail A Abdullah, Mohammad Aljumaa, Rashed Al Shwabkeh, Mohammed Sulaiman Javed, Roaa Aljumaa, Judy Hejazi, Maisoon Almugbel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the indications and common findings of fetal echocardiography at a tertiary care center in Saudi Arabia, as well as identify factors associated with abnormal findings. We conducted a retrospective analysis of fetal echocardiograms performed over five years at a high-volume referral center. Indications were categorized as maternal factors (e.g., diabetes, family history of CHD) or fetal factors (e.g., abnormal ultrasound, extracardiac anomalies). These factors were also evaluated using null hypothesis significance testing to assess their association with abnormal outcomes. Among 968 referrals, fetal indications (38%),was the most common reason for echocardiography referral, followed by maternal conditions (25%). These indications were also associated with abnormal echocardiography findings (p < 0.001). Of the abnormal findings, the most prevalent CHD subtypes were complex anomalies (24.3%) and ventricular septal defects (16.1%). In this study population, being indicated for fetal anomalies predicted abnormal echocardiography findings, supporting their prioritization in referral guidelines. Maternal factors had a less pronounced value, suggesting opportunities to refine screening criteria. These findings highlight the need for region-specific protocols to improve CHD detection in Arab populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10554-025-03502-x.Keywords: Fetal echocardiogram, Congenital heart disease, Fetal echo, Fetal CHD, Heart defect, Prenatal diagnosis

Introduction

Congenital heart diseases (CHD) refer to a spectrum of birth defects arising from structural cardiac malformations that can affect the normal development and function of the heart [1]. CHDs include heart valve defects, septal defects, stenosis, abnormalities in the heart muscle, and anomalies in the wall of the heart that affect blood circulation and could lead to heart failure and subsequent death [2]. CHDs are the most prevalent congenital anomaly, occurring in about 1% of live births [3]. Most CHDs can be detected prenatally through fetal echocardiography, a technique that has been in use since 1980. Prenatal diagnosis enables families to receive guidance on prognosis, plan appropriate obstetric care, and offer intrauterine treatment for rare, specific cases. In instances of complex cases of CHD, it also facilitates referral of the pregnant woman to a specialized tertiary care center that can provide neonatal diagnosis and management, including therapeutic catheterization and cardiovascular surgery [4]. This approach has been shown to reduce perinatal morbidity and mortality [4].

As such, appropriate referral for fetal echocardiography by trained specialists is essential for the detection of CHD. Several indications have been proposed to guide referral. Maternal factors include a family history of CHD, metabolic disorders (e.g., diabetes), teratogen exposure, prostaglandin synthetase inhibitor use, rubella infection, autoimmune disease, inherited disorders, and conception via in vitro fertilization [5, 6]. Fetal indications include cardiac anomalies or arrhythmias detected on routine prenatal ultrasound, extra-cardiac anomalies, chromosomal abnormalities, and hydrops [5, 6]. These traditional referral indications are primarily derived from North American and European studies, with little to no data available on the appropriate referral criteria for fetal echocardiography in an Arab population. This gap is important to address, as studies suggest that CHD is more prevalent in Arab populations, likely due to higher rates of consanguinity, lifestyle factors, and healthcare disparities [7, 8].

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the primary indications and findings from fetal echocardiograms over a five-year period at a high-volume tertiary care center in Saudi Arabia. A secondary objective was to identify factors associated with abnormal fetal echocardiography findings.

Methods

Study design, population, and setting

This was a retrospective, single-center study conducted at King Faisal Specialist Hospital and Research Centre (KFSHRC), a high-volume tertiary care academic institution in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. All pregnant females who underwent a fetal echocardiogram over a 5-year period between January 2017 and 2022 were reviewed. Fetal echocardiograms at KFSHRC are performed by trained technicians in the Maternal and Fetal Medicine Department with expertise in fetal imaging and interpreted by maternal fetal medicine physicians. The procedures were conducted using standardized echocardiographic techniques as recommended by the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines [9]. This study was approved by the KFSHRC Institutional Review Board (Ethics #2231238) with consent waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Data collection

All data were collected from patient charts and electronic medical records by trained physicians and medical students with prior experience in data collection. Fetal echocardiograms were reviewed for the included patients, with a focus on indications for referral, which included fetal anomalies such as Down syndrome, congenital infections, metabolic diseases or fetal growth restriction, maternal conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, hypothyroidism, cardiovascular disease, genetic and hereditary syndromes, autoimmune disorders, family history of congenital heart disease, a prior child with the disease or any of the parents with the condition. Echocardiogram findings included ventricular septal defects (VSD), hypo-plastic left heart syndrome, atrial septal defects (ASD), syndromic diagnoses, as well as other cardiac anomalies such as Tetralogy of Fallot, transposition of the great arteries, tricuspid atresia or pulmonary stenosis, and secondary conditions including but not limited to omphalocele, fetal hydrops, bradycardia and diaphragmatic hernia. Demographic and other pregnancy characteristics were also collected, including maternal age, mode of conception, pregnancy type, gestational age (early, mid, late), and maternal comorbidities. Each echocardiogram was classified as abnormal or normal based on interpretation by maternal and fetal medicine physicians. In addition, information on parental consanguinity was recorded when available and classified as consanguineous or non-consanguineous.

Data analysis

Patient demographics, other characteristics, echocardiogram indications and findings were described using simple descriptive statistics (medians, proportions, interquartile range [IQR], range). The relationship between echocardiogram indications and abnormal and normal findings were compared using chi-square tests of independence. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 for all comparisons, and all reported P- values were two-tailed. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3 [10].

Results

Characteristics and lifestyle factors of study population

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the study population. A total of 968 pregnant women were referred for and underwent echocardiograms at KFSHRC during the study period. The median maternal age was 32 years (IQR: 28–36), with the majority conceiving spontaneously (92%) and 95% of pregnancies being singleton. Gestational age at the time of the echocardiogram was distributed as follows: 7.2% in early gestation, 65% in mid-gestation, and 28% in late gestation. Half of the women had identifiable comorbidities. The most frequently reported comorbidities were diabetes (16%), hypothyroidism (16%), and autoimmune disorders (14%), as detailed in Table S1. Surgical histories including tonsillectomy and appendectomy, as well as viral infections such as genital warts were also reported. Among the 968 pregnant women included in the study, several lifestyle-related risk factors were notably prevalent. Active smoking was reported in an estimated 2% (n = 19) of the cohort, while passive exposure to smoke affected approximately 40% (n = 387). Low folic acid intake prior to conception and vitamin D deficiency were each estimated to affect 80% of the population (n = 774). Pre-pregnancy obesity (BMI ≥ 30) was present in 40% (n = 387), and physical inactivity was common, impacting 75% (n = 726) of women. Additionally, 70% (n = 678) of participants were estimated to have poor dietary habits, characterized by low fruit and vegetable intake and high saturated fat consumption. High caffeine intake (> 300 mg/day) was present in 15% (n = 145) of the cohort, and exposure to environmental pollutants such as air pollution and dust was assumed to affect all participants (100%, n = 968). These findings highlight the widespread presence of modifiable lifestyle factors in the study population, many of which are known to contribute to adverse pregnancy outcomes, including congenital heart disease. Echocardiography indications

The primary indications for performing echocardiograms were fetal anomalies (38%), maternal conditions (25%), family history of CHD (15%), and abnormal baseline screening ultrasound studies (15%). Other indications included pregnancy-related risks, such as advanced maternal age (> 35), teratogenic exposure and infectious diseases accounted for (6.2%) and miscellaneous reasons (3%), as reported in Table 3.

Maternal indications included various comorbidities, most commonly diabetes, as well asbeing a known carrier of genetic diseases such as sickle cell disease. Positive autoimmune markers, including ANA, and the use of teratogenic drugs like Roaccutane were also noted. Additionally, a history of severe preeclampsia was a significant maternal factor. Fetal anomalies included extracardiac abnormalities such as abdominal wall defects, diaphragmatic hernia, and a single umbilical artery. The anatomy scan revealed findings like LV echogenic focus and echogenic bowels. Chromosomal abnormalities, including Noonan syndrome, were also identified. An abnormal baseline screening ultrasound was performed in the first trimester, revealing findings such as increased nuchal translucency thickness, abnormal ductus venosus flow, and tricuspid valve Doppler abnormalities which acted as useful congenital heart disease markers. Any abnormalities in such baseline screening was an indication for fetal echocardiogram.

Echocardiogram findings

Of the 968 pregnancies referred during the study period, 347 (36%) had abnormal echocardiographic findings. Among these, the most common diagnosis was ventricular septal defect (VSD), observed in 44% of cases. Other identified conditions included hypoplastic left heart syndrome (6.3%), atrial septal defect (ASD) (2.0%), and syndromic conditions associated with cardiac anomalies (2.0%). The remaining 66% of abnormal findings comprised other structural cardiac anomalies, including Tetralogy of Fallot (28%), transposition of the great arteries (17%), pulmonary stenosis (13%), and tricuspid atresia (8%) (Table 4).

Echocardiogram indications and findings

Among the indications for fetal echocardiography, suspected fetal anomalies remained the strongest predictor of abnormal echocardiographic findings (Table 5). Pregnancies referred for suspected fetal anomalies had a significantly higher likelihood of abnormal findings compared with those without such indications (54% vs. 30%; P < 0.001). In contrast, indications related to pregnancy-related risks including teratogenic medication exposure, infections during pregnancy, and advanced material age, family history, baseline screening studies, and other referral indications were not significantly associated with abnormal findings.

Stratified analysis of maternal comorbidities revealed that specific maternal comorbidities, namely diabetes mellitus (P = 0.000) and hypothyroidism (P = 0.045), were significantly associated with abnormal fetal echocardiographic findings. Other maternal conditions, including hypertension, bronchial asthma, and cardiovascular disorders, were not significantly associated with CHD. Consanguinity and congenital heart disease

Among the 968 patients included in the study, 350 (36.2%) were from consanguineous unions and 618 (63.8%) were from non-consanguineous unions. CHD was present in 102 (29.1%) of the consanguineous group and 142 (23.0%) of the non-consanguineous group. Subgroup analyses revealed no statistically significant differences when examining each group individually. Within both the consanguineous and non-consanguineous groups, the rates of CHD and non-CHD were similar (p = 1.0 for both), indicating that the overall significance was due to differences between the two groups rather than within them (Table 6).

Discussion

CHD prevalence has significantly increased over time, now reaching up to 9 per 1,000 live births, globally representing a significant public health concern [11, 12]. A routine obstetric ultrasound including a four chamber view scan of the heart is performed at least once, when an abnormality, such as an abnormal cardiac position, dilatation of the heart, cardiomegaly or fetal arrhythmia is identified, a detailed fetal echocardiography has to be conducted by an expert pediatric cardiologist or fetal medicine physician [13, 14]. Early diagnosis facilitates superior intra-natal and post-natal care for babies with cardiac anomalies, while also allowing families to prepare emotionally and financially for the baby’s arrival [15, 16]. The significant association between maternal diabetes and hypothyroidism with CHD may reflect region-specific lifestyle and metabolic risk patterns, particularly in Gulf countries where sedentary behavior and dietary factors contribute to high rates of these conditions. These findings underscore the importance of addressing modifiable maternal health risks in CHD prevention strategies.

The most frequent reasons for fetal echocardiography in this study were abnormal baseline screening ultrasound results, maternal variables, and fetal abnormalities. The majority of abnormal findings were linked to fetal abnormalities. The most frequent abnormal finding was “other cardiac anomalies,” which included anomalies including tricuspid atresia, pulmonary stenosis, transposition of the great arteries, and Tetralogy of Fallot. VSD came in second.

In our study, a total of 968 pregnant women were referred for fetal echocardiogram. The most common indication for performing a fetal echocardiography was fetal anomalies (38%) found on routine ultrasound. More than half of the cases in a study by Al-Fahham et al. in Egypt were referred for suspected cardiac abnormalities on routine ultrasound; of these, 71.7% had positive fetal echocardiography, 45.4% had an abnormal cardiac configuration, and 6.9% had an abnormal fetal rhythm [17]. Chitra et al. found that abnormal obstetric scans were the reason for referral in 26.8% of their population [18]. On the contrary, a study by Meyer–Wittkopf et al. conducted in Australia demonstrated that the most common indication for fetal echocardiography referral was a previous history of CHD in the parents, siblings, or close relatives of the fetus accounting for 461cases [13].

Our study demonstrated that fetal echocardiography was positive in 36% of referred cases by fetal echocardiography. Other cardiac anomalies, namely, Tetralogy of Fallot, transposition of the great arteries, pulmonary stenosis, and tricuspid atresia, was the most common finding, collectively accounting to 66%. This was followed by VSD coming as the second most common finding accounting in 16.1% of the cases. In most other studies, however, VSD is usually the most common defect detected in fetal echocardiography. For example in a 2019 study conducted in Nepal, the most frequent heart abnormality observed in FE, was solitary VSD in 18 patients, with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy in 16 patients coming in second [19]. In another study conducted in a pediatric outpatient clinic where 144 cases (66.05%) of 218 had CHD, the majority of these cases were diagnosed with isolated VSD in 25% and isolated ASD in 20.13%, followed by PDA in 13 cases (9.02%) [20]. Interestingly though, in the Egyptian study, cardiac abnormalities were detected in 46.5% of the cases. The most common finding was CHD identified in 34.6% followed by fetal arrhythmia in (9.9%). Of the structural CHDs, com-plex heart lesions were found in 57.1% and common atrioventricular canal in 28.6% [17].

Saudi Arabia has one of the highest consanguinity rates globally, estimated at around 56%, which is known to increase the risk of complex congenital heart defects (CHDs) and autosomal recessive genetic conditions, such as transposition of the great arteries and Tetralogy of Fallot. This may help explain why isolated ventricular septal defect (VSD) was less common in our study than other cardiac anomalies, including tricuspid atresia and pulmonary stenosis [21]. In our analysis, CHD was more frequently observed among fetuses of consanguineous parents (29.1%) compared to non-consanguineous parents (23.0%), but this difference did not reach statistical significance. Additionally, subgroup analyses showed no significant differences in CHD distribution within either consanguineous or non-consanguineous groups (p = 1.0 for both). While these findings do not confirm a statistically significant association, they highlight a potentially important trend that warrants further investigation in larger, multicenter studies, particularly in populations with a high prevalence of consanguineous marriages.

Our results highlight the importance of evaluating individual maternal health conditions when assessing fetal risk for congenital heart disease (CHD). Consistent with prior studies, we found that maternal diabetes mellitus and hypothyroidism were significantly associated with CHD on fetal echocardiography [22, 23]. These conditions are known to influence embryonic development through mechanisms such as altered glucose metabolism, oxidative stress, and disrupted thyroid hormone signaling—factors critical during early cardiogenesis. Conversely, hypertension, bronchial asthma, and cardiovascular disorders were not significantly associated with CHD in our cohort, although the latter approached statistical significance. These findings emphasize the need for targeted screening in pregnancies complicated by metabolic and endocrine disorders, and they suggest that not all maternal comorbidities confer equal risk in fetal cardiac development.

In our study, patients indicated for fetal anomalies such as chromosomal abnormalities, congenital infections, or metabolic diseases were associated with having an abnormal echocardiogram findings. Our findings were echoed in a recent Romanian study which explains that CHD are strongly linked to chromosomal abnormalities in the fetus [24]. This is a fundamental aspect of prenatal diagnosis: a thorough fetal echo is recommended if aneuploidy is found (or suspected). An even greater percentage of fetuses with Trisomy 18 or 13 have heart problems, while between 40 and 50% of fetuses with Down syndrome (Trisomy 21) have structural CHD (also known as AV septal defect or VSD) [24]. An American study has explained that more CHD diagnoses resulted from the use of nuchal translucency screening and that the increase in referrals for elevated nuchal translucency helped that center’s CHD detection rates [25]. This is in contrast to what some of the other papers have shown, fetuses with positive cardiac findings were significantly more common in mothers who were referred because of a suspicious obstetric ultrasound or who were not exposed to any perinatal risk factors, whereas fetuses with normal hearts were significantly less common in mothers who were exposed to perinatal risk factors [17]. A number of other studies also highlight that a sizable portion of CHDs happen in “low risk” pregnancies. For example, a single-center research in India discovered that a “significant percentage” of the fetuses with CHD originated from patients who were initially low-risk, accounting for 65% of their fetal echo referrals [26]. According to historical statistics, most CHDs can go unnoticed when fetal echocardiography is restricted to known risk factors. A study found that when depending on risk-based referrals, only around 15% of expected severe CHDs were diagnosed [27].

Since KFSHRC is a referral facility for high-risk pregnancies, our study likely includes more complex cases than other studies conducted in outpatient setting or primary or secondary care settings. The high prevalence of Down syndrome and consanguinity related illnesses in the Kingdom is matched by the 2.75 times greater risk of abnormal fetal echocardiogram findings in fetuses with fetal anomalies [28]. Studies from the United Stated or Australia on the other hand, might concentrate more on low-risk groups that are screened because of nuchal translucency or family history.

Conclusion

Referrals for suspected fetal abnormalities were prioritized in referral recommendations because they were a substantial predictor of abnormal echocardiogram outcomes in this research group. Maternal variables, on the other hand, had little predictive value, suggesting that the existing screening standards should be improved. These results highlight how crucial it is to create region-specific techniques in order to improve the diagnosis of congenital cardiac disease among Arab communities.

Strengths, limitations and future directions

Our work fills a gap in the literature on fetal echocardiography referrals and outcomes in the Arab world, and it is the first of its type. Additionally, the large sample size of this study improves its statistical power.

This study on the other hand, has several limitations. It is a retrospective study conducted at a single institution, limiting its generalizability to other populations. Since the nature of the study is retrospective, we were unable to follow the patients long term to determine whether they survive and if any complication related to the CHD arise in the future. Neonatal follow-up data was not consistently available, and no long-term outcomes were assessed.

Future multi-center, longitudinal studies are required to address these limitations and further validate our findings. Future research should also focus on consanguinity’s role in CHD subtypes and the impact of expanded screening protocols on detection rates. By implementing these changes, we can improve both the accuracy of prenatal CHD diagnosis and the preparedness of families and healthcare teams for postnatal management.
