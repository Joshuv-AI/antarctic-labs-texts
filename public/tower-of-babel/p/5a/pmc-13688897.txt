Metallo-β-lactamase mediated resistance and Class 1 integron in carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales isolates from the clinical specimens of Nepal
Authors: Manisha Shrestha, Suchitra Thapa, Basudha Shrestha, Dev Raj Joshi, Yojana Pokhrel, Jivan Shakya, Reshma Tuladhar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) pose an escalating public health threat due to their limited therapeutic options and their potential for rapid dissemination in healthcare settings. Mobile genetic elements (MGEs), such as integrons, facilitate the acquisition and dissemination of antimicrobial resistance genes, contributing to multidrug resistance. This study aimed to detect metallo-β-lactamase (MBL) encoding genes, blaNDM−1, blaVIM, and blaIMP, as well as class 1 integron gene (intI1) among clinical CRE isolates in a tertiary care hospital in Nepal.MethodsA six-month observational cross-sectional study was conducted in a tertiary care hospital in Nepal. A total of 3,255 clinical specimens were processed to isolate Enterobacterales species and to assess their antibiotic susceptibility by Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion. Carbapenemase and MBL production were phenotypically confirmed by the modified carbapenem inactivation method (mCIM) in combination with the EDTA-modified carbapenem inactivation method (eCIM). MBL encoding genes blaNDM−1, blaVIM, and blaIMP and class 1 integron (intI1) were detected by polymerase chain reaction.ResultsAmong 464 Enterobacterales isolates, 58.6% were multidrug-resistant, and 13.6% were carbapenem-resistant. Phenotypic detection confirmed MBL production in 84.1% of CRE isolates. The blaNDM−1 and blaVIM genes were detected in 57.1% and 31.7% CRE isolates, respectively, while blaIMP was not detected in any of the isolates. Notably, the class 1 integron gene was present in 98.4% of CRE isolates, indicating a high potential for horizontal gene transfer.ConclusionsThe high prevalence of MBL genes and class 1 integrons among CRE isolates highlights a substantial risk of carbapenem resistance spreading in healthcare settings in Nepal. These findings emphasize the urgent need for prompt detection, enhanced infection control and antimicrobial stewardship to curb the spread of highly resistant Enterobacterales.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05439-0.Keywords: Carbapenem resistance, Enterobacterales, Metallo-β-lactamase, Integron, Intl1

Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a major global health threat, with the highest mortality burden in South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa [1]. In 2021, an estimated 1.14 million deaths were attributed to antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections, and by 2050, AMR could result in 8.22 million deaths globally [2]. Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) are among the most concerning multidrug-resistant (MDR) organisms, listed as “critical priority pathogens” by the World Health Organization (WHO) [3]. They are resistant to key last-line antibiotics, including carbapenems and third-generation cephalosporins, and are associated with high morbidity and mortality [4]. Resistance in Enterobacterales is largely driven by carbapenemase production, including metallo- β-lactamases (MBLs) and extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) [5–7]. These enzymes, often plasmid encoded, can also be embedded in mobile genetic elements, facilitating rapid horizontal gene transfer [8, 9].

The discovery of NDM-1 in 2009 marked a turning point in global AMR surveillance [10]. In Nepal, class B carbapenemases, such as NDM, VIM, IMP, and DIM, have been identified in Enterobacterales and Pseudomonas spp. from clinical settings [11]. However, most studies from Nepal focus on individual species or phenotypic detection, with limited molecular data on MBL gene across CRE isolates [12–16].

The resistance to antibiotics in pathogenic bacteria is disseminating quickly, particularly among those belonging to the Enterobacterales, because of the inter-species and intra-species transmission of ARGs mediated via mobile genetic elements like integrons, transposons, and plasmids, which is further aggravated by the extensive and irrational usage of antibiotics [17, 18]. The initial discovery of these elements suggests that they are crucial in the transmission of multidrug-resistant genes [19, 20]. However, now integrons are extensively known for their involvement in the acquisition, exchange and expression of ARGs [21]. Depending on the amino acid sequences in intI integrases, there are five integron classes, all of which are linked with MGEs like transposons, plasmids, and insertion sequences [21]. Class 1, 2 and 3 integrons were first identified in association with MGEs and acquired antibiotic resistance, whereas class 4 and 5 integrons were chromosomal integrons [22–24]. Class 1 integrons are the most prevalent form of integrons present in clinical Enterobacterales isolates [17, 25–27]. The majority of common enteric pathogens, including Salmonella spp., Klebsiella spp., E. coli, Yersinia spp., Shigella spp., as well as other pathogenic species such as Enterobacter spp., Serratia spp., Proteus spp., Citrobacter spp., harbor intI1 [28].

All the integrons consist of three characteristic elements located within the 5’ CS: a gene for integrase (intI) encoding site-specific tyrosine recombinase; an extremely efficient promoter (Pc) inserted in the intI gene, and a recombination site (attI) adjacent to intI [29]. The SOS response induces integrase expression, enabling the efficient integration via attC X attI and excision via attC X attC [30, 31]. Typically, the 3′CS of class 1 integrons contains the additional gene for sulfonamides resistance (sul1) and the gene encoding resistance to quaternary ammonium compounds (qacEΔ1). The 3’CS also contains the open reading frame (ORF). Each distinct ORF has the potential to be constructed as a novel gene cassette, which is critical to understanding the process driving cassette generation [30]. Gene cassettes flanked by conserved 5′ and 3′ regions contain one or more antibiotic resistance genes with the potential for emergence and spread of MDR and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) phenotypes [22]. Due to a naturally vast pool of gene cassettes, integrons certainly possess the unrestricted ability to accumulate and exchange functional gene cassettes, facilitating rapid adjustment to selective pressures and promoting the host’s adaptation and fitness [32, 33].

Despite their global relevance, integrons, particularly class 1, in MBL-mediated carbapenem resistance, have been understudied in Nepal. This study aims to investigate the prevalence of MBL encoding genes (blaNDM−1, blaVIM, and blaIMP) and the class 1 integron gene intI1 among CRE clinical isolates in a tertiary hospital in Nepal. The findings will enhance the understanding of local resistance patterns and inform infection control and antimicrobial stewardship efforts.

Methods

Study site and period

A hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted over six months, starting from November 2021, at Kathmandu Model Hospital and the Central Department of Microbiology, Tribhuvan University, Kathmandu, Nepal. Clinical specimens received at the microbiology laboratory of the hospital were processed for investigation and analysis.

Sample collection

Over the study period, clinical specimens from inpatients and outpatients of all ages and genders were collected and promptly transported to the microbiology laboratory following standard microbiological guidelines [34]. In case of anticipated delay, the specimens were refrigerated at 4 °C. Only properly labelled, aseptically collected, and adequately transported samples in sterile, leak-proof containers were accepted. Duplicate isolates of the same organism from the same patient during a single infection episode were excluded to avoid duplication bias. Specimens include urine, blood, sputum, pus, wound swab, high vaginal swab, peritoneal fluid, pleural fluid, tracheal aspirate, tissue, bile, and others (e.g., endotracheal secretion, throat swab, tracheostomy tube, suction tip).

Sample processing and bacterial identification

Samples were inoculated onto appropriate media and incubated at 37 °C. Urine was inoculated into modified cysteine lactose electrolyte-deficient (CLED) agar using the calibrated loop technique, incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and culture-positive specimens showing significant bacteriuria (a bacterial count of ≥ 105 CFU/ml or more from a fresh ‘clean-catch’ urine specimen) were further processed for bacterial identification [34]. Sputum, pus, wound swab, throat swab, endotracheal secretions and body fluids were inoculated into blood agar (BA), chocolate agar (CA), and MacConkey agar (MA). Moreover, 3-5 ml of blood was inoculated into brain heart infusion (BHI) broth at a 1:10 ratio, incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and then subcultured on MA, BA, and CA. BHI incubation was continued for up to 96 h, with subculturing every 24 h when there was no growth. All the MA plates were incubated aerobically at 37 °C overnight, and BA and CA were incubated overnight at 37 °C in a 5–10% CO2 enriched atmosphere [34].

Presumptive Enterobacterales colonies were initially screened based on colony morphology, for example, differential lactose fermentation profiles on MacConkey agar and then Gram staining of colonies to confirm Gram-negative bacilli. Following initial screening, confirmation to the order, genus, and presumptive species level was performed using conventional biochemical tests. Lactose-fermenting and non-fermenting Gram-negative isolates were confirmed as Enterobacterales via positive catalase and negative oxidase reactions. Further phenotypic differentiation was performed using a systematic workflow comprising oxidation–fermentation test, indole production, methyl red/Voges-Proskauer, citrate utilization, urease production, and triple sugar iron agar tests. Final genus and presumptive species identification was made by cross-referencing these biochemical profiles with standard taxonomic keys outlined in Bergey’s Manual of Systematic Bacteriology [35]. Identification was limited to phenotypic species-level assignment; strain-level characterization was not performed.

The primary limitation of this study’s bacterial isolation and identification method is the reliance on conventional cultural, morphological, and biochemical tests. While these classical techniques, aligned with Bergey’s Manual of Systematic Bacteriology, are highly reliable for genus-level identification and presumptive species categorization in resource-constrained laboratory settings, they lack the high-resolution accuracy of automated platforms (such as VITEK 2, or MALDI-TOF MS). Consequently, the potential misidentification of atypical or closely related species could not be entirely ruled out.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby-Bauer Disc Diffusion method on Mueller-Hinton Agar, following CLSI guidelines [36]. The antibiotics tested included: amoxicillin (25 µg), amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (20/10µg), ampicillin/sulbactam (10/10µg), piperacillin/tazobactam (100/10), cefoxitin (30 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), cefepime (30 µg), meropenem (10 µg), ertapenem (10 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), amikacin (30 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), levofloxacin (5 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), cotrimoxazole (25 µg) and doxycycline (30 µg). Enterobacterales isolates resistant to meropenem and/or ertapenem were considered CRE isolates [36]. CRE isolates were preserved in tryptic soy broth containing 20% sterile glycerol at -20 °C until further investigation. Quality control for antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using standard reference strains Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25,923 and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853, in accordance with CLSI guidelines, to ensure the accuracy, reproducibility and reliability of the disc diffusion method.

MDR was defined as resistance to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories, and XDR as non-susceptibility to at least one agent in all but two or fewer antimicrobial categories, i.e. bacterial isolates remain susceptible to only one or two categories [37].

Phenotypic detection of MBL production

Carbapenemase production was confirmed using the modified carbapenem inactivation method (mCIM), and metallo- β-lactamase (MBL) production was identified using EDTA-modified CIM (eCIM), as per CLSI guidelines [36]. Briefly, meropenem discs were incubated in tryptic soy broth with test isolates for 4 h at 37 °C. eCIM was also carried out simultaneously, for which 20 µL of 0.5 M EDTA (pH 8) was added to another set of tubes designated for eCIM. The incubated discs were carefully placed on Mueller-Hinton agar plates that had been swabbed with meropenem-susceptible E. coli (ATCC 25922) indicator strain. After overnight incubation, a zone diameter of 6–15 mm or the presence of pinpoint colonies within a 16–18 mm zone indicated carbapenemase production. An increase of ≥ 5 mm in eCIM compared to mCIM indicated MBL production [36].

Molecular characterization of CRE isolates

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted following the previously described alkali lysis method [38], with slight modifications. CRE isolates were cultured in Luria-Bertani broth at 37 °C for 4 h. Cell pellets, obtained by centrifuging the culture, were suspended in 100 µl of 50 mM NaOH and incubated in a boiling water bath for 1 min. Suspension was then rapidly cooled at 4 °C for 5 min, followed by neutralization with 1 M Tris-HCl and ethanol precipitation. DNA pellets were re-suspended in TE buffer. The DNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific NANODROP ONE).

PCR amplification

The MBL genes (blaNDM−1, blaVIM, blaIMP) and the class 1 integron gene (intI1) were amplified using gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table S1) adapted from the previous studies [39–41]. PCR was performed in a 25 µL reaction containing 12.5 µL 2x master mix (SOLIS BIODYNE, Estonia), 7.5 µL nuclease-free water, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (Macrogen, Korea), and 3 µL of DNA template. Amplification was conducted in a thermal cycler (T100™ Thermo Cycler, Bio-Rad, USA), with conditions listed in Supplementary Table S2.

The PCR amplicons were separated on 1.5% agarose gel with 0.5 µg/ml ethidium bromide in TAE buffer (70 V, 45 min). A 100 bp DNA ladder (GeneDirex, Taiwan) was used as a molecular marker. Gels were visualized using a gel documentation system (Azure Biosystems, USA).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 21.0. The association between variables were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

During the study period, 3,255 clinical specimens were processed for bacterial analysis, and bacterial growth was observed in 20.5% (n = 666) of the specimens. The highest number of bacterial isolates was recovered from urine samples (56.2%), followed by pus (18.2%), wound swabs (8%), sputum (5.6%), and blood (5.4%) (Supplementary Figures S1). Among the total positive cultures, 77.8% (n = 518) were Gram-negative, of which 464 (89.6%) were identified as Enterobacterales. The most common species were E. coli (75.2%) and K. pneumoniae (16.8%), followed by C. freundii (2.6%), K. aerogenes (1.1%), and others (Supplementary Figure S2). The majority of the Enterobacterales were isolated from urine (70.7%), with lower proportions from pus (11.4%), wound swabs (4.7%), blood (4.1%), and sputum (3.4%) (Table 1).

Prevalence of multidrug-resistant and carbapenem-resistant enterobacterales

A high burden of multidrug resistance, i.e. 58.6% (272/464), was observed among Enterobacterales isolates, with varying prevalence across bacterial species. Multidrug resistance was observed in 58.7% (205/349) of E. coli, 62.82% (49/78) of K. pneumoniae, 75% (9/12) of C. freundii, 80% (4/5) of K. aerogenes, 25% (1/4) of E. cloacae complex (ECC), 75% (3/4) of P. mirabilis, and 50% (1/2) of P. vulgaris (Fig. 1).

a Distribution of MDR and non-MDR Enterobacterales isolates. b Pie chart showing overall MDR Enterobacterales composition

Among the total MDR Enterobacterales, 23.1% (63/272) were resistant to carbapenems. All the MDR K. aerogenes were carbapenem-resistant. Likewise, 88.9% (8/9), 53% (26/49) and 12.19% (25/205) of MDR C. freundii, K. pneumoniae, and E. coli were carbapenem-resistant (Fig. 2). These findings indicate a substantial burden of multidrug resistance and carbapenem resistance among Enterobacterales in the study setting, with notable variation between species.

Antibiotic resistance profile of CRE

Among the total CRE isolates, 15.8% (10/63) were MDR, and 49.2% (31/63) were XDR, and the remaining 35% (22/63) were non-susceptible to all the tested antibiotics. Susceptibility of CRE was highest to chloramphenicol (55.6%), followed by gentamicin (42.9%) and amikacin (39.7%). Complete resistance (100%) was observed to amoxicillin and cefotaxime, while high resistance (≥ 93.7%) was noted against other β–lactams, including cefoxitin, ceftazidime, and cefepime. Most CRE isolates were resistant to β–lactam/β–lactamase inhibitor combinations (Fig. 3). Limited susceptibility to commonly used antibiotics and antibiotic combinations leaves very few treatment options for CRE infections.

Resistance profile of CRE isolates

Phenotypic detection of carbapenemase and MBL production among CRE isolates

Phenotypic assays were performed to characterize the enzymatic mechanisms underlying carbapenem resistance among CRE isolates. Among 63 CRE isolates, 93.7% (59/63) were carbapenemase producers based on mCIM, while 84.1% (53/63) were MBL producers based on eCIM. Species-wise analysis showed that MBL production was highest in E. coli (88%, 22/25), followed by K. pneumoniae (84.6%, 22/26), C. freundii (75%, 6/8), and K. aerogenes (75%, 3/4) (Fig. 4).

Distribution of MBL genes among CRE isolates

Molecular detection of MBL genes was performed to identify the genetic determinants contributing to carbapenem resistance. A total of 63 CRE isolates were analyzed for the presence of blaNDM−1, blaVIM and blaIMP, where 69.8% (44/63) of them were positive for at least one of these genes. The most common gene was blaNDM−1 (57.1%) with the highest prevalence among E. coli, i.e. 76% (19/25), followed by K. pneumoniae (50%, 13/26), K. aerogenes (50%, 2/4), and C. freundii (25%, 2/8). blaVIM was detected in 31.7% of CRE, with the highest prevalence among K. pneumoniae (50%, 13/26), followed by C. freundii (50%, 4/8) and E. coli (12%, 3/25), and absent in K. aerogenes. blaIMP was not detected in any CRE isolates (Fig. 5a). These findings suggest that MBL enzymes, particularly NDM-1, are major contributors to carbapenem resistance in the investigated isolates.

Prevalence of class 1 integron (intI1) and association with MBL genes

The intI1 gene was detected in 98.4% (62/63) of CRE isolates. It was present in all carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae, C. freundii, and K. aerogenes isolates, and in 96% of carbapenem-resistant E. coli isolates. Among intI1 positive isolates, blaNDM−1 and blaVIM were found in 56.5% (35) and 29% (18), respectively (Table 2). Fisher’s exact test did not demonstrate a statistically significant association between the presence of intI1 and individual MBL genes (p > 0.05). This likely reflects the high baseline prevalence of intI1 among isolates rather than true independence between variables.

Discussion

The evolution and rapid dissemination of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales represent a major public health threat. This study investigated the prevalence of key MBL-encoding genes (blaNDM−1, blaVIM, and blaIMP) and the class 1 integron (intI1) in CREs isolated from clinical specimens at a tertiary care center in Kathmandu, Nepal.

Among Gram-negative bacteria, Enterobacterales were the most frequently isolated group, exhibiting a high multidrug resistance rate of 58.6%, with 13.6% of isolates resistant to carbapenems. Notably, while E. coli was the most commonly isolated species, carbapenem resistance was more prevalent in K. pneumoniae, which is consistent with its classification as the WHO’s top priority critical pathogen since 2024 [3, 42]. Given the significant burden of K. pneumoniae associated CRE, Nepalese healthcare settings must consider targeted antimicrobial stewardship interventions, including restricted empirical use of carbapenems for suspected K. pneumoniae infections.

A significant proportion of CRE isolates psroduced carbapenemases, with 93.7% confirmed as carbapenemase producers by mCIM and 84.1% identified as MBL producers. This finding is consistent with previous studies in South Asia, in which MBLs, particularly NDM-type enzymes, dominate the landscape of carbapenem resistance [43]. Studies have shown the high mortality rate of infections caused by MBL-producing organisms [44, 45], making their detection in both hospital and community settings a critical public health threat. Thus, their precise identification via molecular diagnostics is crucial to guide therapy and prevent empirical treatment failure.

MBL encoding genes were detected in 69.8% of CRE isolates. Among these, blaNDM−1 was the most prevalent, followed by blaVIM, while blaIMP was not detected. These findings align with prior studies in Nepal and the broader Asian region [43, 46–48]. The burden of blaNDM−1 was highest in E. coli, whereas blaVIM predominated in K. pneumoniae. Originally identified in K. pneumoniae, NDM is one of the most clinically alarming MBLs. It has largely been found in K. pneumoniae and E. coli, with fewer instances also being found among other members of Enterobacterales, such as C. freundii, K. aerogenes, ECC, M. morganii and Providencia spp [49, 50]. Co-existence of both genes was observed in more than one-fourth of MBL-positive isolates, with K. pneumoniae accounting for 66.7% of these. This supports K. pneumoniae’s role as a key reservoir and disseminator of complex resistance determinants, facilitated by its genomic plasticity and ability to acquire MGEs [51]. Prior studies have reported the co-occurrence of blaNDM−1 with other carbapenemases and ESBL genes on plasmids [40, 44, 52], raising concerns about the emergence of pandrug-resistant strains. Only tigecycline, colistin and to a lesser extent fosfomycin are effective treatments for many NDM-1 producers [52]. The presence of multiple MBL genes within the same isolate may indicate a greater capacity for broad-spectrum β-lactam resistance, which may contribute to the emergence of MDR and even the XDR phenotypes. Such co-carriage can further restrict available therapeutic options, making clinical management extremely difficult.

Although blaIMP was not detected in our isolates, a previous study in Nepal reported its presence in 16.06% of carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales [53]. The absence in our study may reflect local epidemiological differences, species-specific distribution, limited antibiotic selection pressure, or even methodological limitations such as primer mismatch or the presence of untested IMP variants. In 18.9% of the phenotypically confirmed MBL-producing CRE, no targeted MBL genes were detected. This may be due to the presence of other MBL genes such as blaSPM, blaGIM, and blaSIM, which were not screened due to resource constraints.

The spread of MBLs is largely driven by their association with MGEs, enabling rapid transmission across bacterial species [54]. We also examined the presence of class 1 integrons (intI1) in CRE. A strikingly high prevalence was observed, significantly exceeding reports from other countries such as Taiwan (79.21%) [55], China (48.4%) [56]. All carbapenem resistant K. pneumoniae, C. freundii, and K. aerogenes isolates, and 96% of E. coli, harbored intI1. These findings suggest that intI1 may have a significant role in the dissemination of resistance genes in Nepalese clinical settings. Although 69.4% of intI1-positive CRE isolates carried at least one MBL gene and 27.9% carried both, the presence of an integron alone cannot confirm the physical location of these genes. Sequencing of integron cassette arrays is needed to determine gene context. One intI1 negative CRE isolate harboured blaNDM−1, demonstrating that resistance genes can exist outside integrons. Generally, acquired VIM and IMP are encoded in gene cassettes of intI1 and sometimes intI3. These integrons are often embedded within mobile genetic elements such as transposons and plasmids, which facilitate horizontal transfer between bacterial species and contribute to the rapid spread of carbapenem resistance in clinical settings. In contrast, blaNDM−1 is not usually organized as a classical integron gene cassette but is mainly associated with plasmids and insertion sequences, which also promote its mobilization and dissemination. However, it may co-occur with integron-bearing regions, allowing multiple resistance determinants to accumulate within the same genetic platforms [9, 21, 57–59]. Hence, class 1 integrons act as important genetic scaffolds for the acquisition and expression of blaVIM and blaIMP, while broader mobile genetic elements contribute to the spread of blaNDM−1, together driving the global dissemination of MBL mediated carbapenem resistance.

Our study included only the CRE isolates, limiting the ability to compare intI1 prevalence between CRE and non-CRE isolates. intI1 was highly prevalent among the isolates, resulting in minimal variability for comparative analysis. Consequently, Fisher’s exact test did not demonstrate a statistically significant association in the distribution of intI1 among blaNDM−1 (p = 1.00) or blaVIM (p = 1.00) harboring and non-harboring isolates. However, this lack of statistical significance should be interpreted in the context of near-universal intI1 carriage rather than a true absence of association between these genetic elements. Overall, the findings suggest that class 1 integrons are highly prevalent among CRE and are commonly found in organisms harboring MBL genes, supporting their potential role in the dissemination of ARGs in the study setting.

Despite offering valuable insights, this study has some limitations: (1) limited scope of screened genes, (2) use of conventional PCR without sequencing to confirm integron-gene association, (3) lack of phenotypic verification of horizontal gene transfer, and (4) absence of clinical outcome correlation. Future studies incorporating whole genome sequencing, expanded resistance gene screening, and clinical data would enhance understanding of MBL-mediated resistance in CRE. Additionally, longitudinal and multicenter surveillance is needed to track trends in resistance across Nepal. Given the scarcity of new antibiotic development, alternative therapeutic strategies must also be explored to address the ongoing AMR crisis.

Conclusions

This study reveals a high prevalence of carbapenem resistance, along with concerning antimicrobial patterns and MBL production among clinical Enterobacterales isolates from a tertiary care hospital in Nepal. Notably, blaNDM−1 and blaVIM were frequently detected, along with a strikingly high prevalence of the intI1 integron, indicating its potential role in the acquisition and dissemination of antimicrobial resistance determinants. These findings highlight the possible contribution of MBL genes and integrons to the burden of CRE in the study setting.

The findings provide local epidemiological evidence that may support antimicrobial stewardship and infection prevention efforts. Further molecular studies are warranted to understand better the mechanisms underlying resistance dissemination and the interactions between integrons and carbapenem resistance genes.
