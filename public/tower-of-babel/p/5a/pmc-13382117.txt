Lactobacillus helveticus GCL1815 induces Th1-skewing T-cell responses via dendritic cell activation
Authors: Yuma Matsumoto, Keisuke Yamane, Keigo Tsuruno, Taisuke Uesugi, Soichi Tanabe, Yasuo Saito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent studies have highlighted how the immunomodulatory effects of probiotics act through dendritic cell (DC) activation. Evaluation of their in vivo efficacy requires understanding their strain-specific functions, the magnitude of their activity, and the differences in active components. Although we previously identified Lactobacillus helveticus GCL1815 as having potent immunostimulatory properties, its ability to induce T cell responses, a critical function of DCs, was unclear. Accordingly, we assessed the DC-activating capacity of GCL1815 and its potential to promote T cell responses. Stimulating human monocyte-derived DCs (MoDCs) with GCL1815 resulted in upregulated expression of activation markers and a marked increase in interleukin-12 (IL-12) p70 production. Furthermore, co-culturing these stimulated MoDCs with allogeneic T cells enhanced the proliferation of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and significantly increased interferon-γ (IFN-γ) secretion. These findings suggest that GCL1815 enhances antigen presentation and co-stimulatory molecule expression in DCs and promotes T helper 1 (Th1)-skewing T cell responses via IL-12 production. In addition, Toll-like receptor (TLR) blockade experiments using THP-1 cells revealed that TLR2 inhibition partially suppressed IL-12p40 production, suggesting that DC activation by GCL1815 partially involves the TLR2 signaling pathway. Collectively, these results show that L. helveticus GCL1815 induces Th1-skewing T cell responses through DC activation, likely mediated by cell wall components recognized via TLR2. Previous clinical trials suggest that continuous intake of GCL1815 may reduce or alleviate common cold-like symptoms. The present findings provide mechanistic insights into the functionality of GCL1815, bridging the innate and adaptive immune responses.

Introduction

We are constantly exposed to pathogens such as viruses and bacteria, and our immune system plays a central role in protecting us from these infections. The immune response consists of two major arms: innate and adaptive immunity. Innate immunity detects invading microbes through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) and quickly responds by producing inflammatory cytokines and interferons (IFNs), thereby limiting early pathogen spread. At the same time, innate immunity initiates and shapes subsequent adaptive immune responses. Dendritic cells (DCs) are particularly important components of innate immunity because they act as professional antigen-presenting cells that bridge innate and adaptive immunity [1].

DCs comprise several subsets with distinct developmental processes and functions, broadly categorized into plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs) and conventional DCs (cDCs). pDCs are characterized by their ability to produce large amounts of type I IFNs, which inhibit viral replication and activate early antiviral defenses [2]. In contrast to pDCs, which have low antigen-presenting capacity, cDCs exhibit a strong antigen-presenting ability and play a key role in activating T cells [3]. The cDC population is further divided into cDC1 and cDC2 subsets. cDC1 cells contribute to antiviral and cellular immunity by cross-presenting antigens to CD8+ T cells [3] and secreting high levels of interleukin-12 (IL-12), which promotes Th1 differentiation and IFN-γ production, both crucial for effective adaptive immune responses [1]. cDC2 cells primarily present antigens to CD4+ T cells and regulate a broader range of helper T cell responses, including Th1, Th2, and Th17 differentiation [3].

DC maturation is generally triggered when PRRs recognize microbial components. Mature DCs upregulate major histocompatibility complex molecules and co-stimulatory factors such as CD80 and CD86 and secrete various cytokines to coordinate downstream immune responses [1]. Probiotics, including lactic acid bacteria (LAB), also modulate immunity by inducing DC maturation [4]. In the gut, these microorganisms interact with innate immune cells and influence multiple aspects of host immune function [5]. Some LAB further affect adaptive immunity by promoting T cell activation through DCs [6,7]. Importantly, their immunomodulatory effects, including the strength of activation and the profile of cytokines induced, vary among species and strains [8], likely reflecting strain-specific differences in cell wall components that engage distinct PRRs [[9], [10], [11]]. Such differences ultimately shape the host's physiological responses to probiotic intake. Therefore, when evaluating the immunomodulatory properties of LAB, it is essential to understand not only the ability of each strain to activate DCs but also the receptors and molecular components involved.

Lactobacillus helveticus GCL1815 has previously been shown inin vitro screening assays to possess strong DC-activating capacity, including the induction of IL-12 production and immunoglobulin A secretion [12]. In clinical trials, healthy adults who consumed 6 × 109 cells of GCL1815 daily for 8 weeks exhibited fewer cumulative days with common cold-like symptoms, a reduction in the number of days with severe symptoms, and an increased salivary secretion rate of secretory immunoglobulin A [13,14]. Moreover, the expression of activation markers on both pDC and cDC1 subsets was enhanced, suggesting that GCL1815 may promote anti-infective defense through the activation of these DC populations [13].

Despite these observations, the extent to which GCL1815-driven DC activation shapes downstream T cell responses remains unclear, and the specific receptors involved in sensing this strain have yet to be identified. To address these points, we first evaluated the DC-activating capacity of GCL1815 using monocyte-derived DCs (MoDCs) and then investigated the impact of GCL1815-stimulated MoDCs on T cell activation in a co-culture system with allogeneic T cells. Although MoDCs differ from tissue-resident DCs in terms of metabolism and ontogeny, they retain key functional properties relevant to this study, including PRR-mediated antigen recognition and the ability to activate T cells via antigen presentation [15]. While they do not fully recapitulate the complexity of in vivo DC subsets, MoDCs are widely used as a tractable in vitro model for dissecting DC-mediated immune responses. Furthermore, using the human monocytic cell line THP-1, we examined the PRRs involved in IL-12 induction by GCL1815. Collectively, these approaches were employed to clarify the DC-activating properties of GCL1815 and its potential contribution to adaptive immune responses.

Materials and methods

Bacterial preparation

LAB were cultured in MRS medium at 37°C for 48 h, collected by centrifugation, and washed twice with endotoxin-free sterile saline. The pellet was resuspended in distilled water, heat-killed at 105°C for 3 min, freeze-dried, and reconstituted in distilled water. A portion of the suspension was centrifuged, the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of 1% SDS, followed by mixing for 10 min. The cells were then washed three times with 1 mL of saline, resuspended in distilled water, and used for subsequent experiments.

Cell culture and differentiation

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs; Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) from healthy donors were used to generate MoDCs. Monocytes were isolated using anti-human CD14 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) and cultured for 6 days in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin–streptomycin, GM-CSF (50 ng/mL; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), and IL-4 (20 ng/mL; PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA). Half of the culture medium was replaced on day 3.

THP-1 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin and differentiated into macrophage-like cells by treatment with 100 nM Phorbol-12-Myristate-13-Acetate (PMA; Nacalai Tesque) for 3 days.

Stimulation with heat-killed lactobacilli and lipopolysaccharide

MoDCs were stimulated with heat-killed L. helveticus GCL1815 or ATCC15009T, the type strain of L. helveticus, at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 for 24 h. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 100 ng/mL; FUJIFILM Wako, Osaka, Japan) derived from E. coli O55 served as a positive control capable of strongly activating MoDCs. For THP-1 cells, GCL1815 was added at a concentration of 50 μg/mL and cultured for 24 h.

TLR inhibition assay

To examine receptor involvement in GCL1815-induced IL-12 production, differentiated THP-1 cells were pre-incubated with anti-TLR2 (clone QA16A01; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) or anti-TLR4 (clone HTA125; BioLegend) antibodies at 5 or 15 μg/mL for 30 min, followed by stimulation with GCL1815 for 24 h.

T cell co-culture assay

MoDCs were stimulated with bacteria or LPS for 24 h, harvested, and co-cultured with allogeneic T cells at a 1:10 ratio (MoDC:T cells) for 5 days. T cell proliferation was assessed using CellTrace™ Violet (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) staining and analyzed with flow cytometry for CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ subsets. IFN-γ concentrations in culture supernatants were quantified using ELISA.

Flow cytometry

Cells were stained in FACS buffer (DPBS with 4% FBS). MoDCs were stained with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Green (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD80 (BV750, clone 2D10; BioLegend), CD86 (BV605, clone IT2.2; BioLegend), and HLA-DR (BV570, clone L243; BioLegend). T cells were stained with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Green, CD3 (BV750, clone SK7; BioLegend), CD4 (PE-Cy5.5, clone S3.5; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and CD8 (PE-Cy7, clone SK1; BioLegend). Samples were acquired on a Sony SA3800 flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software version 10.8.1 (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Ashland, OR, USA). The detailed gating strategies are shown in Supplemental Fig. S1.

Cytokine quantification

The concentrations of IL-12p70 (Human IL-12 p70 ELISA Kit; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), IL-12p40 (AlphaLISA® IL12 p40/IL23 p40 (Human) Detection Kit; Revvity, Walham, MA, USA), and IFN-γ (ELISA MAX™ Deluxe Set Human IFN-γ; BioLegend) in culture supernatants were measured using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Absorbance was read using a microplate reader (Infinite M Plex; Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA or repeated-measures one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey or Dunnett multiple-comparison tests. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphs and statistical analyses were generated using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA).

Results

GCL1815 induces MoDC maturation and robust IL-12 production

To assess the effects of L. helveticus GCL1815 on DCs, MoDCs were stimulated with GCL1815, ATCC15009T, or LPS for 24 h, and the expression of maturation markers was analyzed using flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 1A–C, both LPS and GCL1815 markedly increased the expression of HLA-DR and CD86, two key indicators of antigen presentation and co-stimulation. LPS also enhanced CD80 expression, whereas GCL1815 induced only a modest change. In contrast, stimulation with ATCC15009T did not significantly alter the expression of any markers. We next quantified IL-12 levels in culture supernatants. GCL1815 stimulation markedly increased IL-12p70 production by MoDCs (Fig. 1D). In comparison, ATCC15009T and LPS induced only minimal IL-12p70 secretion, yielding levels substantially lower than those observed with GCL1815. Together, these results demonstrate that GCL1815 uniquely and strongly enhances IL-12p70 production, distinguishing it from the reference strain regarding its Th1-skewing potential.

GCL1815-activated MoDCs strongly promote T cell proliferation and IFN-γ production

To determine whether GCL1815-activated MoDCs enhance T cell stimulatory capacity, we co-cultured MoDCs with allogeneic T cells. As shown in Fig. 2A, co-culture with MoDCs stimulated with GCL1815 or LPS markedly increased the proportion of CellTrace Violetlow CD3+ T cells, indicating significant T cell proliferation. Both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets exhibited enhanced proliferation under these conditions (Fig. 2B and C). Notably, GCL1815-stimulated MoDCs induced particularly robust CD8+ T cell proliferation, with an average 1.6-fold increase relative to the control. We next measured IFN-γ levels in the co-culture supernatants. IFN-γ production was substantially elevated in cultures containing GCL1815-stimulated MoDCs (Fig. 2D). In comparison, ATCC15009T- and LPS-stimulated MoDCs induced only limited IFN-γ secretion, with markedly lower levels than those observed in the GCL1815 condition. Together, these findings suggest that GCL1815 promotes Th1-skewing T-cell activation through MoDC-mediated signaling.

GCL1815-induced IL-12 production is partially mediated through TLR2

Microbial cell wall components often enhance IL-12 production through TLR2 or TLR4 pathways [16,17]. To identify the receptor involved in sensing GCL1815, we performed blockade experiments targeting surface TLR2 and TLR4. THP-1 cells that had been differentiated into macrophage-like cells via PMA treatment were pre-incubated with anti-TLR2 or anti-TLR4 antibodies before stimulation with GCL1815, and IL-12p40 levels were measured after 24 h.

As shown in Fig. 3, TLR2 blockade resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in IL-12p40 production, which fell to approximately 43% and 30% of control levels under 5 μg/mL and 15 μg/mL antibody conditions, respectively. In contrast, TLR4 blockade did not alter IL-12p40 secretion at either antibody concentration. Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the ability of heat-killed L. helveticus GCL1815 to activate MoDCs and enhance downstream T cell responses in an allogeneic co-culture system. Our results demonstrate that GCL1815 upregulates the expression of the co-stimulatory molecule CD86 and the antigen-presenting molecule HLA-DR on MoDCs (Fig. 1). Furthermore, GCL1815-stimulated MoDCs enhanced the proliferation of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and markedly increased IFN-γ secretion in co-cultures (Fig. 2). These findings suggest that GCL1815 enhances DC-mediated T cell activation and promotes Th1-skewing T-cell responses.

Notably, the maturation phenotype of MoDCs induced by GCL1815 was not uniform. While the expression of HLA-DR and CD86 was clearly increased, the change in CD80 expression was not significant. Although CD80 and CD86 are both co-stimulatory molecules, CD86 is generally considered to be expressed earlier and more abundantly, whereas CD80 functions at later stages of antigen-presenting cell activation [18]. Thus, the observed differences may reflect distinct expression kinetics rather than functional discrepancies. In addition, dendritic cells can exhibit qualitatively biased activation states depending on the nature of the stimulus, and it has been reported that the expression levels of surface maturation markers do not always correlate with T cell stimulatory capacity [19]. Taken together, these findings suggest that GCL1815 induces a selective maturation phenotype in MoDCs accompanied by functional activation.

Another notable feature of GCL1815 stimulation was the marked induction of IL-12 (Fig. 1). IL-12 plays essential roles in activating NK cells and CD8+ T cells and in driving the differentiation of naïve CD4+ T cells into IFN-γ–producing Th1 cells [20,21]. Consistent with these functions, co-culture with GCL1815-stimulated MoDCs resulted in enhanced proliferation of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and a marked increase in IFN-γ production. Although overall T cell proliferation was comparable among the GCL1815, ATCC15009T, and LPS stimulation conditions, IFN-γ production was substantially higher in the GCL1815-treated cultures (Fig. 2). These observations suggest that GCL1815 not only increases T cell numbers but also promotes a more cell-mediated immune phenotype through modulation of the cytokine milieu.

IFN-γ production by T cells is known to be driven by IL-12 derived from antigen-presenting cells, and a positive feedback loop between these cytokines has been reported [22,23]. Therefore, the strong IL-12–inducing capacity of GCL1815 likely contributes to the enhanced IFN-γ production observed in this study. In addition, another study has suggested that the efficiency of cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) induction is influenced by the quality and persistence of antigen presentation by APCs [24]. Based on this, it is possible that, in addition to enhanced IL-12 production, qualitative changes in antigen-presenting capacity might also contribute to the strong T cell responses induced by GCL1815-stimulated MoDCs. However, as antigen presentation dynamics were not evaluated in this study, further investigation will be required to address this possibility.

Previous clinical studies have shown that the daily consumption of 6 × 109 cells of GCL1815 for 8 weeks reduces the cumulative number of days with common cold-like symptoms and lowers the proportion of severe symptoms in healthy adults, while also increasing the expression of the activation markers on pDCs and cDC1 [13]. In addition, no adverse events were reported even with a higher intake of 1 × 1011 cells/day for 8 weeks [14]. The present in vitro findings suggest that the immunological effects observed in clinical studies may involve not only innate immune activation but also enhanced adaptive immune responses. However, it should be noted that this study employs a simplified in vitro co-culture model and does not recapitulate the complex immune microenvironment in vivo. In the intestinal immune system, epithelial cells, resident myeloid cells, various lymphocyte populations, and the microbiota interact to maintain local immune homeostasis and regulate systemic inflammation [25]. Thus, the MoDC–T cell interactions observed in this study represent only a limited component of this complex network, and further studies incorporating these factors will be necessary.

As noted above, the strong IL-12–inducing capacity of GCL1815, together with its ability to enhance T cell activation, represents a defining functional characteristic of this strain. These properties are likely attributable to differences in the immunomodulatory components inherent to LAB. It is well known that the cell wall components of LAB are primarily recognized by TLR2 and contribute to IL-12 production [16,26]. In this study, blocking TLR2 with neutralizing antibodies dose-dependently reduced GCL1815-induced IL-12 production, suggesting that TLR2 signaling plays a substantial role in this response. However, IL-12 production was only partially suppressed by TLR2 blockade, indicating that additional receptors or microbial components may also be involved. Further investigation will be required to identify the strain-specific molecular determinants responsible for the strong immunostimulatory properties of GCL1815.

The high immunostimulatory activity of GCL1815 has been reported in previous screening studies [12], in which it exhibited particularly strong dendritic cell activation and IL-12–inducing activity among multiple tested strains. The present findings extend these observations by demonstrating that its DC-activating capacity is associated with enhanced T-cell responses. In this study, we focused on the functional characterization of this pre-identified immunomodulatory strain rather than conducting extensive strain-to-strain comparisons. Therefore, GCL1815 was evaluated in comparison with a reference strain (ATCC15009T) and a positive control (LPS). Further comparative studies with other LAB strains known to induce IL-12, such as Lacticaseibacillus paracasei and Lactobacillus rhamnosus, will be important to better position GCL1815 within the context of probiotic immunomodulation [[27], [28], [29]].

This study has several limitations. First, MoDCs rather than cDCs were used to evaluate T cell activation. As MoDCs are monocyte-derived inflammatory DCs with distinct ontogeny, the findings may not fully reflect the function of cDCs in vivo. Second, THP-1 cells differentiated by PMA stimulation were used to assess receptor involvement. Therefore, the contribution of TLR2 observed in this model may differ from that in MoDCs, and the involvement of other receptors or signaling pathways should be interpreted with caution. Third, the effects of GCL1815 on T cell responses were not evaluated in vivo. In this study, in vivo experiments were not conducted due to the focus on mechanistic analysis and in accordance with our institutional animal welfare policy. As a result, complex interactions such as tissue microenvironment and immune cell trafficking are not captured, which may influence the nature and magnitude of immune responses in vivo.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that GCL1815 activates DCs and promotes T cell activation and Th1-skewing responses in vitro. We further show that GCL1815-induced IL-12 production is mediated partially through TLR2. Future work aimed at elucidating the in vivo effects of GCL1815 on T cell immunity and identifying the strain-specific components responsible for its immunomodulatory activity will deepen our understanding of its mechanisms and support the development of targeted functional applications.
