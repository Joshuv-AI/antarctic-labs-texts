Understanding strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats to organizational-level family engagement in two childhood-onset disability research organizations
Authors: Samantha K Micsinszki, Andrea Cross, Alice Kelen Soper, Donna Thomson, Connie Putterman, Rachel Martens, Dayle McCauley, Jennifer Zwicker, James N Reynolds, Jan Willem Gorter, Michelle Phoenix
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesFamily engagement at a research project-level is well-studied in childhood-onset disability research, yet organizational-level family engagement (e.g., advisory boards, policy review, mentorship) remains less understood. The objective of this organizational research study was therefore to describe (1) how families are currently engaged at two childhood-onset disability research organizations and (2) the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats (SWOT) to organizational-level family engagement.MethodsA qualitative descriptive approach was used, including online surveys, focus groups, and interviews to collect data from organizational leaders, families, researchers, and trainees from two childhood-onset disability research organizations. Structured deductive content analysis using a SWOT matrix was employed to categorize and present the data.ResultsStrengths included each organization’s ability to value and evolve engagement practices and dedicate organizational resources to ensure families felt represented and supported. Simultaneously, limited training, financial support, and formal structures to embed families within the organization were noted as weaknesses. Across both organizations, opportunities to increase infrastructure and training were described. Despite organizational efforts, families are not always seen as part of the academic community, which pose threats to meaningfully embedding families at an organizational level.ConclusionsFindings highlight the value that both organizations place on family engagement, where families have been embedded within organizational processes and structures. Several strengths within the organizations were apparent and were matched with need for greater infrastructure, engagement support, and representation of diverse families. Opportunities described included further training and embedding families across the organization. We anticipate that without broader changes in academia, challenges to organizational engagement of people with lived experience will continue. Organizations such as those presented in this paper can support organizational-level family engagement through continued training, sharing current practices, and developing policies and practices other organizations may adopt.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-026-00932-2.Keywords: Family engagement, Organizational-level engagement, SWOT analysis, Qualitative description, Patient-oriented research, Neurodevelopmental disability, Childhood disability

Background

Family engagement in research (FER) includes “meaningful and active collaboration in governance, priority setting, conducting research and knowledge translation” that occurs between researchers (e.g., graduate students, research coordinators, investigators, clinician-researchers etc.) and families (e.g., youth with experience of disability or a chronic brain-based illness, or their parents, siblings, and/or grandparents etc.) [1, (p5)]. Engagement in research has been well-documented and studied [2–4]. In childhood-onset disability research, family engagement at both the project and program level is philosophically critical as “families should be involved in deciding the research agenda as the research concerns them and those in similar life circumstances” [5, (p769)]. Given this, families should be engaged in all aspects of research, including at various points in the research process, as well as beyond a single project or program of research at an organizational or systems level. However there is limited research to guide these efforts [6–8]. Dworetzky et al. (2023) describe ‘systems-level’ engagement of families where they engage at an organizational level, such as within hospitals or other health care organizations, through activities such as advisory boards, in decision making roles, and policy review [6]. Embedding families at an organizational-level has several benefits such as facilitating learning across the organization, providing opportunities for deeper connection and greater collaboration, and strengthening relationships over time [9].

Organizational infrastructure and a commitment to integrating families in the organization (e.g., governance structure) are needed for meaningful and sustainable engagement [6, 7]. While engagement at the project-level has been well studied and published, formal guidelines in regards to organizational-level engagement have been largely descriptive, and from a single site (see Buchanan et al.; Cady et al.; Vanderhout et al.) [10–12]. Challenges exist in organizations and the broader culture of academia, including a lack of (i) funding when the engagement is not connected to a specific research project, (ii) training, and (iii) organizational infrastructure (e.g., processes and procedures) to support organizational engagement [13, 14]. Some of these challenges are discussed in our training programs, including the Family Engagement in Research (FER) Course [15], a 10-week program that trains researchers and families how to meaningfully work together in research, and the Family Engagement Leadership Academy (Soper et al., under review) [16]. The FER Course is led by researchers and families at CanChild Centre for Childhood-onset Disability Research in partnership with Kids Brain Health Network (KBHN) and McMaster University Continuing Education. As of December 2025, the FER Course had trained over 750 researchers and families, and graduates frequently indicate a desire for advanced training opportunities to support implementation of family engagement at community and organizational levels.

The purpose of this study was to explore how families are engaged at an organizational-level in two childhood-onset disability research organizations. The research questions guiding this study were:

How are families currently engaged at two childhood-onset disability research organizations?

How are families currently engaged at two childhood-onset disability research organizations?

What are the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats to organizational-level family engagement?

What are the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats to organizational-level family engagement?

Methods

Ethics approval

Institutional ethics approval (HiREB#14060) was received from the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board in Hamilton, Ontario, Canada.

Study design

This research was designed using qualitative descriptive methodology [17] drawing from multiple data sources to describe organizational-level engagement of families at two childhood-onset disability organizations. This approach is appropriate to describe examples of organizational-level engagement at a point in time “and then conveying them in a coherent and useful manner” [17, (p336)].

This study was conducted as part of SKM’s postdoctoral fellowship supervised by AC and MP at CanChild Center for Childhood-onset Disability Research at McMaster University. The study team included a team of family partners (DT, CP, RM), a doctoral trainee (AKS), and researchers (DM, JZ, JNR, JWG). Family partners were co-investigators on this study and active decision-makers throughout the research. This included planning the study, supporting recruitment, interpreting findings, and dissemination. Patient and public involvement (PPI) is reported in the GRIPP2 [18] short form checklist (see supplementary file).

Recognizing the importance of reflexivity in qualitative research, our team acknowledges that all members of the study team were affiliated in some way with the organizations that took part in this research. This meant that study participants were often well known to the study team, and we acknowledge that this might have created bias when providing critical feedback. To mitigate this, responses were confidential and only the first and senior authors were aware of participant identity. Additionally, the study team’s connections to both organizations may have unintentionally influenced the design of the study and participant selection, as well as the interpretation of the results. For example, once initial results were written, we met with organizational leaders to discuss the themes and ensure accuracy. This was done to promote trustworthiness, particularly confirmability to reduce researcher bias [19].

Descriptions of the organizations

Two organizations with distinct governance structures, missions, mandates, and deliverables were selected to elucidate the various ways that families are engaged in childhood-onset disability research organizations. While both organizations have their own leadership teams, researchers and trainees often engage with both organizations, and many families also contribute to projects and engagement efforts in both organizations. Participants were asked to respond to questions based on their primary affiliation.

Organization A

CanChild Centre for Childhood-onset Disability Research is an academic research centre established in 1989 and situated within McMaster University in Ontario, Canada [20]. CanChild’s mission is to transform the lives of children with disabilities and their families. As a hub of international researchers, families, educators, and health care providers, CanChild facilitates and supports the conduct and dissemination of innovative clinical, educational, and health services research [21]. CanChild is involved in developing partnerships, providing education and awareness, fostering mentorship, translating knowledge, and building capacity [15, 22–25]. CanChild has a strong commitment to training in family engagement in research and leads the Family Engagement in Research Program [15, 26, 27].

Organization B

Kids Brain Health Network (KBHN) is a leading national research organization in Canada with a commitment to ensuring that children with neurodevelopmental disabilities and their families live their best lives [28]. As a network of researchers, families, service providers, and policy makers, this organization plays a major role in funding research projects across academic institutions and supporting the implementation of research outcomes into practice through implementation science. At the time of this study, KBHN was funded by the Government of Canada through its Networks of Centres of Excellence (2009 to 2024), allowing the organization to not rely on study-by-study grant funding which has supported ongoing relationships with families and sustainability [29]. As a national organization, KBHN has a broad focus on training the next generation of scientists in the field of neurodevelopmental disability, and specifically offers and funds capacity development resources and support for research trainees, and families. Like CanChild, KBHN has a strong commitment to training in family engagement and is one of the primary funders and founding partners of the Family Engagement in Research Program [15].

Participant recruitment & Sample

Participants were recruited between November 2021 and February 2022 and included: (1) organizational administrators and leaders (e.g., scientist on the organization’s research advisory panel; a family member on the board of directors etc.) in paid or volunteer roles who had experience implementing engagement at an organizational level; and (2) all other individuals or groups who contribute to the organization These individuals may not have been directly involved in engagement at the organizational level but offered their experiences of family engagement in research from the individual/project-level within the organization.

We recruited organizational leadership personnel by attending an existing scheduled leadership meeting. A snowball sampling approach was used in the focus group to identify additional participants known by the leadership team as having significant (e.g., multiple, ongoing) experiences of family engagement in research at the organizational level, who were either within or outside of the organizational leadership team. These individuals were then contacted by the study team via email and invited to take part in the study if they met the eligibility criteria. This approach was taken to increase recruitment accuracy and efficiency by identifying potential participants who had multiple and ongoing engagement roles at an organizational level, as there were no consistent publicly available means of identifying people who had this experience. For example, family engagement champions or an organizational team member responsible for planning and overseeing family engagement at the organization were identified. A study invitation via email was sent to these members of the organizations.

Data collection

Focus groups

A 20–30-minute online focus group was conducted with organizational leaders at each organization to understand participants’ perceptions, thoughts, and feelings of implementing family engagement at an organizational level; and understand perceived barriers and facilitators to organizational family engagement. Focus groups were conducted through Zoom Video Conferencing [30]. The audio/video files were recorded and transcribed. Participants who were unable to attend the focus group were offered an individual 30–60-minute interview.

Semi-structured interviews

Online interviews were conducted via Zoom to explore participants’ perceptions and experiences with family engagement at the organizational level, including barriers and facilitators and strategies for improvement. Additional individuals at each organization were identified by participants in the focus groups through snowball sampling. Individuals included additional organizational leaders not in attendance at the focus groups; researchers/scientists; research staff (e.g., research assistants, coordinators, or managers); trainees (e.g., master’s and PhD students/candidates, postdoctoral fellows); and family members (e.g., parent, caregivers, siblings). Interviews were audio/video recorded and transcribed. The interview and focus group scripts were co-created by the study team, which included people with lived experience (PWLE) who had engaged within both organizations. In this way, their lived experience helped to guide the development of the questions used for both the focus groups and semi-structured interviews.

Online survey

An online investigator-developed survey was distributed via McMaster University REDCap [31] to assess participants’ experiences with respect to barriers and facilitators of family engagement at an organizational level. The survey was designed to explore perspectives of the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats to engagement. Multiple members of our team, including PWLE, were involved in the development of the survey questions and considered English language comprehensibility and accessibility. The survey was intended to reach a broader audience of approximately 20–30 participants at each organization to understand perceived barriers and facilitators for those who may not be directly involved in family engagement at the organizational level. It included closed-ended and open-ended questions, such as “What does [organization] do well in terms of family engagement in research?” to understand strengths and “What does [organization] need to do to improve family engagement in research at the organizational level?” to understand organizational opportunities.

The survey data complemented the interview and focus group data and were conducted in parallel. Triangulation by using multiple forms of data collection helped to ensure trustworthiness [19]. Semi-structured interview and focus group questions, and open-ended survey questions are provided in Additional File 1.

Data analysis

Interviews and focus groups were analyzed using a structured deductive content analysis approach [32]. Transcripts were first reviewed by SKM while listening to the audio-recorded interview. A structured data analysis matrix using the SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats) [33, 34] categories to make up the matrix was used to code each transcript. See Table 1 for a description of each SWOT category and example initial codes. This initial coding process occurred in Word by SKM and used different highlighter colours to broadly code SWOT categories. For example, green was used to code strengths and yellow was used to code weaknesses. After all interviews and focus groups were initially coded and checked by a second reviewer MP, two Excel documents were created (one for each organization) to collate highlighted ‘codes’ from all Word documents (interview and focus group transcripts) using a page for each category of the SWOT matrix. Within each category, codes were further analyzed by both SKM and MP to develop sub-categories for each SWOT category. For example, all strengths codes were examined, and similar phrases and patterns were identified, looking for similarities and differences across all ‘strengths’ data. Groups of codes that were similar in each SWOT category were then used to develop sub-categories.

Strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats to family engagement in research

This section describes the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats to family engagement at the organizational level. Sub-categories have been identified within each component. See Fig. 1 for an overview of each category and sub-category.

Overview of strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats to family engagement in research at the organizational level across both organizations

Strengths: Families are valued and represented across the organization

Three key strengths to family engagement at an organizational level were described by participants from both organizations: engagement is valued and continues to evolve; families are represented across the organization; and resources and support exist to guide engagement.

Engagement is valued and continues to evolve

Participants from both organizations described the value that families bring to the organization which contributed positively towards the work of each organization. For example, a family member described how researchers and family partners complement each other: “There is an attitude that we are all learning together and the knowledge/skills that researchers/trainees/family partners are complementary. There is an attitude of cooperation (not competitiveness) within teams”. Similarly, an organizational leader described the value that family stories can bring to the organization: “the parent voice and the family voice is so important. They’re the best ones to tell the story of how some research has made a difference to their lives and the lives of their children”.

Participants described an active and strong culture of engagement at all levels of the organization. A senior researcher indicated: “…it’s in the drinking water…it’s in the air…it is just so much a part of who we are and how we do things.” Another organizational leader described family being at the center of the organization’s mission: “…we put the child and family in the center of everything we do…built on the foundation of collaborative research that embraces, voices the needs of stakeholders, those who are going to use our knowledge…”.

While the organizations differed in their structures, and history with family engagement, participants described a consistent evolution of organizational engagement. For example, CanChild was established over three decades ago and began with engaging families in clinical practice before moving into research and operations. A senior researcher described this evolution as: “you can’t be family centered over here, but not over there”. Families were able to join projects and had opportunities to stay connected and this evolution was a described strength: “…there is an ongoing relationship with our family partners. For example, when projects end, there could be opportunities to stay connected such as through online Facebook groups”. KBHN was established in 2009 and has undergone significant change, which was attributed to the organization’s commitment to listening to families:

we’re evolving, we continue to evolve as an organization, but, but it’s based on our commitment to listen to what families tell us, and then try to build that into our operational structure and decision-making process, so that, so we can say, we’re not we’re not checking a box, we’re truly embedding family members into the business of the organization (organizational leader).

Families are represented across the organization

Participants described the various ways that families were represented at each organization as a strength. Families were often collaborators on grants and co-presented and co-delivered workshops and presentations. One family member described the various ways that families are involved: “CanChild is good at including families at all levels of research and making sure families are at conferences and presentation[s] and giving them the spotlight in e-newsletters, etc.”. At KBHN families were also involved in co-planning the organization’s annual conference and developing a ‘roadmap’ of opportunities of how families can be involved in the organization. At both organizations, families have been involved in different levels of decision making and leadership roles, such as on advisory committees and board of directors or organizational committees.

Sometimes staff at both organizations held dual roles, for example as an employee and as a parent to a child with a disability which was seen as a strength at both organizations. For example, an organizational leader stated “we have staff who have children with neurodevelopmental disabilities. So, you know that, that shapes who we are, as an organization and, and how we operate”. Families are uniquely embedded throughout the organizations, not just via single research projects, as one organizational leader indicated: “we aren’t just doing one off research projects, there’s often a build upon, one project builds upon another, you have researchers who are coming from different projects working together, and then you build that network between the parents”.

Resources and supports exist to guide engagement

Participants at both organizations spoke about a number of resources that support engagement at each organization as strengths. A resource that participants frequently referred to as a strength was the Family Engagement in Research (FER) Course which received initial and ongoing funding from KBHN and is delivered by researchers and families at CanChild. Many families, trainees, and investigators at both organizations have received training from the course. While opportunities to expand formal training are described later (see Opportunities), in terms of strengths, participants also spoke to the informal training and peer to peer mentorship received because of working on projects at the organization, where learning has primarily happened through experience. A research trainee stated: “the research coordinators I’ve worked with, some of them have had extensive experience with partnering with patients and families. And others have, are learning as they go with different projects”.

Both organizations have leaders who strongly believe in family engagement, coordinators that have experience with engaging families, and an engagement specialist to facilitate the connection between families and researchers. Participants described the human resources available to support engagement as well as the space created to connect and support meaningful engagement. A family member indicated that “CanChild connects to community and organizations by providing a platform of engagement that supports all individuals participating with rights-based, family-centred approaches that encourage connection, participation, and enable all aspects of research”. At KBHN, the organization’s policies and guidelines were frequently mentioned as a strength, including the organization’s mandate to include families on grants and formal compensation guidelines. Multiple participants also spoke of the organization’s tailored knowledge translation efforts directed at families, including providing resources on the website and family-centered conferences: “The KBHN annual conference really ensures that it is accessible and relevant to families - financially and in terms of content” (research trainee).

Weaknesses: Challenges with infrastructure, implementation, and representation

Three weaknesses were described: limited training, financial support, and formal structures to embed families; engagement is difficult to do in practice; and a lack of diversity of families engaged.

Limited training, financial support, and formal structures to embed families

Despite having resources and structures in place to support engagement as noted in the strengths, participants spoke about the often co-existing challenges, such as limited infrastructure to train, support, and embed families, that make engagement more difficult at an organizational level. Engagement training is voluntary, and researchers are typically responsible for seeking out their own learning opportunities. This was described by an organizational leader as a gap: “…we encourage all of [the] projects that we fund that their trainees take the [Family Engagement in Research] course. But […] we don’t require them to take the course”. In this way, training was described as both a strength, and a weakness, and opportunities for improvement are outlined later.

At both organizations, participants spoke about the various institutional barriers and lack of infrastructure and awareness of support that made it challenging for families to meaningfully engage within the organization. Financial support was often limited, and a lack of funds and resources made it challenging to compensate families fairly and meaningfully for their time - one family partner stated, “a $5 Tim’s card doesn’t cut it”. Additional infrastructure challenges that were identified across both organizations included a lack of existing roles for family partners at an organizational level, a lack of role clarity for family partners, and limited channels by which to connect interested family partners with roles that may be available.

Engagement is difficult to do in practice

Across both organizations, participants spoke to the real-life challenges of trying to implement family engagement in their work. One organizational leader stated: “they know what it looks like within a research project where things are very tightly controlled. They don’t necessarily know what this is going to look like in a real-world context…”. Despite the best intentions, the execution of meaningful engagement doesn’t always work out in the way that researchers intend it to, where one research staff member indicated: “Like we’ve had best intentions and best laid plans, where we had commitment but then at the end of the day it didn’t work out”.

Efforts to invite, include, and make families feel valued in their work were sometimes seen as tokenistic or an ‘add on’ where meetings seemed formal, used jargon or terms/formats that are unfamiliar to families. Differences in power, and researchers’ beliefs about engaging families were seen to influence these feelings: “some [people] seem to still see FER as unnecessary or annoying. As such, they may not put as much effort into [it] as they should. Lack of time and funding seems to also be an issue” (investigator and family member). At both sites there were indicators on specific projects that suggested family engagement was not valued, for example, when trainees had to advocate for the inclusion of families in roles beyond knowledge translation or when principal investigators don’t attend full meetings where family partners are included.

Participants spoke to the systemic challenges that make meaningful engagement difficult in practice. The two organizations identified different systemic challenges, such as being embedded within a university or as a stand-alone funding organization, that impacted how health research is typically conducted. At both organizations, administrative challenges make engagement difficult to do in practice. One research staff member spoke about the fact that engagement is not well acknowledged in other aspects of the larger university infrastructure. An organizational leader acknowledged the administrative challenges that were tied to research timelines. Time and reporting pressures put demands on researchers and make it difficult to meaningfully engage families: “…organizations like KBHN have policies and guidelines, [they] have ways that things need to be done. And sometimes that isn’t as conducive to the schedules and time demands of families”.

Lack of diversity of families engaged

Participants spoke about a lack of representation of families, and this looked different at each organization. For example, participants spoke to the lack of diversity (gender, race, ethnicity) of those who are engaged and this has been an ongoing challenge at this organization, where they “tend to round up the usual suspects” (senior researcher) who tend to be English-speaking mothers, and often “…people that you might think were more comfortable with these kinds of roles” (senior researcher). An organizational leader further spoke to how they often reach out to people they have previously collaborated with: “we get comfortable with the people that were used to collaborating with….and it’s very easy to reach out to the same people all the time”. In practice, however, the lack of diversity is noticed by families too:

I also see the same ‘patient advisors’ used over and over again over many studies -- they become like expert patient advisors and I’m not sure you are hearing the ‘real stories’ then but are getting people you’ve groomed to behave appropriately how you like to receive them and how you want them to behave (i.e. mostly say yes and be encouraging vs. raise real issues and concerns).

Opportunities: To strengthen how families are engaged throughout the organization

Two overarching opportunities for family engagement at an organizational level were identified: capacity building and training; and embedding families across the organizations.

Capacity building and training

Capacity building through formal training opportunities was described by participants as an important strategy to improve family engagement at an organizational level as training and education activities can help empower families to take on more leadership roles. While there is a formal training program offered in partnership by both organizations (see Strengths), having this opportunity available to trainees and families, or even required, was described as an opportunity. For example, a family member stated: “every one of our trainees should get the opportunity to go through the FER Program…Or some version of it…”. The availability of resources/tools, such as educational toolkits, within the organization was also mentioned to strengthen engagement capacity.

Training opportunities beyond traditional courses that included connecting with and learning from others through processes like apprenticeships and formal mentorship were presented as a novel way for trainees to learn from others, especially directly from parents. A senior researcher offered the example:

They’re going to learn much more from being apprenticed to, attached to a parent than they are to me or you…I say apprenticed to the parents. And sitting listening to the parents sharing ideas, talking with each other, how they’re thinking about things to understand that kind of flavor. I can tell them this, and so, can you and they’ll say oh yes that’s a good idea I should listen to the parents, but they need to actually be there, they need to you know hang out with the parents.

Infrastructure to support capacity building and training were also seen as an opportunity, including having clear pathways to match researchers and family partners to work together, and organizational guidelines to support engaging families, such as compensation guidelines. Additional strategies described by participants to support engagement included building awareness of engagement and increasing knowledge exchange through the organization’s website, recognizing families who are part of research projects, promoting partnership opportunities, and providing opportunities for families to attend and present at conferences.

Embedding families across the organizations

Participants at both organizations spoke of opportunities to include families in all aspects of the work at the organizational level as a model of research moving forward where families are engaged in everything they do: “any study we put in probably should have some consumer involvement, I’m actually seeing that happening more” (senior researcher). To build and sustain this, a research staff stated that family engagement should be “the norm”. While families were often engaged in the organization beyond individual projects (e.g., involved in strategic planning processes), there were opportunities to further “recognize FER as an organizational pillar” (trainee), such as having more families sit on their advisory committees. Moreover, current resources and structures should be protected and maintained in the future - for example the Parents Partnering in Research (PPR) Facebook Group [23] and having a strong knowledge broker within the organization.

A common thread of opportunities to embed families was related to ongoing challenges of engaging diverse families in terms of gender, race, ethnicity, age, socioeconomic status, and ability. While there was an acknowledgement of current efforts to engage people from different backgrounds, there was also a recognition of the opportunity to improve representation to better reflect the multicultural communities served by these organizations. Strategies to improve diversity included gaining a better understanding of how to reach often excluded populations, better representation of families on the organizations’ websites, and dedicated funding and structures to find families. One example of the latter included having a matching service between researchers and family parents to enable networking across projects and the organizations more broadly. Other strategies to support the engagement of families within the organization included the development of a youth advisory group, providing engagement support as a core service to support researchers and projects in navigating family engagement, and harnessing the knowledge and skills of the graduates of the FER Program. Engagement as a formal requirement for funding which was earlier described as a challenge, was also seen as an opportunity to strengthen moving forward:

This will put the pressure on the researchers to form these relationships. To look for those partners who can contribute to the research program. And, or it will single out the investigators who have already decided that engaging families is important for their research. And so those are the ones that will then become more competitive, for the funding we have to offer.

While both organizations are leaders in engagement, collaboration and networking with others who do similar work was described as an opportunity to explore further.

Threats: Across various levels of academia challenge meaningful engagement

Threats to family engagement in research at an organizational level were described at the institutional level, with funders, and within the broader culture of academia.

Institutional

At the institutional level, ethics around engagement was very challenging and this was attributed to the nuances and differences “between a parent or a young person being part of a research team, versus being a research participant” (research staff member). This participant acknowledged that there has been some work done to advance understanding between research project teams and the ethics process but there is still more to do. Moreover, because families often take on multiple roles within the organization, the role overlap can cause threats to the research process regarding when it is unclear which role they are contributing from: “it could be perceived as more of an agenda like there’s, there’s something they really want to put forward and move forward and it, it can help to inform the project, but it can also sometimes make it, some of the conversations uncomfortable” (organizational leader). Other threats stemmed from a lack of human resources within the organization to be able to do more, for example a family partner stated: “Organizations are facing barriers to a connection due to lack of funding, low supports, disconnected or discontinued community engagement, and the volunteers that staff them. Volunteers on a grassroots level are not taught or connected usually to research initiatives, projects or opportunities that may be available”.

One leader noted that the structure of some organizations are not conducive to working with families where ‘flexibility’ is essential: “I think that’s a real tension around like just organizations having to function and like maybe not always being able to kind of be as nimble around like just pivoting and changing direction and like a true co-design principle right”. While there is movement to change the current practices and structures at academic institutions, policies and procedures are slow to change. Moreover, family partners are not always seen as part of the academic community, and face barriers when trying to name family partners as co-investigators on grants, co-authoring publications, or co-presenting at conferences. A research staff member indicated: “some of the biases inherent in “medical” and other institutional systems that don’t value families input or have paternalistic or other values that are in conflict with families being part of research”. This further adds to the challenges experienced with collaborating with funders, journals, publications etc.

Funder

Engagement was challenged by grant timelines, which often do not provide the time needed to engage authentically and meaningfully: “there was always this push that there were timelines that needed to be met with the grants. And you need to produce outcomes at a certain time” (research trainee). This can stem from grant reviewers who are not always understanding that meaningful engagement takes more time and resources to do well. As a funding organization, an organizational leader explained how the organization is often constrained by external structures: “despite an organization maybe wanting to do something, you’ve got to also make sure you continue to be able to function, by doing all the things that are within your incentive structure from the reporting guidelines”.

Broader culture of academia

More broadly, the culture of academia to meet certain criteria can place added challenges on the desire to meaningfully engage with families: “it was a very closed system, there were very few conferences that allowed or enabled family members to participate and understand and discuss the research that was going on. And so that, that lack of communication, I think, has […] done a disservice” (organizational leader). Other academia challenges stem from the pressure on researchers to continually advance on a rigid timeline. While graduate students have certain milestones to complete, these can make meaningful engagement with the community more challenging: “sometimes just decisions have to be made with my supervisor and committee in order for me to proceed with my comprehensive exam” (research trainee). For early career researchers who have time-bound performance metrics that are tied to their advancement within their institution, these conditions pose challenges in developing relationships with families that take time.

Discussion

This paper describes how families are engaged at two childhood-onset disability research organizations. We explored the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats to family engagement at two organizations with different structures, missions, mandates, and deliverables. Our findings highlight the value that both organizations place on family engagement, where families have been embedded within organizational processes and structures in different ways. Engagement is a deeply ingrained core value which continues to evolve across both organizations. As an example, CanChild has recently expanded their membership categories to include PWLE who are actively partnering on organizational research projects. KBHN now requires PWLE to be engaged on all funded research studies. While lack of diversity was described by participants as a weakness where the organizations “tend to round up the usual suspects”, this is also a challenge described in the broader project-level engagement literature. Strategies suggested by Gonzalez et al. [35] could potentially apply to organizational-level engagement efforts to increase diversity, such as “Develop trusting & long-term relationship with community of target participants” and both organizations have current projects underway to understand and increase partnership diversity [35, (p9)]. Participants across both organizations described a history of significant efforts to transform how families are represented and involved in nearly everything they do. However, the difference between project-level and organizational-level engagement was not always clear to participants. This highlights the need for guidelines/best practices for organizational-level family engagement. Given the embeddedness of projects within the organizations studied, it is possible that participants’ positive or negative experiences on a project influenced their perceptions of engagement at the organizational level.

A strong commitment to collaboration is reflective of the ‘culture’ of how research is conducted within a childhood-onset disability research centre described by Pozniak et al. [14], where ‘culture’ of research is rooted in “an organizational infrastructure of training, knowledge mobilization and social networking, underpinned by the values of openness to learning from others, a commitment to relationship building, and a drive to grow and improve” [14, (pp2–3)]. This definition of research culture is in contrast to the culture of academia pervasive in health research which places an implicit expectation on researchers to ‘publish or perish’ [36]. This implicit notion was described in our study as an external threat to engagement at the organizational level. Collaborative engaged research takes time and is not always conducive to the timelines of grants, student projects, and career timelines for promotion. This commitment to collaboration is embodied in the organizations described in this paper [37].

A lack of resources (e.g., financial resources for compensation, formal training to meaningfully engage) continues to be a well-documented barrier to engagement for research projects [3, 5, 15], and our findings suggest that this is also a barrier for childhood-onset disability organizations. Participants from both organizations described resources as both a strength and a weakness. This paradox likely arises because implementation takes time, and resource needs differ by project. For example, guidelines such as those for compensation may not be implemented consistently across projects and may take time to move from policy to practice. Family engagement in research is complex and multi-dimensional, and while resources such as leadership buy-in, organizational values, and a commitment to engagement were evident at these organizations, more tangible resources, such as lack of funding, and systemic infrastructure resources to embed families were described as barriers.

While training like the Family Engagement in Research Course [15] currently exists, there is an opportunity to make training mandatory and embedded within graduate programs. This presented as an opportunity in our study and aligns with recent directions of Canadian patient engagement in research which indicate that “all trainees have the opportunity and are strongly encouraged to learn about patient engagement approaches through supportive training environments that provide them with regular opportunities to interact with and learn from patient partners early in their careers” [38, (p10)]. Increasing knowledge and awareness of family engagement can further support the development of a collaborative culture of research where family voices are valued and engagement is seen as the standard of practice [38]. Learning from others, such as through mentorship and/or apprenticeships, was described as a way to build capacity in family engagement, especially given that formal training opportunities are limited.

Limitations

While the number of family members who are engaged at an organizational level is increasing, there continue to be more families who are engaged as partners on specific projects. Therefore, there was a small sample of family partners to draw from for the interviews and focus groups at both organizations and we had a limited number of family participants. We aimed to resolve this challenge by opening recruitment to increased families in the surveys who had partnered at the project level. Therefore, some of the data refers to project level engagement, which may reflect the only organizational involvement that some family partners had.

We also acknowledge that the survey and interview/focus group questions were investigator-developed. However, PWLE from our team were involved in developing the questions, supporting their comprehensibility and relevance. Moreover, the long-standing relationships and connections among our study team with the organizations included in this case study may have influenced participant perceptions, as well as their willingness to provide critical feedback about the organizations. We acknowledge the position of the study team as ‘insider researchers’, and while beneficial to understand the topic and recruit participants, it created analytic discussions as well as introduced the potential for bias.

Finally, the SWOT framework that was used during data analysis may have unintentionally reduced complex relational dynamics into a narrow framework of four categories. This is highlighted in the paradoxes of categories, for example, where resources were a strength and an opportunity. Future research in this area is recommended to build on these strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats, and explore them across multiple organizations and in different contexts.

Future directions and conclusions

Future directions for both organizations further aligned with several of the directions for patient engagement in research outlined by Chudyk et al. [38]. This included having designated patient partner positions, policies/guidelines for compensation and those that support and encourage engagement, and increasing diversity and recruitment [38]. The field of engagement in health research continues to evolve, and part of this evolution must include the development of organizational engagement practices. In our study, both organizations have made significant strides to intentionally embed families within their organizations but external threats, such as funding constraints and the general culture of academia will continue to pose significant barriers without broader systemic changes. This includes a wider cultural shift in academia to fully recognize and value the contributions of families in research. Overall, we believe there is an opportunity for organizations such as those presented to advance organizational family engagement through continued training, sharing current practices and reflections on organizational engagement, and developing policies and practices in family engagement that other organizations may adopt.
