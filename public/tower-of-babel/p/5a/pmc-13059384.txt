CcMYB24 redirects flavonoid metabolic flux toward flavonols by activating CcFLS and CcCHI to drive leaf variegation in Cymbidium
Authors: Guansong Yang, Chuang Yan, Qinghua Zhang, Yuying Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeaf color polymorphism strongly influences the ornamental and commercial value of orchids, yet the transcriptional mechanisms that partition flavonoid flux between flavonols and anthocyanins in variegated leaves remain poorly resolved, especially in monocots. Based on our previous findings in γ-ray-induced hybrid Cymbidium cultivars, the R2R3-MYB transcription factor CcMYB24 was identified as a candidate regulator. Its transcript levels were selectively enhanced in the yellow leaf sectors and showed positive correlation with flavonol content, suggesting its potential role in the metabolic partitioning of flavonoids.ResultsTo functionally test the regulatory capacity of CcMYB24 in a tractable orchid system, we established an optimized Agrobacterium-mediated protocorm transformation protocol in Dendrobium officinale (transformation efficiency ~ 2.1%) and generated CcMYB24-overexpression lines. In D. officinale, ectopic expression of CcMYB24 resulted in substantial increases in flavonol (~ 2-fold) and total flavonoid contents without significantly altering chlorophyll or carotenoid levels, producing a stable yellow-green foliar phenotype (RHS Green Group 143). Dual-luciferase reporter assays showed that CcMYB24 activates the promoters of CcFLS and CcCHI, while yeast one-hybrid and EMSA assays revealed preferential binding to the MYB core motif 5′-CTACC-3′. UV-B (Ultraviolet-b radiation) irradiation further enhanced this regulatory module by elevating CcMYB24 transcript abundance and amplifying flavonol accumulation.ConclusionsOur study establishes the first monocot model demonstrating that a single MYB transcription factor (CcMYB24) orchestrates spatial leaf variegation through coordinated transcriptional activation and preferential flux partitioning, defining a CcMYB24-CcFLS/CcCHI regulatory module with conserved capacity to bias flavonoid output toward flavonols in orchids. These findings provide new insights into the regulatory basis of flavonoid flux partitioning in planta and offer a molecular tool for precision breeding of patterned orchid cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08457-2.

Introduction

The variegated leaf, defined by the regional coexistence of chlorophyllous and achlorophyllous tissues within a single lamina, is one of the most visually arresting and biologically instructive phenomena in the plant kingdom [1–4]. It is a classic example of the complex trade-offs between plant morphogenesis, physiological adaptation, and ecological strategy [5, 6]. The phenotypic characteristic of variegated leaves is the mosaic distribution of pigmented and non-pigmented areas within a single leaf. This spatially heterogeneous pigment accumulation significantly enhances the plant’s ornamental value and reflects its adaptive strategy for coping with environmental stress [7, 8]. Variegated plants are often regarded as cases where foliage surpasses flowers in ornamental horticulture, thus commanding a market value far exceeding that of ordinary green-leaf varieties [9, 10]. Particularly within Orchidaceae, cultivated varieties exhibiting mosaic leaf patterns hold far greater value than other cultivars, making them both elite germplasm resources and luxury commodities [11]. However, naturally occurring leaf patterns possessing both aesthetic and economic value are extremely rare. Empirical estimates from large-scale mutagenesis screening and population genetic surveys indicate that the spontaneous mutation rate for stably inherited pattern phenotypes is less than 1 × 10⁻⁶ per locus per generation [12]. This rarity limits the natural distribution of variegated plants and poses significant challenges for breeding and mechanistic studies [13]. Moreover, variegation is not caused by a single factor but involves a complex interplay of photosynthetic regulation, pigment metabolism, cell fate determination, and epigenetic modulation [4, 14]. The resulting cellular heterogeneity and metabolic compartmentalization make variegated leaves an ideal model for investigating interactions between plant organelles and the nucleus, as well as metabolic partitioning and tissue patterning.

As the dominant pigments governing foliar variegation, flavonoids branch at a key node in plant metabolism [15–17]. This pigment patterning necessitates precise spatial control at this metabolic branch points, particularly the diversion of dihydroflavonol toward flavonols versus anthocyanins [18]. Two decades have passed since Winkel-Shirley drew the first complete map [19], yet the pathway still impresses by its architectural simplicity: a single phenylpropanoid highway forks, after only a few steps, into two competitive end-product pathways. One exit leads to the largely colorless flavonols that quietly scatter UV light; the other funnels flux into the pigments we see as red and purple anthocyanins [20]. The alternative deployment of flavonol synthase or dihydroflavonol 4-reductase acts as a discrete enzymatic switch that determines whether the cell retains a pale phenotype or proceeds to accumulate anthocyanin pigments [21, 22]. Recent studies on the transcriptomics of variegated grape leaves have shown that plants can exploit the bifurcation of flavonoid biosynthesis with remarkable precision. Specifically, Lu et al. (2023) reported an 8.3-fold enrichment of flavonol synthase (FLS) transcripts in the yellow sectors of variegated grape leaves, while dihydroflavonol 4-reductase (DFR) and anthocyanidin synthase (ANS) exhibited the opposite gradient [12, 23]. Because these compounds absorb strongly in the visible spectrum, flavonoids are universally recognized as the primary pigments that determine floral, fruit and foliar coloration [24]. In Arabidopsis, Schilbert et al. (2024) strengthened this causal link: knocking out F3′H redirected metabolic flux toward FLS, producing stable cream margins on leaves, whereas supplying dihydroquercetin restored purple pigmentation [25]. To date, empirical evidence has been restricted to dicotyledonous model species. Since 2009, 18 R2R3-MYBs regulating flavonoid bifurcation have been validated in eudicots, yet no monocot ortholog has been functionally characterized. Direct investigations of monocot ornamental taxa, particularly Orchidaceae, remain absent. The leaves of orchid plants experience rapid sunfleck-shade cycles in forest gaps, and whether their variegation also depends on spatial flavonoid branches remains unknown. In particular, the regulatory factors that operate at the FLS-DFR node in monocots have not been identified.

Plant MYB transcription factors are classified into four major groups-1R, R2R3, 3R and 4R-based on the number of conserved R/SH3-like DNA-binding repeats in their N-terminal domains. Among these, the R2R3-MYB sub-clade functions as the predominant regulator that enforces transcriptional activation or repression at the metabolic branch points governing secondary metabolism [26]. In dicot model species, several R2R3-MYB transcription factors have been demonstrated to directly bind to the promoters of flavonol synthase (FLS) or dihydroflavonol 4-reductase (DFR), thereby directing metabolic flux. In Arabidopsis thaliana, AtMYB12 specifically recognizes the MYB-binding site (MBS) motif (5′-CTACC-3′) within the AtFLS promoter, elevating its transcription approximately six-fold and concomitantly enhancing flavonol accumulation [27]. By contrast, the grape (Vitis vinifera) R2R3-MYB factor VvMYBF1 concomitantly activates VvFLS transcription while repressing VvDFR, thereby enacting a dual “flavonol-up/anthocyanin-down” regulatory program that generates yellow-mottled sectors on the berry exocarp [28]. Consistent with this, AtMYB12 has been reported to bind the AtFLS promoter and activate its expression, whereas VvMYBF1 interacts with cis-elements in the VvFLS promoter to increase flavonol content in grape [29, 30]. However, among monocotyledonous ornamentals, and particularly within the horticulturally significant orchid family (Orchidaceae), no R2R3-MYB transcription factor has yet been demonstrated to fulfill an equivalent branch-point-switch function, thereby maintaining a critical gap between mechanistic insights derived from model species and the molecular requirements for precision breeding.

To address these key questions, our previous study compared the RNA-seq profiles of yellow and green sectors from γ-ray-induced variegated Cymbidium (C. tracyanum var. ‘Huanghua’ × C. iridioides ‘Yeyi’). CcMYB24 (GenBank: OQ341202) expression was selectively elevated in the yellow leaf sectors and its transcript level exhibited a strong positive correlation with flavonol content per unit area, implicating it as a candidate spatial switch [31]. Before addressing this regulatory network, we recognized that the extremely low transformation efficiency reported for Dendrobium officinale protocorms would limit biological replication; we therefore conducted a four-factor orthogonal screen of phytohormones and natural additives and raised the efficiency to 2.1% (Fig. 1E-G), providing 21 independent CcMYB24 -overexpression lines sufficient for downstream assays. Beyond expression, CcMYB24 carries the structural signature expected of a branch-point regulator: its R2R3 domain shares 71% identity with AtMYB12, retains the intact “ANDV” MBS-interaction motif, and contains a C-terminal CID repressor domain [32]. Phylogenetically, CcMYB24 nests within the flavonol-specific clade that includes VvMYB24 and GbMYB1, whereas anthocyanin-promoting MYBs occupy a distant branch, reinforcing its predicted bias toward flavonol activation [12, 33]. Integrating these sequence, expression and correlation data, we hypothesize that CcMYB24 clusters occupies MBS elements in the CcCHI and CcFLS promoters, selectively up-regulates the flavonol branch and simultaneously restricts the anthocyanin route, thereby engraving yellow sectors with high flavonol but negligible anthocyanin content. Accordingly, this study was designed with four objectives: (i) define the spatiotemporal expression and functional necessity of CcMYB24 in orchid variegation, (ii) dissect its promoter-level control of flavonoid branching via CcCHI/CcFLS activation, (iii) quantify the UV-B-mediated amplification of the ‘light-MYB-flavonoid’ circuit, and (iv) translate the resulting regulatory code into a CcMYB24-driven breeding platform for generating elite variegated cultivars without depleting wild germplasm. We thus interpret our findings as revealing a regulatory module with conserved capacity to bias flavonoid output in orchids, rather than as a complete mechanistic explanation of Cymbidium variegation.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Variegated mutant ‘Yeyi’ (Supplementary Fig. S1A) was isolated from γ-irradiated seedlings of Cymbidium tracyanum ‘Huanghua’ × C. iridioides ‘Yeyi’ and maintained clonally in the axenic collection of Yunnan Agricultural University (Kunming, 25.1 °N, 1 890 m a.s.l.). Stock cultures were kept on ½-strength Murashige and Skoog (½MS) medium (pH 5.8) containing 30 g L⁻¹ sucrose and 2.5 g L⁻¹ Gelrite at 25 ± 1 °C under 100 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹ white LED (16 h / 8 h photoperiod).

Protocorm-like bodies (PLBs) of Dendrobium officinale (Supplementary Fig. S1B) were initiated from surface-sterilised shoot tips of greenhouse-grown plants (0.08 g L⁻¹ ClO2, 8 min) and maintained in darkness on ½MS medium supplemented with 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA, 0.5 mg L⁻¹ 6-BA and 0.5 g L⁻¹ casein hydrolysate; sub-culture interval was 30 d.

Vector construction and plant transformation

The coding sequence (CDS) of CcMYB24 (GenBank OQ341202) was amplified from D. officinale petal cDNA (primer list Supplementary Table S1-3) and inserted between BamHI and EcoRI of pCAMBIA1300-35 S-GFP to generate p35S: CcMYB24-GFP (Supplementary Figure S1). The construct was verified by Sanger sequencing and transferred into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 (pMP90), which carries the disarmed pMP90 Ti plasmid on a C58 chromosomal background and confers rifampicin and gentamicin resistance, was used as the helper strain for plant transformation. The binary vector was maintained in Agrobacteriu with the helper plasmid pSoup, which provides the replication functions required for the stability and mobilization of pGreen-based T-DNA vectors.

To secure uniform transgenic material for downstream assays, a three-stage factorial design was applied to refine the ½MS-based media used after Agrobacterium elimination. In the proliferation stage, a 3 × 3 factorial design with varying levels of 6-benzylaminopurine (6-BA; 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 mg L⁻¹) and 1-naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA; 0.1, 0.2, 0.5 mg L⁻¹) was used (Supplementary Table S4), with all treatments containing fixed organic supplements (50 g L⁻¹ potato homogenate, 50 g L⁻¹ banana homogenate, 0.5 g L⁻¹ activated charcoal, 1 g L⁻¹ Huabao-1). For differentiation, the optimal cytokinin/auxin pair was kept constant while testing elevated levels of 6-BA (1, 3, 5 mg L⁻¹) and NAA (0.1, 0.2, 0.5 mg L⁻¹) in a full factorial layout (Supplementary Table S5). In the rooting stage, 6-BA was omitted and different levels of NAA (0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 mg L⁻¹) were tested with the same organic matrix (Supplementary Table S6). Each experiment was replicated three times, and data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test (α = 0.05)。.

For transformation, 1000 3-day pre-cultured protocorms (1–2 mm) were wounded with a sterile scalpel and immersed for 30 min in Agrobacterium suspension (OD₆₀₀ = 1.0, 100 µM acetosyringone, 28 °C, 220 rpm). After blot-drying on sterile filter paper, explants were co-cultivated on ½MS co-culture medium (1.0 mg L⁻¹ 6-BA, 0.1 mg L⁻¹ NAA, 100 µM acetosyringone, pH 5.8) in darkness for 5 d. Agrobacteria were then removed by three washes with ½MS containing 500 mg L⁻¹ cefotaxime and the protocorms transferred to selection medium (identical to co-culture medium plus 50 mg L⁻¹ hygromycin B and 300 mg L⁻¹ timentin). Resistant PLBs appeared after 4–5 weeks and were proliferated for two additional cycles under the same selective pressure. Putative transgenic lines were screened by PCR with hygromycin-specific primers (Supplementary Table S-2 and S-3) and single-copy insertion was confirmed by Southern blot. Three independent T₁ lines with comparable expression levels were selected for downstream experiments.

Determination of pigment content in overexpression plants

Pigment contents of transgenic and WT (Wild type) leaves were determined using a spectrophotometer. For flavonols, total flavonoids and anthocyanins, fresh leaves were used. Approximately 0.10 g fresh leaf tissue (FW) was finely ground in liquid nitrogen and extracted with 1.0 mL of 70% (v/v) methanol containing 0.1% (v/v) HCl at 4 °C for 24 h in the dark with gentle shaking. The extracts were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were used for measurements.

Flavonol content was determined by scanning the extracts from 300 to 380 nm and recording the absorbance at 360 nm. Quantification was performed using a quercetin standard curve (0–100 µg mL⁻¹), and the results were expressed as mg quercetin equivalents per g fresh weight (mg QE g⁻¹ FW). Total flavonoid content was measured using an aluminium chloride colorimetric assay. Briefly, 100 µL of the extract was mixed with 300 µL of 5% NaNO₂ solution, incubated for 5 min at room temperature, followed by the addition of 300 µL of 10% AlCl₃·6 H₂O solution. After 6 min, 2.0 mL of 1 M NaOH was added, and the volume was adjusted to 5.0 mL with distilled water. The mixture was incubated for 15 min, and the absorbance was read at 410 nm. Total flavonoid content was calculated from a rutin standard curve (0–100 µg mL⁻¹) and expressed as mg rutin equivalents per g fresh weight (mg RE g⁻¹ FW). Anthocyanins were extracted with acidified methanol. About 0.10 g fresh leaf tissue was incubated with 1.0 mL of methanol containing 1% (v/v) HCl at 4 °C overnight in the dark, and then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 530 and 657 nm. Anthocyanin content was calculated as A₅₃₀ – 0.25 × A₆₅₇, converted to cyanidin-3-glucoside equivalents using a standard curve, and expressed as mg cyanidin-3-glucoside equivalents per g fresh weight (mg C3GE g⁻¹ FW). All pigment determinations were performed with at least three independent biological replicates per genotype, each with three technical replicates.

UV-B treatment and pigment analyses

Plants were grown in a controlled growth chamber under white light provided by cool-white fluorescent lamps at a photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) of approximately 150 ± 10 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, with a 16 h light / 8 h dark photoperiod at 25 ± 1 °C and 60–70% relative humidity. For UV-B treatment, plants were exposed to supplemental UV-B radiation at an irradiance of 0.35 ± 0.02 W m⁻² (313 nm, measured with a VLX-313 W radiometer, Vilber, Germany) for 8 h per day over 30 consecutive days, while maintaining the same background white-light conditions as the control. UV-B radiation was supplied by fluorescent UV-B lamps (313 nm) and the irradiance at canopy level was measured and adjusted with a calibrated UV-B radiometer. The control plants were grown under identical white-light conditions without UV-B supplementation.

Pigments were extracted from 100 mg fresh leaf tissue. Chlorophyll and carotenoids were quantified spectrophotometrically; anthocyanins, total flavonoids and flavonols were measured using corresponding kits (SolarBio BC136, BC135, BC134) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbances were recorded on a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek) and calculated as mg g⁻¹ FW using external standards (cyanidin-3-O-glucoside, quercetin).

Gene expression and promoter isolation

Total RNA was extracted with RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (DP441, TIANGEN) and treated with DNase I. First-strand cDNA was synthesised using SuperScript IV (Invitrogen). qRT-PCR (Quantitative real-time PCR) was performed on a QuantStudio 6 Pro system (Applied Biosystems) with PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher). Primer efficiencies (90–105%) were validated by standard curves (R² ≥ 0.99). Relative expression was calculated by the 2-ΔΔCT method with 18 S rRNA as internal control (Supplementary Table S-7).

For promoter cloning, genomic DNA was extracted by the CTAB method. ~1.5 kb upstream sequences of CcMYB24, CcCHI and CcFLS (Supplementary Table S8-10) were obtained by genome walking (Supplementary Table S11-13) (GenomeWalker Universal Kit, Takara) and verified by re-amplification with high-fidelity Q5 polymerase (NEB). The cis-elements were predicted using PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html) and PLACE (https://www.dna.affrc.go.jp/PLACE/action=newplace) databases. Particular attention was paid to the MYB binding sites on the promoters of the CcCHI and CcFLS genes.

Dual-luciferase assay

The CcMYB24 CDS was inserted into pGreenII 62-SK between BamHI and EcoRI (p62S-CcMYB24). The promoters of CcCHI and CcFLS were cloned into pGreenII 0800-LUC (SalI/HindIII) to generate CcCHIpro-LUC and CcFLSpro-LUC, respectively (Supplementary Table S14-15). Constructs were transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101 (pSoup) and strain GV3101 (pMP90).

Agro-infiltration of Nicotiana benthamiana was performed as described by Hellens et al. (2005) with minor modifications. Briefly, overnight cultures were resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl₂, 10 mM MES, 150 µM acetosyringone) to OD₆₀₀ = 0.8, incubated 3 h at room temperature and mixed in a 1:1 ratio (effector: reporter). A 2 mL syringe (no needle) was used to infiltrate the abaxial side of 4-week-old leaves. Three independent biological replicates (three plants, two leaves each) were performed. LUC and REN activities were measured 72 h post-infiltration using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) on a GloMax 20/20 luminometer. Ratios (LUC/REN) were normalised to empty-vector control (set to 1). Statistical significance was assessed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD (α = 0.05).

Yeast One-Hybrid Assay (Y1H)

The transcription factor CcMYB24 was cloned into the pGADT7 vector (AD-CcMYB24) by inserting the CDS sequence of CcMYB24 between the EcoRI (5′) and BamHI (3′) sites. Promoter sequences of the CcCHI and CcFLS genes were cloned into the pABAi vector (BD vector) by inserting the promoter sequences between the KpnI (5′) and XhoI (3′) sites. Primers used for vector construction are listed in Supplementary Table S-16. Y1HGold yeast competent cells were prepared using the Yeastmaker Yeast Transformation System (Clontech, Cat. 630409). Bait strains were selected on SD/−Ura medium supplemented with 350 ng mL⁻¹ aureobasidin A (AbA), which completely inhibited self-activation. Subsequently, AD-CcMYB24 was transformed into bait strains and spotted on SD/−Leu/−Ura + 350 ng mL⁻¹ AbA. Growth was recorded after 3 d at 30 °C. Empty AD vector served as negative control.

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA)

Recombinant CcMYB24 was expressed as N-terminal GST fusion in E. coli BL21(DE3) using pCold-GST vector (Primers: Supplementary Table S-17). Protein was induced with 0.4 mM IPTG at 15 °C for 20 h and purified with Glutathione Sepharose 4B (Cytiva). Purified GST-CcMYB24 was verified by SDS-PAGE and Western blot with anti-GST antibody (1:5 000, Abcam).

Oligonucleotide probes (Supplementary Table S-18-19) were labelled with biotin at the 5’ end. The cis-elements predicted via PLACE (https://www.dna.affrc.go.jp/PLACE). Binding reactions (20 µL) contained 1×binding buffer (100 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 500 mM KCl, 10 mM DTT, 50% glycerol), 0.5 µg poly(dI-dC), 50 ng purified protein and 20 fmol biotin-labelled probe. For competition, 100× unlabelled probe was added. After 30 min incubation at 25 °C, samples were separated on 6% DNA-retardation gels (0.5×TBE) and transferred to positively charged nylon membranes (300 mA, 30 min). Signals were detected using Streptavidin-HRP (1:3 000, Thermo) and ECL Prime (Cytiva).

Data statistics

All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates. Data were tested for normality (Shapiro-Wilk) and homogeneity of variances (Levene). One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test was used for multiple comparisons. Student’s t-test was applied when only two groups were compared. Significance was set at P < 0.05. Analyses were performed with SPSS v26.0 (IBM). Figures were prepared with GraphPad Prism 9. Values are presented as means ± SD.

Results

Generation and molecular validation of transgenic Dendrobium officinale over-expressing CcMYB24

A 1.4-kb CcMYB24 CDS fragment (GenBank OQ341202) was cloned into pCAMBIA1300-35 S-GFP to produce the binary vector p35S:CcMYB24-GFP. A. tumefaciens GV3101 (pMP90) colonies carrying the construct were PCR-positive (Supplementary Fig. S1 C). After infection of 1,000 protocorms, 76 hygromycin-resistant lines survived on selection medium, and 21 of these were PCR-positive for both hptII and CcMYB24, corresponding to an overall transformation efficiency of 2.1% (Supplementary Fig. S1 D). Optimal conditions were 1.0 mg L⁻¹ 6-BA + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA for proliferation (index 5.77) (Supplementary Fig. S1 E1-9), 3.0 mg L⁻¹ 6-BA + 0.2 mg L⁻¹ NAA for differentiation (68.3%) (Supplementary Fig. S1 F1-9), and 0.6 mg L⁻¹ NAA for rooting (4.7 roots/2.3 cm, 100% rooting) (Supplementary Fig. S1 G1-4). All stages included organic supplements, establishing a rapid and uniform micropropagation protocol for transgenic D. officinale.

Determination of pigment content in overexpression plants

Transgenic leaves were classified as RHS 143B (Strong Yellow-Green) versus 141 A (Deep Yellowish-Green) for WT (Fig. 1AB). Total chlorophyll and carotenoids did not differ between genotypes (P > 0.05). In contrast, anthocyanin (+ 73.8%), flavonol (+ 198.7%) and total flavonoids (+ 106.3%) were significantly elevated in OE lines (one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test, P < 0.05; Fig. 1C).

UV-B affects the pigment content and gene expression of transgenic plants

UV-B (0.35 W m⁻², 313 nm, 30 d) elevated the transcript levels of CcMYB24, CHS, FLS, PAL and UFGT by 2.1- to 3.2-fold compared with white-light controls (Fig. 1F; P < 0.01, Tukey’s HSD).

The leaf colour shifted from Green Group 143 (strong Yellow Green B) (Fig. 1D) to Green Group 144 (strong Yellow Green C) (Fig. 1E), accompanied by a 208% surge in anthocyanin, 177.81% in flavonol and 192.64% in total flavonoids (Fig. 1G, P < 0.001). Chlorophyll and carotenoid levels remained unchanged (Fig. 1G, P < 0.001), indicating a specific metabolic flux towards flavonoid branches rather than a general stress response.

Cloning and analysis of the promoter regions of CcMYB24, CcCHI and CcFLS genes

Genome walking yielded 1863 bp (CcMYB24pro) (Fig. 2AB), 1895 bp (CcFLSpro) (Fig. 2CD)and 1988 bp (CcCHIpro) (Fig. 2EF) upstream of the respective ATG codons (Supplementary Fig. S2 A1-4). Validation by end-to-end PCR confirmed all sequences (Supplementary Fig. S2 A1-4, B1-3, C1-4) with > 99.8% identity to genome-walking assemblies. All three promoters contained canonical TATA- and CAAT-boxes plus light-responsive (Box 4, GT1-motif, G-box) and hormone-responsive (ABRE, CGTCA-motif) elements. MYB-core motifs (CAACTG, CCACCTACCA) were present in CcCHIpro (− 1 586 bp) and CcFLSpro (− 1668 bp) (Tables 1, 2 and 3).

Dual-luciferase assay establishes CcMYB24 as a trans-activator of CcCHI and CcFLS

To enable in vivo trans-activation assays, the full-length CcMYB24 CDS was cloned into pGreenII 62-SK between BamHI and EcoRI sites to generate the effector plasmid p62S-CcMYB24 (Fig. 3A). The 1.9-kb CcCHI promoter and 1.8-kb CcFLS promoter were inserted into pGreenII 0800-LUC (SalI/HindIII) to produce CcCHIpro-LUC and CcFLSpro-LUC reporters, respectively (Fig. 3B). Agrobacterium-mediated co-expression in N. benthamiana leaves revealed conspicuous bioluminescence at infiltration sites when CcMYB24 was combined with either promoter, whereas empty-vector combinations produced only background signal (Fig. 3CD). At 72 h post-infiltration, LUC/REN ratios increased 2.51-fold for CcCHIpro and 1.84-fold for CcFLSpro relative to empty-vector controls (n = 6 biological replicates; P < 0.001, two-tailed Student’s t-test) (Fig. 3EF). These data demonstrate that CcMYB24 functions as a trans-activator of both CcCHI and CcFLS promoters in planta.

Yeast one-hybrid confirms direct promoter binding

Promoter fragments were cloned into pAbAi, linearised with BbsI, and integrated into the Y1HGold genome; self-activation was completely suppressed at 300 ng mL⁻¹ AbA (Fig. 3I). Co-transformation of CcMYB24-AD enabled yeast growth on SD/−Leu/−Ura + 300–500 ng mL⁻¹ AbA, with CcFLSpro showing stronger growth than CcCHIpro, implying higher binding affinity. Spot-density quantification (ImageJ) revealed a 1.7-fold higher signal for CcFLSpro at 500 ng mL⁻¹ AbA (n = 3, P < 0.01) (Fig. 3J), corroborating the differential trans-activation observed in planta.

The EMSA analysis determined the binding sites of CcMYB24 with the target genes CcCHI and CcFLS

GST-CcMYB24 was expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) at 15 °C for 20–24 h and purified via Glutathione Sepharose 4B; SDS-PAGE and western blot showed a single band at ~ 72 kDa (Fig. 3H). 46-bp biotin-labelled probes containing the native CTACC motif and mutated GCTCC variant were synthesised for competition assays. GST-CcMYB24-DNA complexes produced a distinct mobility shift; addition of 100× unlabelled competitor abolished the shift (lanes 3–4), whereas mutant probes failed to compete (lanes 5–6) (Fig. 3KL). Densitometry (Image Lab) indicated a > 90% reduction in band intensity upon competitor addition (n = 3, P < 0.001), confirming sequence-specific recognition. Collectively, luciferase, Y1H and EMSA data converge on a model in which CcMYB24 trans-activates CcCHI and CcFLS by directly binding to the CTACC motif.

Proposed model

Based on the integrated luciferase, yeast one-hybrid, EMSA, and flavonoid profiling results, we propose a working model describing how CcMYB24 regulates flavonol-biased leaf variegation in Cymbidium (Fig. 4). CcMYB24 directly binds the CTACC motifs of CcFLS and CcCHI promoters, preferentially activating the flavonol branch. UV-B further amplifies this regulatory module, collectively leading to flavonol-enriched yellow-green variegated leaves.

Discussion

While prior studies have mapped pigment genes in Cymbidium [11, 31], the transcription factor-mediated spatial channeling underlying leaf variegation remained unresolved, particularly in monocots where no MYB factor was known to gatekeep the flavonol-anthocyanin branch point. In this study, transgenic and promoter-activity assays in D. officinale reveal that the Cymbidium R2R3-MYB CcMYB24 can act as a light-amplified switch that directly activates CcFLS and CcCHI, thereby reallocating dihydroflavonol flux toward flavonols (Fig. 1FG) and inducing yellow-green foliar phenotypes (Fig. 1DE). These data establish CcMYB24 as the first functionally characterized monocot MYB regulator of the flavonol-anthocyanin branch point and define a CcMYB24-CcFLS/CcCHI regulatory module with conserved capacity to bias flavonoid output in orchids (Fig. 4). In variegated Cymbidium leaves, CcMYB24 transcript enrichment in yellow sectors, together with flavonol accumulation in these tissues [30], is consistent with a model in which this module contributes to spatial flavonoid partitioning during variegation. However, because our functional experiments were conducted in D. officinale, we cautiously interpret the Cymbidium component of this mechanism as a working model rather than a fully demonstrated variegation mechanism, pending future gain- and loss-of-function analyses directly in Cymbidium.

In this study, we established a CcMYB24-overexpression transformation system in D. officinale that may serve as a genetically manipulable platform for future orchid biotechnology research [34]. By fine-tuning selective pressure (50 mg L⁻¹ hygromycin) and co-cultivation conditions (100 µmol L⁻¹ acetosyringone), we minimized explant browning while ensuring stable T-DNA integration, achieving a transformation efficiency of 2.1% (21 PCR-positive lines from 1,000 infected protocorms). This value exceeds the efficiencies previously reported for recalcitrant monocot species, including orchids [35]. Transgenic D. officinale exhibited strong yellow-green pigmentation (RHS 143B, Fig. 1B) and 2.3-fold higher total flavonoids (Fig. 1C). The phenotypic outcome (Fig. 1A-B) is consistent with earlier observations in Arabidopsis over-expressing the tomato MYB factor LeAN2, which similarly accumulated anthocyanins [31]. Collectively, these data substantiate the pivotal role of CcMYB24 in modulating flavonoid biosynthesis and provide both a mechanistic insight into leaf color determination in Cymbidium spp. and a translational tool for enhancing flavonoid production in medicinal orchids.

Compared with wild-type controls, transgenic D. officinale plants ectopically expressing the Cymbidium R2R3-MYB transcription factor CcMYB24 exhibited a marked shift in foliar pigmentation and accumulated approximately 2.3-fold higher total flavonoids (Fig. 1C), demonstrating that CcMYB24 can function as a positive regulator of flavonoid biosynthesis in this heterologous orchid system [36, 37]. This heterologous evidence supports the notion that the CcMYB24 module has a conserved capacity to enhance flavonol biosynthesis and modify leaf coloration in orchids. This conclusion is corroborated by convergent evidence that ectopic expression of MYB regulators consistently enhances flavonoid flux in divergent plant systems. Arabidopsis lines expressing the tomato LeAN2 allele accumulate anthocyanins to markedly higher levels [38], grape VvMYBF1 transiently transformed in N. benthamiana drives a pronounced rise in foliar flavonols [28], and constitutive activation of the MYB factor MtPAR in Medicago truncatula hairy roots triggers a substantial increase in proanthocyanidin content [39]. These findings further confirm the pivotal role of CcMYB24 in modulating flavonoid biosynthesis, contributing to the understanding of leaf color formation mechanisms in Cymbidium.

Within the flavonoid pathway, flavonols and anthocyanins compete for the common intermediate dihydroflavonol [21, 40]. Our results showed a disproportionate accumulation of flavonols (198.7%, Fig. 1C) compared to anthocyanins (73.8%, Fig. 1C), confirming a metabolic flux diversion towards flavonols rather than anthocyanins (Fig. 1C). This implies that CcMYB24 selectively up-regulates the flavonol-specific branch, thereby reallocating metabolic flux toward flavonol formation. This preferential channeling is consistent with the flavonol enrichment observed in tomato fruit over-expressing SlMYB12 [41], the anthocyanin reduction concomitant with flavonol increase in apple calli expressing MdMYB24-like [42], and the competitive shift from anthocyanin to flavonol production in pepper fruits silenced for MYB24 [43]. This preferential channeling not only clarifies the biochemical basis of leaf-color polymorphism in Cymbidium spp., but also furnishes a mechanistic framework for dissecting the differential regulation of branch-pathway flux in orchid flavonoid metabolism.

Analysis of the promoter of the CcMYB24 gene revealed the presence of light-responsive cis-acting elements. Upon UV-B irradiation, the transcript increased 2.1-fold (Fig. 1G), consistent with UV-responsive elements in CcMYB24 pro (Table 1). Similar observations have been made in other studies, such as the upregulation of GmMYB12 in soybean exposed to UV-B [44], MdMYB10 in apple fruit under high-light/UV-B conditions [45], and VvMYBF1 in grape berries irradiated with UV-B light [28]. These findings collectively underscore the conserved role of light signals, particularly UV-B, in modulating the expression of MYB transcription factors that govern pigment biosynthesis.

Compared with white-light controls, UV-B-irradiated plants exhibited a significant increment in total flavonoids, anthocyanins and flavonols (Fig. 1). This response parallels the UV-B-evoked up-regulation of MYB24 and the concomitant rise in anthocyanin and flavonol content documented in variegated grape berries [12]. At the mechanistic level, UV-B perception is known to trigger the UVR8-COP1-HY5 cascade, which relieves repression of MYB genes and consequently accelerates flavonoid biosynthesis [46]. Parallel evidence from Ginkgo biloba demonstrates that a UV-B-responsive GbHY5-GbMYB1-GbFLS regulatory circuit transduces the light signal into coordinated transcriptional activation of the R2R3-MYB factor GbMYB1 and the downstream flavonol synthase GbFLS, resulting in pronounced flavonol accumulation [33]. Data in Fig. 1G collectively demonstrate that UV-B irradiation enhances flavonoid biosynthesis by upregulating the expression of CcMYB24, thereby altering leaf color. This discovery not only sheds light on the regulatory mechanisms of UV-B irradiation on flavonoid biosynthesis in Cymbidium but also provides a theoretical basis for manipulating pigment synthesis through light signals. This aligns with the UVR8-HY5 cascade model [41], where UV-B enhances but is not essential for CcMYB24’s core regulatory function, evidenced by constitutive flavonol accumulation in OE lines under control light.

The present study demonstrates that the promoters of the orchid CcCHI and CcFLS genes contain MYB transcription factor binding sites (Supplementary Fig. S2 B). Dual-luciferase and yeast one-hybrid assays revealed that CcMYB24 trans-activates both promoters, consistent with the activation of Phalaenopsis PeMYB2 on PeCHS and PeF3H [47] and of strawberry FaMYB24 on FaFLS [48]. Furthermore, EMSA confirmed specific binding to the CTACC motif in CcFLSpro (Fig. 3K-L), with 90% shift reduction upon competition (Fig. 3E), thereby modulating their expression and, consequently, flavonoid biosynthesis, mirroring the interaction between apple MdMYB24-like and the “TACC” box in MdFLS [42]. These findings furnish direct molecular evidence that CcMYB24 governs the expression of CcCHI and CcFLS, thereby reallocating metabolic flux toward flavonol biosynthesis, and establish a mechanistic paradigm for the light-directed manipulation of the flavonoid branch pathway in orchids. The stronger affinity for CcFLSpro (1.7-fold, Fig. 3J) suggests that FLS is the rate-limiting control point, explaining the flavonol-dominated flux observed in Fig. 1F.

Current research indicates that the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway is regulated by a complex network of transcription factors, including the synergistic regulation of MYB, bHLH, and WD40 transcription factors [49, 50]. Given that CcMYB24 regulates the expression of CcCHI and CcFLS, it is plausible that it interacts with other transcription factors to collectively modulate flavonoid biosynthesis. Future research will employ yeast two-hybrid and dual-luciferase assays to explore the interactions between CcMYB24 and other transcription factors and their collaborative roles in flavonoid biosynthesis, thereby refining the regulatory network of flavonoid biosynthesis in Cymbidium.

This study establishes the first monocot model wherein a single MYB transcription factor directly coordinates leaf sectoring through spatial preferential flux partitioning the pivotal role of CcMYB24 as a master transcriptional activator of flavonoid biosynthesis in Cymbidium and elucidates its molecular mechanism-direct activation of CcCHI and CcFLS that reallocates metabolic flux toward flavonol-thereby filling a critical gap in orchid pigment-regulation networks. The resulting, optimized genetic-transformation platform enables precise, targeted modulation of flavonoid content and the generation of novel cultivars with customized leaf coloration, substantially enhancing the ornamental and commercial value of Cymbidium spp. Collectively, our findings not only advance the fundamental understanding of pigment biosynthesis regulation in orchids but also provide an integrative theoretical and technological paradigm for the rational engineering of plant pigmentation.

Conclusion

In this study, we show that the Cymbidium R2R3-MYB transcription factor CcMYB24 possesses the intrinsic capacity to redirect flavonoid metabolic flux toward flavonols. Heterologous overexpression in D. officinale demonstrates that CcMYB24 directly activates CcFLS and CcCHI, leading to elevated flavonol accumulation and a stable yellow-green foliar phenotype, and thereby establishes a conserved regulatory module for biasing flavonoid output in orchids. Transient expression assays in Nicotiana benthamiana, yeast one-hybrid assays, and EMSA experiments collectively verified the specific DNA–protein interaction between CcMYB24 and target promoters. Phenotypic observations coupled with pigment quantification revealed that altered CcMYB24 expression modulates chlorophyll, carotenoid, flavonol, and flavonoid accumulation, resulting in distinct pigmentation patterns between wild-type and transgenic lines. UV-B–induced regulatory responses further demonstrated that CcMYB24 participates in environmental modulation of flavonoid metabolism. Together, these findings establish CcMYB24 as a key regulator that reprograms flavonoid flux and determines leaf coloration by coordinating transcriptional activation of CcFLS and CcCHI. This work provides new mechanistic insights into MYB-mediated metabolic control in orchids and highlights the broader significance of transcription-factor-directed metabolic flux redirection in shaping plant pigmentation traits.
