Successful Treatment of a Benign Recurrent Colorectal Anastomotic Stricture With Creation of a New Colorectal Anastomosis Using Cautery-Enhanced Lumen-Apposing Metal Stent
Authors: Ayushi Jayesh Shah, Sushrut Ingawale, Abinash Subedi, Jeffrey Albright, Hafiz Muzaffar Akbar Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report the first successful use of a cautery-enhanced lumen-apposing metal stent to recanalize a completely occluded colorectal anastomotic stricture in a patient with prior failed surgical revision. Traditional endoscopic approaches were unsuccessful due to the inability to pass a guidewire. Using dual endoscopic visualization and direct needle puncture, a new tract was created with cautery-enhanced lumen-apposing metal stent, restoring luminal continuity. The patient recovered uneventfully, with sustained resolution at 6-month follow-up. This case highlights a novel, minimally invasive option for managing refractory colonic strictures when standard techniques are not feasible.

INTRODUCTION

Benign anastomotic strictures are a common complication following colorectal surgery, with incidence rates up to 30%, depending on surgical technique, ischemia, anastomotic leaks, and predisposing factors such as diabetes or pelvic irradiation.1 First-line endoscopic treatment typically involves endoscopic balloon dilations, sometimes combined with steroid injections.1 In refractory cases, fully covered self-expanding metal stents offer an alternative, but their use is limited by high migration rates.2

Lumen-apposing metal stents (LAMS) have emerged as a promising off-label option for benign gastrointestinal (GI) strictures due to their dumbbell-shaped design, which minimizes migration.3 LAMS has shown comparable effectiveness to endoscopic balloon dilations in managing benign colorectal anastomotic strictures, with the potential advantage of requiring fewer interventions and offering a favorable safety profile.4

However, nearly all published cases describe situations where a guidewire could be passed across the stricture, which is crucial for the safe deployment of a LAMS. To date, there is scant evidence on the feasibility of cautery-enhanced LAMS deployment in cases of complete luminal obliteration where even the guidewire cannot be advanced.

We describe a patient with a postsurgical, colorectal anastomotic stricture that was occlusive, recurrent after a surgical revision of the stricture, in whom luminal patency was restored by creation of a freehand anastomosis using a cautery-enhanced LAMS.

CASE REPORT

A 33-year-old White man with severe diverticular disease underwent extended left hemicolectomy with a transverse colon to rectal anastomosis and diverting loop ileostomy. He developed a symptomatic colorectal anastomotic stricture. Endoscopic dilation and stenting were initially unsuccessful due to the inability to traverse the stricture. A surgical revision was performed with a new hand-sewn colorectal reanastomosis. A month later, he underwent a computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis with oral and rectal contrast which showed that there was no passage of contrast from the rectum to the colon, suggesting complete obstruction of the anastomosis.

A sigmoidoscopy revealed a completely occluded fibrotic stricture that could not be traversed (Figure 1). Guidewires, of varying stiffness and calibers, failed to pass by both antegrade and retrograde approaches with fluoroscopic and endoscopic guidance. After failed attempts to traverse the stricture with a guidewire, a novel approach was undertaken. The patient was placed in a lithotomy position on the fluoroscopy table with an advanced endoscopist and an assisting colorectal surgeon present. The direction of lumen and distance was assessed using 2 scopes from oral and anal side, air insufflation, and indentation of mucosa on both sides under fluoroscopy to assess direction and distance. The lumen on the oral side was accessed using therapeutic single channel gastroscope which was also used for puncturing the stricture with a 19-gauge FNA needle under both endoscopic and fluoroscopic guidance. A wire was passed into the rectum and pulled out of the rectum using a biopsy forceps to create tension for safe LAMS deployment. A 15 mm × 15 mm cautery-enhanced LAMS (AXIOS™, Boston Scientific) was then deployed over the wire, creating a new anastomosis (Figure 2).

Fluoroscopic and endoscopic imaging confirmed stent position and patency. The patient resumed oral intake within 24 hours and was discharged. At 4-week follow-up, endoscopy showed near-complete resolution of the stricture with a luminal diameter increased to 1.5 cm (Figure 3). Three weeks after the ileostomy reversal, the stent was removed (8 weeks from LAMS deployment). At 6-month follow-up after LAMS removal, the patient reported no obstructive symptoms.

DISCUSSION

While LAMS has been used in managing benign colonic strictures, this is the first reported case demonstrating successful recanalization of a completely occluded anastomosis using cautery-enhanced LAMS placement. This could be a feasible option in cases where a wire cannot pass through the stricture, either due to tortuosity or complete occlusion. In addition to fluoroscopy, using 2 endoscopes allowed us to visualize the lumen on both sides of the stricture which is a critical safety mechanism in place to mitigate the risk of mispuncture. In all previous published literature, guidewire access was a prerequisite. The ability to traverse the stricture by creating a new tract through scarred tissue using a fine needle under fluoroscopic and endoscopic guidance offers a minimally invasive alternative to surgery in refractory cases.

Pollack et al studied 35 patients undergoing LAMS placement for benign strictures, 64% of which were anastomotic, and reported high technical and clinical success rates with improved quality-of-life metrics and acceptable safety profile. Three patients had colorectal anastomotic strictures, all with successful outcomes.3 Reddy et al documented the use of LAMS in a short benign colonic anastomotic stricture, noting success without need for multiple dilations, but wire passage was possible.5 A large single-center series by Gökce et al demonstrated feasibility and safety of LAMS in various GI anastomotic strictures but excluded complete occlusions.6

In 2024, Trieu et al reported direct needle puncture for gastroenterostomy creation in 4 patients where a guidewire could not be passed.7 This technique has also been described in completely occluded hepaticojejunostomy strictures, where direct puncture with an injection needle secured access for balloon dilation.8

Our case also highlights the need for careful patient selection and close follow-up. Complications such as bleeding, ulceration, or perforation have been described in the context of prolonged LAMS dwell time as summarized in the systematic review by Giri et al.9 Mekheal et al reported a delayed ileosigmoid perforation after long-term LAMS use in a benign colonic stricture, underscoring the need for early removal protocols.10 Therefore, we followed the patient at intervals of 6-8 weeks for removal of LAMS, as data regarding indwelling time is limited.

This being a single case, generalizability is uncertain. Reproducibility needs to be evaluated in larger cohorts. It requires an experienced therapeutic endoscopist with colorectal surgical backup.

In conclusion, this is the first documented case introducing a new technique using cautery-enhanced LAMS deployment to restore luminal patency in a completely occluded colonic anastomosis. It broadens the therapeutic toolkit for refractory GI strictures and may offer an alternative to repeat surgery in refractory cases for high-risk patients. Prospective studies are needed to validate the safety, cost-effectiveness, and long-term durability of this technique.
