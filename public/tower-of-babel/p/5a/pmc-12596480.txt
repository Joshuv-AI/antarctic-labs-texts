Prospective associations between accelerometer-measured physical activity, sedentary behavior, and healthy longevity: the Women’s Health Accelerometry Collaboration
Authors: Eric T Hyde, Gretchen E Bandoli, Jingjing Zou, Noe C Crespo, Humberto Parada, Kelly R Evenson, Annie Green Howard, Michael J LaMonte, Marcia L Stefanick, Lesley F Tinker, Bernhard Haring, JoAnn E Manson, I-Min Lee, Andrea Z LaCroix, Lewis A Lipsitz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe influence of physical activity (PA) and sedentary behavior (SB) on survival to late age with intact mobility is unclear. This study investigated associations between accelerometer-measured daily PA, SB, and survival to age 90 birthyear with and without intact mobility in the Women’s Health Accelerometry Collaboration (WHAC).MethodsPostmenopausal US women aged 78-89 years without mobility disability were followed for an average of 6.1 years. At age 90 birthyear, participants were categorized as: (1) surviving with intact mobility, (2) surviving with mobility disability, or (3) deceased. Participants wore an accelerometer on the hip for up to 7 days at baseline from 2011 to 2015. Covariate-adjusted multinomial logistic regression models estimated odds ratios (ORs) of PA (light, moderate-to-vigorous [MVPA], total, steps) and SB (sitting time, mean sitting bout duration) with survival outcomes relative to dying.ResultsAmong 2656 women (mean baseline age 83.1 years), 62.8% survived with intact mobility, 22.3% with mobility disability, and 15.0% died. Compared to dying before age 90, the OR (95% confidence intervals [CI]) for every 1-SD increment in accelerometer variables and survival with intact mobility were 1.36 (1.20, 1.54) for light PA, 1.69 (1.47, 1.96) for MVPA, 1.51 (1.33, 1.71) for total PA, 1.75 (1.51, 2.03) for steps, 0.70 (0.61, 0.80) for sitting time, and 0.79 (0.70, 0.89) for sitting bouts. Similar, weaker trends were present for mobility disability.ConclusionsThese findings corroborate the potential role of increasing physical activity in preserving physical functioning as an important element of healthy longevity.Keywords: Sitting, Steps, Light intensity, Aging, Women’s health

Introduction

In the United States, the population of adults 65 years or older will grow from about 15% in 2016 to nearly 25% in 2060, of which more than half will be women.1 As women have longer life expectancies than men,2 the economic, healthcare, and societal burdens experienced by older women due to chronic conditions and other age-related afflictions are substantial.3,4 As such, strategies for the promotion of healthy longevity among older women is a public health priority.5 While definitions of healthy longevity vary, it is often operationalized in cohort studies as survival to a given age, disease status, or functional ability.6 Increasing physical activity (PA) and limiting sedentary behaviors (SB) may effectively reduce risks of premature mortality and development of many chronic conditions, and help preserve physical function.7

Previous studies have consistently reported positive associations between PA and various aspects of healthy longevity in women8–12; however, most evidence has relied on self-reported PA questionnaires. Accelerometry is more accurate than self-reported PA13 and has only recently become available in cohort studies with long enough follow-up time to assess prospective PA and SB associations with health outcomes. In addition, accelerometry can measure light-intensity PA, daily steps, and sitting bout lengths, which are poorly captured using self-report questionnaires.13,14 Accelerometers can produce reliable estimates of these behaviors, providing an opportunity to address limitations in previous research.15

Given the ability of accelerometry to measure time spent in PA and SB accurately, it is important to consider how time spent on one behavior could displace time spent on another and influence subsequent estimated associations with health outcomes. While some studies using isotemporal substitution modeling (ISM)16,17 show that replacing SB with PA could theoretically reduce mortality risk,18 compositional data analysis has become the preferred method for modeling theoretical substitutions in time-constrained movement behavior.19,20 Few studies have examined how displacing sitting time with PA may impact associations with survival to late age with intact mobility using compositional data analysis.10

Using prospective data from the Women’s Health Accelerometry Collaboration (WHAC), we examined associations of accelerometer-measured PA and SB at age 78-89 years with sufficient follow up time to establish whether they survived to age 90 birthyear with intact mobility or mobility disability. In addition, we examined the theoretical impact of substituting sitting time with light PA and MVPA on healthy longevity.

Methods

Study design and participants

The WHAC was created through the harmonization of accelerometry, covariate, and health outcome data from the Women’s Health Study (WHS) and the Women’s Health Initiative Objective Physical Activity and Cardiovascular Health Study (OPACH), described elsewhere.21 From 2011 to 2015, 18 289 WHS women participated in an ancillary study that collected accelerometry data.22 The OPACH study is a prospective study of accelerometry with chronic disease and aging outcomes of 7048 participating women.23 In total, 22 976 WHAC women contributed at least four adherent days of accelerometry wear defined as wearing the device ≥10 h/day while awake, consistent with other protocols.24,25 Exclusion criteria were: born after December 31, 1932 (thus, ineligible to reach age 90 when outcomes were adjudicated on December 31, 2022); ≥89 years at accelerometry baseline; died within one year post accelerometry measurement; lost-to-follow-up prior to their age 90 birthyear; having unusable accelerometer data; having mobility disability or missing physical function data at baseline; or having missing covariate data. The resulting final analytic sample was 2656 women (Figure S1, see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). Compared to excluded eligible women, those in the analytic sample had a higher proportion college graduates, lower BMI, lower prevalence of chronic disease, spent more time in PA and less time sitting, and took more steps/day (Table S1). All women provided written informed consent and Institutional Review Boards at the Brigham and Women’s Hospital and Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center approved these studies.

Exposure assessment

Women in both WHS and OPACH wore the ActiGraph GT3X+ triaxial accelerometer (ActiGraph LLC, Pensacola, FL) secured with a belt over the right hip for up to seven consecutive days in 2011-2015.21–23 In WHS, women removed the device only during sleep or when in water,22 whereas in OPACH, women wore the accelerometer during sleep but not when in water.23 Subsequently, for all analyses, time spent sleeping was removed based on times in and out of bed recorded in daily diaries. Accelerometers recorded three-dimensional raw acceleration signals at 30 Hz, aggregated using ActiLife software (V.6) to counts per 15s epochs with the normal filter setting. Accelerometer nonwear time was removed using the validated Choi algorithm.26,27 Besides daily steps, all PA metrics were derived from the raw acceleration signals based on the Accelerometer Activity Index (AAI).28 Average AAI/15s summarized average intensity/day. Using OPACH calibration study-derived accelerometry cutpoints,29 daily average time in intensity-specific categories measured from AAI/15s cutpoints were defined as light PA (101-586 AAI/15s), MVPA (≥587 AAI/15s), and total PA (≥101 AAI/15s). Steps per day were determined using the ActiGraph GT3X+ manufacturer’s step algorithm. Sitting time and bout duration were classified by the Convolutional Neural Network Hip Accelerometer Posture (CHAP) algorithm, which is intended for application on hip-worn ActiGraph GT3X+ data.30 The CHAP algorithm classifies each 10 s nonoverlapping window of continuous data as either “sitting” or “nonsitting” postures, and measures sitting time and sit-to-stand transitions more accurately than cutpoint methods.30 From this output, total daily sitting time (min/day) and mean sitting bout duration (min/bout) were derived.

Outcome assessment

Exact birthdate data were not provided to research personnel to protect participant privacy; therefore, we assessed survival to age 90 birthyear as a proxy for survival to age 90. We aggregated response scores of items in the RAND-36 physical functioning scale31 to an overall score (0-100), with ≤50 indicating “mobility disability” and >50 indicating “intact mobility.”32 The RAND-36 was administered every year in OPACH and periodically in WHS after accelerometry baseline (Table S2). The response closest in time to their 90th birthyear distinguished intact mobility from mobility disability. For both cohorts, deaths were assessed from family members, postal authorities, medical records, interviews with next of kin, death certificates, and the National Death Index. These data classified women into three mutually exclusive categories: (1) reached age 90 birthyear with intact mobility (“intact mobility”); (2) reached age 90 birthyear with mobility disability (“mobility disability”); or (3) died before 90th birthyear (“died”).

Covariate assessment

Sociodemographic variables, including age, race and ethnicity, and education were collected at study enrollment. All participants regularly completed mailed questionnaires regarding their medical history and health behaviors; the administration closest to the time of accelerometer wear was used. Women self-reported general health, smoking status, alcohol intake, postmenopausal hormone use, and history of diabetes. Cardiovascular disease (history of myocardial infarction, percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty, coronary artery bypass grafting, or stroke) and cancer were self-reported, and then, physician adjudicated using medical records. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated from height and weight—self-reported in WHS; measured by staff in OPACH.

Statistical analysis

Accelerometer awake wear time was accounted for using the residuals method.33 Sociodemographic and health characteristics of women were examined across the three outcomes. The associations of continuous (per 1-standard deviation [SD] increments) and categorical (quartiles) measures of the accelerometer-derived measures with the healthy longevity outcomes were assessed using multinomial logistic regression models, with women who died as the referent group. Covariate-adjusted odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated to approximate the odds of survival to 90th birthyear with or without mobility disability relative to death before 90th birthyear across the exposure measures. The models were adjusted for cohort, age, race/­ethnicity, education, BMI, general health, smoking status, alcohol intake, postmenopausal hormone use, history of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cancer. Trend tests across PA and SB exposure categories were calculated by replacing the exposure quartile variable in the model with the continuous variable.

Using the approach proposed by Dumuid,19 we fit compositional ISMs to quantify the associations of replacing 30 min/day of sitting time for light PA or MVPA with healthy longevity. This method uses isometric log-ratio transformations to account for the dependencies of these behaviors by transforming the data so that the sum of all behaviors equals 15 h, which was approximately the mean device-wear time in our sample. These transformations allow each behavior to be modeled as an independent variable while accounting for the compositional nature of time-use data, enabling ORs to be interpreted relative to the average behavior composition across participants.19,20

Since survival to old age and PA patterns could vary by baseline age, BMI, or chronic disease status, additional analyses tested for multiplicative interaction by age group, obesity, and chronic disease variables using likelihood ratio tests comparing models with and without a cross-product term for each stratifying variable. We also examined whether varying the threshold for mobility disability (ie, RAND-36 score of 40 or 60) meaningfully impacted our findings. Sensitivity analyses explored two separate generalized linear models with a log-link and binomial distribution (intact mobility vs death and mobility disability vs death) to estimate adjusted risk ratios to compare to the primary analysis. All analyses were conducted in R v4.0.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing; Vienna, Austria).

Role of the funding source

Study funders had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, report writing, or decision to publish.

Results

The sample included 2656 WHAC (1722 OPACH; 935 WHS) women (Table 1). Mean (SD) age at baseline was 83.1 (2.6) years. Compared to women in the lowest quartile of total PA, women in the highest quartile had lower BMI, better self-rated health, lower chronic disease prevalence, and better physical function. The mean (SD) of each accelerometer measure were: 296.8 (77.4) min/day for light PA, 40.3 (25.0) min/day for MVPA, 337.1 (88.7) for total PA, 3702 (1903) steps/day, 591.9 (101.1) min/day for sitting time, and 12.8 (4.6) min/bout for sitting bout duration. Distributions were: 1667 (62.8%) intact mobility, 591 (22.3%) mobility disability, and 398 (15.0%) died before 90th birthyear.

Compared to odds of dying before 90th birthyear, all accelerometer measures had statistically significant dose-response associations with odds of intact mobility and mobility disability (all p < .03; Table 2). Per one-SD increment at baseline, the adjusted ORs (95% CI) of intact mobility compared to dying were 1.36 (1.20, 1.54) for light PA, 1.69 (1.47, 1.96) for MVPA, 1.51 (1.33, 1.71) for total PA, 1.75 (1.51, 2.03) for steps, 0.70 (0.61, 0.80) for sitting time, and 0.79 (0.70, 0.89) for sitting bout duration. Compared to women in the lowest quartile of PA measures, the ORs (95% CI) for intact mobility for women in the highest quartile ranged from 2.43 (1.72, 3.45) for light PA to 4.03 (2.82, 5.77) for steps. Similar strong inverse associations were observed comparing the highest quartile of sitting time (OR = 0.41, 0.29, 0.58) and sitting bout duration (OR = 0.62; 0.45, 0.88) to the lowest quartiles. The adjusted ORs (95% CI) of mobility disability compared to dying were 1.17 (1.02, 1.34) for light PA, 1.27 (1.08, 1.49) for MVPA, 1.22 (1.06, 1.40) for total PA, 1.28 (1.09, 1.52) for steps, 0.83 (0.71, 0.96) for sitting time, and 0.82 (0.72, 0.93) for sitting bout duration. Quartile-based analyses displayed similar patterns for all PA and SB measures.

The theoretical replacement of sitting time with either light or MVPA was associated with increased odds of both intact mobility and mobility disability (Table 3). Relative to the estimated mean behavior composition during a 15-h waking day, replacing 30 min/day of sitting with MVPA was associated with 1.6 times the odds of intact mobility (OR = 1.57; 95% CI = 1.51, 1.64) and 1.3 times the odds of mobility disability (OR = 1.30; 95% CI = 1.21, 1.39) compared to the odds of dying before age 90 birthyear. Associations were weaker for replacing sitting with light PA (intact mobility: OR = 1.09; 95% CI = 1.07, 1.11; mobility disability: OR = 1.04; 95% CI = 1.01, 1.07).

Discussion

In our study of postmenopausal women ages 78-89 years at baseline, higher levels of accelerometer-measured PA were associated with improved odds of survival to age 90 both with and without mobility disability, while SB was associated with lower odds. While accounting for time spent in other behaviors, the theoretical replacement of sitting time with light or MVPA was associated with higher odds of both survival outcomes. The associations of steps and sitting time with odds of healthy longevity were magnified among women ages 85-89 years at baseline. Altogether, these findings suggest that reducing sitting time and increasing PA may lead to stronger odds of survival to age 90 among women ages 78 and older.

Independent associations of accelerometer-measured PA with healthy longevity in WHAC women is consistent with previous studies using self-reported PA,9,10 including two previous analyses from WHI,11,12 though effect size magnitudes differed. Our definition of healthy longevity also differed in two ways: we assessed survival to age 90 (previous studies: 70-85 years); and absence of chronic disease status was not a criterion. We focused solely on survival to late age and intact mobility partly due to high prevalence of disease at baseline (29.2% with cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and/or cancer), largely due to older age at baseline (mean = 83.1 years), and the likelihood of few women in our sample being free of chronic disease at age 90. In addition, the inclusion of intact mobility was informed by the recognized relationships between mobility in older adults and functional independence, health-related qualify of life, psychosocial health, and mortality.34

Approximately 1 in 4 older adults (≥65 years) in the United States report a mobility disability,35 and mobility disability prevalence increases sharply with older adult age groups.36,37 This is reflected in WHAC women who survived to age 90, 26.2% of whom had a mobility disability. Substantial evidence demonstrates an inverse dose-response relationship between PA and risk of physical functional limitations among older adults.38,39 While higher PA levels were associated with higher odds of survival both with and without mobility disability, associations with mobility disability were consistently weaker than for intact mobility. Among older adults, several factors (eg, fatigue, lower back pain, joint stiffness) are associated with both PA/SB and limited mobility in later life. These conditions are likely not life threatening, thus, allowing older women to experience survival benefits associated with higher levels PA and lower levels of SB, but not freedom from mobility disability.

Three previous studies examined self-reported total PA only and reported similar dose-response patterns with odds of survival to late age with no chronic diseases or mobility disability among older women.9,11,12 Shi et al. examined light PA and MVPA separately and, similar to our study, reported stronger associations for MVPA than for light PA.10 While the inverse association of MVPA with the odds of healthy longevity are well-established,8 far less is known about light PA, which is poorly assessed by most questionnaires. Most older adult PA is light intensity,40,41 contributing a similar amount to PA energy expenditure as MVPA.40 In addition, absolute light PA (determined by accelerometer cutpoints) and relative PA (percentage of maximal possible effort) are both associated with reduced all-cause mortality risk in older women.42 Our finding of an independent association of light PA with healthy longevity in older women extends those of previous research and warrants further consideration for future potential behavioral interventions.

We observed strong associations between daily steps, which can be taken at any intensity, with healthy longevity outcomes that were similar in strength to those from accelerometer-derived MVPA. For every 1903 steps/day taken (approximated to <1 mile43) in our study, women had nearly a 2-fold higher odds of healthy survival. However, the strong linear association in our study suggests odds of healthy longevity may begin increasing at increments less than 1903 steps/day. While previous studies did not measure daily step counts, two studies assessed frequency and duration of walking outside the home, finding similar beneficial associations with survival to late age with no chronic diseases or mobility disability.9,11 Daily steps are an intuitive metric for total PA and, in light of our findings, clinical and public health practitioners may consider daily step targets as a tool for PA and healthy longevity promotion among older women, particularly those suffering from chronic conditions.

SB was inversely associated with odds of survival to age 90 with/without mobility disability, extending findings of previous studies in older women.10,11 Ours is (to our knowledge) the first to include a sitting bout duration measure. Shi et al. found replacing 1 h/day of sitting time with either light or MVPA increased odds of survival to age 70 with no physical, memory, or mental health disabilities in older women.10 Similarly, we found replacing 30 min/day of sitting time with light or MVPA was associated with higher odds of survival to age 90 with/without mobility disability. These findings underscore that while SB can be harmful, replacing SB with lower intensity PA may confer health benefits and be more feasible for older adult populations. Public health promotion campaigns targeting older adults should consider not only increasing PA but also decreasing total and prolonged SB.

All women experienced higher odds of survival to age 90 with intact mobility with higher PA and lower SB levels, independent of chronic disease or obesity status. While engaging in higher intensity activities can be particularly difficult for women with chronic diseases, findings suggest more accessible behaviors such as more time in light PA, more daily steps, and breaking up prolonged sitting times may effectively promote healthy survival among this population.

Our study has several strengths. To our knowledge, no previous study assessed healthy longevity among older women with accelerometer-measured PA and SB. Self-reported measures are prone to recall and social desirability biases.44 Accelerometry better examines light PA, steps, and sitting bout lengths—all poorly captured by self-report in older women.13 This study leveraged the strength of the WHAC cohort, the product of harmonized activity, covariate, health status, and mortality data from WHS and OPACH, creating a large sample of US women ages 78-89 with hip-worn accelerometry data. There are limitations to our findings. Devices were worn once by participants for 1 week, and behaviors could change seasonally and over the course of follow-up. However, WHS examined physical activity levels over three years and observed that a one-week collection of steps/day had an intraclass correlation of 0.78 after adjusting for seasonality.45 We examined composite measure of SBs, and some domains (ie, screen time, sitting and writing or performing other mentally engaging activities) may have beneficial effects on healthy longevity. ActiGraph devices may underestimate steps taken among older women with slow walking speeds.46 We did not have access to participant birthdate data, which precluded our ability to perform survival analyses that account for time-to-event. Despite this limitation, most age groups experienced significant associations between accelerometer measures and intact mobility. We cannot rule out the possibility of confounding by poor health status during accelerometer measurement,47 where women with poorer health (eg, chronic conditions, low self-reported health, mobility limitations, frailty) are less likely to be active and have higher baseline mortality risk. However, we controlled for major chronic diseases, self-reported general health, and excluded women with mobility disability at baseline and those who died within one year of accelerometry measurement to help mitigate this bias. We were unable to control for some known confounders such as diet, medication use, and other clinical conditions such as cognitive impairment, possibly overestimating effect sizes. We excluded 228 women with usable accelerometer data, eligible to reach age 90 but lost-to-follow-up; their PA levels were slightly lower compared to our analytic sample (Table S2), possibly biasing our estimates away from the null. Compositional ISM approaches are statistical, not actual replacements. Generalizability of findings is restricted to older women. Similar studies are needed in younger, male, and more diverse cohorts with longer follow-up.

In this large cohort of older US women with hip-worn accelerometer data, more PA of any intensity level, more steps, and less total and prolonged SB were associated with higher odds of survival to age 90 with or without mobility disability. Findings extend those of previous studies of PA, SB, and healthy longevity among older women,9–12 providing further justification for the recommendation to move more and sit less to age well into the tenth decade of life.
