The Controversy over Price Transparency: Patients and Plastic Surgeons Disagree
Authors: Parul Rai, Priyasha Pareek, Caitlyn L. Vilas, Jonathan L. Kaplan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:The No Surprises Act (NSA) went into effect on January 1, 2022. This federal law is designed to minimize surprise bills for emergency care, out of network (OON) care, and care for uninsured or self-pay individuals. To achieve these goals, the NSA relies heavily on physicians providing cost estimates to patients prior to administering care. Because healthcare is often price-opaque, this study was conducted to assess physician and patient attitudes toward price transparency in plastic surgery and aesthetic/self-pay medicine.Methods:Two cohorts of providers received a survey asking whether and how their practice offers pricing information prior to a consultation and their views regarding the NSA. Patients who had previously used an online price estimator tool also received a survey regarding their experience and accuracy of estimates received online.Results:Most surveyed providers do not share the costs of their services online, whereas patients had a positive experience when receiving estimates of medical services before consultation. Although many providers cited the inaccuracy of price estimator tools as a reason not to incorporate them into their website, most patients reported that their final cost of service was close to (or even less than) the estimate received online prior to the consultation.Conclusions:This study suggests that price transparency is a patient satisfier. Therefore, plastic surgeons should exercise price transparency to be compliant with federal law and to gain a competitive advantage in the market, as the decision to implement price transparency can be mutually beneficial to patients and providers.

Takeaways

Question: How do patients and plastic surgeons compare in their perceptions regarding the No Surprises Act (NSA) and price transparency?

Findings: Patients and plastic surgeons were surveyed regarding price transparency and the NSA. Although price transparency is a patient satisfier, plastic surgeons hesitate to incorporate it into their websites. Providers cited the inaccuracy of price estimator tools as a reason behind their actions, yet patients reported that their online estimates were accurate to the degree defined by the NSA.

Meaning: Plastic surgeons’ attitudes toward price transparency are incongruent with patient preferences.

INTRODUCTION

The No Surprises Act (NSA) is a bipartisan bill that went into effect on January 1, 2022 to protect patients from surprise medical bills by (1) eliminating out-of-network (OON) billing in emergency situations; (2) requiring upfront out-of-pocket estimates and consent to bill for OON services in nonemergency scenarios; and (3) granting patients the right to a good faith estimate (GFE), a comprehensive breakdown of their services, prior to receiving uninsured or self-pay care, including aesthetic medicine. The GFE must include all expected costs, including the cost of the provider, anesthesia, operating room, and ancillary fees. The NSA is revolutionizing healthcare economics by introducing market forces such as upfront pricing, typical of other sectors of the economy but lacking in healthcare.1

This legislation introduces two new dispute resolution processes. As per the NSA, when an OON physician provides emergency care, the OON charge will be “downcoded” to an in-network rate. If the provider is unhappy with the new rate, the “qualifying payment amount,” the provider may launch a dispute against the carrier. In contrast, in the case of an uninsured or self-pay patient receiving care in any specialty or practice setting, the dispute resolution process is between the patient and the provider, not between the provider and insurer.2 If any surprise bills are sent after services are administered, the patient may launch a dispute against the provider if charges exceed the GFE by at least $400.3

Currently, the Center for Medicaid Services’ (CMS) price transparency regulations require that hospitals and health insurance carriers include downloadable machine-readable files of pricing information and a price estimator tool on their website.4 Although not a requirement for individual healthcare providers, there will be a growing expectation for online price transparency from all providers as patients become accustomed to viewing pricing information from insurers’ and hospitals’ websites. Although an online estimate does not constitute a GFE, an estimate before a consultation can reduce patients’ sticker shock at the time of the consultation.

Price transparency can increase patient satisfaction by empowering patients to make informed decisions about their care and preventing surprise bills and medical bankruptcy.5 Additionally, price transparency tools may benefit providers by serving as lead generators through establishing open communication with the patient. This marketing strategy increases the likelihood that patients will book a consultation and sets realistic expectations with them, even before the initial visit.6–8

The newly implemented NSA’s requirements surrounding price transparency affect patients and physicians on a daily basis more than any other federal healthcare legislation, including the Affordable Care Act. Therefore, this study evaluates physicians’ and patients’ attitudes toward price transparency and the NSA.

METHODS

This survey-based study involves two segments. In the first segment, a consumer-facing survey was sent to prospective patients who had been entered into the BuildMyHealth (BMH) database by using price estimators on providers’ websites. In the second segment, a similar provider-facing survey was sent to plastic surgeons and aesthetic providers in the BMH database and a randomized group of active members of the American Society of Plastic Surgeons (ASPS) through a survey conducted by the division of registry and data analytics.

The provider-facing survey distributed by the authors and sourcing the BMH database included aesthetic providers who had previously signed up for webinars provided by BMH regarding the NSA or price transparency; members of ASPS, The American Society of Aesthetic Plastic Surgeons, the American Academy of Facial Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery, American Academy of Dermatology; and medspas. The provider-facing survey distributed by the ASPS consisted of a randomly selected cohort of US-based, board-certified plastic surgeons, all of whom are active members of the ASPS. Surveys were developed by the authors and distributed via SurveyMonkey, which labels the first question in each survey as Q1, the second question as Q2, and so on; thus, questions will be labeled accordingly in this discussion.

Physicians from the BMH database received a survey entitled “Provider attitudes towards price transparency and the No Surprises Act,” yielding 101 responses out of 463 recipients (21.8% response rate). The same survey was sent to ASPS members, yielding 182 responses from 1934 recipients. With a response rate of 9.4%, this study exceeds 8%, the average response rate of ASPS surveys, as noted by Christopher Simmons, Manager of ASPS Registry and Data Analytics (email communication, July 21, 2023). A survey entitled “Consumer attitudes towards price transparency in healthcare and the No Surprises Act” was sent to patients and yielded 258 responses out of 20,057 opened emails (1.3% response rate).

Respondents on all surveys who selected any response labeled “Other (please specify)” were given 100 characters to elaborate on their selection. To avoid a central tendency bias, there was no “neutral” option provided on questions that required selecting answers on a spectrum (eg, “mostly negative” to “mostly positive”).

Survey data were deidentified to encourage candid responses from all parties. With an estimated completion time under three minutes each, the surveys were as succinct as possible to encourage a high response rate.

RESULTS

Provider Survey

Price Transparency

Most providers (78.22%) do not share the cost of their services online (Fig. 3) according to the BMH provider survey, and an even greater proportion of ASPS members do not share pricing online (86.27%). Among the providers in both surveys who shared pricing information online, many displayed an online menu of prices on their website (68.18%) or encouraged patients to inquire about pricing via a “contact us” submission form (40.48%), which would require a manual response from the office staff (Fig. 4). The 9.52% of providers who marked “other” on Q2 listed pricing information on a frequently asked questions page or mentioned it elsewhere on their website.

The methods through which the surveyed providers allow visitors to check pricing on their website and the percentage of respondents who chose each method, based on Q2 of the provider survey.

A large majority of providers who offer pricing information online (78.57%) did so to set clear expectations with the patient beforehand (Fig. 3). Reduction in the volume of phone calls for office staff, increased likelihood of patients booking procedures due to a lower chance of “sticker shock,” and compliance with the NSA are among the most common advantages that providers cite in implementing online price transparency (Fig. 5).

Reasons why plastic surgeons prefer to show pricing information online based on their responses to Q3.

Of those who do not provide pricing information online, a similar proportion of respondents to both surveys (63.51% from ASPS, 65.33% from BMH) believe that patients should value factors beyond the cost of services, such as the provider’s credentials and quality of service. The most common reason that plastic surgeons do not disclose pricing information on their website is that online price estimators are too inaccurate (Fig. 6).

Reasons why plastic surgeons prefer not to disclose pricing information online based on plastic surgeon responses to Q4 and Q5.

Physicians cite several other reasons for not offering pricing online that were not listed among the options in Q4. For one, some do not have a website where they can disclose this information publicly. Additionally, they note that insurance companies and the institution where they are used control the final cost to the patient. Numerous physicians also find difficulty in providing an itemized list of fees due to various factors, including fluctuation in pricing, a high degree of personalization/customization in the procedures they offer, and a need to evaluate the patient in a consultation to determine costs on a case-by-case basis.

The 12 physicians who previously had a price estimator tool on their website but stopped felt that it was a “race to the bottom” to compete for the lowest price, whereas others were concerned that patients would state, “That’s not what I saw online,” complaining about necessary adjustments to cost of services. Much like the physicians who never highlighted pricing on their website, those who stopped doing so also felt “that it’s not all about price”; rather, patients should consider other variables such as physician credentials (Fig. 6).

No Surprises Act

Most physicians (91.73%) indicate having at least some level of knowledge regarding the NSA, but this leaves a substantial amount with no knowledge of this legislation. Physicians who had at least some knowledge of the NSA were asked follow-up questions, whereas those who had no knowledge were not asked further questions. Physicians who report any degree of knowledge of the law vary greatly in their opinions of the NSA, and 28.42% of them incorrectly believe that the NSA does not apply to them (Fig. 7).

Patient Survey

Price Transparency

Most patients (77.91%) had a positive experience receiving an estimate of medical services prior to scheduling a visit with the provider (Fig. 1), noting their ability to make a more informed decision about where to seek care as the most common reason. Many (46.70%) also indicated that having the estimate beforehand enabled them to get their personal finances in order prior to the consultation. Of interest to plastic surgeons, 30.77% of patients noted that knowing the price beforehand led patients to book a consultation (Fig. 8). This data indicates that disclosing online pricing can be mutually beneficial to physicians and patients. The most common reasons that patients reported having a poor experience receiving a price estimate were that the procedure was too expensive for them to follow through with a consultation and a procedure (44.00%) and that they did not like having to provide their contact information to receive a price estimate (44.00%) (Fig. 9).

Most patients (75.7%) who booked a consultation ultimately paid within $400 of the estimate they had received prior to consultation or even less than the estimate by $400 (Fig. 10). Additionally, 64.3% of patients believe that knowing pricing ahead of time is equally important, regardless of whether they are using insurance or paying out of pocket (Fig. 11).

No Surprises Act

Nearly half of the surveyed patients are unaware of the NSA. However, among the patients who indicated having at least some knowledge about the NSA, 93.3% held a somewhat positive or mostly positive perception of the legislation (Fig. 7).

DISCUSSION

Price Transparency

The results of this survey indicate that there is a disconnect between physician practices and patient preferences. A minority of providers share pricing information on their websites, but patients tend to have a positive experience when receiving an online price estimate. Additionally, providers among the BMH cohort are understandably more price transparent, likely because they are better informed regarding the NSA and price transparency (Fig. 3).

Providers who previously displayed pricing information online but stopped voice concerns similar to those of other providers who hesitate to offer pricing information online: (1) that patients should value other factors besides the cost and (2) that online estimators are inaccurate. Providers who stopped offering pricing information online experienced more patient disputes and found that listing the pricing was a “race to the bottom” to outcompete other providers to offer the lowest pricing (Fig. 6) when they shared pricing information. However, showcasing pricing on their websites does not necessarily compel physicians to lower their prices due to the business environment or patient feedback. Additionally, patients already align with plastic surgeons’ sentiments that factors such as board certification and method of referral take precedent over cost of services.9

This study demonstrates another discrepancy between provider and patient attitudes: physicians believe that showing pricing information beforehand will drive patients away (Fig. 6), whereas patients believe that knowing the cost of the service leads them to book a consultation and contributes to an overall positive experience in receiving care (Fig. 8).

Additionally, although providers may avoid sharing pricing online due to a perception that estimator tools are too inaccurate (Fig. 6), patients who booked a procedure typically receive a price quote within $400 of the online estimate (Fig. 10). Thus, providers’ perceptions contradict patients’ reported experiences regarding the accuracy of price estimator tools.

Rightfully so, patients find it equally important to know the pricing of their healthcare ahead of time, regardless of whether they are using insurance (Fig. 11), as they are typically responsible for at least part of the cost due to deductibles and copays.

No Surprises Act

Although most physicians have some degree of knowledge regarding the NSA, a substantial number of providers are unknowingly noncompliant due to lack of knowledge about the legislation or the misconception that it does not apply to them (Fig. 7).

When considering the patient population, a higher proportion are unaware of the NSA and are thus unaware of their right to a GFE. Although patients are less knowledgeable about the legislation than providers, they have an overwhelmingly positive perception of the NSA, albeit providers’ views regarding the law also lean positively (Fig. 7).

CONCLUSIONS

Medicine may be the only industry that has normalized providing a service without the consumer being aware of an agreed-upon price ahead of time. One patient remarked:

Even when services are estimated the patient never gets an estimate from each procedure or medical professional involved. For example, a surgery could be estimated, and then you get an additional facility bill, anesthesia bill, etc. We are only having one surgery, so it would be nice to have one bill. When you buy a car, you do not pay for it piece by piece.

Interestingly, when asked to provide thoughts on the No Surprises Act, one provider quipped, “Ridiculous…we are not car dealers.” Juxtaposing these comments paints a compelling narrative, as a plastic surgeon and patient thought of the “car purchase” metaphor completely independently of each other yet had such strongly opposing perspectives. Admittedly, medicine is not like a traditional business in that it is deeply intertwined with the consumer’s health; however, this fact further underscores the importance of adhering to patient preferences.

Because patients are not as informed about the NSA as their providers, the medical community should act in good faith and inform patients of their new rights. In fact, the NSA mandates that all providers must include notices at their office/facility front desk displaying the patient’s protections against surprise billing and the right to a GFE.10 Additionally, by providing a GFE, physicians can be compliant with the new legislation while practicing price transparency, a patient satisfier.

Even among providers, there is an abundance of uncertainty and misinformation regarding the NSA. For one, practitioners of certain specialties incorrectly assume that they are exempt from the NSA, perhaps due to the misconception that it exclusively applies to emergent cases or that it does not apply to aesthetic medicine. One provider commented that the NSA “does not feel applicable to outpatient settings where treatment is highly variable, … patients pay out-of-pocket, and services are available in [multiple practice settings].” In reality, a GFE is required for services that require recurring sessions (eg, counseling, physical therapy, laser hair removal) or multiple providers (eg, surgeon, anesthesiologist, pathologist, radiologist).11 Thus, aesthetic providers will be more apt to highlight any expected (required by the NSA) and unexpected (not required by the NSA) expenses, such as the cost of pathology for a cosmetic breast reduction or the potential necessity for a postoperative ultrasound in the event of a high suspicion for deep vein thrombosis.

The study unveils the divisiveness of the NSA: some providers find that it imposes an administrative burden on private practices that should only apply to larger hospitals, whereas others see the legislation as progress. Turnkey price estimator tools present a solution to this dilemma, as they provide detailed cost breakdowns and are compliant with the NSA. Still, some note no impact on their practice; one physician reports to “have always provided detailed quotes to every patient, both surgical and nonsurgical.” At first glance, most providers may believe that they are providing all reasonably expected costs. But for elective self-pay services, are they remembering to include the costs of labs, pathology, or other postoperative items such as cosmetic insurance, skin/scar care, and garments? If a physician attempts to bill postoperatively for such reasonably expected costs and excludes them from the GFE, the patient may launch a dispute with CMS.

Providers who fail to provide a GFE or violate any other aspect of the NSA may incur a $10,000 penalty. Aside from determining provider and patient attitudes toward price transparency, this study aims to raise awareness of the NSA and reduce plastic surgeons’ exposure to penalties. Aesthetic providers may be especially susceptible to penalties, as their services are often not covered by insurance.

Although the overwhelming majority of physicians do not share their pricing online, an overwhelming majority of consumers wish they did. This study aimed to encourage providers to view these regulations as an opportunity, not a burden, to provide better customer service and efficiently answer the question every patient has at the forefront of their mind: how much does it cost?
