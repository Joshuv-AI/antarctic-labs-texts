Multidisciplinary Delphi study validated variables for calculating cost of medication errors in the Sri Lankan context
Authors: Sakunika Ranasinghe, Dewmini Navodya, Abarna Nadeshkumar, Savini Senadheera, Nithushi Samaranayake
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe burden of medication errors needs to be costed but no standard method, nor a standard list of variables, has yet been identified. Hence, this study aimed to establish a standard list of cost variables (SLCV) for calculating the cost of medication errors in Sri Lanka from a provider perspective.MethodsThe study had three discrete phases: a systematic review to identify cost variables used globally to calculate the cost of medication errors (published), followed by a Delphi study with 18 experts to assess appropriateness, accuracy, accessibility and measurability of identified cost variables. Finally, a hospital bill analysis was done to assess accessibility and measurability of cost variables.ResultsIn Delphi round 1, out of 13 cost variables, nine were retained as appropriate for calculating medication error costs. None were rated as accessible or measurable in the healthcare settings in Sri Lanka. Experts highlighted the need for a guideline to use the cost variables, which was then developed and shared among experts in Delphi round 2. The SLCV, including eight variables, was retained as appropriate for calculating medication error cost after Delphi round 2. Thirty-one bills confirmed the accessibility and measurability of most variables in SLCV.ConclusionA standard list comprising eight appropriate cost variables to calculate the cost of medication errors, and a guideline was developed for Sri Lanka. The accessibility of these variables was affirmed through a bill audit.Keywords: Health policy, Patient safety, Medication safety, Healthcare quality improvement, Cost-effectiveness

Introduction

A ‘failure in the treatment process that leads to, or has the potential to lead to, harm to patient’1 may be considered as a medication error. Medication errors can happen at any time during the treatment period, be it prescribing, dispensing, medication administration, compounding or monitoring.1

Unsafe medication practices and medication errors are a leading cause of avoidable harm in healthcare systems globally.2 In addition, medication errors are a burden to a healthcare system.3 The scale and nature of this harm differs between low, middle and high-income countries. According to WHO, the cost associated with medication errors has been estimated at US$42 billion annually.2

The burden of medication errors needs to be costed or otherwise its gravity cannot be justified against the investment made to avoid them. It is beneficial to have a universal formula which can be adjusted according to the country and healthcare setting, and enable objective comparisons across countries. Researchers have used different approaches for calculating the cost of medication errors,4 but approaches vary considerably as reported by Patel et al,5 and Walsh et al,6 who found no standard methodology for calculating the economic burden of medication errors as at 2017. Our systematic review on the same subject similarly found no universal formula, nor a standard list of variables for calculating the cost of medication errors to date. The methodologies used for this purpose, and the cost variables used, differed greatly around the world.7

Sri Lanka is a lower-middle-income country with a population of 23.2 million. While both public and private sectors offer Western healthcare, the public sector provides 95% of inpatient care and 50% of outpatient care services free of charge. The private sector provides access to almost all types of care at a cost.8 Healthcare is delivered by a team including doctors, nurses and pharmacists, where doctors prescribe medicines and nurses administer them. A ward stock system of medicine distribution exists for inpatient care in state hospitals, and hence pharmacists do not directly dispense to in-patients. Clinical pharmacy services are not available in the state sector, but pharmacists directly dispense medicines to outpatients.

According to studies undertaken in Sri Lanka on medication errors, it was evident that medication errors happen in every level of healthcare including hospital care, primary care and community care. However, none had focused on the medication error costs.9,13 Only one Sri Lankan study14 assessed the cost of a ward-based clinical pharmacy service resolving drug-related problems, but that too was an estimated cost.14

No standard method, nor a standard list of variables, has yet been identified to calculate the overall cost for medication errors in Sri Lanka or universally. Although high-income countries have used different methods to calculate medication error costs, the cost variables have varied considerably between studies, and they may not be applicable to Sri Lanka. As estimating the cost of medication errors will be a greater motivation to prevent them, the aim of this study was to establish a standard list of cost variables (SLCV) to be used in calculating the cost of medication errors, and thereby develop a formula/cost sheet to calculate medication error costs for Sri Lanka. For this purpose, a provider perspective was considered as a first step because minimising medication errors is a large economic benefit for healthcare providers, especially through minimising hospital readmissions or reducing prolonged hospital stays. Although the patient perspective is equally important, this study prioritised the provider perspective when deriving cost variables.

Methodology

The study had three discrete phases. A systematic review was initially undertaken (and published) to identify cost variables used globally to calculate the cost of medication errors.7 This was followed by a Delphi study to assess appropriateness, accuracy, accessibility and measurability of identified relevant variables to calculate the cost of medication errors in Sri Lanka. Finally, a hospital bill analysis was used to rigorously assess accessibility and measurability.

A shortlist of cost variables was created after removing duplicate variables (ie, similar variables with different terminology) identified from the aforementioned systematic review, by the research team after a brainstorming session, leaving 13 variables (table 1) to work on. These were a collection of different variables used to cost medication errors by others in published studies. A more detailed variables list with subvariables is provided in online supplemental table 1.

Participants

A panel of 18 participants comprised the Expert Group. It included doctors employed as hospital administrators or engaged in medication safety research and registered at the Sri Lanka Medical Council (SLMC), pharmacists specialised in medication safety or with pharmacoeconomic expertise, or involved in patient care and licensed by the SLMC, biostatisticians and hospital cost accountants.

Experts were first invited to join by email invitation. Consent to participate was achieved by confirmation email. Experts who did not agree to connect online were excluded from the study. Demographic data (eg, name, gender, designation and area of expertise) were collected.

Delphi process

Round 1

Experts rated each variable for appropriateness, accuracy, accessibility and measurability using a 5-point Likert scale (1 indicated ‘Strongly agree’ and 5 indicated ‘Strongly disagree’). Experts were asked to include any other cost variable they considered relevant as well. They were also asked to explain their reasons behind their Likert scale choices. Results were analysed by the authors (SR and NS); variables with a median score of 1–2 (≤2), and which were accepted by more than 75% of Experts, were retained for round 2.

Round 2

As a result of comments provided in round 1, the research team developed a preliminary cost variable guideline on how to use each variable. This guideline, and retained variables with subvariables from round 1, and new variables generated in round 2 (in accordance with Expert comments) were subsequently circulated for comments among the same group of Experts. Only the appropriateness of cost variables was assessed in round 2 because accessibility and measurability scored poorly in round 1 (table 2). Round 2 results were analysed in the same manner as in round 1. Following round 2, a final list of variables was configured and referred to as the SLCV.

As the accessibility and measurability of the cost variables scored poorly in the Delphi, a final refinement of the SLCV followed a hospital bill audit to confirm that SLCV was accessible and measurable.

Hospital bill audit

A descriptive cross-sectional study of inpatient hospital bills in both state and private hospitals was undertaken. Bills were collected using convenience sampling on a voluntary and opportunistic basis after obtaining verbal consent and deidentification of all personal details of patients and healthcare professionals. Bills for routine medical check-ups, maternity care and diagnostic tests such as endoscopies and colonoscopies were excluded. Bills were collected until no new information was found related to the variables in the SLCV. Cost variables available on bills were matched with the SLCV and were interpreted as ‘accessible’ and ‘measurable’ if they matched.

Results

Delphi process results

Eighteen Experts, including 10 doctors, six pharmacists, one statistician and one cost accountant, participated in round 1. Table 2 shows the summary of Likert scale results provided by the Expert group on appropriateness, accuracy, accessibility and measurability of cost variables. All variables except 4 (Therapeutic Drug Monitoring (TDM) cost), 11 (Litigation cost) and 12 (Compensation cost) were retained as appropriate to use for calculating medication error costs. All variables except 4 (TDM cost), 6 (Transport cost), 7 (Emergency department visit cost), 11 (Litigation cost), 12 (Compensation cost) and 13 (Cost for absenteeism) were retained as accurate to use for calculating medication error costs. None of the cost variables were rated as accessible or measurable in the healthcare settings in Sri Lanka. Subvariables of variables 1 (Cost of medication) and 2 (Healthcare material and machinery use cost) were rated as appropriate, but subvariables of variable 9 (human resource cost) needed modification. The Experts specifically highlighted the need for a guideline to use the cost variables in calculating the cost of medication errors. They were concerned by the inability to disaggregate some variables into subvariables as proposed by the research team (n=4), and the lack of clarity regarding the perspective to be considered (eg, patient perspective or healthcare provider perspective) when deciding on the cost variables to be included (n=1). Further, the unavailability of a proper method to detect medication errors in Sri Lanka (n=6), and the unavailability of a medication error reporting system in Sri Lanka were mentioned by some (n=5).

As decided by the research team, only the appropriateness of cost variables was assessed in round 2 as most Experts scored low for accessibility and measurability of these variables. A preliminary guideline was also developed on using appropriate variables for calculating the cost of medication errors. Some subvariables were moved into the guideline for better clarity and simplification of SLCV. As Experts’ comments highlighted a lack of clarity regarding the perspective, a healthcare provider perspective was explicitly mentioned to Experts in round 2.

Hence, changes were made to the list of cost variables before round 2, based on comments from round 1. The total number of cost variables was reduced from 13 to 9. Some variables were combined. TDM cost (V4) and extra diagnostic test cost (V5) were renamed as ‘investigation cost’. Litigation cost (V11) and compensation cost (V12) variables were combined and renamed as ‘litigation and compensation cost’. Emergency department visit cost (V7) and cost for absenteeism (V13) were removed. Subvariables were removed from the variable list and were moved into the guideline (annexure 1) for simplification and clarity. The resultant list of nine cost variables used in round 2 is shown in table 1.

Sixteen out of 18 Experts who took part in round 1 were involved in round 2, including nine doctors, five pharmacists, one statistician and one cost accountant. Table 3 shows the summary of Likert scale results provided by the Expert group on appropriateness of cost variables in round 2. As a result of round 2 ratings, all cost variables except V8 (Meal cost) were retained as appropriate for calculating medication error cost; V8 was removed from the main variable list and inserted as a subvariable under V2 (Hospital room/bed and healthcare material cost) in the guideline. Hence, the total number of cost variables was reduced from nine to eight variables and was finalised as the SLCV (table 1). The preliminary guideline was also amended according to Expert views (online supplemental annexure 1).

Hospital bill audit results

The collection of 31 bills included four bills from state hospitals and 27 bills from private hospitals. The 27 private hospital bills represented 13 hospitals (hospital A=3, hospital B=4, D=5, E=2, F=2, G=1, H=1, J=1, K=5, n=4, M=1, p=1, Q=1). Of the 31, one bill had an ICU stay which was a neonatal ICU stay (K1) and one bill had an ETU admission (A3). Duration of stay of the collected bills varied from 1 day to 7 days. The cost variables that appeared in the collected bills differed among the 27 bills. Online supplemental table 2 shows matching cost variables found on bills with variables in SLCV.

Among the eight main variables and 20 subvariables, 87.5% of main variables and 55% of subvariables were accessible and measurable through bills. Subvariable 4.1 of SLCV which was ‘TDM’ and variable 8: Litigation cost and compensation cost were not present in any of the bills. So, they were not accessible or measurable in Sri Lanka.

‘Admission cost’, ‘administrative cost’, ‘supportive charges’ and ‘rehabilitation cost’ which appeared in bills had not been included in the SLCV.

Discussion

Cost variables deemed suitable for calculating medication error costs were extracted from systematic review (already published)7 and shortlisted by consensus of the research group. The shortlist of variables was shared with Experts to identify the most appropriate, accurate, measurable and accessible cost variables to calculate the cost of medication errors in healthcare settings in Sri Lanka.

The Experts’ overall opinion was that all cost variables in SLCV were appropriate in the Sri Lankan context, but none were accessible or measurable. Experts also highlighted the difficulty with disaggregating some variables, explaining this was challenging and differed by the hospital. Accordingly, the necessity of a guideline to calculate the cost of medication errors was identified. A preliminary guideline for assessing these cost variables was therefore developed by the research team as an outcome of Expert opinion (online supplemental annexure 1).

The guideline specifies options for those who encounter problems in obtaining disaggregated costs for SLCV. The guideline provides flexibility to calculate the cost of medication errors based on the practices of the hospital and guides the researcher on handling the cost variables as either aggregated or disaggregated cost variables in Sri Lanka, but most importantly, it facilitates uniformity in calculating the costs. Even if the costs in SLCV are aggregated and cannot be separated, or if included within another variable, or if unavailable, the guideline provides instructions on documenting them clearly. So that the method of calculating the cost is explicit to anyone interested.

Although the Delphi Experts rated that SLCV is not accessible or measurable in the Sri Lankan context, this limitation was addressed through a hospital bill analysis. We showed that these cost variables are measurable and accessible by analysing hospital bills from private hospitals in Sri Lanka. We determined that most variables in the SLCV were accessible and available on the bill. However, the bill analysis used a convenience sampling and can be used only to show that these variables are available and not the frequency.

The preliminary guideline developed in this study was validated by in-depth interviews with key personnel in hospital accounts/finance departments and using a pilot study carried out on a hypothetical medication error case. Due to the depth of information, the results of the qualitative study will be published separately. In summary, interviews with five accountants from three state hospitals and two private hospitals revealed insights on ‘sources’ and ‘methods of handling’ cost variables which confirmed the measurability. As a result, some variables such as litigation costs and compensation costs, and costs related to TDM were considered less practical in the Sri Lankan context. The case for the hypothetical medication error in the pilot study was designed by two medical professionals, and a working patient record was developed with expert consensus. A cost sheet with SLCV was developed and used to cost the hypothetical medication error using the guideline and methods suggested by accountants. The guideline underwent nine changes during this validation step, including regrouping and rewording for clarifications.

We need to acknowledge that the feasibility of calculating the medication error cost from the provider perspective was only assessed through a hospital bill analysis using a convenience sampling and a qualitative exploration of hospital administrators (to be published separately). So, in an instance where some major cost variables are missing or difficult to measure, the cost calculation of the medication error may not be entirely accurate. Although the guideline for using SLCV was prepared to adjust for different circumstances, this method may not be generalisable to every healthcare setting/medication error incident.

Given that a standard list of variables for calculating the cost of medication errors is not still available universally, this study was able to list a set of cost variables that are most relevant to calculate the cost of medication errors, which may be internationally explored to assess the universal applicability.

Conclusion

This study finalised eight appropriate cost variables through Delphi consensus which can be used to calculate the medication error costs and was named as SLCV. A comprehensive preliminary guideline to describe the use of SLCV and their subvariables was also developed.

Although Experts rated low for accessibility and measurability in the Delphi study, a hospital bill analysis revealed that most SLCVs are accessible and measurable. Although a pilot case study was conducted using a hypothetical medication error (to be published separately), the next step would be to undertake a real-time study to validate the SLCVs and the respective guideline for Sri Lanka.

As there is no universally acceptable list of cost variables or methodology to calculate medication error cost, this SLCV and validated guideline can be used in other countries with similar healthcare systems, but will need validation of the respective country’s healthcare system before application on a large scale.
