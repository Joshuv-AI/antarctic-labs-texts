Association between combined exposure to underground air pollutants and hypertension in coal miners: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Qianwei Chen, Chao Lu, Yongtang Jin, Wanjun Yin, Fengjen Chu, Jia Wang, Yuanjie Zou, Yuanzhen Du, Fenglin Zhu, Yajun Zhang, Ziwen Hui, Min Mu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoal miners are chronically exposed to underground air pollutants, with a notably higher hypertension (HTN) incidence than the general population. Previous studies focused on single pollutants, yet the health effects of occupational multipollutant mixtures remain unclear. This study aimed to elucidate the association between combined exposure and HTN, identifying dominant contributors.MethodsData were collected from 3,432 coal miners employed by three large coal mining companies in Shaanxi Province, China. We assessed cumulative exposure to five air pollutants: coal dust (CD), carbon monoxide (CO), carbon dioxide (CO₂), nitrogen monoxide (NO), and nitrogen dioxide (NO₂). Blood pressure measurements were taken, with HTN defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or a diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg. Four statistical methods were used to evaluate the associations between mixed exposure and HTN in coal miners: multivariate logistic regression, weighted quantile sum (WQS), Quantile g-computation (qcomp), and Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR).ResultsMultivariate logistic regression analysis indicated that all five air pollutants were positively correlated with an increased risk of HTN after adjusting for covariates. Background

Hypertension (HTN), the leading modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular diseases globally [1], is associated with pronounced disparities in disease burden among occupationally exposed populations. Coal miners working in underground environments face significantly elevated HTN risks (prevalence: 33%, SMR = 1.60, 95% CI = 1.06–2.40) [2] because of complex mixtures of air pollutants, including coal dust (CD), carbon monoxide (CO), and nitrogen oxides (NO/NO₂). These pollutants originate from coal extraction processes (e.g., mechanical fragmentation and blasting) and from emissions from diesel-powered equipment. For example, CO from blasting operations and NO₂ from diesel engines accumulate in confined spaces, creating high-concentration exposure scenarios. Prolonged exposure to these pollutants is strongly associated with increased HTN risk [3, 4].

However, most previous studies have focused on individual pollutants, neglecting the synergistic or antagonistic effects of multicomponent exposures. This narrow focus limits the accuracy of risk assessment models and undermines the development of targeted prevention strategies. For example, while CO and NO₂ individually correlate with HTN, their combined effects may amplify risks through pathways such as oxidative stress—a possibility traditional multivariate regression cannot capture owing to its inability to handle collinearity and nonlinear relationships [5].

Emerging mixture analysis methods—including weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression [6], quantile g-computation (qcomp) and Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR)—can dissect combined exposure effects and interactions [7], yet their application to mining pollutants remains nascent [8]. Our study bridges this gap by integrating these approaches to unravel the complex mixture dynamics central to our title.

To address these gaps, this study analysed data from 3,432 Chinese coal miners, employing WQS, qcomp, and BKMR to answer the following questions: (1) Is combined pollutant exposure associated with HTN risk? (2) Which components drive this risk? By integrating these approaches, this work advances methodological frameworks for occupational mixture analysis and informs evidence-based interventions.

This cross-sectional study was conducted in compliance with the ethical standards outlined in the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. All personally identifiable information was encrypted by the investigators to ensure the protection of personal privacy. Study population

This cross-sectional study encompassed data on coal miners employed by three large mining companies in Shaanxi Province, China, between 2020 and 2023. A total of 5,607 participants were initially included. After excluding non-underground workers without occupational pollutant exposure, individuals with missing HTN data, those with incomplete exposure records, participants meeting pre-defined medical exclusion criteria, and those with substantial missing covariate information, a final cohort of 3,432 miners was retained for analysis, as illustrated in Supplementary Figure S1.

Data collection procedures

Exposure assessment

Cumulative exposure to underground air pollutants was quantified via a standardized protocol. Miners wore pre-calibrated personal sampling devices during shifts:

CD: Sensodyne respirable dust samplers (Gilian® 5000, NIOSH-compliant; accuracy: ±5%, range: 1–100 mg/m3).

CO and CO₂: Portable CO/CO₂ analyzers (Beijing Huayun, GXH-3011A; accuracy: ±2% FS, calibrated with NIST-traceable gas).

NO₂: Nitrogen dioxide analyzer (Shenzhen Keernuo, GT-1000-NO2; accuracy: ±3% reading, range: 0–20 ppm).

NO: Portable NOx analyzer (Wuhan Tianhong, TH-2001H; accuracy: ±2.5% FS, range: 0–50 ppm).

Sampling occurred over three consecutive workdays to account for intraweek variability in mining activities (e.g., blasting frequency, equipment usage). Postshift, devices were collected, and exposure durations were cross-verified with shift logs to calculate 8-h time-weighted averages (TWAs).

The exposures to other pollutants were calculated via the same method.

Outcome measurement

Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured by trained physicians via a verified electronic sphygmomanometer on the right arm at heart level. The participants sat at rest for 5 min prior to measurement. Each participant underwent three measurements at 30-s intervals, and the final SBP and DBP were recorded as the average of the last two readings. According to the 2023 Chinese guidelines for the management of HTN, HTN is defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or a diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg.

Covariate collection

Covariates included: age (years), occupational tenure (work age, years), Body Mass Index (BMI) (kg/m2, calculated as weight divided by height squared), noise exposure (dB[A]), behavioral factors (smoking: yes/no; drinking: yes/no), demographic characteristics (ethnicity: Han/other, marital status: never married/married, education level: below high school/high school or equivalent/above high school); socioeconomic status (annual household income: < 100,000/100,000–150,000/150,000–200,000/> 200,000 CNY), occupational patterns (shift work: yes/no), and comorbidities (hyperlipidemia: yes/no; diabetes: yes/no). Hyperlipidemia was defined as triglyceride (TG) ≥ 1.7 mmol/L or total cholesterol (TC) ≥ 5.18 mmol/L. Diabetes was defined as fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or 2-h postprandial blood glucose (2hPG) ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or physician's diagnosis based on these criteria.

Data quality control

All field researchers received unified training and utilized precoded electronic forms to ensure variable definition consistency and formatting accuracy. Daily logic checks were performed to identify outliers (e.g., systolic blood pressure > 250 mmHg) and contradictory entries (e.g., smoking status inconsistent with pack-year records), with immediate verification against source documents.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed via R software version 4.4.1. Descriptive statistics included frequencies (%) for categorical variables (compared via χ2 test), mean ± standard (SD) deviation for normally distributed continuous variables (compared via t-test), and median (P25, P75) for nonnormally distributed variables (compared via Wilcoxon rank-sum test). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To evaluate associations between underground air pollutants and HTN, multivariate logistic regression models were employed. Pollutants (CD, CO, CO₂, NO, NO₂) were categorized into quartiles, with the lowest quartile as the reference. Model 1 estimated crude associations, while Model 2 adjusted for age, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, marital status, education level, shift work duration, and hyperlipidemia. Missing covariates were assigned to a separate “missing” category to minimize data loss.

WQS regression (implemented via the R package gWQS) was used to assess the combined effect of pollutant mixtures. The WQS index quantified cumulative exposure burden, with pollutant weights reflecting their relative contributions to HTN risk. To address nonlinear and interactive effects, BKMR was applied using the bkmr package, estimating exposure–response surfaces while fixing other pollutants at median concentrations. Finally, qcomp (R package qgcomp) relaxed the directional homogeneity assumption (i.e., allowing pollutants to exert opposing effects) and estimated the net effect of a simultaneous one-quantile increase in all pollutants.

Discussion

Our study provides compelling evidence that exposure to underground air pollutants, including CD, CO, CO₂, NO, and NO2, is significantly associated with an increased risk of HTN. After adjusting for covariates such as age, BMI, and smoking status, multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed dose–response relationships between individual pollutants and HTN. Further application of advanced statistical methods—WQS regression, qcomp, and BKMR—consistently demonstrated positive correlations between co-exposure to pollutant mixtures and HTN risk. Notably, CO and NO2 were identified as having the highest weights in the WQS and qcomp models, underscoring their significant contributions to HTN risk. These findings align with previous studies indicating that multipollutant mixtures amplify cardiovascular damage through synergistic effects [9] [10]. However, prior studies [11, 12] primarily focused on the general population, our research highlights the unique composition of air pollutants in underground coal mines, illustrating their specific impact on HTN.

The dominant roles of CO and NO₂ can be explained by multiple biological mechanisms. CO consistently showed strong positive associations with HTN across all models. The role of CO in HTN is well documented, with studies indicating that environmental exposure to CO is significantly associated with increased blood pressure [13–15]. In coal mines, CO originates primarily from coal oxidation, blasting operations, and the operation of mining equipment [16, 17]. CO impacts vascular function through multiple pathways: (1) by inhibiting endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) [18], thereby reducing the production of the vasodilator NO, and simultaneously activating soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC), leading to elevated cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) levels and sustained vascular smooth muscle contraction [19, 20]. (2) long-term low-level CO exposure increases peripheral resistance by altering erythrocyte deformability and blood viscosity [21, 22]. Additionally, CO disrupts the cytochrome system [23], which is integral to mitochondrial function. This interference results in impaired mitochondrial function and disrupts fat metabolism, further contributing to metabolic and cardiovascular disturbances [24].

Our findings align with the literature that highlights the detrimental effects of NO2 exposure on cardiovascular health [25–27]. The primary source [28] of NO2 in coal mines is exhaust from diesel-powered mining equipment. NO₂ promotes HTN by inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and activating the NF-κB pathway, which triggers the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α), leading to endothelial dysfunction [29, 30]. The combined oxidative stress effects of CO and NO₂ may synergistically amplify vascular damage [31, 32]. While our study did not measure biomarkers of inflammation or oxidative stress directly, the consistency of the effects of NO₂ across the models supports its mechanistic plausibility. This synergy is supported by our models: when both CO and NO₂ were at high exposure quantiles, HTN risk significantly increased.

It is worth noting that in our study, even the highest levels of individual exposure to CO and NO2 did not exceed the internationally recommended allowable exposure limits (PELs) [33]. This emphasizes the necessity of formulating stricter exposure restrictions from the perspective of preventing hypertension and cardiovascular diseases. Furthermore, referring to the study by Dominski [34] that linked short-term NO₂ exposure to the risk of stroke, we suggest incorporating the NO₂ threshold for specific mines into the revision of occupational health standards to reduce cardiovascular risks. Furthermore, from the perspective of preventing cardiovascular diseases, we suggest that in addition to focusing on the PELs, the cumulative exposure of CO and NO to miners should also be paid attention to. We found that when the cumulative exposure of CO reached 32 mg/m3-years and that of NO2 reached 4.56 mg/m3-years, the association with hypertension was significantly stronger with OR values of 2.301 and 1.678 respectively. It should attract attention.

Interestingly, while CO and NO₂ emerged as dominant contributors to HTN risk, CO₂ also played a notable role. However, its weight in the qcomp model was slightly lower than that in the WQS model—a discrepancy that may reflect methodological differences between the two approaches. Existing studies indicate that elevated CO₂ levels (often exceeding 5,000 ppm in mines) activate the sympathetic nervous system via hypercapnia, increasing blood pressure [35, 36]. Prolonged exposure to high CO₂ concentrations in poorly ventilated mining environments likely exacerbates these physiological responses, necessitating targeted mitigation strategies.

In our analysis, both logistic regression and BKMR models suggested a positive association between nitric oxide (NO) and HTN, which contrasts with some studies [37]. This discrepancy may arise from biological differences between exogenous NO (derived from diesel exhaust) and endogenous NO (endothelial-derived). Exogenous NO can be converted to peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻) under oxidative conditions, causing endothelial dysfunction through tyrosine residue nitration [38]. In contrast, endogenous NO, produced by eNOS, exerts vasoprotective effects [37]. Therefore, we speculate that there is a nonlinear association between environmental NO and blood pressure. Among them, a low concentration may lower blood pressure through vasodilation, while a high concentration will cause oxidative damage. These findings are supported by studies on NO pollution [39–42].

Although CD exhibited a weaker correlation with HTNin in our analysis than with gaseous pollutants did, this finding contrasts with prior studies linking CD exposure to pulmonary HTN [43], which may reflect differences in disease mechanisms or particle composition [44]. Nevertheless, logistic regression revealed a statistically significant positive association between CD and HTN (OR: 1.65, 95% CI: 1.25–2.10), suggesting that its role may be modulated by exposure to other pollutants. This underscores the need to investigate the synergistic effects of CDs within multipollutant mixtures. Given its pervasive presence in mining environments, CD should remain a priority in occupational health policies, even if its individual contribution to HTN appears modest.

The absence of negative weights in the qcomp model (all pollutants with positive contributions) may reflect the unique exposure characteristics of underground coal mining environments. First, confined and poorly ventilated workspaces are likely to lead to chronic co-exposure to high concentrations of multiple pollutants, which may act synergistically through shared biological pathways such as oxidative stress and systemic inflammation [25]. For example, both CO and NO₂ are known to impair vascular function by reducing nitric oxide bioavailability and inducing endothelial dysfunction [45, 46], potentially amplifying their combined effects. Second, the lack of antagonistic interactions could be attributed to the absence of protective factors (e.g., antioxidants) in such occupational settings, allowing cumulative damage from pollutants to dominate. Additionally, the homogeneity in exposure directionality might stem from the study’s focus on a population with prolonged, high-intensity exposure, where even pollutants with weaker individual effects collectively contribute to HTN risk. These findings underscore the importance of holistic interventions targeting multiple pollutants in mining environments.

Advantages and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, this study is the first to examine the relationship between co-exposure to underground air pollution mixtures and HTN in coal miners. Second, the use of four distinct statistical methods allows for a comprehensive evaluation of the effects of these pollutant mixtures on HTN risk, enhancing the reliability of our results. However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, as reverse causation cannot be ruled out. Second, despite rigorous protocols, exposure measurement errors may arise from intermittent sampler calibration, device placement variability, or incomplete shift logs. Third, unmeasured confounders—including dietary sodium intake, genetic predisposition, and physical activity—could bias effect estimates. Fourth, the modest odds ratios suggest small effect sizes, potentially due to unaccounted pollutants or residual mixture complexity. Fifth, focusing on five pollutants may overlook other hazardous components in mines. Finally, missing covariate data were addressed via a “missing” category but may introduce bias.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our research revealed a significant association between exposure to underground air pollution mixtures and an elevated risk of HTN, with CO and NO₂ identified as primary contributors. The results underscore the need for targeted ventilation strategies, real-time air quality monitoring, and stricter enforcement of exposure limits for gaseous pollutants in mining operations. Future studies should explore the prospective relationships between exposure to these air pollution mixtures and HTN and clarify the complex mechanisms linking these factors to HTN to inform holistic prevention strategies.
