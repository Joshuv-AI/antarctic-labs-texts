Two cases of rare immune-related cystitis glandularis induced by tislelizumab
Authors: Gui-Yan Tang, Liang-Feng Zhou, Hui Huang, Jie Hu, Qiu-Qiu Chen, Chun-Qiao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the increasing clinical application of immune checkpoint inhibitors in cancer therapy, immune-related adverse events have drawn substantial attention. Immune checkpoint inhibitors exert antitumor effects by activating T-cell immune responses; however, they may also trigger autoimmune-like reactions across various organ systems. Although immune-related adverse events involving the urinary tract commonly present as nephritis or prostatitis, cystitis glandularis is rarely reported. This report describes the cases of two patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer who developed symptoms, including urinary frequency, urinary urgency, dysuria, and hematuria following treatment with tislelizumab combined with chemotherapy. Cystoscopic biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of cystitis glandularis. After withholding immunotherapy and implementing symptomatic management, both patients experienced complete symptom resolution. One patient subsequently underwent an immune checkpoint inhibitor rechallenge with tislelizumab without recurrence of urinary manifestations. Clinicians should be aware of the risk of immune checkpoint inhibitor–associated cystitis glandularis. Timely diagnosis and appropriate intervention facilitate effective symptom management.Keywords: Tislelizumab, immune checkpoint inhibitor, immune-mediated cystitis glandularis, adverse events, case report

Introduction

Immune-related cystitis glandularis is an uncommon immune-related adverse event (irAE) in clinical practice. This condition not only significantly impairs patients’ quality of life but may also adversely affect the efficacy and continuity of subsequent antitumor treatment. 1 Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) (such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) inhibitors) primarily exert their antitumor effects by activating T-cell-mediated immune responses. However, in some patients, they may trigger autoimmune-like reactions against normal tissues, potentially involving multiple organ systems, including the urinary tract. Among the reported urinary irAEs, nephritis and prostatitis are more frequently observed, whereas cystitis, particularly cystitis glandularis, characterized by glandular metaplasia of the urothelium, is rarely documented. Cystitis glandularis is a chronic inflammatory lesion of the bladder mucosa. Its pathogenesis is not only associated with long-term chronic irritants (such as infection or calculi) but may also be linked to autoimmune responses. 1 It is hypothesized that during immunotherapy, aberrantly activated T cells induce immune-mediated damage to the bladder mucosa, thereby triggering metaplastic changes in the mucosal epithelium, ultimately leading to the development of cystitis glandularis. This article reports two cases of cystitis glandularis that occurred in patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) following treatment with tislelizumab combined with chemotherapy. By systematically describing their clinical presentations, diagnostic and therapeutic courses, and outcomes, this study aimed to provide clinical insights for the clinical recognition and management of this rare adverse event.

Case presentation

Case 1

A man in his late 60 s presented to Guilin People's Hospital in early 2023 with severe cough and hoarseness. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed a space-occupying lesion in the left lower lobe with obstructive pneumonia, multiple nodules in both lungs, and enlarged hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes; lumbar spine and pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed abnormal signals in the left ilium and femoral head; both adrenal glands were thickened, suggesting metastatic tumors. Pathological examination of the left lung biopsy specimen showed atypical cells with infiltrative growth; immunohistochemical staining results of the atypical cells were as follows: cytokeratin pan (CKpan) (+), cytokeratin 7 (CK7) (+), thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF-1) (mostly positive, +), NapsinA (mostly positive, +), cytokeratin 5/6 (CK5/6) (focal positive), P40 (focal positive), SWI/SNF-related, matrix-associated, actin-dependent regulator of chromatin, subfamily A, member 4 (SMARCA4/Brg-1) (+), neural cell adhesion molecule (CD56) (−), and Ki67 (+, approximately 40%, focal positive). Genetic testing identified a heterozygous missense mutation in the KRAS gene at codon G12C in exon 2, with a variant allele frequency of 29.13%. Serum tumor markers were significantly elevated; carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level was 178.00 ng/mL, carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125) level was 91.40 U/mL, and carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) level was 439.00 U/mL. The patient had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 1, with normal complete blood count and hepatic and renal function. Before receiving immunotherapy, the patient had no history of recurrent urinary tract infections, nephrolithiasis, or benign prostatic hyperplasia (Figure 1(a)). The patient was comprehensively diagnosed with NSCLC (stage T4N3M1c IVB) complicated with pulmonary, mediastinal lymph node, and adrenal metastases.

The patient initially received first-line combination therapy with five cycles of lobaplatin (40 mg on day 1), nab-paclitaxel (200 mg on days 1–8), and tislelizumab (200 mg on day 1) plus bevacizumab (800 mg q3w), and achieved partial response after 2 cycles; however, treatment was temporarily interrupted in August 2023 due to immune-related pneumonitis and fever; it was reinitiated in September 2023 with a modified regimen of nab-paclitaxel (200 mg on days 1–8) and tislelizumab (200 mg on day 1) plus bevacizumab (800 mg). 2 Following the occurrence of ICIs-induced adverse events, subsequent treatment was switched to bevacizumab maintenance monotherapy (800 mg intravenous infusion). In January 2024, second-line therapy was initiated with bevacizumab (800 mg) plus etoposide soft capsules (50 mg on days 1–14), with the etoposide administration duration adjusted to 10 days per cycle due to chemotherapy-related anorexia; stable disease (SD) was confirmed in May 2024. Chemotherapy was subsequently discontinued according to patient preference, and bevacizumab (800 mg) maintenance monotherapy was continued. In August 2024, persistently elevated serum creatinine levels necessitated bevacizumab dose reduction to 600 mg and subsequent treatment suspension. From February to August 2025, maintenance therapy with reduced-dose bevacizumab (600 mg, q3w) was resumed. The patient exhibited SD at the final follow-up (Table 1).

The patient received tislelizumab combined with chemotherapy starting from the first cycle of chemotherapy in April 2023. In October 2023, the patient developed urinary tract irritation symptoms, including increased urinary frequency, urinary urgency, and hematuria. Urinary sediment microscopy and analysis revealed marked abnormalities in multiple indicators, including leukocyte esterase (2+); occult blood (3+); urine protein (2+); white blood cell count, 3+/HP; and red blood cell count, 2+/HP (Table 1). To identify the underlying cause, we preformed plain and contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen. The examination demonstrated thickening of the bladder wall, accompanied with mild bilateral hydronephrosis, mild bilateral ureteral dilatation, and ureteral wall thickening (Figure 1(b)), which were consistent with manifestations of immune-related adverse reactions. Cystitis was clinically suspected; however, the patient refused to undergo cystoscopy. Therefore, targeted treatment was administered for suspected immune-related cystitis and other irAEs; intravenous methylprednisolone was administered at a dose of 120 mg per day for three consecutive days, followed by a tapering regimen of 80 mg per day for 3 days, 40 mg per day for 3 days, and 20 mg per day for 4 days. Oral prednisone tablets were administered at 30 mg/day (6 tablets/day) for 1 week and then tapered to 20 mg/day (4 tablets/day) for another week. Subsequently, the dose was reduced by one tablet per week until complete drug discontinuation (Table 1). After 1 month of continuous treatment, the urinary tract irritation symptoms alleviated (Figure 1(c)).

Notably, the patient had previously declined urological examinations such as cystoscopy, resulting in delayed evaluation, which led to prolonged recurrent cystitis symptoms, persistent abnormal urinalysis results, and a gradual increase in serum creatinine levels. Cystoscopy was performed in January 2025 after the patient presented with intestinal and ureteral exudation. Multiple bladder biopsies revealed chronic active inflammation of the mucosa with inflammatory granuloma formation and epithelial denudation. Pathological examination of bladder mucosal lesions showed typical Brunn nests in the sections (Figure 1(d)), with columnar epithelium in the center of the nests, accompanied with neutrophil infiltration and epithelial desquamation, which was consistent with the diagnosis of cystitis glandularis. Combined with the persistent irAEs, no prior history of urologic disease, absence of infectious signs on imaging, and negative midstream urine culture, the final diagnosis was confirmed as ICIs-induced immune-related cystitis. Intravesical instillation of epirubicin (50 mg once weekly) was initiated for eight cycles. The patient's overall condition was stable at the time of writing this report (Figure 1(e)).

Case 2

A man in his late 70s presented to Guilin People's Hospital with intermittent cough and blood-streaked sputum. Positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET–CT) revealed a space-occupying lesion in the left upper lung lobe, together with a hypermetabolic focus with increased deoxyglucose uptake in the anterior segment of the left upper lobe adjacent to the hilum. Flexible bronchoscopy combined with biopsy and bronchoalveolar lavage showed keratinized atypical squamous epithelial cells, ciliated columnar epithelial cells, macrophages, and neutrophils. Immunohistochemistry indicated squamous cell origin, with positive expression of cytokeratin (CK), CK5/6, Ki67, and P40, whereas TTF-1, NapsinA, and CD56 were negative. The patient was ultimately diagnosed with left lung squamous cell carcinoma (T4N0M0, stage IIIA).

In accordance with the clinical guidelines for first-line treatment of NSCLC, the patient initially received platinum-based docetaxel chemotherapy. 2 Nevertheless, recurrent disease progression was noted after 4 cycles of docetaxel treatment, indicating failure of first-line platinum-based chemotherapy. Chest CT performed in December 2023 revealed enlargement of the hyperdense mass in the anterior segment of the left upper lobe. The patient developed progressive disease after a progression-free survival (PFS) of 5 months. We initiated oral anlotinib (12 mg on days 1–14, q3w). Meanwhile, definitive intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) was administered to the primary pulmonary lesion between January and March 2024. The radiation prescription was 66 Gy in 33 fractions over 58 days to cover 95% of the planning gross target volume and 45 Gy in 25 fractions over 48 days to cover 95% of the planning target volume. Concurrent chemotherapy, comprising cisplatin (40 mg on days 1–8) and etoposide (80 mg on days 1–5) plus continuous anlotinib (12 mg on days 1–14) was administered during radiotherapy to enhance the antineoplastic efficacy. During this period, the patient experienced grade 2 myelosuppression, mild hepatic dysfunction, fever, and cough with sputum production, resulting in interruption of radiotherapy and chemotherapy for >2 weeks. After the completion of radiotherapy, the systemic treatment regimen was adjusted. From May to July 2024, anlotinib (12 mg on days 1–14, q3w) was combined sequentially with etoposide (100 mg on days 1–3, q3w), and nedaplatin (100 mg on day 1, q3w). Subsequently, maintenance therapy consisting of anlotinib (12 mg on days 1–14, q3w) and tislelizumab (200 mg on day 1, q3w) plus capecitabine (0.5 g twice daily) was administered from August to September 2024 (Table 1).

The patient had no history of urinary calculi, recurrent urinary tract infection, or benign prostatic hyperplasia before immunotherapy. Pre-treatment abdominal CT showed multiple small bilateral renal cysts and prostatic calcification, without additional remarkable lesions (Figure 2(a)). During the continuous administration of the combined antitumor regimen in October 2024, the patient developed severe urinary tract irritation symptoms, including urinary frequency, urinary urgency, dysuria, and mild gross hematuria. Urinalysis indicated leukocyte esterase (±); occult blood (3+); vitamin C (1+); white blood cell count, 27.3/μL (reference range: 0–9.9/μL); and red blood cell count, 401.3/μL (reference range: 0–8.2/μL) (Table 1). Multiple consecutive urine cultures were sterile. No significant abnormalities were noted in routine blood tests, routine stool tests, hepatic and renal function examinations, or serum electrolyte levels. Early during tislelizumab therapy, the association between cystitis glandularis and immunotherapy was not considered. Only symptomatic treatments, including tamsulosin (0.2 mg orally once daily) and tolterodine (2 mg po bid), were administered. However, high urinary frequency, urinary urgency, and dysuria persisted, which severely compromised the patient's quality of life. Subsequent abdominal CT revealed decreased parenchymal density of the right kidney; exudative changes around the bilateral kidneys, ureters, and bladder; uneven thickening of the entire bladder wall (maximal thickness: 14 mm) (Figure 2(b)); and bilateral ureteral dilatation, which was suggestive of urinary tract infection. The patient underwent cystoscopy and bladder biopsy. Pathological examination of the bladder biopsy specimens revealed typical Brunn's nests (Figure 2(c)), prominent dilatation of small blood vessels in the bladder mucosa, inflammatory cell infiltration predominantly consisting of neutrophils with focal lymphocytes, and glandular epithelial metaplasia with focal lymphocytic infiltration, consistent with the pathological changes observed in cystitis glandularis. Immunohistochemical staining revealed positive CK expression, weakly positive p53 expression, and low Ki-67 proliferative index in the epithelial tissues (Table 1, Figure 2(c)). At admission, the patient was evaluated to exclude hydronephrosis, nephrolithiasis, active infection, benign prostatic hyperplasia, and other concurrent diseases. Pre-immunotherapy urinalysis revealed negative leukocyte esterase, occult blood, and vitamin C, with no detectable leukocytes or erythrocytes, and no definite evidence of infection or tumor metastasis. Therefore, a definitive diagnosis of cystitis glandularis was established based on comprehensive assessment, which was strongly suspected to be ICI-induced. Subsequently, anti-infective therapy (etimicin injection, 200 mg once daily intravenously, combined with cefmetazole sodium, 1 g twice daily via intravenous drip) was initiated as adjunctive treatment, and immunotherapy was discontinued. Meanwhile, the antitumor treatment regimen was adjusted to include anlotinib (12 mg on days 1–14, q3w) combined with capecitabine (0.5 g twice daily), and the urinary tract irritation symptoms gradually resolved (Figure 2(d)).

In January 2025, a follow-up chest CT confirmed disease progression. The patient received combination therapy with etoposide (0.4 g civ 96 h) plus endostatin (150 mg civ120 h, q3w) from January to February. Disease progression was identified at the end of February 2025, with a PFS of >2 months. After comprehensive and careful evaluation, the patient's urinary symptoms gradually improved following a 2-month tislelizumab washout period. After weighing the risks and benefits, a second-line regimen consisting of gemcitabine (1.4 g on day 1, q3w) and cisplatin (30 mg on days 1–3, q3w) plus tislelizumab (200 mg on day 1, q3w) was reinitiated. Notably, no recurrent cystitis was observed after this immune rechallenge, indicating that the previous irAE was well controlled, and subsequent restaging confirmed SD.

Discussion

By systematically analyzing the clinical data of two patients with advanced NSCLC who developed rare immune-related cystitis glandularis following treatment with tislelizumab-based combination therapy, this report provides crucial empirical evidence regarding the clinical understanding of rare urologic adverse events associated with ICIs. The core findings of this study are summarized as follows. First, tislelizumab combined with chemotherapy or targeted therapy may induce cystitis glandularis, which is typically characterized by urinary irritative symptoms, including increased urinary frequency, urinary urgency, dysuria, and hematuria. Radiological examinations may reveal uneven thickening of the bladder wall, whereas pathological findings indicate glandular metaplasia of the transitional epithelium accompanied with lymphocytic infiltration. Second, there is no consistent temporal pattern for the onset of such adverse events; they may occur several weeks to months after the initiation of immunotherapy, which is consistent with the overall temporal onset pattern of ICI-related adverse events. Third, timely discontinuation of immunotherapy and administration of symptomatic treatments (e.g. anticholinergic agents, alpha-receptor blockers, and local intravesical instillation therapy) can effectively alleviate symptoms. Notably, one of the two patients did not experience symptom recurrence when tislelizumab was reinitiated after the adverse events were well controlled. Fourth, the diagnosis of this type of cystitis glandularis requires rigorous exclusion of other organic lesions such as urinary tract infections, nephrolithiasis, tumor metastases, and radiation cystitis and must be confirmed using cystoscopy in combination with pathological biopsy. Fifth, the development of tislelizumab-related cystitis glandularis may be associated with the underlying tumor type, combination treatment regimens, and individual immune status; thus, it is necessary to perform a comprehensive assessment based on multi-dimensional factors.

The findings of this report are highly consistent with the research conducted by Zhou et al., which has indicated that ICI-associated cystitis glandularis is primarily characterized by urinary irritative symptoms, with pathological examinations revealing lymphocytic infiltration; moreover, symptoms generally resolve following ICI discontinuation. 3 This phenomenon may be attributed to the core mechanism of action of ICIs, which act by blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway and activating the antineoplastic activity of T cells while disrupting immune tolerance, thereby causing immune cells to abnormally attack normal tissues and organs. As a critical component of the immune barrier, the urothelial cells of the bladder mucosa may express PD-L1. Following ICI treatment, excessive T cell activation triggers local immune–inflammatory responses, and chronic inflammation induces mucosal metaplasia, ultimately leading to the development of cystitis glandularis. In addition, Li et al., based on their literature analysis of adverse reactions to tislelizumab, have reported that this agent can induce various novel adverse events not listed in its package insert. 4 The cystitis glandularis reported in the present study further corroborates the complexity and diversity of the adverse reaction profile of tislelizumab.

Notably, the findings of this report differ to a certain extent from the traditional pathogenic mechanism theory for cystitis glandularis. According to the conventional view, cystitis glandularis is mostly associated with local factors such as long-term chronic infection, irritation due to calculi, and urinary tract obstruction, whereas autoimmune factors are only mentioned as secondary predisposing factors. In contrast, neither of the two patients in this study had a definite history of underlying urinary tract diseases. There was a clear temporal correlation between the occurrence of adverse events and the administration of tislelizumab, and common predisposing factors such as infection and calculi were excluded. These findings suggest that immune dysregulation induced by ICIs is an independent etiological factor. The core reason for this difference lies in the fact that etiological studies on traditional cystitis glandularis have mainly focused on chronic local irritants, whereas ICI-related cystitis glandularis originates from targeted tissue damage following the disruption of systemic immune tolerance. Its pathogenic mechanism emphasizes the specific attack of abnormally activated immune cells on the bladder mucosa, representing immune-mediated metaplastic inflammation. 5 Rare mucosal immune injuries such as cystitis glandularis induced by ICIs may be closely associated with the abnormal activation of the biological regulatory network of PD-L1/PD-L2. 6 PD-L1 (CD274) and PD-L2 (CD273) genes are located at 9p24.1. In the inflammatory microenvironment, their constitutive high expression can be achieved through chromatin topologically associating domain remodeling and super-enhancer activation. Cytokines such as interferon gamma (IFN-γ) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) activate transcription factors, including interferon regulatory factor 1 (IRF1) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), through the Janus kinase–signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK–STAT) pathway, which may further upregulate PD-L1 transcription. Meanwhile, increased stability mediated by the long 3′ untranslated region (3’-UTR) of PD-L1 messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) as well as enhanced protein stability regulated by N-linked (N-)glycosylation and CMTM6/4, are jointly involved in maintaining high PD-L1 expression in bladder urothelial cells. Based on the above molecular regulatory characteristics, immune checkpoint blockade may lead to excessive T-cell activation and break peripheral immune tolerance. Activated T cells then aberrantly target PD-L1-rich bladder mucosal epithelium, resulting in sustained immune inflammation. Under chronic inflammatory stimulation, bladder urothelium may undergo glandular metaplasia, which may eventually result in immune-related cystitis glandularis. This process is consistent with the injury pathway of “immune inflammation–mucosal injury–metaplastic hyperplasia” and provides a potential molecular explanation for cystitis glandularis in our patient after tislelizumab treatment. This mechanistic difference also explains why some patients did not experience symptom recurrence after immunotherapy reinitiation, which may be related to the immune adaptation to ICIs, repair of previous inflammatory damage, and optimization of treatment regimens (e.g. dose adjustment of combined chemotherapeutic agents).

Furthermore, this report further advances the mechanistic research on ICI-related adverse events at the theoretical level and enriches the understanding of the multi-organ injury profile of ICIs. According to conventional theories, ICI-related urologic irAEs are mainly dominated by nephritis and prostatitis, whereas cystitis glandularis has not been included in the core research scope due to its rarity. 4 Through pathological confirmation, these two cases demonstrate that ICIs can induce bladder mucosal metaplasia and propose a pathogenic pathway hypothesis of “immune inflammation–mucosal injury–metaplastic hyperplasia,” which provides a new perspective for understanding the specific injury mechanism of ICIs on the urinary tract. Meanwhile, the absence of symptom recurrence in one patient after the rechallenge of tislelizumab suggests that ICI-related cystitis glandularis is not an absolute contraindication to treatment. This challenges the conventional notion that “permanent discontinuation of ICIs is required once severe urologic irAEs occur” and provides theoretical support for individualized treatment decisions. In addition, this report reveals the potential association between tislelizumab-based combination therapy regimens (e.g. combined with chemotherapy or targeted drugs) and the occurrence of adverse events, expands the dimensions of risk assessment for ICI-related adverse events, and lays a foundation for the subsequent construction of multi-factor risk prediction models.

This study provides several specific recommendations for the safe clinical use of ICI treatment. In terms of medical practice, clinicians should strengthen comprehensive pre–treatment assessment before ICI administration, including baseline examinations such as urinalysis and renal bladder ultrasound. Special attention should be paid to patients with a history of urinary tract diseases. During treatment, routine monitoring of urinary symptoms in patients is necessary. Once symptoms such as high urinary frequency and urgency are observed, urinalysis, abdominal CT, and cystoscopy should be promptly performed to prevent delayed diagnosis and misdiagnosis. For confirmed cases, immunotherapy should be immediately suspended, and stratified management should be implemented according to symptom severity. Mild symptoms can be treated with symptomatic therapy, including anticholinergic drugs such as tolterodine tartrate; moderate-to-severe symptoms may require local intravesical instillation of chemotherapeutic agents (e.g. epirubicin), and short-term use of glucocorticoids may be required to inhibit immune inflammation when necessary. At the policy level, drug regulatory authorities should promote the update of ICI package inserts, include cystitis glandularis in the category of potential adverse reactions, and improve relevant clinical practice guidelines. Medical institutions should establish a special monitoring system for ICI-related adverse events to facilitate centralized reporting and data sharing of rare adverse events. In terms of medical education, the identification and management of rare ICI-related adverse events should be incorporated into the continuing education content of relevant departments such as medical oncology and urology. The diagnostic and therapeutic capabilities of clinicians should be improved through case discussions, multidisciplinary consultations, and other forms.

This study has certain limitations that should be viewed objectively. In terms of sample characteristics, only two patients were enrolled in this study. The small sample size and the fact that both participants were male and had advanced NSCLC limited the availability of data on female patients and those with other tumor types. This may have compromised the representativeness of the study findings, making it difficult to reflect the incidence characteristics and differences of tislelizumab-related cystitis glandularis across diverse populations and tumor types. This study was a retrospective case series without a prospective controlled design, which precluded the definitive establishment of a causal relationship between tislelizumab administration and the development of cystitis glandularis, as well as hindered the evaluation of how different treatment regimens affect the prognosis of this adverse event. Meanwhile, owing to the relatively short follow-up duration, there was insufficient data to support conclusions regarding the long-term prognosis of patients and the long-term outcomes of cystitis glandularis. In addition, this study did not investigate the role of genetic factors such as gene polymorphisms in the occurrence of this adverse event, potentially missing important predictive biomarkers for risk stratification.

Despite these limitations, this study still provides important references for clinical research on rare ICI-related urinary tract adverse events. In the future, multicenter, large-sample prospective cohort studies involving patients with different tumor types, sexes, and treatment regimens should be conducted to systematically analyze the incidence, risk factors, and prognostic factors of tislelizumab-related cystitis glandularis. Technical approaches such as immunohistochemistry and gene sequencing could be employed to detect the expression levels of PD-1/PD-L1 in bladder mucosal tissue, distribution of T cell subsets, and changes in related cytokines to elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying its pathogenesis. Meanwhile, it is necessary to construct risk prediction models to screen high-risk populations and develop individualized prevention strategies. In addition, randomized controlled trials should be performed to compare the therapeutic effects of different intervention regimens (e.g. glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, and local therapy) on ICI-related cystitis glandularis to provide high-level evidence support for clinical practice. The reporting of this study conforms to the Case Report (CARE) guidelines. 7

Conclusion

This study confirms that tislelizumab-based combination therapy may induce rare immune-related cystitis glandularis, which is associated with the disruption of immune tolerance by ICIs and is typically characterized by lower urinary tract symptoms. After discontinuing immunotherapy, administering symptomatic treatment or performing local therapy can effectively alleviate symptoms, and some patients do not experience recurrence upon undergoing an ICI rechallenge. Clinicians need to be vigilant about such adverse events, strengthen toxicity monitoring and differential diagnosis, and optimize diagnosis and treatment strategies through multidisciplinary collaboration to ensure safe and effective administration of cancer immunotherapy.
