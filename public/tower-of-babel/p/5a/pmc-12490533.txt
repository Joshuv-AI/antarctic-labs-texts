Metabolic syndrome and the risk of incident heart failure: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Hongye Wei, Ziyi Qiu, Ziting Gao, Yajing Wei, Jun Xiao, Wuqing Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The link between metabolic syndrome (MetS) and multiple cardiovascular diseases has been reported, while the role of cumulative burden of MetS and its specific clusters in heart failure (HF) is lacking.•The increasing cumulative burden of MetS was significantly associated with a growing risk of HF. Although the clusters consisting of increased blood pressure were associated with a higher incidence of HF, this risk was attenuated by the component of increased triglycerides.•This study provided additional information to understand the role of MetS as a potential target for HF prevention.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a life-threatening syndrome with high morbidity and mortality [1,2], and the five-year survival rate was reported to be <50 % [3]. HF has emerged as a global pandemic with the estimated incidence of 1–2 % in developed countries, which is expected to double by 2060 [4]. Given the huge public health and socio-economic burden of HF, it is important to clarify the risk factors of HF and identify population at high risk of HF for early preventive strategies [5].

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) was characterized by a cluster of interrelated cardiometabolic risk factors, including central obesity, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia. Along with the increase in these cardiometabolic risk factors, the prevalence of MetS is steadily rising. MetS has been reported to be associated with an elevated risk of multiple cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) in epidemiological studies, while the evidence of the influence on HF risk was limited [6]. However, a number of studies suggested that specific components of MetS played a role in the risk of developing HF, for example, the elevated HF risk associated with hypertension or diabetes. These MetS components often coexist and interact with each other, resulting in different clusters of MetS and increasing cardiometabolic risk burden. Previous studies indicated that different clusters of MetS might have various impacts on cardiovascular events, but how the cumulative burden or the specific cluster of MetS components affects the risk of HF is largely unknown [[7], [8], [9]].

This study aimed to clarify the association between the cumulative burden of MetS components and HF risk, as well as explored if specific clusters of MetS components had differential impact on the risk of HF.

Methods

Study population

This is a population-based prospective cohort study from the UK Biobank (UKB), that was implemented among >0.5 million adults aged 40 to 69 years. 22 centers recruited participants between March 2006 and December 2010 from the United Kingdom [10,11]. Participants completed online or/and verbal questionnaires, physical measurements and biological samples collection. Participants were excluded if they: 1) had missing data of MetS components; 2) had a history of HF at baseline; 3) were followed up for <180 days. After exclusion, a total of 388,827 participants were included in the final study.

Assessment of exposure

The MetS components were defined using the Harmonized Criteria as following [12]: (1) increased waist circumference: ≥88 cm for women or ≥102 cm for men; (2) increased blood pressure: ≥130 mmHg systolic blood pressure and/or ≥85 mmHg diastolic blood pressure and/or self-reported hypertension and/or antihypertensive drugs use; (3) increased blood glucose: ≥5.6 mmol/L fasting blood glucose level and/or self-reported diabetes and/or antidiabetic drugs use; (4)reduced high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol: 1.3 mmol/L for women or 1.0 mmol/L for men; (5) increased triglycerides: ≥1.7 mmol/L. All components were measured at baseline, in which waist circumference was measured using a 200-cm tape measure (SECA) at the smallest part of the trunk; blood pressure was measured twice consecutively by trained nurses using electronic blood pressure monitor 705IT (Omron) and the mean of two measures was used for definition; serum lipids and blood glucose were measured through the Beckman Coulter AU5800. However, blood samples were collected randomly instead of fasting blood samples in UK Biobank.

The exposures considered in our study included specific components of MetS, cumulative burden of MetS components, and specific clusters of MetS components. Specific component of MetS was defined as the presence of altered component of interest, regardless of the status of other components; cumulative burden of MetS components was defined as the sum of the presence of altered MetS components; specific clusters of MetS components was defined by different combinations of the individual components of MetS.

Assessment of outcome

The outcome was incident HF during the follow-up period, which was identified by using the International Classification of Disease 10 of I50 from category of “First occurrence” (Category ID: 1712) with Field IDs of 131355. The “First occurrence” Category in UK Biobank was derived from self-reported medical conditions, primary health care, hospital admission data and death registry data. The followed up began at the date of baseline assessment until the date of HF occurrence, death, loss-to-follow, or the end of study (May 20, 2022), whichever came first.

Assessment of covariates

Confounders that are known to be associated with MetS and HF were assessed in the study. Age at baseline (continuous) was calculated from the date of birth and the date of beginning in the assessment center. Sex was recorded as female or male. Race/ethnicity was categorized according to the UK Biobank definitions as White, Mixed, Asian or Asian British, Black or Black British, and Other ethnic group. Education was categorized as college/university degree or other professional qualification, second/final stage of secondary education, first stage of secondary education, work-related practical qualifications, or others were captured from the questionnaire. Socioeconomic status was measured by Townsend deprivation index (TDI), which was categorized into quartiles and unknown group. Smoking status was classified as never, former, current and unknown. Alcohol intake frequency was classified as daily, three or four times a week, once or twice a week, one to three times a month, special occasions only, never and unknown. Physical activity was evaluated via the International Physical Activity Questionnaire using summed metabolic equivalent (MET) minutes per week for all activity, and was categorized by quartile of total MET. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated from measured weight in kilograms being divided and measured height in meters squared. C‑reactive protein (CRP) was measured at baseline as a biomarker of low‑grade inflammation and was analyzed both as a continuous variable and in categories (≤3 mg/L vs >3 mg/L) for stratified analyses. Common comorbidities associated with MetS and HF, including coronary heart disease (CHD) and stroke, were collected from self-reported at baseline, inpatient hospital records and primary care records.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics are shown as frequency and percentage for categorical variables and as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) or χ2 test was used for comparison between groups with different cumulative burden of MetS. For ANOVA analyses demonstrating significant between-group differences (p < 0.05), we conducted Student-Newman-Keuls (SNK) post hoc tests to identify specific group differences. Cumulative incidence curves between groups were plotted by Kaplan–Meier methods and tested by Log-Rank.

Cox proportional hazards model was applied to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) of exposures with the incidence of HF, with individuals without altered component of MetS as the reference group. Stratified analyses were performed according to age (<45, 45–54, 55–65, >65 years old) and sex (male vs female), and CRP level (≤3 mg/L vs >3 mg/L). Interactions were tested by including a product term of the stratification factor and exposure in the model. To account for the impact of competing risk from death, Fine-gray sub-distribution hazard models of competing risk regression were performed to assess the association between exposures and risk of HF using mortality as a competing event. Both univariable and multivariable models were conducted for all analyses, and potential confounders with significant difference in distribution between groups were included in multivariable models. All data were analyzed with R version 4.1.0 and SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC). P-value of <0.05 was considered as of statistical significance.

Association between the specific component of MetS and the risk of HF

Table 2 shows differential associations of the different components with HF risk, after adjusting for confounders, there was the highest risk related to increased blood pressure (adjusted HR = 1.51, 95 % CI = 1.42–1.60), followed by increased blood glucose (adjusted HR = 1.35, 95 % CI = 1.29–1.40), increased waist circumference (adjusted HR = 1.27, 95 % CI = 1.20–1.33), and reduced HDL cholesterol (adjusted HR = 1.16, 95 % CI = 1.11–1.22), while increased triglycerides was associated with a lower risk of HF (adjusted HR = 0.89, 95 % CI = 0.85–0.92). Competing risk model observed similar results in Table S1. In stratified analyses by age, Fig. 3 and Table S3 found that the associations of increased blood pressure (adjusted HRs <45 vs 45–54 vs 55–65 vs>65: 1.63 vs 1.75 vs 1.51 vs 1.33), increased blood glucose (adjusted HRs <45 vs 45–54 vs 55–65 vs>65: 1.47 vs 1.49 vs 1.37 vs 1.30) and increased waist circumference (adjusted HRs <45 vs 45–54 vs 55–65 vs>65: 1.54 vs 1.37 vs 1.29 vs 1.22) with HF were more pronounced in younger adults, but the strength of the relation between reduced HDL cholesterol and HF risk was weaker in younger adults, and the association of increased triglycerides with HF was not significant in the youngest participants and became more evident only in older age groups. Discussion

This prospective cohort study found that the increasing cumulative burden of MetS components was significantly associated with a growing risk of HF. Among these components, increased blood pressure played a predominant role in increasing HF risk, followed by increased blood glucose, increased waist circumference, and reduced HDL cholesterol. However, increased triglycerides were related to a reduced risk of HF. Thus, analyses regarding specific clusters of MetS components found higher HF risk in the clusters with higher cumulative burden in general, besides, the clusters consisting of the component of increased blood pressure were associated with a higher incidence of HF while the clusters including the component of increased triglycerides related to a reduced risk of HF, leading to the highest risk in the cluster of PGHW, followed by PGHTW, PGW, PGH, and PGTW. Stratified analyses suggested a stronger association of MetS with HF risk in younger adults or females. Furthermore, our CRP-stratified analysis indicated that the association between MetS and HF risk remained consistent across CRP levels, but appeared slightly stronger among participants with higher CRP levels, suggesting a potential additive effect of systemic inflammation. This observation is supported by previous evidence suggesting that inflammation plays a key role in the pathogenesis of HF among individuals with MetS [13].

A number of studies have demonstrated the adverse impact of MetS on CVDs, but most studies had relatively small sample size and fewer researches focused on HF [[14], [15], [16]]. For example, a previous cohort study of 3035 subjects over 70 years reported a higher risk of cardiovascular events, including HF [13,17], associated with MetS. Another cohort study of 2314 men aged 50 years focused on the outcome of HF and reported an increase in HF risk among those with MetS (adjusted HR = 1.80, 95 % CI = 1.11–2.91) [18]. The largest scale population study was conducted in South Korea of over two million individuals using the Korean National Health Insurance Service database, they also found a higher risk for HF events among individuals who suffered Pre-MetS or MetS, compared with those who were free of MetS [19]. However, although previous studies have examined the cumulative number of MetS components in relation to HF risk, few have assessed the impact of specific combinations of MetS components. Most existing studies considered MetS as a binary condition or used the total count of components, without considering the variability in metabolic patterns. In this study, we systematically analyzed all common clusters of the five MetS components to investigate their associations with HF risk. To our knowledge, few large-scale prospective studies have conducted such an analysis. Our results showed that different clusters carried different levels of HF risk, suggesting that the risk of HF may vary depending on the specific metabolic profile. This approach provides more detailed information for identifying high-risk groups and could help inform tailored prevention strategies. A significant positive dose–response relationship was found between the cumulative numbers of MetS components and the risk incident HF. A population-based, cross-sectional survey found similar results, in which they showed a significantly increasing trend in the prevalence of HF for the number of components of MetS [20].

Specific MetS components have been linked to an increased HF risk [8,[21], [22], [23], [24]]. A review summarized that hypertension, obesity, diabetes mellitus and hyperlipidemia play a significant role in the development of HF [25]. However, elevated triglycerides seemed not significant predictors of incident HF in previous studies [14,20], as well as in our study. Triglyceride levels are significantly influenced by body weight and body fat, and there is a significant correlation between BMI and triglyceride levels [26]. There is an "obesity paradox" in HF [27], and triglyceride levels may also present a similar paradox [28], with a negative correlation with the mortality risks among patients with prior CVD [29]. In addition, a recent meta-analysis found that visceral triglycerides reduced lipotoxicity and systemic inflammation [30], which might explain why elevated triglycerides have been protective in HF. Notably, the measurement of the triglyceride-Glucose (TyG) index in the elderly is susceptible to be influenced by more confounding factors, such as the presence of various diseases and accompanying poor nutritional status, which may lead to inaccurate measurement of TyG index [31]. Additionally, prior findings suggested that elevated remnant cholesterol and triglyceride-rich lipoproteins may contribute differentially to cardiovascular risk, independent of LDL cholesterol [32]. To date, there is no evidence of the potential impact of varying combinations of MetS components on HF risk. A population-based Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities study identified particular clusters of MetS components that carry the highest risk of incident stroke [33]. A cohort study investigated whether specific clusters of MetS were consistently associated with stiffer arteries [34]. A study evaluated whether specific clusters of MetS components differentially impact on arterial structure and function [9]. In our study, we found that some clusters of MetS component groups (PGHW, PGHTW, PGW, PGH, PGTW) were important determinants of HF. Notably, the combination of elevated blood pressure was associated with a significantly increased risk of HF, whereas elevated triglycerides related clusters showed a lower relative risk. Taken together, these results suggest that HF risk may vary substantially across different clusters of MetS components. These findings are also in line with previous smaller-scale investigations showing heterogeneous impacts of distinct MetS combinations on cardiovascular remodeling and function [35].

Our study revealed that MetS was related to an increase in the relative risk of HF in population of all ages, while the effect of MetS on HF differed by age groups. For incident HF, we found significant interaction between age and the cumulative burden of MetS (P for interaction <0.001). To further clarify this effect, we applied a more refined age stratification (<45, 45–54, 55–65, >65 years), and consistently observed that the association between MetS and HF was strongest in participants aged <45 years, with a gradual attenuation in older groups. Specifically, our findings indicated that the cumulative number of MetS components or its specific cluster components for HF carried a greater risk among young people than older people. With the risk of HF by the specific clusters of MetS components, the highest risk for future HF was for PGHT among younger participants. The metabolic risk factors have been linked to a greater increase of incident HF risk among young adults than older adults in two previous studies [36,37]. In this study, we also found a stronger association of waist circumference, increased blood pressure, and increased blood glucose with HF risk among younger participants, the strength of which declined with increasing age. This is consistent with previous evidence that hypertension and diabetes had a minor excess risk of CVD in old age [38,39]. The reasons for the age difference in relative risk of HF may be that compared with young participants, there was a higher baseline risk for older participants, and older participants may have more other risk factors or etiologies, leading to relatively smaller contribution from MetS [[40], [41], [42]].

In this study, we also observed sex differences in the effect of MetS on the development of future HF with significant interaction between sex and the cumulative number of MetS components (P for interaction <0.001). And the role of specific MetS component clusters in HF risk also differed by sex, in which female with PGH had the highest risk of HF. A systematic review study has assessed the sex-specific association between MetS or its components and the development of CVD and showed a greater elevated risk for CVD in women than men linked to MetS and some of its components, including diabetes and hypertension [43]. MetS had a relative risk (RR) of 1.78 for CVD from a meta-analysis of 37 longitudinal studies [44], which also reported a stronger association in females than in males. Furthermore, given that most females were menopausal according to the mean age of the subjects in this study, the postmenopausal reduction in estrogen protection may contribute to sex differences in susceptibility to insulin resistance-induced cardiac injury [45]. The sex differences in dysglycemia, body fat, adipocyte biology, and the hormonal control of body weight probably lead to sex-specific association between MetS and CVD to some extent [46,47]. In addition, lipid and glucose metabolism appear to interact differently with cardiovascular risk pathways in men and women, which may further modulate the impact of MetS on HF risk [48].

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study are its prospective cohort design, large sample size, and long follow-up time, allowing us to explore the role of MetS components clusters in HF risk and age or sex specific associations. To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively examine the association of the cumulative burden of MetS components, specific components and clusters with incident HF. However, we should be aware that there are some limitations in our study. First, the study mainly included middle-aged and white participants with higher socioeconomic status than the general population in the UK [49], which may limit the generalizability of the results to other populations. Second, we did not explore the association between MetS and subtypes of HF, such as HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), which may provide more information. Therefore, more researches are needed to investigate how MetS influence the development of HFpEF and HFrEF in the future. Third, the blood sample was collected from one test only, which may affect the accuracy of the estimate of glucose and lipids.

Conclusion

In summary, the risk of incident HF increased with growing cumulative burden of MetS, but varied by different clusters of MetS components. Although the component of increased blood pressure contributed most to an elevated HF incidence, its effect was attenuated by the component of increased triglycerides, leading to the highest risk in the cluster of PGHW. Besides, the adverse impact of MetS on HF was more pronounced in younger adults or females.
