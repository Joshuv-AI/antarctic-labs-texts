Early clinical efficacy and safety of different surgical approaches in total hip arthroplasty: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Xingfei Wang, Lewei Zhou, Tengbo Shi, Xiangxi Meng, Yanfeng Tang, Chaowei Guo, Chen Yue, Youwen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe choice of surgical approach in total hip arthroplasty (THA) significantly influences early recovery and complication rates. However, comprehensive evidence comparing the efficacy and safety of multiple contemporary approaches remains limited.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) from PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science (inception to July 2025). We included 85 RCTs (6,575 patients) comparing 13 surgical approaches. Frequentist framework NMA was performed, with effects reported as mean differences (MD), standardized mean differences (SMD), or risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals. Treatment rankings were assessed using surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) values.ResultsThe analysis of 85 RCTs (6,575 patients) demonstrated distinct performance profiles among the 13 surgical approaches. For functional recovery, the ranking probabilities suggested that minimally invasive anterolateral approach (MIS-ALA) was most likely to achieve the best early postoperative HHS (SUCRA = 88.7%), while modified direct anterior approach (DAA-Modified) ranked highest for medium-to-long-term HHS (SUCRA = 97.8%). regarding surgical efficiency, the minimally invasive posterolateral approach (MIS-PLA) was associated with the highest probability of having the shortest operative time (SUCRA = 98.4%), whereas MIS-ALA had the highest SUCRA value for blood loss control (SUCRA = 90.9%). For surgical trauma, MIS-PLA had the highest SUCRA value indicating the potential for the smallest postoperative CK increase (SUCRA = 82.4%), while anterior approaches (DAA-Modified, bikini direct anterior approach (DAA-Bikini), direct anterior approach (DAA)) ranked highest for suppressing CRP elevation (SUCRAs: 88.2%, 83.4%, and 80.8%). DAA had the highest SUCRA value for walking speed recovery (SUCRA = 91.8%), and supercapsular percutaneously-assisted total hip (SuperPath) had the highest SUCRA value for the physical component summary score (SUCRA = 95.4%). Safety analysis revealed direct lateral approach (DLA) had the highest SUCRA value for the lowest complication risk (SUCRA = 92.9%), while DAA-Bikini had the highest adverse event incidence.ConclusionTHA surgical approaches present distinct efficacy-safety profiles with varying probabilities of advantage. Clinical selection should be individualized based on patient characteristics and surgical expertise, as no single approach is universally superior across all outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09683-3.

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is widely regarded as one of the most successful surgical procedures of the twentieth century. It is highly effective in alleviating pain and functional impairment associated with end-stage hip diseases and has become the treatment of choice for degenerative joint disorders and severe hip trauma [1, 2]. According to statistics, over 500,000 primary THA procedures were performed in the United States in 2018 [3], and the 2022 annual report from the American joint replacement registry (AJRR) indicated a continued upward trend in THA volumes over the past decade [4]. Despite its overall effectiveness, the procedure carries risks of postoperative complications, including nerve injury, hematoma, infection, and prosthesis dislocation [5]. Although most complications are preventable and manageable, their occurrence can compromise prosthesis function, potentially necessitating revision surgery and increasing patient burden [6]. As a result, surgeons continue to explore optimal THA strategies and surgical approaches to enhance functional outcomes and minimize complication risks.

Research indicates that the choice of surgical approach significantly affects the efficacy and safety of THA [7]. Numerous approaches exist, including the direct anterior approach (DAA), SuperPath, minimally invasive posterior approach (MIS-PA), traditional posterior approach (PA), two-incision approach, minimally invasive direct lateral approach (MIS-DLA), direct lateral approach (DLA), anterolateral approach (ALA), and various modified techniques. However, consensus on the optimal approach remains elusive. Some studies report that DAA facilitates better early rehabilitation compared to PA, albeit with longer operative times [8, 10], whereas Clayton Foster et al. found no significant differences in functional outcomes at 3 months and 1 year postoperatively between DAA and PA [11]. Yang Lu et al. compared DAA with the posterolateral approach (PLA) and suggested that DAA may offer advantages in achieving leg length equality and improving short-term outcomes [12], although it is associated with lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN) injury [13]. Additionally, a three-arm randomized controlled trial (RCT) indicated that DAA and MIS-ALA provided superior early functional recovery compared to DLA [14]. Current RCTs are often limited to pairwise comparisons, frequently hampered by small sample sizes and inconsistent findings [53, 76, 84]. Traditional meta-analyses [15, 16] also suggest that DAA offers better early postoperative functional outcomes than conventional approaches, despite longer operative times. Furthermore, a recent network meta-analysis incorporating SuperPATH, direct anterior, and posterior approaches provided pooled data on short-term outcomes, highlighting the need for a balanced evidence base across emerging techniques [117]. Yet these studies lack comprehensive comparisons of efficacy and safety across multiple surgical approaches.

With advancements in THA techniques, patient expectations for early postoperative functional recovery and outcomes have increased [17]. Building upon prior work that utilized network meta-analysis (NMA) for the evaluation of THA approaches [18], we identified specific avenues for refinement, as prior syntheses were potentially limited by the spectrum of approaches covered and the breadth of outcome definitions, which may not have been fully sensitive to early functional gains and safety profiles. Furthermore, the considerable variation in learning curves across different surgical approaches presents unique challenges for surgeons [19]. To address these issues, this study employs a NMA to integrate direct and indirect evidence, with the goal of systematically evaluating the early clinical efficacy and safety of contemporary THA approaches. Our aim is to provide an evidence-based foundation to guide surgical approach selection, facilitate the learning curve, and ultimately improve patient outcomes.

Methods

This study was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, CRD420251102951, http://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO). Its conduct and reporting adhered to the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses for network meta-analyses (PRISMA-NMA) guidelines [20, 21], with a complete checklist provided in Supplementary Table 1. The project team included multidisciplinary experts: four orthopedic surgeons, one rehabilitation specialist, one epidemiologist, one systematic review methodologist, and one statistician.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

①Population: Patients aged ≥ 18 years undergoing primary THA for any etiology; ②Intervention: Experimental groups using different THA surgical approaches; ③Comparison: Control groups using either a traditional approach or any single THA surgical approach; ④Outcomes: Studies reporting at least one of the following: 1. Early postoperative (≤ 3 months) Harris hip score (HHS) 2. Medium- to long-term (3 months to 1 year) HHS 3. Operative time (minutes) 4. Intraoperative blood loss (milliliters) 5. Postoperative limb length discrepancy (millimeters) 6. Creatine kinase (CK) within 3 days postoperatively (ukat/L) 7. Change in C-reactive protein within 3 days postoperatively (mg/L) 8. Change in hemoglobin (Hb) within 2 days postoperatively (g/dL) 9. Early postoperative (≤ 3 months) gait parameters (walking speed, cadence, step length) 10. Femoral stem malalignment (varus/valgus) 11. Early postoperative (≤ 3 months) quality of life scores [including physical component summary (PCS) and mental component summary (MCS) of SF-12/SF-36] 12. All-cause revision rate 13. Adverse events, with seven outcomes prioritized for patient relevance: nerve injury, infection, thrombosis, hematoma, pain, fracture, and aseptic loosening; ⑤Study design: Only RCTs were included.

Exclusion criteria

①Patients aged ≤ 18 years; ②Duplicate publications or studies with overlapping data; ③Non-clinical studies, including basic research, reviews, conference abstracts, and case reports; ④Studies with missing key data that could not be obtained through author correspondence.

Data extraction

A dual independent screening process was employed. Two researchers (Xingfei Wang and Lewei Zhou) conducted literature retrieval, deduplication, and initial screening using EndNote (Clarivate Analytics, US) according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Extracted data included basic study characteristics (e.g., author names, publication year, patient age, and BMI), study design parameters (e.g., interventions, sample size, length of hospital stay), and outcome measures. For studies with discrepancies or uncertainties, a third statistical expert (Chen Yue) was consulted to reach consensus. Additionally, a data completion process was implemented, with missing information sought via email correspondence with study authors.

When standard deviations (SDs) were not directly reported, they were estimated from available statistical parameters such as standard errors (SEs), confidence intervals (CIs), or t- and p-values. If these measures were also unavailable, corresponding authors were contacted via email at least three times to request the missing data. For studies reporting outcomes only in graphical form without numerical values, data points were accurately extracted using GetData Graph Digitizer software (version 2.20).

Supplementary processing procedures for outcome measures were as follows: (1) HHS were analyzed as raw scores (0–100). (2) For postoperative limb length discrepancy, as most studies reported lengthening while some reported shortening, the absolute value of the pre- to postoperative difference was used. (3) Due to structural differences between SF-12 and SF-36 scales, standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated using Hedges’ method for both PCS and MCS scores. (4) For all outcomes, the last available follow-up within the specified period was used (e.g., for ≤ 3 months, the 3-month time point; if unavailable, the nearest preceding time point was selected).

Literature quality assessment

Two researchers (Yanfeng Tang and Tengbo Shi) independently assessed the quality of the included studies using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool version 5.1.0 [22], evaluating the following domains: ①random sequence generation; ②allocation concealment; ③blinding of participants and personnel; ④completeness of outcome data; ⑤risk of selective reporting; and ⑥other potential sources of bias. Based on this assessment, studies were classified as having a high, low, or unclear risk of bias. Discrepancies were resolved through consultation with a third orthopedic expert (Youwen Liu), and a three-tier arbitration mechanism was implemented to ensure objectivity in the evaluation process.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using RevMan 5.4 and Stata 17.0. Effect sizes were calculated according to data type: risk ratios (RR) for dichotomous variables, and mean differences (MD) or SMD for continuous variables. This NMA combined direct evidence from RCTs (head-to-head comparisons) with indirect evidence derived from interventions compared against a common comparator. The network module in Stata 17.0 was used for analysis and to generate visual network graphs, where nodes represented interventions (size proportional to sample size) and connecting lines indicated direct comparisons (thickness proportional to the number of comparisons) [23]. Pairwise meta-analyses were conducted using random-effects models to estimate inter-study heterogeneity, which was assessed using Cochran's Q test and I2. Network transitivity was evaluated by comparing clinical and methodological features to ensure sufficient similarity across treatment comparisons. Consistency between direct and indirect evidence within each comparison loop was tested using the node-splitting method, with indirect estimates calculated via back-calculation [24]. For each node comparison, a p-value > 0.05 indicated consistency between direct and indirect evidence, while a p-value ≤ 0.05 suggested potential inconsistency requiring further investigation [25]. Treatment ranking was performed using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) and league tables, where a SUCRA value of 0 indicates the worst treatment and 1 indicates the best [26]. Summary heat maps were generated to visualize results, and adjusted funnel plots were constructed to assess publication bias [23]. All hypothesis tests were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Literature quality assessment

All 85 included studies [14, 27, 112] were RCTs. Among these, 59 studies [27, 29, 31, 32, 34, 36, 38, 44, 46, 47, 51, 54, 57, 59, 61, 64, 66, 68, 71, 78, 80, 81, 83, 85, 95, 97, 107, 110] reported using specific random sequence generation methods, such as random number tables, cards, dice, or sealed envelopes (Supplementary Table 4). Twenty-one studies [14, 28, 30, 33, 35, 37, 45, 48, 50, 52, 53, 55, 62, 65, 69, 70, 79, 82, 84, 96] mentioned “random” but did not provide methodological details, while five studies [56, 58, 63, 108, 109] did not report randomization methods. Regarding loss to follow-up and dropouts, three studies [43, 78, 96] reported dropouts without specifying reasons. One study [85] presented a notable risk of bias due to an extremely small sample size (only 8 participants across both groups); all other studies were considered to have normal risk profiles. Medium to long-term postoperative (3 months to 1 year) HHS

Thirty-seven studies [27, 33, 39, 40, 45, 47, 49, 52, 55, 58, 63, 65, 69, 71, 72, 75, 81, 85, 88, 89, 91, 95, 97, 103, 106, 108] reporting medium- to long-term HHS (3 months to 1 year) were included, encompassing 2,680 patients and all 13 surgical approaches. The network evidence graph formed 14 closed loops (Fig. 3B). The DAA and PLA approaches involved the largest number of patients, with the most frequent direct comparisons observed between DAA and PLA, and PLA and SuperPath. The global inconsistency test indicated P = 0.534 (P > 0.05), demonstrating good consistency; therefore, a consistency model was applied. Node-splitting analysis revealed no local inconsistency (P > 0.05), supporting the reliability of the NMA results (see Supplementary file 1 for details).

The NMA indicated that, compared to the ALA approach, the DAA-Modified (MD = 5.88, 95% CI: 1.96–9.80, SUCRA = 97.8%), MIS-PLA (MD = 4.09, 95% CI: 1.07–7.11, SUCRA = 89.5%), and DAA (MD = 3.19, 95% CI: 0.46–5.92, SUCRA = 80.5%) approaches significantly improved medium- to long-term HHS (P < 0.05). Publication bias results

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots for the 13 selected outcome measures. For most outcomes—including early and late HHS scores, operative time, intraoperative blood loss, limb length discrepancy, Hb change, stem malalignment, PCS, MCS, all-cause revision rate, and postoperative complications—the funnel plots showed reasonably symmetrical scatter points, indicating a low risk of publication bias. In contrast, the remaining outcomes displayed overall asymmetry in the scatter points, suggesting a potential risk of publication bias (Supplementary file 4).

Discussion

This study employed a NMA to systematically assess the early clinical efficacy and safety of 13 surgical approaches for THA, incorporating 85 RCTs with a total of 6575 patients. The results demonstrated considerable variability in the performance of different approaches across outcome measures, with no single approach outperforming all others. These findings highlight the importance of individualized clinical decision-making, taking into account the patient’s condition, the surgeon’s expertise, and the specific surgical objectives.

In terms of functional recovery, the MIS-ALA approach achieved the highest early postoperative (≤ 3 months) HHS, likely due to its smaller incision, reduced muscle dissection, and minimal tissue trauma, which together facilitate rapid recovery [76, 111]. In contrast, during the medium- to long-term follow-up (3 months to 1 year), the DAA-Modified approach produced the highest HHS scores, suggesting superior performance in prosthesis positioning and maintenance of joint stability. This advantage may result from enhanced surgical visualization and precision, achieved through modifications such as an extended proximal incision and release of the tensor fascia latae insertion at the iliac crest, effectively reducing soft tissue tension. These results are consistent with Ke Yuqi et al. [107], who reported that the DAA-Modified approach minimizes tensor fascia latae injury; although it may increase the incidence of iliac crest pain, it does not significantly compromise postoperative functional recovery.

Regarding surgical efficiency and trauma-related indicators, the MIS-PLA approach demonstrated the shortest operative time, likely owing to its clear anatomical landmarks and standardized operative protocol. The MIS-ALA approach was most effective in controlling intraoperative blood loss, further supporting the potential of minimally invasive techniques to minimize soft tissue trauma and facilitate rapid recovery [52]. As expected, intraoperative blood loss closely correlated with postoperative Hb changes, and the MIS-ALA approach also showed a significant advantage in minimizing the postoperative Hb drop (ΔHb). In contrast, the Two-incision approach performed poorly in blood loss control, likely due to the challenges of operating through a muscular tunnel between the two incisions, where limited visualization increases technical difficulty. Although this approach may offer some benefit in controlling limb length discrepancy, its suboptimal performance across multiple efficiency-related metrics underscores its technical complexity and steep learning curve, consistent with its declining clinical adoption.

Among biomarkers reflecting surgical trauma, changes in CK levels serve as an important indicator of muscle damage [112]. In this analysis, the MIS-PLA approach produced the smallest postoperative CK increase, suggesting relatively minimal muscle trauma. Anterior approaches—including the DAA-Modified, DAA-Bikini, and DAA—were particularly effective in suppressing postoperative CRP elevation, indicating an advantage in mitigating systemic inflammatory responses. This effect is likely due to the minimal disturbance of posterior soft tissues characteristic of these approaches, a finding consistent with previous retrospective studies [113]. Mitigating the postoperative systemic inflammatory response is not merely a laboratory indicator but holds substantial potential clinical significance. From a clinical perspective, a reduced systemic inflammatory response may be associated with lower postoperative pain levels, earlier achievement of unassisted walking, and shorter hospital stay, as these clinical benefits are often correlated with lower levels of inflammation. Although not all included RCTs directly assessed these correlations, this finding provides a rationale for the potential advantage of anterior approaches within Enhanced Recovery After Surgery protocols. Gait analysis revealed that the DAA approach was superior in restoring early postoperative walking speed; however, no significant advantages were observed in cadence or step length. Notably, some inconsistency was detected in the walking speed results, warranting cautious interpretation, possibly due to heterogeneity in rehabilitation protocols and measurement methods [114].

Regarding safety, the DLA approach ranked favorably for both all-cause revision rate and overall risk of adverse events, suggesting superior operative safety and prosthesis stability. However, its performance in functional outcomes and certain trauma-related indicators was moderate, indicating that although this approach may reduce complication risks, some compromise in functional recovery may occur [115]. Conversely, the DAA-Bikini approach exhibited the highest incidence of adverse events, likely reflecting its technical complexity and the high level of surgeon proficiency required, consistent with existing clinical reports documenting elevated complication rates for this approach [116].

Different surgical approaches exhibited heterogeneous performance profiles, necessitating a holistic assessment of trade-offs between operative efficiency, surgical trauma, and functional recovery. For instance, surgeons prioritizing efficiency might favor the MIS-PLA, which performed best in reducing operative time and minimizing muscle trauma, but this comes at the cost of a moderate complication risk ranking. Conversely, while the DLA may entail a slower early functional recovery and longer operative duration compared to anterior approaches, it offered the most favorable safety profile, making it a potentially safer choice for lower-volume surgeons. Similarly, the decision to utilize anterior approaches (DAA/Modified DAA) involves weighing the benefits of superior inflammatory mitigation and gait recovery against the potential for increased technical complexity. It is important to emphasize that the probability rankings based on SUCRA primarily reflect trend differences, and their clinical significance must be interpreted cautiously alongside effect sizes and confidence intervals.

Crucially, readers must distinguish between statistically significant findings and clinical relevance. While SUCRA values provide a hierarchy of probability, they do not quantify the magnitude of clinical benefit. For example, although certain approaches ranked higher for early HHS, the observed Mean Differences (MD) in our pairwise comparisons often fell within narrow ranges. It remains debatable whether a statistical improvement of a few points in HHS or a slight reduction in operative time translates into a perceptible benefit for the patient. Future studies should focus on establishing the Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) for these specific surgical comparisons to determine if the statistical advantages of approaches like MIS-ALA or DAA-Modified result in meaningful functional changes in real-world scenarios.

This study has several notable strengths, including the comprehensive inclusion of 13 surgical approaches, evaluation of multiple early efficacy and safety indicators, utilization of NMA to integrate both direct and indirect evidence, and provision of SUCRA rankings to facilitate evidence-based clinical decision-making. Limitations This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, our NMA attempted to compare 13 interventions, which inevitably resulted in a sparse evidence network. Many direct comparisons were informed by only one or a few RCTs. This sparsity significantly increases statistical uncertainty and fragility in the rankings. Readers should be aware that high SUCRA values in sparse networks can be unstable, and strictly interpreting these rankings as definitive superiority without considering the wide credible intervals may be misleading.

Second, the included studies span a considerable period (2005–2024). Over this time, substantial evolution has occurred in surgical techniques, implant designs, and perioperative care. This temporal and clinical heterogeneity challenges the transitivity assumption underpinning our NMA. Specifically, the inconsistency detected in gait speed outcomes suggests that varying rehabilitation protocols and measurement standards across two decades likely introduced bias, limiting the validity of these specific findings. Furthermore, as detailed in Supplementary Table 4, many studies exhibited an unclear or high risk of bias, particularly regarding blinding and allocation concealment, which collectively diminishes the strength of the synthesized evidence.

Third, the assessment of inconsistency revealed important nuances. Global inconsistency was significant for early postoperative gait speed (P = 0.00), and node-splitting confirmed local inconsistency for this outcome. For all other outcomes, global inconsistency tests were non-significant (P > 0.05). The detected inconsistency for gait speed suggests potential incoherence between direct and indirect evidence, possibly due to heterogeneity in gait assessment methods or rehabilitation protocols across studies. Consequently, the NMA results for early postoperative gait speed should be interpreted with extreme caution.

Fourth, although funnel plots for major outcomes like HHS and complications appeared generally symmetrical, asymmetry was noted for some biomarker outcomes (e.g., CK, CRP). This indicates the potential for publication bias or small-study effects to have influenced the findings for these measures.

Finally, the inability to quantitatively adjust for surgeon experience and learning curve effects represents a potential source of confounding that may affect the generalizability of the findings. Based on our results, we hypothesize DLA's safety may benefit learners, while MIS-PLA's standardized efficiency could reduce technical risk, whereas technically demanding approaches like DAA-Bikini may require extensive experience to mitigate their higher complication risks. Future research should prioritize large-sample, multicenter RCTs that specifically evaluate learning curve effects across different surgical approaches and long-term clinical outcomes, incorporating patient-reported outcomes (PROs) and radiographic assessments for a multidimensional comparison. Clinically, surgeons should individualize the choice of surgical approach based on their technical proficiency, available resources, and the specific needs of each patient, rather than pursuing a presumed "optimal" approach indiscriminately.

Conclusions

This NMA demonstrates distinct efficacy-safety profiles and notable trade-offs among 13 THA approaches, with no single technique consistently superior across all outcomes. Current evidence suggests that minimally invasive and modified anterior approaches may offer potential advantages in early functional recovery and inflammatory response mitigation, whereas posterior-based approaches appear to be more favorable for operative efficiency. Surgical approach selection should be individualized, taking into account patient anatomy, surgeon experience, and institutional resources to optimize outcomes. Moreover, this analysis focused on early-to-midterm outcomes, while critical long-term results—such as 10-year implant survivorship, aseptic loosening, osteolysis, and late dislocation—remain unaddressed in the current RCT literature. igh-quality, multicenter studies are needed to further assess these long-term outcomes and the impact of the learning curve.
