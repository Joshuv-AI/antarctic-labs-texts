The moderating role of life satisfaction and searching for meaning in life in the relationship between sleep quality and stress, anxiety, and depression in patients undergoing hemodialysis treatment
Authors: Ömer Tanriverdi, Ayşe Soylu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSleep disorders are highly prevalent among hemodialysis patients and may adversely affect psychological well-being, life satisfaction, and meaning in life. However, limited research has examined the moderating roles of life satisfaction and meaning in life in the relationship between sleep quality and psychological symptoms. This study aimed to investigate whether life satisfaction and meaning in life moderate the associations between sleep quality and stress, anxiety, and depression in hemodialysis patients.MethodsThis descriptive, correlational study included 110 hemodialysis patients. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), Depression-Anxiety-Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21), Life Satisfaction Scale (LSS), and Meaning in Life Scale (MLS). Data were analyzed using correlation, multivariate regression, and moderation analyses.ResultsHigher PSQI scores (indicating poorer sleep quality) were positively correlated with stress (r = 0.41, p < 0.05), anxiety (r = 0.32, p < 0.05), and depression (r = 0.32, p < 0.05). Life satisfaction significantly moderated the relationship between stress and sleep quality (β = −0.041, p = 0.024), indicating a buffering effect. However, meaning in life did not show a significant moderating role in the relationships between sleep quality and psychological symptoms.ConclusionHigher stress, anxiety, and depression were associated with poorer sleep quality in hemodialysis patients. Life satisfaction buffered the negative effect of stress on sleep, whereas meaning in life showed no moderating role. Interventions targeting life satisfaction may help improve sleep quality and psychological well-being.Keywords: Sleep quality, Life satisfaction, Meaning in life, Stress, Anxiety, Depression, Hemodialysis

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) has become a global health problem. Renal failure develops as a result of a deterioration in kidney functions, and hemodialysis treatment is applied to restore kidney functions [1]. Although hemodialysis (HD) prolongs the life of patients with renal failure, it can lead to some adverse physical and psychological side-effects. Previous studies have demonstrated that sleep disorders are common among hemodialysis patients and are closely associated with the development of mental health problems such as depression and anxiety [2, 3].

Sleep disturbances are common among patients undergoing hemodialysis and are considered to result from a complex interplay of physiological alterations, treatment-related factors, and psychosocial influences [4]. The prevalence of sleep disorders in dialysis patients has been reported to range from 50% to 80% [5, 6]. Sleep is of great importance for human health, as it is a natural process not only for rest and regaining energy, but is also of critical importance for tissue repair and regeneration [7]. Patients with poor sleep quality have been shown to have decreased life satisfaction with physical and emotional symptoms such as depression, anxiety, and stress [8–10].

The meaning of life is a concept that has been discussed for centuries. According to Frankl, life is not just the pursuit of pleasure or avoiding hurt, but can be said to be the meaning behind life and the person gives life that meaning [11]. When goals and actions are beneficial, people feel that there is meaning to life [12]. Whether or not a person has meaning to life shapes their attitude to life. It has been reported that individuals who see meaning to life are stronger in response to difficult events, can cope more easily with difficult emotions, and have life satisfaction [13]. In chronic diseases such as chronic renal failure, individuals need to achieve a satisfactory level of meaning in their lives in order to develop a positive perception of the disease [14]. It has been stated that individuals who find meaning in their lives have increased life satisfaction and hope levels and are more successful in coping with mental problems [15–17].

Life satisfaction is the degree to which a person positively evaluates the overall quality of their current life. When life satisfaction is considered in terms of Maslow’s theory of the hierarchy of needs, it has been stated that life satisfaction is not possible without meeting an individual’s basic needs [10]. Sleep disturbances in patients with chronic renal failure, a complication of dialysis, can negatively impact life satisfaction, leading to fatigue and psychological problems such as distraction, depression, and anxiety [18].

It has been reported in literature that sleep disorders are frequently seen in hemodialysis patients and this has an adverse effect on quality of life [8, 19]. While previous studies have demonstrated a relationship between sleep quality, psychological symptoms, life satisfaction, and meaning in life, no studies have been found demonstrating how—or to what extent—life satisfaction and meaning in life moderate sleep quality and levels of depression, anxiety, and stress in hemodialysis patients. Therefore, to address this gap in the literature, a conceptual model was developed and tested to explore the moderating role of life satisfaction and meaning in life in the relationship between sleep quality and stress, anxiety, and depression.

In acccordance with the general aim of the study, the following hypotheses were formed:

H1= The status of stress, anxiety, and depression is related to sleep quality in hemodialysis patients.

H1= The status of stress, anxiety, and depression is related to sleep quality in hemodialysis patients.

H2= Life satisfaction has a moderating role on the effect of stress, anxiety, and depression on sleep quality in hemodialysis patients.

H2= Life satisfaction has a moderating role on the effect of stress, anxiety, and depression on sleep quality in hemodialysis patients.

H3= The meaning of life has a moderating role on the effect of stress, anxiety, and depression on sleep quality in hemodialysis patients.

H3= The meaning of life has a moderating role on the effect of stress, anxiety, and depression on sleep quality in hemodialysis patients.

H4= Life satisfaction and the search for the meaning of life together have a moderating role on the effect of stress, anxiety, and depression on sleep quality in hemodialysis patients.

H4= Life satisfaction and the search for the meaning of life together have a moderating role on the effect of stress, anxiety, and depression on sleep quality in hemodialysis patients.

Study model

Conceptual framework

Hemodialysis treatment is a tiring and debilitating process due to its chronic nature. The living standards, regularity of sleep, and social life of patients are adversely affected due to this process. The life satisfaction of patients with a severe condition can be decreased, and low life satisfaction can decrease the meaning given to life. This prevents individuals from being strong against difficult events and causes them to be unable to cope with challenging emotions. However, hope is high in individuals who have meaning to life, levels of stress, anxiety, and depression are under control, and life satisfaction is increased. This relationship can increase sleep quality, which is a physical and mental requirement. The main focus of this research is sleep quality. Method

Study design

This was a descriptive, cross-sectional, correlational study.

Universe and sample

The universe of the study consisted of 160 patients who were treated in hemodialysis units between 20.05.2024 and 22.07.2024. To determine the study sample, participants were subjected to a study conducted by Alradaideh and Bdair (2024) [21] based on their mean “gender and habitual sleep efficiency” score.

The study was conducted with 110 hemodialysis patients. Participation was voluntary, and data were collected using standardized scales administered through face-to-face interviews based on self-reported responses. The sample size calculation was based on the reference study, which reported group differences in sleep-related variables. The resulting sample size was considered sufficient for the planned correlational, regression, and moderation analyses conducted in the present study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for the study were defined as patients aged 18 years and over who were able to communicate, were literate, and had been receiving regular haemodialysis treatment for at least one year. Patients with communication difficulties, those with any psychiatric diagnosis according to the DSM-IV, or those experiencing a serious complication (sudden deterioration in vital signs) during dialysis were excluded from the study. According to the exclusion criteria, twelve patients were excluded due to illiteracy, three due to receiving hemodialysis for less than one year, six due to refusal to participate, and three due to incomplete questionnaires.

Data collection tools

Personal information form

This form was prepared by the researchers with reference to the relevant literature. The 6 items on the form provided information about age, gender, education level, marital status, disease duration, harmful lifestyle habits, and the use of psychiatric drugs [14, 22].

Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI)

The PSQI is a self-reported scale of 19 items to evaluate the sleep of an individual in the last month. There are 15 items with multiple choice responses and 4 that require a written response. The usual time of going to bed, sleep latency, sleep duration, and waking times are recorded. The total scale score can range from 0 to 21, with lower score indicating higher subjective sleep quality [23]. The Cronbach alpha internal consistency coefficient has been found to be 0.80, and in this study was calculated to be 0.74. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the PSQI was conducted by Ağargün, Kara, and Anlar, who demonstrated that the Turkish version of the scale is both valid and reliable for assessing subjective sleep quality [23].

Depression anxiety stress-21 scale (DASS-21)

This scale consists of 21 items [24], with 7 items measuring each of the dimensions of depression, stress, and anxiety. The response for each item is scored from 0 to 3 on a 4-point Likert-type scale, yielding a total score for each sub-dimension ranging from 0 to 21. The depression subdimension is evaluated as 0–4: normal, 5–6: mild, 7–10: moderate, 11–13: advanced, and ≥ 14 very advanced depression. The anxiety subdimension is evaluated as 0–3: normal, 4–5: mild, 6–7: moderate, 8–9: severe, and ≥ 10 very severe anxiety. The stress subdimension is evaluated as 0–7: normal, 8–9: mild, 10–12: moderate, 13–16: advanced, and ≥ 17 very advanced stress. Higher score indicate higher levels of stress, anxiety, and depression. The Turkish version of the scale, whose validity and reliability were previously established by Sarıçam, was used in the present study. In the original Turkish adaptation study, the Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficients were reported as α = 0.87 for the depression subscale, α = 0.85 for the anxiety subscale, and α = 0.81 for the stress subscale [25]. The Cronbach alpha values were calculated as 0.79 for stress, 0.80 for depression, and 0.80 for anxiety.

Life satisfaction scale (LSS)

This 5-item scale was developed to assess overall life satisfaction [26]. Responses are rated on a 7-point Likert-type scale ranging from “I completely disagree” to “I completely agree,” yielding a total score between 1 and 35, with higher score indicating greater life satisfaction. The Turkish adaptation study conducted by Yetim demonstrated that the scale exhibits strong validity and reliability within Turkish populations, reporting a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.86 [27] and was calculated as 0.84 in this study.

Meaning of life scale (MLS)

Meaning in Life Scale (MLS): This scale was developed by Steger, Frazier, Oishi and Kaler [28] in 2006 and its Turkish validity and reliability were made by Akın (2015). The 10-item scale has 2 subdimensions of Meaning that is Present (5 items) and Meaning I am Trying to Find (5 items). Each statement in the scale is scored on a 7-point Likert-type scale from “completely false” to “completely true”. The internal consistency coefficient for Meaning that is Present was found to be 0.77 [29]. In the current study, the 5 items of Meaning that is Present were used and the Cronbach alpha value was calculated to be 0.80.

Ethical aspects of the study

Approval for the study was granted by the Non-Interventional Research Ethics Committee of Mardin Artuklu University (decision no: [2024/5–19], session no: 5, dated: 07.05.2024). The necessary legal permission to conduct the study was obtained from Mardin Artuklu University and Hospital. All the study procedures complied with the Helsinki Declaration. The hemodialysis patients provided informed consent for participation before being enrolled in the study.

Statistical analysis

Data obtained in the study were analyzed statistically using IBM SPSS vn. 23 software. Conformity of the data to normal distribution was examined with skewness-kurtosis (± 2) values. Relationships were examined with Spearman’s rho correlation coefficient for variables not conforming to normal distribution and using Pearson correlation coefficient for variables showing normal distribution. To examine the moderating effects of life satisfaction and the meaning of life in the effect of independent variables (depression, anxiety, stress) on the dependent variable (sleep quality), the Process Macro software program developed by Andrew F. Hayes was used. Model 2 of Process Macro was used to test the model established. A value of p < 0.05 was accepted as the level of statistical significance.

Discussion

The results of this study showed that hemodialysis patients had normal stress levels, moderate anxiety levels, and mild depression levels. Patients had poor sleep quality and low life satisfaction, while their sense of meaning in life was moderate. Higher PSQI scores, indicating poorer sleep quality, were positively associated with stress, anxiety, and depression. Life satisfaction was found to moderate the relationship between stress and sleep quality, whereas meaning in life did not demonstrate a moderating role. The findings are discussed below in light of the literature. The descriptive characteristics of the patients participating in the study (Table 1) are similar to those in previous studies [10, 30]. The results of this study indicate that participants had poor sleep quality (9.05 ± 3.65). A meta-analysis conducted by Mirghaed et al. (2019) revealed that poor sleep quality is a common problem among HD patients [31]. The findings of this study indicate that stress, anxiety, and depression were all positively associated with poorer sleep quality in hemodialysis patients, supporting Hypothesis H1. This result is consistent with previous studies showing that psychological distress contributes to sleep disturbances in this population [32, 33]. Considering the restorative role of sleep in both physiological and psychological functioning [34], these findings highlight the importance of addressing mental health problems alongside physical care in hemodialysis patients. Poor sleep quality in hemodialysis patients is not only a matter of reduced well-being but is also associated with important clinical outcomes, including increased morbidity and reduced treatment adherence [4, 18]. Therefore, identifying and managing factors that negatively affect sleep quality, particularly psychological distress, is essential for improving both clinical outcomes and overall quality of life in this population.

Current study findings indicate that participants have low life satisfaction (14.59 ± 4.80). In the same way, Yanmış and Mollaoğlu (2021) reported low life satisfaction in their study with HD patients [10]. In this study, a negative correlation was found between life satisfaction and sleep quality score. Poor sleep quality may be one of the factors negatively affecting patients’ life satisfaction. The literature also reports that sleep disorders, a complication of dialysis, may have a negative effect on life satisfaction [18]. Furthermore, changes in sleep quality and quantity have been reported as primary complaints in many mental health disorders [35]. In this context, ensuring and maintaining sleep quality, identifying underlying psychological conditions, and taking preventive measures are crucial for increasing life satisfaction.

In this study, the patients’ level of attributing meaning to life was found to be moderate (20.89 ± 7.83). Furthermore, a negative correlation was found between stress, anxiety, and depression score and score indicating meaning in life. The chronic and demanding nature of hemodialysis treatment, including dependence on dialysis machines and lifestyle restrictions, may negatively influence patients’ existential perceptions and life goals. The literature indicates that life-altering events such as chronic illness may undermine individuals’ existing life goals and sense of meaning, thereby increasing feelings of helplessness, aimlessness, and meaninglessness, as well as vulnerability to loss of hope [36, 37]. This can diminish the meaning patients give to life. In this context, psychosocial interventions aimed at strengthening meaning in life may contribute to improved psychological adjustment among hemodialysis patients.

In the analyses of this study, life satisfaction was found to have a negative moderating role on the relationship between stress and sleep quality in Model 1 (Table 3). This finding supported Hypothesis H2. This moderation suggests that high life satisfaction can reduce stress, and reduced stress can lessen its negative impact on sleep quality. This finding is consistent with previous studies emphasizing the importance of managing psychological distress to improve sleep outcomes in hemodialysis patients. As emphasized in the study by Al Naamani et al. (2021), controlling psychological problems such as anxiety and depression in HD patients is crucial for improving sleep quality [4]. Patients with poor sleep quality have been shown to have decreased life satisfaction with physical and emotional symptoms such as depression, anxiety, and stress [8–10]. Reducing the level of psychological distress is very important for increasing life satisfaction associated with sleep quality [9].

In contrast, meaning in life did not show a moderating role in the relationships between sleep quality and psychological variables, leading to the rejection of H3 and H4. This may be explained by the moderate levels of meaning in life observed in the sample, which may not have been sufficient to exert a buffering effect. However, meaning in life was not significantly associated with sleep quality in the present study, although previous studies have reported a positive relationship between meaning in life and sleep quality [38, 39].

Clinical implications

HD is a chronic and exhausting process that can restrict social life, significantly affect quality of life, and have a negative impact on sleep patterns. This situation can adversely affect patients’ life satisfaction and the meaning they derive from life. Psychological and social adjustment sessions should be organised to manage stress, anxiety and depression, and patients should be encouraged to participate in social activities. As Ardahan (2018) emphasises, light physical activity may have a positive effect on the life satisfaction of haemodialysis patients [40]. Patients supported in this way may be able to increase their life satisfaction and the meaning they derive from life. Consequently, sleep quality may also improve simultaneously. If patients are in adequate physiological condition, encouraging [41] them to work and earn their own living can help maintain their psychological well-being.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow for causal inferences regarding the relationships between sleep quality, life satisfaction, meaning in life, stress, anxiety, and depression. Second, the study was conducted with a relatively small sample of hemodialysis patients from two centers, which may limit the generalizability of the results to all individuals with chronic kidney disease. Third, although the questionnaires were administered through face-to-face interviews, the data were based on participants’ self-reported responses, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Future studies with longitudinal designs, larger and more diverse samples, and the inclusion of objective measures of sleep and psychological well-being are needed to confirm and extend these findings. Fourth, the lack of qualitative findings encompassing the patients’ experiences is another limitation. Fifth, the patients were observed only during their hospital stays, and their social lives were not assessed separately. In addition, potential confounding factors such as comorbid conditions and medication use were not controlled for, which may have influenced both sleep quality and psychological outcomes.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that higher levels of stress, anxiety, and depression were associated with poorer sleep quality in hemodialysis patients. High life satisfaction appears to play a moderating role in mitigating the negative impact of stress on sleep quality; this suggests that interventions aimed at increasing life satisfaction may help reduce stress-related sleep disturbances. While life meaning did not show a moderating role, its association with sleep quality suggests that strengthening existential resources can support psychological well-being. Integrating routine psychosocial assessments and targeted interventions into hemodialysis care may contribute to improving sleep quality and overall patient outcomes.
