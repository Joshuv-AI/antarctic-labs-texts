Family support and peer support as strong independent correlates of mental health indices among Albanian adolescents aged 11–15 years
Authors: Eugena Tomini, Gentiana Qirjako, Rudina Çumashi, Romina Hala, Genc Burazeri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSupportive family and peer relationships foster healthy development and enhance adolescents’ capacity to thrive despite adverse life circumstances. Our aim was to assess the association of family support and peer support with selected mental health indices of adolescents, controlling for a wide range of sociodemographic characteristics and behavioural factors. A cross-sectional study, conducted in Albania in 2022, included a nationwide representative sample of 5454 adolescents aged 11, 13, and 15 years (≈52% girls; overall response: ≈96%). Data on mental health indices [Cantril’s ladder-based life satisfaction measure, WHO-5 wellbeing index, and GAD-7], family support and peer support, and sociodemographic and behavioural factors were collected. General linear model and binary logistic regression were used to assess the independent associations of family support and peer support with mental health indices. In fully-adjusted models, mean values of all three mental health indices were higher (indicating better mental health) among adolescents with high levels of family support and/or peer support compared with their counterparts without high support levels (P < .001 for all). Furthermore, the odds of ‘high life satisfaction’, ‘good mood’, and ‘little/no anxiety’ were all higher among adolescents who reported high family support and/or peer support compared to those without high support levels (P < .001 for all). High peer support levels and especially high family support levels were strongly and independently associated with better mental health outcomes among adolescents in Albania. Our findings highlight the importance of fostering supportive social environments to promote youth mental wellbeing across diverse sociodemographic and behavioural contexts.

Introduction

Adolescent development is influenced by the social environments surrounding them [1, 2]. Supportive relationships at home, with peers, and within school settings foster healthy development, buffer adolescents from the effects of structural determinants of health inequalities and enhance their capacity to thrive despite adverse life circumstances [1–4]. Furthermore, social environments including family support and peer support significantly influence the mental well-being of children and adolescents, with effects that can be either positive or negative depending on their quality [1, 5]. This is very important as mental health problems among adolescents are increasing [1, 6]. Hence, according to the last global report on mental health, in 2019, one in seven individuals aged 10–19 years endured a mental disorder [7, 8]. Also, importantly, half of all adult mental disorders have their onset by the age of 14 years [9].

In the European region, 44% of adolescents report experiencing more than two subjective health complaints more than once a week, encompassing both psychological and somatic complaints [10]. The most frequently reported mental health issues among European children aged 11–15 years include such psychological symptoms as nervousness, irritability, difficulties in getting to sleep, and feeling low [10]. Furthermore, the average life satisfaction score (measured using Cantril’s ladder [10]) across all European countries is estimated at 7.5 out of 10 (the highest) [10]. Additionally, the overall average score of WHO-5 wellbeing index is estimated at about 61 out of 100 (the highest) [10].

Surveys of the Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC) study have linked family support and peer support to an array of health behaviours and mental health outcomes [11, 12]. A higher level of family support has been convincingly liked to more favourable adolescents’ health [1, 13], better psychosocial functioning [14], a higher self-esteem and academic performance [1, 15], lower levels of risky health behaviours [1, 15], and a higher level of life satisfaction [16]. Furthermore, a higher level of peer support is associated with adolescent identity development [2], emotional regulation [17], and a higher life satisfaction in young adulthood [18]. Yet, according to the most recent analyses based on HBSC studies, family support provides the strongest safeguard for adolescents’ mental health and psychosocial development, before and after COVID-19 pandemic [1, 19].

For several decades, research has convincingly shown that individual factors alone cannot capture the full spectrum of adolescent health and wellbeing [20]. A comprehensive understanding requires consideration of interrelated contextual risk factors [20]. Bronfenbrenner’s ecological model offers a useful framework for examining how multiple levels of influence interact to shape adolescent health [20]. Within this perspective, key psychosocial determinants of mental health include family interactions, informal social networks, family support, peer support, and peer norms [20].

However, the evidence on the independent association between family support and/or peer support with mental health of adolescents in countries of Southeastern Europe is limited. From this perspective, it is important to assess the standalone influence of family and/or peer support, irrespective of socioeconomic backgrounds and behavioural characteristics of young people pertinent to the Southeastern European region, particularly in the post-communist countries of the Western Balkans including Albania.

Following the breakdown of the communist regime in 1990, Albania has experienced an enormous political and socioeconomic transformation [21], which has been nonetheless characterized by massive emigration [22], social disruption and turmoil [23], including also changes in family structure, norms and values [24]. During the socialist regime in Albania, there were strong family ties, often characterized by multigenerational households. The shift to a marked-oriented economy in Albania has not only increased both internal and international migration [22], but has also led to disruption and fragmentation of traditional family networks [24]. The political and socioeconomic instability during this transition period [21–24] may have supposedly weakened family support for adolescents. Additionally, the liberalization of social norms and exposure to globalization may have inevitably posed new stressors among adolescents for which Albanian families may be less prepared to manage. Conversely, adolescents’ social networks may have expanded and peer support may have become more important, as traditional structures have weakened in Albania and adolescents have become more influenced by globalized norms and digital communication.

In this context, the aim of our study was to assess the association of family support and peer support with mental health indices of adolescents, irrespective of a rich array of sociodemographic characteristics and behavioural factors. We hypothesized a positive relationship of family and/or peer support with adolescents’ mental health indices, regardless of the socioeconomic background and behavioural patterns.

Methods

This was a cross-sectional study relating to the most recent round of the HBSC survey which was carried out in Albania in 2022 [25, 26].

Study population

Study population and sampling have been described in detail elsewhere [25, 26]. In brief, participants included a nationwide representative sample of 5454 adolescents aged 11, 13, and 15 years (≈52% girls; overall response: 96%) [25, 26].

Instrument of data collection

A structured self-administered and anonymous internationally standardized questionnaire included, among other health aspects, measurement of selected mental health indices, family support, peer support, behavioural factors, and adolescents’ sociodemographic factors [27].

Outcome variables

Mental health indices considered in this analysis included life satisfaction, WHO-5 wellbeing index (‘mood’), and anxiety.

Measurement of life satisfaction was based on a visual analogue scale using Cantril’s ladder [10, 27]. Children were shown a ladder with steps numbered from 0 (worst possible life) to 10 (best possible life) and were asked to indicate where they felt standing at the time of the survey. According to Mazur et al. [28], and as specified in the HBSC international protocol [27], scores of 9–10 are classified as ‘high life satisfaction’ [27]. Based on this consideration, in the analysis, life satisfaction was also dichotomized into: ‘not high’ (scores: 0–8) vs. ‘high’ (scores: 9–10).

Measurement of WHO-5 wellbeing index (also, referred to as ‘mood’) was based on five statements for each of which children were asked to indicate the closest frequency category they had been feeling over the last two weeks preceding the survey. For each of the five statements, frequency categories ranged from ‘at no time’ (score: 0) to ‘all the time’ (score: 5). A summary score including all five statements was calculated for each participant ranging from 0 (worst wellbeing) to 25 (best wellbeing). In the analysis, the summary score of the WHO-5 wellbeing index (‘mood’) was also dichotomized into: ‘good mood’ (scores: 13–25) vs. ‘not good mood’ (scores: 0–12) [26, 27].

Measurement of anxiety was based on the ‘Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7)’. For each of the seven statements of GAD-7, frequency categories ranged from ‘nearly every day’ (score: 0) to ‘not at all’ (score: 3). A summary score including all seven statements was calculated for each participant ranging from 0 (most anxious possible) to 21 (no anxiety at all). In the analysis, anxiety was also dichotomized into: ‘anxiety’ (scores: 0–10) vs. ‘little/no anxiety’ (scores: 11–21) [26, 27].

Explanatory variables

Measurement of family support was based on a scale composed of the following four items [27]:

My family really tries to help me.

My family really tries to help me.

I get the emotional help and support I need from my family.

I get the emotional help and support I need from my family.

I can talk about my problems with my family.

I can talk about my problems with my family.

My family is willing to help me make decisions.

My family is willing to help me make decisions.

Each item was coded as a Likert scale from 1 (very strongly disagree) to 7 (very strongly agree). An overall score for the family support scale was calculated by adding the items together and dividing by four. This mean score, ranging from 1 to 7, was used in the analysis as a continuous variable, with higher mean scores indicating higher levels of perceived family support. In addition, the overall family support score was dichotomized into ≥5.5 (high family support) vs. <5.5 (not high family support) [27].

Measurement of peer support was based on a scale composed of the following four items [27]:

My friends really try to help me.

My friends really try to help me.

I can count on my friends when things go wrong.

I can count on my friends when things go wrong.

I have friends with whom I can share my joys and sorrows.

I have friends with whom I can share my joys and sorrows.

I can talk about my problems with my friends.

I can talk about my problems with my friends.

Each item was coded as a Likert scale from 1 (very strongly disagree) to 7 (very strongly agree). An overall score for the peer support scale was calculated by adding the items together and dividing by four. This mean score, ranging from 1 to 7, was used in the analysis as a continuous variable, with higher mean scores indicating higher levels of perceived peer support. In addition, the overall peer support score was dichotomized into ≥5.5 (high peer support) vs. <5.5 (not high peer support) [27].

Covariates

Behavioural characteristics of adolescents included assessment of daily consumption of breakfast, daily consumption of fruit, toothbrushing ≥2 times per day, engagement in adequate (≥3 times/week) physical activity, lifetime smoking, and lifetime alcohol consumption (all dichotomized in the analysis into: ‘yes’ vs. ‘no’) [25, 27].

Sociodemographic factors included adolescents’ gender, place of residence, mother’s and father’s current employment status, and family affluence scale (a material wealth measure based on household assets, whose summary score was dichotomized in the analysis into ‘less affluent’ vs. ‘more affluent’ families) [26, 27].

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tirana Medical University (approval ID: No.700/1). All participants were informed about the objectives and procedures of the study, as well as the aspects regarding the anonymity of the survey and the subsequent aggregated data analysis. Passive consent was obtained from parents via the teachers at each school.

Statistical analysis

General Linear Model was employed to compare mean values of the scores of mental health indices by adolescents’ family support and peer support categories (Table 2). Crude and multivariable-adjusted mean values of the scores of mental health indices and their respective 95% confidence intervals (95%CIs) and P-values were calculated.

Conversely, binary logistic regression was used to assess the association of mental health indices (dichotomized respectively into: ‘not high’ vs. ‘high’ life satisfaction; ‘good mood’ vs. ‘not good mood’; ‘anxiety’ vs. ‘little/no anxiety’) with family support and peer support (introduced as dichotomous terms: ‘high’ vs. ‘not high’ support) (Table 3). Initially, crude (unadjusted) models were run (model 1), followed by multivariable-adjusted models controlling sequentially for different groups of covariates (models 2–4). Odds ratios (ORs) for ‘high’ vs. ‘not high’ scores of family support and peer support and their respective 95%CIs and P-values were calculated for all logistic regression models. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test was used to assess the goodness-of-fit of all multivariable-adjusted logistic regression models [29].

P ≤ .05 was considered as statistically significant for all tests. Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 19.0) was used for all the statistical analyses.

Results

Overall, about 85% of adolescents reported a high family support level and 68% a high peer support level (Fig. 1). There was evidence of an inverse graded relationship of both high family and peer support levels with age. Otherwise, family support level was almost equally distributed among the other sociodemographic groupings. Conversely, a higher peer support level was slightly more prevalent in boys than in girls and particularly among children from more affluent families compared to their less affluent counterparts (Fig. 1).

Distribution of family support and peer support by sociodemographic characteristics in a nationwide sample of Albanian schoolchildren in 2022 (N = 5454).

Notably, higher family support and peer support levels were considerably more prevalent among adolescents with a higher life satisfaction, good mood and little/no anxiety (Table 1—upper panel). Also, mean values of the scores of mental health indices were all the highest (indicating better mental health) among adolescents who reported high support from both their family and peers, followed by those with high support from their family only (Table 1—lower panel).

In crude logistic regression models (lower panel–model 1), the odds of high life satisfaction, good mood and little/no anxiety were each about two times higher among adolescents who reported high peer support (score: ≥5.5) compared with those without high peer support (P < .01 for all). Discussion

Salient findings

Main findings of our study include a high perceived peer support and particularly family support among Albanian adolescents. Additionally, higher levels of peer support and especially higher levels of family support were strongly and consistently related to better mental health outcomes, irrespective of a very wide range of socioeconomic characteristics and behavioural factors of adolescents. Participants who reported high support by both their families and peers exhibited the most favourable mental health indices. Adolescents who perceived high support only from their families had significantly better mental health indices than those who perceived high support only from their peers. Remarkably, among adolescents who perceived high support only from their peers, all three mental health indices were slightly less favourable than among those who did not report high levels of support by either their families or peers (Table 1—lower panel).

Comparison with previous studies

Our findings on a positive association between family support and better mental health outcomes in adolescents are compatible with many previous studies conducted internationally [1, 13–16, 19, 30]. Likewise, our findings on a positive relationship between peer support and adolescents’ mental wellbeing are in line with several previous reports from the international literature [1, 2, 17, 18, 30]. Furthermore, importantly, our findings pertinent to both family support and peer support are compatible with a fairly recent report based on the last round of the HBSC study conducted in 2021–22 in 44 countries in Europe, Central Asia and Canada [1].

In light of the consistent and well-established relationship between peer support and particularly family support with mental health and well-being of adolescents [1, 2], the focus has currently shifted toward examining the relative significance of various sources of support in promoting and safeguarding adolescents’ mental health and well-being [1, 16]. The HBSC surveys provide valuable clues in this regard, highlighting the relative contribution of different sources of support to young people’s mental health and well-being [1]. Recent evidence from HBSC studies indicates that adolescents with high support by both their families and peers have the best mental health outcomes [1], which is compatible with our findings. Additionally, the latest findings from HBSC studies conducted in 44 countries show that adolescents with high family support only have more positive mental health outcomes than their counterparts with high peer support only [1], which is also in line with our findings pertinent to Albanian adolescents of the same age (11–15 years). Conversely, in our study, mental health indices were marginally suboptimal among adolescents with high peer support only compared with those who did not report high levels of support by either their families or peers, whereas in the multi-country HBSC report 2021–22, scores of mental health indices among adolescents with support only from peers were similar to the levels of those without support either by their families or peers [1]. Nonetheless, overall, similar to the recent multi-country HBSC report [1] and previous HBSC reports [16], our findings confirm that family support plays a more significant role than peer support in promoting and safeguarding positive mental health.

According to the most recent multi-country HBSC report [1], 68% of adolescents reported high levels of family support, which is considerably lower than our finding pertinent to Albanian adolescents (85%). As a matter of fact, among all 44 participating countries in the HBSC 2021–22 wave, Albanian adolescents displayed the highest levels of family support, followed by Denmark, Switzerland, and Tajikistan [1]. On the other hand, the lowest levels of family support were evidenced in Canada, Czechia, Poland, and United Kingdom [1].

On average, in the multi-country HBSC analysis [1], boys reported a higher level of family support than girls (71% vs. 65%, respectively), a finding which was not evident among Albanian adolescents. Conversely, our finding on an inverse relationship with age is compatible with the multi-country HBSC report [1], where younger adolescents were more likely to report high family support.

In our study, family support levels were very similar among adolescents from more affluent families and in those from less affluent families. Conversely, the recent multi-country HBSC report indicated an overall positive relationship between high levels of family support and family affluence [1]. The greatest disparity between low- and high-affluence groups was observed in Bulgaria, with a difference of around 20% [1].

In the multi-country HBSC analysis [1], on average, girls reported a higher level of peer support than boys (62% vs. 55%, respectively), a gender-difference which was also evident in our study, albeit less strong. Furthermore, the inverse association with age in our study is in line with the multi-country HBSC report where the youngest adolescents consistently exhibited higher peer support levels than their older counterparts [1]. Similarly, the social gradient highlighted in the multi-country HBSC report [1] resembles our finding, where adolescents from more affluent families reported higher levels of peer support.

Study limitations

Our study may have some limitations. The cross-sectional design hinders causality, rendering the direction of associations between family and peer support and mental health uncertain. Other limitations may include the possibility of selection and/or information biases. Although we included a large and nationally representative sample of adolescents aged 11–15 years and obtained a very high response rate [25, 26], our findings cannot be generalized to children of this age-group who are not enrolled in the formal education system in Albania. Furthermore, notwithstanding the use of a standardized international instrument [27] previously validated in Albania [24], the possibility of information bias cannot be ruled out, especially for assessment of mental health outcomes, due to reliance on self-reported data that may introduce social desirability bias, recall inaccuracies, and subjectivity, which could potentially inflate effect sizes. Thus, the validity of mental health data may be compromised by adolescents’ inclination to provide socially desirable responses–typically underreporting negative symptoms and overreporting positive traits–as well as by recall bias, which can lead to underestimation of transient experiences or exaggeration of salient distressing events. Limited self-awareness regarding their own mental state may further compound these biases. Similarly, assessment of family and/or peer support among adolescents may be subject to information bias due to subjective perceptions, recall limitations, or a tendency to report socially desirable responses. Yet, as the HBSC is an anonymous questionnaire, neither teachers nor parents have access to the responses. As a matter of fact, subjective responses are highly valuable, as the HBSC collects children’s own views, opinions, and experiences. Regarding peer support, family support, and mental health, only the adolescents can truly express how they feel. Also, although analyses controlled for multiple sociodemographic and behavioural variables, unmeasured confounders–such as parental mental health, adverse childhood experiences, and school climate–may still have influenced the results. Additionally, dichotomization of continuous scales (high vs. low support) may have obscured more nuanced dose-response relationships. Furthermore, we did not analyse trends over time in the associations between family support and peer support with mental health indices using data from previous HBSC rounds conducted in Albania. Such a trend analysis would provide valuable evidence on how the role of family and peer support in shaping adolescent mental health has evolved across time, offering Albanian policymakers guidance on whether existing support structures remain effective or require adaptation to emerging social contexts.

Conclusions

Notwithstanding potential limitations, we obtained evidence on strong and consistent associations of peer support and especially family support with several mental health indices of Albanian adolescents. Our findings are generally in line with previous reports from HBSC studies conducted in many countries [1, 16, 19]. Also, notably, our study reveals persistence of cultural norms and familial resilience in Albania, which may explain the remarkably high levels of family support among adolescents in this country, despite the political and socio-economic transition from a socialist regime to a market-oriented system. Considering the cross-sectional design and reliance on the well-established HBSC instrument, the originality of our study lies primarily in the contextual focus and comparative insights rather than in novel measurement or analytical techniques.

Findings of our study have important policy implications for adolescent mental health in Albania and elsewhere. Considering the strong, independent associations between both family and peer support and improved mental health outcomes, national and local policies should prioritize strengthening social support systems within families and schools. Interventions aimed at enhancing parenting skills, fostering positive family communication, and building supportive peer environments through school-based programs could be particularly effective [31]. Additionally, mental health promotion strategies should explicitly incorporate family engagement components and peer-led initiatives, especially in school settings [1, 31].

In conclusion, rooted into Bronfenbrenner’s ecological model [20], our findings underscore the need for a holistic, multisectoral approach that integrates education, health, and social services to reinforce protective social relationships that are crucial for the mental health and psychological wellbeing of adolescents [1, 31]. Yet, results of this study reflect Albania’s specific socio-cultural context and may not fully generalize to settings with differently organized health systems, family structures, or peer dynamics.
