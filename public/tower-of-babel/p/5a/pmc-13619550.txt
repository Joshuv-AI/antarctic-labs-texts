Exploratory analysis of early changes in peripheral immune cell proportions and clinical response following methotrexate and biologic treatment in rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Sei Muraoka, Tomohiro Yamada, Nobuyuki Yasuda, Jungo Kakuta, Hiroshi Sato, Satoshi Mizutani, Makoto Kaburaki, Toshio Imai, Toshihiro Nanki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine the relationships between changes in the proportions of immune cell populations and clinical response at 2 weeks post-treatment with methotrexate (MTX), tumour necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors, or abatacept, with treatment efficacy at week 12 in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA).MethodsPeripheral blood samples were collected from 30 patients with RA who received MTX (n=10), a TNF inhibitor+MTX (n=10), and abatacept+MTX (n=10) in this observational study. Of these, 25 patients completed the 12-week follow-up and were included in the efficacy and correlation analyses (MTX n=9; TNF inhibitor+MTX n=8; abatacept+MTX n=8). Immune cell phenotypes were determined using 11-colour flow cytometry at baseline and 2 weeks after drug administration.ResultsBaseline proportions and changes in several immune cell types at 2 weeks post treatment correlated with the efficacy of arthritis treatment at week 12. Specifically, a higher baseline proportion and a greater decrease at week 2 in class-switched memory B cells following MTX administration were significantly associated with treatment efficacy for arthritis, as were the same changes in activated Th1 cells following abatacept administration. Increases in effector memory CD8+ T cells were also significantly correlated with the efficacy of TNF inhibitor treatment in RA patients.ConclusionThis exploratory study identified a potential association between baseline or early changes in immune cell populations and subsequent clinical responses in RA.Keywords: Abatacept, Methotrexate, Rheumatoid arthritis, Tumor necrosis factor inhibitors

INTRODUCTION

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is characterized by inflammatory arthritis, which leads to destruction of the joints, causing disability. Immune cells, including B cells, T cells, and monocytes/macrophages, in the synovial tissue and peripheral blood play critical roles in RA pathogenesis [1].

In Japan, methotrexate (MTX) has been used for RA since 1999, and biologics, including tumour necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors, interleukin-6 inhibitors, and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA4)-Ig fusion protein, have been used progressively since 2003. These therapies represent great strides in treatment and have improved the prognosis of RA patients. However, no single formulation is effective for all patients, and the treatment effects cannot be predicted in advance. Thus, it is hoped that future therapeutic agents can be pre-selected and tailored to each individual.

Reportedly, existing therapies alter the population of immune cells [2-4]. Proportions of various immune cell types at the start of treatment, or changes in these proportions early in treatment, may predict efficacy against arthritis. Although there have been several observational studies on the relationships among peripheral blood immune cells and the efficacies of various therapeutic drugs, no clear conclusions have been reached [3-8], possibly because of differences in the treatments and patient backgrounds.

In this study, we determined the proportions of different immune cell populations in the peripheral blood using multicolour flow cytometry. For exploratory analysis of early immune dynamics, we analysed correlations between the proportion of each population at the start of treatment or the change in proportion during the first 2 weeks after treatment, early immunologic changes, and the improvement in disease activity at week 12 in patients with RA.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

This observational study enrolled 30 patients with an RA diagnosis that fulfilled the classification criteria of the 2010 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism [9]. All patients visited Toho University Omori Medical Center between January 2018 and June 2020. Patients in the MTX group (n=10), who had not previously been treated with MTX, received standard dosing of MTX at 6~16 mg/week for 12 weeks, at the physician’s discretion. For the TNF inhibitor and abatacept groups, a TNF inhibitor (n=10, of whom 6 received certolizumab pegol, 2 etanercept, and 2 golimumab) or abatacept (n=10) were administered at doses as described in the package inserts for 12 weeks to RA patients who had previously begun receiving a stable dose of MTX (6~16 mg/week) at 4 weeks prior to the start of the study and who had not previously been treated with Janus kinase inhibitors. In the TNF inhibitor+MTX and abatacept+MTX groups, biological agents were initiated based on routine clinical judgment due to an inadequate response to MTX, in accordance with the Japanese treatment guideline [10]. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Toho University (A19097_A19001_A18066_A17061). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to blood collection.

Flow cytometry analysis

Peripheral blood was collected at baseline (before starting treatment) and at week 2 during treatment. A 4-mL sample of blood was transferred to a 50-mL conical tube, to which 36 mL 1×BD PharmLyse solution (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) was added and incubated at room temperature for 5 minutes to lyse the red blood cells. The lysed cells were centrifuged at 290×g for 5 minutes at room temperature, washed once in 50 mL ice-cold wash buffer (1% foetal bovine serum, phosphate-buffered saline with 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid), and resuspended in 4 mL ice-cold wash buffer. To prevent nonspecific antibody binding to immune cells, Fc receptors were blocked by incubating the cells with 400 μL human FcR Blocking Reagent (Miltenyi Biotec B.V. & Co. KG, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) for 30 minutes at 4°C in the dark. A 200-μL aliquot of the cell suspension was transferred to each well of a 96-well plate, centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. Next, a 50-μL cocktail of antibodies (Supplementary Table 1) was added and incubated for 30 minutes on ice in the dark. The cells were then washed with wash buffer, centrifuged at 290×g for 5 minutes, and resuspended in 200 μL wash buffer. The cells were quantified using multicolour flow cytometry (LSRFortessa X-20; BD Biosciences) and analysed with FlowJo (BD Biosciences). The phenotypes of immune cell subsets were determined based on surface marker-based immunophenotyping using comprehensive colour flow cytometric analysis (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Figure 1), in accordance with the Human Immunology Project [11,12].

Data analysis

The Kruskal–Wallis test and Fisher’s exact test were performed to detect statistically significant differences among the three treatment groups. When a significant difference was detected by the Kruskal–Wallis test, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn’s test. The Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test was used to detect statistically significant changes in immune cell populations between the baseline and 2-week measurements. Correlations among the change in 28-joint disease activity score (DAS28) based on erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and proportions of each immune cell population at baseline or the change from baseline to week 2 were assessed using Spearman’s coefficient. The level of significance was set at p<0.05. All analyses were conducted using Python 3.9 with libraries (Pandas [1.1.3], SciPy [1.2.0]).

RESULTS

Patient disposition and baseline characteristics

We included a total of 30 RA patients who started treatment with MTX (n=10) or with a TNF inhibitor (n=10) or abatacept (n=10) in addition to MTX. One patient in the MTX group discontinued MTX, two patients in the TNF inhibitor+MTX group discontinued certolizumab pegol, and two patients in the abatacept+MTX group discontinued abatacept prior to week 12 of the study. Patient demographics and immune cell populations at baseline were analysed in all three groups, and the dropout patients were excluded from measurements of immune cell changes at the 2-week treatment time point and the 12-week efficacy analysis (Figure 1). The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of each treatment group are provided in Table 1. There were no significant differences in age, disease duration, C-reactive protein level, ESR, clinical disease activity index, simplified disease activity index, or swollen joint count 28 among the three groups. The only statistically significant difference that was observed was in the tender joint count 28 (p=0.02).

Characteristics of immune cells at baseline

We examined the phenotypic characteristics of immune cells in the peripheral blood of RA patients at baseline in the three groups. While most cell types showed no between-group differences, there were significant between-group differences in the proportions of activated CD4+ T cells (p<0.01), activated CD8+ T cells (p=0.01), activated T helper (Th)1 cells (p<0.01), activated T follicular helper (Tfh) 1 cells (p=0.03), and activated regulatory T (Treg) cells (p<0.01) at baseline. Among these, statistically significant differences were observed in activated CD4+ T cells, activated CD8+ T cells, activated Th1 cells, and activated Treg cells between the MTX and MTX+abatacept groups (Table 2).

Correlations among the proportions of immune cells at baseline and the response to drug treatment

To evaluate the relationships among pre-treatment immune cell phenotypes and drug efficacy of MTX, TNF inhibitors, and abatacept, we analysed the correlations among the proportions of immune cell populations at baseline and improvement in DAS28-ESR at week 12 (week 12 DAS28-ESR score– baseline DAS28-ESR score). The negative correlation coefficients in Spearman’s correlation analysis indicated that higher proportions of certain immune cells at baseline were associated with better treatment responses at week 12. In the MTX group, significant negative correlations were observed in programmed cell death (PD)-1+CD8+ T cells (rho=–0.77, p=0.02), T-cell receptor (TCR) γδ+CD3++ T cells (rho=–0.75, p=0.02), and class-switched memory B (CSM B) cells (rho=–0.72, p=0.03) (Table 3). In the TNF inhibitors+MTX group, there were significant negative correlations with activated CD4+ cells (rho=–0.76, p=0.03), PD-1+CD8+ T cells (rho=–0.76, p=0.03), activated Th1 cells (rho=–0.79, p=0.02), and activated Th17 cells (rho=–0.76, p=0.03). In the abatacept+MTX group, significant negative correlations were observed for CD8+ effector memory (EM) T cells (rho=–0.75, p=0.03) and activated Th1 cells (rho=–0.79, p=0.02). Only those showing significant correlations in baseline cell proportion and DAS28-ESR improvement at week 12 are shown in Supplementary Figure 2.

Changes in immune cell populations from baseline to week 2 under each treatment

To evaluate the immediate effect of each treatment on immune cell populations, we examined immune cell changes in the peripheral blood from baseline to 2 weeks post treatment with MTX, TNF inhibitor+MTX, and abatacept+MTX therapy, as shown in Table 4. Only those patients who completed the 12 weeks of treatment in each group were included in the analysis of changes at week 2 (MTX: n=9; TNF inhibitor+MTX: n=8; abatacept+MTX: n=8). The MTX group showed significantly decreased proportions of activated CD4+ T cells (p<0.01), activated CD8+ T cells (p<0.01), PD-1+CD4+ T cells (p=0.01), PD-1+CD8+ T cells (p<0.01), activated Th1 cells (p<0.01), activated Th2 cells (p=0.01), CSM B cells (p=0.02), Treg cells (p=0.04), naïve Treg (NV Treg) cells (p=0.04), and activated Treg cells (p=0.02). No immune cell populations showed an immediate increase following MTX administration. In the TNF inhibitor+MTX group, treatment resulted in significantly decreased proportions of CD4+ T naïve cells (CD4 NV) and CTLA4+ CD8+ T cells (p=0.01 and p=0.04, respectively), and significantly increased proportions of CD4+ EM cells (CD4 EM) and CD8+ EM cells (CD8 EM) (p=0.01 and p=0.02, respectively). Treatment with abatacept+MTX significantly decreased the proportions of CD28−CD4+ T cells (p=0.01), activated CD4+ T cells (p=0.01), activated CD8+ T cells (p=0.02), TCRγδ+ CD3++ T cells (p=0.01), activated Th1 (p=0.01), Th2 (p=0.02), Th17 cells (p=0.01), plasmablasts (p=0.01), Treg cells (p=0.01), and PD-1+ Treg cells (p=0.01), but significantly increased that of CD56+CD16− natural killer (NK) cells (p=0.04). The cell populations listed in Supplementary Figure 3 are extracted from only these significantly altered cell populations.

Correlations among therapeutic effects and changes in immune cells at 2 weeks post treatment

Next, we analysed the correlations among changes in immune cell proportions at 2 weeks of treatment and improvement in DAS28-ESR scores at 12 weeks of treatment in the three groups (Table 5). The positive correlation coefficients in this analysis indicated that a decrease in an immune cell population from baseline to week 2 was correlated with improved efficacy at 12 weeks of treatment. In the MTX group, significant positive correlations were observed for TCRγδ+CD3++ cells (rho=0.72, p=0.03), activated Th17 cells (rho=0.68, p=0.04), CSM B cells (rho=0.75, p=0.02), and double negative B (DN B) cells (rho=0.69, p=0.04). Significant negative correlations were observed for intermediate monocytes (iMo) (rho=–0.73, p=0.02), Th1 (rho=–0.75, p=0.02), Th1/17 (rho=–0.70, p=0.04), and B cells (rho=–0.70, p=0.04) in the MTX group. DISCUSSION

Several reports have comprehensively analysed the correlations between immune cells and drug efficacy in RA patients using flow cytometry [3,13]. Most previous studies investigated predictive markers for drug treatment response at week 24, either from pre-treatment immune cell populations or changes in immune cell populations over 24 weeks of treatment [5-8]. However, there is no consensus on predictive markers of response to any of these drugs, possibly because such studies missed changes in immune cell populations early after treatment initiation. Therefore, we hypothesized that there exists an immune state of potential drug susceptibility and that changes in the specific immune cell populations targeted by that drug early after its administration may lead to its therapeutic effects. This is the first study to explore the immune cell subsets associated with specific RA drugs by investigating changes in the percentages of immune cell populations in peripheral blood during the first 2 weeks post administration and correlating them with therapeutic effects at 12 weeks in patients with RA.

We found that baseline proportions of certain immune cell phenotypes correlated with improvement in DAS28-ESR scores at week 12. In the MTX group, higher proportions of PD-1+CD8+ T cells, TCRγδ+CD3++ T cells, and CSM B cells at baseline were associated with greater improvement in RA disease activity. Likewise, greater improvement was associated with higher proportions of activated CD4+ T cells, PD-1+CD8+ T cells, activated Th1 cells, and activated Th17 cells in the TNF inhibitor+MTX group, and CD8 EM T cells and activated Th1 cells in the abatacept+MTX group. In previous studies, immune cells as baseline predictors of treatment efficacy have been inconsistent, with CD4 NV, CD16+CD14++ monocytes, CD4 EM, and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC) reported for MTX [3,5,7,14], and monocytes, CD4+CD28− T cells, CD8 NV, Th1 cells, Tfh cells, and CD24hiCD27+ regulatory B cells reported for abatacept [3,4,15-18]. These discrepancies may be due to differences in patient background and cell identification methods. In this study, a TNF inhibitor or abatacept was added to MTX for bio-naïve patients. Since we usually start treatment with MTX, adding a biological agent including a TNF inhibitor or abatacept only if necessary, the analysis of the baseline immune cell populations in this study may assist in the selection of treatment for such patients.

After 2 weeks of MTX use, we observed decreases in activated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, PD-1+CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, activated Th1 and Th2 cells, CSM B cells, Treg cells, NV Treg, and activated Treg cells. Some of these changes were reflected in the baseline proportions of immune cell populations in the TNF inhibitor and abatacept groups, which were also treated with MTX. It is suggested that baseline differences across treatment arms may reflect the pharmacological effects of prior MTX exposure rather than intrinsic disease characteristics in the TNF inhibitor+MTX and abatacept+MTX groups. Importantly, all correlation analyses were conducted within each treatment group and did not involve comparisons between treatment groups. Treatment with a TNF inhibitor or abatacept further altered immune cell proportions. TNF inhibitors decreased CD4 NV and CTLA4+CD8+ T cells, and increased CD4 EM and CD8 EM. Treatment with abatacept, as a T-cell activation inhibitor, decreased CD28−CD4+ T cells, activated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, TCRγδ+CD3++ T cells, activated Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells, plasmablasts, Treg cells, and PD-1+ Treg cells, but increased CD56+CD16− NK cells. MTX reportedly decreases B cells, Th1 cells, and Th17 cells after 3 months of treatment [2], while TNF inhibitors have been shown to decrease CD4 and CD8 NV, and pDC, and to increase CD4 and CD8 EM, Th17 cells, Tfh cells, and IgG memory B cells at 24 weeks [3]. Other reports showed that TNF inhibitors increased Treg cells at 6 months [4] and B cells at 3 months [2]. Abatacept has been shown to decrease CD8 NV, activated CD8, Th17 cells, and Treg cells, and to increase CD4 NV and CD8+ memory T cells at 6 months [3,4]. Some of these changes observed after 3~6 months treatment in other studies are consistent with our results measured at 2 weeks of treatment, indicating that part of the alteration in immune cell populations develops early after initiating treatment. The discrepancy between our results and those of previous reports may be attributable to differences in the duration of the treatment prior to analysis.

In the MTX group, decreased proportions of TCRγδ+CD3++, activated Th17 cells, CSM B cells, and DN B cells, and increased proportions of iMo, Th1 cells, Th1/17 cells, and B cells at 2 weeks post treatment, were correlated with greater improvement in arthritis at week 12. Increases in cMo, CD8 EM, and NV Treg in the TNF inhibitor+MTX group, and decreases in ncMo, activated Th1 cells, Tfh17 cells, and Tfh1/17 cells, and increases in cMo, CD4 NV, Th2 cells, and activated Tfh2 cells in the abatacept+MTX group, were correlated with greater improvement in arthritis. These altered immune cell populations at 2 weeks post treatment, together with their baseline proportions, may represent candidate correlates of treatment response (Supplementary Figure 3).

A previous study showed that CSM B cells (CD3−CD19+IgD−CD27+) produced TNF in healthy subjects [19]. Although there are no reports of inflammatory cytokine production from CSM B cells in RA patients, the pathological significance of CSM B cells can be inferred from the infiltration of CSM B cells into the synovium of RA patients [20]. Additionally, CSM B cells in peripheral blood from RA patients exhibit higher expression of RANKL compared with that in healthy controls. These cells, which are abundant in synovial fluid, are thought to contribute to joint destruction by promoting osteoclast differentiation in a RANKL-dependent manner [21], consistent with the rituximab-mediated reductions in disease activity and bone destruction in RA in clinical trials [22,23]. While circulating CSM B cells decrease as a result of rituximab treatment, CD27+IgD– cells are reportedly higher in rituximab non-responders than in responders, suggesting a role in the pathogenesis of RA [24,25]. In this study, MTX had significantly reduced the proportion of CSM B cells at 2 weeks post administration, with the degree of this reduction at 2 weeks, as well as a higher proportion at baseline, correlating with the efficacy of MTX at 12 weeks. These results suggested that CSM B cells may be associated with the efficacy of MTX in RA. Interestingly, CSM B cells were not decreased at baseline in the TNF inhibitor and abatacept groups, despite their co-treatment with MTX. Thus, these patients may have had disease that was refractory to treatment with MTX.

Interestingly, RA patients treated with the TNF inhibitor golimumab reportedly had a higher frequency of CD8 EM than those treated without biological agents [26]. In our study, the increase in CD8 EM at week 2 correlated with improvement in disease activity at week 12 in the TNF inhibitor+MTX group, suggesting that early changes in CD8 EM may be associated with subsequent treatment response.

Abatacept significantly reduced activated Th1 cells, and the degree of this reduction together with a higher proportion at baseline correlated with the effectiveness of the drug at week 12. Th1 cells are known to be involved in the pathogenesis of RA, producing cytokines such as TNF and interferon-γ in the joints [27,28]. Th1-type cytokine levels in the serum and synovial fluid are also higher in RA patients than in healthy controls, and have been correlated with disease activity scores [29]. Therefore, abatacept might decrease cytokine production. Additionally, it has been reported that abatacept inhibits the function of CD28-expressing Th1 cells [30]. Thus, it is mechanistically reasonable that a decrease in activated Th1 cells was associated with the efficacy of abatacept.

In previous clinical study, Toho University Omori Medical Center reported that treatment with E6011, an anti-fractalkine monoclonal antibody, significantly reduced the proportion of peripheral blood CD16+ ncMo in RA patients in the first 2 weeks of administration [31,32]. However, in the present study, treatment with MTX, TNF inhibitors, and abatacept did not alter the proportion of ncMo, suggesting that the type of immune cells altered by each drug may depend on its mode of action. Furthermore, the baseline proportion of a cell type is affected by the patient’s background and thus may not necessarily be predictive of treatment response. It is important for clinical applications to show significant changes early after treatment, because these changes may provide insight into subsequent treatment response. The present study demonstrated that the baseline proportions and/or 2-week treatment alterations of several immune cell types, including CSM B cells, CD8 EM, and activated Th1 cells, may represent candidate correlates of response to MTX, TNF inhibitors, and abatacept, respectively.

The study has several limitations. First, the observational study design may have led to selection bias. Second, potential confounding factors, including patient background characteristics, concomitant medications, and comorbidities, should be considered. However, the 12-week observation period was considered sufficient to evaluate treatment effects, as changes in concomitant medications were minimal and consisted mainly of glucocorticoid dose reduction and slight adjustments in MTX dose. Baseline differences in clinical parameters among treatment groups, including tender joint count, may also have influenced DAS28-ESR-based response assessment. The difference in tender joint counts may be due to MTX administration. This difference could have influenced the results. However, in this study, we primarily analyzed immune cell proportions and treatment responses within each treatment group rather than comparing among treatment groups, suggesting that the variation in tender joint counts is unlikely to have affected the results. Third, in this study, Th and Tfh subsets were defined using surface marker-based immunophenotyping according to the Human Immunology Project [11,12]. These definitions did not include transcription factor or intracellular cytokine analyses, which could provide more precise lineage identification. Similarly, Tfh cells were defined as CXCR5+ CD4+ T cells without additional markers such as PD-1. Therefore, the immune subsets analyzed in this study should be interpreted as phenotypic populations rather than fully functionally characterized cell lineages. Fourth, several immune cell populations analyzed in this study were present at low frequencies, which may introduce variability in flow cytometry-based quantification and gating. Fifth, this exploratory, single-center study had a small sample size, which may have resulted in insufficient statistical power for the correlation analyses. Moreover, multiple correlation tests were performed without adjustment, increasing the risk of Type I error and false-positive findings. Future studies should include larger, prospective validation cohorts, apply appropriate statistical corrections such as the False Discovery Rate or Bonferroni adjustment, and further evaluate clinical applicability, including the determination of potential cutoff values for immune cell fractions. Finally, we clinicians cannot immediately apply this study finding to our daily clinical practice. However, it is currently difficult to predict treatment efficacy for RA, making treatment selection challenging. Therefore, this research may be useful for guiding treatment decisions in the future.

CONCLUSION

This exploratory study identified potential associations between baseline or early changes in immune cell populations and subsequent clinical responses in RA. These findings may provide preliminary insights into the immune correlates of treatment response. However, they require validation in larger, independent cohorts.
