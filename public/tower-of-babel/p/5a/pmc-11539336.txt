Koebnerized vitiligo vulgaris following laser treatment of lichen planus pigmentosus successfully treated with topical ruxolitinib
Authors: Tarek Bandali, Lindsey Voller, Justin Ko, Nour Kibbi
Published: 2024

Introduction

Dermal pigmentation is notoriously difficult to treat. Topical agents penetrate poorly into the dermis, and many pigmentary disorders arise in darker skin, which is more prone to the adverse effects of skin lightening treatments. Pigmentary disorders driven by inflammation have the added challenge of needing to address the inflammatory component to prevent further deposition of melanin. One such disorder is lichen planus pigmentosus (LPP), an uncommon variant of lichen planus that is often resistant to treatment. Treatment of LPP is controversial; modalities reported include topical steroids, topical tacrolimus, oral dapsone, oral isotretinoin, oral tranexamic acid, and chemical peels.1 Laser-based modalities have also been successful in the treatment of LPP,2 but expectations must be set that multiple treatments are needed and care must be taken to ensure fluences remain low to prevent undesirable pigmentary alteration, particularly among patients with skin of color.

We present a case of vitiligo vulgaris precipitated by laser treatment of LPP and successfully treated with topical ruxolitinib.

Case presentation

A 42-year-old woman of South Asian descent, Fitzpatrick type IV, presented with a 7-year history of progressively darkening rash on the face and neck. Past medical history was significant for Hashimoto’s thyroiditis. Biopsy of an area of hyperpigmentation revealed a sparse lichenoid infiltrate and pigment incontinence in the dermis. Given these histopathologic findings and photodistribution of the rash, a diagnosis of LPP was favored over erythema dyschromicum perstans (Fig 1, A). The patient elected for treatment with Q-switch neodynium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet laser (Fotona QX Max). Following a test spot on the right supraclavicular neck, she underwent 6 treatments (6 mm, 2.5 J, 5 Hz, 1-2 passes) on the face with noticeable improvement (Fig 1, B).

A, Initial presentation of LPP on the face. B, Presentation at follow-up 3 months following treatment completion. LPP, Lichen planus pigmentosus.

Given sustained improvement on the face 3 months after her last laser treatment, she elected to treat the neck (Fig 2, A). She noted little improvement after 4 treatments (6 mm, 2.5 J, 5 Hz, 1-2 passes), thus settings were increased marginally for the fifth treatment (6 mm 2.8 J, 10 ms, 1 pass). She developed expectant post-treatment erythema without pain or blisters. Within 2 weeks of this last treatment, she was noted to have hypopigmented macules coalescing into patches on the bilateral neck particularly along the horizontal neck lines within the area of laser treatment (Fig 2, B). Wood’s lamp examination revealed depigmentation, suggestive of vitiligo (Fig 2, C). No improvement of the neck patches was observed after 7 weeks of observation. At her follow up, the patient pointed to a chronically depigmented patch on her lower back suggestive of undiagnosed vitiligo vulgaris. She was started on tacrolimus 0.1% ointment and clobetasol 0.05% ointment alternating every 2 weeks. Given lack of improvement, she was switched to topical ruxolitinib 1.5% cream twice daily and experienced near complete repigmentation within 2 months of continued use (Fig 2, D).

D, Successful repigmentation following 2-month course of ruxolitinib 1.5% cream. LPP, Lichen planus pigmentosus.

Discussion

This case highlights the challenges of treating dermal pigmentation at photoexposed sites in skin of color. There are at least 6 reports of laser-induced vitiligo vulgaris, summarized in a systematic review by Post et al.3 The following wavelengths were used: 755 and 1064 nm for laser hair removal (n = 2), 585 nm for port-wine stain (n = 2), intense pulse light for lentigines (n = 1), and an ablative laser (not otherwise specified) for melasma (n = 1). Where reported, the patients were of the following Fitzpatrick skin types: II/III (n = 1), III (n = 1), and IV (n = 2). Two cases were located on the legs, 2 on the face, 1 on the shoulder, and 1 on the trunk. Our patient’s vitiligo presented on the neck, an anatomic site where the skin is thin, photo-exposed, and relatively poor in stem cells and hair follicles, which makes repigmentation after laser treatment challenging.

Our patient was safely treated with Q-switch neodynium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet (1064 nm) laser on the face and did not develop signs of vitiligo on the neck until the fluence was marginally increased at her final treatment. Typically, if fluences are too high, Q-switch lasers can induce melanogenesis and subsequently hyperpigmentation; hypopigmentation and depigmentation are less common, though they have been reported at much higher fluences typically used for tattoo removal, where a photothermal effect is desired.4 Additionally, compared to other Q-switch wavelengths such as Q-switch potassium titanyl phosphate (KTP, 532 nm) or Q-switch Alexandrite (755 nm), Q-switch neodynium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet is safer in skin of color as it more effectively bypasses epidermal melanin. In our case, it is interesting to consider how the background of lichenoid dermatitis or the history of vitiligo in another anatomic region may have predisposed skin in this area to undergo autoimmune melanocyte destruction. Furthermore, the female neck lacks hair follicles which make it a poor environment for wound healing given the lack of hair follicle stem cells. This may have contributed to the patient’s lack of spontaneous pigment recovery after laser therapy perpetuating an inflammatory reaction that may have incited the autoimmune cascade.

Although the patient did not respond to topical tacrolimus and clobetasol, she experienced near complete repigmentation after 2 months of treatment with ruxolitinib. Of the 6 previously reported cases of laser-induced vitiligo, treatments included nbUVB combined with either topical tacrolimus or pigmenting antioxidants,5,6 topical fluocinolone acetonide,7 and 308 nm excimer laser with and without topical tacrolimus.8,9 Complete repigmentation was only reported in 1 case, 3 months after treatment with 308 nm excimer laser. Topical ruxolitinib is Food and Drug Administration-approved for the treatment of atopic dermatitis and nonsegmental vitiligo vulgaris, and at least 1 study suggests it may be effective for lichen planus.10 Ruxolitinib, a Janus Kinase inhibitor was a particularly attractive option for this patient given that her laser-induced vitiligo was superimposed on the inflammatory background of LPP, though additional studies are needed to explore its effects on LPP.

This report adds to the limited number of cases describing laser-induced koebnerized vitiligo and suggests that topical ruxolitinib may be effective for repigmentation in this context.
