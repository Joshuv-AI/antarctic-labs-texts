Elucidating psychosocial outcome profiles as a function of aggregate genetic risk for suicide attempt and alcohol use disorder
Authors: Alexis C Edwards, Henrik Ohlsson, Mallory Stephenson, Séverine Lannoy, Casey Crump, Jan Sundquist, Kenneth S Kendler, Kristina Sundquist
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetic factors contribute to alcohol use disorder (AUD) and suicide attempt (SA) co-occurrence. Whether individuals with high genetic liability to one outcome versus the other, or to both, differ in terms of their risk for other psychosocial outcomes is unexplored. To this end, the authors conducted a cohort study of Swedish individuals born 1965–1985, with follow-up through December 31, 2018. Using deciles of family genetic risk scores (FGRS), a measure of aggregate genetic liability, they characterized individuals as being in a “risk” group with high/low FGRS or a “reference” with median FGRS. Within each of five comparisons, they calculated correlations between genetic profile and psychosocial outcomes. Groups included up to N = 536 312 individuals. The mean (SD) age at end of follow-up was 44.1 (6.1) years. High genetic liability to both AUD and SA was most prominently correlated with negative outcomes, e.g., r = 0.51 for drug use disorder. High FGRSSA or FGRSAUD, irrespective of genetic loading for the other, was less strongly correlated with negative outcomes, as was having high FGRSAUD but low FGRSSA. The risk subsample with high FGRSSA and low FGRSAUD were least likely to experience negative outcomes (r < 0.1) and more likely to exhibit positive educational outcomes (r≥0.1) relative to their reference subsample. These analyses indicate that aggregate genetic risk profiles for AUD and SA are differentially correlated with a range of negative psychosocial outcomes. Having low genetic liability to AUD is potentially protective for some outcomes even alongside elevated SA risk. These findings underscore the imperative for further genetic dissection of these frequently comorbid conditions.Subject terms: Genetics, Human behaviour

Introduction

Suicidal behavior and alcohol use disorder (AUD) frequently co-occur [1–4] and prior evidence indicates that comorbidity is due in part to shared genetic liability [5, 6]. However, their genetic correlation is considerably less than one, i.e., suicidal behavior is influenced to some extent by genetic factors that are unrelated to AUD risk, and vice versa [5]. Although both conditions are genetically correlated with other forms of psychopathology [7–10], little is understood vis-à-vis how aggregate genetic liability to both suicidality and AUD, or to only one or the other, is differentially related to other psychosocial outcomes. Further investigation of this question has the potential to improve the identification of risk indicators and predict the sequelae, clinical and otherwise, of these conditions.

Both suicidal behavior and AUD are serious public health concerns. While the incidence of suicidal behavior fluctuates annually and varies by country and sociodemographic features, there were over 49 000 suicide deaths in the US in 2023 [11] and over 720 000 worldwide [12]. Non-fatal suicide attempt (SA) is far more common, with up to 48 attempts for every suicide death in the US in 2022 [13], and SA is one of the most reliable predictors of later death by suicide [14, 15]. In addition to the personal suffering that suicidality entails, there are steep economic consequences: One study estimated that self-harm cost $510 billion annually in the US from 2015–2020 [16] through its impact on medical expenses, reduced quality of life, and other factors.

Similarly, AUD entails a range of intrapersonal, interpersonal, and economic consequences. It is one of the most common psychiatric illnesses, with an estimated 400 million individuals worldwide having a lifetime AUD [17], and harmful alcohol use accounting for 4.7% of the global disease burden in 2019 [18]. The prevalence of alcohol problems and harmful use are highest in high and upper-middle income regions [19]. In the US, excessive drinking cost $249 billion in 2010 due to costs for health care, productivity loss, and other expenses [20].

In the current study, we examine whether genetic liability to suicidality or AUD, or to both of these, are differentially related to other psychosocial outcomes. Our research question was motivated by the degree of comorbidity between AUD and SA – one US study found that, among individuals with AUD or SA, 12.2% were comorbid [21] – and the increasingly urgent need to identify indicators of latent risk. The high degree of polygenicity of each condition [9, 10] and their substantial but incomplete genetic correlation [5] will correspond to disparate combinations of relative genetic liability for AUD and/or SA, which may result in qualitatively or quantitatively distinct patterns of psychosocial correlates. Clarifying these profiles could help inform efforts by health care providers to evaluate risk of AUD and/or SA even in the absence of genetic information.

To pursue our research aims, we use a genetically informative Swedish birth cohort to classify individuals based on their aggregate genetic liability for SA and AUD, and to calculate correlations between these genetic profiles and a range of psychosocial outcomes. These include educational measures, marital status, psychopathology, and receipt of social benefits (e.g., unemployment) that are indicators of financial precarity. Our primary aims are: (i) to clarify how genetic liability underlying SA and/or AUD could manifest outside the context of these conditions themselves, and (ii) to consider whether distinct genetic risk profiles – e.g., high genetic liability to SA but not AUD, or vice versa – are differentially related to psychosocial outcomes, some of which could be considered risk indicators or opportunities for prevention/intervention. Prior studies suggest that the extent to which genetic factors contribute to suicidal behavior and/or AUD is comparable between Sweden and other high-income countries [5, 22–24]. Detailed national registry data on conditions and exposures relevant to public health outcomes alongside the genetically informative nature of this population enables us to address these research questions in a manner not possible in other contexts.

Methods

Sample

We collected information on individuals from Swedish population-based registers with national coverage linking each person’s unique personal identification number (which was replaced with a serial number by Statistics Sweden to preserve confidentiality). Ethical approval for this study was granted by the regional ethical review board in Lund (No. 2008/409 and later amendments). Participant consent was not required. Our database consisted of all individuals born in Sweden between 1965 and 1985 (N = 1 798 912) to Swedish parents (to ensure sufficient power for genetic risk calculation) followed up through 12-31-2018, with a mean (SD) age at end of follow-up of 43.9 (9.1).

Data resources

We used data from a range of national registers as described in detail in the Supplementary Table 1. We also used information from Primary Care. This is a research dataset including individual-level almost nationwide information on clinical diagnoses from primary health care centers [25]. These resources enable us to identify relatives as needed to generate Family Genetic Risk Scores (FGRS; see below). They further provide information on the exposure and outcome variables (described below) through the use of medical, criminal, educational, health insurance, and military records. At the end of the follow-up period, the registers cover nearly 100% of the Swedish population.

Exposure variable definition

The exposure was a function of aggregate genetic risk for SA or AUD, operationalized as FGRS (FGRSSA and FGRSAUD). The FGRS are calculated from morbidity risks for disorders in 1st–5th degree relatives, controlling for cohabitation effects (see Supplementary Table 2). The scores, which adjust for cohabitation, are based on phenotypes in extended relatives rather that from molecular data, and have been shown to reflect components of genetic liability that are complementary to polygenic scores based on molecular genetic data [26]. FGRS are standardized as described in Supplementary Table 2.

Similar to a previous study [27], we divided the total cohort (of nearly 1.8 million) based on FGRSAUD and/or FGRSSA. In each of five groups, “high” genetic risk was operationalized as being in the highest FGRS decile for the outcome(s) of interest; “median” genetic risk reflected being in the middle two deciles; and “low” genetic risk corresponded to being in the lowest one or two FGRS deciles (depending on group sample size). In each group, the reference subsample had median FGRS for AUD and/or SA. A visual depiction of the genetic profile of each group is provided in Fig. 1, and groups are named based on the genetic profile of the subsample of interest. The first group (N = 536 160) consisted of individuals with high versus median FGRSAUD, irrespective of FGRSSA; it is termed “High AUDG”, where the “G” denotes genetic liability. The second group (N = 536 312), called “High SAG”, included individuals with high versus median FGRSSA, irrespective of FGRSAUD. The “High AUDG/High SAG” group (N = 179 639) compared those with high FGRSSA and FGRSAUD versus those with median risk for both. The fourth group (N = 151 153), “High AUDG/Low SAG”, consisted of individuals with high FGRSAUD and low FGRSSA, compared to those at median risk for both. Individuals in the final group (N = 152 865), “Low AUDG/High SAG”, had low FGRSAUD and high FGRSSA, compared to those at median risk for both. For simplicity, we will refer to individuals in each group with the “median” FGRS as the reference subsample, and those selected for high/low FGRS as the risk subsample. We note that the reference subsample is not identical across groups: Comparisons across groups are conceptual rather than technical. The first two groups essentially serve as baseline conditions for comparison to the remaining groups, whose profiles are of particular interest. Sample sizes for each group are provided in Table 1.

Outcome variable definition

In the database we also include four domains of outcome variables. Educational outcomes were operationalized by academic achievement at age 16 (AA) and educational attainment (EA); Family outcomes were captured by registrations for marriage (MAR) and divorce (DIV); Psychiatric/Psychological outcomes were based on lifetime registrations of anxiety disorders (AD), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), bipolar disorder (BD), drug use disorder (DUD), major depression (MD), posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and a resilience score (available for males only); and Socioeconomic outcomes were registrations for receipt of social welfare (SW), unemployment (UE), early retirement (ER), residing in deprived area (DEP) and sick leave (SL). Supplementary Table 1 provides details for all variables.

Statistical analyses

Within each sample, we calculated the tetrachoric/polychoric correlation (for AA we used the biserial correlation as AA was measured on a continuous scale) between the two selected groups (e.g., risk subsamples with high FGRSSA versus reference groups with median FGRSSA) for the occurrence of each of the selected outcomes. That is, for example, a two-by-two table from which the correlation calculated would be the number of individuals in the risk subsample without and with a particular disorder and the number of individuals in the reference subsample without and with that same disorder. These correlations provide standardized, prevalence-adjusted associations that are comparable across outcomes with different scales. The method assumes that each observed binary/ordinal variable reflects an underlying latent normally distributed variable with fixed thresholds. Higher correlations would reflect greater differences in risk for that disorder between the two subsamples. We included three regression terms for year of birth, sex, and county of residence on the thresholds. In a sensitivity analysis we excluded all individuals with AUD or SA and replicated all analyses. We used Mplus software version 7.31 [28] and SAS 9.4 [29] software for all analyses. Error bars represent 95% confidence intervals and we relied on these, rather than p-values, for statistical inference.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Table 1 presents the sample sizes and risk/reference distributions for each analytic group. The number of risk and reference subsamples varied across group: The High AUDG and High SAG groups included ~536 K individuals (including ~100 K at risk), the High AUDG/High SAG group consisted of ~189 K individuals ( ~ 33 K at risk), and the High AUDG/Low SAG and Low AUDG/High SAG groups included ~150 K individuals (with ~23 K at risk). While patterns of poorer outcomes for educational attainment and academic achievement were observed among the risk subsamples, a notable difference was those in the last group – Low AUDG/High SAG – exhibited higher levels of both relative to their reference subsample. With respect to family-related outcomes, similar differences were observed. Overall, high risk subsamples were less likely to have been married, and if they had married, were more likely to be divorced.

For each of the six psychiatric outcomes, the prevalence was higher among at-risk subsamples in each, though the magnitude of difference varied considerably. For example, within the High AUDG/High SAG group, ADHD was nearly 5 times as common in the at-risk subsample as in the reference subsample, while major depression was approximately twice as common; drug use disorders were 6-fold more common in High AUDG/High SAG subsamples versus their reference, but only 1.39-fold more common in Low AUDG/High SAG subsamples versus their own reference subsample. At-risk subsamples also exhibited higher rates of financial hardship indicators, such as receipt of social welfare, long-term unemployment, and early retirement (similar to long-term disability in the United States).

Education outcomes

With the exception of the Low AUDG/High SAG group, risk subsamples were more likely to have lower levels of academic achievement and educational attainment. The most substantial correlations were within the High AUDG/High SAG group (r = −0.30 to −0.35), while in other cases the correlations were quite small and negative (e.g., r = −0.04 to −0.03 in Group 4). In the Low AUDG/High SAG group, the magnitude of correlations with these outcomes were also small but positive (r = 0.09–0.11).

Family outcomes

For the first four groups, risk status was slightly inversely correlated with having ever been married (r’s ranged from −0.08 to −0.06); for the Low AUDG/High SAG group, the correlation was negative but did not differ from 0. Among individuals who had been married, correlations with divorce were positive and significant, but small (0.03 to 0.12), across all groups.

Psychiatric/psychological outcomes

In general, the strongest correlations between risk/reference status and psychiatric outcomes were observed in the High AUDG/High SAG group, within which the at-risk subsample had high genetic liability for both AUD and SA. Correlations in the first two groups (High AUDG and High SAG) were slightly lower; estimates for the High AUDG/Low SAG group were lower still; and correlations within the Low AUDG/High SAG group for these outcomes were lowest, ranging from only 0.04 to 0.05. The correlation between High AUDG/High SAG risk/reference status and DUD was the strongest observed across all categories and groups (r = 0.51 [0.50; 0.51]). For the first four groups, the highest magnitude correlation within psychiatric outcomes was for DUD; furthermore, relatively strong correlations were also observed for ADHD, the other primary externalizing disorder. Correlations with the internalizing disorders (MD, AD, and PTSD) were lower overall within these groups. In contrast, the Low AUDG/High SAG group was the only one whose highest correlations were with AD and MD, though these were weak. This was also the only group where being at-risk was positively associated with resilience.

Socioeconomic outcomes

The strongest correlations between risk/reference status and adverse socioeconomic outcomes were observed in the High AUDG/High SAG group, where they ranged from 0.16–0.47. For the first four groups (excluding Low AUDG/High SAG), the highest magnitude correlation within socioeconomic outcomes was for social welfare receipt. Correlation patterns were quite similar across those four groups, though the magnitude of effect varied. In contrast, correlations between Low AUDG/High SAG risk/reference status and socioeconomic outcomes were consistently very low. For sick leave, neighborhood deprivation, and unemployment, the correlations were negative (i.e., individuals in the reference group were more likely to have experienced these adverse socioeconomic conditions); the correlation with social welfare receipt was not significant; and a positive correlation was observed only for early retirement (r = 0.07 [0.05; 0.09]).

Secondary analyses

We pursued a sensitivity analysis in which we excluded all individuals with AUD and SA to determine whether our initial findings were driven by this subgroup. The resulting correlations, depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1, shifted modestly in magnitude but did not substantively differ.

Finally, to disambiguate whether findings from the Low AUDG/High SAG group are due to the potentially protective status of having low FGRSAUD versus high FGRSSA, we conducted a post hoc analysis in which one subsample had low FGRSAUD but median FGRSSA, while the reference subsample had median FGRS for both AUD and SA. The test subsample in this case has a lower genetic burden than the reference subsample, hence we do not refer to it as a “risk” subsample. A direct comparison with the Low AUDG/High SAG group is depicted in Supplementary Fig. 2. For educational outcomes, we observed small positive correlations with test/reference status, with point estimates exceeding those for the Low AUDG/High SAG group. We observed a small inverse correlation with divorce, i.e., the subsample with low FGRSAUD were less likely to get divorced than the reference subsample. Likewise, test/reference status was weakly but significantly inversely correlated with all the examined psychiatric diagnoses, with the exception of bipolar disorder, for which confidence intervals spanned 0; the correlation with resilience was weakly positive. Finally, we observed weak but significant inverse correlations with all adverse socioeconomic outcomes other than early retirement.

Discussion

In the current study, we aimed to characterize psychosocial profiles among individuals who were classified according to their relative aggregate genetic risk for alcohol use disorder and suicide attempt. These outcomes often co-occur, and previous studies have found evidence that the co-occurrence is likely attributable to both shared genetic liability [30] and a causal pathway [31]. Key findings were as follows: (i) Being at high genetic risk of both AUD and SA, relative to median risk for both (i.e., High AUDG/High SAG), was consistently most strongly correlated with poorer outcomes across educational, family, psychiatric, and socioeconomic categories; (ii) individuals at high genetic risk for AUD or SA were less strongly, but still consistently, associated with adverse outcomes, with those in the former group faring more poorly; (iii) within the considerably smaller High AUDG/Low SAG group, patterns of correlations overall resembled those in the first three groups, though at generally lower magnitudes; and (iv) the Low AUDG/High SAG group exhibited considerably different correlation patterns, such that correlations with all categories of outcomes were quite low, and risk subsamples were likely to have better educational outcomes than reference groups. Thus, considering bivariate family history-based genetic liabilities could provide potentially informative insight to risk profiles for a wide range of psychosocial outcomes.

The fact that individuals with a high FGRS family history of both AUD and SA are most likely to experience adverse outcomes is common sensical, but our findings add empirical support to this intuition, demonstrating that such individuals likely warrant particular attention. Notably, being in the risk subsample in any of the first three groups was correlated with adverse outcomes even early in life – namely, in risk subsamples’ lower academic achievement at age 16 and lower overall educational attainment (though the latter could stretch into adulthood). This could reflect prodromal difficulties related to AUD/SA. In addition, prior studies have revealed a complex association between educational/cognitive factors and AUD [32–36] and SA [37, 38], including evidence of a protective causal effect of academic achievement and educational attainment [39–41]. The beneficial causal impact of educational measures extends to other psychiatric and substance use outcomes [41–43]. Thus, early interventions that improve educational outcomes might actually reduce the need for subsequent increased resource allocation.

An unexpected feature of our findings is that being in the at-risk subsample within the Low AUDG/High SAG group was correlated with several advantageous outcomes (educational measures, resilience, lower likelihood of living in a deprived neighborhood, and lower likelihood of >1 year of unemployment or sick leave). However, those correlations – alongside the associations with adverse outcomes – were quite weak. A conservative interpretation of these results is that these persons are generally quite comparable to reference groups with respect to psychosocial outcomes. This contrasts slightly to risk subsamples in the High AUDG/Low SAG group, who bear the complementary genetic burden. The profile of that group presents as a less severely affected version of the first three, and we did not observe correlations with advantageous outcomes. However, given the attenuation of positive correlations with negative outcomes relative to the first three groups, it is reasonable to conclude that having low genetic liability to SA does confer some degree of protection against adverse outcomes, though to a lesser degree than having low genetic liability to AUD. Future studies could perhaps further dissect the findings from the High AUDG/Low SAG and Low AUDG/High SAG groups, particularly taking into consideration prior evidence that a non-trivial proportion of suicide deaths [44] and attempts [45] occur among individuals with no known history of psychiatric illness who may otherwise be high-functioning.

Given the patterns of results across these groups, we considered the possibility that having low genetic liability for AUD – which applied only to the at-risk subsample of the Low AUDG/High SAG group in the primary analyses – conveys a general protective effect against adverse psychosocial outcomes that largely outweighs the potential negative consequences of having high genetic liability to SA. The inverse is less well supported given positive correlations with adverse outcomes in the High AUDG/Low SAG group. Furthermore, a scenario in which high FGRSSA is somehow protective is not supported by results within the High SAG group, where the risk subsample has high FGRSSA but still experience poorer outcomes. Both SA and AUD have been genetically and phenotypically associated with various externalizing behaviors and impulsivity [46–50], which in turn are associated with higher rates of social dysfunction [51, 52]. Our findings indicate that the risk subsample in the Low AUDG/High SAG group are considerably less likely to exhibit these outcomes than risk subsamples in other groups. Yet the possibility that the at-risk subsample of the Low AUDG/High SAG, relative to those in the High AUDG/Low SAG group, simply represent individuals with a more “internalizing” phenotype is not borne out by the results: Correlations with major depression, anxiety disorders, and PTSD were only weakly positive, and were lower than for the latter group.

Our post hoc analysis aimed to disambiguate the potentially protective role of low FGRSAUD versus high FGRSSA. Given that the only significant correlations with risk/reference status were positive for advantageous outcomes (e.g., better educational outcomes) and negative for adverse outcomes (e.g., divorce, several psychiatric disorders), we infer that low FGRSAUD is the driving force underlying the initially unexpected Low AUDG/High SAG results. As with our other findings, replication in an independent sample is warranted. In the interim, clinicians who learn of a patient’s dense family history of suicidality should still consider those individuals to be at elevated risk – even in circumstances where they enjoy elevated social capital or exhibit few other risk indicators – as the risk subsample in the Low AUDG/High SAG group had poorer outcomes relative to our post hoc group.

A number of extensions of the current approach can be pursued in samples for which molecular data are available. Polygenic scores for AUD and SA, divided into deciles, can be used to assign risk/reference status and evaluate whether phenotypic outcome profiles reflect similar patterns as reported in the current study. In addition, a multivariate GWAS could identify specific variants that are associated with increased risk of SA but decreased risk of AUD (or vice versa); secondary analyses (e.g., gene-based and tissue enrichment analyses) could be used to further clarify the biological etiology of SA or AUD in this narrow context. Although the at-risk subsample in the Low AUDG/High SAG group did exhibit some minor elevations in risk of adverse outcomes (e.g., divorce, early retirement, and psychiatric disorders), in other respects they could be considered relatively high-functioning (e.g., higher academic achievement and educational attainment, lower likelihood of living in a deprived neighborhood) and therefore of potential interest for additional research on resilience.

We note several limitations to the current study. First, our estimates of aggregate genetic risk for AUD and SA are derived from family-based data rather than molecular data. The scores are adjusted for a wide range of potential factors including cohabitation; furthermore, they have been compared to genotype-based approaches and found to reflect the same underlying liability dimension for psychiatric illnesses [26, 53]. However, replication in samples for which molecular data is available would bolster confidence in our findings.

Second, although FGRS are adjusted for cohabitation by family members, they do not account for environmental exposures unshared by family members that may jointly increase AUD and SA risk. Prior research suggests that such factors contribute to AUD and SA comorbidity [5]. Identification of those exposures – some of which might be among the outcomes explored here – and whether they exacerbate the relative impact of genetic liability should be the topic of future research efforts.

Third, our approach relies on Swedish national registry data. This has the advantage of nationwide coverage, consistent measurement, and avoidance of recall bias. However, the registrations that were used to derive each FGRS, and the registrations for psychiatric outcomes, reflect conditions that were serious enough to warrant medical attention. Because many individuals with AUD do not seek treatment or otherwise escape detection by health care professionals [54], and in the US only a slim majority of suicide attempts require medical attention [55], we are likely capturing mostly severe manifestations of these conditions. Were FGRS estimations to include family members who, for example, engaged in only less serious suicide attempts, FGRS profiles could shift along with corresponding associations with outcomes. Relatedly, more extensive observation periods could lead to shifts in the number of individuals in FGRS-based risk and/or reference subsamples with registrations for psychiatric registrations or marriage/divorce, which could impact the magnitude of observed correlations. However, since the mean birth year of individuals included in the analyses ranges from 1972–1976, we would expect subsequent psychiatric registrations to be minimal.

Fourth, Sweden has a robust social safety net apparatus and universal health care. In countries where such services are harder to access, the magnitude of correlations with risk/reference status across groups might differ from those reported here. Finally, FGRS derivation is dependent on the availability of sufficient health care records from family members; we are thus limited to conducting analyses on individuals with Swedish-born parents, which excludes Sweden’s considerable foreign-born population [56].

In summary, the current findings demonstrate the wide-ranging psychosocial consequences of having a high genetic liability for AUD and/or SA, while also illuminating the nuanced nature of these correlations. As expected, having high genetic liability to both conditions was most strongly correlated with adverse educational, familial, psychiatric, and socioeconomic outcomes, while correlations were more modest for those with elevated genetic risk for only AUD or SA. One of the more compelling observations was that individuals at high genetic risk for SA but low genetic risk for AUD exhibited only minor correlations with most adverse outcomes, alongside weak correlations with several positive outcomes; this raises questions regarding how genetic risk that is specific to SA – to the exclusion of that for AUD, a commonly comorbid condition – is unique.
