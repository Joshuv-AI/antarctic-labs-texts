Clinical characteristics and prognostic analysis of concurrent Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia in patients with malignancies: a retrospective study
Authors: Yujie Shi, Ruxuan Chen, Kai Xu, Chi Shao, Yongjian Liu, Hui Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) is a fatal opportunistic infectious disease. We conducted this study to describe the clinical characteristics and explore the prognostic factors of patients with concurrent malignancies and PJP (malignancy-PJP).MethodsWe retrospectively enrolled consecutive patients with malignancies diagnosed with PJP in our center from January 2014 to December 2022. The participants were classified into a hematological malignancy group and a non-hematological malignancy group. Cox regression models were used to analyze prognostic factors.ResultsFifty-six malignancy-PJP patients were enrolled in our study with an average age of 63 (52, 68) years, 60.7% of whom were males. There were 33 (58.9%) hematological malignancy patients and 23 (41.1%) non-hematological malignancy patients. Overall, 29 (51.8%) patients died. Compared with the non-hematological malignancy group, more patients received mechanical ventilation (p = 0.03) in the hematological malignancy group. Moreover, shorter periods from the onset of underlying malignancies (p < 0.01) and from the first dosage of chemotherapy (p = 0.04) to PJP infection were significantly more common in patients with hematological malignancies. However, patients with non-hematological malignancies presented more pleural thickening (p = 0.03) on chest computed tomography (CT). Multivariate analysis revealed that non-solid malignancies (HR = 2.77, p = 0.03, 95% CI: 1.12–6.89), cytomegalovirus (CMV) viremia (HR = 3.33, p < 0.01, 95% CI: 1.51–7.33), bacterial hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) (HR = 2.21, p = 0.04, 95% CI: 1.03–4.77), and pneumomediastinum (HR = 2.50, p < 0.05, 95% CI: 1.01–6.14) were independent risk factors for survival in malignancy-PJP patients.ConclusionsCompared with patients with non-hematological malignancies, patients with hematological malignancies were more likely to require mechanical ventilation and to be infected with PJP within a shorter time from the onset of underlying malignancies and from the first chemotherapy treatment. Non-solid malignancies, CMV viremia, bacterial HAP, and pneumomediastinum were independent risk factors for survival in patients with malignancy-PJP.Keywords: Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, Malignancy, Clinical features, Prognosis

Introduction

Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) often occurs in immunosuppressed patients, and P. jirovecii was previously one of the most common opportunistic infections in patients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). However, the application of standardized PJP prevention strategies and the development of anti-HIV drugs have reduced the incidence and mortality of HIV-PJP patients in recent years. Compared with HIV-PJP patients, non-HIV-PJP patients exhibit more rapid disease progression, a greater risk of respiratory failure and death, and a mortality rate as high as 30–60% [1–3]. Therefore, non-HIV-PJP patients, especially patients with malignancies, are attracting increased attention.

Many studies have shown that malignancy is one of the most common underlying diseases in non-HIV-PJP patients [4–6]. A retrospective study of 297 patients with PJP revealed that hematological malignancies (36.0%) and non-hematological malignancies (25.3%) were the most common susceptible diseases [4]. Among 485 PJP patients hospitalized in China, 13.8% were complicated by malignancies [6]. The susceptibility to PJP in patients with malignancies arises from both the immunosuppressive effects of the primary diseases and therapies such as cytotoxic chemotherapy, targeted agents, immunotherapy, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. And the guidelines suggested that patients with malignancy-related immunosuppression should take PJP prophylaxis positively [7, 8]. Compared with the treatment of HIV-PJP patients, the treatment of patients with malignancies with PJP (malignancy-PJP) is more challenging, and PJP infection also affects the anti-tumor treatment of patients. Therefore, exploring the clinical characteristics and prognostic factors of patients with malignancy-PJP can aid in early diagnosis, early prevention and the development of standardized treatment.

Materials and methods

Patients

All enrolled patients who were discharged with a diagnosis of PJP and malignancies from January 2014 to December 2022 were screened for eligibility from the electronic medical records system at Peking Union Medical College Hospital. During the screening process, patients with any of the following characteristics were excluded from our study: (1) younger than 18 years old; (2) with a positive HIV test; (3) those without chest CT imaging in our hospital’s data bank.

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 56 hospitalized patients with malignancies who were complicated with PJP were enrolled in our study. Their complete medical records and radiological imaging data were retrospectively reviewed through the hospital’s electronic records. They were followed up from the day that the diagnosis of PJP was confirmed. The final follow-up point was December 30, 2023.

Definitions

Diagnostic criteria of malignancies were based on histopathological examination results. In this study, hematological malignancies included lymphoma, leukemia, myelodysplastic syndromes, multiple myeloma and aplastic anemia, and non-hematological malignancies included lung cancer, gastrointestinal tumors, prostate cancer, thymoma, nasopharyngeal cancer, and breast cancer. While non-solid malignancies referred to hematological malignancies other than lymphoma; solid malignancies referred to lymphoma and other non-hematological tumors.

Diagnostic criteria of confirmed PJP were defined as follows: (1) new presence of relevant respiratory manifestations (cough, dyspnea, sputum, and/or hypoxia) or fever (temperature ≥ 37.3 ℃); (2) new presence of pulmonary shadows (ground-glass opacities, consolidation, or patchy shadows) that were identified by chest computed tomography (CT); (3) Pneumocystis DNA fragments were confirmed by polymerase chain reaction or metagenomic next-generation sequencing, and/or Pneumocystis asci were observed at direct microscopic examination with Grocott’s methenamine silver stain in the respiratory samples, including qualified sputum specimens, aspirates, or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid.

Respiratory failure was defined as a room air pulse oxygenation of < 90%, a room air arterial partial pressure oxygen level of < 60 mmHg (1 mmHg = 0.133 kPa), or the oxygenation index under oxygen inhalation is less than 300 mmHg.

Hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) was defined as a patient admitted to the hospital without infection or the incubation period, who developed pneumonia in the hospital 48 h after admission.

Cytomegalovirus (CMV) viremia was defined as peripheral blood CMV-DNA > 500 copies.

Second-line anti-PJP medications in our center included caspofungin, clindamycin and primaquine. According to the Chinese expert consensus on diagnosis and treatment of pneumocystis pneumonia in AIDS patients (2024 edition) [9], echinocandins targeting 1,3-β-D-glucan may be the only antifungal drugs with therapeutic effect on PJP. Consequently, following multidisciplinary discussion involving the Department of Infectious Diseases, Department of Respiratory Medicine, Department of Pharmacy, and the Medical Affairs Office at Peking Union Medical College Hospital, caspofungin was designated as one of the second-line anti-PJP medications in our center.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (K5596) by the Declaration of Helsinki. We used anonymized health care data. The written informed consent of each patient was waived as this study met the Peking Union Medical College Hospital institutional review board’s minimal risk waiver criteria.

Statistical analysis

All the data were analyzed using the IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 software package (IBM Corporation, North Castle Drive, MD-NC119, Armonk, NY 10504 − 1785, USA). The quantitative variables were presented as the means ± standard deviations (SD) or medians (interquartile ranges [IQRs]), and the categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparison between groups was conducted using t-test, analysis of variance, or the rank sum test for quantitative variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were used to compare the mortality of different groups. Cox regression models were used to identify factors associated with prognosis for malignancy-PJP patients. The statistically significant variables selected via univariate analysis were finally assessed by multivariate analysis. The difference was statistically significant when p < 0.05.

Results

General characteristics of malignancy-PJP patients

Fifty-six patients with an identified diagnosis of malignancy-PJP were enrolled in our study after a detailed medical record review. Thirty-four patients were male (60.7%), 22 patients were female (39.3%), and the mean age was 63 (52, 68) years. The underlying malignancies are shown in Fig. 1. Most patients had solid malignancies (45, 80.4%), and 11 (19.6%) had non-solid malignancies. According to the involved system, 23 (41.1%) patients had non-hematological malignancies, and 33 (58.9%) had hematological malignancies.

The underlying malignancies of enrolled 56 malignancy-PJP patients. Other hematological malignancies: multiple myeloma and aplastic anemia; other non-hematological malignancies: prostate cancer, nasopharyngeal cancer, and breast cancer

The main clinical manifestations of PJP were fever (52, 92.9%), cough (47, 83.9%), expectoration (41, 73.2%), and dyspnea (47, 83.9%). Bilateral (56, 100%), ground-glass opacities (GGOs) (48, 85.7%), and patches (45, 80.4%) were the most common chest CT manifestations. Consolidations (24, 42.9%), nodular (24, 42.9%), and pleural thickening (32, 57.1%) were observed on some chest CTs of patients with malignancy-PJP. Low peripheral CD4+ T-cell [125.0 (66.0, 207.0)/µL] counts were common in patients with malignancy-PJP.

Some patients were complicated with other infections, such as CMV (25, 44.6%), bacterial HAP (23, 41.1%), oral candida infection (6, 10.7%), aspergillus infection (6, 10.7%), and Nocardia infection (2, 3.6%). Most patients experienced respiratory failure (47, 83.9%), approximately half of the patients needed intensive care unit (ICU) support, and 29 patients (51.8%) died.

After PJP diagnosis, most patients (50, 89.3%) were prescribed 15 mg/kg/d trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX). More than one-third of our patients (21, 37.5%) were also prescribed a combination of second-line anti-PJP medications, such as caspofungin, clindamycin and primaquine.

There were no significant differences in age, sex or comorbidities between the two groups. Compared with patients in the non-hematological malignancy group, more patients in the hematological malignancy group needed invasive mechanical ventilation support (60.6% vs. 43.5%, p = 0.03). Patients in the hematological malignancy group were more prone to respiratory failure and higher mortality, but the difference was not statistically significant. The time from diagnosis of oncological disease to PJP infection [72 (38.0, 112.5) days vs. 153 (92.5, 223.5) days, p < 0.01] and the time from chemotherapy to PJP infection [79.0 (46.5, 415.5) days vs. 229.0 (116.0, 677.5) days, p = 0.04] were shorter in the hematological malignancy group than in the non-hematological malignancy group. In terms of chest CT features, pleural thickening was more common in the non-hematological malignancy group than in the hematological malignancy group (73.9% vs. 45.5%, p = 0.03). However, there were no significant differences in the minimal albumin level, peripheral lymphocyte count or inflammatory marker levels between the two groups.

Differences between the survival and non-survival groups of patients with malignancy-PJP

The 56 patients were divided into a survival group (27 patients) and a non-survival group (29 patients) according to their clinical outcome. Compared with those in the survival group, more patients in the non-survival group were complicated with CMV (62.1% vs. 25.9%, p < 0.01) and bacterial HAP (58.6% vs. 22.2%, p < 0.01). However, there were no significant differences in clinical symptoms, chest CT features, chemotherapy before PJP infection or anti-PJP treatment between the two groups.

In terms of laboratory test results, in the non-survival group, the peripheral lymphocyte count [0.4 (0.3, 0.7) × 109/L vs. 0.8 (0.5, 1.4) × 109/L, p < 0.01], platelet count [138.0 (74.0, 197.5) × 109/L vs. 212.0 (160.8, 265.3) × 109/L, p < 0.01], minimal albumin level [21.7 ± 5.3 g/L vs. 26.6 ± 4.6 g/L, p < 0.001], T-cell count [307.0 (151.0, 377.0)/µL vs. 447.0 (245.5, 920.5)/µL, p = 0.01) and CD4+ T-cell count [123.0 (37.0, 163.0)/µL vs. 146.0 (97.0, 417.0)/µL, p = 0.03] were significantly lower than those in the survival group. However, D-dimer [8.3 (2.0, 15.6) mg/L vs. 1.9 (0.9, 6.3) mg/L, p = 0.01], high-sensitivity C-reactive protein [107.0 (36.3, 191.3) mg/L vs. 42.2 (6.9, 87.0) mg/L, p < 0.01] and lactate dehydrogenase [588.0 (441.0, 789.5) U/L vs. 319.0 (255.0, 481.0) U/L, p < 0.01] levels were greater in the non-survival group than in the survival group.

Prognostic analysis for patients with malignancy-PJP

As shown in Table 3, univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that non-solid malignancies, decreased lymphocyte count, CMV viremia, bacterial HAP, and pneumomediastinum were associated with non-survival. Subsequent multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that non-solid malignancies (HR = 2.77, χ2 = 4.83, p = 0.03, 95% CI: 1.12–6.89), CMV viremia (HR = 3.33, χ2 = 8.93, p < 0.01, 95% CI: 1.51–7.33), bacterial HAP (HR = 2.21, χ2 = 4.10, p = 0.04, 95% CI: 1.03–4.77) and pneumomediastinum (HR = 2.50, χ2 = 3.96, p < 0.05, 95% CI: 1.01–6.14) were independent risk factors associated with poor survival in patients with malignancy-PJP.

Discussion

This retrospective study revealed high mortality in malignancy-PJP patients. Compared with non-hematological malignancy patients, more hematological malignancy patients needed invasive mechanical ventilation support. Additionally, PJP occurred within a shorter time after the onset of malignancy and after chemotherapy. Non-solid malignancies, CMV viremia, bacterial HAP, and pneumomediastinum were independent risk factors for survival in patients with malignancy-PJP.

Among malignancy-PJP patients, most (58.9%) had hematological malignancies, of which the most common malignancy was lymphoma (21/33, 63.6%), whereas lung cancer (10/23, 43.5%) was the most common type of non-hematological malignancy. This distribution is consistent with the results of previous epidemiological studies, in which 29.1%−78.6% of non-HIV-PJP patients had hematological malignancies [10, 11], and 7.9%−26% had non-hematological malignancies [12–14]. The underlying non-hematological malignancies were lung cancer, followed by breast cancer [15].

To date, few studies have investigated the prognostic factors of patients with malignancy-PJP. Previous studies have shown that underlying lung diseases, CMV infection, elevated lactate dehydrogenase levels, decreased lymphocyte count, invasive mechanical ventilation and pneumothorax are survival risk factors for non-HIV-PJP patients [16–18]. Our results showed that CMV and bacterial HAP were independent risk factors for the prognosis of patients with malignancy-PJP. Many studies have revealed that PJP patients complicated with CMV infection have longer hospital or ICU stays and higher mortality [19–21]. Perret A et al. analyzed 249 PJP patients (44.2% with hematological malignancies) and reported that the need for ICU support was associated with an increased CMV viral load in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BAL) (78.4% vs. 28.4%, p < 0.001) or peripheral blood (68.7% vs. 29.4%, p < 0.001) [17]. Another retrospective study revealed that in non-HIV-PJP patients, CMV infection was associated with increased 90-day mortality (OR = 9.07, 95% CI: 1.24–2.90, p = 0.02) [21]. This result may be related to the following factors. First, CMV is an opportunistic pathogen that can reflect patients’ immunosuppression degree. Malignancy-PJP patients with CMV infection may have severely impaired immune function. Second, CMV pneumonia may occur, causing a mixed pulmonary infection. Previous studies have shown that the level of inflammatory cytokines in the BAL of CMV-PJP patients is greater than that in patients with PJP alone [22]. Therefore, prophylactic anti-PJP treatment should be actively administered to malignancy-PJP patients with CMV infections. We also found that bacterial HAP was significantly associated with mortality, which might be associated with severely impaired immune function. Pneumomediastinum was another reason for the poor prognosis of patients with malignancy-PJP. Pneumomediastinum is mainly caused by the accumulation of air in the lung interstitium after the rupture of the pulmonary alveolus and develops along the vascular bundle to the hilum of the lung and mediastinum. The incidence of pneumomediastinum in PJP patients can be as high as 36.4% [23].

We also found that non-solid malignancies were a risk factor for mortality in patients with malignancy-PJP. A retrospective study of PJP patients with multiple myeloma revealed that 69% of patients needed ICU support, 78% needed mechanical ventilation, and the mortality rate was 62% [24]. The guidelines for the prevention of PJP in patients with hematological malignancies suggest that patients with acute lymphoblastic leukemia, received allogeneic stem cell transplantation, the treatment of alemtuzumab/fludarabine/cyclophosphamide/rituximab and so on, have a greater risk of developing PJP, and PJP prevention treatment is recommended for these patients [7, 25]. Therefore, in clinical practice, patients with non-solid malignancies, such as leukemia and multiple myeloma, should receive more attention, and active prevention and targeted intervention measures for PJP should be administered. In the future, we will further explore the clinical characteristics and prognostic factors of PJP patients with different malignancies receiving antitumor treatments.

There are several limitations in our study. First, this study was a single-center retrospective investigation, and the enrolled patients were all hospitalized, resulting in a certain degree of selection bias. Second, the heterogeneity of clinical drug regimens among patients with malignancies was substantial, and a more in-depth correlation analysis of treatment and prognosis was not carried out. Finally, the sample size was small owing to the relatively low overall incidence of patients with malignancies and PJP.

Conclusions

Compared with non-hematological malignancies patients, patients with hematological malignancies were more likely to be treated with mechanical ventilation and to be infected with PJP in a shorter period from the onset of underlying malignancies and from the first chemotherapy. Patients with non-solid malignancies, CMV viremia, bacterial HAP, and pneumomediastinum were independent survival risk factors for patients with malignancy-PJP. Early recognition of the risk factors of malignancy in PJP patients with poor prognosis, active prevention, and early effective treatment may improve the prognosis.
