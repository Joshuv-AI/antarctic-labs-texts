Effects of a family-centered empowerment intervention on role adaptation and depressive symptoms in high-risk primigravidas and their partners
Authors: Jia Qu, Min Min, Fen Wu, Cui Xing, Li Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFirst-time parenthood requires substantial role adjustment and may challenge mental health, particularly in families experiencing medically high-risk pregnancies. Evidence is limited on whether empowerment-oriented dyadic interventions can improve parental role adaptation and depressive symptoms among high-risk primigravidas and their partners.AimTo evaluate the effects of an empowerment-oriented dyadic intervention on parental role adaptation and depressive symptoms among high-risk primigravidas and their partners.MethodsA randomized controlled trial was conducted in the maternity clinic of a regional hospital in China. A total of 100 high-risk primigravidas and their partners (100 dyads) were randomized 1:1 to an empowerment-based dyadic intervention (four group sessions, 60 min each) or routine antenatal care. Outcomes were assessed at baseline (late pregnancy, T1), 2–3 days postpartum (T2), and 6 weeks postpartum (T3) using the Family Adaptation Scale and the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS). Intervention effects were evaluated using dyad-adjusted linear mixed-effects models under the intention-to-treat principle.ResultsCompared with controls, mothers in the intervention group showed higher role adaptation at 2–3 days postpartum (T2) and 6 weeks postpartum (T3), with adjusted mean differences (AMD; Intervention–Control) of 5.96 (p < 0.001) and 6.84 (p < 0.001), respectively. Maternal depressive symptoms were also lower in the intervention group at both follow-ups (T2: AMD = − 2.72, p < 0.001; T3: AMD = − 2.26, p = 0.004). For fathers, role adaptation scores were higher in the intervention group at T2 and T3 (AMD = 4.09, p = 0.013; AMD = 3.40, p = 0.046), although the between-group differences in change from baseline were not statistically significant. Paternal depressive symptoms were lower in the intervention group at T2 (AMD = − 2.12, p = 0.006), whereas the between-group difference at T3 was not statistically significant (AMD = − 1.25, p = 0.112). Overall, intervention effects were consistently observed through 6 weeks postpartum for maternal outcomes, while evidence for paternal benefits was less consistent across outcomes and follow-up assessments.ConclusionThis brief empowerment-oriented dyadic intervention was associated with improved maternal role adaptation and reduced maternal depressive symptoms through the early postpartum period. Paternal benefits were observed for some outcomes, but were less consistent over time. Further trials in more diverse and higher-risk populations are warranted.Clinical trial registrationThis trial was retrospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry on 18 March 2025 (ChiCTR2500099055).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09202-1.Keywords: Role adaptation, Depressive symptoms, Empowerment, High-risk pregnancy, Primigravidas, Partners

Introduction

The transition to parenthood represents a major developmental milestone for both mothers and fathers and requires substantial adaptation to new roles and identities [1, 2]. According to Mercer’s framework, parental role adaptation involves developing a strong bond with the child, achieving a sense of harmony and competence, and experiencing pleasure and satisfaction in fulfilling caregiving responsibilities and forming a parental identity [3]. This period involves significant psychological and physical changes that affect both parents [4]. Given the close link between children’s health and overall family well-being [5], supporting parental mental health may benefit both the family unit and the broader community.

High-risk pregnancies, such as preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, or other medical conditions, affect approximately 10–15% of gestations globally [6] and are associated with heightened stress and depressive symptoms in both expectant mothers and their partners [7, 8]. These pregnancies often require intensified monitoring, involve uncertainty about outcomes, and impose lifestyle restrictions, which can prolong stress exposure and increase family-level caregiving demands. Women experiencing high-risk pregnancies are more vulnerable to antenatal depression, which can further complicate maternal and fetal outcomes [9, 10]. Accordingly, high-risk primigravidas and their partners represent a priority population for preventive psychosocial support.

Mercer further conceptualizes adaptation as relational and reciprocal within the family context [3]. Consistent with this perspective, evidence indicates that dyadic coping and partner support can buffer distress and promote adjustment during pregnancy and early parenthood [11]. Existing dyadic programs have been implemented in multiple formats, including coparenting-focused preventive interventions, couple-based psychotherapy, and father-focused postnatal education programs, with outcomes spanning coparenting, parenting adjustment, partner support, and parental mental health [12–14]. However, evidence remains limited on whether a structured empowerment-based dyadic program can improve parental role adaptation and reduce depressive symptoms among high-risk primigravidas and their partners, with benefits extending to both mothers and fathers across the early postpartum period.

Antenatal education is widely implemented and plays an important role in supporting expectant parents by providing foundational knowledge, reducing uncertainty, and facilitating preparation for childbirth and early parenting. However, conventional programs are often lecture-based and delivery-oriented, with comparatively less emphasis on the practical and relational tasks that dominate the early postnatal transition [15, 16]. Postnatal education can be particularly beneficial because many caregiving demands and emotional challenges emerge only after birth, when parents must apply knowledge in daily routines. However, education initiated only after delivery may be constrained by fatigue, shortened hospital stays, and competing caregiving demands [17]. These considerations support accessible, postpartum-oriented, skills-based programs that move beyond information provision to strengthen couples’ practical capacity to support psychological well-being and role adaptation.

To address these gaps, we adopted a family-centered empowerment framework. Empowerment theory traditionally emphasizes individual agency, yet recent research has extended its application to dyadic and family contexts [11, 18]. In the present study, this framework primarily refers to an intervention approach that engages both parents in shared preparation, mutual support, and coordinated coping during the transition to parenthood. Both mothers and fathers may benefit from empowerment-based approaches, although their needs and engagement can differ [18]. Fathers often report uncertainty about how to provide effective support during the perinatal period [19, 20], and in East Asian contexts, masculine norms emphasizing emotional restraint may further reduce men’s engagement in perinatal education and emotional disclosure [21, 22]. These gendered patterns can widen the “support gap”, leaving mothers with disproportionate caregiving burdens while fathers feel peripheral or unsure, which may contribute to couple tension and hinder coordinated coping. By explicitly engaging both partners, a dyadic-based approach may strengthen coordinated coping, clarify caregiving expectations, and support shared responsibility.

Guided by the empowerment process model proposed by Anderson and Funnell [23], we developed an empowerment-oriented dyadic intervention for high-risk primigravidas and their partners, which involves both parents and incorporates skills-focused activities and peer discussion to help couples prepare for early postpartum challenges. The third trimester may represent a practical period when couples remain accessible through routine antenatal care and can prepare for imminent postpartum tasks. This randomized controlled trial examined whether, compared with routine antenatal education, the intervention improves parental role adaptation and reduces depressive symptoms in both mothers and fathers across the early postpartum period. We hypothesized that couples receiving the intervention would show greater improvements in role adaptation and greater reductions in depressive symptoms than those receiving routine care.

Methods

Design

This study used a two-arm, parallel-group randomized controlled trial (RCT) with a 1:1 allocation ratio, including baseline (T1), early postpartum post-intervention (T2, 2–3 days after delivery), and follow-up (T3, 6 weeks postpartum) assessments. Reporting followed CONSORT recommendations for randomized trials. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (TJ-IRB20230415). The study was retrospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500099055).

Setting and participants

The study was conducted in the prenatal clinic of a regional teaching hospital in central China. The hospital manages more than 10,000 deliveries annually and serves as a regional referral center for obstetric complications, providing specialized care for medically complicated pregnancies. This setting offered a large eligible pool for recruiting high-risk primigravidas and their partners.

The participants were recruited through the maternity clinic of the hospital. Eligible participants were high-risk primigravidas receiving routine antenatal care at the study hospital and planning to deliver there, together with their partners. Inclusion criteria were: (a) primigravidas at 28–32 weeks; (b) having at least one maternal medical high-risk condition; (c) primigravidas whose partners were willing to participate in the dyadic program; (d) neither parent had received treatment for depression or other mental health disorders; and (e) pregnancies without known fetal anomalies at recruitment. High-risk conditions were identified by attending obstetricians based on established clinical criteria (e.g., gestational diabetes mellitus, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, and placenta previa) [24]. Pregnancies with known fetal anomalies were excluded because they often involve complex decisions and emotional processes that differ from medical high-risk pregnancies [25, 26]. The recruitment process and the flow of the study

Sample size

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power (version 3.1; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf) [27]. As no previous study has examined the effects of empowerment-based approaches within co-parenting interventions, we assumed a medium effect size of 0.25 based on related literature [28]. To achieve 90% power at a 5% two-sided significance level, 40 dyads were required. Allowing for an anticipated dropout rate of 20%, we targeted a minimum of 50 couples per group.

Randomization and allocation concealment

After eligibility screening and written informed consent, couples completed baseline assessments (T1) prior to randomization. The enrolled dyads were randomized 1:1 to the intervention or control group. The allocation sequence was generated using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 22.0) by an independent staff member who was not involved in eligibility assessment, participant enrolment, or intervention delivery, with no blocking or stratification. To ensure allocation concealment, assignments were placed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared in advance. After completion of baseline questionnaires, a separate coordinator opened the next envelope in sequence and informed the intervention team of the assignment. Different staff members were responsible for sequence generation, participant enrolment, and allocation implementation.

Because this was a behavioral group-based intervention, blinding of participants and facilitators was not feasible. Outcomes were collected using standardized self-administered questionnaires at each time point. Follow-up questionnaires were administered by a research assistant who was not involved in enrolment or intervention delivery and was instructed to remain blinded to group assignment during follow-up data collection; data were analyzed using de-identified group codes.

Study groups

Intervention group

Couples allocated to the intervention group received routine antenatal care plus an empowerment-oriented dyadic program grounded in the Anderson and Funnell empowerment process model [23]. This model conceptualizes empowerment as a five-step process involving (a) identifying problems and needs, (b) encouraging emotional expression and support, (c) setting achievable goals, (d) providing tailored education and skills training, and (e) evaluating progress. In adapting this model to the perinatal context, the intervention emphasized emotional support, caregiving confidence, and role preparation for both parents, while also encouraging fathers to clarify their supportive role and engage more actively in infant care.

To facilitate delivery and group interaction, participants were organized into six cohorts (8–10 couples per cohort) according to the expected month of delivery. The program comprised four 60-minute sessions conducted at approximately 2-week intervals, primarily face-to-face, with the final session delivered online via Tencent Meeting to facilitate attendance and scheduling. The intervention was delivered during late pregnancy and was designed to prepare couples for the practical and emotional demands of the early postpartum period. To support attendance, participants received standardized session reminders via WeChat messages.

The structured curriculum consisted of four sessions, each lasting 60 min and comprising: (1) brief introduction and shared discussion (~ 10 min), (2) topic-focused education (~ 20 min), (3) scenario-based skills practice (~ 20 min), and (4) feedback and Q&A (~ 10 min). Topics addressed across sessions included prenatal care, childbirth preparation, stress management, couple relationships, and postpartum care. Sessions incorporated guided group discussion and rehearsal of common postpartum challenges (e.g., infant crying, breastfeeding difficulties, sleep disruption, and role negotiation). Throughout the sessions, couples were encouraged to identify shared goals, reflect on expectations for early parenting, and practice communication and collaborative coping strategies. Sessions were co-facilitated by two senior registered midwives (> 10 years’ experience). Facilitators completed 6 h of standardized training and used a written intervention manual. Fidelity was monitored through supervision every 2 weeks and an independent observer checklist; fidelity assessments indicated that ≥ 95% of prespecified core components were delivered as intended across sessions.

Control group

Couples in the control group received routine antenatal care, including scheduled antenatal visits, routine physical assessments, and standard health education related to pregnancy complications, breastfeeding, and other clinically indicated topics. Participants also had access to the hospital’s usual free antenatal classes, but did not receive the structured dyadic sessions, scenario-based skills practice, or guided group discussion provided to the intervention group.

Measurement instruments

Family Adaptation Scale

The Family Adaptation Scale [29] was used to assess maternal and paternal role adaptation. It consists of 24 items scored on a five-point Likert scale, with a higher score indicating greater adaptation. The Family Adaptation Scale in Chinese exhibits excellent validity and reliability [29–31]. Lu et al. [30] also demonstrated that Cronbach’s α values were 0.80 for mothers and 0.89 for fathers, further attesting to the reliability of the scale in assessing role adaptation in both mothers and fathers. The Cronbach’s α values at baseline were 0.81 for mothers and 0.79 for fathers.

Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale

The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) [32] was used to evaluate depressive symptoms. Although originally developed for mothers, the EPDS has been widely validated as a reliable screening tool for paternal depressive symptoms [33]. This 10-item self-report tool includes four response options assessing feelings experienced over the previous week. The Chinese version of the EPDS demonstrates good validity and reliability; a score of 13 or above was used as the cutoff for probable depression [34, 35]. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency in our study sample. The Cronbach’s α values at baseline were 0.81 for mothers and 0.83 for fathers.

Socio-demographic and obstetric data

Socio-demographic data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire including age, educational attainment, monthly household income (per person), infant care experience, and planned pregnancy. Perceived social support was assessed using the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support and is reported as a baseline characteristic. Pregnancy complications were obtained from the hospital electronic medical records at baseline, while birth outcome and mode of delivery were extracted from the same records after delivery.

Ethical consideration

The study received ethical approval from the study hospital’s Institutional Review Board (TJ-IRB20230415). None of the participants experienced any physical or psychological harm. They were assured that their decision to participate in the study would not affect the services they received. All their information was kept confidential and utilized solely for research purposes. Participants with an EPDS score of 13 or above, upon giving their consent, were referred to a physician or psychiatrist for further clinical evaluation. However, it was ultimately the participants’ decision whether to accept these services. Out of all the participants in this study with an EPDS score of 13 or higher, only one agreed to the referral to a psychiatrist.

Procedure

The study was conducted from April to September 2023 for recruitment and intervention delivery, with follow-up assessments completed through May 2024. A total of 184 high-risk primigravidas and their partners were screened, of whom 100 eligible dyads provided written informed consent and were enrolled. At baseline (T1, 28–32 gestational weeks), both mothers and fathers completed a sociodemographic questionnaire as well as the Family Adaptation Scale and the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS). Following baseline assessment, dyads were randomized (1:1) to the intervention or control group. Couples in the intervention group attended four 60-minute empowerment sessions conducted at approximately 2-week intervals in late pregnancy, whereas the control group received routine antenatal care. Postpartum outcome assessments were conducted at early postpartum (T2, 2–3 days after delivery) and follow-up (T3, 6 weeks postpartum). At T2 and T3, both parents completed the Family Adaptation Scale and EPDS again during routine postnatal visits or scheduled follow-up contacts. Obstetric data were extracted from medical records by trained research staff. Outcome assessments were collected by a research assistant blinded to allocation.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed in R (version 4.4.2). Continuous variables are presented as mean (SD) and categorical variables as n (%). Baseline characteristics were compared using independent-samples t tests for continuous variables and χ² tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Intervention effects on parental role adaptation and depressive symptoms across the three assessments were evaluated using dyad-adjusted linear mixed-effects models under the intention-to-treat principle, with all randomized participants retained in their originally assigned groups. Missing outcome data were assumed to be missing at random and were handled within the mixed-effects modeling framework using maximum likelihood estimation, so that all available repeated measurements contributed to the analysis. To account for both repeated measurements over time and the non-independence of observations within couples, random intercepts were specified for couples and for individuals nested within couples. Fixed effects included group, time (treated as a categorical factor), parental role (mother vs. father), and the prespecified group × time and group × time × role interactions. The group × time interaction tested whether changes over time differed between intervention and control groups, and the group × time × role interaction tested whether intervention effects differed between mothers and fathers. Degrees of freedom were approximated using the Satterthwaite method. Model-estimated marginal means (EMMs) were obtained for each group at each time point, and adjusted mean differences (AMD; Intervention–Control) with 95% confidence intervals were derived from EMM contrasts using prespecified comparisons. Standardized between-group effect sizes were calculated as Hedges’ g. According to established benchmarks, Hedges’ g values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 represent small, medium, and large effect sizes, respectively. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant flow

Figure 1 shows the recruitment and retention flow of participants. A total of 100 couples were randomized (50 per group), and all participants were recruited and followed as dyads. In the intervention group, 46/50 (92.0%) dyads attended at least three of the four sessions (protocol-defined participation), including 42/50 (84.0%) dyads who attended all four sessions and 4/50 (8.0%) dyads who attended three sessions; 4/50 (8.0%) dyads withdrew during the intervention period due to time constraints. At early postpartum (T2, 2–3 days postpartum), 44/50 (88.0%) dyads in the intervention group and 48/50 (96.0%) dyads in the control group completed the assessment. At 6 weeks postpartum (T3), 42/50 (84.0%) dyads in the intervention group and 43/50 (86.0%) dyads in the control group completed follow-up, indicating low and comparable attrition between groups. Reasons for non-completion or withdrawal are summarized in Fig. 1 and included lack of time, declined assessment, and loss of contact. All randomized dyads were included in the primary intention-to-treat analysis, with missing outcome data handled within the mixed-model framework. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 2.

Participant characteristics and obstetric outcomes

Table 2 presents the demographic and clinical characteristics of participating mothers and fathers. At baseline, no significant between-group differences were observed in maternal age, educational attainment, household income, infant-care experience, planned pregnancy, or high-risk pregnancy conditions. Likewise, paternal age, educational attainment, and infant-care experience were comparable between groups (all p > 0.05). Baseline scores for parental role adaptation, depressive symptoms, and perceived social support also did not differ significantly between the intervention and control groups for either mothers or fathers (all p > 0.05). In addition, subsequent birth outcomes and mode of delivery were similar between groups (all p > 0.05).

Discussion

This study provides empirical evidence that a brief, empowerment-oriented dyadic intervention was associated with improved parental role adaptation and reduced depressive symptoms among high-risk primigravidas and their partners. Compared with those receiving routine antenatal care, participants in the intervention group achieved higher role adaptation scores and experienced greater reductions in depressive symptoms. Overall, the intervention effects were more consistent for mothers, whereas the evidence for fathers was more mixed across outcomes and follow-up assessments. These improvements were evident both in the early postpartum period and at six weeks postpartum, underscoring the potential of short-format, dyad-based interventions in preparing parents for the psychological demands of early parenthood.

Enhanced maternal role adaptation emerged as a prominent benefit of the intervention. Mercer’s framework posits that maternal role attainment occurs when mothers internalize caregiving responsibilities and develop confidence in performing childcare tasks [3]. Empowerment-oriented approaches may facilitate this internalization by enhancing self-efficacy and helping mothers reflect on their transition to motherhood. In this study, mothers in the intervention group demonstrated consistently higher role adaptation scores at both early and later postpartum assessments, and the between-group differences in change from baseline were significant at both follow-ups, indicating that the dyadic empowerment structure effectively facilitated this psychological transition. These strategies may have helped mothers clarify their expectations, practice caregiving skills, and increase their confidence. These findings are consistent with prior research. Previous studies have demonstrated that partner involvement and support facilitate smoother transitions into the parental role [11, 36–38]. By addressing mothers’ emotional needs within a shared learning context and encouraging mutual support and coordinated coping, the intervention may have fostered a more empowered and integrated maternal identity.

Interpretation of the early postpartum outcomes should consider the substantial physiological and emotional instability characteristics of this period. The first assessment at 2–3 days postpartum occurred during a phase of rapid hormonal shifts and physical recovery, which has been associated with heightened susceptibility to mood disturbance and stress reactivity [39, 40]. Given this context, the improvements observed immediately after delivery likely reflect the short-term buffering effects of the antepartum intervention rather than a fully consolidated emotional state. The maintenance of these gains at 6 weeks postpartum suggests that the early benefits may have supported a more stable and sustained adjustment as postpartum recovery progressed.

Although fathers also showed higher role adaptation scores than controls at follow-up, the between-group differences in change from baseline were not statistically significant, suggesting that the longitudinal evidence for intervention-related improvement in paternal role adaptation was less robust than that observed for mothers. This pattern may relate to both structural and cultural barriers to paternal participation. Some withdrawals may have reflected work-related time constraints or limited flexibility in attending the sessions, particularly in the context of competing family and occupational responsibilities [8, 19, 22]. In East Asian contexts, traditional norms emphasizing work priority and emotional restraint may further reduce men’s willingness to engage in programs that involve emotional expression or caregiving discussions [22]. Nevertheless, fathers who completed the intervention achieved higher adaptation scores than those in the control group, indicating that structured and health-oriented formats may help legitimize men’s involvement. These observations highlight the importance of tailoring future dyadic interventions to better meet fathers’ needs and cultural expectations to strengthen long-term paternal engagement.

The intervention was associated with reduced depressive symptoms in both mothers and fathers, although the evidence was more consistent for mothers and less stable for fathers across follow-up assessments. Several components of the program may explain this effect. Participants received prenatal education on emotional awareness, practiced stress-reduction techniques, and took part in guided discussions on postpartum emotional regulation. These activities have been shown to support psychological adjustment during the perinatal period [30, 35, 38].

For mothers, the immediate postpartum period involves substantial physiological and emotional adjustment, which may heighten vulnerability to mood disturbance. The intervention may therefore have provided timely coping tools and support strategies that helped mothers manage this challenging stage more effectively. Improvements in parental role adaptation may also have contributed to lower depressive symptoms, as higher role competence and self-efficacy are associated with a reduced risk of postpartum depressive symptoms [30, 38, 41]. Our findings are also consistent with empowerment-based theory, which emphasizes strengthening confidence, communication, and supportive engagement within couples. For fathers, the intervention was associated with more favorable changes in depressive symptoms over time; however, the between-group difference at 6 weeks postpartum was not statistically significant. The observed reduction in paternal depressive symptoms is particularly noteworthy, as fathers are often underrepresented in perinatal mental health studies. Previous studies have shown that positive dyadic support is associated with greater reductions in depressive symptoms among couples [11, 38]. Although the present study did not directly measure dyadic processes, the findings suggest that couple-oriented and coping-focused intervention approaches may be beneficial for postpartum emotional adjustment in both mothers and fathers, even though the paternal effect appeared less stable across follow-up assessments.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the intervention consisted of only four one-hour sessions, which may have limited opportunities for deeper skill consolidation. The early postpartum timing of the first outcome assessment may not fully capture the evolving trajectory of parental identity formation. In addition, the sample was recruited from a single regional hospital and excluded individuals with prior mental health conditions or fetal anomalies. Therefore, generalizability may be limited. The dropout pattern may also introduce bias, as participants who remained in the study may have been more motivated or engaged than those who withdrew. In addition, feasibility outcomes (e.g., acceptability, implementation costs, and barriers/facilitators to delivery) were not formally evaluated and should be examined in future implementation-focused studies. Although perceived social support was assessed and is presented in Supplementary Table S1 as supplementary contextual information, it was not a prespecified primary or secondary outcome and does not directly capture dyadic processes such as spouse appraisal, co-parenting, or childcare involvement. In addition, although the analyses accounted for within-couple non-independence, dyadic process measures were not collected; therefore, actor–partner pathways and mechanisms of change could not be tested directly. Future studies should incorporate partner-specific dyadic process measures, including constructs such as spouse appraisal, co-parenting, and supportive involvement, and use dyadic analytic approaches to clarify mechanisms of change more directly. Despite these limitations, the dyadic empowerment model provides a potentially promising approach that warrants further testing in broader and more diverse populations, including families with complex psychosocial needs and settings with limited resources where structured support is often lacking.

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence that an empowerment-oriented dyadic intervention can improve parental role adaptation and reduce depressive symptoms among high-risk primigravidas and their partners. The findings suggest that brief, family-centered empowerment sessions delivered during pregnancy can enhance psychological preparedness and promote more effective collaborative coping during the transition to parenthood. However, given the modest sample size and single-site setting, the results should be interpreted with caution. Further research involving larger and more diverse populations, as well as extended follow-up periods, is needed to confirm the effectiveness and broader applicability of couple-based empowerment interventions.
