Zonal endothelial cell heterogeneity underlies murine renal vascular development
Authors: Peter M Luo, Neha H Ahuja, Christopher Chaney, Danielle Pi, Aleksandra Cwiek, Zaneta Markowska, Chitkale Hiremath, Denise K Marciano, Karen K Hirschi, M Luisa Iruela-Arispe, Thomas J Carroll, Ondine Cleaver
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe renal vasculature consists of highly specialized blood vessels with distinct physiological functions. Defining their transcriptional signatures and tracing their developmental ontogeny has thus far been challenging due to a lack of regionally specific endothelial biomarkers. Here, we performed single nuclear RNA sequencing (snucRNA-Seq) to interrogate the transcriptional heterogeneity of embryonic renal endothelial cells (ECs). We identified ten endothelial subtypes, and validated regionally restricted expression of novel marker genes of glomeruli, arteries, vasa recta, and immature capillary subtypes using multiplex RNAscope. We also define previously uncharacterized and heterogeneous molecular signatures of the immature renal vasculature, including putative endothelial progenitors. We interrogate biological characteristics of immature EC types using a variety of in vivo tools. Lineage tracing of Esm1-expressing cells reveals the previously unrecognized multi-origin and multi-clonal endothelial tip cell contribution to the glomerular vasculature. Together, this study provides a validated, tool-focused developmental atlas of the murine renal vasculature and elucidates novel cellular mechanisms of nephron vascularization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10456-025-10000-0.Keywords: SnucRNAseq, Endothelial cell, Artery, Vein, Kidney, Nephron, Esm1, Glomerulus

Introduction

The renal vasculature consists of several specialized vessel types which are required for proper kidney function [1]. These vessels include the glomerular capillaries, vasa recta, and peritubular capillaries, which respectively filter, concentrate, and reabsorb and secrete solutes into the urine [2]. Despite the recognition that organotypic and regionalized vascular beds likely profoundly impact therapeutic outcomes, the developmental origins of this heterogeneity, particularly in the kidney, remain understudied [3].

As the kidney develops, stromal, vascular, and epithelial components all grow concomitantly in a centripetal manner. Newer nephrons are added superficially to older nephrons, which extend into the kidney as they differentiate [4]. These growing tissues form local microenvironments that likely direct regionally tailored blood vessel formation. Progenitors of the stroma and epithelium are located at the kidney periphery, termed the nephrogenic zone (NZ). However, the dynamics of renal endothelial progenitors are yet to be elucidated. We and others have characterized features of the immature vasculature in the cortex where nephrogenesis occurs, including differential vascular density between the NZ and underlying subnephrogenic zone (SNZ) [5–7]. The cellular mechanisms by which embryonic vascular plexuses give rise to mature vessel types, such as glomerular or peritubular capillaries, have not been thoroughly examined.

Notably, glomerular vascularization, despite being of acute interest for ex vivo organogenesis [8], remains poorly understood due to the dearth of useful markers and genetic tools. Glomerular endothelial cells (ECs) were first identified on 2D sections in ‘S-shaped bodies’, the penultimate stage of nephrogenesis before glomerular formation, in a ‘glomerular cleft’ adjacent to immature podocytes [9]. Thus, the prevailing dogma has been that an angiogenic sprout invades the glomerular cleft, representing the first vascularization event in nephrogenesis [10]. However, we recently characterized ECs associated with early stages of nephrogenesis using 3D imaging, showing that developing nephron structures were enwrapped in a dense vascular net as early as the renal vesicle stage [5]. Whether these ECs contribute to glomeruli, and the specific cellular events coordinating nephron vascularization have remained of great interest.

Recent efforts have examined renal EC heterogeneity through single cell sequencing, generating lists of marker genes for both embryonic [6] and adult kidney vascular beds [11, 12]. However, the specificity of many proposed vessel markers remains unvalidated in vivo. In particular, molecular markers of the distinct immature vessel beds unique to the developing kidney vasculature are lacking. Moreover, lineage relationships between developmental and mature vascular beds have not been elucidated in vivo in the kidney, as they have in other organs such as the heart [13].

Here, we generate an embryonic transcriptional atlas of E18.5 kidney ECs. Using multiplex RNAscope, we extensively validate regionalized, heterogeneous endothelial gene expression and identify markers of both mature and transient, immature vessel beds. Moreover, we characterize the immature vasculature of the kidney, describing angiogenic events within the kidney and performing detailed lineage tracing to demonstrate a multi-clonal model of glomerular vascularization by these cells. In sum, this work presents a molecular, developmental map of the murine renal vasculature and elucidates several new cellular mechanisms of nephron vascularization.

Results

The embryonic kidney vasculature comprises several specialized vessel types

We assessed basic kidney vascular architecture at E18.5 by whole mount immunofluorescence (WMIF) for VE-cadherin (VECAD), a pan-EC marker, Endomucin (EMCN), a capillary/venous marker, and Lotus tetragonolobus lectin (LTL), marking proximal tubules (Fig. S1a-b). Broadly, LTL staining divides the kidney into the cortex and the medulla, which contains the ascending and descending vasa recta (AVR, DVR) (Fig. S1a, cyan line). We identified several vessel types in the kidney using previously described marker expression patterns. Arteries notably lacked EMCN (Fig. S1a, red arrowhead) and were flanked by EMCN+ veins (yellow arrowhead), as previously described in the developing embryo and kidney [5]. As these arteries are in the cortex and observed in cross section, we identified them as arcuate arteries (as opposed to interlobar arteries that travel from the hilum to the cortex) [1]. The afferent arterioles (in the cortex) and descending vasa recta (DVR) (red arrowheads) expressed less EMCN than the efferent arterioles or ascending vasa recta (AVR), respectively, corresponding to their more arterial identity (Fig. S1a’-a”) [14, 15].

Within the cortex, we further defined zones of capillary vasculature (marked by fine EMCN+ microvessels) based on whether they associated with LTL+ epithelium, which marks more mature nephrons (Fig. 1a, S1a-b). Peritubular capillaries (PTCs) were found surrounding LTL+ proximal tubules (Fig. S1a, a”’). Glomerular capillaries (GCs) were found in the same topological area, but were architecturally distinct and unassociated with LTL (Fig. S1a’). Together, this inner zone of capillary vasculature constitutes more mature vasculature (Fig. S1a, flanked by green and cyan dotted lines). We defined the more peripheral capillaries, in the region containing developing and immature nephrons, as the immature vasculature (between green dotted line and the surface of the kidney) [16]. This vasculature is cortical to mature tubules, and could itself be further separated into two vascular beds. Peripheral capillaries at the most cortical regions of the kidney, corresponding to the location of the nephrogenic zone, were termed the nephrogenic zone plexus (NZP) and were relatively sparse (Fig. S1a, a’’’’, peripheral to magenta dotted line), as previously described at E13.5 [5, 6]. Finally, we term the dense capillary network between the NZP and PTCs as the sub-nephrogenic plexus (SNZP). This immature vascular bed has previously been defined at E13.5 in relation to SIX2 + nephron progenitor cells (NPCs), before the presence of glomeruli, peritubular capillaries, or vasa recta [5, 6].

To provide a strict definition of these vascular beds at E18.5, we performed immunofluorescence for ERG (a pan-endothelial transcription factor), LTL and DBA (marking proximal tubes and collecting ducts), PODXL (marking podocytes), and SIX2 (marking NPCs) (Fig. S1c). We defined ECs within clusters of proximal tubes and collecting ducts as peritubular capillaries (PTCs) and those encapsulated by podocytes as glomerular capillaries (GCs). The NZP was strictly defined as ECs within 10 µm of SIX2+ NPCs in either a lateral or cortical direction (Fig. S1c-c”’). The SNZP was defined as all remaining ECs, within 150 µm of the kidney surface (Fig. S1c-c’”). Taken together, these experiments define zonation of the cortical embryonic kidney vasculature, including mature capillary vasculature of the PTCs and GCs, and the immature capillary vasculature, which we can distinguish visually and by association with NZ compartments (Fig. 1a). However, whether these zones are molecularly specified is unknown.

Single nuclear sequencing of E18.5 renal ECs reveals known and novel heterogeneity

To develop a transcriptional atlas of the developing mouse kidney vasculature, we utilized a Flk1::H2B-eYFP nuclear EC reporter [17]. In order to validate the Flk1::H2B-eYFP reporter, we performed IF for GFP and ERG on E18.5 kidney sections to assess reporter efficiency. As previously reported in the early embryo, the YFP reporter was variable within ECs of the embryonic kidney (Fig. S2a-b.iv’) [17]. We observed variable levels YFP immunofluorescence in all vessel beds of interest, including the NZP and SNZP (Fig. S2b.i-b.iv’). We isolated and enriched a pure population of endothelial nuclei from E18.5 kidneys using fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS), and we sequenced 2,447 nuclear transcriptomes using a 10 × Genomics platform. Flk1 expression levels were variable but detectable in all ECs sequenced, reflecting the activity observed by immunofluorescence (Fig. S2c). Clustering revealed 10 subtypes of endothelial nuclei (Fig. 1b). Enrichment of previously reported marker genes Gja5, Nr2f2, Mki67, Tbx3, and Slc14a1 identified arterial (cluster 3,4), vein-like (cluster 5,10), proliferative (cluster 6), glomerular (cluster 8), and DVR (cluster 9) clusters, respectively (Fig. 1c, S2d) [6, 18–21]. In addition, two clusters of capillary (Cap 1 and Cap 2) nuclei exhibited high Emcn expression (Fig. 1b-c, cluster 1, 7). To identify putative marker genes for each cluster, differentially expressed genes were identified using the EIGEN algorithm (Table S1) [22]. Clusters 2 and 7 were enriched for non-specific and ubiquitous genes, such as RNA polymerase and ribosomal subunits (Table S1) and were excluded from further analysis.

To assist our understanding of the transcriptional signatures within different endothelial clusters, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to identify enriched pathways in each cluster (Table S2). Within arterial clusters, artery morphogenesis and elastic fiber formation were enriched in cluster 3 and PDGF signaling pathway, angiogenesis, and artery morphogenesis were enriched in cluster 4. Cluster 1 was enriched for cell migration, sprouting angiogenesis and extracellular matrix disassembly pathways. Cluster 8 (glomerular ECs) was enriched for renal cell carcinoma and aldosterone regulated sodium reabsorption pathways. Cluster 9 (DVR ECs) was enriched for water homeostasis pathways, in agreement with previous characterizations of Aquaporin 1 (Aqp1) in DVR [6, 23]. Notably, previous reports of solute transporter gene expression describe a sharp increase in overall transporter gene expression postnatally [6]. We examined expression of transporter genes previously assessed by Barry et al. at E18.5 and found that while many of these were lowly expressed at E18.5, some of these genes were expressed in corresponding clusters at E18.5 (Fig. S2e). For example, Slc8a1, which was expressed in afferent arterioles/large arteries in Barry et al.’s data, was enriched in cluster 4 in our data, which corresponds to smaller arteries. In addition, cluster 1, which did not have a corresponding annotation in Barry et al., was enriched for amino acid transport and cluster 5 was enriched for multiple pathways involved in calcium transport. Together, these results demonstrate the remarkable transcriptional heterogeneity within embryonic renal ECs present at E18.5.

Validation of novel marker genes of developing renal vascular subtypes

To validate expression of marker genes identified using EIGEN in vivo, we performed multiplex RNAscope fluorescent in situ hybridization on E18.5 kidney sections (Fig. 1d). This highly sensitive method allows for multiple transcripts to be assessed on the same tissue by registering using DAPI signal (Fig. 1d’). Because some genes were also expressed in other cell types, non-endothelial signal was excluded using an IMARIS-generated 5 µm mask around Pecam1 probe (Fig. 1d”). We assessed our marker genes within this masked subset of all ECs. Significant non-endothelial and full gene names of marker genes are summarized in Table S3.

Using this method, we identified distinct vessel beds with enriched expression of each marker gene, validating new markers of known vessel types and identifying remaining novel cell states based on regionalized marker gene expression (Fig. 1d.i–d.vi”). Jam2 (cluster 4) was enriched in the distal arterioles and DVR, Fbln2 (cluster 3) in the proximal large arteries, Sgk1 (cluster 8) in the GCs and Tmtc1 (cluster 10) in the AVR (Fig. 1d.iii–d.vi’). Our marker genes for cluster 1 (Esm1) and cluster 5 (Ccdc3) were expressed in vessels in the immature vasculature of the cortex and appeared to be enriched in the NZP and SNZP, respectively (Fig. 1d.i–1.dii’). Using the automated digital pathology software QuPath (https://qupath.github.io/) [24], we quantified expression of these genes in the zones as defined above (see methods) using costains for relevant epithelial markers (Six2, LTL/DBA, PODXL) (Fig. S3a–d.i’) [24]. Esm1 and Ccdc3 expression was on average highest in ECs of the NZP and SNZP, respectively (Fig. S4a-b). Furthermore, most ECs in the NZP and SNZP expressed high levels of Esm1 and Ccdc3, respectively (Fig. S4c–d). Together, these results suggest that Esm1 and Ccdc3, markers of cluster 1 and cluster 5, are enriched in the capillary vasculature of the NZP and SNZP, demonstrating molecular zonation and identifying markers of the immature vasculature. Some vessels of the SNZP did express high levels of Esm1, potentially indicating angiogenic activity in this region (Fig. S4e–e’) These ECs appeared to frequently be associated with developing stages of nephrogenesis, as visualized by DAPI morphology corresponding to S-shaped body stages (Fig. S4e.i-e.ii). However, these ECs were relatively rare compared to more pervasive Ccdc3+ ECs observed within the SNZP.

We further characterized the EC heterogeneity within more mature kidney vessel types which are present deeper within the kidney. IF for PODXL (podocytes) circumscribed Pecam1+ ECs of the glomeruli (Fig. 2a, dotted lines) [25], which displayed variable expression of cluster 8-enriched genes by RNAscope (Fig. 2b, UMAPs in S5a–c). Sgk1 was enriched in glomerular ECs within the cortical vasculature (Fig. 2b, b’). Notably, Sgk1 expression appeared lower in nascent glomeruli, which are more superficial and exhibit cuboidal podocytes (Fig. 2b’, c’, d’). Using distance from the surface as a proxy for maturity, we used QuPath to quantify Sgk1 expression in peripheral (within the outermost 200 µm) and deep glomeruli at E18.5, finding that deep glomeruli had significantly higher Sgk1 expression than peripheral, more immature glomeruli (Fig. S5e–g). Chrm3 was lowly expressed in ECs of both immature and mature glomeruli (Fig. 2b”, c”, d”). Notably, Esm1, while not enriched in cluster 8 (Fig. S5d), was also expressed in some cells in immature glomerular ECs at E18.5 (Fig. 2b”’, c’’’, d’”).

Vasa recta (VR) capillaries in the medulla of E18.5 kidneys (Fig. 2e, within dotted line), mostly expressed either SLC14A1 (cluster 9) or Tmtc1 (cluster 10) (Fig. 2f–i, S5h–j). Neither gene displayed cortical capillary expression (Fig. 2g’–i’). SLC14A1 has been previously shown to mark DVR capillaries [21], indicating that the vein-like cluster 10 represents AVR capillaries, for which current markers are lacking. Notably, a subset of medullary ECs that were Tmtc1-negative and SLC14A1-negative expressed Esm1, our cluster 1 marker which was also expressed at the kidney periphery (Fig. 2f, j, j’, S5k). Interestingly, Clec14a, which was enriched in both clusters 9 and 10, was expressed in both DVR and AVR, with low expression in Esm1+ capillaries or in cortical ECs. (Fig. 2k–l’, S5l).

Clusters 3 and 4 were enriched for arterial markers including Sox17 and Gja4, which showed relatively uniform expression in arterial vasculature (Fig. 2m–m”, S5m-o) [26, 27]. Fbln2 (cluster 3) and Jam2 (cluster 4) were enriched in proximal (large) and distal (smaller) arteries, respectively (Fig. 2n–q, S5p–q). Notably, some arteries displayed a transcriptional gradient transitioning from Fbln2 to Jam2 as they traversed to more cortical regions, where they become smaller arterioles (Fig. 2p). Taken together, these results validate novel marker genes of glomerular, vasa recta, and arterial ECs, and reveal previously uncharacterized heterogeneity within these developing vessel beds.

Embryonic endothelial heterogeneity partially perdures in post-nephrogenic kidneys

To test whether these molecular markers remain regionally restricted post-natally, we analyzed their expression at P16, at least 10 days after nephrogenesis has ceased. Consistent with E18.5 expression patterns, Sgk1, Tmtc1, Fbln2 and Jam2 remained regionally restricted to glomeruli, medullary AVR, proximal and distal arteries, respectively (Fig. S6a–d). Notably, Fbln2 was also expressed in large veins near the hilum (Fig. S6d–d’’, arrowhead). Notably, expression of Ccdc3 and Esm1 change drastically between E18.5 and P16. At P16, Ccdc3 expression was lower, with minimal endothelial-specific expression (Fig. S6e–e’’’), while Esm1 became restricted to the outer medulla (Fig. S6f–f’’’). This contrasts with the cortical plexuses at E18.5 that display largely segregated Ccdc3 and Esm1 expression.

Stromal loss of netrin 1 results in patterning defects of the sub-nephrogenic and nephrogenic zone vessels

We and others previously reported altered vascular development in stromal netrin 1 knockout kidneys (Foxd1GFPCre; Ntn1flox/flox, abbreviated Ntn1SPKO), including the presence of ectopic peripheral vessels at E13.5 [28, 29]. Here, we sought to further characterize aberrant vessels using our novel markers of renal endothelium. EMCN and CX40 WMIF on E15.5 Ntn1SPKO kidneys showed that these ectopic blood vessels along the kidney surface were not ectopic arteries, as previously reported at E13.5 (Fig. S7a–b, arrowhead) [28]. They also stood in stark contrast to the normal NZP vasculature, which is sparse and does not extend to the periphery. To assess transcriptional signatures of embryonic vascular plexuses within ectopic vessels, we performed RNAscope for Esm1 and Ccdc3 (Fig. 3a–d). While Esm1 and Ccdc3 were expressed in the NZP and SNZP of control kidneys as described above, in the mutants we found that only a small subset of ectopic surface-associated ECs (which were defined as part of the NZP) expressed Esm1 or Ccdc3, (Fig. 3c–d.ii’, e). Because ectopic vessels mostly expressed neither gene, this resulted in significantly fewer Esm1+ ECs within the NZ (due to there being relatively fewer ‘normal’ NZP ECs) and significantly more Esm1-/Ccdc3- ECs in ectopic vessels of NZs of Ntn1SPKO kidneys (Fig. 3d–e, white arrowheads). The SNZP of Ntn1SPKO kidneys expressed Ccdc3 as in control kidneys (Fig. 3d.iii). These data suggested that ectopic vessels in Ntn1SPKO are heterogeneous and do not transcriptionally resemble normal immature vasculature (Fig. 3c.iv, d.iv).

To assess potential downstream signaling, we analyzed expression of known Ntn1 receptors Unc5b, Unc5c, and Neo1 in our transcriptomic dataset. Unc5b was enriched in NZP and arterial clusters [30], Unc5c was enriched in the SNZP cluster, and Neo1 was enriched in glomerular/arterial clusters (Fig. S7c-e). Using RNAscope to validate these findings, we found that expression of Neo1 and Unc5c was broad and not endothelial specific in either E15.5 control or Ntn1SPKO kidneys (Fig. S7f–g). Unc5b was expressed in the NZP of control kidneys as indicated by single nuclear data, in addition to epithelial expression in some collecting duct tubules (Fig. S7f.i–f.ii, arrowheads and insets, and data not shown). ECs in ectopic vessels in Ntn1SPKO broadly did not express enriched levels of Neo1, Unc5c, or Unc5b compared to nearby cells (Fig. S7g–g.ii’, arrowheads and insets). Ectopic vessels contained occasional cells expressing Unc5b, corresponding to those cells that expressed Esm1 (Fig. S7g.i–g.i’ and Fig. 3d.i–d.i’, bottom arrowhead). Together, these results provide further insights into vascular defects in Ntn1 knockout kidneys, demonstrating the utility of our atlas and dataset in analyzing models of altered renal vascular development.

Ccdc3 specifically marks a transient sub-nephrogenic plexus

Next, we sought to further characterize immature cortical renal vascular beds. To visualize the NZP and the SNZP through development, we performed WMIF for EMCN, SIX2, and LTL (marking the boundaries of the sub-nephrogenic zone) at E15.5, E18.5, and P15 (Fig. 4a–c). We and others have previously described the dense sub-cortical plexus at E13.5, which is present before the first LTL+ tubules form [5, 6]. At E15.5 and E18.5, the SNZP is found in the regions corresponding to developing nephron structures (Fig. 4a–b). By P15, nephrogenesis has ceased, and LTL+ tubules extend to the surface of the kidney. As such, the immature NZP and SNZP are not present at this stage. At intermediate stages P2 and P6 (Fig. S8a–b), SIX2+ cells progressively diminish as they lose self-replicative capacity.

Ccdc3, which was a marker gene for cluster 5, was enriched in ECs of the SNZP (Fig. S4b), with minimal expression in ECs of the PTCs, GCs, NZP, or VR (Fig. 4d–e””, Fig. S8c’–e’). By contrast, Aplnr, previously shown to mark immature renal capillaries including the SNZP, was also strongly expressed in PTCs and AVR at E18.5, and was not enriched in the SNZP (Fig. 4f–f””, Fig. S8c”–e”, S8f–g) [6]. At P16, when no visible SNZP was present, Ccdc3 expression in cortical capillaries was very low, while Aplnr remained expressed at high levels in cortical ECs (Fig. 4g–i). Together, these results show that Ccdc3 serves as a better marker of the developmental transcriptional signature of SNZP ECs.

The sub-nephrogenic plexus is a vein-like niche of proliferating ECs

Cluster 5 displayed restricted expression of classical venous markers Nr2f2 and Ephb4 along with Ccdc3 (Fig. S9a-d). We assessed the expression of Ccdc3 within veins, which we identified as wide-diameter vessels running along Gja4+ arteries. Ccdc3 was mosaically expressed in veins both at the hilum (corresponding to interlobar veins) and in the inner cortex (corresponding to arcuate veins) (Fig. S9e.i–ii). ECs in the SNZP, despite being smaller caliber vessels, expressed high Ccdc3 and were associated with Gja4+ arterioles in the immature cortex (Fig. S9e.iii). VECAD and EMCN WMIF at E18.5 showed that lumens of corticomedullary veins (Fig. S9f–f’, green arrowheads) connected directly to vessels of the SNZP (Fig. S9f”’, yellow arrowhead). To assess whether the SNZP displayed a venous character, we performed IF for ERG, COUP-TFII (encoded by Nr2f2) and SOX17, transcription factors that drive pan-endothelial, venous, and arterial identity, respectively (Fig. S9g–g’) [20, 26]. ECs in the SNZP expressed more COUP-TFII than ECs in the NZP (Fig. S9g.i–g.ii”), but less than those in large lumenized veins (Fig. S9g.iii). ECs in the NZP, as well as arterial ECs, expressed SOX17. Together, these results suggest a venous identity of the SNZP, potentially corresponding to a venule due to its smaller lumens, in the developing kidney.

Ccdc3 was also highly expressed in cluster 6 (proliferative ECs) (Fig. 1d.ii’, S10a–c). To visualize whether proliferating renal ECs were regionalized, we utilized a Cdh5CreERT2-driven Fucci2a (Cdh5Fucci) reporter, which expresses mCherry-Cdt1 or mVenus-Geminin during late G1 and S/G2/M phase, respectively, to assess EC proliferation at E18.5 and P15 (Fig. 5a) [31, 32]. In E18.5 Cdh5Fucci kidneys (induced with tamoxifen at E15.5), the SNZP was significantly enriched for S/G2/M phase (mVenus+) cells compared to other cortical vessel beds (Fig. 5b–d). At P15 (induced at P4), the cortex contained sparsely labeled proliferative ECs (Fig. 5e–f). In order to assess NZP and SNZP transcriptional signatures within proliferating ECs, we performed IF for pHH3 (marking all proliferative cells) along with RNAscope for Pecam1, Esm1, and Ccdc3 (Fig. 5g–g’). We visually identified all proliferating ECs in cortical kidney regions by overlap of Pecam1 RNAscope and pHH3 IF and quantified Ccdc3 and Esm1 expression (Fig. 5g–g.ii’, yellow and magenta circles). Proliferating cortical ECs expressed significantly more Ccdc3 than Esm1, providing more evidence that endothelial proliferation at E18.5 was enriched in the SNZP (Fig. 5gi–gii’, 5 h). Together, these results demonstrate that the SNZP is a developmental structure marked by Ccdc3 enrichment with venous and proliferative characteristics.

We also analyzed cell cycle state in other vessel types. Within E18.5 Cdh5Fucci kidneys, other proliferative cells included glomerular and vasa recta endothelium (including both AVR and AQP1+ DVR) (Fig. S10d–f) [23]. In P15 Cdh5Fucci kidneys, proliferative ECs were highly enriched in the outer medullary vasa recta (both AVR and DVR) (Fig. S10g-i). Esm1 is expressed in the nephrogenic zone vasculature

Next, we characterized the development of the Esm1+ NZP. NZP ECs, which were directly connected to vessels in the SNZP, were enriched for Esm1 (Fig. S4a) NZP ECs displayed some Ccdc3 expression, but consistently less than SNZP ECs (Fig. S11a-b”’, above white dotted line). We observed occasional vessels with adjacent ECs within the NZP and SNZP that expressed Esm1 and Ccdc3, respectively (Fig. S11b””). We performed RNAscope on glancing sections near the periphery of the kidney that contained predominantly the NZ (a.k.a. ‘edge section’) to visualize expression of cells in continuous vessels of the NZP (Fig. S11c-d). We observed single vessels vessels displaying contiguous but opposing gradients of Esm1+ and Ccdc3+ (Fig. S11c’-c”’, d’-d”’, arrowheads). Together, these observations underscore that the NZP and SNZP are connected despite exhibiting different transcriptional profiles.

Esm1-labeled tip cells are found in the nephrogenic zone plexus

Esm1 classically marks ‘tip’ cells, which are specialized sprouting cells that utilize filopodia to sense microenvironmental growth factors and direct vessel growth at angiogenic fronts [34–36]. We found Esm1, as well as other tip cell markers such as Cxcr4, Dll4, and Adm [37, 38] enriched in cluster 1, suggesting that the NZP contains angiogenic tip cells (Fig. S11e–i).

To visualize putative Esm1+ tip cells within the NZP, we performed a short 24 h 4-OHT pulse with the Esm1CreERT2; mT/mG mouse to induce membrane GFP labeling of Esm1 expressing cells (Fig. 6a) [39, 40]. GFP+ cells in E18.5 short-pulse Esm1-lineage traced kidneys were restricted to the same vessel beds that expressed Esm1 by RNAscope (Fig. S11j-j”’). WMIF on edge sections revealed GFP+ cells with long filopodia, suggesting bona fide tip cells, in the NZP (Fig. 6b–c). Some GFP+ tip cells were superficial to SIX2+ nephron progenitor cells (NPCs), sending projections along the surface of the kidney (YZ orthogonal view, Fig. 6b). By contrast, other GFP+ tip cells were positioned deeper, between the NPC cap and the ECAD+ UB (Fig. 6c, XZ orthogonal view). These cells surrounded a nascent renal vesicle with filopodia (Fig. S11k-l, inset), demonstrating tip cell association with the earliest stages of nephrogenesis.

The cortical Esm1 tip cell lineage contributes specifically to glomeruli and arteries

To assess the fate of Esm1+ tip cells, we performed lineage tracing of NZP tip cells using the Esm1CreERT2; mT/mG with a low dose pulse of 4-OHT at P0 and analyzed GFP expression at P3 and P15 (Fig. 6d). As Esm1 was also expressed in the medulla at E18.5, we restricted our initial analysis to cortical endothelium. At P3, GFP+ cells were found in glomeruli within the cortex (Fig. 6e, inset). More peripherally, single GFP+ cells with highly complex filopodia associated tightly with PAX2+ developing nephrons (Fig. 6e, arrowheads, Fig. 6f–g, arrowheads). Notably, by P15, cortical Esm1-lineage cells were primarily located in glomeruli or distal arteries (Fig. 6h–h’), but not proximal arteries (Fig. 6h”).

We quantified the progression of cortical Esm1-lineage cells using RNAscope at E18.5 and IF for GFP at P3/P15, along with co-stains to identify glomerular, arterial, PTC, and JAG1-associated (developing nephron epithelium) vasculature (Fig. S11m-o) [41]. At E18.5, 31% of cortical Esm1+ nuclei were in the NZP (Fig. 6i). Through development, Esm1-lineage cells progressively diminished in the NZP, then in JAG1-associated ECs, with corresponding increases in proportions of glomerular (32% at E18.5 vs. 45% at P15) and arterial (8% at E18.5 vs 37% at P15) ECs (Fig. 6i). Importantly, the proportion of other GFP+ vessel labeling, including in PTCs, did not increase significantly (14% at E18.5 vs. 18% at P15). Together, these results show that NZP ECs contribute to glomerular and arterial ECs. JAG1 (developing nephron, from renal vesicle to S-shaped body stage)-associated ECs, which express Esm1 as well, likely represent an intermediate stage (Fig. 6j).

Esm1 lineage in the medulla contributes to descending vasa recta

We next aimed to characterize the Esm1 lineage in the medulla. At E18.5, Esm1 was also expressed in Tmtc1-negative, SLC14A1-negative medullary ECs (Fig. S12a–b, arrowheads). We traced the medullary Esm1-lineage in relation to SLC14A1+ DVR using Esm1CreERT2; mT/mG as above. At E18.5 (induced at E17.5), GFP+ cells were found in SLC14A1-negative medullary vessels (Fig. S12c). When traced from P0 to P3, a portion of GFP+ cells had incorporated into the DVR, including some cells that connected SLC14A1- vessels and SLC14A1+ DVR capillaries (Fig. S12d–d”). By P15 (traced from P0), GFP+ cells in the inner medulla were primarily located in DVR capillaries (Fig. S12e–e”). Outer medullary GFP+ cells were variably in DVR, with numerous GFP+ cells labeled in isolation within non-DVR capillaries still present 15 days after labeling (Fig. S12e–e’”, arrowheads). Together, these results suggest that Esm1+ cells in the medulla contribute to DVR in a centripetal manner. The overall findings of Esm1 fate are summarized in Fig. 6j.

Glomerular vasculature arises from multiple endothelial sources

To understand the cellular mechanism by which the Esm1-lineage participates in glomerular vascularization, we analyzed the cellular dynamics of Esm1-lineage glomerular vascularization in P3 lineage traced tissue at ‘comma-shaped body’ (CSB) or ‘S-shaped body’ (SSB) late stages of nephrogenesis (Fig. 7a–c, S13a–b’). GFP + Esm1-lineage cells were frequently found in the ‘glomerular cleft’, initially extending filopodia towards future podocytes (Fig. 7a, Fig. S13a–b’) [9, 42]. As SSBs progressed, these GFP+ cells anastomosed with non-GFP+ endothelium across the podocyte lip of the SSB (Fig. 7b’, arrowhead). In late SSBs, a SOX17+ arteriolar vascular tube partially composed of GFP+ cells traversed the glomerular cleft, suggesting an Esm1-lineage contribution to efferent or afferent arterioles (Fig. 7c’–c”).

GFP+ cells were also found forming tight associations with other portions of the SSB (Fig. 7a.i, b.i, c.i, S13b.i). Notably, GFP+ cells with filopodia were found tucked ‘behind’ the medial segment of the SSB (Fig. 7a.i”, S13b.i’, Movie S1). A vascular net, variably containing GFP+ cells, formed in this region as the SSB progressed (Fig. 7a.i’–7b.i’). GFP+ cells in this vascular net did not appear to contribute to SOX17+ arterioles in late SSBs, but were found in close proximity at late stage SSBs, suggesting that they may incorporate into glomerular vasculature (Fig. 7c.i’–7c.i”).

Stromal cells, including mesangial cells (a specialized type of pericyte), play an important role in glomerular development and function [43]. Using E17.5 Cspg4-mEGP reporter mice, we visualized Cspg4+ stromal cells associated with PECAM1+ ECs in the glomerular cleft of NCAM1+ SSBs, analogous to Esm1-lineage arteriolar cells within the glomerular cleft (Fig. S13c). In addition, Cspg4-mEGFP+ cells densely associated with the vascular net ‘behind’ the SSB (Fig. S13c.i). These cells may represent mural, pericyte, or mesangial cells that associate with ECs early in glomerular development.

Mosaic and multi-clonal contribution of Esm1-lineage tip cells to glomerular vasculature

In glomeruli of P3 Esm1-lineage traced tissue, GFP+ cells did not homogeneously label the entirety of EMCN+ glomerular capillary vasculature (Fig. 7d–e). GFP+ labeling frequently included a SOX17+ arteriole (Fig. S13d), supporting our findings that Esm1-lineage cells within the glomerular cleft of SSBs form a vascular tube that becomes arterioles. Intriguingly, some glomeruli contained multiple discontinuous clusters of GFP+ cells that were separated by non-GFP+ vasculature, including labeled cells within glomerular tufts (Fig. 7e, arrowhead). In P15 Esm1-lineage traced tissue, GFP labeling continued to be mosaic and discontinuous in the majority of glomeruli analyzed (Fig. 7f–g, S13e).

The discontinuity of GFP+ cells suggested that glomerular capillaries may not arise clonally and may involve multiple Esm1-lineage contributions. We therefore utilized a Esm1CreERT2; Rainbow reporter [44], inducing tri-color reporter recombination with 4-OHT at P0, to assess clonality of the Esm1-lineage within glomeruli at P4 (Fig. S13f). At SSB stages, cells within the glomerular cleft and the SSB-associated vascular net were labeled with different fluorophores (Fig. S13g). Glomeruli were similarly observed containing multiple discontinuous labeled clones (Fig. 7h–i), demonstrating multiple Esm1+ tip cell contribution to the forming glomerular vasculature (Fig. 7j).

Discussion

Organotypic heterogeneity of vascular ECs has been of major interest in recent years [3, 45]. In this study, we provide a unified view of the heterogeneity of the developing kidney vasculature using single-nucleus sequencing and in vivo validation. We identify novel molecular markers of both mature and immature renal vasculature and query aberrant vasculature in Ntn1 knockout mouse mutants with these markers. We extensively characterize the molecular zonation of the immature renal vasculature, defining a proliferative, venous sub-nephrogenic plexus (SNZP) and a connected, angiogenic nephrogenic zone plexus (NZP) (Fig. 8). We trace the lineage of tip cells within the NZP to glomerular and arterial vasculature, supporting a new model of nephron vascularization. Together, this study demonstrates several important advances in understanding vascular specialization in the kidney.

Lineage relationships postulated given the different data observed. Esm1+ cells or Ccdc3+ cells may represent a reservoir of vascular progenitor cells giving rise to subsets of kidney blood vessels

A transcriptional atlas of the mouse embryonic kidney endothelium

Previous efforts to map the renal vasculature have had limited success producing validated specific markers of renal vessel subtypes. Genes such as Plvap, Aplnr, Emcn, found in silico to mark AVR, progenitor, and glomerular ECs respectively [6, 46], have been shown to be relatively non-specific in vivo [5, 47]. Here we focus on validating new markers such as Sgk1, Chrm3, Tmtc1, Fbln2, Jam2 with restricted expression in renal vessel beds both embryonically and postnatally. We expect these markers will be invaluable to researchers studying renal vasculature in vivo and in silico.

In addition, we show that the immature renal vasculature is molecularly zonated, similar to the developing renal stroma [48]. While we and others have previously characterized differential vascular density between what we term the sub-nephrogenic zone plexus (SNZP) and the nephrogenic zone plexus (NZP), here we provide specific markers and establish a transcriptional gradient between these two flavors of immature renal vasculature. We show that these markers are enriched in their respective vessel beds using advanced quantitation methods. Importantly, neither Ccdc3 nor Esm1 retains strong expression in the cortex of the kidney postnatally, making them highly specific and useful markers to study the transient immature cortical renal vasculature. These results provide several advances on previous work characterizing the immature vasculature of the kidney. In particular, previous studies have proposed Aplnr as a vascular progenitor marker in the kidney based on single cell sequencing data, but here we demonstrate that it is not restricted to immature capillaries both at E18.5 and post-natally. In addition, while others have characterized angiogenic cells in kidney endothelial datasets including by Esm1 expression, we show here the localization of Esm1+ ECs within the nephrogenic zone plexus (NZP). A majority of ECs in the NZP express Esm1, supporting the possibility that the NZP is a site of sprouting angiogenic activity.

We further utilize these markers to characterize ectopic vasculature in netrin 1 knockout mice. We observe an increase in ECs expressing neither Esm1 nor Ccdc3 at the kidney surface in these mutants, corresponding to a loss of Esm1+ cells in the NZP and indicating a loss of normal developmental EC states. These results support the previously suggested idea that smaller kidneys seen with loss of Ntn1 is not simply a developmental delay, as the ectopic vasculature and its transcriptional signature is not representative of normal kidney development [28, 29]. These ectopic ECs could arise from a distinct origin outside of the NZP and kidney, explaining their lack of NZP-associated gene expression. This could be associated with alterations in EC tip cell behavior, setting up future avenues of investigation. We further use our dataset to interrogate potential downstream netrin 1 activity by assessing expression of known receptors Unc5b, Neo1, and Unc5c. Unc5b is expressed in vessels of the NZP in control kidneys, corresponding to co-expression in cluster 1 and reported roles in regulating angiogenesis [49], but the lack of a clear enrichment of a known netrin 1 receptor suggests a non-endothelial (or unknown) receptor-mediated mechanism of vascular growth. In addition, we believe that these tools will be useful to benchmark progress of organoid models, which we recently characterized as lacking functional vasculature [50]. Given intense efforts improve organoid vascularization, these EC markers should prove useful [8, 51].

Lineage relations of immature cortical renal blood vessels

We posit that renal vascular progenitors may lie specifically within either the SNZP or NZP (Fig. 8). The SNZP, which underlies the NPCs as early as E13.5 [5], has been posited as a vascular progenitor source for the kidney [6]. Building upon previous studies, we characterize the SNZP as a renal endothelial network with enriched proliferative capacity within the cortex. Though it is connected directly to venous lumens and vessels of the NZP, the enrichment of proliferation as well as Ccdc3 expression demonstrates that it is molecularly distinct, including from other veins. ECs of the SNZP express COUP-TFII, indicating that they may have a venous identity. In the murine retina, proliferative progenitor ECs in veins and a plexus behind the growing retinal front contribute to sprouting tip cells, which then in turn contribute to arterial development [31, 39, 52]. In a similar fashion, the proliferative, venous Ccdc3-expressing SNZP may extend new angiogenic sprouts into the NZP which ultimately contribute to arterial, as well as glomerular development. Future studies will be needed to ascertain whether the same principle holds true in the kidney.

We show via careful lineage tracing studies using the Esm1CreERT2 that cortical Esm1 lineage cells contribute to glomerular development. Importantly, as we labeled all cells that expressed Esm1 at P0, this was not exclusive to NZP cells. In fact, only 30% of cells expressing Esm1 in the cortex were found in the NZP (Fig. 6i). When traced to P3 and P15, very few GFP+ cells were present in the NZP, allowing us to assess the fate of the NZP ECs. Other GFP cells in Esm1 lineage tracing included JAG1 associated cells and glomerular cells. We show that Esm1-lineage cells in the NZP begin to associate with early stages of nephrogenesis. These nephrons will progress to JAG1+ S-shaped bodies, where we show as well as that JAG1+ and PAX2+ epithelium-associated cells contribute to glomeruli. Together, our results suggest that Esm1 marks all ECs in a progression of angiogenic activity, beginning with the NZP, associating with JAG1 epithelium, and then incorporating into glomeruli.

In addition, we cannot exclude the possibility that NZP cells also contribute to the medullary vasculature, as part of the S-shaped body contributes to the Loop of Henle. However, we show that the medullary Esm1 lineage remains at P15 in non-DVR cells in the outer cortex, many days after the NZP ceases to exist. The Esm1 lineage appears to contribute to DVR vessels as the medulla grows postnatally, with the outer cortex serving as a separate nidus of angiogenic activity similar to the NZP. In addition, due to the presence of Esm1+ cells in the medulla at the timepoint of labeling, it is also possible that Esm1+ cells in the medulla contribute to glomerular vasculature. However, this is unlikely, as we and others have shown that emergence of the cortical vasculature precedes that of the medulla. As such, the vasculature present at E13.5 gives rise to all microvasculature found at E18.5, including vasa recta [6]. Further studies to identify additional specific markers of different areas of angiogenesis, perhaps by assessing the heterogeneity of cells within the Esm1 lineage using the Esm1CreERT2, would be of great interest and would allow for clarification of these questions.

Notably, this schema of renal vascular development stands in contrast with other cell types of the developing kidney, which house their progenitors in the NZ. Specifically, NZ epithelial and stromal progenitors give rise to transit amplifying populations found in the sub-nephrogenic zone, which then proliferate to contribute to the many types of epithelia and stroma [53–55]. An alternative model of renal vascular development could mirror other cellular components, whereby NZP Esm1+ vessels serve as an endothelial progenitor, contributing to the SNZP which then proliferates. Our data suggest that Esm1+ cells are indeed multipotent progenitors of kidney specific endothelium as they ultimately largely give rise to both arterial and glomerular vasculature when labeled at E18.5. Whether the potential of these Esm1+ cells, potentially including other renal vascular subtypes, changes throughout nephrogenesis remains an important question. Further studies into the differentiation and proliferation dynamics of both the SNZP and NZP are required, although currently genetic tools for the SZNP are lacking.

Importantly, we characterize a multiclonal and mosaic contribution of Esm1-lineage cells to the glomerular vasculature, identifying a novel and organ-specific role of Esm1+ tip cells. Our results strongly suggest that tip cells found in the NZ associate with the developing epithelium from an early stage. These ultimately give rise to ECs within the glomerular cleft, as well as within a vascular ‘net’ that forms behind the S-shaped body. This contrasts with classic models of glomerular vascularization, wherein an individual endothelial sprout is recruited from the nearby vasculature into the glomerular cleft at the S-shaped body stage [9]. Additionally, we show that Esm1-lineage cells within the glomerular cleft only contribute to a portion of the arterioles, anastomosing with another endothelial sprout from beyond the podocytes. Importantly, we posit that the vascular ‘net’ adjacent to more medial segments of the S-shaped body is an important player in glomerular development. The presence of Esm1-labeled cells within this vascular net with associated NG2+ stromal cells, along with the multi-clonal and disconnected nature of Esm1-lineage labeling within glomerular vasculature, suggests that glomerular capillaries receive contributions from this net, rather than simply forming via looping and intussusceptive angiogenesis of a glomerular cleft sprout, as has previously been suggested [56]. An important question remains as to how this vascular net eventually becomes integrated into the glomeruli and surrounded by the podocytes. Vascular pruning, perhaps assisted by other cell types such as macrophages, may be required for this process [57, 58].

Novel insights into vasa recta biology

While we primarily focused on characterizing steps involved in vascularization of cortical nephron components, we also uncover new biological features of the medullary endothelium. Restricted medullary expression of Clec14a, as well as widespread G1 arrest in both AVR and DVR suggest overarching transcriptional and cellular differences between medullary and cortical endothelium. Whether these medullary specific signatures drive vasa recta development or are an adaptive response to the local environment remains to be answered.

The growth of the vasa recta is poorly understood during both embryonic development and post-natally. We show that Esm1 is expressed in embryonic medullary vessels outside of either the ascending or descending vasa recta. Lineage tracing of these cells in the medulla reveals a centripetal contribution to the DVR. Interestingly, at P15 individually labeled Esm1-lineage cells remained scattered within the vasculature of the outer medulla. In addition, both Esm1 expression and a pool of proliferative ECs are also restricted to the outer medulla at P15. The relation of medullary Esm1+ cells to proliferative ECs and whether they represent tip cells will further clarify the development of the vasa recta post-natally.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we present a detailed molecular atlas of the embryonic murine renal ECs. This work represents an impactful advance in the field as we focus on validation of regionalized marker genes in vivo and characterize previously unidentified molecular zonation of the immature renal vasculature. We also provide functional insight into the cellular mechanisms of renal vascular development, identifying putative cortical endothelial progenitors and developing a model of glomerular vascularization that challenges current understanding. We hope this study will be a valuable resource to researchers interested in kidney, developmental, and vascular biology, and will inform and direct future studies into renal vascular development.

Materials and methods

Mouse handling

Experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at UT Southwestern, Northwestern University School of Medicine, or University of Virginia School of Medicine. All mouse embryos and postnatal pups were used without sex identification (mixed sexes). For a complete list of all strains used in this study, please see Table S4. For embryonic studies (E15.5–E18.5), timed pregnancies were induced and developmental stage of embryos was verified by various morphological landmarks. P0 is defined as the date of birth for postnatal studies.

Single nuclear isolation and sequencing

Nuclei collection and sequencing: Single nuclear isolation and sequencing was performed based on previously published protocols [59]. Briefly, 10 kidneys were isolated from pooled E18.5 Flk1::H2B-eYFP littermate embryos. Homogenization was performed via douncing and nuclei were isolated using Nuclei EZ Lysis Buffer (Sigma) and centrifugation. YFP+ cells were sorted at the Children’s Research Institute (CRI) Flow cytometry core using a FACSAria II. All steps were performed at 4 °C. Nuclei were sequenced at the UTSW Next Generation Sequencing Core using the 10X Genomics Next GEM Single cell 3’ Reagent Kit v3.1 on the Illumina NextSeq500 High Output Flowcell using V2.5 chemistry.

Data processing: Filtered feature-barcode matrices were generating using the CellRanger count pipeline. Nuclei were called based on deviation of expression profiles from the ambient RNA pool [60], and nuclei with low gene numbers or library sizes, or high mitochondrial gene counts, were excluded (cutoff: three median absolute deviations). Nuclei were pre-clustered and a deconvolution method was applied to compute size factors for all cells [61] and normalized log-expression values were calculated.

Single-nucleus RNA-seq analysis: Cells were clustered by building a shared nearest neighbor graph [62] on the normalized expression data and executing the Louvain algorithm. Normalized expression data was imputed applying Markov Affinity-based Graph Imputation of Cells (MAGIC) to diffusion maps [63]. Marker genes for each cluster were calculated by applying the EIGEN algorithm [22]. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed for sets curated in MsigDb using fgsea Bioconductor package with the earth-mover distance (Wasserstein-1) between the distribution of imputed expression of each gene supported by the subsets of cells being compared as the ranking statistic [64]. GEO accession code: GSE288799.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining on tissue sections

After dissection, tissue was fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C. Paraffin embedding, sectioning, and immunofluorescence was performed as previously described [28]. Briefly, 10 µm sections were obtained onto SuperFrost Plus slides (Fisher Scientific). Slides were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated through an ethanol gradient. Slides were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton-X 100 in PBS for 10 min. Heat induced antigen retrieval in R-Buffer A (Electron Microscopy Sciences) for nuclear antigens or R-Buffer B (Electron Microscopy Sciences) for cytosolic or membrane-bound antigens was performed using a Retriever 2100 (Electron Microscopy Sciences). Slides were blocked in CAS-Block (Invitrogen) for at least one hour and incubated in primary antibody mixture overnight at 4° secondary antibody mixture for 1.5 h at room temperature. After antibody binding, slides were incubated in a blood lysis solution (10 mM CuSO4, 50 mM NH4Cl, pH 5) for 15 min, followed by 5 min in de-ionized water. Slides were then mounted in DAPI-Fluoromount G (Southern Biotech) and imaged on Nikon CSU-W1 or Nikon CSU-W1 SoRa confocal microscopes at the Quantitative Light Microscopy Core at UTSW. Antibodies used are summarized in Table S5.

RNAScope

Tissue preparation: Kidneys were dissected as above and flash frozen in OCT (Tissue Tex). 10 µm cryosections were obtained using a Leica cryostat on SuperFrost Plus glass slides (Fisher Scientific).

RNAscope: Hiplex experiments were carried out using manufacturer protocol using the RNAScope Hiplex v2 kit (Advanced Cell Diagnostics). Sections were pretreated with ProIII for 15 min (E15.5–18.5 sections) or 30 min (P16 sections) at room temperature. Probe hybridization, amplification and fluorophore conjugation steps were carried out per manufacturer protocol. Slides were mounted in Prolong Gold mounting media (Invitrogen) and confocal imaging was performed as in the previous section. Fluorophore cleaving and re-conjugation was performed per manufacturer instruction. Immunofluorescence was performed after the final cleaving step as in the previous section, beginning with Triton X-100 permeabilization. Probes are tabulated in Table S5.

Data processing and Pecam1 masking: Maximum intensity projections were created in ImageJ/Fiji and registered with either ACD Hiplex Registration software or ImageJ/Fiji. Background and non-endothelial signal RNAscope signal was removed as noted in figure legends from Fig. 1D (all probes), Fig. 2F, G, I, and Fig. S7A (Tmtc1 only). For Fig. 1D, the Spots function in IMARIS was used to identify Pecam1 RNAscope dots and Spot size was adjusted to 5 µm (calculated to the nearest whole pixel) to create a Pecam1 mask. A ROI for the Pecam1 mask was created in Fiji using Analyze Particles, selecting ‘vessels’ with at least two contiguous spots. Signal outside of the ROI was cleared. For Fig. 2F, I (Tmtc1 only), a 3um Gaussian blur was applied to Pecam1 signal in Fiji and signal was thresholded. A ROI was created using Analyze Particles in Fiji and signal outside of the ROI was cleared. Occasionally, significant autofluorescent signal (distinguished from RNAscope signal by continuous, wavy fluorescence rather than punctate staining) in the elastic lamina of mature arteries (Fig. S2D”, E”’), was observed and noted in figure captions but not removed.

Whole mount immunofluorescence (WMIF)

After dissection and fixation, tissue was embedded in 2.5–5% low melting point agarose (Invitrogen). 100 µm sections were cut using a Leica vibratome. Floating sections were permeabilized with Triton X-100 in PBS (1% for tissue younger than P4 and 3% for P15 tissue) for 1.5 h at room temperature. Sections were blocked in CAS-Block for 1 h, and incubated in primary antibody mixture overnight at 4° with gentle nutation. Sections were washed in PBS (5 × 1 h), and were incubated in secondary antibody mixture overnight at 4°. Sections were washed in PBS (5 × 30 min). Sections were placed on Superfrost Plus glass slides and cleared in a bubble of Rapiclear 1.47 (Cedarlane Labs) for at least 10 min. Slides were mounted in Rapiclear and imaged as in previous sections.

Esm1CreERT2 lineage tracing

Esm1 lineage tracing was performed by crossing Esm1CreERT2 containing mice (male or female) to mice containing the Rosa26::mT/mG or Rosa26::Rainbow reporter alleles. For short-pulse lineage tracing, timed pregnancies were set up and pregnant dams were orally gavaged with 1 mg 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT, Millipore Sigma) dissolved in acetone and sunflower seed oil at E17.5. Embryonic kidneys were retrieved at E18.5. For long term lineage tracing, pregnant dams were monitored for date of birth, and pups were gavaged with 5 µL of either 6 mg/mL or 20 mg/mL 4-OHT dissolved in ethanol and sunflower seed oil at P0. Pups were sacrificed at either P3 or P15 (mT/mG lineage tracing) or P4 (Rainbow lineage tracing).

Fucci2a endothelial cell cycle labeling

Endothelial cell cycle reporter labeling was performed by crossing Cdh5CreERT2 containing mice to mice containing the Rosa26::Fucci2a allele. For E18.5 kidneys, timed pregnancies were set up and pregnant dams were injected intra-peritoneally with 2.5 mg (250 μL, 10 mg/mL) tamoxifen (Sigma) dissolved in ethanol and corn oil at E15.5. For P15 kidneys, pups were injected intraperitoneally with 0.1 mg (25 μL, 4 mg/mL) tamoxifen at P4.

Data processing and statistics

RNAscope quantification

For Fig. 3D, quantification of Esm1 or Ccdc3 expressing cells within the nephrogenic zone was performed as follows. The nephrogenic zone (NZ) was identified using DAPI staining, as the epithelial UB tip morphology and its associated NPCs have well defined morphologies that can be recognized by eye (the UB epithelium is surrounded by condensed NPC mesenchyme that is 2–4 cells thick) [16]. We defined the NZ as all nuclei superficial to the deepest NPC (this includes all NPC caps, associated UB epithelium and surrounding stroma). Endothelial nuclei were visually identified within the nephrogenic zone using Pecam1 RNAscope signal. Esm1 or Ccdc3 positivity was determined by at least 3 specific RNAscope dots.

QuPath RNAscope quantification

For Fig. S3, S4a-d, and S9d-e, images were acquired using a 40 × objective, with at least two outer cortex images (with surface visible), two inner cortex images (primarily containing mature tubules), and one medulla image being acquired. Composite images were created for each gene analyzed, along with Six2 RNAscope, LTL/DBA, PODXL, and ERG staining, and either Sox17 or Gja5 RNAscope. For each image, regions of interest (annotations) were drawn as follows: NZP—only in outer cortical images, tracing the border of Six2+ NPC nuclei with a 20 μm selection brush in a lateral and cortical direction (i.e., the innermost border of Six2 RNAscope was the innermost border of the NZP); PTC – using a selection brush to trace borders of clusters of LTL or DBA+ tubules in inner cortical images; GC—using a selection brush to trace the border of PODXL staining in both inner cortical and outer cortical images; VR—entire medullary image. SNZP was then defined by using a 300 μm selection brush to trace the border of the kidney in outer cortical images, and removing regions corresponding to NZP, PTC, or GC in the outer 150 μm. Cell detection was then used to identify ERG + nuclei in all regions, with the following settings: Use opening by reconstruction—YES; Background radius—0; Median filter—0; Sigma—10; Minimum area—300 pixels^2 (7.92 μm^2); Maximum area—5000 pixels^2 (132.03 μm^2); Split by shape—YES; Expansion—10 pixels (1.63 μm); Include cell nucleus—YES; Smooth boundaries—YES; Threshold was manually adjusted for each image but kept the same for all regions in each image. AVR and DVR were distinguished by labeling VR ECs with Gja5 or Sox17 RNAscope as DVR. Sub-cellular detection was then used to quantitate number of RNAscope dots in each EC in each nucleus using the following settings: Smooth before detection—NO; Split by intensity—YES; Split by shape—YES; Expected spot size—2 px^2 (0.05 μm^2), Minimum spot size—1 px^2 (0.03 μm^2), Maximum spot size—20 px^2 (0.53 μm^2); Include clusters—YES. Threshold was adjusted per image but kept the same for all regions within a single image. Total dots were counted using total dots = number of single spots + 3*number of clusters. Proportion of high expressing cells was calculated using the following dot thresholds—Esm1: > 10 dots/cell; Ccdc3, Aplnr: > 15 dots/cell.

For Sgk1 quantifications in Fig. S5e, Sgk1 dots were quantified in glomerular ECs as above. For some images, ECs were identified using positive cell detection for Pecam1 with threshold of 75. Sgk1 dots were detected with a threshold of 200 and expected spot size of 5 px^2. Glomeruli were classified as peripheral based on whether they were at least 50% in the outer 200 μm of the kidney.

For Esm1 and Ccdc3 quantifications in proliferating (pHH3+) ECs in Fig. 5h, pHH3+ /Pecam1+ ECs were detected in QuPath using positive cell detection with a threshold of 200 for pHH3 signal and a threshold of 400 (nucleus: max) for Pecam1. Cells were manually curated for proper identification of pHH3+ /Pecam1+ proliferating ECs. Esm1 and Ccdc3 expression were quantified as above.

Fucci2a quantification

All quantifications were performed on maximum intensity projections of 100um sections through the center of the kidney (medulla is visible). Each data point represents a cortical ROIs selected using LTL immunofluorescence. Vessels of the nephrogenic zone plexus, sub-nephrogenic zone plexus, and peritubular/glomerular capillaries were identified visually using EMCN and LTL immunofluorescence. Total number of FucciRed and FucciGreen+ nuclei in each zone were counted.

Statistical analyses and visualizations

All data were plotted and graphs were generated in PRISM 10. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes. All quantifications and experiments were carried out with at least n = 3 embryos from two separate litters, with the exception of P15 Esm1CreERT2; mTmG experiments, which were performed on n = 3 embryos from one litter. No samples were excluded from analysis. No experiments were randomized for animal studies, and investigators were aware of group allocation for outcome assessment. Statistical comparisons were performed as appropriate and noted in figure captions. For Fig. S5e and 5 h Student’s t test was used to compare dots/cell between different zones or different genes. For Fig. 3e, Student’s t test was used to compare cell proportions of Esm1+ cells and Esm1-/Ccdc3- cells. For Fig. 5k, chi-square test was used to compare cell counts in each group, and relative proportions of cell numbers were graphed. For Fig. S4a-b, S8f, and 4L, one way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons was utilized to compare groups. Significance was annotated as such: ns, p > 0.05 (not significant); *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001. Linear manipulations of images were carried out using ImageJ. Figures and models were made using Microsoft Powerpoint, BioRender.com and Adobe Illustrator.
