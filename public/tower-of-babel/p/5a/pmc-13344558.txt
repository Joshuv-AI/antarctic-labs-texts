Diabetes polygenic risk scores and maternal characteristics in Hoosier Moms Cohort participants
Authors: Allison Ellis, Surya Bhamidipaty‐Pelosi, Maha Aamir, Aric Kotarski, Rafael F Guerrero, David M Haas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/ObjectiveGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) increases the risk of adverse perinatal outcomes. Understanding genetic‐based risk for GDM is important for clinical risk prediction. The objective of this study was to examine participant and pregnancy characteristics in different polygenic risk score (PRS) quartiles in a study cohort of people at high risk for GDM.MethodsThis was a secondary analysis of a prospective observational pregnancy cohort study. A total of 411 participants with a singleton gestation were recruited with samples obtained for DNA analysis. Ancestry‐adjusted PRS for diabetes was calculated for the cohort via PLINK v1.9. Continuous variables were analyzed via ANOVA, and categorical variables were analyzed via Chi‐square to examine participant differences by PRS risk quartile, with quartile 1 (Q1) at lowest genetic risk and quartile 4 (Q4) at highest genetic risk.ResultsPRS values were calculated for 391 participants. Distributions of maternal characteristics were similar among the four PRS quartile groups. Participants in Q4 demonstrated a higher rate of positive family history of diabetes (61.9%, p = 0.06) and a higher rate of personal history of past GDM (8.2%, p = 0.08). Compared to individuals only in Q1 (46.4%), the rate of diabetes family history was significantly higher in Q4 (61.9%, p = 0.03). Dichotomizing PRS by combining Q1 and Q2 compared to Q3 and Q4 showed that rates of GDM may be higher in the higher risk group (12.8% vs. 7.1%, p = 0.06).Conclusion and Potential ImpactPregnant individuals in the highest diabetes PRS risk group tended to be more likely to have a positive family history of diabetes and personal history of GDM. Higher risk quartiles tended to have higher rates of GDM. Ancestry‐adjusted PRS calculations facilitated our ability to account for differences in baseline demographic characteristics such as race and ethnicity and thus are likely preferred to isolate the PRS effects on outcomes. These preliminary findings exploring PRS risk group characteristics, if replicated, may help clinicians understand how to stratify genetic‐based GDM risk using only clinical characteristics.Keywords: ancestry‐adjusted, diabetes, gestational diabetes, polygenic risk score, pregnancy

INTRODUCTION

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a transient form of diabetes affecting 1 in 10 US pregnancies, and up to 25% of pregnancies globally [1, 2]. GDM confers more than sevenfold increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus with ∼10% women being diagnosed soon after delivery and 20%–60% of women being diagnosed within 5–10 years post‐index pregnancy [3]. Additionally, children born to mothers with GDM are at a higher risk to develop T2DM and obesity [2]. There are various risk factors for GDM, including high body‐mass index (BMI), higher maternal age, racial and ethnic group, and excessive weight gain during pregnancy [2]. There is also increasing evidence of GDM heterogeneity in both the timing of its diagnosis (early vs. late), and its underlying pathophysiology, for example, insulin resistance and/or insulin deficiency [4, 5]. Polygenic risk scores (PRS) have become a powerful tool providing insight into an individual's genetic predisposition for conditions based on aggregated genome‐wide association study results. PRS offers a modality to predict genetic disease risk burden in an effort to reduce associated morbidities through improved screening and earlier interventions.

Currently, there are many GDM prediction and association studies, but there are very few that utilize PRS scores combined with clinical characteristics in pregnant individuals [6, 7]. Most GDM association publications focus on variables that are in electronic health records, such as age, weight, and body measurements. However, by integrating additional predictive characteristics, such as sociodemographics, behavioral, and biochemical factors, this could improve accuracy for a GDM risk prediction model, and help stratify individuals who may benefit from knowing their PRS to determine optimal intervention strategies [8].

The objective of this study was to look at clinical characteristics in PRS quartiles of individuals in the Hoosier Mom's Cohort (HMC), which preferentially recruited individuals with higher risk of development of GDM. We hypothesized that by better understanding characteristics within the PRS quartiles, we could more accurately identify individuals at risk for GDM eligible for early preventive interventions. Additionally, it will continue to expand research looking at clinical characteristics of pregnant individuals based on a genetic‐risk outcome.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This was a secondary analysis of data from the HMC, an observational cohort study of pregnant individuals designed to find predictive associations with GDM to develop a clinically useful tool to identify high‐risk individuals for potential personalized preventive therapies [9]. Participants were recruited from prenatal clinics if they were under 20 weeks gestational age. Baseline surveys and laboratory measures performed to rule out type 2 diabetes. Participants had to be at least 18 years of age at time of consent and with a singleton gestation. Exclusion criteria included any type of pre‐gestational diabetes, a first trimester screening HbA1c of ≥ 6.5%, chronic systemic steroid use, planned pregnancy termination, inability to provide written informed consent in English or Spanish, the presence of major fetal anomalies, and/or an inability to complete longitudinal study activities. Participants had two visits in the prenatal period (V1[< 20 weeks] and V2 [24–32 weeks]), and at delivery (V3). Surveys were completed and biospecimens were collected at each study visit. Surveys covered a range of areas such as depression symptoms, psychosocial and stress, pregnancy experiences, sleep, activity, and diet and nutrition [9]. Maternal demographics and survey responses were stored in a REDCap database [9].

We genotyped participants who had contributed whole blood samples during their first visit (n = 393). DNA extractions were carried out on a QIAsymphony instrument (from Qiagen; extraction kit DSP DNA Midi Kit #937355, protocol Blood_1000_V7_DSP) at the Center for Genomics and Bioinformatics (Indiana University, Bloomington), and genotyping was completed at the Van Andel Institute (Grand Rapids, MI, USA). Genotyping was carried out using the Infinium™ Global Diversity Array‐8 v1.0 with 1,825,277 markers. Full details are published elsewhere [8].

The sample size of our cohort was not large enough to conduct a genome‐wide search for association with GDM, so we used the genetic data in two ways: (1) to validate loci previously associated with diabetes and (2) to evaluate the performance of reported diabetes PRS [10, 11]. PRS represent a weighted sum of the number of risk alleles carried by an individual, where the risk alleles and their weights are defined by the loci and their measured effects. The full details of PRS generation was described previously [8]. PRS was calculated for the cohort via PLINK v1.9, an open‐source software toolset to efficiently handle and analyzed vast amounts of genotype data (6), with the effect sizes of the 150 markers provided by Powe et al, previously validated for GDM [11]. This PRS was chosen as it accounted for multiple ancestries, had been used in pregnant individuals, and was a good model fit for our genotyping data [7, 10]. Individuals with missing phenotype data were excluded from the analysis. PRS continuous variables were separated into quartiles for comparisons of group characteristics. As an internal validation measure for our attempt to recruit an at‐risk cohort, we compared the PRS distribution for our HMC participants to a previously published pregnancy cohort distribution [8].

We performed descriptive analyses of the cohort and compared characteristics of those in the four PRS quartiles. Continuous variables were compared via ANOVA or t‐tests, and categorical variables were compared via Chi Square to examine participant differences by PRS risk quartile, with quartile 1 (Q1) at lowest genetic risk and quartile 4 (Q4) at highest genetic risk. We did not perform a logistic regression of these descriptive data due to the dependent values not being cardinal values.

RESULTS

A total of 411 participants were recruited and completed study activities in the HMC. Of those participants, there was complete genotype and outcome data for 391 participants. The 20 participants excluded from the analysis were missing data for obstetric outcomes due to inability to obtain delivery records. Table 1 displays the characteristics of the analyzed groups in aggregate and by PRS quartile. Participants had an average age of 29.7 (± 5.3) years, BMI of 27.8 (± 6.8), and 16.6% self‐reported as Hispanic/Latina ethnicity. The most common self‐reported races for the cohort were White (71.4%) and Black (15.9%). A total of 54% of participants reported a family history of diabetes, and 4.3% reported a prior history of GDM.

The distribution of ancestry adjusted PRS in the HMC was similar to that seen in a large cohort of nulliparous individuals (nuMoM2b cohort, Figure 1, p > 0.05).

HMC versus NuMoM2b PRS for GDM development distribution. The figure displays the distribution of polygenic risk scores in two pregnancy cohorts, the nuMoM2b cohort of nulliparous individuals (red) and the Hoosier Moms Cohort (HMC, blue) reported here. The distributions are similar (p > 0.05).

Distributions of maternal characteristics were similar among the four PRS quartile groups (Table 1). There were no significant differences in characteristics among the quartiles. There was a trend toward those in Q4 having a higher rate of positive family history of diabetes (61.9%, p = 0.06) and a higher rate of personal history of past GDM (8.2%, p = 0.08). Lipid panel values, collected on all participants at V1, were not different between quartiles. Hemoglobin A1c at study entry was essential the same in each quartile group (5.1 ± 0.3 mg/dL, p = 0.75, Table 1). Similarly, there were no significant differences in participant self‐reported sleep, diet, physical activity, stress, or pregnancy experiences (Table 1).

Compared to individuals only in Q1, the rate of diabetes family history was significantly higher in Q4 (61.9% vs. 46.4%, p = 0.03). Comparing just this highest risk quartile to the lowest risk quartile did not reveal any further significant differences in any characteristics (Table 1). Dichotomizing PRS by combining Q1 and Q2 compared to Q3 and Q4 showed that rates of GDM were likely higher in the higher risk group (12.8% vs. 7.1%, p = 0.06).

An exploratory use of standard (non‐ancestry‐adjusted) PRS for diabetes demonstrated significant differences in distribution of race and ethnicity groups (Supplemental Table 1). In that comparison, participants in Q4 compared to those in Q1 had a higher self‐reported minority racial status (68.1% vs. 3%, p < 0.001) and higher self‐reported Hispanic/Latina ethnicity (29.2% vs 9%, p = 0.015). Participants in Q4 had a lower educational attainment (p = 0.02), as well as shorter estimated gestational age at delivery compared to Q1 participants (38.1 ± 2.5 weeks vs. 38.7 ± 2.1 weeks, p = 0.006). They also had a lower birth weight in grams (3067.7 vs. 3344.9 g, p < 0.001). Compared to Q1, participants in Q4 had lower cholesterol (187.9 ± 36.5 vs. 201.7 ± 43.3, p = 0.025) and low density lipoprotein (LDL) levels (96.5 ± 29.0 vs. 105.9 ± 37.0, p = 0.037). However, Q4 participants had higher hemoglobin A1c levels at study entry (5.2 ± 0.3% vs. 5.0 ± 0.3%, p = 0.007).

DISCUSSION

In this cohort, we found that those participants in the highest ancestry adjusted diabetes PRS genetic risk group quartile tended to be more likely to have a positive family history of diabetes and personal history of GDM. Higher risk quartiles tended to have higher rates of GDM. Ancestry‐adjusted PRS calculations accounted for differences in baseline demographic characteristics such as race and ethnicity and thus are likely preferred to isolate the PRS effects on outcomes. Thus, using ancestry adjusted PRS should be standard for these types of studies, as using PRS that do not account for ancestry may only identify differences that are based more on racial social constructs or social determinants of health.

A study deriving GDM PRS using the UK Biobank found that higher PRS scores were associated with higher BMI [7]. In the HMC, there was a trend toward this association only when using PRS without ancestry adjustment, likely limited by sample size. Other GDM PRS calculations focused on calculation methods or were in specific populations and thus did not report similar characteristic associations with scores such as self‐reported race and ethnicity [11, 12].

PRS stratification is becoming increasingly used to identify individuals at high risk for type 2 diabetes [13, 14]. However, the vast majority of studies within the literature depict results from non‐gravid populations and did not use ancestry‐adjusted PRS. Hayes et al. provide genomic evidence for maternal metabolic traits being similar between the gravid and nongravid states [15]. There are, however, a handful of PRS studies performed in patients with GDM using known type 2 diabetes variants given the similarity in genetic susceptibility between GDM and type 2 diabetes [8, 15, 16, 17]. Kwak et al. identified eight variants associated with GDM based on genetic risk scores and found that weighted and unweighted genetic risk scores were strongly associated with clinical parameters such as the effect of insulin treatment, fasting glucose, glucose area under the curve, and decreased insulin concentrations [16]. Interestingly, that study also demonstrated that the genetic risk scores had a limited value in identifying GDM at the population level. However, there may be value in the ability to more precisely stratify an individual's risk of GDM. Lamri et al. outlined methodology for the GDM predictive ability of the PRS which could be enhanced by integrating GWAS data and extracting aggregate statistics from a multi‐ethnic genome‐wide meta‐analysis [12].

Outside of pregnancy, Li et al. performed a longitudinal cohort study evaluating the association of PRS with type 2 diabetes in two cohorts of women, both with previous history of GDM, in USA and in Denmark [18]. Their results demonstrated a relationship between a set of candidate genes and the risk of type 2 diabetes specifically showing that those within the higher quartiles have increased risk respectively. However, similar to the majority of published studies, their study was designed and evaluated within a mostly European population and may not have the same predictive accuracy for other ethnicities [19]. Notably, Ge et al. developed a trans‐ancestry PRS by integrating type 2 diabetes genomic data from multiple populations including European, Asian, and African Americans [20]. Their model presented a validated trans‐ancestry PRS and its potential as a meaningful index of risk among diverse patients in clinical settings. Different from our results, their trans‐ancestry PRS depicted Hispanic individuals with high PRS having a substantially increased risk of type 2 diabetes.

Interestingly, even with differences in cohort characteristics, the HMC ancestry adjusted PRS had a similar distribution as the large nuMoM2b cohort (Figure 1). For HMC, only 66% of the participants were nulliparous, with 54% having a positive family history of any type of diabetes and just over 4% having had GDM in the past. HMC set out to recruit a cohort with enriched risk for GDM. This enhances the generalizability of the findings for similar groups of pregnant patients.

In the original analysis of the HMC cohort, while we did identify three genetic markers associated with GDM, PRS quartile was not ancestry adjusted and was not associated with developing GDM (p > 0.05) [9]. However, we noted the rates of GDM in the four quartiles was skewed towards the ends (Q1 13.6%, Q2 7.6%, Q3 8.5%, Q4 13.8%). In this current analysis using ancestry‐adjusted PRS, the higher rates of GDM are seen in the two highest risk quartiles, which makes more sense and is consistent with what is reported in the literature [8, 11]. Given the cost and time involved in obtaining genotyping or PRS, the clinical utility may be limited to a subset of the pregnant population. Thus, understanding the clinical characteristics within that cluster in higher PRS quartiles is important as those individuals may not benefit from PRS. We plan to use several cohorts including HMC to explore a machine‐learning informed model to predict which patients may clinically benefit most from knowing their GDM PRS early in pregnancy. This may help identify patients who may benefit from early interventions, but would not necessarily be identified as being high risk based on other clinical characteristics.

If PRS identified highest risk individuals before conception, then it is possible that early interventions could reduce GDM development [8, 21]. This could go beyond the standard diet and exercise clinical advice given to all pregnant individuals or those seeking pregnancy. Early partnerships with primary care practitioners to identify women of childbearing potential, screen for family planning status, offer PRS blood test option, and facilitate early referral to obstetricians could facilitate preventive strategies for those at highest risk. Further prospective trials to evaluate the effectiveness of different aspects of early intervention (antenatal education, nutritional guidance, exercise programs, and pharmacotherapy) would be needed. If these studies demonstrated that dietary or exercise or other interventions particularly helped those at highest genetic risk for GDM, similar to the Pagel paper [8], it could aid in counseling and motivation for patients who might otherwise not engage in behavior change. This could potentially help reduce both GDM and later type 2 diabetes.

Dyslipidemia and GDM have been associated in past studies [22]. Pregnancy is a state of dynamic physiologic changes which contribute to alterations to maternal lipid metabolism across pregnancy [23]. These changes are clinically seen as progressively increasing concentrations of maternal triglycerides, total cholesterol, LDL‐C concentrations, and triglyceride/HDL‐C ratios. Wang et al demonstrated that lipid profiles in pregnant women with GDM were significantly different from the control group in that the GDM group had higher total cholesterol and LDL‐C concentrations across pregnancy [24]. We did not find baseline lipid differences in the PRS groups, possibly further demonstrating that these factors may be independent contributors to GDM risk. This could be an area of future research to determine the role that lipids may play in a baseline GDM predictive model.

GDM is heterogeneous, and the risk stratification of genetically predisposed individuals may play an important role in disease diagnosis and management. All participants in HMC who developed GDM were diagnosed with routine clinical testing in the early 3rd trimester. There is current discussion about early GDM and its clinical significance and physiology. All HMC participants had screening HbA1c measures, ruling out undiagnosed type 2 diabetes. However, the ancestry‐adjusted PRS and the current methodology will allow for analysis in larger cohorts where genetic susceptibility is not as well understood, for example, South Asians, Latinas, and people of African descent. This may discover different genetic characteristics for those with early versus late GDM, potentially paving the way for different clinical strategies based on PRS.

The study is limited in its sample size. This may have played a role in some characteristics having p‐values near significance. For genomic studies, typically the sample size is much larger. We also included all racial and ethnic groups. While the original GDM PRS work was only performed in those of European ancestry [11], recent work has demonstrated that the PRS also performs well for non‐European ancestry individuals for type 2 diabetes [20]. This work will need to be further validated for GDM, as very limited data exist on ancestry‐adjusted PRS specific to GDM. Race and ethnicity were self‐reported in our cohort. These are social constructs which are currently understood to be surrogates of systemic racism. While the use of ancestry adjusted PRS did not improve our initial prediction or association with GDM in HMC (data unpublished and not shown here), using ancestry markers in GWAS and PRS is a more accurate way to characterize risk based on ancestry.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, in our cohort of individuals at higher risk for GDM, we found that higher risk quartiles tended to have higher rates of GDM. Using ancestry‐adjusted PRS calculations was able to account for differences in baseline demographic characteristics such as race and ethnicity and thus are likely preferred to isolate the PRS effects on outcomes. These data may help inform the development of a multi‐cohort, clinically useful tool that can be used for GDM prevention strategies.
