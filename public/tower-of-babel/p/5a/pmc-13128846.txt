A new free surface identification method for 3D MPS method
Authors: Chong Geng, Wen-hua Wang, Meng-yuan Heng, Yu Zhao, Hao Yang, Yi Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In three-dimensional applications of the Moving Particle Semi-Implicit (MPS) method, free-surface identification is commonly based on particle number density, which can lead to misclassification of surface particles and consequently degrade the accuracy of pressure field predictions. To address this issue, an improved formulation for computing the relative position divergence (RPD) is first developed to achieve better consistency with theoretical values. Based on geometrical considerations and test cases involving internal cavities, a set of surface identification criteria suitable for three-dimensional conditions is established. Building upon the improved RPD formulation, a new free-surface particle detection method is proposed by coupling the relative position divergence with an approximate surface normal vector estimation (RPD + NV). Validation using geometrical models demonstrates that the proposed method can accurately identify surface particles on convex and concave boundaries as well as within cavity-containing configurations. Further assessments through a series of MPS simulations—including hydrostatic, dam-break, and damped oscillation cases—show that both the improved RPD method and the combined RPD + NV scheme effectively reduce pressure oscillations. In particular, the RPD + NV method significantly decreases false negatives and false positives in surface particle identification, leading to smoother and more physically consistent pressure fields, without introducing a noticeable increase in computational cost. Therefore, the proposed surface particle detection method provides a robust and efficient solution for three-dimensional MPS simulations.

Introduction

The Moving Particle Semi-Implicit (MPS) method1 is a mesh-free numerical approach, and has demonstrated strong capabilities in simulating liquid flows with large deformations and highly nonlinear free surface phenomena, owing to its freedom from mesh distortion. It has been successfully applied to scenarios such as dam break flows2–4 and liquid sloshing5–7. The original MPS formulation is known to suffer from severe pressure oscillations. To obtain an accurate pressure field for fluid flow problems, extensive efforts have been devoted to improving the MPS method from various perspectives, including modifications of the source term8–13, gradient forms10,14–16, and particle shifting techniques17–21. In addition, free-surface particle identification plays a crucial role in the accuracy of the pressure field, as the construction of the pressure Poisson matrix directly depends on the identification of free-surface particles. An inappropriate identification criterion may incorrectly classify internal particles as free-surface particles, thereby artificially enforcing a zero-pressure condition on these particles and leading to non-physical pressure distributions. Therefore, the accurate identification of surface particles remains a critical issue in the MPS method.

Extensive research has been conducted on the identification of free surface particles. In the original MPS method, S. Koshizuka et al22. employed a particle number density (PND) approach. However, this method heavily relies on inter-particle spacing, often leading to misidentification of surface particles. To address this limitation, Dilts23 developed a curvature-based identification method suitable for two-dimensional particle systems. Tanaka and Masunaga24 proposed a correction to the PND method by incorporating the number of neighboring particles as an auxiliary criterion. Considering the inherently non-uniform distribution of surface particles, Zhang et al6,25. proposed a vector that represents the asymmetry of neighboring particles, and established two-dimensional identification standards for dam-break and sloshing flows. Shibata et al26. developed a virtual light-source and virtual screen-based method to identify surface particles. By evaluating the proportion of occluded area on the screen, they formulated two- and three-dimensional identification criteria. While this method provides accurate identification in two dimensions, its applicability in a three-dimensional spherical coordinate system is limited, particularly near the poles, where occlusion calculations become less reliable. Wang et al27. assumed that particle pressure does not vary drastically between successive time steps. Based on this assumption, they used the pressure field from the previous time step as a reference for current surface identification. In the ISPH framework, the particle relative position divergence (RPD) was often employed28,29 to identify surface particles with a divergence value of 1.5 in two-dimensional simulations. This approach was later introduced to the MPS method30, while the cutoff value was changed to 2.25 in 2D, noting that for uniformly distributed particles, the theoretical value should be 3—indicating a deviation from the reality. Zhu31 proposed a global scanning–based free-surface identification scheme, in which sector-shaped regions in two dimensions and conical regions in three dimensions are rotated over the entire angular domain. This method is capable of identifying free-surface particles with a maximum concave angle of up to 90°; however, it requires a substantial increase in computational cost. Sun et al40. adopted a similar approach in two dimensions, where free-surface particles are identified through continuous global scanning using a 90° sector region.

As another mesh-free method, the SPH method also faces challenges in free-surface particle identification, and several criteria developed within the SPH framework can be adapted for use in the MPS method. Kernel-based approaches32,33 detect free-surface particles by evaluating the summation of gradients over neighboring particles. This strategy is conceptually similar to the particle number density criterion employed in MPS and generally suffers from limited accuracy. Dilts23 developed an arc-based detection method for two-dimensional particles, in which a circle centered at the target particle is intersected with circles associated with neighboring particles. The particle is classified as a free-surface particle if the resulting arcs fail to fully cover the circumference of the target particle. This approach was later extended to three dimensions by Haque and Dilts34, where boundary circles were replaced by boundary spheres. Candidate boundary spheres are intersected with neighboring spheres to form a set of curved intersection circles; if any surface arc remains exposed, the particle is identified as a free-surface particle. Lee et al.35 employed the positional divergence to filter particles in two dimensions; however, not all free-surface particles can be accurately identified using this criterion alone. In the work of Marrone et al.36, the eigenvalues of a renormalization matrix were first used to preselect candidate free-surface particles, after which a normal-vector–based scanning procedure was applied to achieve accurate identification. Although this method was validated in both two and three dimensions, it involves matrix inversion operations and thus incurs a high computational cost. Wang et al.20 combined the positional divergence criterion proposed by Lee et al.35 with the “umbrella-shaped” scanning region introduced by Marrone et al.36 to further refine the identification of free-surface particles. More recently, Liu et al.37 proposed a semi-geometrical method that differs from the normal-based umbrella-shaped scanning strategies adopted in previous studies20,36. In three dimensions, particles with positional divergence values between 1.9 and 2.9 are subjected to a global scanning procedure using conical regions within a sphere. If at least one conical region with an opening angle of 45° contains no neighboring particles, the particle is classified as a free-surface particle.

Among the aforementioned methods, two-dimensional surface identification tends to be more accurate. However, in three dimensions, identification accuracy generally decreases with increasing dimensionality, and the use of more sophisticated algorithms often leads to significantly higher computational costs.

In this study, an improved positional divergence–based formulation for free-surface particle identification in the MPS method is developed to correct the inconsistency with theoretical values reported in previous work30, and its validity is confirmed in three spatial dimensions. Based on static geometrical models, the limitations of divergence-based criteria for three-dimensional surface identification are systematically analyzed. To overcome these limitations, an MPS-based approximate normal vector evaluation method is proposed and combined with the positional divergence to establish an accurate three-dimensional free-surface detection scheme, for which reference threshold values are quantitatively justified. The proposed method is computationally efficient and does not introduce a significant increase in computational cost compared with the RPD approach. Validation using geometrical models and a series of MPS simulations demonstrates that the method can reliably identify free-surface particles. Owing to the improved accuracy of surface detection, the numerical results exhibit better agreement with experimental observations, accompanied by reduced pressure oscillations and a more uniform pressure distribution.

Under two-dimensional conditions, the RPD methods28,29 generally consider a particle i to be a free surface particle when the divergence ∇·ri is less than 1.5. However, the critical coefficient β requires redefinition in three dimensions. To establish an appropriate criterion, a regularly arranged solid containing a cubic cavity was analyzed. The outer cube has an edge length of 1.0 m, with a central cubic cavity measuring 0.4 m per side. The particle spacing d0​ is set to 0.05 m. Figure 2 illustrates the divergence distribution of particles in 1/8 of the domain.

Fig. 2Divergence distribution of 1/8 domain.

Divergence distribution of 1/8 domain.

The divergence values of the outer surface particles are all below 1.85. For the inner surface, special attention must be given to two types of critical particles. The first is particle A, located at a corner with coordinates (0.2, 0.2, 0.2), and the particles on the inner side wall, such as particle B at (0.0, 0.2, 0.15). Their divergence values are 2.8951 and 2.3025, respectively. Additionally, two particles from the inner layer near the external surface are introduced to assist in defining the threshold coefficient. These are particle C at (0.45, 0.45, 0.45) and particle D at (0.45, 0.45, 0.4), with divergence values of 2.3992 and 2.5960, respectively. If the threshold β is defined as the maximum inner surface divergence, i.e., β = 2.8951, then the criterion ∇·ri < β would falsely classify internal particles C and D as surface particles. Conversely, setting β = 2.3992 to exclude these internal particles would result in particle A being incorrectly considered an interior particle. Therefore, no single threshold within the range β ∈ [2.3992, 2.8951] can perfectly separate surface and internal particles.

Validation

Geometry validation

Firstly, two geometric models were selected to verify the effectiveness of the proposed 3D surface particle identification method, as shown in Fig. 4(a). The two models were constructed by combining a cube and a sphere using Boolean addition and subtraction operations, denoted as cube + sphere and cube − sphere, respectively. These configurations represent two typical simulation scenarios: a liquid with a convex free-surface deformation and a liquid with a concave free-surface deformation. Both geometries were discretized into particles with a uniform particle distance of 0.04 m. The total numbers of particles were 261,353 and 193,314 for the two cases, respectively, while the corresponding numbers of true surface particles were 26,997 and 25,114. The particle distributions of the two models are presented in Fig. 4(b).

Fig. 4Geometry model and discrete points.

Geometry model and discrete points.

To investigate the influence of the interaction radius and divergence threshold in the RPD method, simulations were performed using interaction radii of 2.1d0, 3.1d0, and 4.1d0. For each interaction radius, the divergence value ∇∙r was varied from 2.00 to 2.90 with an increment of 0.10. In addition, considering that some particles identified as free-surface particles may in fact be located inside the solid body, the number of matched particles—defined as the number of detected free-surface particles that are truly located on the surface—was evaluated. Precision (defined as the ratio of matched particles to detected particles) and recall (defined as the ratio of matched particles to the true number of surface particles) were adopted as reference criteria for assessing the RPD method, with target thresholds of precision > 95% and recall > 85%.

Figure 5 presents the identification results for the two geometrical models. As shown in Fig. 5(a), for the cube–sphere model, the recall criterion is satisfied only when the divergence reaches 2.9, whereas the precision remains below 90%. Therefore, an interaction radius of 2.1d0 does not meet the required accuracy criteria. In Fig. 5(b), the cube–sphere model satisfies both criteria within the divergence range ∇∙r ∈ [2.5, 2.7]. At ∇∙r = 2.5, the precision reaches 99.96%, indicating that nearly all detected particles are true surface particles. For the cube–sphere subtraction model, both criteria are satisfied within ∇∙r ∈ [2.0, 2.6], and within the narrower range of [2.2, 2.5], both precision and recall exceed 99.50%.

When both criteria are simultaneously satisfied, a divergence threshold of 2.50 is recommended for the RPD method with an interaction radius of 3.1d0. Under this condition, the precision exceeds 99.60% for both models, while the recall is 85.58% for the cube–sphere model and 100% for the cube–sphere subtraction model.

As shown in Fig. 5(c), for an interaction radius of 4.1d0, both models satisfy the criteria within the divergence range [2.3, 2.4]. At ∇∙r = 2.40, the precision exceeds 95.30% and the recall exceeds 94.10% for both models. However, for the cube–sphere subtraction model, the precision does not exhibit a stable near-100% plateau similar to that observed in Fig. 5(b) within the range [2.0, 2.5], and a similar behavior is observed for the cube–sphere model. Considering that precision should be maximized to avoid misclassifying internal particles as free-surface particles, an interaction radius of 3.1d0 is therefore selected for the RPD method.

Fig. 5Identification results for different interaction radii and divergences.

Identification results for different interaction radii and divergences.

It is observed in Fig. 5(b) that, when ∇∙r < 2.7, the recall for the cube–sphere model falls below 90%, indicating that a considerable number of true free-surface particles are not detected. To address this limitation, the normal-vector (NV) method is introduced as an auxiliary identification criterion. Notably, when the divergence value is less than or equal to 2.3, the precision reaches 100% for both models, implying that no misclassification occurs prior to the application of the auxiliary criterion. Accordingly, a divergence value of 2.3 is selected as the lower bound for introducing the auxiliary NV-based identification, while the upper bound is set to 2.9.

Conclusion

In this study, a free-surface particle detection method tailored for the three-dimensional MPS framework is developed. The proposed method is validated through both geometrical models and MPS simulations, including hydrostatic, dam-break, damping decay, and irregular three-dimensional cabin sway cases, to demonstrate its effectiveness in free-surface particle identification and its capability to improve pressure predictions.

First, an improved relative position divergence (RPD) formulation for the MPS method is proposed to achieve better consistency with theoretical values. However, it is observed that some particles cannot be reliably identified using divergence-based criteria alone. To address this limitation, an approximate normal vector (NV) estimation method is further developed and combined with the positional divergence to form a new free-surface particle detection approach, referred to as RPD + NV.

Systematic investigations using geometrical models provide reference values for the interaction radius, divergence threshold, and angular parameter, demonstrating that the proposed method can accurately identify concave surfaces, convex surfaces, and internal cavities. Convergence analyses based on hydrostatic simulations confirm the robustness of the new method with respect to particle spacing and time step size. Comparisons among different identification strategies verify the reliability of the improved RPD method, particularly in reducing pressure oscillations, while the RPD + NV method achieves more accurate free-surface particle identification. Moreover, the proposed approach exhibits a computational efficiency comparable to that of the RPD method, without introducing a significant additional computational cost. In dam-break simulations, the RPD + NV method accurately detects free-surface particles under highly dynamic flow conditions and captures richer surface details, leading to more reasonable impact pressure predictions and an improved pressure field distribution. Smaller pressure fluctuations and smoother pressure evolution produced by the RPD + NV method are consistently observed throughout the damping decay simulations. Furthermore, simulations of a chamber subjected to sway motion, which involve typical three-dimensional characteristics, further demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method in complex three-dimensional scenarios.

Overall, the proposed free-surface particle detection method effectively enhances the accuracy and robustness of three-dimensional MPS simulations, leading to improved pressure predictions and more physically consistent results.
