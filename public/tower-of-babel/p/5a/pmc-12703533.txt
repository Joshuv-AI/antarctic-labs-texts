Introduction

Care coordination is essential to delivering high-quality care, especially for patients with chronic or complex conditions. Since 2013, Medicare has financially reimbursed coordination of care for patients after discharge from acute care settings through transitional care management (TCM). Additionally, since 2015 and 2020, respectively, Medicare has reimbursed clinicians for coordinating care for patients with multiple chronic conditions (chronic care management; CCM) and then with one primary condition (principal care management; PCM). However, there has been limited overall uptake, with fewer than 20% and 5% of the Medicare-eligible patients receiving TCM and CCM, respectively. Notably, studies have shown that administrative challenges in documentation (eg, recording every phone call, message, or email to the patient), and substantial investment of resources into nonclinical staff or electronic medical record compliance, can impede uptake of and limit coordination services to the majority of eligible patients. 1–5

Recognizing these challenges, Medicare recently began reimbursing for new advanced primary care management (APCM) services. APCM builds directly on the design of PCM, CCM, and TCM, integrating elements from each to support care coordination in primary care settings and reimbursing primary care clinicians through monthly, subcapitated payments. 6 Key differences between APCM services and predecessor care coordination services include the use of monthly billing based on patients’ number of chronic conditions and income status, and regardless of number of services provided. This is in lieu of time-based billing requirements, which may be onerous for practices and clinicians to track. APCM is anticipated to achieve similar goals and can be used similarly to PCM, CCM, and TCM in most instances. Therefore, practice and policy leaders would benefit from insight about collective use of PCM, CCM, and TCM as APCM adoption begins in the United States. In turn, the authors sought to conduct a national analysis of the collection of 3 services to provide context that informs APCM.

Methods

This analysis used 2020–2023 Medicare data encompassing 100% of professional services billed to and reimbursed by Medicare. 7 Current Procedural Terminology codes 99424, 99425, 99426, 99427, G2064, and G2065 were used to capture PCM (meant to reimburse clinicians for services provided to patients with a single chronic condition or with multiple chronic conditions, but focused on a single high-risk condition; introduced 2020). Codes 99437, 99487, 99489, 99490, 99491, G3002, G03003, and G0506 were used for CCM (meant to reimburse clinicians for services provided to patients with 2 or more chronic conditions; introduced 2015). Codes 99,495 and 99,496 were used for TCM (meant to reimburse clinicians for coordinating care between acute care and postacute settings during the post–hospital discharge period; introduced 2013). These codes were combined together as coordination services for evaluation. 8,9

The authors calculated the cumulative sum of service counts and respective reimbursements, overall and by clinical specialty, grouped into primary care (eg, internal medicine, family medicine, and general practice), medical subspecialty (eg, cardiology, pulmonology, nephrology), and other (eg, orthopedics, dermatology) specialty types. The authors also stratified use by place of service, classified as hospital outpatient departments/home-based clinics, physician offices, home, or other settings (eg, nursing facilities, assisted living facilities).

The authors used Kruskal-Wallis tests to compare overall differences between the cumulative sum of service counts by specialty type and place of service. Statistical tests were 2-tailed and considered significant at α = 0.05. The authors’ analysis followed Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards guidelines where applicable. Given the publicly available nature of all study data and the self-determination checklist to aid the decision in accordance with institutional policy, the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center institutional review board waived approval.

Results

A total of 25,269,895 coordination services corresponding to $2,257,471,660 were provided by clinicians to Medicare beneficiaries between 2020 and 2023. Coordination services increased each year, from 2,898,905 counts and $422,233,052 in 2020 to 9,495,152 counts and $757,003,100 in 2023, corresponding to a growth of over 227% in counts and 79% in payments.

Between 2020 and 2023, coordination services were most frequently delivered by primary care clinicians, which as a group accounted for 17,210,579 (68%) services, compared to medical subspecialists, which accounted for 5,718,337 (23%) services, and other specialists, which accounted for 2,340,980 (9%) services (P = .017). Among primary care clinicians, coordination services increased from 2,087,515 in 2020 to 5,873,933 in 2023, corresponding to an increase from $356,924,613 to $522,198,634 in payments (Table 1). Among medical subspecialists, coordination services increased from 528,866 in 2020 to 2,594,681 in 2023, with a concurrent increase from $46,975,239 to $166,019,072 in payments. Among other specialists, coordination services increased from 282,524 in 2020 to 1,026,538 in 2023, accounting for an increase from $18,333,199 to $68,785,393.93 in payments.

Discussion

Between 2020 and 2023, care coordination services were most frequently delivered by primary care clinicians in physician offices. Over time, services increased across all clinical specialties and care sites. The findings are notable for several reasons.

First, study results underscored that Medicare’s coordination service codes have succeeded in their intent to largely support primary care clinicians in providing care coordination services. Consistent with prior work, the authors observed that primary care clinicians provided the majority of services both in each year examined and for the overall study period. 5 This finding confirmed the potential benefit of policy decisions to target this new coordination service of APCM toward primary care clinicians. As the health care community gains experience over time with APCM and data about utilization become available, future work could assess how primary care clinicians choose to deliver APCM vs predecessor services (such as PCM, CCM, or TCM), along with the underlying drivers of those decisions. Drivers could include those related to administrative burden (eg, flat-fee per-patient reimbursement under APCM vs time-based per-activity reimbursement under historical codes) and implementation (eg, practice infrastructure requirements).

Second, this study showed steady growth in care coordination services each year across all clinical specialties, not just primary care. Indeed, medical subspecialists accounted for 18% in 2020 and over 27% of all coordination services delivered to Medicare beneficiaries nationwide by the end of 2023. Such findings highlight the need to design policies in ways that engage non–primary care clinicians in coordination activities, along with the potential missed opportunities of not doing so for common chronic conditions.

One policy solution could be to introduce analogous specialty coordination services (eg, flat-fee billing for care coordination services rendered by specialists) in future rulemaking. Alternatively, standalone TCM and CCM policies could be adjusted to address the detailed documentation requirements and personnel/technology needs facing medical subspecialists and other specialists, given that their use of these services will become increasingly paramount as primary care clinicians leverage APCM for coordination activities. Such emphasis would also acknowledge the unique roles that these clinicians can play in coordinating care for common chronic conditions (eg, transitions after hospitalization for exacerbations of congestive heart failure or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), ideally in partnership with primary care.

Study limitations included the descriptive design and data limitations that precluded additional geographic-, clinician-, and patient-level analyses. Also, by combining 3 care coordination services (PCM, CCM, and TCM) into one analytical framework (APCM), the differences in utilization between primary care clinicians and specialists may have been amplified. Future work should evaluate the impact of coordination services on patient outcomes, along with the effect of regional variation on outcomes. Furthermore, future studies must also address changes in implementation barriers and facilitators as APCM shifts to a flat-fee billing (ie, per member per month reimbursements based on billed codes) vs the time-based billing associated with predecessor codes (ie, reimbursements for time spent on coordination activities based on billed codes). Nonetheless, as an exploratory national analysis, this study yielded insights about a category of important coordination services that can support policy and practice leaders seeking to improve patient and population outcomes.

Conclusion

Although care coordination services were most frequently delivered by primary care clinicians in physician offices, services increased across all clinical specialties and care sites over time. This study corroborated the potential benefit of policy decisions to target new coordination services (ie, APCM) toward primary care clinicians, while also highlighting the need to design policies in ways that engage non–primary care clinicians for common chronic conditions (eg, congestive heart failure or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease). Future work must evaluate longitudinal impact of new coordination services on patient outcomes, effects of regional variation, and implementation barriers and facilitators compared to predecessor services.
