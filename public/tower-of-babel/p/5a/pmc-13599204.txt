Psychological inflexibility and depressive symptoms as key correlates of suicidal risk among university students in a U.S.–Mexico border city
Authors: Rotimi Oguntayo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuicidal risk among university students remains a pressing public health concern, particularly in high-stress border contexts. While depressive symptoms are well-established correlates of suicidal risk, the adjusted association of psychological inflexibility and the potential roles of resilience and perceived social support remain underexplored among university students in structurally stressed regions.ObjectiveThis study examined whether psychological inflexibility was independently associated with suicidal risk beyond depressive symptoms and assessed whether resilience and perceived social support statistically accounted for indirect associations between psychological inflexibility and suicidal risk, and whether these pathways differed by sex.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 385 university students (M_age = 27.45, SD = 8.15) using snowball sampling. Participants completed validated measures of depressive symptoms, psychological inflexibility, resilience, perceived social support, and suicidal risk. Pearson correlations, simultaneous multiple regression, and moderated mediation analyses (PROCESS Model 7; 5,000 bootstrap resamples) were conducted.ResultsSuicidal risk was strongly positively correlated with depressive symptoms (r = .813, p < .001) and psychological inflexibility (r = .701, p < .001) and negatively correlated with perceived social support (r = -.448, p < .001) and resilience (r = -.118, p = .020). In multivariable regression (R2 = .739), depressive symptoms (β = .607, p < .001) and psychological inflexibility (β = .294, p < .001) showed the strongest adjusted associations with suicidal risk; however, resilience and perceived social support were not independently associated with suicidal risk in adjusted models. Moderated mediation analyses indicated no significant statistical indirect associations through resilience or perceived social support, and the indices of moderated mediation by sex were not significant.ConclusionsDepressive symptoms and psychological inflexibility were the key adjusted correlates of suicidal risk in this border-region sample. Findings suggest that psychological inflexibility may represent a clinically relevant and potentially modifiable correlate of suicidal risk. However, longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal and causal pathways.

Background

Suicide remains one of the leading causes of death among young people globally, with a substantial proportion of suicide deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries. Depressive symptoms are consistently associated with elevated suicide-related risk, although this association is shaped by interacting social, cultural, biological, psychological, and environmental factors [1, 2]. Latin America remains underrepresented in suicide research, despite evidence suggesting a recent increase in suicide rates in the region, in contrast to declining global trends [3]. In Mexico, suicide risk among young adults has also become an increasing public health concern, with young people reporting elevated psychological distress and unmet mental health needs [4, 5]. These trends underscore the importance of identifying modifiable psychosocial processes associated with suicidal risk in high-stress environments.

The U.S.–Mexico border region has been described as a structurally and psychosocially stressful context due to overlapping social, economic, environmental, and migration-related pressures [6–8]. Previous studies suggest that cumulative stressors, including violence, extreme weather conditions, immigration-related tension, and limited access to mental health resources, may contribute to psychological distress and suicide-related vulnerability among people living in this region [9–12]. Ciudad Juárez, located in northern Mexico in the state of Chihuahua along the U.S.–Mexico border, is especially relevant for suicide-risk research because Chihuahua has reported one of the highest suicide rates in Mexico. In INEGI’s 2025 report on 2024 suicide statistics, Chihuahua recorded a suicide rate of 16.4 per 100,000 inhabitants, substantially above the national rate of 6.8 per 100,000 inhabitants [4]. As a major urban centre within a high-burden border state, Ciudad Juárez provides an important local context for examining suicide-related psychosocial risk among university students [4]. Local reports have also highlighted recent concern about rising suicide cases in the municipality [13]. University students may be particularly vulnerable because emerging adulthood often involves identity development, academic adjustment, separation from family support, financial responsibility, and changes in social networks, all of which may interact with psychological distress and help-seeking barriers [2, 5, 14]. However, empirical studies focusing on how depressive symptoms and psychological inflexibility are associated with suicidal risk in university students in the border context remain limited.

Depressive symptoms are consistently associated with elevated suicide-related risk, although this association is shaped by interacting social, cultural, biological, psychological, and environmental factors [1, 2, 15–17]. Recent university-based evidence indicates that depressive symptoms and related emotional difficulties are associated with suicidal thoughts among college students, even after accounting for demographic factors [14, 18]. Beyond depression, psychological inflexibility has increasingly been identified as a transdiagnostic process relevant to suicide-related vulnerability. Psychological inflexibility refers to a maladaptive pattern in which individuals become rigidly attached to distressing thoughts and emotions, avoid painful internal experiences, and struggle to act consistently with personally meaningful values [19–21]. Individuals with higher psychological inflexibility may be more likely to engage in dichotomous thinking, perceive distress as inescapable, and experience reduced access to adaptive coping strategies, all of which are relevant to suicidal risk [22]. Emerging evidence further suggests that psychological inflexibility may be associated with suicide-related outcomes independently of depressive severity, challenging models that treat entrapment vulnerability only as a consequence of mood disturbance [23]. Nevertheless, many studies have examined psychological inflexibility mainly as a mediator between depression and suicidality, often without considering resilience and social support as related psychosocial resources.

Resilience and perceived social support are frequently discussed as psychosocial resources that may be associated with lower psychological distress and suicide-related vulnerability. A study among university students found that higher resilience is associated with better emotional adjustment, lower experiential avoidance, and improved learning-related outcomes [24], another study reported that general resilience was associated with lower suicidal ideation among university students [18]. Similarly, emotional and practical social support may reduce the impact of stress and depressive symptoms on suicide-related vulnerability [25, 26]. However, recent findings suggest that the protective roles of resilience and social support may not always be direct, but may operate through psychological processes such as enhanced flexibility, adaptive coping, and perceived connectedness [21, 27]. In the present study, perceived social support refers to students’ perceived availability of emotional, relational, and practical support from close others and the broader social environment [28]. Resilience refers to the capacity to recover from stress and maintain adaptive functioning under adversity [18]. However, resilience was not treated as a fixed personality trait; rather, it was conceptualised as a dynamic process shaped by interactions among individual, relational, and contextual protective resources. Accordingly, resilience and perceived social support were examined as related but distinct psychosocial resources that may be associated with suicidal risk in the context of depressive symptoms and psychological inflexibility.

Sociodemographic factors also require careful consideration in suicide-risk research among university students. Age, sex, income status, sexual orientation, and financial support status may influence students’ exposure to stress, access to psychosocial resources, and patterns of suicide-related vulnerability [25, 29, 30]. For instance, evidence suggests that sex differences in suicidal thoughts and behaviours may vary by outcome, with some studies reporting higher non-fatal suicidal behaviour among females and higher suicide mortality among males [30], while Mexican national statistics provide important context for suicide mortality patterns [4]. Suicide-related vulnerability may also be elevated among sexual and gender minority individuals, particularly when stigma, discrimination, or social marginalisation is present [29]. However, findings are not entirely consistent across contexts. For example, a border-city study reported that being male and being a young adult were associated with elevated suicidal risk [10]. These mixed findings highlight the need for further context-specific evidence on how sociodemographic characteristics are associated with suicidal risk among university students in structurally stressed border-region settings. Sex was examined as a potential moderator because previous evidence suggests that coping resources, perceived support, and suicide-related vulnerability may differ by sex; however, given limited prior evidence on sex differences in the psychological inflexibility–resilience/social support pathways, this analysis was interpreted as theoretically informed but exploratory.

This study was guided by Acceptance and Commitment Therapy/Training theory, which proposes that psychological suffering is not produced only by painful thoughts or emotions, but also by rigid attempts to suppress, control, or escape them, especially when these efforts reduce contact with values, social connection, and adaptive action [19, 31]. From this perspective, experiential avoidance may intensify depressive symptoms because individuals become fused with negative self-evaluations, hopeless thoughts, and painful affect while withdrawing from meaningful activities that could otherwise restore purpose and belonging. Recent evidence supports experiential avoidance as a transdiagnostic process associated with depressive symptoms, while ACT-based reviews indicate that greater psychological flexibility is linked with better mental health outcomes and reduced suicide-related vulnerability [32–34]. Therefore, even after accounting for social support and financial support status, psychological inflexibility/experiential avoidance may remain theoretically important because it reflects the internal process through which individuals respond to distress. Students experiencing depressive symptoms may be more likely to perceive suicide as an escape from intolerable private experiences when they lack flexible acceptance, values-based action, and effective engagement with available support. At the same time, social support and financial support status remain important contextual factors because perceived support may protect against suicidal thoughts, whereas socioeconomic strain may worsen distress and restrict access to coping resources [35, 36].

The theoretical lens of the present study is that depressive symptoms represent an affective correlate of suicidal risk, whereas psychological inflexibility represents a cognitive-behavioural vulnerability associated with experiential avoidance, reduced flexible problem-solving, hopelessness, and perceived entrapment. Resilience and perceived social support were examined as psychosocial resources that may statistically account for, or weaken, the association between psychological inflexibility and suicidal risk. Sex was examined as a potential moderator because previous literature suggests that coping resources and suicide-related vulnerability may vary across demographic groups. This study therefore examined whether psychological inflexibility was associated with suicidal risk beyond depressive symptoms, whether resilience and perceived social support statistically accounted for indirect associations between psychological inflexibility and suicidal risk, and whether these pathways differed by sex.

Most participants were aged 18–34 years (86.0%). Undergraduate students represented 60.5% of the sample and postgraduate students 39.5%. Sample size adequacy was determined using Cochran’s [37] formula for estimating proportions in large populations. Assuming a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), a conservative population proportion (p = 0.50), and a 5% margin of error (e = 0.05), the minimum required sample size was estimated at 384 participants. A total of 402 students accessed the survey, 392 provided electronic consent, and 385 completed all required measures and were included in the final analysis. The final sample therefore met the minimum estimated sample size requirement and also exceeded common recommendations for multiple regression based on 10–20 cases per predictor.

Eligibility criteria were: (a) current enrolment at UACJ, (b) age 18 years or older, and (c) ability to read and understand Spanish. Enrolment in university mental health services was not required because recruitment targeted the general student population. Before participation, respondents reviewed an informed consent form describing the study purpose, confidentiality, voluntary participation, right to withdraw without penalty, and referral information where necessary. Only participants who provided electronic consent were allowed to proceed with the survey. No incentives were provided. The survey took approximately 15–20 min to complete.

Participants were recruited using non-probability snowball sampling. Initial recruitment occurred through institutional mailing lists, student networks, social media platforms, WhatsApp groups, and university-affiliated online groups. Participants were also encouraged to share the survey link with other eligible students. Because the survey link was disseminated through open networks and peer sharing, a precise response rate could not be calculated. Because the survey was distributed through open networks, the number of students who viewed the invitation could not be determined. Accordingly, findings were interpreted as association-based rather than prevalence estimates.

Measures

A structured questionnaire was used to collect sociodemographic and psychological data. Sociodemographic items assessed age, sex, financial support status, sexual orientation, and relationship status. Psychological variables were assessed using standardised self-report instruments, and continuous scale scores were used in regression and PROCESS analyses.

Plutchik Suicidal Risk Scale (PSRS; [38]) was used to assess suicidal risk in participants. The PSRS is commonly administered as a 15-item self-report screening instrument with dichotomous Yes/No responses, where each affirmative response is scored as 1 and higher scores indicate greater suicidal risk. A recent Mexican university student study reported acceptable reliability for the PSRS (α = 0.796) and evidence of convergent validity with depression measures [1]. In the present study, this modified PSRS demonstrated good internal consistency in the present sample (α = 0.83), and evidence of convergent validity was supported by its positive association with PHQ-2 scores. The scale was administered using a modified three-point response format: yes = 2, indifferent/unsure = 1, and no = 0. This modification was intended to reduce forced responding among participants who felt uncertain or ambivalent, consistent with literature on neutral response options and suicidal ambivalence [39, 40]. Higher scores indicated greater suicidal risk. Internal consistency recorded in this sample was α = 0.83.

Oslo Social Support Scale (OSSS-3; [41]) was used to assess perceived social support. The OSSS-3 is a three-item instrument assessing the number of close people available for support, others’ perceived interest and concern, and access to practical help when needed. Total scores range from 3 to 14, with higher scores indicating greater perceived social support. Scores may be interpreted as poor social support (3–8), moderate social support (9–11), or strong social support (12–14, [42]). Previous studies have reported acceptable reliability and validity evidence for the OSSS-3 among Spanish-speaking populations, including studies conducted in Mexico and Latin American contexts [28]. In the present sample, internal consistency was acceptable, α = 0.72.

Acceptance and Action Questionnaire–II (AAQ-II; [43]) was used to assess psychological inflexibility and experiential avoidance. The AAQ-II is a seven-item unidimensional scale rated from 1 = never true to 7 = always true, with higher scores indicating greater psychological inflexibility. Evidence of validity and measurement invariance has been reported among Mexican university students [44]. In the present study, AAQ-II scores were treated as an index of psychological inflexibility and discussed as psychological inflexibility for conceptual alignment with psychological inflexibility models. Internal consistency in the present sample was excellent (α = 0.91).

Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2; [45]) was used to assess depressive symptoms. The PHQ-2 contains two items assessing anhedonia and depressed mood over the previous two weeks. Responses are scored from 0 = not at all to 3 = nearly every day, producing a total score from 0 to 6, with higher scores indicating greater depressive symptom severity. Prior evidence among Mexican university students has supported the reliability and validity of the PHQ-2 [46]. Internal consistency in the present sample was excellent (α = 0.94).

Brief Resilience Scale (BRS; [47]) was used to assess general resilience, defined as the perceived ability to recover from stress. The BRS contains six items rated on a five-point Likert scale from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree, with three items reverse-scored. Total scores were computed by summing the six items after reverse-scoring of relevant items, with higher scores indicating greater resilience. Evidence from Chilean and Mexican university student samples supports the factorial structure, reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of this scale [48]. In the present sample, internal consistency was acceptable, α = 0.70.

Design and procedure

This study used a cross-sectional quantitative design with an online self-report survey. Suicidal risk was specified as the outcome variable, while psychological inflexibility was examined as the main predictor. Resilience and perceived social support were examined as proposed statistical indirect associations, and sex was examined as a moderator of the association between psychological inflexibility and each proposed mediator. Depressive symptoms, age, financial support status, and sexual orientation were included as covariates.

The study was conducted among undergraduate and postgraduate students enrolled at the Universidad Autónoma de Ciudad Juárez (UACJ); a public university located in the U.S.–Mexico border region. Established in 1973, UACJ is one of the major public universities in Ciudad Juárez, Mexico, and offers programmes across architecture, design and art, biomedical sciences, social and administrative sciences, engineering, and technology [49]. The border-region setting is relevant for examining suicide-related psychosocial risk factors because students may be exposed to intersecting structural and psycho-dispositional stressors, including economic insecurity, community violence, migration-related stress, discrimination, and limited access to mental health services, as discussed in the study background.

Participants were recruited through institutional mailing lists, student networks, WhatsApp groups, social media platforms, and university-affiliated online groups. The survey link was disseminated through open networks, and participants were encouraged to share it with other eligible students. Because recruitment used non-probability snowball sampling and open online dissemination, a precise response rate could not be calculated.

Before accessing the survey, participants were presented with an electronic informed consent form explaining the study objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits, confidentiality, voluntary participation, and the right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Only participants who provided electronic consent were allowed to proceed to the survey. No incentives were provided. The online survey took approximately 15–20 min to complete. All responses were collected anonymously, and no identifying information was retained for analysis. Data were stored on encrypted, password-protected systems accessible only to the principal investigator and designated supervisors.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Comité de Ética en Investigación, Universidad Autónoma de Ciudad Juárez (Approval No. CEI-2023-1-63). Electronic informed consent was obtained before survey access. The study was conducted in accordance with established standards for observational research and was reported in line with the STROBE guidelines for observational studies [50] and the CHERRIES checklist for internet-based surveys [51]. Deviations from full checklist compliance, such as the inability to calculate a response rate due to open recruitment, are acknowledged in the limitations section. Given the sensitive nature of the study, participant support procedures were included. Contact information for the university’s psychological services was provided at the end of the survey to facilitate access to professional support for participants who experienced distress or required referral.

Analysis of results

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 21 and the PROCESS macro-Version 4.2 [52, 53]. Descriptive statistics were computed for demographic and psychological variables, including means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages as appropriate. Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to examine bivariate associations among age, perceived social support, resilience, psychological inflexibility, depressive symptoms, and suicidal risk. A simultaneous multiple regression model was estimated to examine the independent associations of depressive symptoms, psychological inflexibility, resilience, perceived social support, age, sex, sexual orientation, and financial support status with suicidal risk. Before regression analysis, assumptions of linearity, independence of errors, multicollinearity, and influential cases were examined. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors and tolerance values. Visual inspection of residual plots did not indicate major violations of linearity or homoscedasticity. No influential cases were detected based on Cook’s distance values. Collinearity diagnostics did not indicate severe multicollinearity (variance inflation factors were below 2.25, tolerance values above 0.44, and a maximum condition index of 24.95). Although the maximum condition index suggested possible moderate collinearity, VIFs and tolerance values were within acceptable ranges, and results were interpreted with caution. Missing data were minimal (< 5%) and were handled using listwise deletion. Regression assumptions and diagnostics were examined before interpreting the model. Variance proportions did not suggest shared dependency among predictors. Age was entered as a continuous variable in years. Sex was dummy coded as 0 = female and 1 = male. Financial support status was dummy coded as 0 = no financial support and 1 = financial support. Sexual orientation was dummy coded as 0 = heterosexual and 1 = homosexual, and marital status was assessed using the categories as reported in Table 1.

Moderated mediation analyses were conducted using Hayes [52] PROCESS Model 7 with 5,000 bootstrap resamples. Psychological inflexibility was specified as the predictor, suicidal risk as the outcome, resilience and perceived social support as parallel mediators, and sex as the moderator of the paths from psychological inflexibility to each mediator. Depressive symptoms, age, financial support status, and sexual orientation were included as covariates. Statistical indirect effects were considered significant when 95% bootstrap confidence intervals did not include zero. Given the cross-sectional design, the mediation and moderated mediation findings were interpreted as conditional indirect associations rather than evidence of causal mechanisms. Accordingly, the results indicate patterns of statistical association and should not be interpreted as establishing temporal ordering or causal pathways.

Moderated mediation analysis

A moderated mediation analysis was conducted using PROCESS Model 7 to examine whether resilience and perceived social support statistically accounted for indirect associations between psychological inflexibility and suicidal risk, and whether sex moderated the paths from psychological inflexibility to each proposed mediator. Psychological inflexibility was entered as the predictor, suicidal risk as the outcome, resilience and perceived social support as parallel mediators, and sex as the moderator. Financial support status, sexual orientation, age, and depressive symptoms were included as covariates. Indirect effects were estimated using 5,000 bootstrap samples with 95% confidence intervals.

Discussion

This study examined whether psychological inflexibility was associated with suicidal risk beyond depressive symptoms and whether resilience and perceived social support statistically accounted for indirect associations between psychological inflexibility and suicidal risk among university students in a U.S.–Mexico border context. Consistent with the cross-sectional design, the findings are interpreted as adjusted statistical associations rather than evidence of temporal or causal effects. Overall, depressive symptoms and psychological inflexibility emerged as the strongest correlates of suicidal risk. Perceived social support and resilience were inversely associated with suicidal risk at the bivariate level, but these associations were not retained in the fully adjusted outcome models. The proposed indirect associations through resilience and perceived social support were not statistically significant, and sex did not significantly moderate these pathways.

The strong association between depressive symptoms and suicidal risk is consistent with a substantial body of evidence identifying depressive symptom severity as a central psychological correlate of suicide-related vulnerability [15–17]. In the present study, depressive symptoms remained the strongest adjusted correlate of suicidal risk even after psychological inflexibility, resilience, perceived social support, age, sex, sexual orientation, and financial support status were included in the model. This pattern is also consistent with university-based evidence showing that depressive symptoms and other forms of emotional distress are closely associated with suicidal ideation among students [5, 14]. In the Mexican context, where concerns about suicidal ideation and mental health needs among students have been increasingly documented, this finding reinforces the importance of early identification of depressive symptoms within university-based mental health systems [2, 5].

Importantly, psychological inflexibility remained positively associated with suicidal risk after depressive symptoms and other covariates were controlled. This finding suggests that psychological inflexibility may represent a clinically meaningful psychological process that is not fully explained by depressive symptom severity. This interpretation is consistent with the psychological flexibility model underlying Acceptance and Commitment Therapy/Training (ACT), which proposes that distress becomes more impairing when individuals respond to painful thoughts, emotions, and memories through experiential avoidance, cognitive fusion, rigid rule-governed behaviour, and reduced engagement in values-based action [19, 31]. In this framework, suicidal thoughts may be partly understood as escape-oriented responses that become more salient when distress is experienced as intolerable, inescapable, and disconnected from flexible coping alternatives. The present finding aligns with evidence that psychological inflexibility predicts suicidal risk over time among college students [54] and with recent work suggesting that psychological flexibility and inflexibility processes are relevant to suicidal ideation [27]. It is also broadly consistent with meta-analytic evidence linking cognitive flexibility deficits with suicidal thoughts and behaviours [22].

The present findings therefore extend previous evidence by showing that psychological inflexibility was associated with suicidal risk in a university sample located in a structurally stressed U.S.–Mexico border context. Although the current design does not establish directionality, the adjusted association suggests that suicidal risk assessment among students may benefit from moving beyond depressive symptom screening alone. Specifically, assessment may also consider how students relate to distressing thoughts and emotions, whether they become fused with hopeless or self-critical narratives, and whether they retain access to flexible, values-consistent coping during periods of psychological strain. This interpretation is consistent with transdiagnostic accounts of psychological inflexibility as a process that cuts across depressive, anxiety-related, and stress-related symptoms [21, 32].

Perceived social support and resilience showed inverse bivariate associations with suicidal risk, which is broadly consistent with theoretical and empirical literature suggesting that relational support and adaptive recovery capacity may protect against suicide-related vulnerability [26, 42, 55]. The association between lower perceived social support and higher suicidal risk is also consistent with the interpersonal theory of suicide, which emphasises the relevance of perceived burdensomeness, thwarted belongingness, and social disconnection in suicide-related outcomes [26, 56]. Similarly, the negative bivariate association between resilience and suicidal risk is compatible with studies suggesting that resilience may reduce vulnerability to distress and suicidal behaviour, particularly when it reflects adaptive coping, recovery from adversity, and perceived capacity to manage stress [36, 57].

However, neither perceived social support nor resilience retained an independent association with suicidal risk in the fully adjusted outcome model, and neither variable explained a significant indirect pathway between psychological inflexibility and suicidal risk. This result appears to diverge from studies that have reported stronger protective roles for resilience or social support in relation to suicidal ideation and psychological distress [35, 36]. Nevertheless, the finding is not necessarily contradictory. Rather, it may suggest that in this sample, the protective associations of social support and resilience overlapped substantially with depressive symptoms and psychological inflexibility. For example, perceived social support was negatively associated with psychological inflexibility and depressive symptoms, indicating that students with lower support also tended to report more distress and greater inflexible responding. Once these more proximal psychological variables were included, social support may no longer have contributed unique variance to suicidal risk. This pattern suggests that social support may remain clinically important, but its protective role may be partly expressed through reduced depressive symptoms, lower experiential avoidance, greater connectedness, or more flexible coping.

The resilience findings also require careful interpretation. Resilience was negatively associated with suicidal risk at the bivariate level, but it was not independently associated with suicidal risk in the adjusted outcome model. In addition, the adjusted mediator model showed a positive association between psychological inflexibility and resilience, which is theoretically unexpected and should not be overinterpreted. One possible explanation is a statistical suppression effect after depressive symptoms and other covariates were included. Another possibility is that the Brief Resilience Scale captured general perceived capacity to recover from stress rather than suicide-specific resilience. This distinction is important because resilience in the context of suicidal risk may involve more specific protective processes, such as reasons for living, perceived meaning, crisis coping, emotional regulation under suicidal distress, and access to supportive relationships [57]. Therefore, the non-significant resilience pathway may reflect measurement specificity rather than the absence of any protective process. Future studies may need to include suicide-specific resilience or recovery-oriented measures to clarify whether resilience operates differently when suicidal risk is the focal outcome.

The non-significant moderated mediation findings also refine the interpretation of the model. Sex did not significantly moderate the paths from psychological inflexibility to resilience or perceived social support, and the conditional indirect effects were not significant for either male or female students. This suggests that, in this sample, the association between psychological inflexibility and suicidal risk was not statistically explained by sex-specific indirect associations through resilience or perceived social support. However, sex showed a small adjusted association with suicidal risk in the regression model. This finding should be interpreted cautiously because the effect was modest relative to depressive symptoms and psychological inflexibility. It may suggest that sex-related differences in suicidal risk exist, but they may be weaker than transdiagnostic psychological processes in this sample. This cautious interpretation is consistent with literature showing that sex differences in suicidal thoughts and behaviours vary across populations, outcomes, and sociocultural contexts [30]. Similarly, although sexual orientation was theoretically relevant, it was not independently associated with suicidal risk in the adjusted models. This does not negate the importance of minority stress or discrimination in suicide-risk research, particularly given evidence that sexual and gender minority populations may experience elevated suicide-related vulnerability under conditions of stigma and discrimination [29]. Rather, it indicates that sexual orientation did not explain unique variance in suicidal risk in the present adjusted model.

Financial support status and age were inversely associated with suicidal risk in both the multiple regression and the conditional outcome model. This finding is clinically and contextually important because it suggests that younger students with financial support may experience lower suicidal risk after accounting for depressive symptoms, psychological inflexibility, and demographic covariates. Financial support and being older may reduce risk indirectly by easing material strain, improving academic continuity, supporting access to care, and reducing perceived entrapment. This interpretation is consistent with broader evidence that socioeconomic stressors and limited resources may contribute to psychological distress and suicide-related vulnerability [2, 10, 35]. In a border-region university context, financial support may be especially relevant because students may face overlapping pressures related to family responsibilities, employment demands, migration-related uncertainty, and uneven access to mental health services. Thus, the association between financial support and suicidal risk suggests that suicide prevention in universities should not be limited to individual psychological intervention but should also consider institutional and structural support systems.

The U.S.–Mexico border context provides an important ecological lens for interpreting these findings. Border regions may involve chronic and overlapping stressors, including economic insecurity, exposure to violence, migration-related uncertainty, family separation, environmental strain, enforcement-related fears, and barriers to culturally responsive mental health care [6, 7, 9, 58, 59]. Although these contextual stressors were not tested as a validated latent construct in the final model, they provide a plausible local explanation for why depressive symptoms, psychological inflexibility, and financial support status were especially relevant. In settings marked by chronic uncertainty and structural strain, students may have fewer opportunities for recovery, support-seeking, and flexible problem-solving. Under such conditions, depressive symptoms may become more severe, and psychological inflexibility may intensify the perception that distress is inescapable. Therefore, the local context may not simply be a background characteristic, it may shape the conditions under which psychological vulnerability becomes clinically salient.

Clinical and prevention implications

The findings suggest several clinically relevant implications for university-based suicide prevention in structurally stressed border-region settings. First, depressive symptoms should remain central to screening and referral protocols because they showed the strongest adjusted association with suicidal risk. Second, psychological inflexibility may be considered a clinically important and potentially modifiable process. Students who experience distress as intolerable, become fused with hopeless or self-critical thoughts, or disengage from values-based action may require interventions that go beyond symptom monitoring alone. ACT-informed strategies, including cognitive defusion, acceptance, values clarification, and flexible problem-solving, may be particularly relevant when used alongside evidence-based depression care [19, 31, 34, 60].

Third, the non-significant adjusted effects of resilience and perceived social support should not be interpreted as evidence that these resources are unimportant. Rather, their inverse bivariate associations with suicidal risk suggest that they may still be clinically meaningful, especially when integrated with interventions targeting depressive symptoms and psychological inflexibility. For example, peer-support programmes, gatekeeper training, and campus belonging initiatives may be strengthened by explicitly teaching flexible coping, help-seeking, and values-based behavioural activation. Finally, the association between financial support status, age and suicidal risk suggests that suicide prevention should include practical and institutional supports, such as financial counselling for younger adults, scholarship guidance, emergency assistance pathways, and accessible psychological services. In a border-region university setting, such integrated prevention may be especially important because psychological distress may be shaped by both individual vulnerability and structural stress.

Limitations and future directions

Despite the contributions of this study, it has some limitations to be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits conclusions about temporal ordering and causality. Although mediation and moderated mediation analyses were conducted, the indirect effects should be interpreted only as statistical indirect associations rather than evidence of causal mechanisms [52, 61]. Second, the study relied on self-report measures, which may increase shared method variance and response bias. Conceptual and item-level overlap among the PHQ-2, AAQ-II, and PSRS should also be considered. Also, the PHQ-2 assessed depressive symptoms over the previous two weeks, whereas the AAQ-II, OSSS-3, and BRS assessed more general psychological and psychosocial functioning, and the PSRS may reflect both current and past suicide-related experiences. Although multicollinearity diagnostics did not indicate severe redundancy, the measures may share distress-related content, particularly around hopelessness, emotional suffering, and inflexible responses to internal experiences. This overlap may have inflated associations among depressive symptoms, psychological inflexibility, and suicidal risk, and future studies should examine suicide ideation, plans, attempts, and behaviours separately where possible. Third, the snowball sampling strategy and recruitment from a single university limit the generalisability of the findings to other student populations or border-region institutions. Fourth, resilience was assessed with a general resilience measure rather than a suicide-specific resilience instrument. This may have limited the ability to detect protective processes that are more directly relevant to suicidal risk, such as reasons for living, crisis coping, perceived meaning, and recovery from suicidal distress [57]. Fifth, although the study was located in a U.S.–Mexico border context, border-community stress was not fully tested using a validated multidimensional measure.

Future research should use longitudinal designs to clarify whether psychological inflexibility prospectively predicts suicidal risk beyond depressive symptoms. Intervention studies are also needed to examine whether ACT-informed strategies reduce suicidal ideation or related risk indicators among university students in border-region contexts. Future studies should also validate culturally appropriate measures of border-community stress and examine how financial strain, minority stress, migration-related uncertainty, social support, and access to care interact with psychological inflexibility and depressive symptoms. Such work may provide a more contextually grounded understanding of suicide-related vulnerability among students living in structurally stressed regions.

Conclusion

This study concludes that depressive symptoms and psychological inflexibility were the strongest adjusted correlates of suicidal risk among university students living in the U.S.–Mexico border city. Psychological inflexibility remained associated with suicidal risk after accounting for depressive symptoms and contextual covariates, suggesting that flexibility-related processes may be clinically relevant in suicide-risk assessment and prevention. Although perceived social support and resilience were inversely associated with suicidal risk at the bivariate level, they did not show independent adjusted associations and did not explain significant indirect associations. These findings suggest that suicide prevention in border-region universities may benefit from combining depression-focused screening and intervention with strategies that reduce experiential avoidance and strengthen flexible, values-based coping, while also addressing structural stressors such as financial strain and access to support.
