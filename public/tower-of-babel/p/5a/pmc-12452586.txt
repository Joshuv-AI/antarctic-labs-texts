The ‘traffic dilemma’: Rethinking emergency medical logistics in megacities
Authors: Gilles Paché
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFast-growing megacities face a critical logistical challenge: urban congestion significantly disrupts emergency medical systems, delaying access to urgent care and compromising patient outcomes. High traffic density, particularly during peak hours, undermines ambulance responsiveness and the equitable provision of emergency services. While existing solutions – such as compact emergency vehicles, medical drones and intelligent traffic management systems – offer promising improvements, they remain insufficient in isolation. Addressing this issue requires a systemic and coordinated response involving local authorities, emergency services, urban planners and citizens. This opinion paper argues for the integration of logistics and healthcare planning within urban policy frameworks, emphasising the need for strategic infrastructure investment and enhanced inter-agency coordination. The relevance of this analysis extends beyond local contexts: it directly contributes to international public health agendas, particularly the World Health Organization’s recommendations on emergency preparedness and different United Nations’ sustainable development goals. Ensuring rapid and equitable access to emergency care is no longer an operational concern alone – it is a public health and social equity imperative.Keywords: Ambulances, Emergency medical services, Logistics, Megacities, Urban congestion

Introduction

Megacities, frequently hailed as engines of global economic growth, now face an escalating logistical and public health crisis: chronic urban congestion. Driven by rapid population growth and the intensification of commercial and transport activities, this phenomenon not only exacerbates environmental degradation – through alarming levels of air pollution – but also severely compromises the efficiency of emergency healthcare delivery. Dense traffic patterns increasingly obstruct ambulance mobility, delaying critical interventions for patients in life-threatening situations. Across major urban centres in Europe, North America and Asia, emergency medical teams struggle to reach patients within clinically recommended response times,1 with dire consequences for survival rates, particularly in cases of severe trauma or cardiac arrest. This situation exposes a stark paradox: despite benefiting from technologically advanced hospital infrastructures, metropolitan areas are hampered by traffic conditions that systematically undermine emergency logistics. The resulting delays highlight a fundamental misalignment between healthcare capacity and urban mobility systems – an issue that demands urgent attention from both public health and urban planning authorities.

It is equally important to recognise that this challenge extends to rapidly expanding African megacities such as Lagos, Nairobi and Cairo, where urban congestion poses a serious barrier to the mobility of emergency services. These megacities are undergoing intense population growth, often coupled with informal and unplanned urban development, and characterised by inadequate and underdeveloped road infrastructure. As a result, traffic congestion is frequent and severe, significantly hindering the ability of ambulances and other emergency vehicles to reach patients in a timely manner. This situation undeniably exacerbates inequalities in access to healthcare, particularly in peripheral neighbourhoods where the most vulnerable populations tend to reside. The concern is further compounded by the fact that, although urban health systems in these regions are generally better resourced than their rural counterparts, they remain ill-equipped to offset delays caused by chronic traffic congestion. Incorporating the African context is therefore essential to fully grasp the true scale of challenges related to emergency healthcare access – both in megacities of the Global South2 and those of the Global North.

In recent years, response times for medical emergencies have increased markedly in many major urban centres, complicating the effective management of emergency care. In Paris and its surrounding suburbs, the average time for an ambulance to reach a patient in distress has risen by several min due to worsening congestion, at times exceeding 30 min during peak traffic periods.3 In New York city, emergency services are struggling to sustain a response time under 10 min – a threshold widely regarded as critical for the successful treatment of cardiac arrest.4 These figures underscore a central challenge in contemporary healthcare management: beyond the performance of hospital systems, timely access to emergency care is increasingly obstructed by what may be termed a ‘traffic dilemma’. This issue warrants as much attention as efforts to improve emergency provision in rural areas.5 The rising volume of vehicles, coupled with ageing or insufficient infrastructure, intensifies the problem, demanding a comprehensive reassessment of emergency mobility strategies to prevent medical advances from being nullified by logistical delays.

By 2050, an estimated 70% of the global population will reside in urban areas,6 suggesting that the challenges posed by the ‘traffic dilemma’ will become even more acute. In response, a variety of innovative measures are being developed, including intelligent mobility management systems designed to prioritise and streamline the movement of emergency vehicles. The deployment of lightweight emergency units – such as medical motorcycles – provides a practical way to navigate traffic and deliver timely care to patients with non-critical conditions. Likewise, emerging technologies such as medical drones are beginning to enhance conventional response mechanisms, adding a new layer of agility to emergency logistics. Yet the question persists: are such technological innovations, however promising, sufficient to address the deeper structural issues? This opinion paper contends that they are not. Lasting progress requires a systemic rethinking of urban emergency logistics, grounded in integrated strategies that combine technological advancement, spatial planning, traffic governance and public engagement. In doing so, I align with international public health priorities, by advocating for equitable and resilient access to emergency care in an increasingly urbanised world.

Breaking through traffic for faster emergency response

Despite often benefiting from state-of-the-art medical infrastructure, major metropolises face a critical logistical challenge: the growing dysfunction of emergency systems caused by increasingly congested road networks.7 This issue extends well beyond isolated traffic jams; it reflects a broader incapacity of megacities to meet the demands of health emergencies effectively. Much of the existing road infrastructure – particularly in historic city centres – was never designed to accommodate modern traffic volumes and is ill-suited to the specific operational requirements of emergency services. As congestion intensifies, intervention times – vital to the survival of critically ill patients – are becoming unacceptably long. This situation exposes a deeper systemic shortcoming: the failure of urban policy to adequately anticipate and manage emergency scenarios. Ambulance services, once engineered for rapid response, are now increasingly hampered not only by obsolete infrastructure, but also by urban planning frameworks that fail to prioritise emergency access and mobility.

In summary, a clear disconnect has emerged between the evolution of urban road infrastructure and the operational needs of emergency services – a gap that is particularly pronounced in megacities. A range of strategies has been trialled to address this critical misalignment. In London, for instance, designated priority corridors have been introduced to facilitate the movement of emergency vehicles,8 while Singapore has implemented an intelligent traffic system that dynamically adjusts signal timings to prioritise emergency response.9 The centralised management of traffic lights by urban control centres enables the creation of ‘green waves’, significantly improving the mobility of priority vehicles; Stockholm has been a pioneer in this domain. Other megacities, such as Dubai, are exploring more radical solutions, including the deployment of flying ambulances capable of bypassing ground-level congestion to reach patients swiftly. Furthermore, some urban areas have opted for lighter emergency vehicles better suited to dense traffic conditions. In Jakarta, for instance, the Indonesian Escorting Ambulances – a volunteer motorcycle escort group – assists medical teams in navigating through congested roads, often providing immediate care prior to the arrival of conventional ambulances.10

The reliance on light vehicles – particularly motorcycles – for emergency response raises significant safety concerns for both healthcare personnel and other road users. While these vehicles are well suited to navigating heavily congested urban environments, they are also exposed to elevated accident risks, especially in densely populated areas where high speeds and precarious road conditions are common. To mitigate these risks, it is imperative to implement rigorous training protocols for emergency drivers, encompassing advanced urban driving techniques and strict adherence to safety procedures. In parallel, protective equipment must be optimised to provide maximum safety without compromising the rider’s mobility. On the regulatory side, local authorities must establish tailored traffic standards to legally frame and facilitate the safe deployment of light emergency vehicles within urban areas. Apiratwarakul et al11 underscore the necessity of a holistic approach, emphasising that ensuring the safety of motorcycle ambulances is essential not only for protecting emergency personnel, but also for maintaining the speed and effectiveness of medical intervention.

The growing integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into urban traffic management offers substantial potential for transforming emergency response systems. By anticipating congestion and assessing alternative routes in real time, AI can significantly reduce response times and enhance adaptability to unforeseen traffic conditions. In Tel Aviv, for instance, technology developed by Magen David Adom analyses live traffic data to optimise ambulance routing, resulting in a 25% reduction in response times.12 This data-driven approach not only increases the responsiveness of emergency services, but also improves their operational efficiency. Other megacities are following suit by adopting similar AI-based systems that enable emergency services to predict traffic patterns more accurately and dynamically adjust routes. Furthermore, mobile applications linked to emergency networks are empowering citizens to report accidents or medical incidents, thereby improving coordination and accelerating intervention. The deployment of such intelligent technologies represents a pivotal development in the evolution of urban healthcare management.

In the medium to long term, the widespread adoption of intelligent technologies is poised to fundamentally transform emergency mobility in urban environments. As autonomous vehicles become more prevalent, emergency transport control systems will be able to communicate directly with them, automatically clearing priority lanes and facilitating the swift passage of ambulances and other medical units. Machine-to-machine coordination – enabled by AI and advanced communication networks – holds the potential to dramatically reduce response times, particularly in high-density urban settings. However, realising these benefits depends critically on the adaptation of urban infrastructure to support emerging technologies, including the deployment of intelligent sensors and integrated traffic management platforms. Only under these conditions will autonomous vehicle systems revolutionise emergency response by enhancing both the speed and safety of interventions.13 Yet this progress risks deepening global disparities, as many megacities in the Global South may lack the financial capacity to implement such advanced infrastructure at the same scale as their counterparts in the Global North.

Enhancing emergency logistics through smart planning

While the promising innovations offer considerable potential, the implementation of technological solutions poses significant logistical and financial challenges. Their deployment demands substantial investment in infrastructure, as well as close coordination among local authorities, emergency services, and private ambulance operators. Each urban context presents distinct structural and regulatory constraints, necessitating context-specific solutions. Furthermore, the successful adoption of these systems relies heavily on the acceptance and engagement of both healthcare professionals and the public.14 Achieving lasting improvements in urban emergency care logistics therefore requires a systemic and collaborative approach, underpinned by enhanced coordination between all relevant stakeholders. Singapore offers a compelling example: its centralised traffic management system enables real-time communication between ambulance services and traffic control centres, significantly reducing response times. This model provides valuable insights that could be adapted to other large, densely populated megacities facing similar challenges related to congestion and delayed emergency response.

Urban planning plays a pivotal role in facilitating the efficient movement of emergency vehicles. The introduction of dedicated lanes and the strategic reorganisation of high-congestion zones around hospitals have proven particularly effective. In Copenhagen, a ‘soft mobility’ policy15 aimed at reducing car density near healthcare facilities enables faster and safer access for ambulances, thereby minimising delays that could have life-threatening consequences for patients in critical condition. This approach necessitates a redefinition of urban priorities, placing accessibility to healthcare at the centre of planning in sensitive zones. During major crises – such as the COVID-19 pandemic – such measures are vital to the optimisation of emergency care delivery and resource allocation. The overarching aim is to balance urban traffic management with the imperative of timely and equitable medical intervention. An integrated planning strategy of this nature not only accelerates response times, but also enhances the resilience and long-term safety of urban infrastructure.

Implementing integrated emergency health policies in many African countries remains a significant challenge, primarily due to financial constraints, inadequate infrastructure and limited stakeholder coordination. Common obstacles include insufficient technological integration, limited training for healthcare personnel, and unreliable communication systems between emergency services and hospitals. In Addis Ababa, for instance, pre-hospital emergency care is hindered by structural deficiencies, ranging from a lack of essential equipment to the absence of standardised response protocols. The failure to establish effective institutional collaboration directly impairs both the speed and quality of emergency medical interventions, further exacerbated by delays in patient transfer to healthcare facilities. A 2024 study underscores that these limitations severely restrict the capacity to deliver timely and coordinated care in urban environments.16 Its findings reinforce a critical insight: the success of emergency healthcare strategies depends not only on technological investment, but equally on robust governance and inter-institutional cooperation.

Finally, public education and awareness-raising remain essential components in addressing the persistent 'traffic dilemma'. Prevention campaigns – such as those run by Canadian driving schools – have demonstrably reduced incidents involving ambulances being obstructed by improperly parked vehicles. These initiatives must also promote a sense of collective responsibility, cultivating a civic culture grounded in respect and cooperation with emergency services. When citizens understand how their individual behaviour directly influences emergency response times, they become active participants in ensuring efficient access for emergency vehicles. The integration of geolocation technologies and real-time communication systems further strengthens these efforts by enabling authorities to swiftly detect and mitigate obstructions caused by negligent road users. In conclusion, the optimisation of emergency care logistics in urban areas depends on a synergistic approach – one that combines technological innovation, thoughtful urban planning and engaged public participation. Such an integrated model is essential to accelerating interventions and, ultimately, saving lives.

Implications for policymakers and stakeholders

Improving emergency response times in megacities cannot rely on isolated technological fixes or localised efforts. Instead, a fundamental rethinking of how urban infrastructure, mobility systems and healthcare logistics intersect is required. Effective solutions demand the integration of existing technologies, such as GPS-enabled dispatch systems, real-time traffic analysis and centralised traffic command centres. Just as importantly, institutional cooperation among emergency services, municipal authorities and transport operators must be strengthened to ensure coherence and continuity in crisis response. As underlined, the case of Singapore illustrates how real-time monitoring and prioritisation protocols can reduce ambulance delays significantly. These successful models underscore a key message: optimising emergency logistics is not just about speed, but about redesigning systems for reliability, adaptability and equity. Without a clear political mandate and long-term urban planning strategies that prioritise emergency care, megacities risk falling short in their obligation to protect the most vulnerable in critical situations.

Urban policies must embrace a new model of shared responsibility that prioritises healthcare access as a central component of mobility systems. This involves restricting private car use in dense areas, expanding lanes exclusively for emergency vehicles, and promoting a modal shift toward sustainable transport. Public engagement campaigns – including widespread first aid training and mobile platforms for incident reporting – can support faster and more coordinated responses. At its core, this issue goes beyond logistics; it reflects a deeper societal question about how we allocate space, resources and urgency in cities under stress. Resilient healthcare delivery requires a political commitment to act preventively, not reactively. It also calls for public investment strategies that align mobility infrastructures with broader health equity goals in rapidly growing urban areas.

In addition to infrastructure investment and improved institutional coordination, embedding AI within emergency medical logistics demands careful consideration of the practical challenges inherent in the system. Within the highly sensitive context of urban emergency care, technological solutions must demonstrate reliability, minimal intrusiveness, and alignment with healthcare professionals’ established practices; failure to do so risks complicating interventions rather than facilitating them. A recent analysis of ambient AI systems for clinical documentation provides evidence that discreet, well-integrated technologies can elevate care quality without disrupting medical workflows.17 This paradigm offers a compelling blueprint for the development of intelligent traffic management and ambulance dispatch systems, capable of anticipating congestion, recalculating routes in real time, and communicating effectively with emergency teams. The value of such systems resides not only in their algorithmic sophistication, but equally in their proven capacity to integrate flexibly within existing operational processes.

Finally, the future logistical transformations align directly with several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) set by the United Nations for 2030. Enhancing access to emergency care in urban environments advances both SDG 3 (good health and well-being) and SDG 11 (sustainable cities and communities), by reducing spatial inequalities in healthcare provision and improving medical mobility. This international framework offers researchers a robust normative foundation for developing interdisciplinary approaches that integrate public health, urban planning, and logistics. It likewise equips policymakers with a broader, dual-level agenda – both local and global – to embed emergency medical reforms within a long-term sustainability strategy. These imperatives are becoming increasingly urgent, as megacities are projected to accommodate most of the global population in the coming decades. Emergency medical logistics must therefore be understood not merely as an operational function, but as a strategic pillar of health equity – central to the formulation of 21st-century urban policy.
