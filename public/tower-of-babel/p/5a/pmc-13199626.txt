A Successful Monitoring of a Pregnant Woman Suffering from Mixed Connective Tissue Disease-Associated Interstitial Lung Disease: A Case Report
Authors: Xue Cui, Wenying Zhou, Yinrui Sun, Yajie Duan, Haiyan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCurrent evidence on managing mixed connective tissue disease-associated interstitial lung disease (MCTD-ILD) during pregnancy remains scarce, and no established guidelines address this unique clinical scenario. By reporting this case, in which maternal-fetal health was optimized through multidisciplinary collaboration, dynamic monitoring, and individualized delivery planning, we aim to propose a practical and effective clinical management strategy for healthcare providers.Case PresentationA 36-year-old primipara with an 8-year history of MCTD-ILD had long-term treatment with immunosuppressive agents such as tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil and hydroxychloroquine, as well as prednisone before pregnancy. Her condition was stable prior to pregnancy, and she received strict multidisciplinary team (MDT) monitoring during pregnancy. Serial assessments revealed moderate restrictive ventilatory dysfunction on pulmonary function tests and stable interstitial changes on chest CT scans, before delivery, the ILD functional categorization was comprehensively assessed as moderate based on the patient’s clinical manifestations, exercise tolerance, and pulmonary function test results. Due to concerns that labor-induced stress could trigger cardiopulmonary decompensation, an elective cesarean section was performed at 39 weeks under neuraxial anesthesia, yielding a healthy male infant (Apgar score 10). Postoperative management included hydroxychloroquine, methylprednisolone initiation and continuous oxygen saturation monitoring (consistently >95%), with no maternal complications observed. The prognosis for both mother and fetus is favorable.ConclusionStable CTD-ILD patients can achieve successful pregnancy outcomes through preconception optimization, pregnancy-compatible immunosuppression, and MDT-guided delivery planning. Vigilant monitoring of pulmonary function, imaging, and oxygen saturation is critical, with cesarean section recommended for high-risk cases to mitigate peripartum complications. This case reinforces the need for structured risk stratification and multidisciplinary protocols to safeguard maternal and fetal well-being.Keywords: pregnancy, connective tissue disease, interstitial lung disease

Introduction

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is a general term for a heterogeneous group of non-neoplastic and non-infectious pulmonary diseases characterized by inflammation of the alveolar units and interstitial fibrosis as the basic pathological changes, also known as diffuse parenchymal lung disease. ILD is a common pulmonary complication of connective tissue disease (CTD). ILD can be present in most types of CTD, including rheumatoid arthritis, scleroderma, systemic lupus erythematosus, polymyositis or dermatomyositis, Sjögren’s syndrome, and mixed connective tissue disease.1 Connective tissue disease-associated interstitial lung disease (CTD-ILD) accounts for 7.5% to 34.8% of all ILD cases,2 and it predominantly affects women of childbearing age. However, connective tissue disease-associated interstitial lung disease in pregnancy is relatively rare, and large-scale epidemiological data are lacking. Substantial variability in prevalence and outcomes reported across CTD subtypes. Systemic sclerosis, idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, and mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD) are the most common subtypes of connective tissue disease-associated interstitial lung disease, among which the prevalence of MCTD-ILD is 56%.3 The ILD secondary to different types of connective tissue diseases shows significant differences in clinical symptoms, imaging findings, pathological features, as well as disease progression and outcomes,4 this heterogeneity necessitates the involvement of multiple clinical subspecialties (multidisciplinary diagnosis and treatment, MDD) in the diagnosis and management of CTD-ILD.5 During pregnancy, the maternal functional residual capacity decreases significantly, and oxygen consumption increases, which exacerbates CTD-ILD. CTD-ILD also increases the risks of preterm birth, miscarriage, fetal growth restriction and other complications. Individualized differences are significant in gestational management and treatment regimens, which further require multidisciplinary participation, including rheumatology and immunology, respiratory medicine, radiology, obstetrics, and other departments. However, large-scale clinical research data are limited, and standardized protocols are still lacking for the gestational management of patients with CTD-ILD. In February 2025, the Department of Obstetrics at the Second Hospital of Dalian Medical University admitted a rare case of pregnancy complicated by MCTD-ILD, with the joint assessment and full guidance from a multidisciplinary team during pregnancy, favorable maternal and fetal outcomes were achieved. To better illustrate the mutual impacts between pregnancy and connective tissue disease-associated interstitial lung disease, clarify the key points of diagnosis and treatment for such special cases, this article conducts a retrospective analysis of the clinical data of this case and discusses it in conjunction with relevant domestic and international literature reports, aiming to provide references for the clinical management of similar cases.

Case Report

The patient was a 36-year-old female, admitted on February 26, 2025, with the chief complaint of “a history of mixed connective tissue disease-related interstitial lung disease for more than 8 years, and 39 weeks of gestation”. The last menstrual period was May 29, 2024 and the expected date of confinement was March 5, 2025. The patient’s initial symptoms commenced in 2014, characterized by swelling and pain in the interphalangeal joints of both hands, accompanied with Raynaud’s phenomenon. She did not seek medical attention for these symptoms and remained undiagnosed. In 2016, a chest CT during a physical examination at another hospital revealed pulmonary fibrosis. Rheumatological and immunological tests were performed, which showed antinuclear antibody (ANA) and anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibody (anti-U1 RNP antibody) were positive. She was diagnosed with “mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD)”. Treatment was initiated with medications, including prednisone (maximum dose: 60mg), hydroxychloroquine (0.2 g twice daily), mycophenolate mofetil (0.75 g twice daily), tacrolimus (3mg once daily) and calcium (600 mg once daily) supplements. Later, she attended regular follow-ups at the rheumatology department for medication adjustments. Pre-pregnancy evaluation showed stable disease control, with glucocorticoids had been discontinued for 6 years, and only hydroxychloroquine was taken orally for treatment. At 13+6 weeks of gestation the patient visited the rheumatology department of our hospital. Rheumatological and immunological tests revealed: antinuclear antibody (ANA) titer 1:320 (+), ANA fluorescence pattern: granular type (+), anti-U1 RNP antibody (+), anti-SSA antibody (+) and anti-Ro52 antibody (+); no obvious abnormalities were found in lupus anticoagulant tests, anti-phospholipid antibody panel (6 items), complement levels, immunoglobulin levels, or anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) antibody. A multidisciplinary consultation (MDC) was conducted in the outpatient department of our hospital. In accordance with medical advice, she took hydroxychloroquine 0.2 g twice daily and aspirin 100 mg orally every night before bedtime; aspirin was discontinued at 35 weeks of gestation. At 38+5 weeks of gestation, arterial blood gas analysis showed: blood pH 7.41, partial pressure of oxygen (PO2) 95.30 mmHg, oxygenation index (PO2(a)/FiO2(I)) 454.00 mmHg. Pulmonary function tests indicated “increased residual volume/total lung capacity, increased airway resistance, and moderate restrictive ventilatory dysfunction”. Chest CT showed: interstitial inflammatory changes in both lungs, bilateral pleural thickening; local nodular thickening of the bilateral interlobar fissures; small amount of fluid in the bilateral pleural space and right interlobar fissure. Currently, the patient has no discomfort such as dyspnea, cough, or expectoration. Her daily activities were basically unrestricted, she was able to perform daily household chores and walk normally without significant fatigue. She has no history of other chronic diseases, surgeries, or similar conditions. The patient was primigravida. Her spouse is in good health. There is no special family history. On admission examination: Vital signs were stable Auscultation of the lungs revealed end-inspiratory crackles, which were prominent at the bilateral lung bases. The abdomen was soft, and the fundal height and abdominal circumference were consistent with the corresponding gestational week, and fetal heart rate: 145 beats per minute. Preliminary diagnosis: primigravida at full term with mixed connective tissue disease–associated interstitial lung disease. After admission, a hospital-wide consultation was arranged involving the Departments of Rheumatology and Immunology, Respiratory Medicine, Radiology, Anesthesiology, and Medical Administration. Synthesizing the consultation opinions and incorporating results of recent examinations (including arterial blood gas analysis, chest CT, pulmonary function tests, and echocardiography), transthoracic echocardiogram showed normal chamber dimensions/function without evidence of pulmonary hypertension. The patient’s cardiopulmonary function was assessed as being in the compensatory stage. Therefore, a trial of vaginal delivery was not recommended; instead, a cesarean section was advised. The surgery-related risks were fully communicated to the patient and her family, who acknowledged understanding of the information, refused a trial of vaginal delivery, and requested termination of pregnancy via cesarean section. They subsequently signed the informed surgical consent form. A KL-6 test was completed, showing a result of 1647.3 U/mL. The Department of Rheumatology and Immunology recommended after surgery initiating methylprednisolone 20 mg daily to control disease activity, while continuing hydroxychloroquine at its current dosage. On February 28, 2025, the patient underwent a lower uterine segment cesarean section under neuraxial anesthesia. The surgery proceeded smoothly, and a live male infant was delivered, with a birth weight of 2845g and an Apgar score of 10 at 1 minute. Postoperatively, symptomatic treatments were proactively administered, including antibiotics for infection prophylaxis, oxytocin to promote uterine involution, and enoxaparin sodium for thromboembolism prevention. Over the first 3 postoperative days, the patient’s blood oxygen saturation was monitored daily, fluctuating between 96% and 98%. She had no subjective complaints of discomfort such as chest tightness, shortness of breath, or palpitations. The patient exclusively breastfed her newborn postoperatively. By the 3rd postoperative day, her recovery was favorable, the surgical incision healed well, and then a discharge appointment was scheduled. Follow-up has been conducted to date, and both the mother and the fetus have a favorable prognosis.

Discussion

We present the successful delivery of a patient with connective tissue disease-associated interstitial lung disease. Despite suffering from interstitial lung disease and moderate restrictive pulmonary ventilatory dysfunction, she tolerated pregnancy relatively well. Under the joint evaluation and full-course guidance of a multidisciplinary team, the patient showed no signs of interstitial lung disease progression related to this pregnancy and eventually delivered by cesarean section. Up to the 1-year postpartum follow-up, both the mother and the infant have a favorable prognosis.

Colbenson et al6 reported 3 patients with CTD-ILD who tolerated pregnancy relatively well despite having ILD, 2 of whom had severe restrictive pulmonary impairment None exhibited evidence for progression of ILD related to their respective pregnancies, and all were delivered via cesarean delivery. They made the literature review of pregnancy outcomes in previously reported patients with connective tissue disease-related interstitial lung disease, and they summarized 12 cases reported by multiple researchers from 1995 to 2020, among which 10 patients achieved favorable maternal and fetal outcomes through multidisciplinary management during pregnancy. Mayu et al7 reported the first case wherein cyclosporine was used concomitantly with a steroid for the treatment of polymyositis diagnosed during pregnancy, with successful outcome of childbirth without any complications Park et al8 described a 25-year-old pregnant woman with anti-SS with worsening ILD in the latter part of pregnancy, requiring emergency delivery at 36 weeks and 3 days of gestation, and both the mother and the fetus had a favorable prognosis.

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is one of the severe complications of connective tissue diseases (CTDs). Due to the fundamental pathological changes of alveolar inflammation and pulmonary interstitial fibrosis, ILD causes impaired lung function; in severe cases, it leads to respiratory failure, significantly increasing the mortality rate of CTDs.9 The main clinical manifestations are divided into two categories: CTD-specific manifestations, such as morning stiffness, xerostomia and xerophthalmia (dry mouth and dry eyes), joint swelling, and Raynaud’s phenomenon; Common clinical manifestations of ILD, including dyspnea, dry cough, hemoptysis, basal crackles (Velcro rales), and tachycardia.10,11 LoMauro et al12 have indicated that during pregnancy, maternal functional residual capacity (FRC) is significantly reduced due to the effects of hormonal changes, diaphragmatic elevation, and alterations in thoracic morphology Additionally, the increased oxygen consumption during pregnancy substantially impairs the mother’s oxygen reserve capacity. Crapo et al noted that the elevated metabolic demands of the fetus, placenta, and other maternal organ systems lead to increased oxygen requirements and carbon dioxide excretion in pregnant women at rest.13 Although dyspnea in the third trimester of pregnancy is a common physiological phenomenon, for CTD-ILD patients with underlying lung disease, this symptom may be significantly exacerbated, even triggering an acute exacerbation of the disease. Therefore, for pregnant patients with connective tissue disease-associated interstitial lung disease, close monitoring of blood oxygen saturation (SpO2) is essential throughout the entire pregnancy to ensure the fetus receives an adequate oxygen supply. Whether at rest or during physical activity, SpO2 should be maintained at 94% or higher.14 During antenatal check-ups and postpartum, both blood pressure and oxygen saturation were monitored in this case, with oxygen saturation consistently exceeding 95%. There is no clear diagnostic standard for CTD-ILD. The diagnosis is primarily made by integrating findings from medical history, pulmonary function tests, imaging data, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid examination, and pathological results of lung tissue biopsy, while excluding interstitial lung diseases caused by other factors.15 In the clinical assessment of CTD-ILD, pulmonary function testing (PFT) serves as a crucial screening tool and is also used for dynamic monitoring of disease activity and progression. Its typical manifestations include restrictive ventilatory dysfunction and reduced diffusing capacity. The assessment of disease severity is based on pulmonary function indices, specifically using the lowest forced vital capacity (FVC) or lowest diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) (to reflect the degree of abnormality) from either: Pre-pregnancy pulmonary function tests, or all second-trimester pulmonary function tests during the current pregnancy. If no pre-pregnancy or second-trimester pulmonary function test results are available, post-pregnancy pulmonary function test results obtained within 1 year after delivery shall be used for assessment. Severity is categorized into four groups: (1) Normal: DLCO ≥ 80% of the predicted value and FVC ≥ 80% of the predicted value; (2) Mild-to-moderate: DLCO = 61–79% of the predicted value or FVC = 60–79% of the predicted value; (3) Severe: DLCO = 40–60% of the predicted value or FVC = 40–59% of the predicted value; (4) Very severe: DLCO < 40% of the predicted value or FVC < 40% of the predicted value.16 PFS is closely associated with pregnancy outcomes in patients with CTD-ILD. Clowse et al17 considered that compared with women with normal to mild-to-moderate restrictive pulmonary function, women with very severe ILD have a significantly higher incidence of adverse pregnancy outcomes, including miscarriage, preterm birth, preeclampsia, small for gestational age (SGA) infants, or pulmonary embolism. During pregnancy, dynamic monitoring of pulmonary function changes should be conducted every 3 months or as clinically indicated. However, the third trimester of pregnancy and patients with cervical insufficiency are relative contraindications for pulmonary function testing, as such tests may increase the risk of delivery. Pulmonary Function Test

As one of the non-invasive examination methods for ILD, high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) can clearly display subtle structural abnormalities of the lungs due to its high-resolution advantage. This allows for a comprehensive assessment of the extent of lung involvement and the degree of fibrosis, providing critical evidence for the diagnosis and disease evaluation of ILD. In CTD-ILD, the imaging manifestations are mostly symmetric involvement of the bilateral lower lungs, predominantly in the subpleural regions. Based on different imaging features, ILD can be classified into: usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP), non-specific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), organizing pneumonia (OP), lymphocytic interstitial pneumonia (LIP), and diffuse alveolar damage (DAD). MCTD-ILD is predominantly non-specific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), which on HRCT typically presents with reticular opacities, ground-glass opacities, and these findings are most prominent in the bilateral lung bases.18 Regarding the safety of performing HRCT during pregnancy, the Committee on Obstetric Practice of the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) states that the radiation dose of chest HRCT under high resolution ranges from 1.6 to 4.0 millisieverts (mSv). Although there may be potential fetal exposure due to radiation beam scattering during the examination, the estimated actual radiation dose received by the fetus is only 0.01 to 0.66 mSv, which is far below the dose threshold that would cause harm to the fetus. Gjelsteen et al19 have argued that the radiation exposure from chest computed tomography (CT) is extremely low, and no association with fetal teratogenic risk has been identified to date. Therefore, when clinically necessary, chest HRCT may be considered during pregnancy. Lung tissue biopsy is the gold standard for confirming the diagnosis of ILD. However, due to its invasive nature and the potential risk of triggering disease progression, there is controversy regarding the clinical benefits of its application, and its actual implementation rate is relatively low. Beyond lung involvement, ILD may also affect cardiac function: ILD causes hypoxemia, and long-term hypoxemia can increase pulmonary arterial pressure, leading to pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). This increases the afterload of the right ventricle. Additionally, systemic fibrosis associated with CTDs may involve the heart, resulting in heart failure.20 During pregnancy, the factors of blood volume increases, and after delivery, there is a rapid fluid shift may raise the risk of peripartum and postpartum cardiopulmonary failure. Therefore, close monitoring of the pregnant woman’s echocardiogram and changes in cardiac function is necessary. For patients with autoimmune-related ILD who do not have comorbid heart failure, PAH, or highly active rheumatic diseases, attempting conception or continuing a pregnancy is considered safe.17 At 38+6 weeks of gestation, the patient underwent a chest CT scan, which revealed reticular opacities and ground-glass opacities, predominantly in the bilateral lung bases, consistent with nonspecific interstitial pneumonia. The main therapeutic measures for CTD-ILD are controlling the primary disease and anti-fibrotic treatment. Pharmacological treatments for CTDs include: Glucocorticoids; Immunosuppressants: methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, cyclophosphamide, azathioprine, etc. Biologic agents: rituximab, tocilizumab, etc. Anti-fibrotic drugs include nintedanib and pirfenidone. Glucocorticoids can be used when the disease exacerbates during pregnancy, but caution is needed to prevent the occurrence of renal crisis.21 The American College of Rheumatology (ACR) classifies methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, and cyclophosphamide as contraindicated medications before conception, during pregnancy, and during lactation,22 Rituximab can be continued before conception but should be discontinued during the pre-pregnancy preparation period or after confirmed pregnancy.23 There is a lack of relevant trial data regarding the safety of anti-fibrotic drugs when used during pregnancy. Therefore, their clinical use requires extreme caution, and the benefits to the mother and fetus must be fully weighed against the risks.24 Jeganathan et al25 have recommended that women with CTD-ILD should ensure their disease is in an inactive or low-activity state for 6 months before planning conception, in order to reduce pregnancy risks. During pregnancy, a multidisciplinary discussion involving departments such as rheumatology and immunology, respiratory medicine, obstetrics, radiology, and anesthesiology is required to develop an individualized treatment plan. Pregnancy-compatible medications should be selected and used continuously until the lactation period to prevent acute exacerbation of ILD or connective tissue disease symptoms due to medication discontinuation. Grant-Orser et al26 have emphasized that pregnancy in patients with ILD requires pre-pregnancy counseling and planning to ensure disease stability, optimize medication use, and complete vaccinations-laying the foundation for a safe and healthy pregnancy. After the disease in this case was stabilized with drug treatment (prednisone, mycophenolate mofetil, tacrolimus, hydroxychloroquine) and pregnancy occurred, medication such as hydroxychloroquine was used during pregnancy to control disease activity.

Currently, there is no clear literature consensus on the timing and mode of pregnancy termination. Platts et al27 have argued that intrapartum management should be based on obstetric indications; the timing and mode of pregnancy termination should be reasonably selected based on the patient’s cardiopulmonary function status, fetal growth and development, presence of severe complications, and comprehensive assessment results from the multidisciplinary team. In cases where the patient’s condition is stable, fetal development is normal, and there are no severe complications, expectant management can be adopted to maintain the pregnancy until term before termination. However, if a trial of vaginal delivery was attempted, factors such as prolonged labor, fatigue, increased energy expenditure, and the Valsalva maneuver (straining during delivery) could potentially trigger a sudden exacerbation of her underlying disease, leading to decompensation of cardiopulmonary function. King et al have suggested that for ILD patients with a vital capacity (VC) of < 1 L, pregnancy should be avoided or therapeutic abortion should be considered If such patients decide to continue the pregnancy, they should receive optimal medical management for their underlying disease, and cesarean section should be considered for delivery.28 During the labor process, the phenomenon of pregnancy-related hyperventilation can be further exacerbated. Adequate oxygen supplementation for the parturient and effective analgesia can improve ventilation.

Conclusion

In summary, reproductive-aged women with stable CTD-ILD can safely plan pregnancy under multidisciplinary guidance. Dynamic monitoring should include rheumatologic indicators, SpO2, echocardiography, PFTs, and HRCT. Vaginal delivery may be attempted when tolerated; otherwise, cesarean section is recommended. However, this study has limitations as a single case report, and the interaction between pregnancy-related physiological changes and disease progression remains poorly understood. Future large-scale, multicenter studies are needed to identify independent risk factors for adverse pregnancy outcomes and to establish validated risk stratification models, thereby improving individualized management and maternal-fetal prognosis.
