Development and Validation of an Ipsilateral Breast Tumor Recurrence Risk Estimation Tool Incorporating Real-World Data and Evidence From Meta-Analyses: A Retrospective Multicenter Cohort Study
Authors: Yasuaki Sagara, Atsushi Yoshida, Yuri Kimura, Makoto Ishitobi, Yuka Ono, Yuko Takahashi, Takahiro Tsukioki, Koji Takada, Yuri Ito, Tomo Osako, Takehiko Sakai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEIpsilateral breast tumor recurrence (IBTR) remains a critical concern for patients undergoing breast-conserving surgery (BCS). Reliable risk estimation tools for IBTR risk can support personalized surgical and adjuvant treatment decisions, especially in the era of evolving systemic therapies. We aimed to develop and validate models to estimate IBTR risk.PATIENTS AND METHODSThis multicenter retrospective cohort study included 8,938 women who underwent partial mastectomy for invasive breast cancer between 2008 and 2017. Prediction models were developed using Cox proportional hazards regression and validated via bootstrap resampling. Model performance was assessed using Harrell's C-index, Brier scores, calibration plots, and goodness-of-fit tests.RESULTSDuring a median follow-up of 9.0 years (IQR, 6.6-10.9), IBTR occurred in 320 patients (3.6%). The initial model, based on variables from Sanghani et al, achieved a Harrell's C-index of 0.74. Incorporating hormonal receptor status, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 status, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy as predictors reduced the C-index to 0.65, despite their clinical relevance. Importantly, the inclusion of these factors improved calibration, demonstrating better alignment between predicted and observed IBTR probabilities. Although the hazard ratios (HRs) for radiotherapy aligned with the Early Breast Cancer Trialists’ Collaborative Group meta-analyses (MA), those for chemotherapy and endocrine therapy showed slight differences. Therefore, HRs from the MA were used to represent treatment effects in our model.CONCLUSIONWe have developed and internally validated a new risk estimation model for IBTR using Cox regression and bootstrap methods. A Web-based risk estimation tool is now available to facilitate individualized risk assessment and treatment planning.

INTRODUCTION

Ipsilateral breast tumor recurrence (IBTR) remains a significant concern for patients undergoing breast-conserving surgery (BCS) for early-stage breast cancer. Despite advancements in surgical techniques, radiotherapy, and systemic therapies, the risk of IBTR persists and may adversely affect long-term outcomes.1-4 Understanding the factors contributing to IBTR is essential for developing risk estimation models that can guide personalized treatment strategies.

Several clinicopathological factors have been consistently associated with increased IBTR risk.3-6 Among these, younger age at diagnosis is one of the most established predictors, with younger patients exhibiting significantly higher recurrence rates than older individuals. Larger tumor size is also associated with an elevated risk. Surgical margin status plays a critical role; positive margins (no tumor on ink) are linked to increased recurrence, underscoring the importance of achieving negative margins during BCS.7,8 However, re-excision is not always pursued in current practice, with considerable variability depending on the clinical context—particularly when adjuvant radiotherapy is planned—reflecting a shift toward individualized local control strategies.9-11 Although residual tumor burden at the margin may influence clinical decisions, such data are not routinely standardized or readily available. These issues highlight the need for risk models based on widely available and standardized clinicopathological variables.

Large-scale meta-analyses by the Early Breast Cancer Trialists’ Collaborative Group (EBCTCG) have demonstrated that adjuvant therapies significantly reduce the risk of isolated local recurrence.12-14 Radiotherapy after BCS reduces the 5-year local recurrence risk from 26% to 7%, endocrine therapy reduces it by nearly half, and chemotherapy also contributes substantial benefit. These findings underscore the critical importance of tailoring local and systemic therapies to individual risk profiles.

One of the most widely used IBTR risk models, developed by Sanghani et al, was based on patient data from the 1990s.15,16 As such, it does not reflect advances in systemic therapy, radiotherapy planning, or imaging technologies. There is a clear need to update and refine IBTR risk estimation tools to align with contemporary treatment paradigms.

In this context, we conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study of patients treated with BCS, with the goal of developing and validating a new risk estimation model for 5- and 10-year IBTR. By identifying robust clinicopathological predictors, our model aims to support shared decision making and personalized treatment planning.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study Design and Data Source

This study was conducted as a collaborative project of the Japanese Breast Cancer Society Collaborative Research Group and involved seven institutions across Japan. Upon obtaining approval from the institutional review boards of each participating institution, we performed a retrospective analysis using clinical databases from the following hospitals: Cancer Institute Hospital of the Japanese Foundation for Cancer Research (JFCR), St Luke's International Hospital, Kyoto University Hospital, Osaka Metropolitan University Hospital, Mie University Hospital, Okayama University Hospital, and Hakuaikai Sagara Hospital.

Participants

A total of 14,552 eligible patients were women diagnosed with invasive breast cancer who underwent partial mastectomy between January 2008 and December 2017. Inclusion criteria were patients with breast cancer with unilateral or metachronous bilateral breast cancer (only the first cancer included), postoperative pathological T stage of T1-T3, allowance of multiple primary cancers, and no restrictions on patient age.

Exclusion criteria were receipt of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, conversion to mastectomy, simultaneous bilateral breast cancer, multifocal breast cancer, male patients, presence of distant metastasis (M1 stage), and missing critical clinicopathological data such as age, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status, estrogen receptor/progesterone receptor status, surgical margin status, lymphovascular invasion, histological or nuclear grade, and details of adjuvant therapies. Patients with insufficient follow-up duration or incomplete medical records were also excluded.

After applying these criteria, a total of 8,938 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig 1).

Outcome of Interest

The primary outcome of interest in this study was IBTR, defined as the reappearance of carcinoma within the preserved breast after initial breast-conserving surgery (BCS). IBTR events were identified exclusively based on histopathologic confirmation from surgical or biopsy specimens, as recorded in institutional pathology reports. All histologically confirmed ipsilateral breast recurrences were included in the analysis, regardless of whether IBTR was the first event or occurred after other recurrences.

Events were treated as time-to-event data, with follow-up measured from the date of definitive surgery to the date of histologically confirmed IBTR, death, or last documented follow-up. Death from any cause was treated as a competing event in the analysis of cumulative incidence of IBTR. Patients without IBTR were censored at the time of last follow-up. Predictors included in the risk estimation models are described in detail in the Data Supplement.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted using STATA/SE version 18. A multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was used to develop the prediction model.17-21 Predictor selection followed a structured approach. Initially, variables were adopted from a previously published model by Sanghani et al,15,16 which served as the foundation for model 1. These predictors included age category, final surgical margin status, pathological tumor classification (pT), lymphovascular invasion (present/absent), tumor grade (nuclear or histological), perioperative chemotherapy, and perioperative endocrine therapy.

Subsequent models (models 2-6) were constructed by sequentially adding clinically relevant variables and interaction terms known to influence the risk of IBTR, based on current literature and clinical reasoning. Radiation therapy, hormone receptor status, HER2 status, and the use of anti-HER2–targeted therapy were incorporated. Interaction terms between tumor grade and chemotherapy were evaluated in models 3 and 4.

Model performance was assessed in terms of both discrimination and calibration. Discrimination was evaluated using Harrell's concordance index (C-index). Calibration was assessed using the calibration slope, calibration intercept, and calibration plots. To account for potential overfitting and to internally validate the models, 500 bootstrap resamples were applied. The overall model performance was evaluated using the Brier score, which was compared across models. The final model was selected based on a combination of internal validation metrics, including the bootstrap-corrected C-index, Brier score, and visual inspection of calibration plots.

In accordance with recent recommendations, data splitting at the time of model development was avoided, as it can reduce reliability by discarding valuable information. Instead, all available data were used to build the most robust model possible using bootstrap resampling for internal validation. This approach aligns with best practices for prediction model development, emphasizing efficient use of data and the application of internal-external validation where appropriate to assess model generalizability across clusters.22

Although we initially planned to handle missing data using multiple imputation by chained equations, the proportion of missing data was <3% for all candidate predictors. Therefore, a complete-case analysis was conducted by excluding patients with missing values in key variables.

The estimated cumulative incidence of IBTR, which served as the baseline for prediction, was calculated using the Fine and Gray model, treating death as a competing risk. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P < .05 was considered statistically significant. Model development and reporting adhered to the TRIPOD statement.20

A total of 8,938 patients were included in the final cohort for model development, with a median follow-up of 9.0 years (IQR: 6.6-10.9 years). Model Development, Performance Comparison, and Final Specification

The final prediction model (model 6) was selected based on its superior calibration and inclusion of clinically relevant biomarkers and treatment variables.19,20,23 We developed and compared seven models of increasing complexity, beginning with a validated model by Sanghani et al (model 1).15,16 Full model definitions and performance comparisons are provided in the Data Supplement.

Briefly, model 1 demonstrated the highest discrimination (Harrell's C-index = 0.74), whereas model 6 offered improved calibration despite a slightly lower C-index (0.65). Calibration plots (Fig 3) show strong alignment between predicted and observed IBTR probabilities for model 6 across risk strata. All models had identical Brier scores (0.036), indicating consistently low overall prediction error. Model 7, which included institutional variables, showed similar calibration but reduced discrimination (C-index = 0.55), suggesting limited incremental value. Therefore, model 6 was adopted as the final model for clinical implementation. In line with previous methodological guidance,21,24 we incorporated treatment effect estimates derived from EBCTCG meta-analyses into our prediction model, especially for variables with well-established effects and limited representation in our data set. These meta-analyses reported HRs of 0.63 (SE = 0.08) for chemotherapy, 0.54 (SE = 0.07) for endocrine therapy, and 0.31 (SE = 0.04) for radiation therapy with respect to the reduction in isolated local recurrence. The HRs observed in our cohort were consistent with the meta-analytic estimate for radiotherapy, whereas those for chemotherapy and endocrine therapy differed slightly from the corresponding meta-analytic values. Therefore, we adopted the HRs from the meta-analyses in the final model.

Using the Fine and Gray competing risk model, we estimated that the 5-year and 10-year cumulative incidences of IBTR were 5.7% and 12.5%, respectively, for a reference patient age 40-49 years with clear surgical margins, a pT1 tumor, no lymphovascular invasion, grade 1 histology, hormone receptor–positive and HER2-negative status, and no adjuvant therapy.

This final model provides a clinically interpretable and statistically robust framework for individualized IBTR risk estimation. To facilitate practical application, the model has been implemented as a publicly accessible, Web-based tool designed to support shared decision making and personalized treatment planning.

Implementation and Availability of the Prediction Tool

To facilitate clinical implementation, we developed a Web-based tool that provides individualized 5- and 10-year risk estimates for IBTR. Users can input relevant patient and tumor characteristics, and the tool returns the estimated cumulative incidence of IBTR along with corresponding 95% CI.

The tool was developed using the Streamlit framework and deployed via the GitHub platform. It is freely accessible online at IBTR Risk Estimation.25 The tool incorporates the final Cox regression model (model 6), including the baseline cumulative incidence estimated by the Fine and Gray competing risk model and treatment effect estimates derived from EBCTCG meta-analyses. The interface is designed for ease of use by both clinicians and patients, supporting effective risk communication and shared decision making in the postoperative setting. The Data Supplement (Fig S2) shows the Web-based prediction tool and a preview of the iPad version, which will be launched shortly.

DISCUSSION

We developed and internally validated a multivariable Cox regression model to estimate 5-year and 10-year IBTR in patients with early-stage breast cancer. Although the model demonstrated modest discrimination, it exhibited satisfactory calibration and provided reliable absolute risk estimates for individual patients.

During model development, we examined a range of candidate predictors informed by existing literature and clinical plausibility. Neither invasive lobular carcinoma histology nor lymph node positivity improved model discrimination—as measured by Harrell's C—nor were they statistically significant predictors of IBTR.

The status of the surgical margin is a key predictor in IBTR risk modeling. According to the SSO-ASTRO Consensus Guideline, wider margins beyond no tumor on ink are not associated with a further reduction in IBTR rates. However, in our study, patients with close margins had a slightly higher risk of IBTR compared with those with clear margins. This finding aligns with more recent meta-analyses indicating that close margins are associated with an increased risk of local recurrence.4 Nevertheless, margin status alone may not fully reflect the extent of residual disease. Future prediction models may be improved by incorporating more detailed information, such as the volume of tumor near the margin or preoperative imaging findings, including mammography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Patients under age 40 years exhibited a markedly higher risk of IBTR. Although age is a well-established prognostic factor, this observation may also reflect the increased prevalence of hereditary breast cancer syndromes in younger women. For instance, a Japanese study by Momozawa et al26 reported a higher rate of pathogenic germline mutations among young patients with breast cancer. Notably, patients under 40 without such mutations may not share the same elevated risk of recurrence, suggesting that germline testing could refine individual risk estimation in this subgroup.

Although the treatment effect estimates derived from our cohort showed similar trends to those reported in the EBCTCG meta-analyses, the HRs for chemotherapy and endocrine therapy were slightly different. These differences may be partly attributable to residual confounding in our observational data. To reduce bias and enhance the robustness of our model, we adopted meta-analytic estimates derived from randomized controlled trials (RCTs). However, it should be noted that patients enrolled in RCTs are often highly selected and treated under optimal conditions. In contrast, real-world populations include patients with comorbidities or other factors that may limit treatment adherence or completion, particularly with chemotherapy. This discrepancy may affect the generalizability of RCT-derived effect sizes when applied to heterogeneous clinical populations.

The observed cumulative incidence of IBTR in our cohort was lower than that reported in historical cohorts, likely due to advances in adjuvant systemic therapy and the widespread MRI use. Although MRI use was not incorporated as a covariate, its potential role as a modifier is discussed in the Data Supplement.

Finally, IBTR risk estimation models have clinical utility in treatment de-escalation, including the potential omission of radiotherapy. Further discussion of their role in supporting radiotherapy de-escalation strategies is provided in the Data Supplement. In addition, for patients contemplating mastectomy primarily due to fear of recurrence, individualized IBTR estimates can help contextualize their risk and support informed, preference-sensitive surgical decisions.

Our study has several limitations. First, the proportional hazards assumption was violated for age, as younger patients (<40) tended to recur earlier. Although we explored time-varying and flexible modeling approaches, these increased model complexity and reduced interpretability. Therefore, we retained the original Cox model for its clinical relevance and robustness. Future studies should assess advanced modeling techniques to better accommodate nonproportional hazards.

Second, the model showed modest discriminatory performance, partly due to the inclusion of radiotherapy, which reduces recurrence risk across risk groups and narrows outcome differences. IBTR includes both true recurrences and new primaries, which standard clinical variables may not adequately distinguish. Future models may benefit from separating these entities. Our model also tended to overestimate IBTR risk in high-risk patients, consistent with previous models. This highlights the need for cautious interpretation, especially in younger individuals, whose IBTR risk will be lower than expected when no pathogenic germline mutation is identified. Importantly, previous IBTR prediction models have also reported modest discrimination, with C-indices typically ranging from 0.60 to 0.65. For example, the IBTR! nomogram demonstrated an AUC of 0.607 in an external Korean validation cohort, and the ductal carcinoma in situ nomogram from Memorial Sloan-Kettering Cancer Center reported a concordance index of 0.63.27,28 These findings underscore the intrinsic difficulty of predicting IBTR, a relatively rare outcome influenced by treatment and biologic heterogeneity. Although our model shares these limitations, it demonstrated acceptable calibration, particularly in the low- to intermediate-risk range, supporting its potential utility in clinical decision making. Despite these limitations, our tool provides individualized risk estimates that may facilitate shared decision making regarding the need for additional surgical intervention or postoperative radiotherapy. Prospective evaluation of the model's clinical utility in real-world settings is warranted. Future research should assess how the tool influences physician and patient decision making, such as the omission of radiotherapy or avoidance of additional surgery. Planned approaches include decision impact analyses, pragmatic clinical trials, and assessments of patient understanding through surveys or interviews. Additionally, although the median follow-up period was 9 years, some patients had shorter observation times, which may have led to underestimation of late IBTR events. External validation is also necessary to confirm our findings and evaluate the generalizability of our model across broader clinical settings, particularly in non-Japanese or more ethnically diverse populations. Considerations for NAC-treated patients and emerging precision oncology tools are detailed in the Data Supplement.

Our study also has several important strengths. We analyzed data from 8,938 patients across seven major Japanese institutions, providing robust statistical power and enhancing generalizability within the Japanese population. The proportion of missing data for key predictors was <3%, allowing for complete-case analysis and minimizing bias from imputation. Compared with the previously published IBTR model by Sanghani et al, which was developed based on clinical trial data and validated in 7,811 patients treated in the 1990s, our model was constructed using a larger and more contemporary real-world data set comprising 8,938 patients treated between 2008 and 2017. Furthermore, our updated model incorporates clinically relevant variables—such as HER2 status and the use of anti-HER2 therapies—that were not included in earlier models, thereby enhancing its applicability in modern clinical practice. Importantly, our model allows estimation of individualized IBTR probabilities along with 95% CI, offering more informative and clinically actionable assessments. The 10-year IBTR rate in our cohort was substantially lower (3.6%), likely reflecting improvements in surgical techniques, systemic therapies, and the widespread use of preoperative imaging, such as breast MRI. However, the IBTR rate increases when multiple risk factors are present, underscoring the continued importance of estimating individual IBTR risk. Furthermore, the integration of real-world observational data with effect estimates derived from high-quality meta-analyses—such as those from the EBCTCG—strengthens the validity and generalizability of the model. The treatment effect sizes observed in our cohort were largely consistent with those reported in RCTs, supporting the robustness of our modeling approach and its potential clinical applicability.

In conclusion, in developing our model, we prioritized calibration (ie, agreement between predicted and observed risks), which is essential for individualized risk estimation. In addition to model development and internal validation, we created a user-friendly, Web-based prediction tool that enables clinicians to estimate individualized 5- and 10-year IBTR risks along with corresponding 95% CI. This accessible tool may help support surgical and postoperative treatment planning based on individualized risk assessment, including decisions regarding additional surgical intervention for involved margins and the omission of adjuvant radiotherapy in older patients.
