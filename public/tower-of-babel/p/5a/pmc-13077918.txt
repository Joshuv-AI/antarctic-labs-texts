Evaluating PAC visits and time to fitness in a tertiary care cancer hospital: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Pradeepika Gangwar, Uddalak Chattopadhyay, Nikhil Kumar Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEfficient pre-anesthesia check-up (PAC) clearance is essential for timely cancer surgery; however, delays are common in high-volume oncology centres, where complex comorbidities, fragmented workflows, and logistical barriers create process bottlenecks. Evidence quantifying the combined impact of these factors—particularly the burden of repeated visits and long travel distances—remains limited.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included adults (≥ 18 years) attending the PAC clinic of a tertiary cancer centre in North India between December 2023 and May 2024. Of 1,016 attendees, 33 were excluded due to incomplete PAC documentation, yielding 983 patients for analysis. Time to PAC fitness was recorded in days; delayed PAC fitness was defined a priori as > 4 days (upper quartile). Travel distance was calculated as the one-way road distance from the registered residential address to the hospital using Google Maps. The Visit Burden Index (VBI) was defined as: (number of PAC visits) × log₁₀ (distance in km + 1). Predictors of delayed PAC fitness were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression with bootstrap internal validation (1,000 iterations). A Cox proportional hazards model (PAC fitness as the event) was additionally performed due to right-skewed clearance times.ResultsThe mean age was 50.2 ± 14.1 years, and 533 patients (54.2%) were male. The median time to PAC fitness was 1 day (IQR 1–4), while the mean was 5.4 days. Known comorbidities were present in 409 patients (42.6%), and newly detected medical conditions were identified in 170 (17.7%). PAC fitness was achieved in one visit by 401 patients (40.8%), two visits by 503 (51.2%), and three or more visits by 79 (8.0%). Among those requiring multiple visits, the predominant reasons were pending investigations (n = 415, 71.4%) and uncontrolled medical illness (n = 167, 28.6%). Delay rates increased across VBI tertiles (4.8%, 26.5%, and 41.4%). In multivariable analysis, known comorbidity (aOR 2.98, 95% CI 2.08–4.26), newly detected medical conditions (aOR 3.52, 95% CI 2.39–5.19), and VBI (aOR 1.23 per unit, 95% CI 1.17–1.29) independently predicted delayed PAC fitness (all p < 0.001). Model discrimination was good (AUC 0.807; optimism-adjusted AUC 0.77). In Cox analysis, higher VBI (HR 0.88 per unit, 95% CI 0.84–0.92), known comorbidity (HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.61–0.83), and newly detected medical conditions (HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.59–0.85) were independently associated with longer time to PAC fitness (all p < 0.001).ConclusionDelayed PAC fitness reflects both comorbidity burden—including newly detected conditions—and modifiable system-level factors, particularly incomplete investigations. Introduction

Efficient pre-anesthesia evaluation is a critical component of perioperative care, particularly in oncology, where surgical timelines directly influence treatment planning, disease progression, and overall outcomes [1, 2]. Tertiary cancer centres in India serve large and geographically diverse catchment areas and manage patients with cancer who often have complex oncologic and medical profiles [3]. In such high-volume settings, delays in obtaining pre-anesthesia check-up (PAC) fitness can create workflow bottlenecks, increase logistical and financial burden on patients with cancer, and postpone time-sensitive cancer surgeries.

Achieving PAC fitness is influenced by several interrelated factors, including inadequately controlled baseline comorbidities, medical conditions newly identified during evaluation, and delays in completing laboratory or imaging investigations, often necessitating repeat PAC visits [4]. Despite the clinical relevance of these determinants, evidence characterising their combined effects in real-world oncology settings remains limited. Prior studies have examined demographic and clinical contributors to surgical delay [5, 6], but few have quantified the substantial logistical burden imposed by repeated hospital visits and long travel distances, a concern particularly relevant in India and other low- and middle-income countries.

To address this gap, we developed the Visit Burden Index (VBI), a novel composite metric designed to quantify the cumulative impact of travel distance and number of PAC visits using logarithmic scaling. This index provides a more holistic representation of patient burden by capturing the combined effect of travel-related effort and repeated healthcare encounters, domains that are typically assessed in isolation. Additionally, preliminary observations from our PAC clinic suggested that a large proportion of patients with cancer require multiple visits due to pending routine investigations, highlighting potentially modifiable system-level inefficiencies that have not been adequately explored in prior literature.

In this context, the present study was undertaken to systematically evaluate the performance of the PAC process at a tertiary cancer centre. The primary objective was to determine the number of days required to obtain PAC fitness. Secondary objectives were to quantify overall comorbidity burden, including both pre-existing illnesses and medical conditions newly identified during PAC evaluation; to assess visit burden by analysing the number of PAC visits and travel distance and by developing and validating the Visit Burden Index (VBI); to identify the major causes of multiple PAC visits; and to determine independent predictors of delayed PAC fitness.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective observational study was conducted at the Pre-Anesthesia Check-up (PAC) clinic of a tertiary cancer care institute in North India between December 2023 and May 2024. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Reference: OIEC/11000785/2024/00002 dated 23 October 2024), conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and registered with the Clinical Trials Registry–India (CTRI/2024/11/076616; registered on 11 November 2024). A waiver of informed consent was granted owing to the retrospective nature of the study. All patient data were anonymized and stored on password-protected institutional servers accessible only to authorized study personnel, and no direct patient contact occurred during the study period.

Study population

All adult patients (≥ 18 years) attending the PAC clinic for evaluation prior to planned cancer surgery during the study period were eligible for inclusion. Patients undergoing emergency procedures, those with incomplete PAC documentation, and those who did not complete the PAC process were excluded. During the study period, 1,016 patients attended the PAC clinic; 33 patients were excluded due to incomplete PAC documentation, resulting in a final study cohort of 983 patients.

The primary objective was to determine the total number of days required to obtain PAC fitness. Secondary objectives were:

Data collection

Data were extracted from electronic medical records and PAC documentation. Recorded variables included demographic characteristics, comorbidities, newly diagnosed medical conditions, number of PAC visits, investigations advised, travel distance, and the number of days required to obtain PAC fitness.

Measurement of travel distance and construction of the visit burden index

Travel distance was calculated using the residential address recorded at hospital registration. For each patient, the one-way road distance from the place of residence to the tertiary cancer centre was estimated in kilometres using Google Maps and treated as a patient-level variable. As a regional referral centre, the institution receives patients from a wide geographic area, resulting in a markedly right-skewed distribution of travel distances.

In this formulation, the number of PAC visits was retained as a linear component to reflect the discrete and additive burden imposed by each additional hospital visit. Travel distance was logarithmically transformed to limit the influence of extreme values inherent to tertiary referral patterns and to account for diminishing marginal burden at higher distances. The multiplicative structure of the VBI captures the combined effect of visit frequency and travel distance, yielding a balanced and interpretable measure of patient-level visit–travel burden.

Categorization of reasons for multiple PAC visits

Causes of multiple PAC visits were categorized using predefined operational criteria. Pending investigations were defined as the requirement for additional laboratory tests, imaging studies, or specialist consultations advised during PAC evaluation that were incomplete or awaited at the time of the initial visit, in the absence of active medical instability. Uncontrolled medical illness was defined as the presence of a known or newly detected medical condition requiring active medical optimization, treatment initiation or modification, or clinical stabilization prior to PAC clearance.

When multiple reasons for repeat visits coexisted, a hierarchical coding approach was applied to ensure mutually exclusive classification. Cases were assigned to the uncontrolled medical illness category if any condition requiring medical optimization was present, irrespective of concurrent pending investigations. The pending investigations category was assigned only when no uncontrolled medical illness was identified.

Definition of delayed PAC fitness

Delayed PAC fitness was defined as clearance requiring more than four days. This threshold was selected a priori based on the upper quartile (75th percentile) of the observed distribution of time to PAC fitness (median 1 day; interquartile range 1–4 days), thereby identifying delays exceeding routine workflow expectations.

Statistical analysis

Missing data were infrequent (< 5% for all variables); therefore, analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach. Candidate predictors for multivariable analysis were selected based on a priori clinical relevance and univariate screening, with variables demonstrating p < 0.10 in univariate analysis entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. Univariate analyses (Student’s t-test, χ² test, or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate) were used to explore associations between clinical variables and delayed PAC fitness (> 4 days). Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of delay. Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUC), and calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) to estimate optimism in model discrimination, and an optimism-adjusted AUC was calculated. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Time-to-event analysis

Given the markedly right-skewed distribution of time to PAC fitness, a time-to-event analysis was additionally performed using a Cox proportional hazards regression model. Time (in days) from the first PAC visit to attainment of PAC fitness was used as the time scale, with PAC fitness treated as the event of interest. The model included age, gender, BMI, presence of known comorbidities, newly detected medical conditions, and Visit Burden Index (VBI) as covariates. Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 983 patients underwent PAC evaluation during the study period. The mean age was 50.2 ± 14.1 years, with 533 males (54.2%) and 450 females (45.8%). The mean body mass index (BMI) was 23.7 kg/m². Patients travelled substantial distances for evaluation, with a mean travel distance of 180.8 ± 165.2 km (Table 1).

Time required to obtain PAC fitness

In line with the primary objective, the time required to obtain PAC fitness demonstrated marked variability. The median clearance time was 1 day (interquartile range [IQR] 1–4 days), indicating that most patients were cleared rapidly, whereas the mean duration was 5.4 days, reflecting the influence of a small subset of patients with prolonged delays (Fig. 1).

Comorbidity burden

Overall, 409 patients (42.6%) had at least one known comorbidity at presentation. The most common conditions were hypertension (n = 124), diabetes mellitus (n = 119), and thyroid disorders (n = 46), followed by anemia, coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and chronic kidney disease. Newly detected medical conditions were identified in 170 patients (17.7%), most frequently anemia (n = 72), hypertension (n = 30), and diabetes mellitus (n = 23), with additional diagnoses including hypothyroidism, chronic kidney disease, and upper respiratory infections. These findings underscore the role of PAC evaluation in identifying previously unrecognized medical illness (Fig. 2).

Visit burden index (VBI) analysis

To quantify the combined impact of travel distance and visit frequency, the Visit Burden Index (VBI) was developed. VBI values showed a wide distribution, with a mean of 3.50 ± 1.62 and a median of 3.57 (IQR 2.18–4.61), ranging from 0.60 to 11.12 (Table 2).

When stratified by delay status, patients cleared within four days had significantly lower VBI values (mean 3.13 ± 1.44; median 2.51; IQR 2.08–4.36) compared with those experiencing delayed clearance beyond four days (mean 4.66 ± 1.61; median 4.47; IQR 3.91–5.10). This difference was highly significant (Mann–Whitney U test, p < 0.001).

For further assessment of discriminatory performance, VBI values were categorized into tertiles using data-driven cut-offs (≤ 2.36, 2.36–4.30, and > 4.30) (Table 3). A clear dose–response relationship was observed, with delay rates increasing from 4.8% in the low VBI group to 26.5% in the medium VBI group and 41.4% in the high VBI group. This monotonic gradient demonstrates strong construct validity, confirming that VBI effectively stratifies patients according to visit–travel burden and risk of delayed PAC fitness (Fig. 4).

Multivariable analysis

On multivariable logistic regression analysis, several factors were independently associated with delayed PAC fitness (> 4 days). The presence of a known comorbidity significantly increased the odds of delay (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 2.98; 95% confidence interval [CI] 2.08–4.26; p < 0.001), as did newly detected medical conditions (aOR 3.52; 95% CI 2.39–5.19; p < 0.001). The VBI remained a strong independent predictor, with each one-unit increase associated with a 23% increase in the odds of delayed clearance (aOR 1.23; 95% CI 1.17–1.29; p < 0.001). Age, gender, and BMI were not significant predictors.

Sensitivity analysis treating time to PAC fitness as a continuous outcome yielded consistent results, with higher VBI, presence of known comorbidities, and newly detected medical conditions remaining significantly associated with longer clearance times (p < 0.001 for all), supporting the robustness of the primary dichotomous analysis.

The final logistic regression model demonstrated good discriminative ability, with an area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.807 (Table 4; Fig. 5, Panel B). Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (χ² = 24.62; p = 0.002), with graphical calibration demonstrating acceptable agreement (Supplementary Figure S1). Internal validation using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) showed minimal optimism, with an optimism-adjusted AUC of 0.77, indicating stable model performance.

Time-to-event (Cox) analysis

In the time-to-event analysis using a Cox proportional hazards model with PAC fitness as the event (Supplementary Table S1), higher VBI was independently associated with a significantly lower hazard of achieving PAC fitness, indicating prolonged time to clearance (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] 0.88 per unit increase; 95% CI 0.84–0.92; p < 0.001). Similarly, known comorbidities (adjusted HR 0.72; 95% CI 0.61–0.83; p < 0.001) and newly detected medical conditions (adjusted HR 0.71; 95% CI 0.59–0.85; p < 0.001) were associated with delayed attainment of PAC fitness. Age, gender, and BMI were not significantly associated with time to PAC fitness.These findings were directionally and statistically consistent with the primary logistic regression results, confirming the robustness of the associations under a time-to-event modeling framework.

Discussion

In this retrospective analysis of 983 patients with cancer undergoing pre-anesthesia evaluation, we observed substantial variability in demographic characteristics, comorbidity profiles, and logistical demands. Patients required a mean of 5.4 days to obtain PAC fitness, although the median clearance time was 1 day, reflecting a right-skewed distribution driven by a small subset of markedly delayed cases; notably, more than half required multiple visits, highlighting the combined influence of clinical complexity and operational constraints. Comorbidity burden was considerable: 42% had at least one known chronic illness at presentation, while nearly 18% were newly diagnosed with conditions such as anemia, hypertension, diabetes, renal dysfunction, and thyroid disorders during PAC evaluation. Importantly, the leading cause of multiple PAC visits was not medical illness but pending investigations, which accounted for over 70% of repeat visits. This finding highlights a major process gap in pre-PAC preparation and pre-referral investigation completion. While incomplete investigations and newly detected comorbidities are well recognized contributors to perioperative delay, this study advances existing knowledge by quantifying their relative impact within a high-volume oncology PAC pathway and by integrating logistical burden into a predictive framework.

To quantify the cumulative logistical strain experienced by patients, we developed the Visit Burden Index (VBI), a composite metric integrating travel distance and number of PAC visits using logarithmic scaling. The logarithmic transformation of travel distance and linear weighting of visit number were deliberately chosen to balance statistical stability with clinical interpretability, ensuring that the VBI reflects real-world patient burden without being disproportionately influenced by extreme values. This approach allows the VBI to capture both the magnitude of travel burden and the incremental impact of each additional visit—dimensions that have traditionally been assessed separately. The use of a > 4-day threshold, derived from the upper quartile of observed clearance times, enabled identification of clinically and operationally meaningful delays while maintaining relevance for workflow improvement initiatives. In this study, the VBI demonstrated strong clinical and statistical validity. First, VBI values were significantly higher among patients requiring more than four days to achieve PAC fitness, with each unit increase independently raising the odds of delay by 23%. Second, tertile-based stratification revealed a clear dose–response gradient across visit–travel burden categories, with delay rates increasing from 4.8% in the low VBI group to 26.5% and 41.4% in the medium and high VBI groups, respectively. This monotonic rise across tertiles provides robust construct validity, confirming that the VBI effectively discriminates between low- and high-burden patients and reliably predicts delay. Third, its performance remained strong in multivariable modeling, reinforcing the VBI as a practical, intuitive, and meaningful tool for quantifying patient-level logistical burden—an aspect rarely captured in prior perioperative studies.

These findings are consistent with existing literature from oncology and low- and middle-income country (LMIC) surgical settings, where delayed medical optimization has been recognized as a key barrier to timely surgery [7, 8]. Prior studies have emphasized the impact of chronic disease burden on perioperative readiness [9, 10], and our results reinforce these observations by demonstrating that both previously known and newly identified comorbidities significantly influence clearance timelines. This study extends prior evidence in two important ways. First, it shows that newly diagnosed comorbidities—identified for the first time during PAC—carry substantial implications for workflow delays, highlighting PAC as a critical point of first clinical contact for patients with limited access to primary care. Second, by introducing the VBI, this study captures the combined effect of multiple visits and long-distance travel in a unified, validated metric. Whereas previous research assessed travel distance and visit frequency separately [11, 12], the VBI provides a more realistic representation of patient-level burden and enhances understanding of non-clinical contributors to delay.

Several mechanisms likely underlie these findings. Comorbidities, whether known or newly detected, frequently necessitate further diagnostic evaluation, medication adjustments, or specialist consultation, inherently prolonging the clearance process [13]. However, the predominance of pending investigations as the primary cause of multiple PAC visits suggests that system-level inefficiencies—rather than clinical factors alone—play a major role. Incomplete pre-referral investigation ordering, delays in laboratory or radiology reporting, fragmented interdepartmental communication, and inconsistent patient counselling may contribute to avoidable revisits. Many patients arrive at PAC without completion of the required work-up, resulting in repeat visits that could potentially be prevented. Addressing these gaps through standardized pre-PAC investigation bundles, mandatory checklists at surgical clinics, real-time tracking of pending test reports, and structured patient counselling may substantially reduce revisit burden and shorten time to clearance.

These observations carry important clinical and operational implications. Strengthening pre-PAC pathways by ensuring completion of required investigations prior to referral could significantly improve efficiency. Embedding dedicated coordination mechanisms between surgical units, laboratories, and the PAC clinic may further reduce workflow bottlenecks. The VBI offers a practical tool for identifying patients likely to experience disproportionate logistical burden, enabling targeted interventions such as fast-track pathways, same-day investigations, or coordinated appointment bundles for high-burden individuals. Moreover, given the high prevalence of newly diagnosed comorbidities identified at PAC, integrating chronic disease screening and management strategies into perioperative workflows may improve both surgical readiness and broader population health outcomes in resource-limited settings. The need to streamline preoperative assessment and reduce unnecessary revisits aligns closely with principles of the perioperative surgical home initiative, which emphasizes coordinated, patient-centered, and efficiency-driven perioperative care pathways [14].

The strengths of this study include its large real-world dataset, comprehensive PAC-derived clinical information, and the introduction of the VBI—a rigorously evaluated composite metric with strong discriminative performance. From a generalizability perspective, it is important to distinguish findings that are context specific from those that are broadly applicable. Travel distance, visit burden, and delays related to pending investigations are strongly influenced by regional geography, referral patterns, and institutional workflows and may therefore vary across healthcare systems. In contrast, the association between comorbidity burden—particularly newly detected medical conditions—and delayed PAC clearance reflects fundamental perioperative principles and is likely to be generalizable across diverse surgical settings. Although the magnitude of logistical burden captured by the VBI may differ between centres, the underlying concept that repeated visits and travel demands contribute meaningfully to perioperative delay is likely to remain relevant beyond this institution.

This study has several limitations. Time to PAC fitness represents an intermediate, process-level outcome and should not be interpreted as a surrogate for surgical delay, treatment modification, or oncologic outcomes, as downstream clinical effects were not assessed. Exclusion of a small proportion of patients due to incomplete PAC documentation may have introduced selection bias; however, exclusions were related to missing data rather than apparent systematic differences in patient characteristics. The > 4-day threshold used to define delayed PAC fitness was institution specific, as no externally validated benchmark exists, and should therefore be interpreted in the context of local workflows. The single-centre design may limit generalizability, as referral patterns, investigation workflows, and travel distances in this tertiary oncology setting may differ from those in other healthcare systems. Additionally, several non-clinical factors—including socioeconomic status, health literacy, and access to primary care—were unavailable, introducing the possibility of residual confounding, particularly in the observed association between higher VBI values and delayed PAC fitness. Although the VBI demonstrated strong internal construct validity, it has not yet undergone external validation; multicentre studies incorporating broader sociodemographic variables are required to confirm its generalizability and utility.

Conclusion

Delays in obtaining PAC fitness arise from a combination of medical complexity and modifiable system-level factors, particularly incomplete investigations. Both known and newly detected comorbidities were strongly associated with prolonged PAC clearance, highlighting the importance of timely medical optimization before PAC referral. The Visit Burden Index (VBI) quantified the combined impact of repeated visits and long travel distances, demonstrated a clear dose–response relationship with delay, and remained an independent predictor in multivariable analysis. In routine PAC practice, the VBI could function as a simple screening or audit tool to identify individuals at higher risk of delayed clearance, enabling targeted workflow adjustments such as same-day investigations, coordinated appointments, or enhanced pre-PAC counselling. Although multicentre validation is required, incorporating burden-based metrics such as the VBI into PAC workflows may support data-driven quality improvement and enhance perioperative efficiency in resource-limited settings.
