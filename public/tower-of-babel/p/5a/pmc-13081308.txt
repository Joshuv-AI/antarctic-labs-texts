Finite element analysis of the pedicle screw in lumbar transpedicular dynamic stabilization that penetrates the lateral cortex of the vertebral pedicle
Authors: Chen Zhao, Meichao Zhang, Pei Li, Lei Luo, Qiang Zhou, Liehua Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe mechanical stability of lumbar transpedicular dynamic stabilization (LTDS) was determined by simulating the degree to which the pedicle screws penetrated the lateral pedicle cortex.MethodsA finite element model of the L4-5 segment was established. Four states of pedicle screw implantation were simulated: in Type 1, the pedicle screw was located entirely within the pedicle; in Types 2, 3, and 4, the pedicle screw broke through the lateral pedicle cortex by ≤ 2 mm, 2.1–4 mm, and > 4 mm, respectively. The L4-5 segment was fixed with LTDS with seven pedicle screw fixation models: Groups A (all screws: Type 1), B (left screws: Type 2; right screws: Type 1), C (left screws: Type 3; right screws: Type 1), D (left screws: Type 4; right screws: Type 1), E (all screws: Type 2), F (all screws: Type 3) and G (all screws: Type 4). The corresponding mechanical indicators were recorded.ResultsAmong Groups A, B, C and D, Group B had the smallest overall displacement and overall strain, followed by Groups A, B, C, and D; the overall stress and pedicle screw stress of Group D were the lowest, followed by Groups C, B, and A; and there were no significant differences in overall displacement or pedicle screw stress among the groups (p > 0.05). However, there were significant differences in overall strain and overall stress among the groups (all p < 0.001). Among Groups A, E, F and G, the overall displacement and overall strain of Group E were the lowest, followed by Groups A, F, and G; the overall stress and pedicle screw stress of Group G were the lowest, followed by Groups F, A, and E; and there was no significant difference in overall displacement within the groups (p = 0.718). However, there were significant differences in overall strain, overall stress, and pedicle screw stress among the groups (all P < 0.001).ConclusionsWithin the limits of the present model, it may be acceptable for the screw to be located within the pedicle and for the screw to penetrate past the lateral pedicle cortex by ≤ 2 mm. The mechanical strength of the pedicle screws decreases progressively when they penetrate past the lateral pedicle cortex by 2.1–4 mm and > 4 mm, so such penetration and should be avoided.Keywords: Lumbar transpedicular dynamic stabilization, Pedicle screws, Finite element analysis, Biomechanics

Background

Lumbar transpedicular dynamic stabilization (LTDS) is a surgical method for treating lumbar degenerative diseases [1–2]. Surgical procedures can maintain the height of the fixed intervertebral space and reduce the load of the fixed segment when moving in different directions, which is the biomechanical basis for slowing degeneration [3]. Gao et al. reported that LTDS could slow the degeneration of adjacent segments and improve the quality of life of patients over a follow-up of more than 5 years [4]. Gan et al. demonstrated that a low-pressure stress environment could promote the synthesis of a functional matrix in nucleus pulposus cells and facilitate the self-repair of the intervertebral discs [5]. Li et al. reported that degenerated intervertebral discs treated with LTDS underwent nucleus pulposus rehydration [6].

LTDS surgery requires internal fixation instruments to continuous play a role in mechanical processes in the body. Therefore, the correct implantation of pedicle screws can promote the subsequent reconstruction of lumbar function [7]. Some studies have suggested that LTDS requires the insertion point of the pedicle screw to be the lateral side of the facet joint [8–9]. In this way, the transverse section angle (TSA) of the pedicle screw increases, and the tulip head of the pedicle screw can be located on the outer side of the facet joint, minimizing the degree of invasion of the facet joint by internal fixation. At the same time, this positioning is conducive to the implantation of the dynamic rod.

In conventional lumbar pedicle screw implantation, abnormal screw positions, including penetration of the pedicle cortex by the pedicle screw, might occur. Chen et al. suggested that after the pedicle screw is implanted, at least 0.5 mm of cortical bone should be retained on both the inner and the outer sides of the screw to ensure that the pedicle does not rupture [10]. The literature reports that the incidence of abnormal pedicle positions ranges from 3% to 55% [11]. An abnormal screw position might affect the mechanical stability of pedicle screws. Several finite element studies have been conducted to evaluate the pull-out force of screws, observe the mechanical conditions of the nail–bone interface, and predict the risk of screw loosening [12–13]. Rouyin et al. observed the pull-out force of different screw positions using a finite element model and reported that, in clinical practice, improper screw positioning (4 mm) significantly reduced the pull-out force of the screw and increased the risk of screw loosening [14].

Given that, compared with conventional screw implantation, screw implantation in LTDS requires a larger TSA, the possibility of the screws breaking through the lateral pedicle cortex is greater. This penetration might lead to biomechanical changes in internal fixation. Therefore, we conducted finite element simulations to observe the mechanical stability of LTDS by simulating different degrees of pedicle screw penetration past the lateral pedicle cortex using three-dimensional finite element analysis.

Materials and methods

Establishment of a finite element model of the bony structure of normal lumbar vertebrae

Normal male volunteers (40 years old, 170 cm tall, 60 kg in weight, with no history of a lumbar degenerative disease) were selected. The L4-5 segment was scanned by thin-layer computed tomography (CT). The tomographic images were saved in DICOM format and then imported into Mimics software, version 15.0 (Materialise, Belgium). After the grayscale threshold and regional segmentation were adjusted, three-dimensional models of the bony structures of the L4-5 segments were constructed.

Establishment of the pull-out strength model

The L4 bony structure model was selected. With respect to the clinical screw placement method, a pedicle screw implantation model was established. The relationship between the screw and the bone was defined as a contact pair model (with a friction coefficient of 0.1). For the solid parts, such as the threads/bone cortex, including the vertebral bodies and instruments, we adopted solid 8-node tetrahedral elements (element type: solid 185, brick 8 nodes), and for the ligaments, we used rope units (element type: link 180); the element density was defined as an element side length of 1–2 mm. Four states of pedicle screw implantation were simulated: In Type 1, the pedicle screw was located entirely within the pedicle, and the pedicle cortex was not penetrated; in Type 2, the pedicle screw broke through the lateral pedicle cortex by ≤ 2 mm; in Type 3, the pedicle screw broke through the lateral pedicle cortex by 2.1–4 mm; and in Type 4, the pedicle screw broke through the lateral pedicle cortex by > 4 mm. Taking the left pedicle screw in L4 as an example, a simulation experiment on the maximum pull-out strength was conducted. The coordinate system of the model was adjusted, taking the long axis direction of the pedicle screw on the left side of L4 as the Y-axis direction. The L4 vertebral model was then fixed, uniform Y-direction displacement was applied to the pedicle screws in the four states, the maximum pull-out strength was recorded at the same time, and displacement–pull-out strength curves were drawn.

Establishment of an LTDS model at L4-5

The initial graphics exchange specification (IGES) format file of the L4-5 bony structure was imported into ANSYS finite element software, version 14.1. After the intervertebral disc structure was constructed, some of the intervertebral disc tissue was removed using geometric processing to simulate discectomy (Fig. 1). Afterward, the model was meshed, and on the basis of the meshed model, ligaments, small joints and other elements were added to establish an L4-5 instability finite element model [15]. With respect to clinical LTDS, the pedicle screw model established in previous studies was introduced to construct the fixation model. In accordance with the experimental design, a total of seven pedicle screw fixation models were established (Fig. 2). In Group A, the L4 and L5 pedicle screws were Type 1. In Group B, the left pedicle screws in L4 and L5 were Type 2, whereas the right pedicle screws in L4 and L5 were Type 1. In Group C, the left pedicle screws in L4 and L5 were Type 3, whereas the right pedicle screws in L4 and L5 were Type 1. Load model and analysis

In these dynamic fixation models, the freedom of motion of all nodes on the lower surface of the L5 vertebral body was restricted to simulate the fixation constraint, and the vertical compression state was simulated by uniformly loading a vertical load of 400 N on the upper surface of the L4 vertebral body. By adding loading to the upper surface of L4 to apply a pure coupled moment of 5 N•m, the flexion, extension, lateral bending and axial rotation states of the L4-5 segment were simulated. After the static analysis and solution operation were performed, mechanical indicators such as overall displacement (meters), overall strain, overall stress (N/m²), and pedicle screw stress (N/m2) in the L4/5 segment were analyzed through postprocessing. The overall displacement, overall strain, and overall stress adopted the global maxima, and the peak von Mises stress was adopted for the pedicle screw stress.

Statistical analysis

Statistical processing was conducted using SPSS software, version 19.0, and each measurement data point is expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. The average of the values in the left bending and right bending states was calculated. Group A was the control group, whereas Groups B, C, and D represented the normal screw position on one side and the abnormal screw position on the other side, and Groups E, F, and G represented the abnormal positions of the bilateral screws. One-way analysis of variance was used to compare the overall displacement (meters, m), overall strain, overall stress (N/m2), and pedicle screw stress (N/m2) among the groups under the five stress conditions. A p value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Discussion

Biomechanical studies of pedicle screws are common. Through finite element analysis, Sone et al. studied the stress of three types of pedicle screws with different needle insertion points in the lumbar vertebrae during bending, extension, lateral bending and rotation [17]. The average stress values of the three types of pedicle screws with different insertion points, namely Roy-Camille, Magerl and Krag, were 80.12 MPa, 92.77 MPa and 66.80 MPa, respectively. Compared with the other two methods, the screw implantation method with the smallest average stress was superior. Therefore, owing to its relatively dispersed stress, the Krag implantation technique is safer for pedicle screw implantation. Without considering the size of the screw, the bone density or the bone structure, the Krag implantation technique is recommended as the first choice for restoring lumbar stability. Compared with the Roy–Camille and Magerl methods, the Krag method results in more outward screw insertion points, with a larger TSA, which is closer to the screw implantation method for LTDS. It can be indirectly inferred that pedicle screws with a large TSA in LTDS have better mechanical stability. Within the limits of the present model in this research, it may be acceptable for the screw to be located within the pedicle and for the screw to penetrate past the lateral pedicle cortex by ≤ 2 mm.

A large TSA of the pedicle screw increases the likelihood of the screw breaking through the lateral pedicle cortex. In this study, a mechanical simulation was conducted on the basis of the most common abnormal screw position in the LTDS. The pull-out strength was greatest when the screw broke through the lateral pedicle cortex by less than 2 mm, followed by when the screw was in the normal position, where it broke through the pedicle cortex by 2.1–4 mm, and the pull-out strength was smallest when it broke through the lateral pedicle cortex by more than 4 mm. Within the limits of the present model of the L4–5 segment, when the screw broke through the pedicle cortex by ≤ 2 mm, the overall displacement and overall strain were the smallest in the states of compression, flexion, extension, lateral bending and rotation, whereas the overall stress and pedicle screw stress were the greatest. The second consideration was the normal position state of the screw. In ideal screw placement, the pedicle screw does not break through the pedicle cortex and is located entirely within the cancellous bone of the pedicle. However, this position had slightly lower mechanical strength than when the pedicle screw broke through the lateral pedicle cortex by ≤ 2 mm. The possible reasons include increased engagement of cortical bone and differences in boundary conditions. The mechanical strength of the pedicle screws that penetrated 2.1–4 mm and more than 4 mm past the lateral pedicle cortex was relatively poor. Minor lateral breaches (≤ 2 mm) may not compromise LTDS segmental mechanics in this idealized model; larger breaches likely reduce stability. Intraoperative navigation to minimize breaches remains advised. With respect to LTDS, long-term failure was more strongly related to toggle motion and cyclic fatigue loosening than to pure pull-out strength. Since this study evaluated only pull-out and static loading conditions, conclusions regarding long-term mechanical safety require further research.

Wang et al. compared the pull-out resistance of intrapedicular and extrapedicular screws in the thoracic vertebrae and included 5 studies with a total of 27 cadaver specimens and 313 screws [18]. The results revealed that the pull-out strength of the two types of screws was comparable, but the strength of the extrapedicular screw was slightly lower than that of the intrapedicular screw. Compared with the intrapedicular screw, the extrapedicular screw was safer to insert. The cortex at the screw insertion point, the cortex of the vertebral body and the rib jointly ensure the stability of the extrapedicular screw. In this study, the lumbar pedicle screw breaking through the lateral pedicle cortex was a bone breach, but the entire screw did not reach outside the pedicle, similar to the extrapedicular screw in the thoracic vertebra. In the lumbar vertebra, the more the screw breaks through the lateral pedicle cortex, the greater the degree is to which its stability gradually decreases, and there is also a risk of nerve damage [19].

In this study, we improved the production of a lumbar instability model. The traditional model involves the removal of some bone and ligament structures. The lumbar instability model designed by Schmoelz et al. included dissection of the ligamenta supraspinous, ligamenta interspinous, and ligamenta flavum; tenotomy of the facet joint capsules; and nucleotomy [15]. Zhang et al. observed biomechanical changes in lumbar intervertebral discs after percutaneous endoscopic transforaminal discectomy [20]. With increasing BMI, the von Mises stress and maximum deformation of the intervertebral disc increased across the seven directions and angles. These findings indicate that discectomy reduces the stability of the lumbar segments. We simulated a discectomy to create a model. The annulus fibrosus incision was incised approximately from the medial side of the pedicle on the decompression side to the annulus fibrosus in the center of the spinal canal. During surgery, the dura mater is usually pulled to the center of the spinal canal. Direct nucleus pulposus forceps were used to remove the nucleus pulposus tissue on the ipsilateral front, and curved nucleus pulposus forceps were used to remove the nucleus pulposus tissue in front of the dura mater. LTDS through the pedicle exerted a cantilever support effect, reducing the load between the vertebral bodies and thereby reducing the compressive stress on the intervertebral discs. Schmoelz et al. studied intervertebral disc pressure under dynamic fixation. The stress of the intervertebral disc was significantly reduced during extension, whereas this decrease was not obvious during flexion [21].

The current experimental research inevitably has certain limitations. First, the geometric shape of the lumbar vertebrae varies across individuals, but the model established in this study was based on one man. Second, the theoretical numerical model based on the finite element method simplified the highly complex spinal system to a large extent without considering factors such as intervertebral disc fibers, muscles, and follower loads. There was some risk of misranking configurations under more realistic constitutive laws. Third, owing to the inherent limitations of the model, the biomechanical data obtained in this study could not be fully equated with the data of actual clinical surgical patients. Fourth, in the present study, the pull-out and static loading conditions in dynamic fixation systems were evaluated, and long-term failure related to toggle motion and cyclic fatigue loosening was lacking. In addition, the following limitations were also relevant: a deterministic single-case finite element, with no variability and no uncertainty quantification; linear, isotropic, and material models; no viscoelastic annulus; no ligamentocapsular nonlinearity; bonded interfaces and an absence of screw thread fidelity; no validation, either against cadaveric ROM/IDP or against published Dynesys/Isobar test data; No follower load/muscles; no facet contact calibration; and the use of P-values for deterministic simulations, which constitutes a methodological limitation.

Conclusion

The mechanical strength of a screw penetrating past the lateral pedicle cortex by ≤ 2 mm was comparable to or even slightly greater than that of a pedicle screw located within a pedicle that did not break through the pedicle cortex. Within the limits of the present model, it may be acceptable for the screw to be located within the pedicle and for the screw to break through the lateral pedicle cortex by ≤ 2 mm. Pedicle screws that penetrate past the lateral pedicle cortex by 2.1–4 mm and > 4 mm result in progressively decreasing mechanical strength and should be avoided. In the future, the mechanical parameters of the lumbar model, such as under conditions of osteoporosis and when the screws break through the medial cortex of the pedicle, should be studied further.
