Evaluation of the quality and reliability of infertility information on Chinese social media platforms Bilibili and TikTok : a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jianjie Zhang, Jixia He, Quanzhi Wei, Meng Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfertility has become a major global health issue, affecting approximately one in six couples worldwide; this problem is particularly pronounced in China. With the advent of the digital age, an increasing number of people struggling with infertility are turning to social media platforms for health information. However, the quality and reliability of such video content remain unclear. Therefore, this study aims to assess the overall quality and reliability of infertility-related information on mainstream social media platforms in China.MethodThis study used the Chinese term “Infertility” as a search term to retrieve the top 100 videos on Bilibili and TikTok. Video quality was assessed using the DISCERN tool and the Global Quality Scale (GQS), and statistical analysis was conducted to explore the relationships among various variables.ResultsThis study ultimately included 163 videos in the analysis, comprising 72 videos from Bilibili and 91 from TikTok. The results showed that the average daily engagement metrics on Bilibili were significantly higher than those on TikTok (P < 0.05). The proportion of medical personnel on the TikTok platform was as high as 90.1%, significantly higher than the 27.8% on Bilibili (P < 0.001). Videos on the TikTok platform were more concentrated in the medium-quality category, while the Bilibili platform had a higher proportion of low-quality videos (GQS low quality: 41.7% vs. 21.1%, adjusted P = 0.024). Multivariate regression analysis showed that the DISCERN score was significantly positively correlated with the number of shares (β = 0.045, 95% CI: 0.002–0.088, P = 0.040). Both the number of likes and the number of comments were negatively correlated with video duration (β = −0.001, P = 0.040; β = −0.001, P = 0.022).ConclusionThe overall quality of videos on infertility-related topics on TikTok needs improvement. Bilibili has a significant gap in this content, suggesting that high-quality content creators—such as medical professionals—should not limit their science communication efforts to a single platform. Creators of science communication videos may consider adopting narrative techniques or focusing on specific topics for in-depth explanations to enhance the accessibility of their content.

Introduction

Infertility is generally defined as the failure to achieve pregnancy after one year of regular, unprotected sexual intercourse or artificial insemination, without the use of any contraceptive methods [1]. According to World Health Organization statistics, approximately 17.5% of adults worldwide are affected by infertility, with an average of one in six couples experiencing infertility [2]. In China, the prevalence of infertility is rising rapidly, increasing from 7.5% in 2007 to 18.2% in 2020, and now affects approximately one in five couples of reproductive age [3]; after excluding couples who do not wish to have children, the prevalence of infertility among couples of childbearing age reaches as high as 25%, and this rate continues to rise [4]. In the Chinese cultural context, “carrying on the family line” is regarded as the foundation of family continuity, and fertility is directly linked to personal dignity, marital stability, and even family honor. As a result, individuals struggling with infertility often face widespread and severe stigma. This topic also involves privacy, and patients are often reluctant to discuss their condition openly, instead relying on anonymous online sources for information [5–7]. Although infertility can stem from either partner, social norms often attribute blame to women, causing them to bear greater psychological pressure [8]; men with infertility, meanwhile, are prone to experiencing shame, family disappointment, and social marginalization [6]. Regardless of gender, individuals tend to seek emotional resonance and shared experiences on social media [9]. Furthermore, infertility treatment is fraught with uncertainty; from diagnosis to assisted reproductive technologies (such as in vitro fertilization), every step may involve the risk of failure, repeated attempts, and high costs. Patients are particularly sensitive to information regarding “hope” and “success stories,” and they generally find themselves in a state of emotional vulnerability characterized by anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem [10, 11]. Consequently, an increasing number of infertility patients are turning to social media platforms to access reproductive health information and seek comfort and support within online communities [7, 9].

With the widespread development of internet technology, short-video platforms have gradually become an important source of medical and health information for users [12]. Among these, Bilibili and TikTok are key channels for the online dissemination of health information in China [13]. While the two platforms complement each other in terms of positioning and content ecosystems, they also exhibit significant differences. In China, TikTok has a total user base of over 1.24 billion, with an average of 587 million daily active users. Forty-eight point 6% of users are between the ages of 25 and 44. Characterized by short, frequent, and fast-paced videos, and relying on algorithm-driven personalized content recommendations, TikTok pushes over 48 million pieces of health-related content daily [14, 15]. Bilibili, on the other hand, has 338 million monthly active users, with the user base being younger ( primarily under 24 years old ) and the platform focusing on medium- to long-form videos [16]. These platforms provide convenient channels for information access and emotional support for individuals facing infertility. However, inaccurate or even misleading information may interfere with patients’ treatment decisions, adversely affecting their treatment process and outcomes [17]. Consequently, existing studies have evaluated the reliability and quality of medical information on social media regarding various systemic and disease-specific conditions, such as gallstones, liver cancer, diabetes, and osteoporosis [18–21]; however, the quality of video content related to infertility on social media platforms has not yet been investigated. Consequently, this study systematically evaluates and compares the quality and reliability of infertility-related information on these two representative platforms. The aim is to provide an evidence-based foundation for content management, health information creation, and public health practices, thereby better leveraging the positive role of social media in the education and health guidance of patients with infertility.

Methods

Video retrieval strategy

This study conducted video searches on the social media platforms Bilibili (version 8.83.0) and TikTok (version 37.8.0) from February 24 to 28, 2026, using “infertility” as the search term. The study included only videos published on or before February 23, 2026. To simulate the authentic search behavior of ordinary users and minimize bias caused by platform personalization algorithms, all searches were performed using a newly registered account with no associated personal information, and the platforms’ default “Comprehensive” sorting method was used. All video searches were conducted on the same device with location services disabled. Research indicates that analyzing more than 100 videos has no significant impact on overall conclusions [22, 23]; therefore, this study included the top 100 videos from the search results in the analysis, selecting 100 videos from each platform. Inclusion criteria: (1) Videos related to the topic of “infertility”; (2) Chinese-language videos. Exclusion criteria: (1) Videos with duplicate or highly similar content; (2) Videos that cannot be played normally or are silent; (3) Videos lacking substantive educational content (e.g., pure music, or videos with visuals but no narration); (3) Commercial promotional videos;

Data collection

Researchers manually coded each retrieved video and then extracted basic information from each video, including the type of publisher: (1) Medical personnel: individual or institutional accounts with a medical background, such as doctors, nurses, and official hospital accounts, which are further categorized into two types: those officially verified by the platform and those that are not. (2) Science communicators: individual or institutional accounts primarily dedicated to science communication, such as science bloggers and health-related self-media accounts; ③ News Media: Accounts of media organizations whose primary functions are news dissemination and public information release; ④ Ordinary Users: Individual users who do not fall into the above categories, typically sharing personal experiences or insights; ⑤ Commercial Accounts: Accounts operated for commercial promotion, brand marketing, or profit; only videos that are purely science popularization in nature were included, while content suspected of commercial promotion was excluded; upload date, username, video duration, days since upload (cutoff date: February 23, 2026), number of likes, number of comments, number of shares, and number of favorites. To identify and filter out hidden advertisements disguised as science popularization or personal experience sharing, researchers first conduct a preliminary screening based on account verification badges, profiles, and historical content to identify accounts suspected of commercial promotion. They then review the actual video content; once they identify any clues indicating product promotion or brand marketing—such as recommendations for specific products, embedded QR codes or watermarks, or sales pitches—the video is immediately excluded.

Quality assessment

Two researchers, after completing relevant training, independently assessed the content quality of all videos using the DISCERN and GQS tools. In cases of scoring discrepancies, a third researcher acted as an arbitrator to re-evaluate the disputed videos and provide a final score. To minimize inter-rater bias, all evaluators underwent standardized training prior to the evaluation, covering the use of the DISCERN and GQS tools and detailed scoring criteria. To ensure inter-rater consistency, the two evaluators randomly selected 30 infertility-related videos outside the study scope for a pilot evaluation. The results showed high consistency in the evaluators’ understanding and application of the tools (DISCERN ICC = 0.92; GQS grade-weighted Kappa = 0.75). For any discrepancies identified during the pilot evaluation, the two evaluators engaged in in-depth discussions until consensus was reached, ensuring consistency in the evaluation of all items.

Evaluation tool

The DISCERN tool for assessing the quality of health information was jointly developed in 1999 by the University of Oxford and the National Health Service (NHS) in the United Kingdom. The tool consists of 16 questions divided into three sections: reliability of the publication (questions 1–8), quality of information on treatment options (questions 9–15), and an overall score for the publication (question 16). DISCERN uses two grading systems: (1) Grading video quality based on the score in the third section: video scores of 1–2, 3–4, and 5 correspond to poor, moderate, and excellent, respectively; (2) Grading videos based on the combined scores from the first and second sections: total video scores of 63–75, 51–62, 39–50, and 27–38 correspond to excellent, good, moderate, and poor, respectively [24]. Although the DISCERN scale was originally designed for written health information, its core dimensions align closely with the need to evaluate the quality of information presented in short videos; as a result, it has been widely validated in similar studies. Furthermore, the scale’s assessment criteria align with the focus of this study, which is to evaluate the quality and reliability of video information related to infertility.

The Global Quality Score (GQS), developed by Bernard et al., is used to evaluate the quality of video content based on the practical value of the information it contains. The scale consists of five items that assess dimensions such as information quality, fluency of expression, and ease of use, employing a 5-point Likert scale (1–2 points: low quality; 3 points: moderate quality; 4–5 points: high quality). A higher score indicates better video quality, and this scale is widely used to assess the quality of online video content [25]. This study addresses the limitations of the DISCERN tool by evaluating videos on infertility topics in terms of overall quality, fluency, and practical value for patients.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses in this study were performed using R version 4.2.0. After testing for normality, most continuous variables did not follow a normal distribution and were described using the median (interquartile range); comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages), and comparisons between groups were performed using the Pearson χ² test, the adjusted χ² test, or Fisher’s exact test, depending on the expected frequencies; comparisons of the overall distributions of multi-category variables were performed using the Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test. For pairwise comparisons between “Medical personnel vs. non-Medical personnel,” 2 × 2 contingency tables were constructed, and either the continuity-corrected χ² test or Fisher’s exact test was selected, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons. Correlation analysis was performed using Spearman’s rank correlation. Log-transformed engagement metrics (likes, comments, shares, and favorites) were used as dependent variables in a multiple linear regression model, which included video quality scores (DISCERN or GQS) and pre-specified confounding factors (uploader type, video duration, days since upload, and platform). All tests were two-sided; P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

In this study, a total of 200 videos were identified using search terms on the TikTok and Bilibili platforms; ultimately, 163 videos related to infertility were included in the statistical analysis. Thirty-eight videos were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria, including 8 videos with duplicate content and 29 videos unrelated to the topic (Fig. 1).

Video characteristics and engagement metrics

In terms of video length, the median (IQR) duration of Bilibili videos was 330 (196.5–726), which was significantly longer than TikTok’s 57 (34–98); this difference was statistically significant (P < 0.001). Regarding upload age, the median (IQR) upload age of Bilibili videos was 632 (189–1,140.25) days, significantly longer than TikTok’s 186 (119–383) days, with a statistically significant difference (P < 0.001). Regarding user engagement metrics, to eliminate the influence of upload age on engagement metrics, this study calculated the average engagement per 30 days. The results showed that the daily average engagement metrics for Bilibili videos (likes/30 days, comments/30 days, shares/30 days, and favorites/30 days) were all significantly higher than those of TikTok (P < 0.05) (Table 1).

Distribution of uploader types

There was a significant difference in the distribution of uploader types between the two platforms (Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test, P < 0.001). On the TikTok platform, Medical personnel accounted for as high as 90.1% (82/91), significantly higher than the 27.8% on Bilibili (20/72, P < 0.001); conversely, general users accounted for 38.9% (28/72) on Bilibili, significantly higher than the 7.7% on TikTok (7/91, P < 0.001). Furthermore, the proportions of science communicators and commercial promoters on the Bilibili platform (12.5% and 12.5%, respectively) were also significantly higher than those on the TikTok platform (0% and 1.1%, P < 0.05) (Table 2, Fig. 2).

Video quality assessment

DISCERN Rating: Videos on the TikTok platform were more concentrated in the “moderate” quality category (59.3%), while the Bilibili platform had a higher proportion of “poor” quality videos (31.1% vs. 17.3%). Intergroup comparisons showed that the proportion of “moderate” quality videos on Bilibili was significantly lower than on TikTok (31.1% vs. 59.3%, adjusted P = 0.006) (Table 3). GQS Rating: The proportion of “low-quality” videos on the Bilibili platform was significantly higher than on TikTok (41.7% vs. 21.1%, adjusted P = 0.024), while the proportion of “medium-quality” videos on the TikTok platform was significantly higher than on Bilibili (45.6% vs. 23.6%, adjusted P = 0.019) (Table 3).

Multivariate regression analysis

The results of the multivariate regression analysis showed that the GQS score was significantly positively correlated with the number of shares (β = 0.523, P = 0.014). The number of likes was negatively correlated with video duration (β = -0.001, P = 0.017), and the number of comments was also negatively correlated with video duration (β = -0.001, P = 0.005). Additionally, non‑verified medical personnel were positively correlated with the number of comments (β = 1.973, P = 0.004), shares (β = 2.616, P < 0.001), and favorites (β = 1.84, P = 0.008) (Table 6).

Discussion

Platform differences

The results show that videos on TikTok are generally shorter (median of 57 s), while those on Bilibili are longer (median of 330 s). This is consistent with the findings of Su [18] on gallstone disease and Zhang [26] on HPV-related diseases regarding information quality, which may align with the inherent positioning of the two platforms and user habits. TikTok centers on “short, frequent, and fast” short videos, whereas Bilibili tends to host medium- to long-form video content, providing space for the presentation of in-depth information [27]. However, in terms of information quality, although videos on Bilibili are longer, the proportion of low-quality GQS videos is significantly higher than on TikTok (41.7% vs. 21.1%). This seemingly contradictory result suggests that video length may not be a direct guarantee of information quality. While longer videos should allow creators to provide more detailed explanations, clarify complex concepts, and offer sufficient background information, the absence of professional organization and rigorous verification may actually lower overall quality due to information redundancy. This highlights an ongoing challenge for content creators: how to strike a balance between conciseness and informational completeness?

Multivariate regression analysis revealed that both the number of likes and the number of comments were negatively correlated with video length (β = −0.001, P = 0.040; β = −0.001, P = 0.022). In an era of information overload, “less is more” often proves more effective in spreading content than “more is better.” In this study, long-form videos on the Bilibili platform that attempt to “cover every aspect” (such as those that address multiple topics including etiology, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention in a single video) often fail to achieve ideal dissemination outcomes. Despite striving for comprehensiveness, these videos struggle to retain users until the end due to insufficient information density and a sluggish narrative pace. In contrast, videos that focus on a single, specific knowledge point and provide an in-depth explanation are better suited to the fragmented reading habits and cognitive load characteristics of contemporary mobile users. Users can complete the learning of a single knowledge point in a short amount of time, gaining a sense of “immediate fulfillment,” which makes them more likely to engage in interactive behaviors such as liking, commenting, and sharing. Therefore, health science popularization creators may consider adopting a strategy of “focusing on a specific niche topic for in-depth explanation” to enhance dissemination efficiency while ensuring information accuracy.

Mismatch in user demographics and lack of professional information

Research data indicates that medical professionals account for only 27.8% of the user base on Bilibili, far lower than the 90.1% on TikTok; meanwhile, general users make up as much as 38.9% of the user base. This is similar to the findings of Lei [27] regarding pancreatic cancer and Zhou [28] regarding the quality of palliative care information. Low-quality videos account for 41.7% of the total. At the same time, the user base on Bilibili is generally younger (primarily under 24 years old) [28, 29], whereas the high-incidence group for infertility is concentrated in the childbearing age range of 18–49 years [30]. Based on the aforementioned age structure of the platform’s users and the characteristics of disease incidence, we speculate that among the large number of young users on Bilibili, the majority may not yet have entered the family planning stage or may be currently experiencing infertility-related difficulties; however, less than 30% of healthcare professionals are providing health information support related to infertility. Chinese society is currently facing a widespread trend of delayed marriage and childbearing, with the average age of first marriage and first childbirth continuing to rise; an increasing number of young people are postponing their childbearing plans until age 30 or later [31]. However, as age increases, women experience a gradual decline in ovarian reserve, a decrease in both the quantity and quality of oocytes, and an elevated risk of chromosomal aneuploidy, while men face an increase in sperm DNA fragmentation rates, leading to an irreversible decline in fertility [32, 33]. Many factors affecting fertility are most effectively addressed through early intervention; without scientific knowledge, the optimal window for treatment is often missed. Therefore, providing young people with scientific knowledge about infertility prevention and treatment at an early stage, and helping them establish correct concepts regarding reproductive health, holds significant preventive value. Although this study did not directly collect data on the audience’s age, reproductive status, or information needs, a reasonable inference can be drawn from existing indirect evidence: this young demographic—which may face or is currently facing infertility-related issues—encounters a large volume of low-quality personal experience-sharing content on the platform, while professional content that provides genuine scientific guidance remains relatively scarce. Therefore, we believe that the very young people who most need access to information on infertility and the establishment of correct understanding may, in fact, face a significant information gap. We recommend that healthcare professionals’ science communication efforts should not be limited to platforms like TikTok, where “people with immediate needs” congregate, but should also actively expand to platforms with a high concentration of young users, such as Bilibili. By proactively disseminating scientific knowledge on the prevention and treatment of infertility to the relevant age group, we can truly achieve an earlier intervention in health communication.

The balance between information quality and communication effectiveness

This study further confirms the decisive role of the uploader’s identity in determining information quality. The analysis revealed that videos uploaded by Medical personnel scored significantly higher than those uploaded by non-Medical personnel on both the DISCERN score (median 46 vs. 37, P < 0.001) and the GQS score (median 3 vs. 2, P < 0.001) were significantly higher than those of non-Medical personnel, consistent with the findings of Uslu’s [34] study on non-surgical root canal retreatment and Xu’s [35] study on asthma information quality, underscoring the critical value of professional qualifications in the creation of health science popularization content. However, the value of videos created by non-medical personnel should not be entirely dismissed. Infertility is often viewed as a major crisis in an individual’s life, potentially leading to depression, anxiety, and social isolation. While personal experiences shared by ordinary users (such as the emotional journey of trying to conceive or medical treatment experiences) may have lower information quality, they often possess greater empathy and emotional resonance, offering supplementary value in alleviating patients’ psychological stress and providing “practical knowledge.” An ideal information ecosystem should feature a complementary structure where “professional information takes precedence, supplemented by experience-sharing.” On Bilibili, non-medical professionals (especially ordinary users) account for as much as 38.9% of the user base—significantly higher than on TikTok—reflecting the platform’s unique value in meeting users’ emotional support needs.

Multivariate regression analysis showed a significant positive correlation between GQS scores and the number of shares (β = 0.523, 95% CI: 0.108–0.938, P = 0.014). Sharing indicates users’ deep appreciation of content value and their willingness to actively disseminate it; users share or recommend videos to others who may have similar needs, suggesting that high-quality information, while it may not generate large-scale likes or discussions, still holds significant dissemination and retention value among the target audience.

However, the study revealed a paradox between content quality and dissemination effectiveness: although the proportion of low-quality videos was higher on Bilibili (41.7% vs. 21.1%), its average daily engagement metrics (likes/30 days, comments/30 days, shares/30 days, and favorites/30 days) were all significantly higher than those on TikTok. In other words, information quality and dissemination effectiveness do not have a simple positive correlation on social media; high-quality content posted by professionals does not necessarily garner high engagement, while low-quality content posted by ordinary users may, conversely, receive higher engagement.

Behind this paradox may lie the profound and unmet psychosocial needs of the infertility patient community. In the Chinese cultural context, bearing children is viewed as the fundamental mission of family continuity and a core manifestation of filial piety, carrying profound social expectations and cultural significance [36, 37]. Fertility is often directly linked to personal worth, marital stability, and even family honor. Consequently, infertility patients frequently face social stigma and are commonly trapped in states of emotional vulnerability and repression, such as anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem [6, 10, 11]. Consequently, the need for emotional resonance and social support among individuals with infertility may be just as strong as their need for objective knowledge. Although personal experiences shared by ordinary users may be of lower informational quality, they often authentically depict the struggles, courage, and daily realities of coping with the condition; this storytelling approach possesses greater emotional impact and resonance [38]. Patient narrative videos with high engagement levels may provide a crucial “outlet” for these suppressed emotions [38, 39]. On Bilibili, non-medical personnel (especially ordinary users) account for as much as 38.9% of the user base, significantly higher than on TikTok, reflecting the platform’s unique value in meeting users’ emotional support needs. However, this does not mean that the dissemination of professional knowledge should take a back seat to emotional storytelling. On the contrary, it points to a path of integration. For example, a video posted by medical professional “Hua Hua in the Delivery Room” (which received 33,000 likes and 17,000 shares) serves as a successful case study: the video employs a progressive structure of “narrative—empathy—education,” starting with a real-life story to establish an emotional connection, then providing psychological support, and finally seamlessly integrating rigorous scientific knowledge, thereby achieving excellence in both content quality and dissemination effectiveness. This case demonstrates that the “disconnect” between quality and dissemination is not insurmountable—when professional content is cloaked in narrative, it can gain an emotional advantage in dissemination while maintaining scientific rigor. Future health communication should perhaps not be limited to mere knowledge dissemination. Healthcare professionals might draw inspiration from the principles of narrative medicine, embedding authoritative knowledge within authentic, empathetic stories. This approach enhances the content’s impact and resonance while ensuring informational accuracy, thereby achieving a synergy between “scientific rigor” and “emotional warmth” [40, 41]. By maintaining scientific rigor, we can improve the dissemination effectiveness of science communication and ensure that professional voices reach a broader audience.

Limitations

This study has the following limitations. First, it included only Bilibili and TikTok, without covering other platforms such as Xiaohongshu or international platforms like YouTube. Second, the evaluation relied solely on the DISCERN and GQS scales, resulting in relatively limited assessment dimensions. Third, the cross-sectional design reflects the platform’s content ecosystem at a single time point and cannot track changes in content quality and dissemination effects. Additionally, only the top 100 videos from each platform were analyzed. Since the total number of infertility-related videos may differ significantly between the two platforms, this sampling method may introduce selection bias, as the top 100 videos represent different percentile rankings. Therefore, the findings primarily reflect the quality and engagement characteristics of the “top-performing content” on both platforms, and caution should be exercised when extrapolating these results to the entire platform. Future research could expand the platform scope, introduce multidimensional evaluation tools, adopt a longitudinal design, and conduct user behavior surveys to further deepen the systematic assessment of health information quality on social media.

Conclusion

The overall information quality on China’s mainstream social media platforms (Bilibili and TikTok) is generally poor, with certain differences between them: video quality on TikTok tends to be concentrated at an intermediate level, while Bilibili has a higher proportion of low-quality videos. Videos uploaded by medical professionals generally exhibit higher information quality than those uploaded by non-medical professionals, highlighting the important role of professional sources in ensuring content reliability. Furthermore, Bilibili exhibits a mismatch between its user base and content offerings, with a high proportion of young users but a relative shortage of professional content. Based on these findings, the following recommendations are proposed: Platforms should further optimize their recommendation algorithms to provide appropriate support for professional content; for science popularization video creators, they may consider adopting narrative techniques or focusing on in-depth explanations of specific knowledge points rather than attempting to cover every aspect.
