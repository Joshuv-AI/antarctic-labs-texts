Baseline and long-term frailty transitions and rapid estimated glomerular filtration rate decline: evidence from two large population-based studies
Authors: Ying Deng, CaiLian Cheng, JianHao Kang, XingHua Guo, ShaoMin Li, HuaLiang Liang, JiaHui Lai, LeiLe Tang, Xun Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFrailty is widely recognized to be associated with kidney function decline, but its longitudinal relationship with rapid estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decline remains unclear. This study examined associations between frailty measures and rapid eGFR decline using both frailty phenotype (FP) and frailty index (FI) in two large cohorts. We analyzed baseline frailty data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and Health and Retirement Study (HRS), with longitudinal trajectory analysis in HRS participants. Rapid eGFR decline was defined as annual decrease ≥4 mL/min/1.73 m2/year. Multiple imputation with inverse probability weighting addressed missing data and selection bias. Using FP criteria, frailty was significantly associated with higher odds of rapid eGFR decline compared to non-frail individuals (CHARLS: OR 1.93, 95% CI 1.13–3.29; HRS: OR 1.48, 95% CI 1.10–1.99). Longitudinally, progression from non-frail to pre-frail/frail status was associated with 27% higher odds, while improvement from pre-frail status was associated with 35% lower odds. Frailty improvement in baseline-frail individuals showed no significant effect. Each 1-point annual increase in FP score was associated with 91% higher odds (OR 1.91, 95% CI 1.46–2.51). FI assessment yielded consistent results with optimal population-specific thresholds (FI ≥ 0.25 for CHARLS, FI ≥ 0.32 for HRS). Reverse association analysis revealed bidirectional relationships, more pronounced among participants with baseline eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Both baseline frailty and frailty progression are independently associated with rapid eGFR decline, supporting the potential value of frailty assessment in identifying patients at risk for kidney function decline.

Introduction

Age-related structural changes in the kidneys contribute to a progressive decline in renal function. In healthy individuals, the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) typically decreases by 0.8–1 mL/min/1.73 m2/year after age 40 [1,2]. In high-risk populations, however, the median eGFR decline accelerates, ranging from −1.5 to −4.0 mL/min/1.73 m2/year [1,2]. Substantial evidence links declining kidney function to adverse outcomes, including cardiovascular events and increased mortality [3–6]. Consequently, early identification of rapid kidney function decline and timely intervention have become critical priorities in clinical practice.

Frailty is a dynamic, age-related process marked by diminished physiological reserve and heightened vulnerability to adverse outcomes [7]. Growing evidence suggests an association between frailty and kidney function decline [8,9]. However, existing research has predominantly focused on baseline frailty associations, leaving the relationship between longitudinal frailty transitions and renal function poorly understood. Additionally, research has demonstrated associations between declining eGFR and incident frailty development [10]. The relationship between frailty and rapid eGFR decline may be bidirectional, potentially forming a vicious cycle. Therefore, understanding the temporal associations between frailty trajectories and accelerated kidney function loss is of critical importance, as it may help identify high-risk populations and inform clinical risk stratification strategies.

The frailty phenotype (FP) model and frailty index (FI) represent the two most widely adopted approaches for frailty assessment [11]. Leveraging data from both the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), this study utilized both methods to comprehensively evaluate frailty. Our objectives were twofold: (1) to validate the association between baseline frailty and rapid renal function decline across diverse populations, and (2) to examine the temporal relationship between frailty transitions and accelerated kidney function deterioration. We hypothesized that progressive frailty transitions are independently associated with subsequent rapid decline in renal function.

Methods

Study design and population

The CHARLS and HRS are prospective, nationally representative cohorts focusing on middle-aged and older adults (aged ≥45/50 years) in China and the United States, respectively. Detailed study design information has been described previously [12,13]. This study utilized Wave 1 (2011) of CHARLS and Wave 8 (2006) of HRS as baseline. In this investigation, we implemented a comprehensive dual-method assessment strategy, equally employing both FP and FI to ensure robust evaluation of frailty status. The first analysis included 7,551 participants with baseline FP data and repeated eGFR measurements to evaluate the association between baseline frailty status and rapid eGFR decline. Parallel analyses using the frailty index encompassed 10,029 participants with complete baseline FI and repeated eGFR data. Due to limited blood sample collection in CHARLS (only at baseline and in 2015), changes in frailty status prior to outcome assessment could not be analyzed. Consequently, the second analysis was confined to HRS participants.

In HRS, blood tests were conducted in a subset of participants from 2006 to 2016, with non-overlapping samples in consecutive waves. To optimize sample size, Wave 9 (2008) or Wave 10 (2010) served as the final frailty assessment. The second analysis comprised 7,078 participants who completed baseline FP assessment in Wave 8, follow-up FP assessments in Wave 9, Wave 10, or both, and had eGFR data at and after the final frailty assessment. Parallel analyses using the frailty index included 7,873 HRS participants with full FI and eGFR measurements across the designated timepoints. The final assessment date corresponded to the last available eGFR measurement in either Wave 9 or Wave 10. The study endpoint was defined as the last blood test survey: Wave 3 (2015) for CHARLS and Wave 13 (2016) for HRS. Follow-up duration was calculated from baseline (first analysis) or final assessment (secondary analysis) to the last available eGFR measurement. The participant selection process is illustrated in Figures 1–2. Both CHARLS and HRS received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Boards of Peking University and the University of Michigan, respectively, with written informed consent obtained from all participants.

Assessment of frailty

The FP, originally developed by Fried et al. was assessed using five components: slowness, weakness, exhaustion, low physical activity, and weight loss [14]. We implemented modified criteria adapted for CHARLS and HRS datasets (Supplement Table S1), with these modifications previously validated [15–17]. Participants were classified as: non-frail (0 criteria), pre-frail (1–2 criteria), or frail (≥3 criteria) [14]. Those with missing data were included if their available criteria permitted definitive classification. For instance, participants meeting ≥3 criteria were considered frail irrespective of missing components.

FI was calculated as the accumulation of age-related health deficits [18]. Based on Rockwood’s frailty index concept, Li et al. developed a modified frailty index for CHARLS and HRS, incorporating 30 items including chronic diseases, self-reported health status, functional limitations, depression, and cognitive measures (Supplementary Table S2) [19]. We calculated the FI by dividing the number of present deficits by the total possible (range: 0–1). Participants with ≤20% missing items were included in the analysis [20]. FI scores were categorized as: no-frail (FI <0.10), pre-frail (0.10 ≤ FI < 0.25), and frail (FI ≥0.25) [19,21].

Assessment of covariates

The covariates included age, sex, marital status (married or partnered vs. other marital status), education (below high school, high school or equivalent and some college or higher), smoking status (ever smokers vs. never smokers), drinking status (ever drinkers vs. never drinkers), body mass index (BMI), eGFR, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), C-reactive protein (CRP) and comorbidities. Comorbidities, based on self-reports, comprised hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, and cancer. In the first analysis, covariates were assessed at baseline; in the second analysis, they were evaluated at the final frailty status assessment.

Study outcome

To ensure consistency between CHARLS and HRS, we calculated estimated glomerular filtration rate using the CKD-EPI cystatin C equation [22]. Rapid eGFR decline was defined as an annual decrease ≥4 mL/min/1.73 m2 [23–25]. For participants with ≥2 eGFR measurements, we determined individual ΔeGFR slopes (mL/min/1.73 m2/year) using linear regression of all available eGFR values from follow-up [24]. Rapid progression was defined as ΔeGFR ≤-4 mL/min/1.73 m2/year.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics were summarized using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables. Group comparisons at baseline were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test for continuous variables and the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, for categorical variables.

Assessment of frailty trajectories and transitions

Frailty was assessed using both continuous and categorical approaches. Individual-level frailty trajectories were quantified as the slopes of change in the Frailty Phenotype (ΔFP) and Frailty Index (ΔFI) over the follow-up period [26]. These slopes were derived from linear regression models incorporating all available frailty assessments for each participant. For categorical analysis, participants were classified into one of seven mutually exclusive frailty transition patterns: stable non-frail, progression from non-frail (to pre-frail/frail), regression from pre-frail (to non-frail), stable pre-frail, progression from pre-frail (to frail), regression from frail (to pre-frail/non-frail), and stable frail. The distribution of the seven frailty transition patterns under the FP and FI methods is compared in Figure 3. This composite figure uses a polar (radar) chart to show the overall pattern profile and a bar chart for precise percentages. To connect these transitions with clinical outcomes, Sankey diagrams (Supplementary Figure S1) were constructed to map patient pathways from baseline frailty to the final renal outcome. Primary analysis and covariate adjustment

Multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs), absolute risk differences (ARDs), and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between frailty measures (baseline status and longitudinal changes) and the primary outcome of rapid eGFR decline. Covariates were selected based on established clinical relevance and significant differences observed at baseline. We constructed two sequentially adjusted models: Model 1: Adjusted for demographic factors (age, sex, marital status, education), health behaviors (smoking, drinking status), and baseline eGFR. Model 2: Further adjusted for the covariates in Model 1 plus key clinical biomarkers (BMI, HbA1c, total cholesterol, HDL-C, CRP) and prevalent comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, cancer).

Handling of missing data and selection bias

To address missing data (all variables <20% missing; Supplemental Tables S3-S5), we performed multiple imputation under the assumption that data were missing at random (MAR). To mitigate potential selection bias from attrition due to death or loss to follow-up, we applied inverse probability weighting (IPW). Our primary analysis employed a combined approach of multiple imputation followed by inverse probability weighting to maximize statistical power and minimize bias.

Additional and sensitivity analyses

We conducted several additional and sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings. To explore potential non-linear associations between continuous frailty measures and rapid eGFR decline, we employed restricted cubic splines in our modeling approach. Subgroup analyses were performed to examine potential effect modification by stratifying analyses according to age, sex, marital status, education, health behaviors, baseline eGFR, BMI, and key comorbidities. To evaluate methodological robustness, we repeated the primary analysis using three different approaches: a complete case approach excluding participants with any missing data, multiple imputation only without inverse probability weighting, and inverse probability weighting only without multiple imputation.

We also assessed threshold sensitivity by redefining frailty categories using alternative FI cut-points, specifically non-frail (FI <0.10), pre-frail (FI 0.10–0.21), and frail (FI ≥0.21), and additionally used restricted cubic splines to empirically derive optimal cut-points for frailty classification. To explore potential dose-response relationships with the outcome, we categorized the annual rate of FI change into tertiles and examined their associations with rapid eGFR decline.

Reverse association analysis

To evaluate potential reverse association, we established a clear temporal sequence using two distinct 4-year windows in the HRS (2006–2010 or 2008–2012). We first defined rapid eGFR decline occurring within these windows as the primary exposure. Subsequently, using the end-point of each window (i.e., 2010 or 2012) as a new baseline, we assessed the change in frailty trajectories (ΔFI and ΔFP slopes) in the following years as the outcome. This design allowed us to formally test whether a prior decline in kidney function predicted subsequent progression of frailty.

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.0). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

Participant characteristics stratified by FP are presented in Tables 1-2. The first analysis included 3,255 participants from CHARLS (44.1% male; median age 60.0 years) and 4,296 from HRS (41.7% male; median age 66.0 years). Compared to non-frail participants, prefrail and frail individuals in both cohorts were older, more likely to be female, less likely to be married/partnered, and had lower education levels and alcohol consumption. They also demonstrated elevated cystatin C and CRP levels, reduced eGFR, and greater comorbidity burden. Notably, BMI and smoking patterns differed between cohorts. CHARLS prefrail and frail participants showed lower BMI and smoking rates than non-frail individuals, while HRS participants exhibited the opposite trend.

The second analysis included 7,078 HRS participants (40.6% male; median age 65.0 years), with characteristics detailed in Table 2. Baseline frailty distribution was: non-frail (40.1%), prefrail (47.1%), and frail (12.8%). At final assessment, these proportions shifted to 38.7%, 47.3%, and 14.0%, respectively. Compared to the non-frail group, prefrail and frail groups showed similar trends to those observed in the first HRS analysis.

Participant characteristics stratified by FI are presented in Supplementary Tables S6-S7. While most findings aligned with Tables 1-2, both cohorts showed higher BMI among prefrail and frail participants compared to non-frail individuals when using FI classification.

Associations of baseline frailty with rapid eGFR decline

Detailed results are presented in Table 3. Using the FP criteria, frail individuals were associated with a significantly higher risk of rapid eGFR decline than non-frail participants in multivariable-adjusted models (CHARLS: OR 1.93, 95% CI 1.13–3.29; HRS: OR 1.48, 95% CI 1.10–1.99). The corresponding absolute risk differences were 3.56% and 5.30%, respectively. In the HRS cohort, pre-frailty was also associated with an elevated risk (OR 1.31, 95% CI 1.08–1.68). Furthermore, each one-point increase in the baseline FP score was associated with a 26% (OR 1.26, 95% CI 1.08–1.48) and 12% (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.03–1.22) increase in the odds of rapid eGFR decline in CHARLS and HRS, respectively.

Frailty transitions and associations with rapid eGFR decline

Figure 3 illustrates frailty transition patterns under the FP and FI methods. Stable status was most common (non-frail: 27.68% FP, 18.58% FI; pre-frail: 30.26% FP, 33.72% FI; frail: 7.12% FP, 20.84% FI). Progression from non-frail occurred in 12.46% (FP) and 9.23% (FI), while pre-frail to frail progression was observed in 6.23% (FP) and 9.59% (FI). Regression from pre-frail to non-frail was noted in 3.47% (FP) and 10.6% (FI). Supplementary Figure S1 suggests that frailty progression may be associated with higher rates of rapid eGFR decline.

To further quantify these associations, we examined the longitudinal relationship between frailty transitions and rapid eGFR decline risk (Table 4). Using FP criteria, baseline non-frail participants who progressed to pre-frail/frail status were associated with 27% higher risk than those remaining non-frail (adjusted OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.04–1.55; ARD 4.10%, p = 0.018). However, improvement from frail status was not associated with significant risk reduction (adjusted OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.68–1.36, p = 0.835). Additional and sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses using complete case analysis, multiple imputation only, and inverse probability weighting only yielded consistent results with the primary analysis (Supplementary Tables S8-S9). Restricted cubic spline analyses revealed distinct patterns by frailty measurement approach. For baseline frailty assessed by FP, linear positive associations with rapid eGFR decline were observed in both CHARLS (p overall = 0.001, p non-linearity = 0.492) and HRS (p overall = 0.005, p non-linearity = 0.242) cohorts. In contrast, when baseline frailty was evaluated using FI, both cohorts exhibited similar U-shaped association patterns. While the non-linear relationship was not statistically significant in CHARLS (p overall = 0.002, p non-linearity = 0.108), the HRS (p overall < 0.001, p non-linearity = 0.019) cohort demonstrated a significant non-linear association between baseline FI and rapid eGFR decline risk. Threshold analysis identified an optimal FI cut-point of 0.323 in HRS, determined where the confidence interval intersected with OR = 1. In CHARLS, restricted cubic splines indicated a significantly elevated association with rapid eGFR decline when baseline FI exceeded 0.245 (Figure 4).

Reverse association analysis

To evaluate potential reverse association, we examined whether rapid eGFR decline was associated with changes in frailty trajectories (Supplementary Table S12). Participants experiencing rapid eGFR decline were associated with significantly greater increases in both ΔFP and ΔFI compared to those without rapid eGFR decline (ΔFP: adjusted β 0.016, 95% CI 0.002–0.033, p = 0.024; ΔFI: adjusted β 0.002, 95% CI <0.001–0.003, p = 0.040). However, after full multivariable adjustment, this association showed differential patterns by baseline kidney function status. Among participants with baseline eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2, no significant association was observed between rapid eGFR decline and subsequent frailty progression. In contrast, among those with baseline eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, rapid eGFR decline remained significantly associated with accelerated frailty progression.

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study with repeated assessments, we evaluated the associations of baseline frailty and frailty transitions with rapid eGFR decline. Our key findings demonstrate that baseline frailty and worsening frailty status were consistently associated with an elevated risk of rapid eGFR decline. In the first analysis, frail participants were associated with a significantly higher risk of rapid eGFR decline across both cohorts compared to non-frail individuals, whereas the association for pre-frailty was less consistent. Notably, when frailty was analyzed as a continuous variable (using both FP and FI methods), a graded association with rapid eGFR decline risk was observed with advancing frailty severity. Secondary analyses revealed that frailty progression was associated with a higher risk of rapid eGFR decline in initially non-frail and pre-frail participants. However, among those with baseline frailty, improvement in frailty status was not significantly associated with risk mitigation. These results were robust regardless of the frailty assessment method (FP or FI). Importantly, our reverse association analysis revealed bidirectional associations between frailty and kidney function decline. Rapid eGFR decline was associated with subsequent acceleration of frailty progression, suggesting potential reciprocal relationships where declining kidney function is associated with frailty development, which in turn correlates with further renal function changes. This bidirectional association was particularly pronounced among participants with baseline kidney impairment, indicating that these relationships may be more relevant in the context of established kidney dysfunction. Clinically, our findings reveal important associations that may inform patient care strategies. The observed relationships between frailty trajectories and kidney function decline suggest that non-frail and pre-frail individuals represent populations with differential associations with renal functional deterioration. However, once frailty is established, the association between its improvement and kidney function preservation appears to be attenuated, highlighting that early identification of at-risk individuals may be particularly valuable for clinical monitoring. The demonstration of bidirectional associations further emphasizes the interconnected nature of these conditions and suggests that patients presenting with either condition may warrant closer surveillance for the development of the other.

Previous studies by Wang et al. and Li et al. have consistently demonstrated the association between baseline frailty and accelerated kidney function decline, though these investigations were limited by their relatively small sample sizes and reliance on single frailty assessment tool [8,9]. The current study addresses these limitations by extending the investigation to a broader population of middle-aged and older adults across the spectrum of renal function, while employing two independent cohorts and validating findings through complementary frailty assessment methods (FP and FI), thereby providing robust evidence that greater baseline frailty severity is significantly associated with rapid eGFR decline.

Current evidence supports the dynamic nature of frailty status. When assessed using the FP criteria, 65.06% of participants maintained their baseline frailty status throughout the study period, while 16.25% demonstrated improvement and 18.69% experienced deterioration in their frailty status. These proportions shifted to 73.15%, 8.03%, and 18.82%, respectively, when assessed using the FI method. Our findings are consistent with existing literature. A meta-analysis of 16 studies (n = 42,775) in community-dwelling older adults (≥60 years) reported similar transition patterns over 3.9 years: 13.7% improved, 29.1% declined, and >50% remained stable [27]. Gill et al.’s longitudinal study (n = 754, ≥70 years) further demonstrated that frailty deterioration occurred more frequently than improvement across 18-month intervals [28]. Collectively, these studies corroborate our observation that frailty status remains stable in most individuals, with improvement being less common than decline.

Building upon this understanding of frailty dynamics, we investigated associated clinical findings. To our knowledge, this represents the first longitudinal study to establish temporal associations between frailty transitions and rapid kidney function decline. Previous studies have examined frailty transitions and incident chronic kidney disease (CKD), demonstrating that frailty improvement is associated with reduced CKD risk while frailty progression correlates with increased risk, indirectly supporting the relationship between frailty transitions and kidney function changes [29]. However, these studies did not specifically examine rapid kidney function decline and were limited by single cohorts and assessment methods. The current study addresses these limitations through an expanded multi-cohort design with sequential assessments using both FP and FI approaches, yielding consistent results across methods. We also investigated the reverse association, establishing the bidirectional nature of this relationship. Key findings demonstrate: (1) a robust dose-response association between frailty progression and accelerated eGFR decline; (2) clinically meaningful associations between frailty maintenance/improvement and preserved kidney function in non-frail and pre-frail populations; and (3) no significant association between frailty improvement and kidney function preservation in established frail patients. Our results provide evidence for bidirectional associations: frailty progression is associated with accelerated kidney function decline while rapid eGFR decline is associated with frailty progression among those with preexisting renal impairment, suggesting interconnected pathophysiological processes that may be associated with each other in vulnerable populations.

The association between frailty and kidney function impairment involves multiple interconnected pathophysiological mechanisms. Frailty accelerates biological aging processes, including renal aging, through chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysfunction. Both conditions are characterized by elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and C-reactive protein [30,31]. IL-6 serves as a pivotal mediator, promoting muscle protein degradation while mediating vascular endothelial dysfunction [32]. These inflammatory mediators activate nuclear factor-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathways, promoting protein catabolism and inhibiting muscle protein synthesis, establishing a vicious cycle between frailty and kidney function decline [33]. Frailty development involves mitochondrial damage and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, triggering age-related vascular changes that induce kidney function decline [34]. Conversely, reduced kidney function increases ROS production while diminishing antioxidant capacity. This creates a self-perpetuating cycle where oxidative stress impairs mitochondrial function through protein oxidation and lipid peroxidation, resulting in decreased ATP production and accelerated muscle atrophy [35].

Our findings demonstrate that frailty improvement is not associated with reduced rapid eGFR decline risk in baseline-frail populations, potentially reflecting the complex interplay of pathological changes and inflammatory processes that may persist despite frailty status improvements. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that the limited sample size of baseline-frail individuals or relatively short follow-up period may have precluded detection of potential associations between frailty improvement and kidney function preservation. Future studies with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up are warranted to further explore this relationship.

FP and FI represent two distinct yet complementary approaches to frailty assessment. While FI is sometimes implemented clinically as a categorical variable to distinguish normal from abnormal risk states, its key distinguishing feature is its continuous nature [36,37]. This continuous property minimizes misclassification risks inherent in arbitrary threshold definitions and demonstrates greater sensitivity to longitudinal changes compared to FP [38]. Our analyses reflect this methodological distinction: when FI was analyzed categorically, baseline frailty showed no significant association with rapid eGFR decline. However, continuous FI analysis revealed significant associations in both cohorts.

Restricted cubic spline analysis in both cohorts revealed similar U-shaped patterns between the baseline Frailty Index (FI) and the risk of rapid eGFR decline. In the HRS cohort, this non-linear association was statistically significant (p for non-linear = 0.019). The spline curve indicated that while risk was lowest at moderate FI levels, it began to increase significantly once the baseline FI exceeded the threshold of 0.323, the point at which the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval crossed the null-effect line of 1.0. Of particular note is the other end of the U-shaped curve in the HRS cohort, which suggested a seemingly counterintuitive increase in risk at very low FI levels. However, this observation must be interpreted with considerable caution, as this apparent risk elevation lacks statistical significance, evidenced by a wide 95% confidence interval that spans across the null-effect line of 1. This statistical instability is likely attributable to the small sample size at the extreme low end of the FI distribution and may also reflect the inherent complexities of frailty assessment, where extremely low scores could potentially be associated with underlying subclinical pathophysiological processes. In contrast, the non-linear relationship in the CHARLS cohort did not reach statistical significance (p for non-linear = 0.108). Although the spline curve visually suggested a potential risk elevation when the baseline FI exceeded approximately 0.245, this observation requires cautious interpretation given the lack of statistical power. The divergence in statistical significance and risk thresholds between the cohorts may be explained by key demographic and clinical differences. It is plausible that in the HRS cohort, characterized by an older age profile, higher comorbidity burden (particularly diabetes and cardiovascular disease), and a greater baseline inflammatory state, the association between lower-level frailty and kidney function is obscured by these competing risk factors. Consequently, a more substantial degree of frailty-associated physiological dysregulation may be necessary for its specific association with rapid eGFR decline to become statistically apparent.

While our study advances current understanding, several limitations warrant consideration. First, BMI calculations in the HRS cohort relied on self-reported height and weight due to incomplete physical measurements across study waves, potentially introducing reporting bias. Nevertheless, previous validation studies have demonstrated strong concordance between self-reported and measured BMI, mitigating concerns about substantial measurement error [39,40]. Second, our analysis of frailty transitions and renal outcomes was limited to the HRS cohort because CHARLS lacked multiple eGFR measurements, precluding external validation from independent populations and longitudinal frailty analysis in the Chinese cohort. Nevertheless, the consistent results obtained using both FP and FI assessment methods strengthen the internal validity of our findings. Third, our study population consisted of middle-aged and older adults, which may limit the generalizability of findings to younger populations. Fourth, while we used cystatin C-based eGFR to minimize confounding from muscle mass variations—particularly relevant given the relationship between frailty and sarcopenia—eGFRcysc has its own limitations [9]. Cystatin C levels may be influenced by thyroid function, systemic inflammation, and corticosteroid use, factors that could potentially confound the frailty-kidney function relationship [41]. Additionally, like all eGFR equations, eGFRcysc provides estimates rather than direct GFR measurements and may have reduced accuracy at extreme values. Fifth, the study did not examine individual components of the FI and FP measures or their differential contributions to rapid eGFR decline, representing an important avenue for future research into the multidimensional nature of frailty’s impact on renal function. Sixth, our study was limited by conventional visualization methods that may not fully capture the data’s complexity. To mitigate this limitation, We used a composite radar-bar chart to directly compare the distribution of frailty transition patterns between the FP and FI methods, and employed Sankey diagrams to visualize the flow of these transitions and their association with rapid eGFR decline. This highlights the potential of data mining techniques, such as those discussed in the literature [42–46] to uncover deeper patterns. Although comprehensive modeling was not our focus, this visual framework provides a strong foundation for future investigations. Finally, although we accounted for major known confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be excluded.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that baseline frailty and frailty progression are independently associated with rapid eGFR decline, and that rapid kidney function deterioration is associated with accelerated frailty development, indicating a bidirectional association between these conditions. Although improvement in frailty status showed no significant association with slower renal function decline in patients with established frailty, these findings collectively highlight the clinical importance of frailty status for risk stratification in at-risk populations. The results further suggest that the association patterns may differ across frailty stages, with stronger associations observed during the pre-frail stage, potentially reflecting different underlying pathophysiological mechanisms before the establishment of more advanced frailty states.
