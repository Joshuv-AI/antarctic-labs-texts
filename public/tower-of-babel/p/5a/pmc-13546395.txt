A Retrospective Study of Infections and Antibiotic Susceptibility in Diabetic Foot Ulcers
Authors: Shuai Wang, Zunjiang Zhao, Xiangjian Meng, Hongfei Guo, Yinqiao Sun, Dalun Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to investigate the predominant pathogenic microorganisms, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and antibiotic sensitivity profiles of diabetic foot infections in the southern region of a province in central-eastern China, thereby providing evidence-based guidance for the rational use of antibiotics for treating foot infections in patients with diabetes.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on the clinical data from patients with diabetic foot infections who were treated at a large tertiary hospital between 1 July 2019 and 31 July 2024. Wound specimens were collected for bacterial culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing, and both clinical and microbiological data were analyzed.ResultsA variety of pathogenic microorganisms, predominantly Gram-positive (G+) and Gram-negative (G−) bacteria, were isolated. The top five pathogens identified were Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Streptococcus agalactiae (group B), and Proteus mirabilis. As the Wagner grade increased, there was a shift in pathogens from G+ to G− bacteria, along with a rise in polymicrobial infections, primarily coinfections involving G+ and G− bacteria. The G+ bacteria exhibited high resistance to penicillin and certain cephalosporins but remained highly sensitive to vancomycin and linezolid. G− bacteria showed increased resistance to quinolones, carbapenems, and aminoglycosides while maintaining relative sensitivity to tigecycline and polymyxins. Fungal infections were highly sensitive to commonly used antifungal agents. Overall, significant variations in resistance to antimicrobials were observed among different strains, with some exhibiting notable multidrug resistance, underscoring the high risk of empirical monotherapy.ConclusionsThe issue of antimicrobial resistance in foot infections associated with diabetes mellitus is becoming increasingly severe. Clinical treatment should be guided by antimicrobial susceptibility testing to ensure rational antibiotic use, improve therapeutic outcomes, reduce the emergence of resistant bacteria, and lower recurrence rates.Keyword: Antibiotic susceptibility, Diabetic foot ulcers, Microorganisms, Drug sensitivity

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are a severe complication of diabetes, associated with high amputation rates, heavy medical burdens, and increasing antimicrobial resistance. Local pathogen and resistance data are urgently needed to guide rational antibiotic use.This study aimed to define the microbiological spectrum and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns in patients with DFUs in southern Anhui, China, to support empirical and targeted antimicrobial therapy.What was learned from this study?A total of 339 pathogens were isolated from 303 positive cultures; Gram-negative bacteria were predominant, with Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa being the most common strains. Multidrug resistance was highly prevalent, especially in Pseudomonas aeruginosa (>75%) and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA; 53.6%).Pathogen distribution shifted from Gram-positive to Gram-negative and mixed infections with increasing Wagner grade. Introduction

The prevalence of diabetes is increasing globally and in China in recent years [1], making its complications a major public health concern. Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are among the most serious complications, frequently accompanied by infection, gangrene, and a high risk of amputation and mortality [2, 3]. These conditions impose substantial social, psychological, and economic burdens [4]. Approximately one-third of patients with diabetes will develop DFUs in their lifetime [4, 5], and nearly half experience secondary infections, which delay healing and worsen outcomes [6–9].

. DFU infections are primarily caused by microbial invasion, involving both Gram-positive (G+) bacteria (e.g., Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus spp.) and Gram-negative (G−) bacteria (e.g., Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa) [10]. With the increasing misuse of antibiotics, the prevalence of drug-resistant and even multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria has been rising annually [11, 12], further complicating the treatment of diabetic foot infections and increasing costs, while also significantly reducing the effectiveness of anti-infective therapies.

Given the large diabetic population in China and the marked regional variability in pathogen distribution and resistance patterns, up-to-date local microbiological data are essential. In clinical practice, empirical antibiotic therapy is often initiated before culture results are available, making knowledge of local pathogen profiles critical for appropriate early treatment.

Therefore, this study aimed to retrospectively analyze the distribution of pathogenic microorganisms and their antimicrobial susceptibility patterns in diabetic foot infections at a tertiary hospital in eastern-central China, providing evidence to guide rational antibiotic use and improve clinical outcomes.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

Patients were included if they met the following criteria: (1) diagnosis of diabetic foot infection based on established clinical criteria and (2) availability of complete clinical data during hospitalization. As this was a retrospective real-world study, no strict exclusion criteria were applied. Patients with prior antibiotic use, comorbidities, or varying immune status were not excluded. This study collected data from 449 patients with diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) who were hospitalized at the Burn and Plastic Surgery Department and the Endocrinology Department of a large provincial tertiary hospital in southern China between 1 July 2019 and 31 July 2024. This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Wannan Medical University (approval no. 2024, ethics review no. 176). Written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee owing to the retrospective design of the study. DFUs were graded using the Wagner classification system as follows: grade 0: risk factors for foot ulceration without current ulceration; grade 1: superficial foot ulceration without signs of infection, penetrating through the superficial or full thickness of the skin; grade 2: ulceration extending into ligaments, tendons, joint capsules, or deep fascia without abscess or osteomyelitis; grade 3: ulceration extending beyond ligaments, tendons, joint capsules, or deep fascia, accompanied by abscess, osteomyelitis, or sepsis; grade 4: ischemic gangrene (toes, heel, or dorsum of the forefoot), often with neuropathy; grade 5: gangrene involving the entire foot [13]. The diagnostic criteria for DFU infections were based on the International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot (IWGDF) guidelines for managing diabetic foot-related infections [14].

Specimen Collection and Microbiological Culturing

Wound specimens were collected as early as possible at admission or during the initial wound assessment, preferably before the initiation of antibiotic therapy. During hospitalization, repeat cultures and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were performed when clinically indicated, such as in cases of delayed healing or suspected infection progression. The wounds were first irrigated with normal saline, and necrotic tissue and exudate were removed. Sterile cotton swabs were used to collect secretions from the surface or deeper parts of the wound cavity, which were then stored in sterile tubes and immediately sent to the hospital’s microbiology laboratory. Cultures were performed for aerobic bacteria, anaerobic bacteria, and fungi, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted. The identification results and procedures were assessed following the standards set by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). The multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains were identified on the basis of the interim standard definitions for acquired resistance proposed by Magiorakos et al. [15].

Positive cases were counted on a per-patient basis: One case was counted for each single infection, and one case was also counted for each mixed infection. The number of pathogenic strains was counted according to the actual number of isolated and identified strains; each pathogenic strain detected in a mixed infection case was counted as one strain. Antibiotic therapy was initiated empirically at admission on the basis of the clinical assessment of infection severity. Subsequently, antimicrobial regimens were adjusted according to the results of microbiological culture and antibiotic susceptibility testing when available. The selection of antibiotics generally followed established clinical practice and was individualized according to patient condition.

Statistical Analysis

In descriptive statistics, the distribution frequency of isolates and antibiotic resistance were treated as categorical variables. Chi-squared tests or two-sided Fisher’s exact tests were used to evaluate significant differences in distributions between groups. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20.0.

Results

The age distribution of the patients was as follows: < 30 years (1 patient), 30–39 years (17 patients), 40–49 years (57 patients), 50–59 years (151 patients), 60–69 years (96 patients), 70–79 years (101 patients), and ≥ 80 years (26 patients). The specific data are shown in Fig. 1. Distribution of Pathogenic Microorganisms

In this study, among the 449 enrolled patients, 325 underwent microbiological examination, yielding a culture testing rate of 72.38% (325/449). Because some patients underwent repeated microbiological testing during hospitalization, a total of 474 culture specimens were analyzed. Of the cultures performed, 303 were positive (303/474, 63.92%). Among these, 267 patients had monomicrobial infections, and 36 patients had polymicrobial infections. In polymicrobial infections, multiple pathogens were isolated per patient, resulting in a total of 339 isolates. The isolates comprised 147 Gram-positive bacteria (147/339, 43.36%), 184 Gram-negative bacteria (184/339, 54.28%), and 8 fungi (8/339, 2.36%) (Fig. 3).

Distribution of pathogenic microorganisms in diabetic foot ulcers (n = 339 isolates, 184 isolates of Gram-negative bacteria,147 isolates of Gram-positive bacteria, 8 isolates of fungi)

The top five pathogens identified in diabetic-related foot infections were Staphylococcus aureus (69 strains, 69/339, 20.35%), Escherichia coli (29 strains, 29/339, 8.55%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (27 strains, 27/339, 7.96%), Streptococcus agalactiae (group B) (25 strains, 25/339, 7.37%), and Proteus mirabilis (25 strains, 25/339, 7.37%). Among the Gram-positive bacteria, the top five species were Staphylococcus aureus (69 strains, 69/147, 46.94%), Streptococcus agalactiae (group B) (25 strains, 25/147, 17.01%), Enterococcus faecalis (20 strains, 20/147, 13.61%), Streptococcus lactis (11 strains, 11/147, 7.48%), and Staphylococcus epidermidis (7 strains, 7/147, 4.76%). Among the Gram-negative bacteria, the top five species were Escherichia coli (29 strains, 29/184, 15.76%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (27 strains, 27/184, 14.67%), Proteus mirabilis (25 strains, 25/184, 13.59%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (13 strains, 13/184, 7.07%), and Morganella morganii (10 strains, 10/184, 5.43%). The fungi identified included Candida parapsilosis (4 strains, 4/8, 50%), Candida glabrata (2 strains, 2/8, 25%), Candida krusei (1 strain, 1/8, 12.5%), and Candida tropicalis (1 strain, 1/8, 12.5%) (Table 2).

Among the isolated pathogens, multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains were identified in several species. The proportions of MDR isolates were as follows: Staphylococcus aureus(29/69, 42.03%), Streptococcus agalactiae (group B) (7/25, 28.00%), Enterococcus faecalis (11/20, 55.00%), Streptococcus lactis (1/11, 9.09%), Staphylococcus epidermidis (4/7, 57.14%), Escherichia coli (16/29, 55.17%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (21/27, 77.78%), Proteus mirabilis (17/25, 68.00%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (2/13, 15.38%), and Morganella morganii (6/10, 60.00%).

In addition, specific resistance phenotypes were analyzed separately. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) was identified in 37 isolates (53.62% of S. aureus isolates). Extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacteriaceae were detected in 11 isolates, including Escherichia coli(8/29, 27.59%) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (3/13, 23.08%).

Statistical analysis revealed that among patients with Wagner stage 1, there were six patients with single G+ bacterial infection, three patients with single G− bacterial infection, and one patient with mixed infection. Among patients with Wagner stage 2, there were 24 patients with single G+ bacterial infection, 9 patients with single G− bacterial infection, 7 patients with mixed infection, and 1 patient with fungal infection. Among patients with Wagner stage 3, there were 68 patients with single G+ bacterial infection, 73 patients with single G− bacterial infection, 13 patients with mixed infection, and 4 patients with fungal infection. Among patients with Wagner stage 4, there were 22 patients with single G+ bacterial infection, 50 patients with single G− bacterial infection, 15 patients with mixed infection, and 2 patients with fungal infection. Among patients with Wagner stage 5, there were five patients with single G+ bacterial infection.

In Wagner stages 1 and 2 in patients with diabetic foot ulcers, the number of patients with G+ bacteria was greater than that of G− bacteria, fungi, and mixed infections. However, as the Wagner stage increased, the number of G− bacteria, fungi, and mixed infections gradually increased. A special case was observed in Wagner stage 5 ulcers, which consisted entirely of G+ pathogens. The incidence of mixed infections in Wagner stages 2 and 4 was significantly greater than that in the other Wagner stages, whereas fungal infections had the highest incidence in Wagner stage 3 ulcers. The variation in pathogen types across different Wagner stages was statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Table 3 and Fig. 4).

Pathogen Antibiotic Sensitivity Test Results

All antimicrobial resistance rates were calculated on the basis of the number of isolates, and the corresponding denominators are specified for each analysis. Overall, Gram-positive (G+) bacteria were highly sensitive to third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins, tigecycline, vancomycin, linezolid, quinupristin/dalfopristin, daptomycin, teicoplanin, and rifampin. However, resistance rates were notably high for penicillin, erythromycin, clindamycin, and ciprofloxacin. Notably, the major Gram-positive pathogen Staphylococcus aureus exhibited high sensitivity to levofloxacin, whereas Enterococcus faecalis showed good sensitivity to penicillin and ampicillin but extremely low sensitivity to quinupristin/dalfopristin (Table 6).

Discussion

Studies conducted in different countries suggest that variations in the microbial composition and antibiotic sensitivity profiles are associated with diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) [16, 17]. While it is essential to draw on microbial and resistance patterns from other regions, a precise understanding of local pathogen distribution and antimicrobial susceptibility remains crucial. In clinical practice, broad-spectrum antibiotics are often selected empirically before culture results are obtained. To prevent antibiotic misuse, reduce the emergence of resistant strains, and improve diagnostic and therapeutic outcomes, accurate identification of the pathogen spectrum and effective antibiotics for DFU infections are imperative. Importantly, by integrating clinical indicators such as CRP levels, length of hospital stay, and amputation rates with microbiological characteristics, this study enhances its clinical relevance and depth, enabling a more comprehensive evaluation of infection severity and patient outcomes, and providing evidence to guide individualized treatment strategies.

In this study, Staphylococcus aureus was the most frequently isolated pathogen, followed by Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Consistent with previous reports, Staphylococcus aureus remains predominant in Western countries [18], whereas Pseudomonas aeruginosa is more common in Asia and Africa [19, 20]. Our findings further confirm that Gram-negative bacteria predominate overall in DFUs [21–23], and their proportion increases with advancing Wagner grade, accompanied by a rise in polymicrobial infections [24]. The mixed infections were primarily composed of G+ and G− bacteria, and some studies [25] have found that the coinfection of Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a typical type of mixed infection.

In this study, we analyzed the distribution of pathogenic microorganisms in diabetic foot infections and found that Gram-negative bacteria gradually became predominant with increasing Wagner grade, while Gram-positive bacteria were more common in early stage infections. In the present study, repeat wound cultures were performed in some patients during hospitalization on the basis of clinical indications. Notably, the majority of cases did not demonstrate significant changes in the types of pathogenic microorganisms, suggesting that initial culture results are generally representative of the infecting pathogens. This finding supports the clinical value of early microbiological assessment in guiding empirical antibiotic therapy. However, in a minority of patients, particularly those with severe infections or prolonged hospital stays, potential shifts in microbial profiles may occur under antibiotic selection pressure. Further prospective studies with longitudinal sampling are warranted to better characterize these dynamic changes. Therefore, early and accurate microbiological evaluation is essential for optimizing empirical antibiotic therapy in patients with diabetic foot infections.

The detection rate of fungal infections in this study was 2.36%, consistent with reports from China [26]. This finding may be linked to the long-term misuse of antibiotics and patients being referred from other hospitals after unsuccessful treatment. These infections are likely associated with imbalances in the bacterial flora of wounds, necessitating antifungal treatment when indicated.

Polymicrobial infections were mainly composed of mixed Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, consistent with previous studies [27]. Such coinfections, including typical combinations such as Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, may involve synergistic interactions that enhance infection persistence and severity [28]. The higher proportion of mixed infections in Wagner grade 2–4 ulcers further supports the need for broader antimicrobial coverage in advanced cases.

A major concern identified in this study is the high prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) organisms. MDR rates exceeded 50% in several major pathogens, with Pseudomonas aeruginosa exceeding 75%, and a high proportion of MRSA (53.62%) among Staphylococcus aureus isolates. These findings are consistent with previous reports [29] and indicate that empirical monotherapy may carry a substantial risk of treatment failure, underscoring the necessity of susceptibility-guided therapy.

In terms of antimicrobial susceptibility, Gram-positive bacteria remained highly sensitive to vancomycin, linezolid, and teicoplanin, in agreement with previous studies [29–31]. Gram-negative bacteria showed relatively high susceptibility to carbapenems, amikacin, and piperacillin/tazobactam, although resistance to cephalosporins and quinolones varied. These findings highlight the complexity of resistance patterns and emphasize the importance of individualized antibiotic selection rather than reliance on uniform empirical regimens.

Fungal pathogens do not exhibit significant resistance and remain highly sensitive to primary antifungal agents such as voriconazole and itraconazole [26]. However, only eight fungal strains were isolated in this study, necessitating further investigation to assess antifungal resistance comprehensively.

From a clinical perspective, our findings support a stratified approach to empirical therapy. For early stage infections (Wagner grades 1–2), narrower-spectrum agents targeting Gram-positive bacteria may be appropriate, avoiding unnecessary broad-spectrum coverage. In contrast, advanced infections (Wagner grades 3–4) often require broader-spectrum regimens covering both Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms, including MDR pathogens [20, 27, 32, 33]. In patients with prolonged disease course, the possibility of fungal infection should also be considered. Importantly, antibiotic regimens should be promptly adjusted on the basis of culture and susceptibility results to ensure treatment effectiveness and minimize resistance development.

In this study, the duration of antibiotic therapy was not systematically analyzed. In clinical practice, antibiotic treatment duration is typically individualized on the basis of infection severity, clinical response, and wound healing progression. However, owing to the retrospective nature of this study and the transition of the hospital information system during the study period, part of the antibiotic usage data was archived in the previous system, making comprehensive data retrieval challenging within the revision timeframe. Although this may introduce some limitations, it is unlikely to substantially affect the overall findings related to pathogen distribution and antimicrobial resistance patterns, which were the primary focus of this study. Future studies with more complete clinical data are needed to further evaluate the impact of antibiotic treatment duration.

The data analyzed in this article are sourced exclusively from a single center within the institution and cover a relatively short time period, thus lacking sufficient representativeness. It is therefore necessary to further expand the scope of data sources and extend the statistical time frame. By doing so, more objective data can be obtained to guide clinical practice. Another limitation of this study is that prior antibiotic exposure before specimen collection could not be fully assessed owing to the retrospective design. Some patients may have received antibiotics before admission or sampling, which could suppress susceptible organisms and potentially influence microbiological results and antimicrobial resistance patterns. In addition, subgroup analysis based on prior antibiotic use was not performed owing to incomplete data. Future prospective studies with detailed recording of antibiotic exposure are needed to better evaluate its impact.

Conclusions

The pathogen spectrum and antibiotic sensitivity profiles of wound infections vary across different Wagner grades in patients with diabetic foot ulcers. Physicians should have a thorough and accurate understanding of local microbiological and antibiotic resistance patterns to guide empirical antibiotic selection, reduce the emergence of resistant strains, and achieve timely and effective infection control for better therapeutic outcomes.
