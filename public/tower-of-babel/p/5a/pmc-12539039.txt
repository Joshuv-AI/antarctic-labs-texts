Association between recombinant human soluble thrombomodulin administration dosages and mortality in patients with sepsis-induced disseminated intravascular coagulation
Authors: Tatsuro Kobayashi, Ryusei Mikami, Mineji Hayakawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground The optimal dosing strategy for recombinant human soluble thrombomodulin (rhTM) in clinical practice for sepsis-induced disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) has not been comprehensively evaluated. This study aimed to investigate whether different rhTM dosing strategies influence mortality outcomes in patients with sepsis-induced DIC.Methods This retrospective, single-center cohort study included hospitalized patients aged ≥ 18 years who were diagnosed with sepsis and received rhTM for DIC treatment between 2011 and 2024. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality across different rhTM dosing strategies (standard-dose or reduced-dose). The distribution of mortality between the two groups was compared using the log-rank test, and mortality estimates were analyzed using Cox proportional hazards analysis. To address confounding bias, we employed a double robust method that adjusted the model with covariates while accounting for inverse probability weighting of the treatment.Results A total of 167 patients were included in the analysis. Of these, 84 patients were in the standard-dose group and 83 patients were in the reduced-dose group. The median rhTM dosage for the entire cohort was 328 U/kg/day, with estimated plasma trough concentrations of 1622 ng/mL and 835 ng/mL in the standard-dose and reduced-dose groups, respectively. The mortality rate was 30% in the standard-dose group and 42% in the reduced-dose group, showing significantly better outcomes (adjusted hazard ratio: 0.561; 95% confidence interval, 0.323–0.973; P = 0.039).Conclusions This study demonstrated that a kidney function-based dose reduction strategy for rhTM administration is associated with inferior mortality outcomes in patients with sepsis-induced DIC. Although our findings are limited by the retrospective nature of this study, they provide valuable insights for future verification.Keywords: Sepsis, Disseminated intravascular coagulation, Recombinant human soluble thrombomodulin

Background

Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is a form of major organ dysfunction caused by underlying diseases and is associated with poor outcomes in patients with sepsis [1, 2]. DIC induces microvascular thrombotic occlusion through excessive intravascular coagulation activation [3, 4]. While treating the underlying cause remains the primary therapeutic approach, adjunctive anticoagulation therapy may provide additional benefits. Recombinant human soluble thrombomodulin (rhTM), which possesses both anti-inflammatory and anticoagulant properties, is expected to improve clinical outcomes in sepsis-induced DIC [4–6]. However, the SCARLET trial, a large randomized clinical trial, failed to demonstrate effectiveness [7]. In contrast, post-hoc analysis suggested the effectiveness of rhTM, particularly in patients exhibiting severe coagulopathy phenotypes [8]. This trend has remained consistent across subsequent meta-analyses, demonstrating the effectiveness of rhTM for sepsis-induced DIC [9–11]. Recent DIC practice guidelines recommend administering rhTM for this condition following an integrated evaluation of previous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [12]. Based on current evidence, the benefits of rhTM administration in patients with sepsis may outweigh the risks of DIC.

While discussions regarding the use of rhTM itself have taken place, there has been little examination of the differences in specific clinical administration practices. The recommended rhTM dosage for sepsis-induced DIC is 380 U/kg or 130 U/kg, with dose reduction advised for patients with kidney failure [13]. However, plasma rhTM concentrations do not substantially differ across various levels of kidney function. In patients with DIC, the relative contribution of kidney excretion to systemic rhTM clearance is diminished, and the selection of a reduced dose based on kidney function may impede the achievement of therapeutic concentrations [14]. Selecting higher dosages to maintain effective plasma rhTM concentrations may improve outcomes in patients with sepsis-induced DIC; however, clinical rhTM administration strategies vary according to institution-specific practices. Currently, limited literature exists regarding the effectiveness of different rhTM dosing strategies for this indication. Given that rhTM administration therapy may provide potential benefits in patients with sepsis-induced DIC, evaluating optimal rhTM administration dosages could yield critical insights. This study aimed to investigate the survival outcomes associated with different rhTM dosing strategies in the treatment of sepsis-induced DIC.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective study included patients aged ≥ 18 years with sepsis-induced DIC who received rhTM at Isesaki Municipal Hospital between 2011 and 2024. We defined sepsis according to the Sepsis-3 criteria [15]. Sepsis-induced DIC was diagnosed using the Japanese Association for Acute Medicine (JAAM) DIC diagnostic criteria, which enable rapid diagnosis in patients with sepsis and have demonstrated strong prognostic value for mortality [16]. Patients with evident pre-existing coagulopathies due to liver failure or chemotherapy before diagnosis of sepsis were excluded.

Outcome measures

The primary endpoint was in-hospital mortality. Additionally, hemorrhagic complications were evaluated as a secondary endpoint. Hemorrhagic complications were defined as all bleeding events requiring transfusion of two or more units of blood during the observation period. Furthermore, we assessed temporal changes in coagulation and fibrinolytic parameters across different rhTM dosing strategies.

Dosing strategy classification

We stratified rhTM dosing (standard-dose or reduced-dose groups) based on actual clinical practices. The generally recommended dose of rhTM is 380 U/kg, with a reduction to 130 U/kg recommended in patients with kidney failure [13]; however, this decision is left to the treating clinician’s discretion. Considering that strict weight-based dosing is often not implemented in clinical practice, we defined the standard-dose and reduced-dose groups based on the median dose within our analyzed population. Data collection

We retrospectively collected individual patient data from electronic medical records. Demographic and clinical parameters included the systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) score, sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score, and JAAM DIC score. Additional variables comprised primary infection site, blood culture results, infectious microorganisms identified, antimicrobial therapy appropriateness, surgical intervention for infection, hemorrhagic complications, and in-hospital outcomes. We documented daily rhTM doses and administration frequencies; no patient experienced dosage changes during treatment. For serial coagulation and fibrinolytic parameters, including platelet counts, prothrombin time-international normalized ratios (PT-INRs), fibrin/fibrinogen degradation products (FDPs), and D-dimer levels, time-course data were collected on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 of rhTM treatment.

Statistical analyses

We analyzed the association between rhTM dosing strategies and clinical outcomes using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on propensity scores (PSs) to minimize baseline confounding. The PS model included only variables demonstrating a clear prognostic impact. Potential confounders for PS estimation comprised age, body surface area, SOFA score, Clcr, primary infection site, and the implementation of surgical interventions for infections. We calculated standardized mean differences to assess group balance, considering values < 0.2 acceptable. We estimated survival times using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared survival curves using the log-rank test. For the primary endpoint (in-hospital mortality), we employed a double robust method incorporating a regression model while accounting for IPTW, and reported adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using a Cox proportional hazards model with covariates similar to those used for PS estimation. For the secondary endpoint (hemorrhagic complications), we similarly used weighted logistic regression analysis to present adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. Temporal trajectories of coagulation and fibrinolytic parameters across different rhTM dosing strategies were assessed using IPTW-adjusted linear mixed effects models with rhTM dosing strategies (standard-dose and reduced-dose groups), time points (days 1, 3, 5, and 7), and their interactions as fixed effects, and individual patient identifiers as random effects.

Given the study’s exploratory nature, we set statistical significance at α = 0.05 without adjustment for multiple comparisons. We conducted all statistical analyses using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [20].

Results

Patient characteristics

Among 195 patients with sepsis-induced DIC who received rhTM during the study period, 167 met the eligibility criteria and were included in the analysis cohort (Fig. 1). The rationale for selecting the rhTM dose reduction strategy was predominantly severe kidney dysfunction (n = 45) and receipts of kidney replacement therapy (n = 23), followed by advanced age (n = 4), concerns regarding high bleeding risk (n = 1), and unknown reasons (n = 10). Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. In the original cohort, patients in the reduced-dose group had significantly lower weights, body surface areas, and Clcr rates and tended to have higher disease severity than patients in the standard-dose group. The primary infection site was abdominal, with half of the patients having concurrent bacteremia. Gram-negative bacilli were the predominant causative organisms compared to gram-positive cocci. After IPTW adjustment, baseline characteristics and disease severity were balanced between the two dosing groups. Estimated plasma concentrations were significantly higher in the standard-dose group (1622 ng/mL; interquartile range [IQR]: 1341–1905 vs. 835 ng/mL; IQR: 597–1078; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). This trend remained consistent after IPTW adjustment (1621 ng/mL; IQR: 1321–1895 vs. 941 ng/mL; IQR: 647–1070; P < 0.001), and the proportion of patients exceeding the optimal cutoff value of 1010 ng/mL [18] was also higher in the standard-dose group than in the reduced-dose group (77.8/83.7 [93%] vs. 41.2/85.9 [48%]; P < 0.001).

Primary outcomes

The overall mortality rate was 60/167 (36%) at a median follow-up period of 24 days (IQR: 16–46) for surviving patients. In the original cohort, the standard-dose group showed a 29.4% relative reduction in all-cause mortality (HR, 0.541; 95% CI, 0.316–0.926; P = 0.025), with a median overall survival time of 99 days (95% CI: 51–NA) compared to 52 days (95% CI: 30–98) in the reduced-dose group (Fig. 3a). This survival benefit persisted in a double robust analysis with IPTW adjustment and Cox proportional hazards model (adjusted HR, 0.561; 95% CI: 0.323–0.973; P = 0.039), with a median overall survival time of 99 days (95% CI: 39–NA) compared to 69 days (95% CI: 22–71) (Table 2; Fig. 3b).

Temporal trajectories of coagulation and fibrinolytic parameters

IPTW-adjusted linear mixed effects modeling revealed significantly different temporal patterns of platelet count changes between the two dosing groups over the 7-day rhTM treatment period (interaction P = 0.017), with the standard-dose group demonstrating consistently greater improvement in platelet count (Fig. 4). Although PT-INR, FDP, and D-dimer values declined progressively over time in both groups, no significant between-group differences were observed for these coagulation and fibrinolytic parameters.

Hemorrhagic complications

There was no significant difference in the frequency of hemorrhagic complications between the two groups (14.6/83.7 [9%] in the standard-dose group vs. 25.1/85.9 [15%] in the reduced-dose group). In double-robust analysis with covariate adjustment accounting for IPTW, the standard-dose rhTM strategy was not a significant factor for hemorrhagic complications (adjusted OR: 1.880; 95% CI: 0.632–5.600; P = 0.256) (Table 3).

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that a kidney function-based dose reduction strategy for rhTM administration is associated with inferior in-hospital mortality outcomes in patients with sepsis-induced DIC. Furthermore, hemorrhagic complications did not differ between the different rhTM dosing strategies. This study highlights the additional benefits of standard-dose administration in the clinical practice of rhTM therapy.

As reported in phase I trials, rhTM is primarily excreted by the kidneys [21]. Based on these data, package inserts recommend dose reductions for patients with DIC and kidney failure [13]. However, previous population pharmacokinetic analyses using data from patients with DIC revealed that kidney failure had only a minimal impact on rhTM pharmacokinetics [17, 22]. Clinical reports based on more accurate kidney function assessment (measured Clcr) indicated that urinary excretion of rhTM was minimal even in patients with DIC and normal kidney function [14]. In patients with DIC, other metabolic pathways, such as degradation by activated elastase, may contribute to rhTM pharmacokinetics, potentially reducing the relative contribution of kidney excretion [14]. Based on these reports, the existing dose reduction based on kidney function may fail to achieve therapeutic concentrations. The essential mechanism of rhTM’s clinical pharmacological activity is the indirect inhibition of thrombin generation through protein C activation, which is concentration-dependent [23]. Considering these pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic properties of rhTM along with our research findings, a standard-dose strategy of rhTM for patients with sepsis-induced DIC characterized by systemic thrombin generation may be more beneficial than a dose-reduction strategy.

Conversely, rhTM functions as a dual modulator, exerting not only anticoagulant effects through protein C activation but also antifibrinolytic potential through thrombin-dependent activation of thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI) [24]. Genetically identified TM disorders, characterized by deficient thrombomodulin function, have been associated with bleeding phenotypes, and plasma from these patients demonstrates a complete absence of TAFI activation [25]. Given this pharmacological mechanism, there remains a legitimate concern that rhTM administration may induce fibrinolytic suppression. However, although laboratory data on thrombin generation markers and TAFI were not available in our retrospective study design, the temporal decline in fibrinolytic markers, including FDP and D-dimer, demonstrated no significant differences between the different rhTM dosing strategies employed.

In a study examining the optimal plasma concentrations when rhTM was administered repeatedly in patients with DIC, the estimated trough cutoff value associated with beneficial therapeutic effects was 1,010 ng/mL [18]. In our cohort, most patients in the standard-dose group exceeded this cutoff, whereas less than half the patients in the reduced-dose group did. While several reports have shown that rhTM administration improves mortality in patients with sepsis-induced DIC compared to non-administration [9–11], outcomes may differ depending on the rhTM administration strategy employed.

It is significant that the standard-dose rhTM strategy not only provided survival benefits to patients with sepsis-induced DIC but also demonstrated a comparable frequency of hemorrhagic complications to the reduced-dose strategy. The plasma concentration of rhTM required to directly inhibit thrombin activity (the concentration needed to double thrombin time) is 8,200 ng/mL, which is considerably higher than clinical concentrations [23]. The maximum plasma concentration in our standard-dose group was 2,737 ng/mL, which did not reach a level that directly inhibits thrombin. Therefore, the bleeding risk in actual clinical rhTM administration practices is considered to be low.

The effectiveness of rhTM in the treatment of DIC has been previously investigated, with the SCARLET trial failing to demonstrate it [7]. Therefore, international guidelines for sepsis management do not consider the use of rhTM for sepsis-induced DIC treatment [26]. Nevertheless, post-hoc analyses and multiple meta-analyses have suggested the efficacy of rhTM, particularly in patient populations with distinct coagulopathy phenotypes [8–11]. A potential factor contributing to these seemingly contradictory findings is the difference in coagulopathy characteristics at the time of trial intervention. While the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis (ISTH) overt DIC diagnostic criteria used in the SCARLET study have sufficient accuracy for diagnosing DIC, it has been pointed out that treatment intervention based on these criteria may be delayed [1, 27]. In other words, the SCARLET trial included numerous patients with uncontrollable coagulopathies at the point of therapeutic intervention, potentially diluting the treatment effect of rhTM in this specific disease entity. In contrast, the JAAM DIC criteria enabled rapid diagnosis of DIC in patients with sepsis [16, 28]. The results of our study, based on the JAAM DIC criteria, suggest that standard-dose administration of rhTM provides survival benefits to patients with DIC.

This study had certain limitations due to its retrospective, single-center design. Due to these characteristics, the number of cases was not designed to demonstrate statistically significant differences, and the sample size may have been small. Therefore, to compensate for the limited sample size, we performed survival analysis using PS matching and IPTW. Additionally, we used the Cockcroft–Gault equation for estimating kidney function, which can sometimes be inaccurate in critically ill patients. However, there is no standardized approach for kidney function assessment in critical care settings [29]. Estimating glomerular filtration rates is particularly challenging, especially in patients receiving continuous kidney replacement therapy, as this affects serum creatinine values. Finally, we investigated factors involved in rhTM dosing strategy decisions as comprehensively as possible using available clinical records. The results revealed that kidney dysfunction and advanced age are the predominant reasons for dose reduction, which are commonly encountered in routine clinical rhTM administration practice. Although these standard practice-related factors were appropriately adjusted through our double robust analysis, the retrospective nature of our study inherently cannot address all unmeasured confounding factors. Although our findings are limited by the retrospective nature of this study, they provide valuable insights for future verification.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that a kidney function-based dose reduction strategy for rhTM administration is associated with inferior in-hospital mortality outcomes in patients with sepsis-induced DIC. Our study provides compelling evidence for the clinical administration practice of rhTM in DIC management.
