Biochemical recurrence following definitive treatment of prostate cancer in Brazil: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Murilo de Almeida Luz, Jonatas Pereira, Ronald Kool, Wagner Eduardo Matheus, Roberto Soler, Marcos Freire, Giovanni Bomfim, Sarah Carolina Gonçalves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite apparent cure after treatment of localized prostate cancer (PC), a significant proportion of patients who undergo radical prostatectomy (RP) or radiotherapy (RT) will experience biochemical recurrence (BCR) within 10 years. This study was conducted to evaluate the main aspects of BCR after primary definitive treatment in a real-world setting in Brazil.MethodsIn this retrospective, single-center, observational cohort study, data were extracted from electronic medical records of patients aged ≥18 years with PC, treated at one clinical site in Brazil, who underwent definitive curative-intent treatment during January 1, 2005–December 31, 2018. The primary objective was to estimate 5-year BCR rate, defined per European Association of Urology (EAU) criteria as PSA >0.2 ng/mL after RP, or PSA nadir + 2 ng/mL above nadir after RT.ResultsOf 971 patients included, 131 (13.5%) experienced BCR within 5 years. Of these, 48 (36.6%) patients underwent RP as primary definitive treatment; 33 (25.2%) underwent RT. Of 5-year BCR patients, 40 (30.5%) underwent computed tomography, and 27 (20.6%) underwent bone scintigraphy. In addition, 69 (52.7%) were deemed non-metastatic hormone-sensitive PC (nmHSPC; 29 high risk, 33 low risk) and 30 (22.9%) were diagnosed with metastatic hormone-sensitive PC (mHSPC). The median time from definitive treatment to BCR was shorter for patients with high-risk BCR nmHSPC than low-risk BCR (27.7 vs 37.0 months, respectively).ConclusionsThis retrospective cohort study provides the first real-world characterisation of BCR after definitive treatment in Brazil, demonstrating that patients with high-risk nmHSPC BCR experienced earlier recurrence and shorter time to first-line treatment than low-risk patients. These data highlight the need for improved post-treatment monitoring and earlier therapeutic intervention in high-risk BCR populations in Brazil.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12894-026-02249-4.Keywords: Monitoring, Prostate cancer, Radiotherapy, Radical prostatectomy

Background

Prostate cancer (PC) is the second most frequently diagnosed malignancy [1] and is among the leading causes of cancer-related deaths in men worldwide, with 1.47 million new cases and 397,430 deaths reported in 2022 [2]. In Brazil, PC is the most prevalent cancer among men, representing approximately 30% of all cancer cases in this demographic [3]. According to the International Cancer Research Agency, Brazil saw 102,519 new PC cases and 19,958 deaths in 2022 [4].

Treatment for clinically localized or locally advanced PC is guided by several clinical and pathological factors, including pre-treatment prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels. The European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines recommend radical prostatectomy (RP) and/or radiotherapy (RT) [5]. Despite these treatments, biochemical recurrence (BCR), defined as an increase in serum PSA (>0.2 ng/mL after RP, or increase of 2 ng/mL above nadir after RT) after primary definitive treatment [6], occurs in 19.0–41.0% of patients within 10 years [7–14]. BCR after definitive treatment is a predictor of distant metastases, PC-related morbidity, and mortality [5], with risk severity varying based on PSA levels and PSA doubling time (PSADT) [5, 8]. Per EAU guidelines, high risk for BCR is defined as PSADT ≤1 year or if the International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) grade is 4–5; low risk if PSADT is >1 year and ISUP grade is <4 [5]. Patients post-RT are categorized as high risk for BCR if the interval to biochemical failure is ≤18 months or biopsy ISUP grade is 4–5; low risk if the interval to biochemical failure is >18 months and biopsy ISUP grade is <4 [5].

BCR necessitates secondary treatments, such as salvage external beam radiation therapy (sEBRT), systemic treatments like first- and second-generation androgen-deprivation therapy (ADT), sEBRT with ADT, and surgical and non-surgical salvage treatments. These treatments often adversely affect the quality of life of patients with PC, making the assessment of progression risk crucial for informed decision-making regarding treatment strategies [15].

However, data on treatment approaches for patients with BCR are limited due to the underrepresentation of those from developing countries and minority groups in phase 3 PC studies [16, 17]. Limited studies from Europe and Asia have reported the incidence of high-risk BCR [18–21]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the key aspects of BCR after primary definitive treatment in a real-world setting in Brazil with a focus on characterizing patients with high-risk BCR after definitive treatment for localized or locally advanced PC.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective, observational, cohort study used data from electronic medical records (EMRs) from Erasto Gaertner Hospital, a comprehensive cancer reference centre in Brazil. Index date 1 corresponded to the initiation of definitive treatment (RP or RT), used for the primary endpoint and all endpoints assessed before or at BCR. Index date 2 corresponded to the date of BCR, used for baseline clinical and pathological characterisation, PSA assessment, and clinical staging prior to initiation of post-BCR treatment. Follow-up extended from index date 1 to 30 September 2022.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Full ethics committee approval details are provided in the Declarations section.

Fig. 1 depicts the study design. Patients were aged ≥18 years, had histologically or cytologically confirmed adenocarcinoma of the prostate at initial biopsy, and underwent definitive curative-intent treatment for PC (RP or RT, including brachytherapy ± hormone therapy, or both) between January 1, 2005 and December 31, 2018. Patients were excluded if they had: no accessible disease characteristics; a history of another invasive cancer; prior/present evidence of distant metastatic disease as assessed by either conventional or next-generation imaging; a history of cytotoxic chemotherapy, ketoconazole, novel hormonal therapy, or systemic biologic therapy, including immunotherapy, before or at index date; participated in a clinical study of definitive treatment for PC or management of BCR.

Study endpoints

The primary objective was to determine the 5-year BCR rate in patients with localized or locally advanced PC undergoing definitive treatment, per European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines [5]. Supplementary Materials (Online Resource 1) describe secondary and exploratory objectives.

Statistical analysis

The study analyzed retrospective patient data with endpoints calculated for the overall population and stratified by low- versus high-risk BCR. Clinical and demographic variables were summarized using absolute/relative frequencies for categorical variables and mean, standard deviation, median, and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables. Time-to-event analyses (time to BCR and time from BCR to initiation of first-line treatment) used the Kaplan–Meier method. Deaths and loss to follow-up were treated as non-informative censoring events; patients who died before experiencing BCR were censored at the time of death. Standard Kaplan–Meier analysis was retained on the following grounds: (i) this cohort comprised patients completing curative-intent treatment for localised or locally advanced PC, a population in which 10-year overall survival typically exceeds 70–80%, resulting in a relatively low probability of death before BCR; (ii) BCR is an intermediate endpoint ascertained by PSA monitoring, not vital status; and (iii) cause-specific mortality records were unavailable in the retrospective EMR data, precluding formal competing-risk modelling. The potential for modest overestimation of BCR cumulative incidence is acknowledged in the Limitations. Exploratory subgroup comparisons (low-/high-risk non-metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer [nmHSPC] BCR; low-/high-volume metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer [mHSPC]) employed log-rank tests. Survival estimates with 95% confidence intervals were provided annually. For the Kaplan–Meier time-to-BCR analysis, high-risk BCR was defined as PSADT ≤9 months and low-risk as PSADT >9 months, consistent with the pre-specified study protocol. For nmHSPC risk classification in Table 3, EAU guideline criteria were applied: high-risk as PSADT ≤1 year or ISUP grade 4–5; low-risk as PSADT >1 year and ISUP grade <4 [5]. The differing PSADT thresholds between these two applications are explained by the use of study-protocol thresholds for the Kaplan–Meier analysis versus current guideline thresholds for the risk-classification table; both are stated explicitly. Patients with missing imaging or metastatic classification data (n = 32; 24.4% of 5-year BCR patients) were included where complete data were available and reported as an ‘Unavailable’ category in Table 3; no multiple imputation was performed. No multivariable regression analysis is reported in this manuscript; the rationale is provided in the Limitations section. Analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.0) and Python (version 3.10.6).

Results

Sample description

Of 1,003 patients eligible for this study, 32 were excluded: 22 due to discrepancies between PSA values and collection dates and 10 due to screening failure or data duplication. The final analysis included 971 (96.8%) patients (Fig. 2).

Primary endpoint

At 5 years, 131 patients (13.5%) experienced BCR. At diagnosis, the median (IQR) age of patients with BCR by 5 years was 65.0 (60.0, 71.0) years. For these patients, the ISUP grade of the diagnostic biopsy was grade 1 for 41 (31.3%) and grade 2 for 37 (28.2%) patients. Similar data were reported in non-5-year BCR patients (Table 1).

Secondary endpoints

Type of imaging modality used for disease monitoring at 5-year BCR

Table 3 describes post-definitive treatment characteristics. Computed tomography (CT) and bone scintigraphy were the most-used imaging modalities. Of the 131 patients with 5-year BCR, 40 (30.5%) underwent CT, and 27 (20.6%) underwent bone scintigraphy Table 3. Fewer than 10% of patients with 5-year BCR underwent magnetic resonance imaging (n = 12; 9.2%), 68Ga-PSMA-PET (n = 9; 6.9%), or other unspecified imaging (n = 11; 8.4%). Imaging was unavailable for 32 (24.4%) patients.

Proportion of patients with nmHSPC, mHSPC, and high-risk BCR

Among the 131 patients who experienced 5-year BCR, 69 (52.7%) were deemed non-metastatic (nmHSPC) and 30 (22.9%) were diagnosed with metastatic disease (mHSPC). The metastatic status was unavailable for 32 (24.4%) patients (Table 3).

Of the 69 patients with nmHSPC 5-year BCR, 29 (42.0%) were classified as high risk and 33 (47.8%) as low risk. It was not possible to classify 7 (10.1%) patients by risk status (Table 3).

Discussion

This study evaluated the main aspects of BCR after primary definitive treatment in a real-world setting and determined the 5-year BCR rate at a single site in Southern Brazil.

The 5-year BCR rate of 13.4% in this study aligns with the lower end of the previously published range of 16.0–26.0% [8, 10–12, 22, 23]. Differences in BCR rate could be attributed to two factors. First, this study included patients who underwent various treatment modalities: RP or RT (including brachytherapy) ± hormone therapy, or a combination of these. Studies like Freedland et al [8], Han et al [12], and Amling et al [13] involved patients who received RP only. Others, such as Boehm et al [22] and Escobar et al [11], involved RT alone or combined with RP. Second, the definition of BCR varied among studies. A PSA level of >0.2 ng/mL [8, 12, 22] is the most common criterion for defining BCR after RP, although some studies [13] used a PSA level of >0.4 ng/mL. Conversely, the Phoenix definition applies a PSA level of 2 ng/mL above the nadir for patients treated with RT [12, 22], contributing to an approximate 10-fold difference [24]. This study’s 10-year BCR rate of 18.9% also aligns with the previously published range of 19.0–41.0% [7–14]. The minor variations observed in this study can be attributed to aforementioned factors.

Previously published studies have shown that the most significant determinants of BCR include pre-treatment serum PSA and Gleason score [25]. This aligns with the findings of the present study, which revealed that patients experiencing BCR exhibited higher PSA levels before undergoing definitive treatment and had elevated Gleason scores.

In this study, only 30.5% of patients underwent CT scans and 20.6% had bone imaging, with 24.4% lacking any imaging records at the study site. Imaging conducted at other sites was not included in this analysis. Most patients who experienced BCR after surgery proceeded directly to salvage radiation therapy, with or without hormone therapy. Routine scans were not performed in patients with PSA levels around 0.2 ng/mL due to low sensitivity and specificity of conventional imaging for detecting PC at low PSA levels, representing a practical approach to avoid increased costs and delayed treatment. The limited availability of PSMA-PET imaging (used in fewer than 7% of patients) reflects access constraints in the Brazilian public health system during 2005–2018 and may have led to underclassification of micrometastatic disease in patients categorised as nmHSPC. Emerging data support the integration of PSMA-PET into BCR evaluation: the EMPIRE-1 trial demonstrated that PSMA-PET-guided salvage radiotherapy improved 4-year event-free survival compared with conventional imaging-guided therapy in patients with BCR after RP [26]. These findings underscore the potential clinical value of expanding PSMA-PET access in the Brazilian healthcare system.

Regarding first-line treatment after BCR in the present study, 8.0% of patients with low-risk nmHSPC were kept under surveillance (no treatment). This aligns with published guidelines [27], which suggest that active surveillance can be considered for patients with low-risk nmHSPC. In the present study, a majority (48.7%) of patients with mHSPC underwent ADT treatment and only 10.3% underwent local treatment (RP, RT). In contrast, 25.0% and 7.3% of patients with nmHSPC underwent ADT monotherapy without and with local treatment (RP, RT), respectively. These differences were expected, considering patients with mHSPC were primarily treated for systemic disease. It is important to note that although ADT is mainly recommended for high-risk nmHSPC [28], similar numbers of high-risk and low-risk patients underwent ADT therapy in the present study. This finding highlights the importance of accurate risk stratification in the BCR population and reflects real-world practice patterns during the study period (2005–2018), prior to the broader availability of evidence-based risk-stratified treatment algorithms.

Since the study accrual window closed, the treatment landscape for high-risk BCR has evolved. The EMBARK trial demonstrated improved metastasis-free survival with androgen receptor signaling inhibitor (enzalutamide), with or without ADT, in patients with high-risk BCR [29]. The potential to delay metastatic disease at an earlier disease stage underscores the importance of timely risk stratification after definitive treatment. Future Brazilian studies should evaluate the real-world uptake and equity of access to these newer agents. The present findings provide a real-world baseline of BCR patterns against which the impact of newer treatment strategies may eventually be assessed [28].

This is the first real-world evidence (RWE) study in Brazil characterising BCR after primary definitive treatment. The data generated contribute to the scarce evidence base on post-treatment BCR patterns in Brazil and Latin America, and offer context on regional clinical practice during 2005–2018. The proportion of patients with high-risk BCR nmHSPC identified here underscores the burden of disease that may benefit from evolving risk-stratified management strategies.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the retrospective single-centre design at a tertiary oncology referral centre (Erasto Gaertner Hospital, Curitíba) introduces potential selection bias: patients treated here may differ systematically from those managed in community or private-sector settings, limiting generalisability to Brazil’s broader PC population. Second, the 14-year accrual window (2005–2018) spans major shifts in diagnostic and therapeutic practice, including wider adoption of intensity-modulated radiotherapy, evolution of ADT protocols, and the introduction of PSMA-PET imaging. These temporal changes in standard of care were not adjusted for in the analyses and may introduce confounding. Third, imaging and metastatic classification data were unavailable for 32 patients (24.4% of the 5-year BCR cohort). The mechanism of missingness was not formally evaluated; the complete-case approach adopted here may introduce bias if data are not missing at random, and may underestimate the true proportion of patients with metastatic disease. No sensitivity analyses or multiple imputation were performed. Fourth, death was treated as a non-informative censoring event in Kaplan–Meier analyses. Although this approach is statistically acceptable given the low competing-event probability in this curative-intent cohort (as justified in the Statistical Analysis section), the absence of formal competing-risk modelling (e.g., Fine–Gray model) remains a limitation: the degree of overestimation of BCR cumulative incidence may increase at longer follow-up times, particularly where non-cancer mortality contributes. Fifth, no longitudinal outcome data beyond BCR — including progression to castration-resistant disease, metastasis, cancer-specific mortality, or overall survival — were captured, limiting conclusions about the downstream clinical impact of BCR in this cohort. Sixth, the multivariable analysis of treatment allocation factors was not feasible due to sparse data across risk strata and is not reported. Finally, because PSMA-PET was available in fewer than 7% of patients, metastatic staging was heavily reliant on conventional imaging, which has limited sensitivity at low PSA levels; this likely results in underclassification of low-volume metastatic disease.

Conclusions

This retrospective cohort study provides the first real-world characterisation of BCR after primary definitive treatment for prostate cancer in Brazil. In this single-centre cohort, the 5-year BCR rate was 13.5%, with RT patients experiencing higher rates than RP patients. High-risk BCR nmHSPC patients had a significantly shorter time to BCR and to initiation of first-line treatment compared with low-risk patients. Access to advanced imaging such as PSMA-PET was limited (<7%), and a notable proportion of patients with low-risk nmHSPC received ADT outside guideline recommendations, highlighting real-world practice gaps. These data provide a regional baseline against which future improvements in risk stratification, imaging access, and treatment adherence in Brazil can be measured. Multi-centre prospective studies are needed to validate and generalise these findings.
